Comparative Renal Safety of Tenofovir Alafenamide vs. Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate in Chronic Hepatitis B: A Korean Nationwide Study
Authors: Jihye Lim, Jiwon Yang, Ye-jee Kim, Hwa Jung Kim, Jonggi Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe long-term renal safety of tenofovir-based therapy in patients with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains uncertain, particularly regarding end-stage kidney disease requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT). We investigated the comparative risk of renal dysfunction between tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF).MethodsWe identified treatment-naïve adult patients with chronic HBV infection who initiated TAF or TDF between 2017 and 2020 from the Korean National Health Insurance Service. After 1:1 propensity score (PS) matching, we compared the incidence of RRT and chronic kidney disease (CKD) between the two groups.ResultsAmong 46,663 eligible patients (TAF, n = 16,885; TDF, n = 29,778), the median follow-up was 2.61 years. The incidence of RRT were 0.56 and 0.96 per 1,000 person-years in the TAF and TDF groups, respectively (hazard ratio [HR], 0.74; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.47–1.16). In the PS-matched cohort (n = 31,218), the adjusted HR for RRT was 0.67 (95% CI, 0.40–1.10) in TAF compared to TDF. Male sex, older age, hypertension, cirrhosis and current smoking were associated with the increased risk of RRT.ConclusionIn this nationwide claims-based cohort, TAF and TDF showed comparable renal safety with respect to RRT and the development of CKD. Male sex, older age, hypertension, and liver cirrhosis were associated with increased renal risk and warrant close monitoring.Keywords: Hepatitis B, Chronic; Renal Replacement Therapy; Tenofovir Alafenamide; Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate; Renal Insufficiency; Korea

INTRODUCTION

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection affects over 290 million people globally and remains a major cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver-related mortality.1,2 Oral nucleos(t)ide analogues (NUCs) are the cornerstone of HBV treatment and have been shown to significantly reduce the risk of liver disease progression, including the development of HCC.2,3,4 Current international guidelines recommend tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), and entecavir as first-line therapies.3,4,5 However, given that NUCs rarely achieves HBV cure, patients typically require indefinite treatment.

Considering the chronic nature of HBV and the increasing life expectancy of affected patients, preserving renal function during long-term NUC treatment is a growing concern. Patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) already have a higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) than the general population, likely due to shared metabolic risk factors and virus-related kidney injury.8,9 Moreover, as patients age, the accumulation of comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension can further accelerate renal function decline.8

TAF, a newer tenofovir prodrug, has greater plasma stability and achieves higher intracellular tenofovir concentrations at a lower dose (25 mg vs. 300 mg for TDF), resulting in significantly lower systemic tenofovir exposure. This pharmacologic profile is thought to reduce tenofovir-related nephrotoxicity. Several clinical trials and observational studies have reported favourable renal safety profiles for TAF compared to TDF.9,10,11,12,13,14 However, these trials often included relatively healthy patients with preserved baseline renal function, limiting their generalizability to broader real-world populations.9,10,11

To address this gap, we conducted a large, nationwide, claims-based cohort study in South Korea to compare the incidence of renal replacement therapy (RRT) and CKD between patients treated with TAF and those treated with TDF. This study aimed to provide real-world evidence on the comparative renal safety of TAF and TDF in diverse populations receiving long-term treatment for CHB.

METHODS

Study design and population for the nationwide cohort

We conducted a nationwide, population-based matched cohort study using claims data from the Korea National Health Insurance Service (NHIS).15,16 South Korea operates a universal health coverage system that includes the entire population, collecting extensive medical data such as demographic information, hospital visit records categorized by International Classification of Disease, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, prescription and procedure details, registration records for rare intractable diseases (RIDs), and results from routine health screenings.15,16,17 All insured individuals are eligible for biennial general health check-ups, which include assessments through questionnaires and laboratory tests.15

We obtained NHIS data for 240,343 patients diagnosed with HBV infection between 2011 and 2022. The study period was restricted from 2017 to 2022, corresponding to the availability of TAF and TDF under NHIS coverage since May 2017 and December 2012, respectively. A total of 87,355 patients with chronic HBV infection who initiated treatment with either TAF 25 mg per day or TDF 300 mg per day and maintained therapy for at least 6 months during the study period were included. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of TAF or TDF during the study period. Patients who switched to the alternative NUCs during follow-up were censored from the analysis. Patients were excluded if they had 1) prior use of other antiviral agents (lamivudine, telbivudine, clevudine, adefovir, entecavir, or TAF for the TDF group and vice versa) within 2 years before the index date; 2) presence of other chronic liver diseases; 3) coinfection with HIV; 4) history of malignancy, including HCC; 5) prior organ transplantation, including liver or kidney transplantation; 6) history of RRT; and 7) CKD defined as having ICD-10 code of N18. According to these criteria, 16,885 patients in the TAF group and 29,778 patients in the TDF group were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Follow-up continued from the index date until death, emigration, the study end date (December 2022), or the last prescription date of the assigned NUC.

Data collection

We collected data on demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, medications, lifestyle habits, body measurements, and laboratory results from health screenings. Socioeconomic status was determined based on income information from the NHIS contribution system. Diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia were identified based on ICD-10 codes and corresponding disease-specific medications.15,16 The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated to quantify overall comorbidity burden.18 Lifestyle habits, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, were evaluated through self-reported questionnaires. Physical measurements included body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and blood pressure. Laboratory data comprised liver biochemistry, fasting glucose and lipid profiles. Supplementary Table 1 provides a complete list of the ICD-10 codes used in this study.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of RRT, including dialysis and kidney transplantation, in patients with CHB who initiated treatment with TAF or TDF. RRT was defined as having claims for haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis procedures on > 2 occasions, or registration with a relevant RID code.16 Kidney transplantation was identified using ICD-10 codes and the RID code.16 The RID registration process for RRT is highly reliable because patients must receive dialysis for at least 3 months or undergo kidney transplantation to qualify. Once registered, patients are eligible for 90% reimbursement of medical costs related to dialysis, transplantation, and associated complications under the copayment system.16 The secondary outcome was the cumulative incidence of CKD between TAF- and TDF-treated groups and its related risk factors.

Statistical analysis

All eligible study participants were included in the analysis. Baseline characteristics were summarized as counts and percentages for categorical variables and median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. The incidence of RRT or CKD was calculated per 1,000 patient-years (PYs), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) estimated using the Poisson distribution. Cumulative incidence was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between the groups using the log-rank test. Both univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis were performed to identify risk factors for RRT and the development of CKD. A propensity score (PS)-matched analysis was conducted with a 1:1 matching ratio and a calliper width of 0.1 to reduce the impact of confounding factors. The PS-matching accounted for variables such as sex, age, socioeconomic status, index year, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia), cirrhosis, CCI, participation in health check-ups, smoking history, alcohol consumption, weekly exercise habits, blood pressure, and fasting blood glucose. Statistical significance was set at a P < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using SAS software, version 7.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Korea (IRB number: 2022-0196), with a waiver of informed consent due to the use of de-identified data.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of the nationwide cohort

The cohort included in the study comprised 16,885 patients with chronic HBV infection who were initially prescribed TAF and 29,778 patients who received TDF. After PS matching, 15,609 patients remained in each treatment group for comparative analysis. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the entire and PS-matched cohorts. The median age of the study population was 48.0 years (IQR, 40.0–56.0 years), and 61.3% were male. The prevalence of comorbidities was 19.0% for hypertension, 16.7% for diabetes followed by dyslipidaemia and stroke. Liver cirrhosis was present in 19.3% of patients. In total, 70.3% of the cohort underwent a health check-up. The proportion of current smokers was 17.8%, while 4.5% were classified as increased or excessive alcohol consumers. In the PS-matched cohort, demographics, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors showed no significant differences between the two groups, as evidenced by standardized mean differences of < 0.1 for all variables.

RRT

During a median follow-up of 2.61 years (IQR, 1.52–3.75 years), 133 patients required RRT. Among the 16,885 patients treated with TAF, 25 patients underwent RRT, with an incidence of 0.56 per 1,000 PYs (Table 2). In comparison, among the 29,778 patients receiving TDF, 108 patients required RRT, with an incidence of 0.96 per 1,000 PYs.

Although the cumulative risk of RRT was lower in the TAF group, the difference was not statistically significant (crude hazard ratio [HR], 0.74; 95% CI, 0.47–1.16). The cumulative incidences of RRT were consistently lower in the TAF group than in the TDF group, with rates of 0.19 vs. 0.89 per 1,000 PYs at 1 year, 1.88 vs. 2.75 per 1,000 PYs at 3 years, and 2.87 vs. 4.67 per 1,000 PYs at 5 years, respectively (Fig. 2A).

In the PS-matched cohort of 15,609 pairs, 25 patients with TAF and 40 patients with TDF underwent RRT, with an incidence of 0.91 and 0.58 per 1,000 PYs, respectively. Although the TAF group exhibited a lower risk, this difference was not statistically significant (crude HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.40–1.10). The cumulative incidences of RRT after PS matching were also consistently lower in the TAF group compared to the TDF group, with rates of 0.20 vs. 0.93 per 1,000 PYs at 1 year, 1.95 vs. 2.76 per 1,000 PYs at 3 years, and 2.94 vs. 4.24 per 1,000 PYs at 5 years, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Factors associated with an increased risk of RRT

The risk of RRT did not significantly differ between TAF and TDF groups in the entire and PS-matched cohorts (Table 3). After adjustment for sex, age, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, liver cirrhosis, and availability of health examination data (including the smoking and drinking statuses, exercise habits, and BMI), the impact of TAF on the occurrence of RRT was not statistically different from TDF (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.74; 95% CI, 0.47–1.16). Male sex was identified as a strong predictor of RRT compared to females. The risk of RRT increased progressively with advancing age, with a particularly steep rise observed in patients aged 70 years or older. Additionally, comorbidities such as hypertension, stroke, and liver cirrhosis were independently associated with a higher risk of RRT. Current smoking was also found to be a significant risk factor for RRT.

CKD

During the follow-up of 3.44 years (IQR, 2.02–4.70 years), 128 of 16,885 patients receiving TAF and 329 of 29,778 patients receiving TDF were diagnosed with CKD (Table 2). The incidence of CKD was 2.86 per 1,000 PYs in the TAF group and 2.94 per 1,000 PYs in the TDF group. Patients receiving TAF had an increased risk of CKD (crude HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.01–1.57; Fig. 3A). In the PS-matched cohort for the follow-up of 2.85 years, (IQR, 1.72–3.86 years), 128 patients with TAF and 111 patients with TDF were diagnosed with CKD, with an incidence of 2.99 vs. 2.52 per 1,000 PYs and an HR of 1.20 (95% CI, 0.93–1.56; Fig. 3B).

DISCUSSION

This nationwide, population-based cohort study, which included all patients with HBV infection who received TAF or TDF in South Korea, showed no statistically significant difference in the incidence of RRT between TAF- and TDF-treated groups after PS matching and multivariable analysis. TAF appeared to increase the risk of CKD in the multivariable analysis, although this association did not reach statistical significance after PS-matching.

In our study, the incidence of RRT was 0.56 per 1,000 PYs in the TAF group and 0.96 per 1,000 PYs in the TDF group. Given that the overall incidence of end-stage kidney disease in Korea is approximately 0.34 per 1,000 PYs, the risk of RRT for patients with CHB using TAF or TDF appears relatively high.19 In general, patients with CHB have a higher risk of developing CKD, as both the HBV infection itself and the use of NUCs for HBV treatment can contribute to renal disease.6,7,20 According to a meta-analysis, HBV infection is associated with an increased risk of CKD (odds ratio, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.12–1.29),21 as various renal diseases, including membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, and polyarteritis nodosa, are well-known extrahepatic manifestations of HBV infection.20 Furthermore, NUCs, including tenofovir, are associated with CKD.22,23,24 Since tenofovir is primarily cleared by the kidneys, concerns have been raised regarding its impact on a decline in tubular function and estimated glomerular filtration rate, particularly with TDF use.25,26 In a pivotal study comparing the efficacy and safety of TAF and TDF, patients receiving TDF exhibited more deteriorated renal function compared to the patients receiving TAF which was comparable with our previous study with 2,747 TAF- and TDF-treated patients.11,13 Our study did not show a statistically significant difference in RRT risk between TAF and TDF despite the apparent trend. First, we applied stringent inclusion criteria by excluding patients with pre-existing CKD or prior RRT, as well as those who developed these outcomes within the first 6 months after initiating antiviral therapy. While this strengthens the internal validity of our findings, it also reduced the number of outcome events, with only 25 and 40 RRT cases observed in the TAF and TDF groups, respectively. Given the small number of outcomes, our study may have been underpowered to detect a true difference between groups. While TAF was associated with a numerically lower HR (aHR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.47–1.16), the result did not achieve statistical significance, potentially reflecting a limited number of events and insufficient statistical power. Also, this may be explained by real-world practices, where patients receiving TDF are often switched to TAF when a decline in renal function is detected. In our study, patients who switched from TDF to TAF were censored at the time of regimen change. As a result, the risk of CKD or RRT in the TDF group may have been underestimated, since patients who might have developed CKD or RRT had they continued on TDF, were instead censored after switching to TAF. Therefore, a longer-term, large-scale cohort study with more comprehensive clinical data is needed to better assess the renal safety of TAF and TDF.

We also identified several clinical risk factors that were consistently associated with both RRT and CKD including male sex, older age, hypertension, and cirrhosis. Additionally, current smoking and diabetes were associated with increased risk of RRT and CKD, respectively. These are well-known risk factors for impaired renal function.27 Cirrhosis also plays a critical role in renal dysfunction.28 Increased intrahepatic vascular resistance contributes to portal hypertension and splanchnic vasodilation, which, in turn, reduce effective circulating volume, causing renal vasoconstriction and sodium and water retention. Systolic and diastolic dysfunction with decreased cardiac output also results in renal hypoperfusion which in turn decreases kidney function.29 Additionally, systemic inflammation, marked by elevated proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, further impairs renal function in patients with cirrhosis.30 Renal impairment is strongly associated with increased long-term morbidity and mortality. The one-year mortality among dialysis patients reaches approximately 20%.31 Adverse outcomes of renal impairment caused by TAF or TDF can often be prevented or delayed through early detection, treatment withdrawal, or renal dose adjustment.23 Therefore, close monitoring and proactive intervention are essential to prevent renal dysfunction requiring RRT.

This study has several limitations. First, selection and confounding biases may exist due to the observational nature of the study even though we attempted to mitigate these biases through PS matching and multivariable adjustment. Second, clinical data such as laboratory or imaging findings were unavailable due to inherent limitations of the claims database. Nonetheless, we captured RRT, a robust endpoint reflecting end-stage renal disease. Also, HBV related laboratory results such as HBsAg, HBeAg, or HBV DNA levels were not available. However, both TAF and TDF are highly potent antivirals with comparable viral suppression rates and since HBsAg seroclearance is less than 1%.11 We included only treatment-naïve patients who initiated therapy under identical criteria, we believe this limitation did not substantially affect the comparison. Third, although we captured CKD or RRT as clinically meaningful renal endpoints, their low incidence and the relatively short study duration (median 2.6 years) may have limited our ability to detect long-term renal toxicity associated with TAF or TDF. Censoring due to treatment discontinuation or switching may also have led to underestimation of renal events. In particular, early drug switching from TDF to TAF upon eGFR (estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate) decline receptor decline might underestimate CKD incidence in TDF group considering the results of previous studies.9,10,11,12,13,14 Well-designed, long-term real-world studies with comprehensive clinical data are needed to validate our findings.

In conclusion, our study suggests that the type of tenofovir used did not significantly affect the risk of RRT or CKD. However, the overall incidence of RRT in patients with tenofovir remained higher than that of the general population. Male patients, older patients, and those with hypertension or cirrhosis are at elevated risk of renal complications, requiring careful renal function monitoring to improve long-term outcomes.
