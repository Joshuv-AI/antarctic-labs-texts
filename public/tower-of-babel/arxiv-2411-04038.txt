Nondeterministic particle systems
Andreas Knauf, Manuel Quaschner

Abstract
We consider systems of n n particles that move with constant velocity between collisions. Their total momentum but not necessarily their kinetic energy is preserved at collisions. As there are no further constraints, these systems are nondeterministic. In particular we examine trajectories with infinitely many collisions.



1
Introduction

Motivation:

The system described in the
abstract

models the kinematic question about the consequences of
conservation of total momentum in multi-particle systems.
If one would additionally demand conservation of energy, then
it would exhibit an upper bound on the number of collisions, only depending on the
masses of the
n
particles
[
FKM
]
. Such an upper bound does not exist
here (if
n\geq 3
).

Conservation of both total momentum
and
energy are elementary
properties of particle systems.
So, before presenting our results, the question arises whether
our model does nevertheless describe traits of realistic
n
–particle systems,
which interact deterministically via pair potentials. This is indeed the case,
if some of the particles are tightly bound and if one only observes the momenta
of these clusters, and not their internal energy.

1.1 Example

(Celestial mechanics)

An example is provided by the
n
–body problem of celestial mechanics.
There, of course, total momentum
and
total energy are preserved
by the dynamics.

\bullet

n=2
particles with positive total energy move on Kepler hyperbolae that
are asymptotic to straight lines. Yet, after a near-collision,
their
directions
are hard to predict if one does not know
the initial conditions precisely.

\bullet

For
n=3
consider
clusters composed of a single body and a binary, respectively,
also experiencing a near-collision.
In the future, the clusters could separate again and move
asymptotically on near straight lines. But without precise knowledge of the initial
conditions one cannot even predict their
speed
. This is because
during near-collision, the internal energy of the nontrivial cluster
can change by an arbitrary amount, which
equals the change of kinetic energy, up to sign, see
McGehee

[
McG
]
.

\Diamond

So our nondeterministic billiard can describe some traits of
n
–body
problems. In fact, our main motivation was to model
non-collision singularities

in celestial mechanics, where particles move to spatial infinity in finite time.
These are known to exist for
n\geq 4
bodies, see
Xia

[
Xia
]
and

Xue

[
Xue
]
, and the
solutions of the non-deterministic model with infinitely many collisions
could serve as a kind of skeleton.

Contents:

We set the stage in Section
2

where we fix notation, mainly concerning the
combinatorics and kinematics of
n
particles.

Their nondeterministic dynamics is formally introduced in Definition
3.1
:
For the center of mass configuration space
M
of
n
particles in
d
dimensions,
a
nondeterministic trajectory

q\in C(I,M)
is defined on a maximal interval

I=(T^{-},T^{+})
.

On the set
{\cal T}\subseteq I
of
collision times
, the clustering changes.
At times
t\in I
the
collision set

\mathrm{CS}(t)
is the set partition of

\{1,\ldots,n\}
that corresponds to the clustering.
The momentum
p\in M^{*}
is constant on the components of
I\backslash{\cal T}
,
and the cluster barycenters for
\mathrm{CS}(t)

have constant momenta near points
t\in{\cal T}
.

As kinetic energy is not preserved, any projection
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

transforms a nondeterministic trajectory
into a (part of) a nondeterministic trajectory, too
(Lemma
3.4
and Remark
3.5
).

An important physical quantity that we study is the total moment of inertia

J={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\|q\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}
of the particle system (Theorem
3.7
),
It is convex in its time dependence, which has several
implications. In particular the moments where there is a change of clustering
form a nowhere dense countable set (Remark
3.8
).

T_{\mathrm{coll}}\in{\cal T}
is called a
total collision time

if
q(T_{\mathrm{coll}})=0
.
By convexity there can be at most two such events
(the second one is then an ejection).

Already for three particles,
{\cal T}
can have accumulation points at

escape times

T^{\pm}
and at total collision times
(Remark
3.9
).
For
n\geq 4
it may have a larger set of accumulation points, but these are
organised hierarchically by the partition poset of the particles.

Three nondeterministic particles exhibit a relatively simple behaviour.
If their moment of inertia grows initially, it does so exponentially in the number
of collisions (Example
3.12
). This is not anymore the case if
n\geq 4
, as
then subsystems of an expanding system can contract.
The times where all particles interacted with
one another – if only indirectly – are called
chain-closing
. One major result
(Theorem
3.15
) is that, under some hypotheses, size and
speed of the expansion is exponential
in the number of chain-closing events.

In Proposition
3.18

we show that, similarly to the deterministic case, locally uniform limits of
sequences of nondeterministic trajectories are trajectories, too.

Finally, we treat Lagrangian relations for multiple scattering events in
Section
4
.
That is, instead of considering individual nondeterministic trajectories, we embed them into families. Here, non-conservation of energy at collisions needs special attention.
It is natural that because of the non-deterministic nature of the dynamics, Lagrangian relations instead of Lagrangian submanifolds arise.
Still, these form a potential bridge for comparing our nondeterministic dynamics with the deterministic one of many-body motion with interactions.

(2.5)

2.3 Lemma

The vector space automorphisms

\left(\widehat{\Pi}^{E}_{\cal C},\widehat{\Pi}^{I}_{\cal C}\right):T^{*}M\longrightarrow T^{*}\Delta^{E}_{\cal C}\oplus\bigoplus_{C\in{\cal C}}T^{*}(\Delta^{I}_{C})\qquad\big({\cal C}\in{\cal P}(N)\big)

(2.18)

are symplectic w.r.t. the natural symplectic forms on these cotangent bundles.

Proof.

This follows from

T^{*}\big(\Delta^{E}_{\cal C}\oplus\bigoplus_{C\in{\cal C}}\Delta^{I}_{C}\big)=T^{*}\Delta^{E}_{\cal C}\oplus\bigoplus_{C\in{\cal C}}T^{*}(\Delta^{I}_{C})
.
\Box

Total angular momentum

L:T^{*}M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\wedge{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad\mbox{,}\quad L(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\wedge p_{i}

(2.19)

and
total kinetic energy

K:T^{*}M\to{\mathbb{R}}\quad\mbox{,}\quad K(p,q)\equiv K(p)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle p,p\right\rangle_{M^{*}}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle p,p\right\rangle_{{\cal M}^{-1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left\langle p_{i},p_{i}\right\rangle}{2m_{i}}

(using a sloppy notation) both split into sums of
barycentric

\displaystyle L^{E}_{\cal C}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle L\circ\widehat{\Pi}^{E}_{\cal C}\quad\mbox{,}\quad L^{E}_{\cal C}(p,q)=\sum_{C\in{\cal C}}q_{C}\wedge p_{C}\,,

\displaystyle K^{E}_{\cal C}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle K\circ\widehat{\Pi}^{E}_{\cal C}\quad\mbox{,}\quad K^{E}_{\cal C}(p,q)=\sum_{C\in{\cal C}}\frac{\left\langle p_{C},p_{C}\right\rangle}{2m_{C}}

and

\displaystyle L_{C}^{I}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle L\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{C}^{I}\quad\mbox{,}\quad L_{C}^{I}(p,q)=\sum_{i\in C}q^{I}_{i}\wedge p^{I}_{i}

\displaystyle K_{C}^{I}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle K\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{C}^{I}\quad\mbox{,}\quad K_{C}^{I}(p,q)=\sum_{i\in C}\frac{\left\langle p^{I}_{i},p^{I}_{i}\right\rangle}{2m_{i}}\,.

That is,

L=L^{E}_{\cal C}+\sum_{C\in{\cal C}}L_{C}^{I}\quad\mbox{and}\quad K=K^{E}_{\cal C}+K^{I}_{\cal C}\quad\mbox{with}\quad K^{I}_{\cal C}:=\sum_{C\in{\cal C}}K_{C}^{I}\,.

3
Nondeterministic systems

In
[
Bo
]

Bolotin
defined degenerate billiards as ones with scattering manifold
of codimension larger than one. Upon reflection, the change of momentum was assumed
to be perpendicular to its tangent space, the total energy being conserved.
In
[
FKM
]
, scattering by a union of linear subspaces in configuration space
was considered.
Here we further generalise these ideas by not demanding conservation of energy during
collisions.

3.1
Definition and elementary properties

The free flow on phase space
P=T^{*}M
with canonical symplectic form
\omega

is induced by the (purely kinetic) Hamiltonian

K:P\to{\mathbb{R}}\quad\mbox{,}\quad K(p,q):={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\|p\|_{{\cal M}^{-1}}^{2}

and takes the usual form

\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\times P\to P\quad\mbox{,}\quad\Phi(t;p,q)=(p,q+{\cal M}^{-1}p\,t)\,.

(3.1)

We now model the asymptotic motion of
n
particles on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

by a nondeterministic motion where between collisions
the particles move with constant velocity,
that is via
\Phi
.
These particles are allowed to have equal positions for non-trivial time intervals,
imitating close deterministic clusters. Spontaneous breakup of such clusters is allowed, too.
During collision, the total momentum (but not necessarily the total energy) of the colliding
group is assumed to be constant.

3.1 Definition

The
nondeterministic system
of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
particles with masses

m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}>0
and in
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
dimensions is the set of
(nondeterministic) trajectories
q\in C(I,M)
, having the following properties:

1)

I:=(T^{-},T^{+})
for
escape times

-\infty\leq T^{-}<0<T^{+}\leq\infty
.
If
T^{\pm}\neq\pm\infty
, the limits
\lim_{t\to T^{\pm}}q(t)
do not exist in
M
.

2)

With the
collision set

\mathrm{CS}:I\to{\cal P}(N)
defined by

q(t)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{CS}(t)}
, we set

{\cal T}:=\{t\in I\mid\mathrm{CS}\mbox{ is not locally constant at }t\}\,.

(3.2)

The momentum
p={\cal M}q^{\prime}
is constant in each interval of
I\backslash{\cal T}
.

3)

For all
\tau\in I
the map
(
see (
2.22
)
)

q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}:I\to\Delta_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}

is continuously differentiable in an open interval
U\ni\tau
, and the external momentum

U\ni t\mapsto p_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}(t):={\cal M}\,(q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E})^{\prime}(t)

is locally constant at
\tau
.

We use the shorthand
t\mapsto f_{\mathrm{CS}}(t)
for
maps
t\mapsto f_{\mathrm{CS}(t)}(t)
.

3.2 Remarks

(nondeterministic system)

1.

Assumption 1) means that the nondeterministic trajectory is not the restriction of a
nondeterministic trajectory on a strictly larger time interval.

2.

Assumption 2) implies that in each interval of
I\backslash{\cal T}
the particles
in a cluster of
\mathrm{CS}(t)

share the same position and move with the same constant velocity.

3.

By Assumption 3) the cluster centers move freely in the time intervals
U
.

4.

Note that the nondeterministic systems are invariant under time translations
and reversible: for a trajectory
q\in C(I,M)
and
t\in I=(T^{-},T^{+})
,

q(\bullet-t):(T^{-}-t,T^{+}-t)\to M\quad\mbox{and}\quad q(-\bullet):(-T^{+},-T^{-})\to M

are trajectories, too. Thus it suffices to prove properties for time zero and in the future
direction. The orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(d)
acts diagonally on

M\subseteq({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
and
maps nondeterministic trajectories to nondeterministic trajectories.

5.

For every trajectory
q\in C(I,M)
we have the one-parameter family of
trajectories:

q_{\lambda}\in C(I/\lambda,M)\quad\mbox{,}\quad q_{\lambda}(t)=q(\lambda t)\qquad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}).

Additionally, rescaling by
q\mapsto\mu q
is possible.

\Diamond

3.3 Lemma

\mathrm{CS}
is a Borel measurable map and
{\cal T}
a closed Borel subset of
I
.

Proof:

Measurability of
\mathrm{CS}
follows, as the
\Xi_{\cal C}\subseteq M

are measurable and
q
is continuous.
An accumulation point
\tau\in I\backslash{\cal T}
of
{\cal T}
cannot exist,
since then
\mathrm{CS}
would not be locally constant at
\tau
by definition (
3.2
).

\Box

Unlike ordinary billiards with energy conservation, nondeterministic systems are
projectable in the following sense:

3.4 Lemma

(projections of nondeterministic trajectories)

1.

Every orthogonal projection
\Pi_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
gives rise to an
{\cal M}
–orthogonal
projection

\Pi_{n}:=(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{1}):{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}
with
\Pi_{n}(M)\subseteq M
.

2.

\Pi_{n}
maps trajectories to parts of trajectories.

3.5 Remark

(extension of nondeterministic trajectories)

Note that we can always extend a trajectory on
(a,b)
,
for which the positions and the momenta have a limit at the boundary points,
by just letting the momenta be constant outside the interval and the positions
evolve linearly.

\Diamond

Proof of Lemma
3.4
:

1.

{\cal M}
–orthogonality of
\Pi_{n}

follows from the definition (
2.1
) of the inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\cal M}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}
.
\Pi_{n}
–invariance of
M
is immediate
from (
2.2
): For
q\in M
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}\Pi_{1}(q_{k})=\Pi_{1}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}q_{k})=0
.

2.

First note that (unlike
\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\pm}}q(t)
) the limits

\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\pm}}\Pi_{n}(q(t))
may exist, a simple example being that of the trivial
projection
\Pi_{1}\equiv 0
.
But we can prove that on the interval
I=(T^{-},T^{+})
the properties of
Definition
3.1
are satisfied.
Therefor we define
\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}:I\to{\cal P}(N)
by

\Pi_{n}(q(t))\in\Xi_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)}
. Then we get a new collision set

\widetilde{{\cal T}}:=\{t\in I\mid\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}\mbox{ is not locally constant at }t\}\,.

We need to show that
\tilde{p}={\cal M}\Pi_{n}(q^{\prime})
is constant in each interval of
I\setminus\widetilde{{\cal T}}
and that for all
\tau\in I
the map

q_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(\tau)}^{E}:I\to\Delta_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(\tau)}^{E}

is continuously differentiable in an open interval containing
\tau
.

The second statement is the simpler one. Note that for any time
t\in I
we must have

\mathrm{CS}(t)\preccurlyeq\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)
.
Denote with
\Pi_{\mathrm{CS}(t),\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)}:\Delta_{\mathrm{CS}(t)}^{E}\rightarrow\Delta_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)}^{E}

the well-defined projection between these subspaces.
Then for any
\tau\in I
we have that
q_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(\tau)}^{E}=\Pi_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau),\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(\tau)}\circ q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}
is a
composition of a linear projection and a differentiable map (in a neighborhood of
\tau
) and
hence differentiable (in a neighborhood of
\tau
).

For
\tilde{p}={\cal M}\Pi_{n}(q^{\prime})
being constant on any interval in
I\setminus\widetilde{{\cal T}}
it
is sufficient that for any
t\in I\setminus\widetilde{{\cal T}}
there is a neighborhood of
t
on
which
\tilde{p}
is constant.

Note that we neither can guarantee
\widetilde{{\cal T}}\subseteq{\cal T}
nor

{\cal T}\subseteq\widetilde{{\cal T}}
. But if
q
is differentiable and
p
is constant in a
neighborhood of some time
t\in I
, the same holds true for
\Pi_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{p}
.
Thus we only have to consider times
t\in\left(I\setminus\widetilde{{\cal T}}\right)\cap{\cal T}
, i.e. where
\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}
is locally constant, but
\mathrm{CS}
is not. Now as

\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}
is constant on some neighborhood
U_{0}\ni t
we know that

\Pi_{n}(q)=\Pi_{n}(q_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)}^{E})=(\Pi_{n}(q))_{\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)}^{E}

(note that it does not matter whether or not we project first and then take the cluster
coordinates or vice versa). Therefore,

\Pi_{n}(q)
is (on some possibly smaller neighborhood which we still denote with
U_{0}
)
continuously differentiable by the result from the first part of the proof.
Thus we only need to show that the derivative is constant.

Therefor, we need that
{\cal T}
is nowhere dense, compare Theorem
3.7
below.
We know that (again possibly in a smaller neighborhood)
p
is locally constant on every
interval of
U_{0}\setminus{\cal T}
and
\mathrm{CS}(s)
is always at most finer for
s\in U_{0}
than
\mathrm{CS}(t)
which is finer than
\widetilde{\mathrm{CS}}(t)
. Hence, we
can project
p(s)
to
\tilde{p}(s)
on intervals where
p(s)
is defined in the same way
as above to obtain
\tilde{p}(s)
. So
\tilde{p}
is constant on these intervals, too, and as

{\cal T}
is nowhere dense and
\tilde{p}
is continuous, it has to be constant on the whole
interval
U_{0}
, proving the statement.

\Box

3.2
Moment of inertia

Next we consider the
scalar moment of inertia

along a trajectory

J\in C\big(I,[0,\infty)\big)\quad\mbox{,}\quad J(t)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\big\langle q(t),{\cal M}q(t)\big\rangle\,,

and similarly
J_{\cal C}^{E},J_{\cal C}^{I}\in C\big(I,[0,\infty)\big)
for
{\cal C}\in{\cal P}(N)

(see (
2.13
)),

J_{\cal C}^{I}(t)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\big\langle q^{I}_{\cal C}(t),{\cal M}q^{I}_{\cal C}(t)\big\rangle\quad\mbox{,}\quad J_{\cal C}^{E}(t)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\big\langle q^{E}_{\cal C}(t),{\cal M}q^{E}_{\cal C}(t)\big\rangle\,.

(3.3)

We thus have
J=J_{{\cal C}_{\min}}^{E}=J_{{\cal C}_{{{\rm max}}}}^{I}
and

J=J_{{\cal C}}^{E}+J_{{\cal C}}^{I}
for all
{\cal C}\in{\cal P}(N)
.

In order to show convexity of
J
, we use the following simple fact.

3.6 Lemma

A function
f\in C(U,{\mathbb{R}})
on an open interval
U
is convex if and only
if for every
x\in U
there exists an open interval
U_{x}\subseteq U
containing
x
with

f|_{U_{x}}
convex.

Proof:

It is obvious that convexity on
U
implies local convexity on the
U_{x}
.

In order to conversely show convexity of
f
, assuming local convexity, we consider a
compact interval
[y_{0},y_{1}]\subseteq U
. As
x\in U_{x}
,
it is covered by the
U_{x}
for
x\in[y_{0},y_{1}]
.
By compactness there exists a finite subcover of
[y_{0},y_{1}]
.
By induction it suffices to consider
two of these open intervals, say
U_{a}
and
U_{b}
with non-empty overlap and prove
that the restriction of
f
to
U_{a}\cup U_{b}
is convex, too. It also suffices to consider

x_{0}\in U_{a}\backslash U_{b}
, and
x_{1}\in U_{b}\backslash U_{a}
and to show
(assuming
x_{0}<x_{1}
without loss of generality) that

f(x_{t})\leq(1-t)f(x_{0})+tf(x_{1})\mbox{ with }x_{t}:=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}\mbox{ and }t\in(0,1)\,.

(3.4)

\bullet

If
x_{t}\in U_{a}
, then there are
s_{2}>s_{1}>t
with

x_{s_{2}}>x_{s_{1}}\in U_{a}\cap U_{b}
.
By assumption of convexity of
f|_{U_{a}}
, with the notation

{\rm sl}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\frac{f(x_{t_{2}})-f(x_{t_{1}})}{x_{t_{2}}-x_{t_{1}}}
, the secants have slopes

{\rm sl}(0,t)\leq{\rm sl}(t,s_{1})\leq{\rm sl}(s_{1},s_{2})\,.

Similarly, concerning
U_{b}
, we know that

{\rm sl}(s_{1},s_{2})\leq{\rm sl}(s_{1},1)\leq{\rm sl}(s_{2},1).

Combining these, we infer from
{\rm sl}(t,s_{1})\leq{\rm sl}(s_{1},1)
that

{\rm sl}(t,s_{1})\leq{\rm sl}(t,1)

and obtain the convexity condition for
x_{t}
:

\textstyle{\rm sl}(0,t)\leq{\rm sl}(t,s_{1})\leq{\rm sl}(t,1)\qquad(0<t<1).

This is equivalent to (
3.4
).

\bullet

The case
x_{t}\in U_{b}
is similar.

\Box

To show that
{\cal T}
is countable, we decompose
{\cal T}
into the disjoint union

\textstyle{\cal T}=\bigcup_{{\cal C}\in{\cal P}(N)}{\cal T}_{\cal C}\ \mbox{, with }\ {\cal T}_{\cal C}:=\{t\in{\cal T}\mid\mathrm{CS}(t)\in{\cal C}\}\,.

(3.5)

3.7 Theorem

(moment of inertia)

1.

For every
\tau\in I
there is a maximal open interval
U_{\tau}\subseteq I
containing
\tau

with

\mathrm{CS}(t)\preccurlyeq\mathrm{CS}(\tau)\qquad(t\in U_{\tau}).

2.

J
is convex. More precisely, for every
\tau\in I
and

\mathrm{CS}(\tau)=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}
the functions

J_{C_{\ell}}^{E}
and
J_{C_{\ell}}^{I}

(\ell=1,\ldots,k)
are convex on
U_{\tau}
.

3.

Accumulation points of
{\cal T}_{\cal C}
in
I
are contained in the
{\cal T}_{\cal D}
with

{\cal C}\precneqq{\cal D}
.

So
{\cal T}
is countable and nowhere dense.

4.

The velocity
q^{\prime}:I\setminus{\cal T}\to M
is locally bounded in
I
.

So for compact intervals
C\subseteq I
the length of the curve
q|_{C}
is finite.

5.

J
is continuously differentiable, with

J^{\prime}(t)=\big\langle q(t),p_{\mathrm{CS}}^{E}(t)\big\rangle\qquad\big(t\in I\big).

(3.6)

6.

The distributional derivative of
J^{\prime}=\langle q_{\mathrm{CS}}^{E},p_{\mathrm{CS}}^{E}\rangle

is a Radon measure with density

\langle p_{\mathrm{CS}}^{E},p_{\mathrm{CS}}^{E}\rangle_{{\cal M}^{-1}}
.

Proof:

1.

The existence of an open interval
\widetilde{U}_{\tau}\ni\tau
with

\mathrm{CS}(t)\preccurlyeq\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
for all
t\in\widetilde{U}_{\tau}

follows as the
\Xi_{\cal C}\subseteq\Delta_{\cal C}^{E}

are relatively open and
q
is continuous.
Then the union
U_{\tau}
of these
\widetilde{U}_{\tau}
is open.

2.

To show that
J
is convex, by Lemma
3.6
it suffices to prove that

J
is convex on
U_{\tau}
, for all
\tau\in I
. Using for

{\cal C}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}:=\mathrm{CS}(\tau)

the decomposition
J=J_{{\cal C}}^{E}+J_{{\cal C}}^{I}
, see (
3.3
),
we note that

J_{{\cal C}}^{E}|_{U_{\tau}}
is convex, since it is the restriction of a quadratic polynomial with
leading coefficient
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\|\dot{q}_{\cal C}^{E}(\tau)\|_{\cal M}^{2}\geq 0
.

We show that
J_{{\cal C}}^{I}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}J_{C_{\ell}}^{I}
is convex, by considering the
closed subsets
V_{\ell}:=\{t\in U_{\tau}\mid J_{C_{\ell}}^{I}(t)=0\}
. If always

V_{\ell}=U_{\tau}
, we are done. Otherwise we choose an
\ell
violating the condition
and restrict to the corresponding subsystem
N^{\prime}:=C_{\ell}
of
|N^{\prime}|\leq n
particles.
Since
U_{\tau}\backslash V_{\ell}
is the disjoint union of non-empty open intervals,
if the restriction of
J_{C_{\ell}}^{I}
to
U_{\tau}
were not convex, there would exist such an
interval
I^{\prime}\subseteq U_{\tau}\backslash V_{\ell}
on which
J_{N^{\prime}}^{I}
is not convex.
But for all
\tau^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{CS}_{N^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})
is strictly finer than
N^{\prime}
.

Iterating this argument at most
n
times, we end up with cluster of one particle,
for which the internal moment of inertia vanishes. This proves that

J_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{I}
is convex on
U_{\tau}
.

So
J
, too, is convex on
U_{\tau}
.
Lemma
3.6
then shows that
J
is convex on its domain of definition
I
.

3.

To show that
{\cal T}
is countable, we use the decomposition (
3.5
)
of
{\cal T}
into the disjoint union of
{\cal T}_{\cal C}
.
As
q(t)\in\Delta_{\cal C}^{E}
for

and
\Delta_{\cal C}^{E}\subseteq M

is closed, we have
q(\tau)\in\Delta_{\cal C}^{E}
for an accumulation point
\tau
of
{\cal T}_{\cal C}
.

So
{\cal C}\preccurlyeq{\cal D}
. But
{\cal C}\neq{\cal D}
, since if

. By Part 2)

For

\bullet

\bullet

The external part

We have

for any compact interval

containing

So the (ordinary) derivative of

exists and has the form (

6.

The Radon measure is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, since

\bullet

its pure point part vanishes, as

is continuous by Part 2),

\bullet

its singular continuous part vanishes, as by Part 4)
{\cal T}
is countable.

As the second term in the distributional derivative

\Box

3.8 Remark

(convexity of

and properties of
{\cal T}
)

J

is of Lebesgue measure zero.

However, convexity of

is countable nor that

For a convex function

define

as the set of points where

does not
exist.

\bullet

A counterexample to countability of
{\cal T}
is the integral

of the continuous, monotone increasing Cantor function.

\bullet

The second integral of the distribution

\sum_{p/q\in{\mathbb{Q}}}q^{-a}\delta_{p/q}
,
with
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)=1
and

is an example of an

is dense.

\Diamond

When

we call

a

.

So

and by convexity of

(the larger one corresponding to ejection).

3.9 Remark

(accumulation points of
{\cal T}
)

By Part 3 of Theorem
3.7
,
{\cal T}
is countable and nowhere dense.
However, this does not mean that
{\cal T}\subseteq I
could only accumulate at
the escape times
T^{\pm}
.

A simple counterexample is the case of
n=3
particles with equal masses
in
d=1
dimension.
Here the center of mass configuration space
M
is two-dimensional.
If we assume that the inner particle is keeping to be the same,
the motion corresponds to one in the cone
C=\{q\in M\mid q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq q_{3}\}

of opening angle
\pi/3
, with
the total collision point
0
as its tip. We assume that
J^{\prime}(0)<0
.
Then the trajectory
q:I\to C
can be reflected successively by one and
then the other ray forming
\partial C
. As long as these reflections at times
t_{k}
are ingoing
(
J^{\prime}(t_{k})<0
), then one is free to choose the next collision with the other ray so that

J^{\prime}(t_{k+1})<0
, too. By iterating one obtains a strictly increasing sequence of times

(t_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
The simplest choice is self-similarity of the trajectory.
Then the outgoing angles
\alpha_{k}
at these rays are all equal to some
\alpha
, which
can be chosen in the interval
(\pi/3,\pi/2)
.

This leads to a decrease in distance from total collision and in speed which both are
exponential in
k
:
By elementary geometry and the constancy of tangential velocity during collision
we conclude that

\|q(t_{k+1})\|_{\cal M}=c\,\|q(t_{k})\|_{\cal M}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|q^{\prime}(t_{k+1}^{+})\|_{\cal M}=s\,\|q^{\prime}(t_{k}^{+})\|_{\cal M}\qquad(k\in{\mathbb{N}})

for
c:=\cos(\alpha)/\cos(\alpha-\pi/3)\in(0,{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})
and

s:=\sin(\alpha-\pi/3)/\sin(\alpha)\in(0,{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})
.

If
\alpha=5\pi/12
, then
c=s
and thus
t_{k+1}-t_{k}
is constant. More generally,

\bullet

for
\alpha\in(\pi/3,5\pi/12]
the trajectory reaches the
total collision point
0
only in the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}=+\infty
.

\bullet

Instead, for
\alpha\in(5\pi/12,\pi/2)
the time
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}
of total collision is finite.

Thus we cannot assume that
{\cal T}
has a monotone enumeration

\{t_{n}\mid t_{n}<t_{n+1}\}
.
\Diamond

Next we examine the asymptotics of the motion near the escape time
T^{+}

(the one for escape time

following by reversibility).
As the lattice

is complete,
the set partitions

The atoms of

3.10 Lemma

{\cal C}_{\inf}={\cal C}_{\sup}
if and only if escape time

Proof:

This is obvious from the definition

As the partition lattice

We call a nondeterministic trajectory

expanding

However, this is not the only possible formalisation of that word.
The function

3.11 Lemma

(expansion and radius-expansion)

1.

J/m_{N}\leq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{rad}^{2}\leq J/m_{\min}
.

2.

If a nondeterministic trajectory

.

3.

Conversely, if a nondeterministic trajectory

\sqrt{m_{N}/m_{\min}}\,\frac{\mathrm{rad}(0)}{\mathrm{rad}^{\prime}(0)}

1.

This follows from the norm inequalities

2.

With the notation from Subsection

\textstyle J(t)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}R(t)^{2}\geq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(R(0)+P_{R}(0)t)^{2}=J(0)\big(1+\frac{J^{\prime}(0)}{2J(0)}t\big)^{2}.

So, using Part 1., for

{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{rad}^{2}(t)\geq J(t)/m_{N}\geq J(0)/m_{\min}\geq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{rad}^{2}(0)\,.

\Box

Note that both

\displaystyle\geq P_{R}(T_{\ell-2})\left(1+D^{2}\frac{\Delta_{l-1}}{\Delta_{l}}\right)+\frac{1}{C}D^{2}R(T_{\ell-2})\geq

\displaystyle\geq P_{R}(0)\left(1+D^{2}\right)^{U_{\ell}}+\frac{D^{2}}{C}R(0)\left(1+D^{2}\right)^{G_{\ell-2}}.

But as

3.

Considering the growth of the radius

that there is a

and an interval

.
We consider the tangent space

of the sphere of radius

.

For collisions with a hyperplane

The relative radial velocity

is not necessarily monotone increasing. Still:

3.17 Lemma

If the escape time

is finite, then
\lim_{t\nearrow T^{+}}\frac{P_{R}(t)}{R(t)}=+\infty
.

Proof:

\bullet

Since
\lim_{t\nearrow T^{+}}R(t)=\infty
, for

the limes superior of

is infinite.

\bullet

This logarithmic derivative of

fulfils the differential equation

\textstyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{P_{R}}{R}=\|W\|_{{\cal M}^{-1}}^{2}-\big(\!\frac{P_{R}}{R}\!\big)^{2}\geq-\big(\!\frac{P_{R}}{R}\!\big)^{2}.

For all

\textstyle x(t)=\frac{x(T)}{1+x(T)(t-T)}\geq\frac{x(T)}{1+x(T)(T^{+}-T)}\geq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\min\big(x(T),\frac{1}{T^{+}-T}\big)>0

This implies
\frac{P_{R}}{R}(t)\geq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\min\big(\frac{P_{R}}{R}(T),\frac{1}{T^{+}-T}\big)

For all

3.4
Closedness of nondeterministic particle systems

Definition
3.1
of nondeterministic systems is concise,
but the intuitive interpretation of particles
having collisions at which the momenta are changed (conserving total
momentum but not necessarily kinetic energy) might be hidden.

Additionally, this definition allows for accumulation points of the collision times in the
interior of the domain of definition
[0,T^{+})
.
These might occur as some limiting behavior of trajectories with finitely many collisions
in a given time interval when letting the number of collisions tend to infinity, e.g. in the model considered in
[
Qu
, Def. 3.2]
(but without allowing mass exchange).

As in both definitions the information about the system is given by a continuous curve

q
, one natural notion of convergence is the convergence of the curves on any (or a
given) compact subinterval of the domain of definition.

In this section we want to consider
two questions
of this kind:

1.

Is the set of trajectories given by Definition
3.1
closed with respect to
convergence on compact intervals, i.e. is the limit curve again part of a
nondeterministic trajectory?
This would show that nondeterministic systems share an important property of
deterministic dynamical systems.

2.

Can one uniformly approximate a nondeterministic trajectory
q

with accumulation points of
{\cal T}
in the domain of definition
(T^{-},T^{+})

by nondeterministic trajectories with only finitely many collisions in any
compact interval?

Then one could use this approximation to prove properties of the trajectory
q
,
using results obtained by combinatorial methods in
[
Qu
]
.

We will answer positively both questions, beginning with Question 1:

3.18 Proposition

(Convergence of trajectories)

Let
(q^{(k)})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of nondeterministic trajectories that converges
uniformly on a compact interval
K
to some map
q:K\to M
.
Then
q
is part of a nondeterministic trajectory.

Proof:

Denote with
{\cal T}^{(k)}
respectively
{\cal T}
the corresponding sets of
q^{(k)}
and
q

that were defined in Definition
3.1
and analogously for
\mathrm{CS}^{(k)}
and
\mathrm{CS}
.

We prove now the properties 2) and 3) of Def.
3.1
for
q

to be part of a nondeterministic trajectory.
We begin with considering a
\tau\in K
and proving the differentiability of the map

q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}
in a small neighborhood of
\tau
.
We treat the case
\tau\in\mathrm{int}(K)
,
\tau\in\partial K
being similar.
By continuity of
q
and the definition of
\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
there is a
neighborhood
U=(a,b)\subseteq K
of
\tau
and an
\epsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in N,i\not\sim_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}j,\\
t\in U\end{subarray}}\left\|q_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t)\right\|>\epsilon\,,

where
\sim_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}
denotes the equivalence relation with equivalence classes

\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
.
Due to the uniform convergence there exists an index
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
k\geq N_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in N,i\not\sim_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}j,\\
t\in U\end{subarray}}\left\|q^{(k)}_{i}(t)-q^{(k)}_{j}(t)\right\|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,.

This implies that for
t\in U
we always have
\mathrm{CS}(t)\preccurlyeq\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
and
even for indices
k
sufficiently large we have
\mathrm{CS}^{(k)}(t)\preccurlyeq\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
.
Note that this implies that
p_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}
is constant on
U
, as we can project the
locally constant functions
p_{\mathrm{CS}^{(k)}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}
on this coarser partition (compare the
proof of Lemma
3.4
).
But then we get a simple expression for
q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}
in the interval
U=(a,b)
,
as the motion of the external component is affine:

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}(t)=q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}(a)+\frac{t-a}{b-a}\left(q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}(b)-q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E,(k)}(a)\right).

Now we can use the uniform convergence and obtain

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}(t)=q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}(a)+\frac{t-a}{b-a}\left(q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}(b)-q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}(a)\right)\qquad(t\in U).

This implies that
q_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}
is affine in
U
and hence especially differentiable.
So Part 3) of Definition
3.1
is satisfied.

What remains is to show Part 2), constancy of
p
on any interval of
K\setminus{\cal T}
.
On such an interval
U\ni\tau
we know that
p_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{I}
is 0 and hence it is
sufficient if
p_{\mathrm{CS}(\tau)}^{E}
is constant. But this can be proven in the same way as above.

\Box

Now we consider the
second question
on Page
3.4
,
namely whether one can approximate any trajectory
q:I\to M

with accumulation points of
{\cal T}

by trajectories without accumulation points in
I
.
This will be answered in Prop.
3.26
.

We will focus on the one-dimensional case and consider first an isolated
accumulation point of
{\cal T}
.

3.19 Remark

(Simplifications for isolated accumulation points)

If in a given compact time interval
there is only one isolated accumulation point of
{\cal T}
for the
nondeterministic trajectory, we can use the following simplifications.

1.

By regarding the corresponding sub-clusters colliding at
T_{\mathrm{coll}}
,
we assume without loss of generality that this is a total collision

T_{\mathrm{coll}}
(which is always isolated).

2.

We can treat both sides of the time interval around
T_{\mathrm{coll}}
separately,
starting with
[T_{\mathrm{coll}}-\epsilon,T_{\mathrm{coll}}]
. (The other case can be
treated analogously by time reversibility of nondeterministic trajectories).

In this interval, we can enumerate all collision times in

[T_{\mathrm{coll}}-\epsilon,T_{\mathrm{coll}})\cap\mathcal{T}

in a monotone way (see Item 3. below):

t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots
, with

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}t_{k}=T_{\mathrm{coll}}
.

3.

Additionally, we can split up simultaneous collisions or collisions of more than
two bodies in a sequence of binary collisions happening at the same physical time.
This was treated in
[
Qu
, Section 3.1.2 and Section 3.1.5]
.
Note that this is only possible as we do not demand conservation of energy.

\Diamond

By Theorem
3.7
.3 we know that an accumulation point
T_{\mathrm{coll}}

can be the limit of
other accumulation points of collision times
\tau
only if the latter have a partition

\mathrm{CS}(\tau)
of the particles that is strictly
finer than
\mathrm{CS}(T_{\mathrm{coll}})
.

Hence the above analysis can be applied to the finest partitions first and
then will yield the statement for arbitrary accumulation points.
In such a case we must have a total collision of all particles
of the same cluster at some time, as otherwise the diameter
or the radial component of the momentum of the corresponding system
would explode in backwards time direction.

We will now approximate the trajectory by ones that have only finitely many collisions
within compact intervals.
As we can start this approximation arbitrarily close to the time of total collision,
we will get a converging sequence of trajectories with only finitely many collisions.

To obtain such an approximation, we use a particular class of trajectories that
we call
sticky
.

3.20 Definition

(sticky trajectories)

A non-deterministic trajectory
\,q:I\to M
of
\,n
particles in
d
dimensions
is
sticky
, if its set partition gets coarser in time:

\mathrm{CS}(t_{1})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{CS}(t_{2})
for
t_{1}<t_{2}\in I
.

We also apply the notion of stickyness to restrictions of
q
to subintervals of
I
.
But since in general one cannot uniquely extend a sticky trajectory backwards in
time, for our purposes it will be sufficient to consider the time interval
[0,\infty)
.
Then sticky trajectories are parameterised by their initial conditions in the ’sticky’
phase space

P_{S}:=\{(q,v)\in M\times M\mid v_{\cal C}^{I}=0\mbox{ if }q\in\Xi_{\cal C}\}\,.

In this sense they form a deterministic backbone of the
nondeterministic system:

3.21 Lemma

(sticky trajectories)

For all initial conditions
(q_{0},v_{0})\in P_{S}

there exists a unique sticky trajectory
q:[0,\infty)\to M
with

\big(q(0),q^{\prime}(0)\big)=(q_{0},v_{0})
.

Proof:

Consider the maximal interval
[0,t_{1})
of the ray
t\mapsto q(t):=q_{0}+v_{0}t

that is contained in
\Xi_{\mathrm{CS}(0)}
.
As the
\mathrm{CS}(0)
–internal velocity
(v_{0})_{\mathrm{CS}(0)}^{I}
equals
0
,
either
t_{1}=\infty
or
q(t_{1})\in\Xi_{\mathrm{CS}(t_{1})}
with

\mathrm{CS}(0)\prec\mathrm{CS}(t_{1})
. In the latter case we set
v(t_{1}^{+}):=\Pi^{E}_{\mathrm{CS}(t_{1})}(v(0))
,
see (
2.8
), and proceed with
the new initial data
(q(t_{1}),v(t_{1}^{+}))\in P_{S}
.
As the rank of the partition decreases at each collision time

t_{0}:=0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\,,

one has
k\leq n-1
,
and the inductive definition yields a sticky forward trajectory
q:[0,\infty)\to M
.

\Box

3.22 Remark

(sticky total collisions on a line)

In the case
d=1
of
n
ordered (
q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\ldots\leq q_{n}
)
particles on a line, initial conditions

(q,v)\in P_{S}
give rise to a sticky trajectory ending in a total collision,
if
v_{i}>v_{i+1}
for
1\leq i<n
.

This condition, however is not necessary.

E.g.
for
n=3
it could be that
v_{2}\leq v_{3}

but we still get a total collision if
v_{\{1,2\}}>v_{3}
.

\Diamond

In all dimensions

, the cluster barycenter moment
J_{\cal C}
, defined in
(

from the initial data of a sticky trajectory:

3.23 Lemma

(total collision time for sticky trajectories)

For a sticky trajectory

with initial conditions
(q_{0},v_{0})\in P_{S}
and

{\cal T}=\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{k}\}
,
that has a total collision at time
T_{\mathrm{coll}}:=t_{k}\in(0,\infty)
,

T_{\mathrm{coll}}\ =\ S_{\mathrm{coll}}:=-\tfrac{2J_{\cal C}(0)}{J_{\cal C}^{\prime}(0)}\qquad\mbox{, with }{\cal C}:=\mathrm{CS}(t_{k-1})\,.

(3.18)

Proof:

Before
T_{\mathrm{coll}}
particles of different clusters of
{\cal C}
do not
collide. So the mean velocity
v_{\cal C}=\Pi^{E}_{\cal C}(v_{0})

is constant on
[0,T_{\mathrm{coll}})
,
the centers of mass of the clusters moving on the straight line

q_{\cal C}(t)=(t-T_{\mathrm{coll}})v_{\cal C}
.
The condition
T_{\mathrm{coll}}>0
implies that
v_{\cal C}\neq 0
. So
the quotient

is well-defined and equals

\tfrac{\|v_{\cal C}(0)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}T_{\mathrm{coll}}^{2}}{\|v_{\cal C}(0)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}T_{\mathrm{coll}}}=T_{\mathrm{coll}}
.
\Box

3.24 Remark

(sticky total collisions in dimension

)

We enumerate the particles and clusters so that
q_{i}\leq q_{i+1}
,
and
{\cal C}=\mathrm{CS}(t_{k-1})=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{R}\}
with

if

,
q_{j}\in C_{b}
and

. The ordering

1.

Then for any set partition
{\cal D}
with
{\cal C}\preccurlyeq{\cal D}\prec{\cal C}_{{{\rm max}}}=\{N\}

whose atoms are unions of neighbouring atoms of
{\cal C}

2.

In particular, there exist such set partitions

For

(3.19)

3.

\{{\cal D}_{1},\ldots,{\cal D}_{n-1}\}\subseteq{\cal P}(N)

For

3.25 Lemma

(Lower bound for the time to a total collision)

1.

At all collision times

S_{\mathrm{coll}}(t_{k}^{+})\geq S_{\mathrm{coll}}(t_{k}^{-})

1.

and

We assume without loss of generality that

.

R(t):=\min\big\{r\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\mid S_{r}(t)=S_{\mathrm{coll}}(t)\big\}\mbox{ and }R_{k}:=R(t_{k}^{-})

\bullet

If

\bullet

\bullet

So we are left with

\displaystyle S_{r+1}-S_{r}

As we assumed maximality of

For

But if the particles

S_{R}

and/or minimality of

But without a cluster-external collision at time

for

3.26 Proposition

(Approximations with finitely many collisions)

For every nondeterministic (forward) trajectory
q:[0,T)\rightarrow M
and every

\epsilon>0
there exists a nondeterministic trajectory

q^{(\epsilon)}:[0,T)\rightarrow M
such that:

\bullet

For any compact interval
K\subseteq[0,T)
we have

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{t\in K}\left\|q^{(\epsilon)}(t)-q(t)\right\|=0\,.

\bullet

Each
q^{(\epsilon)}
has only finitely many collision in any compact interval

K\subseteq[0,T)
.

Proof:

Let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary. We construct the approximating trajectory as follows:
We start with the collision times
t
of finest partitions
{\cal C}
that are
accumulation points of collision times in the interval
[0,T-\epsilon]

(after time
T-\epsilon
we continue the trajectory in an arbitrary way, e.g. the particles
do not exchange any momenta anymore).
As these collision times are isolated by Theorem
3.7
, there is an interval
around each
t
, say
[t-\delta_{t},t+\delta_{t}]
, in which only particles from the same
clusters of
{\cal C}
collide.
If there are infinitely many collisions within one cluster, these particles must contract
to their common center of mass.

Now we can apply Lemma
3.25
and
Lemma
3.23
just for the motion of the particles of
this subsystem having a total collision at their relative center of mass.
Without loss of generality (otherwise take a smaller
\delta_{t}
) we assume that

J(t\pm\delta_{t})\leq\operatorname{Const}\cdot\epsilon^{2}
,
where the constant compensates the norm equivalence of the norms
\left\|q\right\|
and
the norm with respect to the mass metric
\left\|q\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=2J
,
and some other factors.
We approximate the trajectory by the sticky trajectory from
t-\delta_{t}
up to time
t

and from time
t+\delta_{t}
to time
t
in backward-time direction.
The above lemmas guarantee that the sticky trajectory has a time of total collision which is at
most
t
, hence this approximation is well-defined and yields a nondeterministic trajectory.
This depends on
\epsilon
and can guarantee that the distance between our first
approximation
q^{(\epsilon),1}
and
q
is smaller than
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
on each of these
intervals (the distance between the trajectories can be estimated in terms of the
difference of the internal coordinates which is small as the total internal moment of inertia
is small on this interval due to convexity).

Next we can take all collision times from the next coarser partitions for
q^{(\epsilon),1}
.
These are now isolated accumulation points of collisions (due to the first step) and hence
we can repeat the above procedure and approximate this trajectory by
q^{(\epsilon),2}

that has a distance of at most
\frac{\epsilon}{4}
to
q^{(\epsilon),1}
at any time.
We repeat this procedure a finite number of times (for each coarser and coarser partition)
until we finally reach the desired
q^{(\epsilon)}
.

The convergence of the trajectories is clear as by construction we even have

\sup_{t\in[0,T-\epsilon]}\left\|q^{(\epsilon)}(t)-q(t)\right\|\leq\epsilon\,.

That
q^{(\epsilon)}
only has finitely many collisions is also true by construction.

\Box

3.27 Corollary

(Exponential lower bounds for system quantities)

For an expanding nondeterministic system with
J^{\prime}(0)>0
the moment of inertia and the
kinetic energy are exponentially lower bounded in the number of chain-closing collisions.

Proof:

This follows from the corresponding statements
[
Qu
, Prop. 3.51]

(concerning the kinetic energy) and

[
Qu
, Prop. 3.61]
(concerning the moment of inertia),
for systems that only have an accumulation point at time
T
, using the above
approximation from Proposition
3.26
.
Note that the number of chain-closing collisions of the
approximating systems differs from the original system at any time by at most 1.
The approximation can have a chain-closing collision that happens during
a total collision of a subsystem of particles earlier or later than the original system,
but at least at the time of the total collision of the subsystem the number of
chain-closing collisions match again.

\Box

4
Lagrangian relations for the non-deterministic system

A
Lagrangian relation
is a Lagrangian submanifold
of the symplectic product manifold

(X\times Y,\omega_{\ominus}:=\pi_{X}^{*}\omega_{X}-\pi_{Y}^{*}\omega_{Y})

of symplectic manifolds
(X,\omega_{X})
and
(Y,\omega_{Y})
.
An example is the graph of a symplectomorphism
X\to Y
.
Nondeterministic billiards do
not
lead to such graphs, even with
energy conservation.

4.1 Example

(Lagrangian relation for a nondeterministic billiard)

The cotangent bundle,
X=Y:=T^{*}{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
of the sphere is naturally symplectic.
The submanifold
L:=\{(0,x;0,y)\in X\times Y\mid x,y\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\}
is Lagrangian
but not a graph. It could model the reflection of an otherwise
free particle in configuration space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by the origin, with
incoming data
(p_{x},x)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
outgoing data
(p_{y},y)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and energy conservation
(say,
\|p_{x}\|=\|p_{y}\|=1
).
\Diamond

The last example has been generalised in
[
FKM
]
to repeated reflections of
a point particle by finitely many linear subspaces of configuration space.
Unlike in the present setting,
the scattering events were assumed to preserve kinetic energy.
In this section we analyse multiple scattering without energy preservation.
We show that by prescribing finite sequences of scattering subspaces and
of kinetic energies a Lagrangian relation is defined.

4.1

Our general goal is to use the non-deterministic system in the analysis of the
set
{\rm NC}
of deterministic non-collision singularities.
However, it is not obvious how to compare the two
dynamics in phase space
P
,

\bullet

since, unlike the total energy of the Hamiltonian
dynamics, the non-deterministic total (kinetic) energy can depend on time,

\bullet

and since the deterministic dynamics is complicated at near-collisions.

Since we are interested in measure-theoretic statements, we partially ignore these
difficulties by using Poincaré surfaces erected over submanifolds of
M

near
\Delta
, much like in Example
4.1
.

We now want to compose scattering by different subspaces. This amounts to

4.4 Remark

(composition of relations)

1.

For
sets

X,Y,Z
the
composition
of relations
L\subseteq X\times Y
and

M\subseteq Y\times Z
is given by the relation
(which we write as for the case of composition of maps)

M\circ L:=\{(x,z)\in X\times Z\mid\exists\,y\in Y:(x,y)\in L\mbox{ and }(y,z)\in M\}\,.

(4.5)

2.

For
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces
a relation is called
linear
if it is a
linear subspace of the respective
product space. The composition of linear relations is a linear relation. Even if

X
,
Y
,
Z
,
L
and
M
are of the same finite dimension
(as it is the case for the Lagrangian relations we consider),

\dim(M\circ L)
can be different if
L
or
M
is not a graph.
As examples, consider

L:=X\times\{0\}
and
M:=\{0\}\times Z
with
M\circ L=X\times Z
, or

L:=\{0\}\times Y
and
M:=Y\times\{0\}
with
M\circ L=\{0\}\times\{0\}
.
This defect is healed by assuming strong transversality.

3.

However, as shown in
[
GS
, Section 4]
of
Guillemin
and
Sternberg
,
the composition of

linear Lagrangian relations
is a linear Lagrangian relation.

4.

Smooth relations

L,M
,

are trivially minimisers of

. So for

q_{1}\neq q_{3}
the minimiser would not be unique.

By a variation of that argument one sees that then the minimiser
Q
in Part 3 of
Lemma
4.6
would not be Lipschitz continuous.

\Diamond

For
N>3
the functional
I_{q_{N},q_{1}}
is not differentiable on the full domain

\Delta^{E}_{{\cal C}_{N-1}}\times\ldots\times\Delta^{E}_{{\cal C}_{2}}
.
Hence (unlike for the case
N=3
of Lemma
4.6
) unique existence
of a minimiser cannot be expected. By restricting the domain, we still can show uniqueness:

4.8 Theorem

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, arbitrary
q_{1}\in\Xi_{{\cal C}_{1}}
,
q_{N}\in\Xi_{{\cal C}_{N}}
,
minimisers of
I_{q_{N},q_{1}}
on the open dense submanifold

\Xi_{{\cal C}_{N-1}}\times\ldots\times\Xi_{{\cal C}_{2}}
are unique for

{\cal C}_{1},\ldots,{\cal C}_{N}\in{\cal P}_{\Delta}
with
{\cal C}_{k+1}\neq{\cal C}_{k}
.

Proof:

For the sequence (
4.11
) of
{\cal C}_{k}
and

q_{k}\in\Xi_{{\cal C}_{k}}

(k=1,\ldots,N)
, see (
2.22
), we have
q_{k+1}\neq q_{k}
.
Then
I_{q_{N},q_{1}}
is smooth at
q=(q_{N-1},\ldots,q_{2})
, with

\displaystyle D_{q}I_{q_{N},q_{1}}(\delta q)=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{E_{N-1}}\,\langle\hat{q}_{N-1,N},\delta q_{N-1}\rangle_{\cal M}-\sqrt{E_{1}}\,\langle\hat{q}_{1,2},\delta q_{2}\rangle_{\cal M}

\displaystyle\textstyle+\sum_{\ell=2}^{N-2}\sqrt{E_{\ell}}\,\langle\hat{q}_{\ell,\ell+1},\delta q_{\ell+1}-\delta q_{\ell}\rangle_{\cal M}

and

\displaystyle D^{2}_{q}I_{q_{N},q_{1}}(\delta q^{I},\delta q^{I\!I})=

\displaystyle\textstyle\,\sqrt{E_{1}}\,\langle\delta q^{I}_{2},\frac{{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}-P_{\hat{q}_{1,2}}}{\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{\cal M}}\delta q^{I\!I}_{2}\rangle_{\cal M}

\displaystyle+\textstyle\sqrt{E_{N-1}}\,\langle\delta q^{I}_{N-1},\frac{{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}-P_{\hat{q}_{N-1,N}}}{\|q_{N-1}-q_{N}\|_{\cal M}}\delta q^{I\!I}_{N-1}\rangle_{\cal M}

\displaystyle+\textstyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{N-2}\sqrt{E_{\ell}}\,\langle\delta q^{I}_{\ell}-\delta q^{I}_{\ell+1},\frac{{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}-P_{\hat{q}_{\ell,\ell+1}}}{\|q_{\ell}-q_{\ell+1}\|_{\cal M}}(\delta q^{I\!I}_{\ell}-\delta q^{I\!I}_{\ell+1})\rangle_{\cal M}\,.

All terms are obviously positive semidefinite.
In fact
D^{2}_{q}I_{q_{N},q_{1}}
is positive definite, since
for
\delta q^{I}=\delta q^{I\!I}
the third term only vanishes if

\delta q^{I}_{2}=\ldots=\delta q^{I}_{N-1}
, and then the first two terms only vanish if

\delta q^{I}_{2}=0
.

Since
I_{q_{N},q_{1}}:\Delta^{E}_{{\cal C}_{N-1}}\times\ldots\times\Delta^{E}_{{\cal C}_{2}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is convex, this property suffices to show uniqueness of a minimiser.

For arbitrary
N\geq 3
,

D^{2}_{q}I_{q_{N},q_{1}}\geq 0
has a matrix representation of the form

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}M_{1}+M_{2}&-M_{2}&0&0&\ldots&0\\
-M_{2}&M_{2}+M_{3}&-M_{3}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&-M_{3}&M_{3}+M_{4}&-M_{4}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&&0&-M_{N-2}&M_{N-2}+M_{N-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

with
M_{k}:=\sqrt{E_{k}}({\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}-P_{\hat{q}_{k,k+1}})/\|q_{k+1}-q_{k}\|_{\cal M}
.
So

\bullet

this symmetric matrix has positive diagonal entries.

\bullet

It is
weakly diagonally dominant
, meaning that in each line the sum of the off-diagonals
is smaller or equal in absolute value than the diagonal.

\bullet

Furthermore, there exist lines (the first and the last one), where this inequality is strict.

\bullet

It is also
irreducible
, meaning that it cannot be conjugated by permutation matrices
to a matrix of the block form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}L_{1,1}&0\\
L_{2,1}&L_{2,2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
This can be inferred from the directed graph of the matrix
(whose adjacency matrix is the indicator of the non-zero entries of the matrix),
which is strongly connected.

These properties imply that the matrix is positive definite, see
Horn
and

Johnson

[
HJ
, Cor. 6.2.9]
.

\Box

4.9 Remark

(Comparison with Lemma
4.5
)

With some extra work, Theorem
4.8
implies the existence of
Lagrangian relations for the data
{\cal C}_{1},\ldots,{\cal C}_{N}\in{\cal P}_{\Delta}

with
{\cal C}_{k+1}\neq{\cal C}_{k}
. This follows, since the restriction of the functional
(
4.12
) to the open dense subset

\Xi_{{\cal C}_{N-1}}\times\ldots\times\Xi_{{\cal C}_{2}}
is not only smooth and
strictly convex, but also has been shown to have strictly positive second derivative.
So by the implicit function theorem the minimisers depend smoothly on

q_{1}\in\Xi_{{\cal C}_{1}}
and
q_{N}\in\Xi_{{\cal C}_{N}}
.

The proof of Lemma
4.5
is much more involved than the one of
Theorem
4.8
. Moreover, it treats only the case

N=3
(the composition of two Lagrangian relations).
In that case and for

(x_{1},x_{3})\in L_{{\cal C}_{2},{\cal C}_{3};E_{2},E_{3}}\circ L_{{\cal C}_{1},{\cal C}_{2};E_{1},E_{2}}

with
p_{3}^{I}=0
, one can uniquely reconstruct
x_{2}
as well as the

q_{i}\in\Delta_{{\cal C}_{i}}
(
i=1,3
), and then
q_{2}
is the unique minimiser of
I_{q_{3},q_{1}}
.

\Diamond
