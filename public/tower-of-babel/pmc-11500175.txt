Propionibacterium freudenreichii MJ2-derived extracellular vesicles inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis and improve collagen-induced rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Hee-Eun Woo, Joo-Young Cho, Young-Hee Lim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis causes excessive bone loss by stimulating osteoclast differentiation. Extracellular vesicles are valuable disease markers, conveyors of distant cell-to-cell communication, and carriers for drug delivery. The aim of this study was to investigate the anti-osteoclastogenic effects of extracellular vesicles derived from dairy Propionibacterium freudenreichii MJ2 (PFEVs) and the improvement effect of PFEVs on collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) animal model. PFEVs were observed by scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and LC–MS/MS. The inhibitory activity of PFEVs against receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)-induced osteoclast differentiation was investigated in RAW 264.7 cells. PFEVs significantly decreased the expression levels of genes and proteins related to osteoclast differentiation. PFEVs decreased RANK–RANKL binding. In a CIA mouse model, PFEVs treatment significantly reduced arthritis scores and collagen-specific immunoglobulins. PFEVs treatment also reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines. The anti-inflammatory effects were confirmed by H&E staining, and PFEVs treatment inhibited osteoclastogenesis in the CIA mouse model. In conclusion, PFEVs inhibited osteoclast differentiation by inhibiting RANK–RANKL signaling, thereby decreasing the expression of osteoclast differentiation-related genes. PFEVs also improved collagen-induced arthritis by inhibiting inflammation and osteoclastogenesis.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a prevalent immune-mediated disorder that primarily presents as symmetrical polyarticular inflammation, affecting the small joints of the hands and feet. RA is described as a systemic autoimmune condition marked by chronic inflammation capable of harming the joints and various non-joint organs, including the heart, kidneys, lungs, gastrointestinal system, eyes, skin, and nervous system. Its elusive etiology is characterized by inflammatory alterations in joint synovial tissue, cartilage, bone, and occasionally, extra-articular locations1–3. Despite significant advancements in managing RA, there is currently no medication that can completely cure the condition, highlighting the need for more potent and efficacious treatment approaches4,5.

RA causes excessive bone loss due to the stimulation of osteoclast differentiation by abnormal activation of the immune system6,7. Osteoclasts are giant multinucleated cells that function in bone resorption and play an important role in RA development8. Osteoclasts are derived from osteoclast precursors, mainly mononuclear macrophages, and differentiate from myeloid progenitors arising from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow9. Osteoclast differentiation is induced by two molecules, RANKL and macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and involves many downstream factors. In RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation, the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and calcium signaling pathways play integral roles in osteoclastogenesis. In these pathways, a transcription factor, the nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), is activated, and osteoclast-specific genes, such as cathepsin K (Ctsk), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (Trap), and calcitonin receptor (Calcr), are expressed. Various activators and inhibitors related to the development of RA have been shown to affect osteoclasts10,11. In addition, transcription factors, such as runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF), NF-κB, and c-FOS, are involved in the proliferation and differentiation of osteoclasts12,13. Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) is a type IV collagenase that is highly expressed in osteoclasts and degrades the extracellular matrix, contributing to joint destruction and stimulating arthritis14. Osteoprotegerin (OPG), a soluble receptor for RANKL, inhibits RANK–RANKL binding and prevents osteoclast differentiation15.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membranous structures containing diverse cell-derived molecules, such as nucleic acids, lipids, proteins, and metabolites. EVs are increasingly being acknowledged as valuable disease markers and conveyors for distant cell-to-cell communication. EVs also have significant potential as carriers for drug delivery, being cell-secreted particles that exhibit exceptional stability, biocompatibility, low toxicity, and bear surface proteins similar to those of their originating cells. EVs include several subgroups, including exosomes, microvesicles, and autophagic cells16. Among these, mammalian cell-derived exosomes have been studied for various biological functions using antitumor molecules, disease diagnostic markers, and drug delivery systems17,18. Bacterial cell-derived EVs mediate various functions, such as secreting useless cellular metabolites, promoting pathogenesis by carrying virulence factors, and contributing to cell–cell communication19,20. Bacterial EVs play important roles in disease progression21,22 but also show therapeutic potential23. EVs derived from Mycobacterium tuberculosis can act as therapeutic agents against tuberculosis, owing to their immunomodulatory activities24. Clostridium butyricum-derived EVs exert therapeutic effects in ulcerative colitis by alleviating intestinal inflammation25. EVs from Bacillus licheniformis generate reactive oxygen species, thereby increasing the expression levels of pro-apoptotic genes and resulting in increased cytotoxicity in cancer cells26. In addition, EVs derived from gram-positive bacteria, such as Bacillus anthracis, Clostridium perfringens, M. tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae, have been shown to act as effective vaccines in animal trials27.

In our previous study, Propionibacterium freudenreichii MJ2 was shown to inhibit osteoclastogenesis and collagen-induced arthritis (CIA)28. Thus, we hypothesized that EVs derived from P. freudenreichii MJ2 (PFEVs) inhibit osteoclastogenesis. In this study, we isolated PFEVs and investigated their anti-osteoclastogenesis and improvement effects on collagen-induced RA in a mouse model, as well as the associated mechanism of action.

Identified proteins in PFEVs

To identify the PFEVs, the overall proteins contained in the PFEVs were analyzed using nano-LC–MS/MS. A total of 585 proteins were identified in the PFEVs, including the chaperonin GroEL and methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase 12S and 5S subunits (Supplementary Table 1). The identified proteins underwent annotation using the Gene Ontology database and were divided into three categories: biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions (Fig. 2A). In the biological processes group, most proteins were related to cellular and metabolic processes. In the cellular component group, most proteins were related to cellular anatomical entities, intracellular anatomical structures, and the cytoplasm. Proteins related to catalytic activity, organic cyclic compound binding, and heterocyclic compound binding were identified in the molecular functions group. The analyzed proteins were classified through the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway and annotated keyword analyses (Supplementary Tables 2, 3). To investigate the relationship between these proteins and enrichment analysis, STRING analysis was performed on the 300 proteins with the highest Sequest HT score. Cytotoxic effects of PFEVs on RAW 264.7 cells

The cytotoxic effects of PFEVs on RAW 264.7 cells were determined using an MTT assay. No cytotoxicity was observed at concentrations of up to 108 particles/mL (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, cell viability significantly increased at concentrations of 106 and 107 particles/mL compared to the negative control. Therefore, in this study, we used PFEVs at concentrations of 106–108 particles/mL.

To investigate whether PFEVs inhibit the formation of the RANK–RANKL complex, the colocalization rate of RANK and RANKL in the presence of PFEVs was measured by immunofluorescence. The RANKL–RANK complex fluorescence signal decreased in cells treated with PFEVs (Fig. 3D). The colocalization rate of RANK and RANKL significantly decreased in the cells treated with 108 particles/mL PFEVs compared to that in the RANKL-only-treated cells (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that PFEVs may affect the binding of RANKL to RANK.

PFEVs decrease the expression levels of osteoclast differentiation-related genes and proteins in RAW 264.7 cells

To evaluate the effect of PFEVs on the expression levels of genes and proteins related to osteoclast differentiation, the expression levels of RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis-related genes were measured using qPCR, while RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis-related proteins were measured using western blotting. The expression levels of RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis-related genes, RANK, c-fos, NFATc1, Atp6v0d2, Ctsk, Calcr, MITF, TRAP, TRAF6, and PLCγ1, significantly decreased in cells treated with 107 and 108 particles/mL PFEVs compared to those in RANKL-only-treated cells (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the expression levels of OPG were significantly increased in cells treated with 107 and 108 particles/mL PFEVs compared to those in RANKL-only-treated cells (Fig. 4B). PFEVs inhibit osteoclastogenesis in CIA mice

Expression levels of the osteoclast differentiation-related genes, NFATc1, Ctsk, and Calcr, were significantly decreased in the PFEV-treated groups, except for the expression of Calcr in the 107 particles/mL PFEV-treated group, compared to those in the model group (Fig. 7D). In contrast, the OPG/RANKL ratio, an indicator of osteoclast differentiation inhibition, was significantly increased by PFEV treatment (Fig. 7E). These results are consistent with the in vitro results of the PFEV-treated groups. Expression levels of MMP9 significantly decreased in the PFEV-administered groups compared to those in the model group (Fig. 7F). These results suggest that PFEV administration inhibited osteoclast differentiation in CIA mice.

Effect of PFEVs on hepatotoxicity in CIA mice

To evaluate the liver toxicity of PFEVs, we measured the AST and ALT levels in PFEV-administered CIA mice. No significant differences in AST or ALT levels were observed between groups (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results indicate that PFEV treatment did not induce hepatotoxicity in CIA mice.

Discussion

EVs from probiotics have been studied for their beneficial health effects, such as the reduction of inflammation in colitis29, skin inflammation30, and antidepressant effects31. Surface proteins from P. freudenreichii MJ2 show significant anti-osteoclastogenic activity32. Among the surface proteins, chaperones are the main components. In this study, although we did not identify the active component(s) in the PFEVs, they contained a 60 kDa chaperonin that showed the highest score for HT. PFEVs inhibited RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation. TRAP staining revealed that PFEVs inhibited the development of mature multinucleated osteoclasts. Double immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that PFEVs were presumed to affect the binding of RANKL to RANK, which regulates osteoclast formation, thereby inhibiting osteoclast differentiation. Additionally, PFEV administration improved collagen-induced RA in mice.

Previously, 319 proteins were identified in EVs from P. freudenreichii CIRM-BIA 129 and showed immunomodulatory effects by modulating the NF-κB pathway33. We identified 585 proteins in PFEVs. EVs from P. freudenreichii CIRM-BIA 129 contained diverse Clusters of Orthologous Group-categorized proteins, including immunomodulatory proteins. Through in silico protein–protein interaction prediction, it was determined that the proteins in the EVs could interact with human immunomodulatory proteins, such as NF-κB and Toll-like receptors. The proteins in the PFEVs spanned diverse Clusters of Orthologous Group categories and biological processes, and most proteins were related to cellular and metabolic processes. Among the proteins, the chaperonin GroEL, a moonlighting protein, showed the highest Sequest HT score. P. freudenreichii is a well-known propionate producer. Notably, proteins involved in the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) and propanoate metabolism were included in the 300 proteins with the highest Sequest HT scores. Further studies are required to determine whether these proteins are related to the inhibitory effects of PFEVs on osteoclast differentiation.

In osteoclastogenesis, through RANK–RANKL binding, NF-κB, MAPK, and calcium signaling pathways facilitate the development of osteoclasts. Through these pathways, transcription factors, such as NF-κB, MITF, c-Fos, and NFATc1, are activated. Among them, NFATc1 acts as a master transcription regulator of osteoclastogenesis34. Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) is the major adaptor protein mediating RANKL-induced signaling cascades, and phospholipase Cγ (PLCγ) is also recruited to RANK35. These proteins regulate osteoclast differentiation by mediating RANK–RANKL signaling. Expression levels of RANK, Traf6, and PLCγ1 decreased in PFEV-treated cells in this study. In the calcium signaling pathway, inhibition of PLCγ activity blocks early osteoclast development and function36–38. PLCγ 1 and 2 both modulate osteoclast development independently through the calcium signaling pathway39. The expression levels of Mitf, c-fos, and Nfatc1 significantly decreased by PFEV treatment, thereby inhibiting the expression of the genes related to osteoclast differentiation. This finding indicates that PFEVs contribute to osteoclast differentiation inhibition by decreasing the expression levels of these transcription factors. In addition, the immunohistochemistry results suggested that PFEVs may hinder RANK–RANKL binding. Therefore, PFEVs inhibited RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation by inhibiting RANK–RANKL binding, transcription factor expression, and osteoclastogenesis-related gene expression.

CIA susceptible DBA/1 mice are the most frequently used animal models of arthritis40. PFEV administration reduced the incidence of arthritis and significantly decreased the arthritis score by decreasing paw swelling and the degree of redness. PFEV administration improved inflammation in the knee joints of CIA mice and decreased TRAP( +) osteoclast formation, indicating that PFEV administration inhibited osteoclast differentiation in CIA mice. Collagen-specific immunoglobulins aggravate arthritis by recruiting neutrophils and macrophages to inflamed sites41. Thus, reduced collagen-specific immunoglobulin levels, especially the significantly reduced IgG2 levels by PFEVs, might reduce the inflammatory response, thereby resulting in the alleviation of RA severity. Furthermore, PFEVs decreased the levels of important pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased the levels of IL-10, a representative anti-inflammatory cytokine. IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17 exacerbate RA by promoting osteoclastogenesis42. Thus, PFEVs may contribute to the improvement of RA by decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. MMP-9 is highly expressed in osteoclasts and is a type IV collagenase that contributes to the degradation of the extracellular matrix of bone in patients with RA43. MMP-9 expression is increased upon RANKL stimulation during osteoclastogenesis, indicating that MMP-9 contributes to osteoclast differentiation. OPG binds RANKL and prevents RANK–RANKL binding, thereby preventing osteoclast differentiation. In this study, expression levels of MMP9 and OPG significantly decreased and increased, respectively, in PFEV-treated CIA mice, thereby preventing osteoclast differentiation and resulting in the prevention of joint bone destruction in the CIA model. Thus, PFEV administration reduced RA symptoms by inhibiting collagen-specific IgG production, enhancing the production of IL-10, inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and inhibiting the expression of genes related to osteoclast differentiation. Although further in vivo studies on the detailed mechanisms are needed, PFEVs may have the potential to improve RA symptoms.

In conclusion, PFEVs inhibit osteoclast differentiation by hindering RANK–RANKL binding, thereby decreasing the expression levels of downstream genes related to osteoclastogenesis. PFEVs derived from next-generation probiotic bacteria showed no cytotoxicity or hepatotoxicity at the concentrations used in this study, indicating their safety for clinical application. Therefore, PFEVs may be used to improve or prevent RA, especially RA caused by excess osteoclast formation. Further investigations are required to identify the active component(s) in EVs and demonstrate their effects.

Materials and methods

Culture of P. freudenreichii MJ2

P. freudenreichii MJ2 (KCCM12272P)44 was grown in YELA (tryptone 1%, yeast extract 1%, K2HPO4 0.025%, MnSO4 0.005%, and sodium lactate 1%) medium for 48 h at 30 °C in an anaerobic chamber (GasPak EZ container; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Preparation of PFEVs

To isolate EVs, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, effectively separating the cellular debris. Subsequently, the supernatants underwent filtration through a 0.22 μm bottle-top vacuum filter to eliminate any residual cell debris. To isolate EVs with a size of 100 kDa or greater, those below this threshold were separated using ultrafiltration (Amicon® Stirred Ultrafiltration Cell from Millipore, USA). Further purification of the EVs involved ultracentrifugation (CP100WX, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) after an additional round of filtration through a 0.22 μm vacuum filter to ensure thorough removal of any remaining cellular debris. To eliminate components of the culture medium, the EV pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 150,000×g for 3 h at 4 °C. The collected PFEVs were carefully diluted in PBS and stored at ‒80 °C for subsequent analysis. The concentration of PFEV was 8.78 × 1010 particles/mL.

Electron microscopy analyses

For transmission electron microscopy, purified PFEVs were placed on Formvar/Carbon Copper Grids (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), incubated for 1 min, and then stained negatively with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min. The PFEVs were examined using an 80 kV JEM1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). For scanning electron microscopy, cultured P. freudenreichii MJ2 cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was carefully washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the pellet was fixed using Karnovsky’s fixative at 4 °C for 4 h. After fixation, the bacterial cells underwent a series of three washes with 0.05 M sodium cacodylate buffer before being further fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (Sigma) at 4 °C for 1 h. To remove any excess chemicals, the sample was washed three times with distilled water. For sample preparation and imaging, a gradual dehydration process was employed using 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and finally, 100% ethanol, and each concentration was applied for 10 min. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated using hexamethyldisilazane (Sigma) for 20 min. Prior to observation, the samples were coated with a layer of platinum to improve their conductivity. The prepared samples were observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM-6701F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The PFEV sizes and counts were determined using a NanoSight LM-10 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK) and nanoparticle tracking analysis with constant flow injection. Analysis settings were as follows: Frames processed: 900 of 900; Frames per second: 30.00; Calibration: 166 nm/pixel; Blur: auto; Detection threshold: 5 Multi; Min track length: auto; Min expected size: auto; Temperature: 22.00 ℃; Viscosity: 0.95 cP. The data were subsequently analyzed using NanoSight NTA 2.3 software (https://www.malvernpanalytical.com/en/learn/knowledge-center/user manuals/man0514en).

Protein identification by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

Mass spectrometric analyses were conducted using a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with a Dionex U 3000 RSLCnano HPLC system, according to previously described procedures32. Data were collected using Xcaliber software version 4.3, with the Orbitrap analyzer scanning precursor ions within a mass range of 350–1800 m/z at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200. Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany) was employed for peptide identification using the SEQUEST algorithm, searching against Propionibacterium freudenreichii proteome databases derived from UniProt sequences. The search considered up to 2 missed cleavages with trypsin as the enzyme. Variable modifications included methionine oxidation and cysteine carboxylmethylation. The initial parent and fragment ion maximum mass deviation were set at 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. To filter the identified peptides, a significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied, ensuring the selection of statistically significant results. Proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD051291. We analyzed the top 300 proteins with the highest Sequest HT scores among the isolated proteins using STRING database version 12. Interactions between proteins were assessed using a high-confidence cutoff (0.7). The protein networks were analyzed using Cytoscape version 3.7.1.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity assay

RAW 264.7 cells from the Korea Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To measure cytotoxicity, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL. After a 24-h incubation period, the cells were treated with PFEVs (1 × 106, 1 × 107, 1 × 108, and 1 × 109 particles/mL) for 4 days. Following the treatment period, the culture medium was aspirated and cytotoxicity was measured by MTT (3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide) assay (Amresco, Solon, OH, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax 340PC384, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), and the viability percentage was calculated relative to the negative control group.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase staining

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL overnight at 37 °C, and the medium was changed to Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) to induce osteoclast differentiation. Subsequently, the cells were treated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) and PFEVs (1 × 106, 1 × 107, or 1 × 108 particles/mL) for 4 days. Following treatment, the cells were stained using a tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining kit (Takara Biotechnology, Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing more than three nuclei were quantified under a microscope. TRAP activity was measured using a TRAP activity assay kit (Takara Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

The cells were treated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) (Rocky Hill) and PFEVs (1 × 106, 1 × 107, or 1 × 108 particles/mL) for 4 days. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and total RNA was extracted using Ribo-Ex reagent (GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis was performed using a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with a Kapa SYBR Fast qPCR kit (Kapa Biosystems, Woburn, MA, USA). The qPCR reaction consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The reference gene used in this study was glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4. The normalized expression change was represented as 2-∆∆Ct, with the GAPDH control set as 145. For the extraction of mouse RNA, knee joints were excised, homogenized using Ribo-Ex reagent, and centrifuged at 13,000×g for 10 min. qPCR was performed using the supernatant as described above.

Western blotting

The cells were treated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) (Rocky Hill) and PFEVs (1 × 106, 1 × 107, or 1 × 108 particles/mL) for 4 days. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer (Bylabs, Korea) containing Halt™ protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific), and the concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). An equal amount of protein (10 μg) was subjected to denaturation and separation via 10% SDS-PAGE. The proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 6% dry non-fat skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) for 2 h, washed with TBST, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies against β-actin (endogenous control; 1:10,000 dilution, GTX109639; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), RANK (1:500 dilution, sc-374360; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), NFATc1 (1:500 dilution, sc-7294; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), c-fos (1:1,000 dilution, 2250; Cell signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), phosphorylated p65 NF-κB (1:1,000 dilution, 3033; Cell Signaling), and p65 NF-κB (1:1,000 dilution, 8242; Cell Signaling) were used. After further washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at around 25 °C. Anti-RANK and NFATc1 antibodies were detected using a goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (HRP) (1:4,000 dilution, GTX213111-01; GeneTex), whereas the other proteins were detected using a goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (HRP) (1:4,000 dilution, GTX213110-01; GeneTex). After washing with TBST, the proteins were observed using the SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate kit (Thermo Scientific). Western blot images were captured using UVP Chemstudio 515 (Anlytik Jena GmbH, Jena, Germany), and band densities were quantified using ImageJ software version 1.54 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) (https://imagej.net/ij/download.html).

Immunofluorescence

The cells that differentiated into osteoclasts following RANKL (50 ng/mL) treatment were washed with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The cells were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS containing 0.5% Tween 20 (PBS-T) at room temperature for 30 min. The primary antibodies used were against RANKL (1:100, AF462; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and RANK (1:100, sc-374360; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The antibodies were diluted in 1% BSA in PBS-T and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were washed with PBS-T three times for 5 min and then incubated with secondary antibodies in 1% BSA in PBS-T at room temperature for 1 h. Donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa 546 (1:500 dilution, A10036; Thermo Scientific) and donkey anti-goat IgG Alexa 488 (1:500 dilution, A11055; Thermo Scientific) were used as the secondary antibodies for magenta and cyan fluorescence. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, D9542; Sigma) for 10 min, and the coverslips were mounted with ProLong gold antifade mountant (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Images were obtained using an SP8 X confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). RANK and RANKL colocalization rates were analyzed with Leica LAS X 1.4.6 software (https://www.leica-microsystems.com/products/microscope-software/p/leica-las-x-ls/downloads/).

Animal model of CIA

Eight-week-old male DBA/1J mice weighing 19–21 g were obtained from Central Lab Animal Inc. (Seoul, Korea). All experimental procedures were approved by the Korea University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. KUIACUC-2023–0023). All experimental procedures were in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23, 1996). All experiments were performed according to the ARRIVE Guidelines Checklist for animal in vivo experiments. The mice were housed under controlled conditions with a temperature of 22 ± 1 ℃, humidity of 55 ± 10%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were fed LabDiet 5053 (Lab Diet, St. Louis, MO, USA) and water. A total of 32 mice were acclimated for 7 days and then randomly divided into four groups, each consisting of eight mice (Supplementary Table 5): the normal control group (control), CIA model group (model), CIA mice treated with a low dose of PFEVs (1 × 107 particles/mL) (LDEV), and CIA mice treated with a high dose of PFEVs (1 × 108 particles/mL) (HDEV). PFEVs were intraperitoneally injected three times per week. For the induction of CIA, an emulsion of 100 μg of bovine type II collagen (Chondrex, Redmond, WA, USA) in an equal volume of Freund’s complete adjuvant (Chondrex) was intradermally injected into the tail. After 21 days, a booster emulsion of type II collagen (100 μg) in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Chondrex) was injected at the tail. The severity of CIA was assessed every 4 days by monitoring and scoring the condition of all four feet of each mouse on a scale of 0 to 4 (Supplementary Table 6). The arthritis score was calculated as the sum of the scores for all four feet of each mouse.

Blood biochemical analysis

To examine the hepatocellular toxicity of PFEVs in collagen-treated mice, the activity levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were assessed using a FUJI DRI-CHEM 3500i instrument using FUJI DRI-CHEM slides (FUJIFILM Co., Tokyo, Japan).

Histological analysis

The hind legs of the mice were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histological analysis. Subsequently, the specimens were demineralized using a 10% EDTA solution and then embedded in paraffin blocks. Paraffin-embedded joint tissues were sectioned and stained using a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) protocol. To investigate osteoclast differentiation, TRAP staining was performed using a TRAP Staining Solution Mix (Takara Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The slides were examined under an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Measurement of collagen-specific IgG and cytokine levels

Serum was obtained by collecting whole blood from sacrificed mice through cardiac puncture while anesthetized by breathing isoflurane and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 3000×g at 4 °C for 20 min. The serum was then analyzed to quantify the levels of total type II collagen-specific IgG (CII-IgG), CII-IgG1, and CII-IgG2a using ELISA kits (Chondrex), as well as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 using ELISA kits (Thermo Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The divergence between the two groups was assessed using Student’s t-test, whereas distinctions spanning multiple groups were elucidated through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the application of Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc analysis. Statistical significance was acknowledged for p-values < 0.05.
