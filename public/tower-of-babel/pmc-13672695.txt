Dual-Eligible Patient Burden Is Independently Associated With Lower Standardized Reimbursement Among Lumbar Laminectomy Surgeons: A National Medicare Analysis
Authors: Alexander Kucherina, Steven Govinda Persaud, Paul Mastrokostas, Charla Fischer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignRetrospective cross-sectional analysis of a national Medicare administrative dataset (2023).ObjectivesDual eligibility for Medicare and Medicaid is an established marker of socioeconomic vulnerability, but its association with surgeon reimbursement is uncharacterized in the lumbar laminectomy market. We evaluated whether surgeon-level dual-eligibility proportion is associated with laminectomy volume and standardized reimbursement.MethodsSingle-level lumbar laminectomy surgeons were identified by CPT code 63047 in the 2023 Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Physician and Other Practitioners file. Dual-eligibility proportion, the share of dual-eligible beneficiaries in each surgeon’s Medicare panel, was categorized into quartiles. Outcomes were laminectomy volume and mean standardized reimbursement per procedure; panel comorbidity, minority composition, behavioral health diagnoses, and rurality were also assessed. Quartile and multivariable analyses were restricted to the 3,075 surgeons (73.7%) with complete panel data.ResultsWe identified 4,170 surgeons performing 102,006 procedures. Higher dual-eligibility proportion was associated with lower standardized reimbursement (Q1, $672.81 vs. Q4, $502.53), greater beneficiary risk (Hierarchical Condition Category score, 1.11 vs. 1.38), higher minority panel composition, and more anxiety (28.88% vs. 34.21%) and depression (26.00% vs. 32.30%); all P < 0.001. Each 1-percentage-point increase in dual-eligibility proportion was independently associated with $5.68 lower standardized reimbursement (β = −5.676; 95% CI, −7.75 to −3.60; P < 0.001).ConclusionsSurgeons who disproportionately serve dual-eligible beneficiaries receive lower standardized reimbursement despite treating more medically and psychosocially complex patients. Because analyses required complete CMS panel data, estimates may not generalize to lower-volume or rural providers.

Introduction

Single-level lumbar laminectomy is among the most performed spinal procedures within the United States, with annual utilization continuing to rise with a growing older population and expanded surgical indications.1,2 Despite its widespread use and established efficacy for relieving spinal compression, access to lumbar laminectomy has not been uniformly accessible across the population. Socioeconomic disadvantage, insurance status, and minority demographics, for instance, have all been associated with lower utilization of elective spine surgery, higher perioperative complication rates, and worse patient-reported outcomes following lumbar procedures.3-5

Among the most robust administrative markers of socioeconomic vulnerability available in large claims datasets is dual eligibility status for Medicare and Medicaid. 6 These patients represent a severely disadvantaged subset of the Medicare population which is disproportionately affected by chronic medical comorbidities and social complexity. As a result, they consistently experience worse health outcomes than their Medicare-only counterparts. 7 Prior work in other surgical specialties has shown that these dual-eligible patients experience higher rates of postoperative readmission, greater utilization of post-acute care, and persistently lower rates of elective procedures compared to Medicare-only enrollees.8,9

While socioeconomic disparities in spine surgery have been characterized from the patient perspective, the downstream implications for surgeons who disproportionately care for dual-eligible populations remain poorly understood. 10 In particular, the relationship between the proportion of dual-eligible patients within a surgeon’s Medicare panel and that surgeon’s procedural volume and reimbursement has not been examined within the lumbar laminectomy market. Understanding this relationship is important, as financial and volume-related pressures at the surgeon level may represent a mechanism through which socioeconomic disparities in surgical access are amplified.

This study utilized dual-eligibility proportion as a marker of socioeconomic patient panel complexity for surgeons, and it examined whether it was independently associated with annual single-level lumbar laminectomy volume and standardized Medicare reimbursement per procedure. We hypothesized that surgeons caring for higher proportions of dual-eligible patients would receive lower standardized Medicare reimbursement despite managing patients with greater medical and psychosocial complexity.

Methods

Data Source

A retrospective, cross-sectional analysis of the publicly available Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) “Physician and Other Practitioners” dataset for 2023 was performed. This dataset reports annual procedure-level billing records for Medicare fee-for-service patients, including National Provider Identifier (NPI), procedure counts, beneficiary panel characteristics, and reimbursement data. Standardized payments, as defined by CMS, remove geographic variation in reimbursement by adjusting for the Geographic Practice Cost Index (GPCI) and local wage indices, allowing direct cross-regional comparisons independent of practice location. Use of standardized rather than actual payments ensures that observed reimbursement differences across dual-eligibility quartiles reflect patient panel characteristics rather than regional cost-of-living variation. Single-level lumbar laminectomy procedures were identified using CPT code 63047. Surgeons were included for any year in which they submitted at least 11 Medicare claims for this procedure, consistent with the minimum threshold for inclusion in the CMS database. As this dataset contains no individually identifiable patient information, Institutional Review Board approval was not required.

Study Variables

The primary exposure was surgeon-level dual-eligibility proportion: the percentage of each surgeon’s Medicare beneficiary panel dually enrolled in Medicare and Medicaid during the 2023 study year. This variable was categorized into quartiles within the complete-case analytic sample, with Q1 representing the lowest and Q4 the highest proportion. Annual laminectomy procedural volume per surgeon and mean standardized Medicare reimbursement per procedure served as the primary outcomes. Surgeon panel covariates included mean Hierarchical Condition Category (HCC) risk score, minority beneficiary proportion, anxiety and depression diagnosis rates as indices of behavioral health complexity, and practice rurality classified by Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes within the CMS dataset.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for the full cohort of 4,170 surgeons. Comparative and regression analyses were restricted to the 3,075 surgeons (73.7%) with complete data. The remaining 1,095 (26.3%) were excluded solely because one or more panel-level covariates were suppressed in the public CMS file, which redacts beneficiary characteristics derived from fewer than 11 beneficiaries; no surgeon was excluded on the basis of an outcome or any additional criterion. Included and excluded surgeons were compared across all variables reported for both groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher exact tests. Quartile comparisons used one-way analysis of variance with prespecified Q1-versus-Q4 contrasts evaluated post hoc by the Tukey method; categorical variables were compared by chi-square, or Fisher exact where expected cell counts were fewer than five. Multivariable ordinary least squares linear regression, rather than a multilevel model, assessed the independent association between continuous dual-eligibility proportion and mean standardized reimbursement per procedure, adjusting for practice rurality, HCC risk score, and minority beneficiary proportion, and was fit in the complete-case sample (n = 3,075). Estimates are reported as β coefficients with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals. Significance was defined as P < 0.05; analyses used R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Comparison of Included and Excluded Surgeons

Of the 4,170 surgeons in the descriptive cohort, 1,095 (26.3%) were not carried into quartile-based and multivariable analyses because at least one panel-level covariate was suppressed in the public CMS file. Annual laminectomy volume was essentially identical between the analytic and excluded groups (24.5 vs. 24.3 cases), indicating that exclusion did not track surgical activity. Excluded surgeons did have lower mean standardized reimbursement per procedure ($373 vs. $578) and were more frequently based in rural or small-town settings (9.0% vs. 0.2%; P < 0.001, Fisher exact test). This pattern is consistent with the suppression rule itself, which is triggered by small Medicare beneficiary panels rather than by low procedural volume, and which therefore falls most often on surgeons in smaller and more geographically dispersed practices. Patient Complexity and Practice Rurality

As seen in Table 2, surgeons in higher dual-eligibility proportion quartiles cared for patients with substantially greater medical and psychosocial complexity. Mean HCC risk score increased from 1.11 in Q1 to 1.38 in Q4 (P < 0.001), representing a 24% greater comorbidity load among surgeons treating the highest proportion of dual-eligible patients. Behavioral health complexity was also markedly elevated, with rates of anxiety diagnoses rising from 28.88% in Q1 to 34.21% in Q4, and depression rates from 26.00% to 32.30% (both P < 0.001). Minority panel composition was highest in Q4 (9.66%) and differed significantly across quartiles (P < 0.001, ANOVA), although the gradient was non-monotonic (Q1, 6.15%; Q2, 5.09%; Q3, 6.06%); the Q1 versus Q4 contrast remained significant on post hoc pairwise testing (Tukey-adjusted P < 0.001). Across the full cohort, most laminectomy surgeons practiced in metropolitan settings (93.96%), with 3.53% in micropolitan and 2.52% in rural or small-town locations (Table 3). The rurality estimate was imprecise, consistent with the small number of non-metropolitan surgeons retained in the analytic cohort. Discussion

This retrospective, cross-sectional analysis of the 2023 CMS Physician and Other Practitioners dataset was performed to evaluate whether surgeon-level dual-eligibility proportion was associated with annual single-level lumbar laminectomy volume and standardized Medicare reimbursement. Higher dual-eligibility proportion was independently associated with lower standardized reimbursement per laminectomy, declining from $672.81 in Q1 to $502.53 in Q4, a 25% reduction, persisting after multivariable adjustment for rurality, HCC risk score, and minority panel composition. Higher dual-eligibility proportion also co-occurred with lower laminectomy volume across quartiles, though the directionality of this association cannot be established from panel-level data alone.

The clinical relevance of the volume co-occurrence is underscored by prior work establishing that surgeons below volume benchmarks for lumbar decompression face a 63% increase in complication risk compared to higher-volume peers, with similar increases in 90-day readmission rates.11,12 The Q4 surgeons in this study averaged 20.04 annual laminectomies, falling below the 25-case threshold identified by Schoenfeld et al as the volume benchmark for lumbar decompression, below which complication risk increases by 63% and readmission rates rise comparably. 11 While the causality of this relationship between dual eligibility proportion and case volume cannot be fully established from the aggregated panel-level data available in the dataset, this co-occurrence warrants further investigation using patient-level claims data.

In addition, dual-eligible patients face competing medical priorities, transportation limitations, and social instability that reduce elective surgical uptake independent of clinical candidacy.13-15 At the practice level, the administrative demands of managing dual-eligible patients, including prior authorization complexity and Medicaid coverage variability across states, may reduce willingness to accept or pursue referrals from this population.16-18 This is consistent with prior work demonstrating that low Medicaid reimbursement for common spine codes, averaging 13% below Medicare nationally and ranging from 46% to 160% lower by state, creates tangible financial disincentives at the practice level. 19 Surgeons whose panels are concentrated with socioeconomically disadvantaged patients may also face structural constraints on practice growth, including limited access to the inpatient infrastructure and ancillary resources that support high-volume elective surgery. Practice rurality did not differ across dual-eligibility quartiles (P = 0.87); however, non-metropolitan surgeons were sparsely represented in the analytic cohort, so this comparison has limited power to exclude a geographic contribution to the observed gradients.

The decreased reimbursement findings for these surgeons warrant particular attention in the context of the broader Medicare payment environment. Inflation-adjusted Medicare reimbursement for CPT 63047 declined 31.9% between 2000 and 2020, a trend well documented across lumbar spine procedures. 20 Against this backdrop, the additional 25% reimbursement gap observed between Q1 and Q4 surgeons represents a compounding financial disadvantage for those who disproportionately serve dual-eligible patients. Standardized payments are designed to remove geographic variation from reimbursement comparisons, yet the gradient persisted after this adjustment, implying that factors other than location drive differential compensation. Dual-eligible patients may be more likely to undergo laminectomy in lower-cost outpatient settings, experience more frequent claims modifications, or require perioperative resource utilization not reflected in current payment structures.21,22 This is consistent with the hospital-surgeon reimbursement asymmetry described in the arthroplasty literature, where high-risk patients have been associated with higher hospital reimbursement but not corresponding increases at the surgeon level, suggesting that the financial demands of complexity fall disproportionately on the operating surgeon. 23

The stepwise increase in anxiety and depression rates across dual-eligibility quartiles, from 28.88% to 34.21% for anxiety and 26.00% to 32.30% for depression, adds a dimension not captured by HCC risk scores alone. Both conditions are independently associated with worse patient-reported outcomes and lower satisfaction following lumbar decompression, and are not adequately captured by HCC-based risk adjustment or standard comorbidity indices used in current reimbursement models.24,25 As value-based payment programs incorporating patient-reported outcome measures become increasingly embedded in Medicare reimbursement structures, surgeons serving higher dual-eligibility panels face a compounding disadvantage.

Current Medicare payment frameworks do not account for the care coordination demands and social complexity associated with high dual-eligible panel concentration at the surgeon level. Risk-adjusted reimbursement mechanisms modeled on Medicaid disproportionate share hospital payments represent a potential structural remedy, as do social determinants-informed risk adjustment frameworks within value-based payment programs. Without such adjustments, the financial pressures documented here are likely to persist. If reimbursement continues without accounting for dual-eligible panel complexity, these disparities may widen as dual enrollment grows, making it progressively harder for the most vulnerable patients to access some of the most commonly performed spinal procedures.26,27

These findings should be interpreted in the context of the limitations inherent to a retrospective database study. The analysis is cross-sectional and restricted to 2023 Medicare fee-for-service claims; surgeons practicing predominantly within managed care or commercial environments are not captured, and the 11-case minimum reporting threshold excludes the lowest-volume providers, which may attenuate the observed gradients. Furthermore, dual-eligibility proportion is a panel-level exposure, and individual patient-level insurance status, surgeon selection behavior, and referral patterns cannot be assessed. As such, the observed association between dual-eligibility proportion and laminectomy volume should be interpreted as correlational since the directionality of this relationship cannot be established from the available data. In addition, standardized reimbursement adjusts for geographic payment variation but does not account for case complexity, procedure duration, or site-of-service differences, all of which may independently influence payment. Lastly, the modest adjusted R2 value in the regression model reflects the expected limitations of surgeon-level administrative data; residual confounding by practice model, fellowship training, referral network structure, and surgeon experience cannot necessarily be excluded.

Finally, analyses were restricted to the 3,075 surgeons (73.7%) with complete panel-level data. This reflects the CMS file’s suppression of beneficiary characteristics derived from fewer than 11 beneficiaries rather than any selection applied here, and falls disproportionately on surgeons with smaller Medicare panels, whose practices are more often non-metropolitan. Laminectomy volume was equivalent between included and excluded surgeons, so the analytic cohort is representative with respect to surgical activity; excluded surgeons did have lower standardized reimbursement, suggesting the reported gradient is conservative rather than inflated. The null association for rurality therefore reflects how few non-metropolitan surgeons were retained rather than evidence that geography is unimportant. Imputation was not undertaken because the suppressed values are censored by design, and extending these findings to low-volume and rural surgeons will require patient-level claims data.

Conclusion

Surgeons caring for higher proportions of dual-eligible Medicare beneficiaries receive lower standardized Medicare reimbursement per laminectomy despite managing patients with greater medical comorbidity, behavioral health complexity, and socioeconomic vulnerability. This association persists after adjustment for geographic practice setting, comorbidity burden, and minority panel composition. Higher dual-eligibility proportion also co-occurs with lower laminectomy procedural volume, though the directionality of this relationship cannot be established from panel-level data. These findings suggest that the current reimbursement structure may create financial disincentives for surgeons treating this vulnerable population, with implications for equitable access to lumbar laminectomy care. Policy interventions that account for dual-eligible panel complexity at the surgeon level will be essential to addressing these structural disparities.

Ethical Considerations

This study utilized de-identified, publicly available Medicare claims data (CMS Physician and Other Practitioners dataset) and was determined not to constitute human subjects research. Accordingly, Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required.
