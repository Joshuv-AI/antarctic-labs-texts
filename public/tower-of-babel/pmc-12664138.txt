Proliferation of CD10+ mature plasmacytoid dendritic cells in lymph nodes of patients with chronic non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection: a case report
Authors: Longyi Zhang, Jinwen Jiang, Xuya Chen, Yingqian Sun, Yan Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlasmacytoid dendritic cells are essential immune system components, critical in antiviral and antibacterial defense. Although plasmacytoid dendritic cells are typically present in small numbers in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymph nodes, their expansion within the lymph nodes accompanied by CD10 expression is a relatively uncommon phenomenon.Case presentation.A 54-year-old Chinese female had experienced recurrent lymphadenopathy for over 2 years. She was diagnosed with Mycobacterium chimaera infection more than 1 year before presentation, and her condition was assessed as being in remission 4 months prior. She was readmitted to the hospital because of lymphadenopathy and underwent a lymph node biopsy. Clinicians and pathologists should consider the possibility of plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation in patients with chronic or refractory nontuberculous mycobacterial infections, particularly in those with lymphadenopathy. Background

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are opportunistic pathogens; notably, they are widely distributed in the environment and can cause a spectrum of infections ranging from localized to disseminated diseases [1]. Owing to their intrinsic drug resistance, NTM infection treatment involves prolonged regimens (often exceeding 12 months), significant drug toxicity, uncertain efficacy, and high relapse rates, posing major clinical management challenges [2, 3]. Immunocompromised individuals, including those with human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, recipients of solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplants, those receiving chronic corticosteroid therapy, and those with underlying hematological malignancies, are at risk of developing chronic NTM infections [4].

Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) represent a specialized subset of dendritic cells (DCs) characterized by their unique plasmacytoid morphology and exceptional capacity to produce large quantities of type I interferons, which represent key cytokines involved in the early host defense against viral infections [5]. pDCs are vital in bridging innate and adaptive immune responses; moreover, their dysregulation has been implicated in various diseases, including autoimmune disorders [6], malignancies [7], and infectious conditions [8].

This article reports a case of chronic NTM infection characterized by prominent proliferation of CD10+ mature pDCs in the lymph nodes, thereby enhancing understanding of the immunological alterations induced by chronic NTM infection and underscoring the importance of including pDC proliferation in the differential diagnosis of similar clinical contexts. Notably, a pDC subset expressed CD10. Therefore, it is essential to strictly differentiate it from diseases with similar phenotypic characteristics, such as blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN) and angioimmunoblastic T cell lymphoma (AITL), to avoid misdiagnosis-induced treatment direction deviations.

Case presentation

This case involved a 54-year-old female Chinese patient with chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm and lymph node infection caused by Mycobacterium chimaera, who had been previously reported by our institution [9]. NTM infection diagnosis was confirmed based on clinical presentation, histopathological features, and positive MGIT960 culture. After 11 months of sustained anti-NTM therapy, the patient exhibited substantial clinical improvement on 10 July 2024.

On 18 November 2024, the patient was readmitted for cervical lymphadenopathy. Color Doppler ultrasound revealed multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the bilateral cervical regions (largest right node: 16 × 12 mm; largest left node: 19 × 10 mm), which had increased in size compared with the ultrasound findings from 10 July 2024 (Fig. 1). The patient had previously undergone a lymph node biopsy in April 2022 and February 2023. To investigate the cause of recurrent lymph node enlargement, a third biopsy was performed during admission.

The biopsy tissue was subjected to flow cytometry, histopathological analysis, and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Flow cytometry demonstrated that no lymphoma cells were detected in the tests conducted in April 2022 and February 2023, and none were found in the current study; however, a cell population with a specific phenotype was identified, accounting for 2.95% of all the nucleated cells. The phenotype of this cell population was CD3−CD4+ CD8−CD10+CD123+CD303+CD304+ CD56−PD-1−Ki-67lowCXCR5−CD57−CD2+CD5−CD7+CD20−CD19−HLA-DR+CD30− (Fig. 2). Histopathological assessment revealed plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated partial positivity for CD10 and CD56; positivity for CD4, CD123, and CD303; negativity for TCL1; and a Ki-67 proliferation index of less than 10% (Fig. 3). NGS detected the predominant sequences belonging to Mycobacterium intracellulare.

Subsequently, the original anti-NTM treatment regimen was continued. A reexamination via color Doppler ultrasound in May 2025 revealed a smaller bilateral cervical lymph node size (largest right node: 13 × 5 mm; largest left node: 16 × 7 mm) compared with that recorded on 18 November 2024.

Discussion and conclusions

This study is the first to observe CD10+ mature pDC proliferation in the lymph node microenvironment of a patient with chronic Mycobacterium chimaera infection. The patient had been previously diagnosed with a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm, placing her at a high chronic NTM infection risk. A follow-up biopsy performed more than 2 years following the initial presentation revealed mature pDC hyperplasia in the lymph nodes, along with CD10-positive expression, indicating the emergence of a new immunopathological feature during disease progression.

DCs play a dual immunoregulatory role in antimycobacterial immunity. Specifically, they mediate protective responses through antigen presentation and T cell activation centered on the interleukin (IL)-12/interferon (IFN)-γ axis [10]; nonetheless, dysfunction may facilitate immune escape. Recently, the number of circulating DCs, particularly the pDC subset, has been shown to be significantly reduced in active tuberculosis (TB) [11, 12], a finding that correlates with disease severity and may reflect DC migration to infection sites (such as granulomas) or infection-related apoptotic mechanisms [13]. In chronic NTM infection, pDC increases within lymph nodes may be due to several mechanisms. First, sustained antigen stimulation in chronic infection leads to abnormal pDC retention in the lymph nodes, gradually forming a local cell expansion pool [14]. Second, NTM nucleic acids activate TLR9-mediated IFN-α secretion, amplifying chemokine-driven immune recruitment [15]. Third, chronic NTM infection may disrupt the tissue distribution and functional homeostasis of DCs, manifesting as a reduction in circulating DCs, similar to TB, coexisting with an increase in local pDCs in the infected lymph nodes. This seemingly contradictory phenomenon is essentially an adaptive response that controls persistent infections and enhances local immune surveillance. Nevertheless, sustained activation may also lead to pDC functional exhaustion. As observed in this case, pDCs exhibited low Ki-67 proliferation and TCL1 negativity, indicating an activated state without malignant transformation. Notably, pDC-derived IFN-α may suppress IL-12/T helper 1 responses [16], potentially undermining bacterial clearance and perpetuating chronic infection.

pDC proliferation in lymph nodes is a nonspecific finding that can occur in both benign reactive conditions triggered by infections or autoimmune diseases. Importantly, it is a key feature of malignancies, such as BPDCN [17]. Given the dramatically different prognoses and treatment strategies for these entities, accurate discrimination through a comprehensive multidimensional assessment is essential. As summarized in Table 1, from the clinical context and disease course perspective, benign pDC hyperplasia is often associated with identifiable triggers such as chronic infections or autoimmune diseases. Lymph node enlargement typically progresses slowly and may gradually regress with infection control or immune dysregulation correction. Contrastingly, BPDCN is a highly aggressive malignant tumor that mostly occurs in middle-aged and older adult patients, often without clear triggers; it has an acute onset and rapid progression. Besides generalized lymphadenopathy, patients frequently exhibit multisystem involvement such as skin nodules, cytopenia due to bone marrow infiltration, and hepatosplenomegaly. Furthermore, complications, including infections and bleeding, can arise quickly, and median survival is significantly shortened. From A histopathological perspective, benign pDC hyperplasia typically preserves the nodal architecture. Moreover, pDCs are arranged in a scattered or small clustered distribution, exhibit mature cytomorphology, and show no significant atypia. Inflammatory infiltrates composed of lymphocytes and macrophages are common, and necrosis or structural destruction is absent. Conversely, BPDCN is characterized by complete nodal architecture effacement because of diffuse tumor cell infiltration, demonstrating marked cytological atypia. Necrosis and hemorrhage may occur, and the inflammatory background is typically sparse, presenting a striking contrast to benign hyperplasia features. Notably, immunophenotypic features are critical for diagnosis. Benign hyperplastic pDCs express typical mature markers (CD123+, CD4+) but are negative for CD56 and TCL1. The proliferation index Ki-67 is low (typically < 10%), indicating a reactive process. Additionally, in BPDCN, neoplastic pDCs express pDC-specific markers, such as CD123 and CD4, while they aberrantly express CD56 and TCL1, with a high Ki-67 index, reflecting active proliferation. Flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry can further support diagnosis by confirming the absence of T- or B-cell marker expression (for example, CD3 and CD19), thereby verifying pDC-derived malignant proliferation.

Traditionally, pDCs do not express myeloid markers (for example, CD13 and CD33), T/B cell markers (for example, CD3 and CD20), or conventional hematopoietic lineage differentiation antigens. CD10 is primarily expressed in lymphoid progenitor cells and germinal center B cells. It can also be detected in neutrophils, as well as in some T cell lymphomas (for example, AITL) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia [19]. CD10 expression on normal pDCs is exceedingly rare; to date, there has been only one reported case of a patient with T-lymphoblastic leukemia demonstrating concurrent proliferation of mature pDCs with CD10 positivity in the bone marrow [20]. Notably, CD10 expression in pDCs supports, to a certain extent, the idea that lymphocytes and dendritic cells share a common clonal origin [21, 22]. This case highlights the need to clarify the cellular lineage of a CD4+CD10+ cell population detected in lymph nodes. Immunophenotyping, particularly focusing on the cellular source of CD10 expression and lineage-specific marker profiles, is critical for this distinction. Here, pDCs expressed CD10. Flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry confirmed that CD10+ cells co-expressed pDC-specific markers (CD123+, CD4+, CD303+), did not express T cell markers (CD3−, CD5−), and had a low proliferation index Ki-67 (usually < 10%), indicating reactive proliferation. However, in AITL, the main CD10 carriers are neoplastic follicular helper T cells (Tfh). The core neoplastic cell phenotype includes CD3+, CD4+, CD8−, CD10+, CXCR5+, PD-1+ (typical Tfh markers), with a significantly increased Ki-67 index (mostly 20–40%), reflecting malignant proliferative activity. There are clear differences in the lineage affiliation and proliferative activity of CD10⁺ cells between the two conditions, which can be used as the basis for differentiation.

In conclusion, we present the first case of significant proliferation of CD10⁺ mature pDCs in the lymph nodes of a patient with chronic NTM infection. Clinicians and pathologists should be aware of the possibility of pDC proliferation in patients with chronic or refractory NTM infections, particularly when lymphadenopathy is present. Further studies are needed to investigate the clinical significance of significant proliferation of CD10⁺ mature pDCs and to explore its impact on patient prognosis. Ultimately, this case report may contribute to the development of diagnostic strategies for related diseases.
