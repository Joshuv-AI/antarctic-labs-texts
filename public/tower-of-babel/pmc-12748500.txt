Targeted drug conjugates and molecular mechanisms in overcoming lung cancer drug resistance
Authors: Lu Ye, Shuang Xiang, Rou Xue, Yihao Zhang, Yanan Wang, Xinrong Xiong, Chuan Qin, Song Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer, a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, is frequently characterized by late-stage diagnosis and the development of resistance to conventional therapies, including chemotherapy and tyrosine kinase inhibitors. While novel agents like antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged, challenges such as off-target toxicity, limited tumor penetration, and acquired resistance persist, highlighting a critical gap in targeted therapeutic strategies with sustained efficacy and improved safety profiles. This review systematically delineates the molecular mechanisms underpinning drug resistance in lung cancer, focusing on key oncogenic drivers (EGFR, ALK, KRAS, MET) and their evasion pathways. Furthermore, we comprehensively evaluate the evolving landscape of drug conjugates—not only ADCs but also Peptide–drug conjugates (PDCs), radionuclide conjugates, and other novel modalities—as a strategic approach to overcome these resistance mechanisms by enhancing target specificity and drug delivery. Our analysis concludes that drug conjugates represent a paradigm shift in lung cancer treatment; however, their full potential is hampered by linker instability, payload-related toxicity, and complex resistance pathways. We recommend future research focus on the development of next-generation conjugates with optimized linker chemistry, novel payloads with bystander effects, and rational combination therapies to address the dynamic tumor microenvironment and prevent resistance.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), Antibody–drug conjugates, Peptide–drug conjugates, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, driven by its high incidence and historically poor prognosis [1]. It is typically classified into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of cases [2]. NSCLC includes subtypes such as adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma [3]. In contrast, SCLC, which makes up 10–15% of cases, is known for its rapid growth, early metastasis, and aggressive clinical course [4].

For decades, treatment options for lung cancer were limited, with platinum-based chemotherapy as the mainstay [5]. Although chemotherapy provided some survival benefit, it was accompanied by severe side effects and lacked tumor specificity. As a result, the 5-year survival rate for patients with advanced disease remained as low as 15%, highlighting the need for better therapies [6]. The discovery of driver mutations, such as those in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene, and the subsequent approval of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in the early 2000s marked a turning point [7, 8]. This breakthrough improved outcomes for specific patient subsets and spurred research into other oncogenic drivers (ALK, ROS1, KRAS), heralding the era of precision oncology [9–11].

However, challenges persist in lung cancer treatment. The first is resistance—whether to chemotherapy, targeted therapies, or immunotherapy. Resistance mechanisms are complex, involving secondary mutations, activation of bypass pathways, and histological transformation [12–14]. The second challenge is the lack of tumor selectivity in many therapies, leading to off-target toxicities that reduce efficacy and impact quality of life [15].

To address these challenges, novel therapeutic strategies are essential. Drug conjugates have emerged as a promising approach, combining a specific targeting molecule (an antibody or peptide) with a potent cytotoxic agent via a stable linker [16]. This design allows for selective delivery of the drug to tumor cells, maximizing efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

This review aims to provide an in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of drug resistance in lung cancer and explore the potential of drug conjugates as a transformative therapeutic approach. We will examine the current landscape of drug conjugates, their design, mechanisms of action, and clinical applications in both NSCLC and SCLC. Additionally, we will discuss ongoing challenges, such as linker stability and new resistance pathways, and propose directions for future research. By consolidating existing knowledge, we aim to inspire the development of next-generation therapies that can overcome resistance and improve survival outcomes for lung cancer patients.

The epidermal growth factor receptor

The EGFR is a membrane-spanning receptor found in an inactive state and is a regulator of cellular division, growth and survival. The tyrosine kinase dependant the epidermal growth factor receptor, gets activated after binding with the epidermal growth factor and other growth factors. Activation of epidermal growth factor receptors results in activation of signalling cascade viz., Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase pathway and Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase. This results in the synthesis of DNA and the proliferation of cells. Activation of epidermal growth factor receptors induces multiple events and pathways that aid in immune escape in lung cancer as well as halt the process of autophagy [24, 25]. Mutation of the EGFRresults in inherent activation of the receptor which promotes cellular proliferation leading to tumourogenesis [26]. The epidermal growth factor receptor-sensitizing mutations usually occur in the exon region 18–21 [27]. The EGFR plays a significant role in different types of cancer and its mutation is implicated in lung cancer, in ~ 50% of Asian subjects and ~ 10% of Western subjects. Overexpression of EGFR is found in NSCLC. In NSCLC, the mutation in the tyrosine kinase domain of the EGFR occurs (frameshift deletion mutation in the exon region and point mutation for leucine-to-arginine in exon 21). Almost 85% of frame deletion mutations occur in exon-19 or L858R [28–31]. Inhibitors of EGFR are being widely explored for the treatment of NSCLC. The tyrosine kinase inhibitors hinders the mutated EGFR and its persistent activation. Some of the inhibitors of epidermal growth factor receptors viz., afatinib, erlotinib and gefitinib, have been approved by the FDA for NSCLC [32]. Generally, epidermal growth factor receptors target therapy includes monoclonal antibodies and small molecule inhibitors. Gefitinib, a small molecule inhibitor of epidermal growth factor receptor-specific tyrosine kinase, was first evaluated in NSCLC with overexpressed EGFR [33]. Other small molecule inhibitors include another agent, Erlotinib, a reversible inhibitor, which has been found to be effective in cases of NSCLC exhibiting failure of first and second-line agents [34]. The findings of clinical evaluation have led to the inclusion of Erlotinib and Gefitinib in the first-line chemotherapeutic agents for NSCLC having mutations L858R/ E19del [35–37]. Next-generation inhibitor of epidermal growth factor receptors include irreversible inhibitors viz., dacomitinib and afatinib. Both agents have been found to possess a wide-range anti-cancer activity and are approved for usage in NSCLC as first-line modality [38, 39]. However, T790M mutation in NSCLC subjects results in the development of drug resistance to erlotinib and gefitinib [40]. A highly selective third-generation inhibitor of epidermal growth factor receptor, Osimertinib, has been found to be effective with reduced side effects in patients having T790M mutation [41, 42]. Thus, Osimertinib is a preferred therapeutic modality as compared to first- or second-line drugs in NSCLC subjects with T790M mutation [42, 43]. Another, EGFR inhibitor, almonertinib, has been found to be effective in cases of NSCLC with T790M mutations [44, 45].

Cetuximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody that has been found to be clinically effective in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy in phase-II trials [46–51]. Further, in the phase III trial (BMS-099), patients (n = 676) were randomly administered carboplatin and a taxane, with or without Cetuximab. The results, however, didn’t show significant improvement in patient survival or overall survival, although there is a statistically significant improvement in relative risk (25.7% as against 17.2%) [52]. In the FLEX clinical trial, combination therapy of cisplatin and vinorelbine, with or without cetuximab is randomly administered to 1125 patients. The findings of this clinical trial exhibit improvement in median overall survival in subjects who were administered cetuximab (11.3 months as against 10.1 months). This effect is specifically beneficial in patients with lung cancer with squamous cells [53]. However, cetuximab exhibits dermatological toxicity and higher rates of febrile neutropenia in both trials. Other monoclonal antibodies that block the EGFR are nimotuzumab and necitumumab. A combination of nimotuzumab and chemotherapeutic agents gemcitabine and cisplatin was compared in phase III clinical trial (SQUIRE) with chemotherapeutic agent alone, and results show that chemotherapeutic agents in combination with nimotuzumab significantly improved the overall survival of the patients having metastatic stage IV squamous cell cancer [17].

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase receptor

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene present on chromosome 2 encodes the receptor tyrosine kinase and its mutation is often associated with lung cancer. The central nervous system and peripheral nervous system express anaplastic lymphoma kinase receptors [54, 55]. The binding of ligands viz., midkine and pleiotrophin with the anaplastic lymphoma kinase receptors results in its dimerization followed by its activation. The activation of anaplastic lymphoma kinase receptors is associated with different signalling cascades viz., JAK-STAT, RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR, that regulate cellular propagation and its viability [56, 57]. Magnification, rearrangement and mutation in anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene have been attributed to induce lung cancer [57–59]. Almost 5% of the cases of NSCLC have been found to be associated with the rearrangement in the anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene [59]. In NSCLC, rearrangement in the anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene results in its fusion with gene echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 4 (EML4). As a result of this fusion of genes anomalous expression and activation of anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene occurs [60]. This fusion gene, EML4- ALK gene results in the overexpression of activated anaplastic lymphoma kinase-tyrosine kinase receptors in the lungs, which are normally not present in the lungs [61]. Typically, this mutation in anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene is observed in young, non-smoker subjects [62]. Crizotinib is an anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitor which was first approved by FDA for the therapeutic management of ALK-positive NSCLC [63]. The efficacy of Crizotinib has been reported in patients with advanced NSCLC in a phase III clinical trial [64]. LDK378 (or Ceritinib) an anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitor, was granted ‘break through therapy’ status by FDA in 2013 based on the positive findings (~ 80% response) of phase I clinical trials in patients of NSCLC. Based on further clinical trials Ceritinib is considered to be an alternative to Crizotinib [65]. Other inhibitors that hav been found to effective in cases of Crizotinib resistance includes, brigatinib and alectinib [66, 67].

Further, studies in the mouse model have shown hampering HSP90 (a molecular chaperone), can result in the dismantling of the fusion gene, EML4- ALK, thus resulting in a reduction in tumour growth [68]. These findings indicate the potential of HSP90 inhibitors as a potential therapeutic approach for the management of lung cancer. Ganetispib is one of the potential HSP90 inhibitors. In vitro study findings indicate that Ganetispib has better efficacy as compared to crizotinib [69]. Clinical evaluation of Ganetispib, alone or in combination with crizotinib in patients with ALK-positive NSCLC has reported positive outcomes, especially in patients that have been on therapy [70]. Further, Ganetispib has been found to be effective in cases that exhibit resistance to crizotinib [69]. Additionally, another HSP90 inhibitor, AUY-922, is being clinically evaluated in Phase II clinical trials based upon positive findings in Phase I clinical trials carried out in pretreated ALK-positive NSCLC patients [71]. Cases of resistance to crizotinib are highly prevalent, although varied mechanisms of resistance are reported. Some of the mechanisms of crizotinib resistance include, subsidiary mutations in anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene, increase in copy number in anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene, elevated levels of serum amphiregulin and L858R mutation in the EGFR gene [72]. Study findings have shown subsidiary mutations in the anaplastic lymphoma kinase gene, EGFR gene and kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene, to be cause for resistance to crizotinib [73]. Lorlatinib is the third-generation anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitor which is especially effective in ALK-positive NSCLC subjects having metastasis to the central nervous system and also exhibiting subsidiary mutations causing resistance to other inhibitors [74]. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitors, alectinib, lorlatinib and brigatinib are the first-line agents used in ALK-positive NSCLC [75]. However, further findings reported L1198F mutations to induce resistance to lorlatinib. Interestingly, resistance to lorlatinib induces resensitization of cancer cells to crizotinib treatment [76].

B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (B-RAF)

B-RAF encodes Rapidly Accelerated Fibrosarcoma kinase and is part of serine / threonine protein kinases group. Rapidly Accelerated Fibrosarcoma kinase mediates the downstream signalling of RAS cascade which results in the induction of Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase pathway. Activation of Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase pathway results in unrestricted cellular proliferation and subsequent mutations [77]. Although mutations in B-RAF is commonly associated with melanoma, however, 2–3% cases of NSCLC have been linked to B-RAF mutations. In NSCLC, majorly mutation V600E in B-RAF has been reported, although, some cases of G469A, K601N, L597Q and D594G mutations have also been reported [78, 79]. B-RAF mutations are distinctly more prevalent in smokers [78]. A B-RAF inhibitor, dabrafenib, have been found to be effective in phase II clinical trials in NSCLC patients having V600E mutations [80]. Although subsequent studies have reported induction of resistance to dabrafenib after eight months of therapy. Mutation G12D mutation in kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene is suggested to be the cause of resistance towards dabrafenib [81]. Vemurafenib, a B-RAF inhibitor effective in melanoma, was found to be ineffective in NSCLC. However, a combination of vemurafenib and trametinib has shown promising results [82, 83].

ROS proto-oncogene 1 or ROS-1

In NSCLC rearrangement in ROS-1 gene is observed in ~ 2% which also results in the formation of nine fusion proteins [84]. In NSCLC patients with ROS-1 rearrangement, entrectinib and crizotinib have been granted by the FDA for therapeutic usage [85, 86]. Another selective ROS-1 gene inhibitor, Taletrectinib has been found to be efficacious in multicenter, randomised, open-label trial, with low incidence of adverse effects in patients with ROS-1 rearrangement NSCLC that exhibit resistance to crizotinib. Based on these encouraging results, further phase II, multicentre trials were undertaken globally in patients with ROS-1 rearrangement NSCLC as well as in patients having either refractory/naiive tyrosine kinase inhibitor sensitivity. Repotrectinib, is another potentially active ROS-1 gene inhibitor which is being evaluated for its efficacy [87].

Mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET)

MET proto-oncogene encodes tyrosine kinase receptor protein (also referred to as hepatocyte growth factor receptor- HGFR), which is overexpressed, magnified and enhances mutations in certain types of lung cancer. Induction of MET gene results in phosphorylation of remnants of tyrosine, which in turn induces several signalling cascades viz., SRC kinase, Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (RAS/MAPK) and PI3K. For instance, ~ 7% of cases of Lung Adenocarcinoma exhibit MET-enhanced mutations and ~ 4% of cases of lung adenocarcinomas and ~ 1% cases of Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma, exhibit MET-magnification [88, 89]. MET exon 14 skipping mutations also play a key role in metastasis in lung adenocarcinomas and neoplasmas [90]. Capmatinib, crizotinib, tepotinib, cabozantinib and glesatinib are some of the tyrosine kinase inhibitors that were found to be clinically effective in MET exon 14 skipping NSCLC. A highly selective inhibitor, Capmatinib has been granted approval by FDA for its efficacy in targeting MET exon 14 skipping NSCLC, overexpression and magnification of MET [91]. Another selective inhibitor being clinically evaluated and granted approval in few countries like Japan and The United States of America, is Tepotinib [92].

Neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene

Gene fusion of NTRK is observed in non-smoking cases of lung adenocarcinomas or in NSCLC (~ 0.5% cases). Interestingly, NTRK mutations are more prevalent in women subjects. Tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Larotrectinib, has been found to be effective in cases of NTRK gene fusion and was granted express approval for its usage in NTRK gene fusion NSCLC cases [93]. Entrectinib, is another tyrosine kinase inhibitor approved for NTRK gene fusion NSCLC cases [20].

Anexelekto (AXL) gene

AXL gene is a member of Transient Abnormal Myelopoiesis (TAM) tyrosine kinase receptor which contributes to the development of resistance to therapy. Study findings show that AXL gene exhibits an augmented expression and induction of erlotinib-resistant cell lines of NSCLC. Further, it was found that blocking AXL results in restoring the sensitivity to erlotinib in NSCLC cell lines, thus indicating the potential usage of AXL inhibitors as a therapeutic tool to overcome drug resistance [94]. One of the selective AXL inhibitors, BGB324, is being evaluated in phase II clinical trial for its efficacy in cases of NSCLC exhibiting resistance to anti-PD1 therapy [95]. Another AXL inhibitor candidate, Dubermatinib is also being evaluated for its efficacy in a randomised, open-label phase Ia/Ib trial [20].

Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) gene

KRAS is a minor regulatory protein Guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) that mediates signal cycling between the bound form of GTP (active) and the bound form of guanosine diphosphate (GDP)- inactive form. This cyclic form of GTP and GDP regulates the signalling cascade of cell growth and persistence. The mutated form of KRAS gene is the most prevalent in many cancers including lung cancer [96].In almost 25% of NSCLC cases, KRAS mutations are observed and also in 30% of cases of lung adenocarcinoma wherein codon substitutions Q61, G12 and G13 is seen [89, 96]. Mutation in KRAS gene is more prevalent in smokers (~ 26%) as compared to non-smokers (~ 6%) [97]. Historically, KRAS inhibitors have been found to be ineffective and have been labelled as ‘undruggable target’. However, certain KRAS G12C inhibitors, sotorasib, MRTX849, ARS1620, ARS853, GDC-6036, 1_AM, BI 1,701,963 are some of the efficacious candidates. Of them, the most potent and selective inhibitors of KRAS G12C are sotorasib and MRTX849. These two inhibitors have also been proven to be efficacious in xenograft tumor model in mice [98, 99]. In a phase I, open-label, multi-centre clinical trial, Sotorasib was evaluated in 59 subjects of NSCLC and study findings indicate encouraging positive results (~ 32.2%) [100]. Sotorasib is being further evaluated for its efficacy and mechanism of action.

Therapeutic management of lung cancer—current status and need for drug conjugates

Lung cancer is a highly fatal form of cancer. Therapeutic management of lung cancer includes chemotherapy, surgical intervention to extract the tumorigenic part and radiotherapy to treat the tumours locally [101–103]. However, with the induction of metastasis, therapeutic management of the cancer becomes difficult. Often patients exhibit relapse of tumors. Chemotherapy is a critical part of the therapeutic management of cancer, however, it often exhibits a lack of selectivity as a result of which normal tissue cells undergo cytotoxicity along with the tumour cells [104]. Consequently, immune cell disruption along with the development of drug resistance is often seen during chemotherapy [105, 106]. Also, chemotherapeutic drugs are known to have poor tolerability and a narrow therapeutic index and hence induce severe adverse effects [107]. Immunotherapy is also used for the management of lung cancer. Immunotherapy works on the principle of activating the immune system to identify and act against the tumour cells. However, immunotherapy exhibits inter-individual variability and induces autoimmune adverse effects [108–110].

Targeted systems help to reduce the adverse effects and improve tolerability [111]. Selectivity of the drug candidate and its delivery system to the target helps to achieve targeted delivery to the tumour cells. Selective inhibitors of tyrosine kinase found in epithelial growth factor receptors have revolutionized the therapeutic management of NSCLC [107]. These tyrosine kinase inhibitors of epithelial growth factor receptors effectively target tumour cells along with reduced adverse effects due to selective targeting of tumour cells [112–114]. However, after an initial efficacious response, resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors develops post-treatment (almost after 9–12 months) inescapably in tumour cells [43, 115–120]. Thus, developing a selective drug candidate and a targeted drug/drug delivery system seems to be most appropriate approach, to effectively target tumour cells with reduced adverse events. Drug conjugates are formed when a cytotoxic drug is linked to a targeting ligand via a linker moiety. Targeting ligand help to specifically direct the drug to reach the target site. A daigrammatic representation of drug conjugates is shown in Fig. 1. Classical example of drug conjugate includes antibody–drug conjugate wherein a monoclonal antibody is linked to a targeting moiety with the help of a linker moiety. This construction of the antibody–drug conjugate helps to target cytotoxic antibodies to the lung cancer cells and simultaneously reduces the adverse effects [121]. Monoclonal antibodies are highly cytotoxic agents, however being a macromolecular agent, its ability to permeate and reach the tumor site is difficult. Also, these monoclonal antibodies can induce immunogenic responses. Hence, linking antibodies with target sites aids in permeation and targeting it to tumour cells. Thus, when the antibody–drug conjugate enters the blood, the targeting moiety helps the conjugate to reach the tumour site. At the tumour site, the drug conjugate releases the antibody, which is endocytosed in the lung cancer cell and eventually exerts the cytotoxic effect [122–124].

Antibody–drug conjugates

Antibody–drug conjugates are macromolecular anticancer drugs that are designed to possess target selectivity as well as cytotoxicity towards tumour cells [128]. The three basic components of antibody–drug conjugate includes shown in Fig. 2 [129].

These antibody–drug conjugates on entering circulation the drug conjugates form a complex with the targeting antigen and are endocytosed to internalise the antibody–drug conjugates in the tumour cell. Eventually, the linker in the internalised antibody–drug conjugate complex is cleaved to release the cytotoxic drug to exert its cytotoxic effect on the tumour cells [130–135]. The first-generation antibody–drug conjugates had mouse-derived antibodies (e.g., CD33-targeting Mylotarg), as a result, these antibody–drug conjugates were susceptible to inducing immunogenic responses in the patients. Also, the linker moieties were not stout and would prematurely cleave to release the cytotoxic drug, thus reducing the efficacy as well as inducing adverse events [136, 137]. Consequently, second-generation antibody–drug conjugates (for e.g., HER2-specific, Kadcyla and CD30-specific, Adcetris) were prepared with human-mouse chimeric antibodies as well as humanised monoclonal antibodies. However, these antibody–drug conjugates have a non-uniform drug-to-antibody ratio which resulted in higher clearance, higher incidence of non-specific adverse effects, aggregation of antibodies and reduced therapeutic efficacy [138–143]. Fixed-point conjugation technology was utilised in developing third-generation antibody–drug conjugate (for e.g., HER2-specific, Enhertu) which resulted in a more stable drug conjugate having an improved pharmacokinetic profile and drug-to-antibody ratio. Also, hydrophilic linkers like, pegylation are being widely employed in third-generation antibody–drug conjugates [144–149].

While designing the antibody–drug conjugate, the targeting antibody is selected in such a way that the antigen should either be uniquely expressed only by the cancer cells or expressed by normal cells in extremely low levels. Also, preferably the target antigen should be expressed on the surface of the tumour cell. This helps to impart selectivity in the antibody–drug conjugate. Some of the widely explored surface antigens that are overexpressed by the tumour cells are- Human epidermal growth factor receptors 2 and 3 (HER2 and HER3), prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) and mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-MET) [150]. Another pertinent requirement of antibodies is that antibody should be efficiently endocytosed by the tumour cell and the expression of antigen should not be attenuated after some time of treatment with antibody–drug conjugate. Dimension of a tumour, cellular anatomy of tumoral cells, the vascular supply to the tumour site and the extent of fibrosis, determine the uptake of antibody–drug conjugate at the tumour site [151, 152]. The antibody–drug conjugates are designed not only to target the tumor cell but also the components of the tumour microenvironment viz., vascular supply to the tumour, stem cells that instigate the tumour cell propagation and stromal fibroblasts [153–155].

Another critical component of antibody–drug conjugates is the payload. Characteristically, the payload must be potent, cytotoxic, stable, capable of conjugating with the linker moiety and have suitable solubility. Payload can exert cytotoxic effects via, transcriptional inhibition, antimitotic or tubulin filament damage and DNA-damaging [156].

Antimitotic agents alter the stable structure of tubulin filament thus inducing mitotic seizure, for e.g., derivatives of taxol, vinblastine, auristatins, maytansinoid mertanzine which is a component of FDA-approved antibody–drug conjugate, ado-trastuzumab emtansine [157–159].

Antibody–drug conjugate targeting human EGFR 2/3

Human epidermal growth factor receptors are the tyrosine kinase and four kinds of receptors exist viz., Human epidermal growth factor receptor-1, 2, 3, 4. Of these, human EGFR 2 is overexpressed or undergoes frequent mutation in lung cancer, which results in the induction of a signalling cascade involved in cellular proliferation viz., MAPK, AKT, PKC and PI3K [160–162]. Thus, targeting human EGFR 2 with antibody–drug conjugate is a promising target in lung cancer.

Different antibody–drug conjugates have been developed to target human EGFR 2. T‑DM1 is one of the antibody–drug conjugates wherein the cytotoxic agent, emtansine (DM1) is linked to a monoclonal antibody, trastuzumab (T), with a sulphur linkage. The payload to antibody ratio is kept at ~ 3.5 [163, 164]. T‑DM1 is therapeutically used for the management of human EGFR 2 positive NSCLC [165]. Phase II clinical evaluation of T-DM1 in human EGFR 2 positive NSCLC patients (n = 18) exhibited limited response showing median progression-free survival of 5 months in 50% of patients [166]. Similar results were observed in another phase II clinical trial (n = 49), wherein 51% of the human EGFR 2 positive NSCLC patients exhibited showing median progression-free survival of 5 months [167]. These results prompted the categorizing T-DM1 as class 2 A agent for the human EGFR 2 positive NSCLC patients [168, 169]. In another phase I clinical study in human EGFR 2 positive NSCLC patients (n = 49), T-DM1 showed an overall response rate was found to be 20% with showing median progression-free survival of 2.7 months [169]. Similarly, T-DM1 also exhibited a significantly improved clinical response in patients having human EGFR 2 exon 20 insertion mutation [170]. However, nausea, thrombocytopenia and elevation in transaminase level were observed with the therapy of T-DM1 [166].

Another human EGFR 2 targeting antibody–drug conjugate is trastuzumab deruxtecan or T-DXd or DS-8201, wherein trastuzumab is linked to deruxtecan (a topoisomerase I inhibitor), with a payload to antibody ratio of 8 [171–173]. T-DXd has a suitable membrane permeability and efficiently terminates the tumor cells. In a global, open-label, clinical trial (DESTINY-Lung01) evaluation of T-DXd in NSCLC patients (n = 91), an overall response rate was found to be 55%, median progression-free survival of 8.2 months, median overall survival of 17.8 months and disease control rate of 92%, was observed [174]. Further evaluation of T-DXd in human EGFR 2 NSCLC patients (n = 49), an overall response rate of 24.5%, disease control rate of 69% and median progression-free survival of 5.4 months. The antibody–drug conjugate, T-DXd is further being evaluated in combination with durvalumab [175].

Another human EGFR 2 targeting antibody–drug conjugate is A166 wherein trastuzumab is present. Clinical evaluation of A166 in patients having solid tumours (n = 81) showed an overall response rate of 60%. However, side effects like, reduced appetite and ocular side effects like, dry eye, blurred vision and keratitis, were observed [176]. Another human EGFR 3 targeting antibody–drug conjugate is U3-1402 i.e., patritumab deruxtecan. Human EGFR 3 is amplified in NSCLC (~ 19%) and adenocarcinomas (~ 46%). Human EGFR 3 is also associated with the development of resistance to inhibitors of tyrosine kinase [177–179]. In a phase I study, the evaluation of U3-1402 monotherapy in human EGFR 3 positive NSCLC patients exhibited an overall response rate of 39% and a median progression-free survival of 8.2 months [180]. Further analysis of the subgroup of the clinical trial showed that U3-1402 is effective in patients having resistant mutations [180].

Antibody–drug conjugate targeting trophoblast cell surface antigen 2

Trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 is a membrane-spanning protein which is associated with cellular multiplication and differentiation. Trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 is either not present or at a low level in normal cells and is upregulated in epithelial lung cancer [181]. Datopotamab deruxtecan or DS-1062a is the antibody–drug conjugate that targets trophoblast cell surface antigen 2. Evaluation of DS-1062a in NSCLC patients exhibits an overall response rate of 31% and disease control rate of 79% at a dose of 8.0 mg/kg [182].

Another the antibody–drug conjugate that targets trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 is sacituzumab govitecan or IMMU-132. Phase I evaluation of IMMU-132 in patients having refractory metastatic solid lung exhibited positive results which prompted further phase II evaluation in solid tumor patients (n = 495). Of these, an overall response rate of 16.7%, median overall survival of 7.3 months and a median progression-free survival of 4.4 months was observed in NSCLC patients. IMMU-132 is further being evaluated clinically in combination with atezolizumab for NSCLC and berzosertib for ScLc [183, 184].

Another antibody–drug conjugate that targets trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 is Dato-DXd, which is being evaluated in a global clinical trial, TROPION-Lung02, wherein the combination of pembrolizumab and platinum chemotherapy with Dato-DXd was administered to metastatic NSCLC patients. The overall response rate was found to be 62% (dual therapy) and 50% (tri-therapy). While the disease control rate was found to be 100% (dual therapy) and 90% (tri-therapy) [185].

Antibody–drug conjugate targeting mesenchymal-epithelial transition

The mesenchymal-epithelial transition tyrosin kinase receptor is characteristically upregulated in NSCLC patients. The mesenchymal-epithelial transition tyrosine kinase receptor is associated with cellular differentiation, multiplication and vasculogenesis. Gene mesenchymal-epithelial transition fusion and upregulation is associated with resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors in NSCLC patients [186–189].

Teliso‑V (Telisotuzumab Vedotin), is the antibody–drug conjugate that targets mesenchymal-epithelial transition tyrosin kinase receptor. Clinical evaluation of Teliso-V, alone or in combination with erlotinib, established the efficacy of Teliso-V in patients of mesenchymal-epithelial transition-positive NSCLC. Teliso-V was found to be effective even as monotherapy in mesenchymal-epithelial transition-positive NSCLC. However, phase II clinical evaluation was terminated due to the occurrence of severe adverse events and low efficacy [190, 191]. Another antibody–drug conjugate that targets mesenchymal-epithelial transition tyrosin kinase receptor is ABBV-399 exhibited an overall response rate of 18.8%, median progression-free survival of 5.7 months and duration of response of 4.8 months, in mesenchymal-epithelial transition-positive NSCLC patients. However, the phase II trials were terminated for this drug conjugate [190, 191]. Though, ABBV-399 was found to be efficacious in nonsquamous NSCLC in phase II trial [191, 192].

Antibody–drug conjugate targeting delta-like protein 3

Delta-like protein 3 is a ligand which is expressed in ScLc (~ 75–85%) and normal tissues rarely express them. Delta-like protein 3 hinder the Notch signalling cascade which is involved in cellular development and proliferation [193–195]. Rova-T i.e., rovalpituzumab tesirine, is the antibody–drug conjugate that targets Delta-like protein 3, was found to be efficacious in ScLc patients with an overall response rate of 18%, median overall survival of 4.6 months and median progression-free survival of 3.1 months [196, 197]. In phase II clinical trial, TRINITY study, carried out in 339 patients with Delta-like protein 3 as a third line of treatment and exhibited an overall response rate of 12.4%, median overall survival of 5.6 months and median progression-free survival of 3.5 months [198].

Antibody–drug conjugate targeting carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5

Carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5 also known as CD66e, is a glycoprotein which is overexpressed in lung cancer, especially in NSCLC. SAR408701 is the antibody–drug conjugate that targets carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5, was evaluated in patients of advanced NSCLC and exhibited an overall response rate of 7.1% in patients having relatively lower expression of carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5, while the patients having overregulated expression of carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5 exhibited an overall response rate of 20.3% with a average duration of response of 5.6 months [149].

Peptide–drug conjugates

Most of the third-generation antibody–drug conjugates are indifferent and tolerant to microtubule protein inhibitors. Also, third-generation antibody–drug conjugates are tough to reproduce [199]. On the other hand, peptide–drug conjugates can easily be produced and purified with a cost-effective procedure [200, 201]. Also, peptides used as an analogue to the ligand have a strong affinity to the target as well as the ability to conjugate with the drugs. Thus, conjugating peptides with chemotherapeutic drugs resulted in efficacious and targeted peptide–drug conjugates [202]. A diagrammatic representation of peptide drug conjugates is shown in Fig. 3. Also, the ease of structural modification in peptides enables the designing of peptide–drug conjugates with increased bioavailability, stability and affinity to target. Peptides have low molecular weight as compared to antibody and thus have better tumour cell permeability. Peptides have a simple structure and low immunogenicity and thus reduced probability of inducing immunogenic response. Peptides have lower toxicity as they are eliminated by the kidney [203]. Due to these advantages of peptide–drug conjugates over conventional chemotherapeutic agents as well as antibody–drug conjugates, peptide–drug conjugates are being widely explored as a therapeutic modality for various forms of cancer including lung cancer. A recombinant fusion protein of CNGRCG with human tumour necrosis factor- α, referred to as NGR-hTNF, has been extensively evaluated in phase I /II clinical for its efficacy in different types of cancer and has been found to be effective in NSCLC, colon cancer, ovarian cancer, metastatic sarcomas to name a few [204]. However, peptide–drug conjugates have low drug stability and undergo swift metabolism [205].

Diagrammatic representation of peptide drug conjugate with possible target receptors and some of commonly used polymers used in the peptide drug conjugate

Solid-phase peptide synthesis is the most commonly adopted method to synthesise the tumor-targeting peptide–drug conjugate. The payload in the peptide–drug conjugate is the potent cytotoxic drug which is linked to the tumor-targeting peptide with the help of a linker moiety. The linker moiety has at least two functional groups through which it is conjugated to the drug at one end and with a tumor-targeting peptide at the other end. The target specificity of the peptide–drug conjugate is imparted by the tumour specificity of the peptide. The uptake of the peptide–drug conjugate by the tumour cell is also dependent on the molecular targeting ability of the peptide [206–211]. Aminopeptidase N (APN)-CD13 of mammalian origin is a cellular surface ectoenzyme which is upregulated in lung cancer [212]. The peptide NGR having a structural composition of Asn-Gly-Arg, is the tumor-targeting peptide which is overexpressed during vasculogenesis. The NGR peptide has an affinity for the Aminopeptidase N (APN)-CD13 expressed by the vascular cells. Two peptide–drug conjugates have been developed wherein NGR is conjugated viz., NGR–human tumour necrosis factor and NGR- truncated tissue factor. Both of these peptide–drug conjugates are being clinically evaluated. In NGR- truncated tissue factor peptide conjugate, tumour targeting peptide (Gly-Asn-Gly-Arg-Ala-His-Ala) is conjugated to the C-terminus of truncated tissue factor. The peptide drug conjugate, NGR- truncated tissue factor, exhibits an effective reduction in tumours along with low incidence of adverse events [213].

Integrins are the cellular adhesion receptors that regulate the movement and invasion of the tumor cells. These receptors also control the tumour cell survival and growth, especially during their fleeing from the vascular infiltration. Structurally, integrins are composed of two subunits viz., α and β [214]. Of these, the α chain has 4 extracellular domains through which it can bind with the peptides having Arg-Gly-Asp composition [215]. Accordingly, the integrins namely ανβ3/5, α5β1 and ανβ6 are involved in the tumour cell development and its spread. The integrins ανβ3/5 are upregulated in the cancer cells while normal cells do not express them and the peptide, Arg-Gly-Asp, has high affinity for them. This forms the basis of developing peptide drug conjugates that target integrins expressed at the site of vasculogenesis with the peptide Arg-Gly-Asp, for e.g., 68Ga-NOTA-bombesin (BBN)–RGD, [18F]RGD-K5 [216].

Somatostatin receptors, five types, are found in different parts of the body like the pituitary, pancreas and brain. Naturally occurring somatostatins have a very short half-life. The analogue of somatostatin is octreotide, having a longer half-life, has an affinity for somatostatin receptors 1, 2,3, 4 and 5. Of these, the highest affinity of octreotide is for somatostatin receptor 2 and the lowest for somatostatin receptor 4. Octreotide has been used for developing peptide drug conjugates (like, 68Ga-DOTATOC, 177Lu-DOTATATE, 68Ga-DOTATATE, 111In-DTPA-octreotide etc.), which are used for targeting tumours for imaging and diagnostic purposes [217–219].

Membrane type-1 matrix metalloproteinase is upregulated in lung cancer and is often considered a prognostic biomarker for lung cancer. A low molecular weight, membrane type-1 matrix metalloproteinase peptide drug conjugate, BT1718, that can easily infiltrate and kill tumour cell, has been found to be effective in solid tumours [220].

Although peptide drug conjugates have higher target specificity, are easily taken up by tumour cells and effectively kill tumour cells, still certain shortcomings are associated with peptide drug conjugates. Most of the peptide drug conjugates are administered by the parenteral route and thus, are associated with patient inconvenience and non-compliance. Peptide drug conjugates have a short biological life and thus have a short therapeutic window to act specifically on the tumour cells. To overcome these certain strategies like, disulfide bond cyclization, peptidomimetics, head-to-tail cyclization, substitution with non-natural amino acids and bicyclic peptides have been explored [182, 221–223]. Also, the catalysis of the linkage to release the payload from peptide drug conjugate is often dependent on the pH, activity of enzymes and redox condition, which can affect the efficient release of payload from peptide drug conjugate [211, 224, 225].

Radionuclide drug conjugates

In Radionuclide drug conjugates, cytotoxic agents in the antibody–drug conjugates are swapped with radionuclides via nucleotides [226]. Thus, radionuclide drug conjugates are emerging as a precision targeting system wherein a radionuclide can be delivered directly to the tumour site for brachytherapy [227]. Some of the radionuclides used for radionuclide drug conjugates are, β-emitting radionuclides (131I, 188Re, 90Y, 177Lu) and α-emitting radionuclides (211At, 213Bi). However, toxicity in non-target organs and cells, along with challenges in the efficient delivery of radionuclide drug conjugates, limits their application, particularly in solid tumors. Also, optimizing the selection of radionuclides with their therapeutic efficacy and limiting the non-specific tissue toxicity is difficult to achieve. The radionuclide drug conjugates clinically evaluated for their efficacy in lung cancer can be categorized into two groups: imaging and therapeutic agents. Imaging agents include Cu-64 DOTATATE, 99mTc-EDDA, 68Ga-DOTATOC, and 68Ga-PSMA-11, while therapeutic agents include ITM-41 and Lu177dotatate [230–233].

Small‑molecule–drug conjugates

Similar to antibody–drug conjugates in design, in the small molecule-drug conjugate, the antibody is replaced by a small molecule which functions as a targeting ligand [234, 235]. Small molecule-drug conjugates offer several advantages over antibody–drug conjugates, including non-immunogenicity, ease of synthesis and reproducibility, simpler storage, transportation, and administration. Also, the small molecule-drug conjugates have low molecular weight which increases their tumoural permeability and improves their in-vitro/in-vivo stability [236–240]. Small molecule-drug conjugates are efficiently eliminated by the kidneys via glomerular filtration, which helps maintain a low toxicity profile. However, this also reduces the time available for effectively targeting tumor cells [241]. The potent targeting ability and affinity of the targeting ligand not only help in the efficient targeting of the tumour cells but also aid in reducing systemic adverse events [235, 242]. The targeting moieties include either a ligand or inhibitors of the cell-surface receptor or enzymes of the signalling cascade for e.g., low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1, imatinib (inhibitor or fusion protein), somatostatin receptor, heat shock protein 90 inhibitors, aminopeptidase N and prostate-specific membrane antigen [243–250]. Similar to antibody–drug conjugates, the linkers in small molecule-drug conjugates affect the release of drug, pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of the conjugate [244]. The linkers in first-generation small molecule-drug conjugates include carbohydrate units while second-generation small molecule-drug conjugates have epimers viz., glutamine and glutamic acid. Also, linkers having disulphide bonds are utilised as linker moiety [235]. Small molecule-drug conjugates, referred as PEN-866, having an inhibitor of heat shock protein 90 and is conjugated via carbamate link to cytotoxic agent SN-38 (a metabolite of irinotecan). Heat shock protein 90 is upregulated in tumour cells [251–253]. PEN-866 has been compared with first line and second line of treatment in ScLc cell lines and was found to have superior efficacy. Based on these results, PEN-866 is being evaluated in phase I/II clinical trials [253]. Small molecule-drug conjugates of folic acid have been evaluated in different forms of cancer including lung cancer for e.g., SN-38. Folate conjugate of vinca alkaloid like, EC 145 (vintafolide) was evaluated for its efficacy in patients of NSCLC in as monotherapy as well as in combination with docetaxel. The results showed that combination therapy resulted in Progression-Free Survival for 7.1 months along with overall survival of 10.9 months. These encouraging results have prompted evaluation of other vinca alkaloid-folic acid-based small molecule drug conjugates like, EC0489, EC0492 in lung cancer patients [254–256]. A dual-drug small molecule drug conjugate of folic acid and vinca alkaloid, SMDC EC0225, consists of a combination of desacetylvinblastine monohydrazide and mitomycin C, attached to a folic acid unit. SMDC EC0225 has been found to result in significant improvement in the treatment outcome of NSCLC [257].

Other approaches to macromolecules in lung cancer

Certain novel approaches are being evaluated for their efficacy in different forms of cancer and may have potential applications in lung cancer later. Therapeutic oligonucleotides like, siRNA, phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer etc. can be delivered to specific tissues/cells using antibodies. Antibody–oligonucleotide conjugates approach also helps to overcome the shortcomings of delivering oligonucleotides [258]. Conjugating oligonucleotides with antibodies help to expand the span of application of oligonucleotides. This approach has been explored for delivering siRNA using the conjugate, AOC1001, for the treatment of ankylosing myotonic dystrophy type 1, wherein siRNA is conjugated with a monoclonal antibody that targets transferrin receptor 1.

Another approach for delivering macromolecules involves antibody-cell conjugates, where a linker, such as 5’ NHS ester single-strand DNA, is conjugated to the antibody’s amino group on one end and to the cell surface protein of cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells or natural killer (NK) cells on the other. Antibody‒cell conjugates are quite similar to Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell therapy, however, antibody‒cell conjugates involve only chemical modification and not genetic modification. This approach helps to potentiate the antitumor efficacy of Cytokine-induced killer cells/ natural killer cells [259, 260].

A novel approach to delivering macromolecules to tumour cells is virus‑like drug conjugates, wherein the capsid of the virus is manipulated to prepare a non-infectious proteinaceous nanoparticulate delivery system. In one such delivery system (AU-001) for targeting solid tumours, proteinaceous nanoparticulate developed from human papillomavirus is conjugated to modified heparan sulfate proteoglycans [261].

Immune‑stimulating antibody conjugates are a novel approach wherein the immune agonist or regulator is the payload that modifies the immunological response in such a way that cold humour is transformed into a hot tumour [262]. These immune conjugates have the ability to modulate the immune cells in the tumour microenvironment and thus alter the sensitivity of the tumour cells [263]. Agonists of toll-like receptors conjugates (BDC-1001, SBT-6050, SBT-6290), Stimulators of Interferon Genes agonists conjugate (XMT-2056) and the Treg cell regulatory immune-stimulating antibody conjugates (ADCT-301) have been explored in various types of cancers [264–267]. For lung cancer, immune-stimulating antibody conjugates BDC-2034 are being evaluated.

In antibody fragment–drug conjugates, have smaller antibody fragments and thus are smaller in size and hence have good cellular permeation [268, 269]. Also, antibody fragment–drug conjugates have a higher drug-to-antibody ratio which results in higher drug efficiency. The small size of antibody fragment–drug conjugates also aid in its rapid cellular as well as systemic elimination [270].

In aptamer–drug conjugates, aptamer is linked with the drug via a linker moiety. A diagrammatic representation of aptamer-drug conjugates is shown in Fig. 5. The aptamer acts as a target guiding ligand which helps the aptamer–drug conjugate to reach the target tumour site [271]. The aptamers are nucleic acid-based oligonucleotide sequences that have high target specificity and affinity. Aptamers are easy to screen, have low immunogenic response, are easily reproducible at low cost, possess thermal and chemical stability, have higher cellular permeability due to low molecular weight and have negligible immune-related adverse effects [272]. However, aptamers are liable to nucleases and thus have shorter biological life. Also, the absorption of aptamer drug conjugates by non-target tissues can result in an altered pharmacokinetic profile [273].

Challenges in developing macromolecular drug conjugates

The development of drug conjugates has improved the efficacy of anticancer drugs and has widened their application in the management of cancer therapy. However, there are certain challenges associated with drug conjugates that limit their application. The development of resistance to drug conjugates is one of the major limitations which can be induced via multiple mechanisms viz., resistance to the antigen, malfunctioning in the process of endocytosis and apoptosis, enhanced activity of efflux pumps, target site mutation and lysosomal abnormality. Unfortunately, currently, there is a lack of therapeutic modalities to manage resistance to drug conjugates [156, 274–282]. The effectiveness of drug conjugates depends on the amount of the payload or the drug-to-antibody ratio. A high drug-to-antibody ratio is suggestive of better efficacy of the drug conjugate. However, higher drug payload also increases the probability of adverse effects. Thus, the drug-to-antibody ratio is generally limited between 2 and 4 for the majority of the drug conjugates [283–287]. Consequently, optimizing the drug-to-antibody ratio is a critical part of developing drug conjugates. Further, many antibodies possess cysteine and lysine residues which are highly reactive and affect the stability of the conjugates. As a result, random conjugation may occur, compromising conjugate stability and causing premature or off-target drug release, leading to toxicity [288–295]. Due to the heterogeneity of target antigens produced by tumor cells, drug conjugates need to be developed based on the specific mutations and antigens expressed [296]. Swift disintegration of the drug conjugates as well as failure of linker cleavage poses a significant challenge in developing a stable and efficacious drug conjugate [297–302].

Conclusion

Lung cancer remains one of the deadliest cancers due to its high metastatic potential and late-stage diagnosis. While treatment strategies like surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have improved outcomes, the efficacy of most therapies is still limited by poor bioavailability, non-specific targeting, and drug resistance. Drug conjugates offer a promising solution, enhancing specificity, tumor cell permeability, and minimizing side effects. Clinical studies have shown their efficacy across various cancers, including lung cancer, with advancements in optimizing payloads, linker stability, and drug chemistry.

However, challenges remain in optimizing drug conjugates, particularly in payload delivery, linker stability, and avoiding premature payload release. Future research should focus on addressing these issues to maximize therapeutic effectiveness. Additionally, integrating molecular profiling into clinical care can enable more personalized treatments, improving targeting accuracy and patient outcomes.

With ongoing advancements, the next generation of drug conjugates is expected to offer significant therapeutic benefits with minimal adverse effects, potentially revolutionizing lung cancer treatment by providing more precise, individualized therapies.
