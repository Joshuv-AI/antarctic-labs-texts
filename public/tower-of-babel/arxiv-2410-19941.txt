Privacy without Noisy Gradients: Slicing Mechanism for Generative Model Training
Kristjan Greenewald, Yuancheng Yu, Hao Wang, Kai Xu

Abstract
Training generative models with differential privacy (DP) typically involves injecting noise into gradient updates or adapting the discriminator’s training procedure. As a result, such approaches often struggle with hyper-parameter tuning and convergence. We consider the slicing privacy mechanism that injects noise into random low-dimensional projections of the private data, and provide strong privacy guarantees for it. These noisy projections are used for training generative models. To enable optimizing generative models using this DP approach, we introduce the smoothed-sliced f f -divergence and show it enjoys statistical consistency. Moreover, we present a kernel-based estimator for this divergence, circumventing the need for adversarial training. Extensive numerical experiments demonstrate that our approach can generate synthetic data of higher quality compared with baselines. Beyond performance improvement, our method, by sidestepping the need for noisy gradients, offers data scientists the flexibility to adjust generator architecture and hyper-parameters, run the optimization over any number of epochs, and even restart the optimization process—all without incurring additional privacy costs.

Introduction

Just as oil fueled the industrial revolution, data now propels innovation and progress in today’s digital life. However, data sharing faces challenges due to privacy risks and regulatory policies (e.g., GDPR, CCPA, FTC). Synthetic data offers a promising solution as it closely resembles real data, retains its formats and essential properties, and can be seamlessly integrated into existing workflows. Additionally, they encounter a dilemma when determining the number of training epochs: with a fixed privacy budget, increasing the number of epochs requires injecting more noise into gradient update per iteration, while fewer epochs might not be sufficient for the optimizer to converge. In such a setting, if the training does not converge, the only recourse may be to increase the privacy budget.
This motivates a fundamental question:

How can we train generative models with DP guarantees while ensuring

easy fine-tuning, stable convergence, and high utility?

In this paper, we introduce a new learning paradigm for training privacy-preserving generative models.
Our approach decouples the training process into two steps: (i) computing noisy low-dimensional projections of the private data along random directions, and (ii) updating the generative models to fit these noisy projections.
We establish DP guarantees for the first step and leverage the random projection to further tighten our privacy bound. This decoupling strategy offers several advantages.
The post-processing property of DP ensures that any deep learning techniques can be applied in the second step.
In other words, our approach is model-agnostic, allowing for smooth integration into existing training pipelines of generative models.
With our method, data scientists have the flexibility to adjust generator architecture and hyper-parameters, optimize the generative model for any number of epochs, and even restart the optimization—all without worrying about additional privacy costs.

Based on this paradigm, we are motivated to introduce a new information-theoretic measure: the

f

Additionally, we present a kernel-based, differentiable estimator for this divergence. It circumvents the need for adversarial training in generative models, thereby enhancing convergence stability and robustness to different choices of hyper-parameters.
Finally, we establish the statistical consistency of training generative models using this divergence.

In terms of the slicing mechanism, we build upon the work of

However, their approach is limited to 1-dimensional projection spaces (

In contrast, we propose a generic framework for training privacy-preserving generative models, applicable to any

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method through numerical experiments. We compare generative models trained using our method with those trained by standard privacy mechanisms (DP-SGD, PATE, or smoothed-sliced Wasserstein distance) among various real-world datasets.
The results indicate that our approach consistently produces synthetic data of higher quality compared with baseline methods.

Definition 2

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{M}(\textnormal{X})\in\mathbb{S})\leq\exp(\epsilon)\Pr(\mathcal{M}(\textnormal{X}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{S})+\delta.

3.2
Slicing Privacy Mechanism

The loss function in (
2

Definition 5
.

Let
k

m

m^{\prime}=mk

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m^{\prime}}

\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m^{\prime}}

\textnormal{U}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,d^{-1})

\textnormal{V}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})\triangleq(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V}).

(3)

Remark 1
.

Our mechanism outputs the random slicing matrix
\mathbf{U}

[

In Definition

This choice simplifies the sampling procedure and streamlines our privacy analysis by making the the mechanism output jointly Gaussian with zero mean, conditioned on the data. Next, we establish the DP guarantees for our noisy slicing mechanism.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\gamma\triangleq\sigma^{-2}(\alpha^{2}-\alpha)<d
. For all
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>1

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})=(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V})
in Definition

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m^{\prime}\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}(d-\gamma)}+\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1},\delta\right)-\text{DP}.

where
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP is as defined in Definition
2
with dataset adjacency specified in Definition
1

Our privacy bound relies on
k
(the dimension of projection spaces) and
m
(the number of slices) only through their product
m^{\prime}=mk

aids generative models in capturing higher-order information of the original data, while increasing

[

We prove in Appendix Proposition
3

-DP if the projection matrix

Remark 2

(Choosing

.

The bound in Theorem

5
Final Remarks and Limitations

In this paper, we consider the slicing mechanism for training privacy-preserving generative models and derive strong privacy guarantees for it. This mechanism motivates us to combine it with
f
-divergence to yield a smoothed-sliced
f
-divergence that can be estimated with a kernel-based density ratio estimator.
Our approach circumvents the need for injecting noise into gradient updates and avoids adversarial training for optimizing generative models. It provides flexibility in selecting neural architectures and tuning hyper-parameters without incurring additional privacy costs. Through experiments on synthetic data generation, we validate our approach and compare it with existing baselines. Our findings suggest that the slicing privacy mechanism is a powerful tool for training generative models to create private synthetic data, and the smoothed-sliced
f
-divergence provides a promising avenue for advancing the field of privacy-preserving data synthesis in sensitive, high-dimensional datasets. Additionally, we hope our effort can be of particular interest to the information theory community and open up a new application frontier for classical information-theoretic tools.

There are several promising avenues worth exploring. The
f
-divergence has many nice properties, including (strong) data processing inequalities, inequalities among different
f
-divergences, and variational representation. It would be interesting to investigate whether similar properties extend to the smoothed-sliced
f
-divergence
\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}
. Additionally, deriving sample-complexity bounds for estimating
\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}
from finite samples would offer valuable insights.
Finally, the use of synthetic data presents both opportunities and challenges. On the one hand, synthetic data is well-suited for various tasks like early model development, educational demonstrations, simulation, and testing. On the other, synthetic data can never fully replicate all aspects of the original data, and adding DP guarantees introduces an additional layer of complexity requiring a careful balance between privacy and utility. Therefore, any machine learning models trained on synthetic data or any insights drawn from synthetic data should undergo thorough evaluation before deployment in real-world scenarios. If biases are detected, it is crucial to diagnose their source—whether stemming from the synthetic data generation algorithm, the noise added to the real data, or other factors—to ensure that the models perform robustly and reliably in practice.

Proposition 2

(Prop. 3 in

If

We remind readers that two datasets
\mathbf{X}
and

are considered adjacent if they differ in a single row, say the

\|\mathbf{X}_{i,:}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{i,:}\|_{2}\leq 1

We revisit privacy amplification by subsampling, a useful technique for handling large-scale datasets that can save computational resources and memory. Lemma 1

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A})\triangleq[a_{1,1},\cdots,a_{m,1},\cdots,a_{1,n},\cdots,a_{m,n}]^{T}.

For two matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}\mathbf{B}&\cdots&a_{1,n}\mathbf{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\|\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime})^{T}((1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2(\alpha-1)}\ln{\frac{|(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}|}{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1-\alpha}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{U}^{T})\\
\mathrm{vec}((\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V})^{T})\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=d^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{dm^{\prime}}&\mathbf{X}^{T}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}}\\
\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}}&(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}})+d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{nm^{\prime}}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=d^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{X}^{T}\\
\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}+d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{array}\right]\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=d^{-1}\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}},

(14)

where we define

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})\|\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\ln{\frac{|(1-\alpha)\mathbf{B}+\alpha\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|}{|\mathbf{B}|^{1-\alpha}|\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\left[\big((1-\alpha)\ln|\mathbf{B}|+\alpha\ln|\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|\big)-\ln{|(1-\alpha)\mathbf{B}+\alpha\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|}\right].

|\mathbf{B}|=|\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|=|d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}|.

For the linear combination, we have

\displaystyle|(1-\alpha)\mathbf{B}+\alpha\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{d}&(1-\alpha)X^{T}+\alpha{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}^{T}\\

\displaystyle=\left|d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+\left[(1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}+\alpha\mathbf{X}^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}^{T}-\left((1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}+\alpha{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}\right)\left((1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}+\alpha{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}\right)^{T}\right]\right|.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}

\displaystyle\triangleq(1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}+\alpha\mathbf{X}^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}^{T}-\left((1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}+\alpha{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}\right)\left((1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}+\alpha{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=-\alpha(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}^{T})-\alpha^{2}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime})(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+\alpha\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+\alpha(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})\mathbf{X}^{T}

\displaystyle=-\alpha(-(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})\mathbf{X}^{T})

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha^{2}(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+\alpha\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}+\alpha(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})\mathbf{X}^{T}

\displaystyle=(\alpha-\alpha^{2})(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})(\mathbf{X}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})^{T}.

Since
\mathbf{X}
and
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
only differ in the
i
-th row, only one entry of this matrix can be nonzero, specifically,

\alpha-\alpha^{2}\leq\Delta_{ii}\leq 0.

Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})||\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\left[\big((1-\alpha)\ln|\mathbf{B}|+\alpha\ln|{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}|\big)-\ln{|(1-\alpha)\mathbf{B}+\alpha\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\left[\ln|d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}|-\ln|d\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\left[\ln(d\sigma^{2})-\ln(d\sigma^{2}+\Delta_{ii})\right].

Recall the assumption that
|\Delta_{ii}|\leq\alpha^{2}-\alpha\leq\gamma\sigma^{2}
for
\gamma\in(0,d)
. Then using the fact that
\log(a)
is concave in
a
and has derivative
1/a
for
a>0
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})||\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\frac{1}{d\sigma^{2}+\Delta_{ii}}\Delta_{ii}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\frac{1}{d\sigma^{2}+(\alpha-\alpha^{2})}(\alpha^{2}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(\alpha-1)}\frac{1}{(d-\gamma)\sigma^{2}}(\alpha^{2}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{\prime}}{2(d-\gamma)\sigma^{2}(\alpha-1)}(\alpha^{2}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{\prime}\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}(d-\gamma)}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Theorem
1
follows directly from combining Lemma
2
with Proposition
2
.
∎

Next, we establish a privacy bound assuming the projection matrix
\mathbf{U}
is deterministic. Comparing this with Theorem
1
, we observe that by randomly selecting the projection matrix, we can achieve a tighter privacy bound, even if it is disclosed by the privacy mechanism.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
represents the original dataset. Let
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m^{\prime}}
be a random noise matrix with each element independently drawn from
\textnormal{V}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Let
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m^{\prime}}
be a deterministic matrix such that for any
j\in[m]

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{:,(j-1)k+1},\cdots,\mathbf{U}_{:,jk}\text{ are orthonormal}

The privacy mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\text{det}}(\mathbf{X})\triangleq\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1},\delta\right)-\text{DP}.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

The privacy mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\text{det}}
maps
\mathbf{X}
to a
nm^{\prime}
-variate Gaussian distribution with mean and covariance being:

\displaystyle\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V})=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}),\quad\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{V})=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{nm^{\prime}}.

Using (
7
) yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})\|\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{U})^{T}(\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{nm^{\prime}})^{-1}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{vec}((\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\mathbf{U})^{T}\mathrm{vec}((\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}\|(\mathbf{X}_{i,:}-\mathbf{X}_{i,:}^{\prime})\mathbf{U}\|_{2}^{2}.

Without loss of generality, we assume

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{i,:}-\mathbf{X}_{i,:}^{\prime}=[x,0,\cdots,0]\quad\text{with }|x|\leq 1.

Additionally, recall the assumption that for any
j\in[m]
,
\mathbf{U}_{:,(j-1)k+1},\cdots,\mathbf{U}_{:,jk}
are orthonormal. Hence,
U_{1,(j-1)k+1}^{2}+\cdots+U_{1,jk+1}^{2}\leq 1
, leading to

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X})\|\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}U_{1,j}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha m}{2\sigma^{2}}.

As a result, our privacy mechanism is
(\alpha,\frac{\alpha m}{2\sigma^{2}})
-RDP. Combining it with Proposition
2
yields the desired DP guarantee.
∎

Appendix B
Other Omitted Proofs

Proof of Proposition
1
.

The property
\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{X}}\|Q_{\textnormal{X}})\geq 0
follows by the non-negativity of the
f
-divergence in the integrand.
If
P_{X}=Q_{X}
, then clearly

\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{X}}\|Q_{\textnormal{X}})=\frac{1}{\rm{vol}(\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}}\|Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}})\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}=0

since the
f
-divergences
\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}}\|Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}})
are all zero by definition.

Conversely, by the nonnegativity of the
f
-divergence,
\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{X}}\|Q_{\textnormal{X}})=0
implies

\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}}\|Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}})=0

for almost every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Additionally, since
f
is strictly convex at
1
, we have

P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}}=Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}+\textnormal{N}}.

As a result, by the invertibility of Gaussian convolution,

P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}=Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}

for almost every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

since
f
-divergence nullifies iff the arguments are equal. Since the distributions are equal, the moment generating functions of these projections are equal, i.e. for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}}[e^{t^{T}Z}]=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}}[e^{t^{T}Z}]

i.e.

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\textnormal{X}}}[e^{t^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{\textnormal{X}}}[e^{t^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\textnormal{X}}].

Since
\boldsymbol{\theta}t
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have that for any
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\textnormal{X}}}[e^{s^{T}\textnormal{X}}]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{\textnormal{X}}}[e^{s^{T}\textnormal{X}}].

But these are just the moment generating functions of
P_{\textnormal{X}}
and
Q_{\textnormal{X}}
. Since the moment generating functions exist and are equal for all
s
, the densities
P_{\textnormal{X}}
,
Q_{\textnormal{X}}
must be equal.

∎

Proof of Corollary
3
.

By the assumption that
\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{f}
is a consistent estimator of the
f
-divergence,
b\rightarrow\infty
with
\sigma
constant implies that for any fixed
\omega
,

\lim_{n,b\rightarrow\infty}L(\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\textnormal{D}_{f}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}G_{\omega}(Z)+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}X+\textnormal{N}}\right),

(15)

where
\textnormal{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k})
.

In the first case, we assume
G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)\sim P_{X}
. Substituting
\omega=\omega^{\ast}
into (
15
) yields

\lim_{n,b\rightarrow\infty}L(\omega^{\ast})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\textnormal{D}_{f}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}X+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}X+\textnormal{N}}\right)=0,

where equality with zero follows because the
f
-divergence nullifies if its arguments are the same distribution.

For the second case, we assume that for some
\omega^{\ast}\in\Omega

\lim_{m,n,b\rightarrow\infty}L(\omega^{\ast})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\textnormal{D}_{f}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}X+\textnormal{N}}\right)=0.

Since the
\textnormal{D}_{f}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}X+\textnormal{N}}\right)
terms are all
\geq 0
, the fact that this limit exists implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n,b\rightarrow\infty}L(\omega^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rm{vol}(\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)+\textnormal{N}})\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{SD}_{f,k,\sigma^{2}}(P_{G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)}\|P_{\textnormal{X}}).

By Proposition
1
, this can equal zero only if
P_{G_{\omega^{\ast}}(Z)}=P_{\textnormal{X}}
.
∎
