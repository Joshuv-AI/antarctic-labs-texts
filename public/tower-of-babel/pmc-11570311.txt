Migration, human capital, and decent work: Venezuelans in Peru
Authors: Mirza Marvel Cequea, Valentina Gomes Haensel Schmitt, Agnieszka Ewa Olter-Castillo, Oscar Melo-Vega-Angeles
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study explores the human capital, vulnerability and integration of Venezuelan migrants and refugees in Peru, emphasizing their difficulties in obtaining decent work and assimilating as productive members of Peruvian society. This study aims to investigate the challenges faced by Venezuelan migrants and refugees in integrating into Peruvian society as productive members. Using a descriptive and exploratory approach, data were collected via a questionnaire from 1193 Venezuelan migrants and refugees seeking assistance from an NGO in Lima, Peru. This study's findings address a critical gap in the existing literature, revealing that despite high levels of education, many migrants struggle utilize their skills and knowledge effectively, leading to significant brain waste due to precarious employment conditions and suffering through their exposure to various vulnerabilities. These findings underscore the need for public policies and practices that address the improvement of migrants' and refugees' living conditions and the promotion of Decent Work.

Introduction

Migration is perceived as part of broader transformation processes [1,2] that contributes to global justice and human rights [3,4], and human mobility is intrinsically tied to social change [1]. In social context, mobility is the result of a selection of individuals, through various agents' own mechanisms - family, school, religion, bureaucracies -, with different importance in each society, which control, guide, determine the position of people in a category of strata itself and, indirectly, status at a macrosocial level [5].

Therefore, when considering migration, countries worldwide strive to attract highly skilled workers, as their mobility presents different implications for regional development compared to unskilled mobility [6], and immigration is an economically and politically acceptable phenomenon [7]. For these reasons, scholars have explored the cause and effect of migration on development [1,4,8] and its potential to stimulate economic growth and alleviate poverty.

Migrant status often serves as a noteworthy indicator of precariousness, especially in certain labour sectors and within unregulated and substandard forms of employment [9]. Although the decent work agenda has gained attention, there remains a need to explore the formulation and implementation of appropriate policies in developing countries [10,11]. Moreover, a moral gap exists in the research and on people-management practices [12].

Globalization, migration, and the demand for decent work are intertwined [10], and by integrating Sustainable Development Goals into global policies, it is possible to address the underlying causes of migration from a rights-based perspective. This aligns with the vision for "safe and orderly migration" and addresses the challenge of inadequate "decent work" opportunities [9], A major concern is the marginalisation of immigrants within the workplace [13]. Therefore, countries need to enhance their capacity to identify migration patterns and the necessary training and assistance for successful integration [14].

Nonetheless, limited research attention has been given to the issues related to forced migration and the refugee crisis, and their implications for theory and practice [15,16]; the experiences of refugees and displaced individuals and how organisations, leaders, and policymakers can effectively address their needs [17]; the availability of suitable data on migration and migrant characteristics that can facilitate the advancement of high-skilled migration research [18]; and prioritizing expatriates’ experiences, resulting in assumptions and perspectives that may not be applicable to other groups that move across borders [16]. There is therefore need for more inclusive management approaches [15,19], as skilled human capital increasingly becomes a strategic factor for development and emerging economies. Consequently, greater attention from administration [15], international business [16], economics academics and from other fields will be necessary to address these gaps.

In addition, according to the International Labour Organization [ILO], migrant workers comprise 5 % of the global labour force [20,21], and there is an increasing proportion of international migration from south to north [2,20]. However, developing countries host approximately 86 % of the world's refugees [17,21]. Moreover, the recent coronavirus pandemic has intensified the vulnerability experienced by refugees and displaced individuals across the world [17].

There is therefore a research gap regarding the understanding of the challenges faced by migrants and refugees, from forced migration processes to their integration into and becoming productive members of the host society, within a developing countries context. This study thus aims specifically to investigate the challenges faced by Venezuelan migrants and refugees in integrating into Peruvian society as productive citizens, choosing Metropolitan Lima, Peru, as the study area.

Theoretical framework and literature review

Migration

Migration refers to the act of individuals relocating to a new place of residence, influenced by factors of attraction and repulsion [2,22] that cause the movement of individuals within and between different regions. International migration involves the movement of individuals across national borders, leading to a change in their legal status [2].

The push-pull theory, as outlined by Ref. [23], provides a foundational framework for understanding migration by categorising the factors that drive migration into "push" factors, which compel individuals to leave their place of origin (e.g., conflict, economic hardship, environmental degradation), and "pull" factors, which attract individuals to a new location (e.g., job opportunities, better living conditions, political stability). This theory helps explain how individuals' decisions to migrate are influenced by the interplay between adverse conditions in their home country and favourable prospects in the destination country [8,[24], [25], [26]].

10.13039/100031212Network theory further explains how migration decisions are influenced by social networks, which provide information, resources, and support to potential migrants [27,28]. Social networks reduce the costs and risks associated with migration by offering assistance with employment, housing, and navigating the new environment. These networks often lead to chain migration, where individuals from the same origin follow one another to the same destination, thereby reinforcing migration flows over time. Similarly, financial motives are often significant determinants of migration [29].

The various groups within migration exhibit variations across several dimensions, including their mode of movement, received support, motivations, duration of stay, and social status in the host country, in addition to the immigration policies and processes of acceptance and integration in the destination country [16]. Likewise, precarious immigration status is an important factor contributing to marginalisation [30], and when considering migration from the labour perspective, it entails the freedom of workers to practice their occupation where opportunities exist [14]. However, immigration impacts the areas where migrants settle, influencing wages, taxes, and the overall composition of the local population [31], as well as labour market dynamics [26]. This can potentially disrupt the comfort of the native population in their daily lives, which is especially true for individuals with lower levels of education [31].

Migrant human capital

Human capital (HC) refers to the skills and knowledge acquired through education, years of experience, language proficiency, and other abilities and traits [32]. Skill is understood as the ability to accomplish an activity or job or to acquire occupational qualifications, especially due to experience [33], or as individual attributes that contribute to situational effectiveness in relation to a specific objective for the effective exercise of an action [34]. In contrast, knowledge is represented by the understanding or information that individuals obtain through experience during study, information and know-how transfer [33], or by any idea that is or has been the subject of collective support by a significant group, prioritizing its social impact as a selection criterion [35]. Therefore, HC theory posits that migration is driven by individuals' investments in their own skills and education to improve their economic prospects [36,37]. This theory suggests that individuals assess the potential return on their investments in education, skills, and work experience when deciding whether to migrate.

Migrants often relocate to areas where they believe their human capital can be better utilised, leading to higher earnings and improved quality of life [1]. This aligns with the observation that successful migration requires the possession of necessary human, financial, and social resources, as well as a desire to relocate [8]. In the professional development of skilled migrants and their integration into the labour market, factors such as language skills, gender expectations, social networks, local knowledge, and employer attitudes play a significant role.

Another aspect relevant to HC and migration is gender, which may present similar challenges but lead to different experiences in the job search due to gender norms associated with family responsibilities [38]. For instance, the context of gender norms is relevant for the understanding of precarious work [39]. The gender discussion also includes the subject of migrants leaving family behind, such that household separations may be unevenly distributed across factors such as age, gender, and country of reception [40]. Moreover, being a single female labour migrant may, for instance, be strongly marked by the gendered dimension of transnational motherhood and a loss of social status [41]. Therefore, all these elements may particularly affect female professional development and job search experiences.

The level of self-employment among immigrants varies considerably, depending on the average educational level in their country of origin. However, educational qualifications from the home country, for instance, may not directly influence entrepreneurship [42].

The relationship between migration, HC, and skills stems from the territories and organisations needing to attract and retain trained professionals, so the concept of capacity is thus essential in the study of migration [4]. Highly skilled individuals tend to emigrate more than those with lower levels of education [7], or they migrate facing fewer difficulties. Moreover, while host countries often prefer highly skilled immigrants, there is also a growing demand for filling low-skilled positions [14].

However, the employment of skilled migrants frequently highlights significant underutilization of their skills and a limited match between qualifications and the available job opportunities, resulting in the “waste of human capital” [38,43] or “Brain Waste” [44]. Furthermore, even refugees with qualifications face challenges in finding employment that matches their skills [19]. Therefore, the migration “brain waste” occurs when individuals’ qualifications acquired overseas are not recognized in destination countries, and when qualified emigrants cannot employ properly their specialized skills and previous experience, this generates a “a triple loss” (to source countries, to destination countries, and to migrant workers [44]. Considering migration in terms of capabilities can therefore encourage long-term planning to reduce vulnerability and address migration beyond economic aspects [4].

Decent work in migration

Decent work (DW) involves productive employment that is fairly compensated and carried out in conditions of freedom, equity, security, and respect for human dignity [45]. This includes four key components: employment, social protection, workers’ rights, and social dialogue. DW encompasses fair remuneration, workplace safety, social security, opportunities for personal growth, trust, diversity in the workplace, equal opportunities and treatment, freedom, and social integration [12]. Moreover, a fair remuneration is understood as one that allows workers to give them and their families a decent standard of living [46].

Decent and productive work enhances the quality of life for workers and contributes to poverty reduction [47], and it is beneficial for both society and companies as it reflects concern and respect for workers [12]. However, the intertwining of migrant irregularisation and profiling, with migrants being perceived as both exploitable and suspicious, significantly hinders individuals with precarious legal migration status from accessing social benefits, DW, and the vital networks needed to improve their social circumstances [30]. Irregular migration refers to the movement of people that takes place outside the regulatory norms of the sending, transit, and receiving countries [48]. Migrants who enter or stay in a country without proper authorization often find themselves excluded from formal labour markets, which are governed by laws that protect workers' rights and ensure fair wages and safe working conditions [49]. This exclusion can lead to situations where migrants are forced into informal or exploitative employment, where they are vulnerable to abuse, underpayment, and hazardous working conditions [50]. The legal framework surrounding irregular migration typically criminalizes unauthorized entry or stay, leading to the stigmatization and marginalisation of migrants [51], and often results in the profiling of migrants as both exploitable due to their lack of legal protection and suspicious due to their perceived illegality [30]. Such profiling not only prevents migrants from accessing decent work but also restricts their ability to utilize essential social services, such as healthcare and education, which are crucial for improving their social and economic circumstances [52]. Moreover, the fear of detection and deportation further isolates irregular migrants, making it difficult for them to form the social networks necessary for integration and upward mobility [53]. In this context, the law plays a dual role: while it is designed to regulate labour markets and protect workers, it also serves as a mechanism of exclusion for those who do not meet legal residency requirements. Therefore, the concept of labour decency cannot be fully understood without considering the legal status of workers and the significant barriers faced by irregular migrants in accessing the rights and protections afforded to regular workers [54].

The DW agenda recognizes the significance of formalizing employment and establishing a regulatory framework that protects employees’ rights [11], acknowledging that development relies on upholding human rights [55]. Therefore, DW has the potential to unite the efforts of migrant organisations and labour unions in addressing persistent precariousness concerning work rights abroad and the right to work in the host country [9]. Moreover, it entails the involvement of influential state and non-state actors [10].

However, the current global economy has given rise to labour markets that are increasingly deregulated and characterized by the growth in the “informal economy”, which comprises non-standard forms of work [56], which is particularly pronounced in developing countries. Consequently, even though migrant workers are granted permission to work, many struggle to find employment that matches their pre-migration qualifications, leading to the dilemma of accepting whatever job they can find, withdrawing from the labour market, or returning to their home country [57].

In this sense, migrants may be compelled to engage in precarious forms of work due to residence and work permit requirements, and the recognition of job qualifications. This often leads to the most vulnerable migrants ending up in the informal sector, where regular employment contracts and job security are absent [56]. They can also face specific inequalities referring to gender, race, class, and nationality, increasing their susceptibility to informal and precarious forms of work [58].

Another aspect is the application of low-skilled and low-paid temporary contracts, which leads to marginalisation, limited rights, exploitation, and discrimination [59]. The indicators of DW practices may encompass aspects such as immigration debt, overwork, abuse at work, work-related injuries, religious rights, adequate food, absenteeism, social security, homesickness, extended family separation, and family issues at home [47].

Vulnerability under migration processes

Vulnerability represents a multidimensional condition, encompassing various aspects of social, material, environmental, and relational life, with labour vulnerability being closely linked to the concept of DW, and its absence leading to individual and social distress, impacting family, housing and community security [60]. International migration, while intended to be legal, safe and orderly, can vary depending on the efforts and policies of each country [2]. Migrants often face multiple forms of precariousness and vulnerability [9].

In the labour context, migrants face vulnerability in various situations, such as difficulties in entering the labour market due to problems with documentation and identification and the regularization of migration status, discrimination, discrepancies between educational systems, difficulties with the recognition of qualifications, and limited personal networks beyond their communities [61]. In addition, the conditions of vulnerability to which migrants and refugees are exposed may be related to factors such as the possibility of legal regularization of their stay in the country and administrative barriers; the susceptibility to different forms of exploitation and abuse, violence, discrimination, and harassment; and the limited access to basic and essential services [62]. These situations, all together or even separately, may thus lead to a situation that impedes individuals’ access to safe and fair job conditions.

Therefore, the erosion of employment conditions is perpetuated through social and political processes that divide the workforce based on ethnic or racial origins and gender differences [56]. Precarity manifests itself in practices such as informal work, short-term and seasonal contracts, temporary employment, recession-driven labour market conditions, industries that rely heavily on casual and temporary work, policy gaps, regulatory deficiencies, barriers to social mobility, and the depoliticization of labour [60], and the lack of quality representation further exacerbates the dichotomy between workplace and migration issues [63]. Moreover, the experiences of temporary migrant workers can be situated along a spectrum that encompasses elements of modern slavery [9] and restrictive policies and anti-immigration public discourses contribute to the marginalisation of immigrants [13], fostering xenophobia, racism, and affecting social cohesion [8].

Integration of migrants and refugees

Integration encompasses the process by which migrants and refugees become part of the host society and culture [3]. It involves various domains such as accessing employment, housing, education, and healthcare services, as well as understanding and practising their citizenship and rights. Social integration also involves establishing connections within and between groups in the community [64].

Legal aspects play a crucial role in the successful integration of migrants into the host society. The influx of Venezuelan migrants into Peru has led to the establishment of various legal statuses, including the Temporary Stay Permit (PTP), later replaced by the Temporary Permit Card (CPP), which grants legal residency, work rights, and access to public services. Humanitarian Residency offers similar benefits on humanitarian grounds, while Special Migration Status provides temporary legal residency for those unable to secure other permits. Migrants can further regularize their stay by obtaining a Foreigner's Card, leading to Permanent Residence Status, which allows indefinite stay and access to nearly all rights of Peruvian citizens. These legal frameworks have been crucial for the integration of Venezuelan migrants, though challenges remain in transitioning from temporary to permanent statuses [[65], [66], [67]].

Social integration can be understood through factors such as the length of stay in a country, maintaining one's own cultural identity, while also adapting to the culture of the destination country, and receiving social support from both the migrant and the host communities [32,61]. These aspects contribute to fostering a sense of belonging and social cohesion for migrants in their new environment.

In addition, economic inclusion depends on employment, which provides economic solvency to assume commitments. However, according to Ref. [68], in South American countries, the unemployment rate of Venezuelan migrants and refugees is significantly higher than that of the population of the receiving country and/or the local community, which is aggravated by gender differences.

Methodology

This study employs a quantitative and cross-sectional approach, with an exploratory and descriptive scope. It characterises Venezuelan migrants in terms of HC, vulnerability, integration, and DW, focusing on the challenges they face while integrating into Peruvian society as productive members. The study area was Lima, which is the city with the largest number of Venezuelans in Peru. There are currently over 1.49 million Venezuelans in Peru, with the majority (82.50 %) concentrated in Lima [69]. This study was submitted to the ethics committee of the University of Lima for approval and was approved under Dictamen N.027-CEI-IDIC-ULIMA-2022.

The sample. Non-probability convenience sampling was used to select participants based on their availability [[70], [71], [72], [73]], which made it difficult to ensure an equal distribution across genders. The inclusion criteria were as follows: individuals over 18 years of age, professionals or non-professionals of all genders, who were part of the economically active population of Peru and who resided in Metropolitan Lima. To calculate the sample size, it was considered that 889,809 Venezuelans resided in Lima [74], with a margin of error of 3 % and a confidence level of 95 %, having an estimated sample size of 1067 subjects. However, this study received a total of 1193 valid responses to the survey and 98 invalid ones.

The questionnaire study. The study instrument consisted of various sections, including living conditions, HC, DW, integration and vulnerability, and comprising by 86 questions, including multiple-choice, dichotomous, Likert scale, and open-ended questions. The researchers utilised questionnaires previously used by other organisations, such as the Survey of the Venezuelan Population Resident in Peru [69] as well as the National Household Survey [75].

The data collection for this study took place between July and September 2022 and targeted Venezuelan migrants who visited the NGO Unión Venezolana. The survey was initially sent to groups of Venezuelans in Lima via a Google Form, and to different groups of Venezuelans through social networks; however, the response rate was extremely low, with only 58 people responding via this means, so the decision was made to print the questionnaire and attend places where Venezuelans were concentrated, such as the NGO "Unión Venezolana" and support fairs for migrants held in different parts of Lima. Data collection was performed in person, and the subjects were approached while waiting in line to be attended, which took between 40 and 60 min, during which time the researchers explained the reason for the survey and provided them with a questionnaire printed on paper, a pen and a support surface for writing, to facilitate the filling out of the questionnaire, which took about 20 min. Prior consent was obtained from the participants in written form, through a question included in the questionnaire provided, and their anonymity was ensured throughout this study. Participation in this study was voluntary.

The survey instrument for measuring these variables comprised the following:

Human Capital: This comprises an HC assessment (with eight questions) and Knowledge, skills, and experience - this section uses a Likert scale, consisting of nine questions with responses ranging from 1 to 5, used to measure how migrants utilised their HC in Peru.

Vulnerability: This comprises seven questions that analyse living conditions, which are frequently associated with vulnerability.

Integration: Support and coexistence. This comprises 13 questions, each rated on a scale of 1–5, that focus on the support networks and assistance available to migrants and refugees in Peru, as well as their level of integration within their community or neighbourhood.

Decent work. The scale to evaluate these aspects consists of 32 questions, with response options ranging from 1 to 5, a scale referring to the current employment situation of migrants and refugees in Peru and the professional context in which their work is carried out.

Based on the evaluation scale presented in Table 1, the findings reveal that 42 % (from 38 to 51) of the respondents effectively utilised their support networks, trusted the people and institutions in their surroundings, engaged in social activities within their community, appreciated the culture and traditions of the host country, had equal opportunities, contributed to community organisations, identified with the community, and reported their overall satisfaction with integration as “Good situation”. However, 37 % (from 27 to 37) of the respondents rated their integration as average or a “Poor situation” and 17 % rated it as a “Precarious situation”. Only 4 % of participants rated their integration as "Excellent".

Decent work: assessment of work

Based on the evaluation scale presented in Table 1, 42 % of respondents rated their work situation as a “Good work situation”, 30 % rated it as a “Poor work situation”, and 21 % considered it a “Precarious work situation”. Only 7 % rated it as an “Excellent work situation”. These findings highlight that 50 % of the respondents assessed their work situation negatively, taking into consideration factors such as job security, the value attributed to their work, respect for their human dignity, conditions of equality and freedom, access to formal employment, and fair wages and benefits.

Discussion

Our study revealed that the respondents exhibit a notable level of education, particularly when compared to the overall population of Peru, as mentioned in Ref. [68]. In addition, these findings demonstrate that the surveyed individuals possess significant potential in terms of skills, competencies, and knowledge, which could be leveraged to enhance the performance of local businesses, - as proposed in Ref. [7]. However, findings regarding HC utilization reveal a notable disparity, primarily caused by unrecognized degrees and the inability to practice their profession, leading to the phenomenon known as "human capital waste" - as described in Ref. [43]. Therefore, this study confirmed previous statements—such as the one in Ref. [56]—that the requirement for residence permits, work permits, and job qualifications can often limit migrants' opportunities and push them towards informal sector employment, which lacks regular contracts and job security.

In addition, the results reveal the low utilization of migrants' knowledge, skills, and experience, i.e., their HC, which implies a deficient or precarious utilization of the migrants’ capabilities and potential. Migrants have limited or no opportunities to enhance their competencies and skills, with their previous work experience being undervalued. Moreover, they had minimal expectations for professional development and growth.

However, despite skills being instrumental in improving productivity, adding value, and fostering social and economic change, many migrants struggle to secure employment that aligns with their pre-migration skills [57], primarily due to various factors restricting their access to DW [30]. Therefore, a significant number of migrants decide to be self-employed and have their own businesses as a survival strategy.

Also, migrants often work long shifts, with barely any days off for resting—most working over 8 h per day and 6 days a week—which, according to Ref. [9], could be considered a serious labour rights violation, an indication of poor working conditions, and an example of what [9] defines as modern slavery. Moreover, there is a lack of health insurance and poor access to healthcare coverage and the healthcare system, facts that amplify migrants’ vulnerability.

It was also found that a migrant's work situation may be classified as poor, with a lack of participation in decision-making processes; thus, their expectations regarding their professional future and retirement are almost non-existent. Labour marginalisation [13], was evident, along with the precariousness migrants experienced, as suggested by Refs. [9,59]. These conditions contribute to their vulnerability, leading to stressful situations such as long working hours and days, limited access to healthcare, and experiences of discrimination, - as previously noted by Refs. [8,9]. In summary, the lack of access to basic services exacerbates their vulnerability.

Migrants tend to concentrate in middle-to low-income areas [74], which is primarily driven by their income levels, which further increases their vulnerability. Most respondents reported living in a condition analogous to poverty or extreme poverty, - according to the [69] classification. The consequences of this include being forced to depend on informal and precarious employment for their livelihood, and accessing informal and deregulated labour markets, leading to non-standard forms of work [9,56], precarious work, or unemployment, which hinders their integration [68] into the economy and host society.

Moreover, the findings reflect migrants' efforts to obtain legal recognition and access their corresponding rights and obligations. However, in practice, this confirms the situation whereby the complex relationship between migrants' legal and illegal status constrains the opportunities available to those in precarious legal migration situations [9,30,59]. Therefore, within the context of this study, a condition of vulnerability is identified due primarily to migrants' legal status, according to the regulations of the host country and how it grants access to services, migrants’ working conditions, and the ways in which they exercise their citizenship.

This study also revealed a notable number of migrants and refugees with separated families, where their children are in diverse places across Peru, Venezuela, and other countries. This highlights a significant proportion of families in which migrant parents and children live apart, underscoring their vulnerabilities and the challenges faced in terms of family separation and caregiving responsibilities. This situation may also be intertwined with issues related to DW, as labour precariousness hinders family reunification, thus affecting family security and other factors [60].

Regarding discrimination, the most relevant issues are as follows: 1. gender as the relevant issue and with women being the most affected; 2. race or skin colour, primarily affecting men; 3. sexual orientation and disability, with similar proportions of discrimination for both men and women. These findings reinforce the existence of specific groups who experience inequalities based on gender, race, class, and nationality [58,59], and these groups require special attention according to those most affected.

With respect to integration, less than 45 % of respondents effectively utilize their support networks, trust people and institutions in their surroundings, engage in social activities within their community, appreciate the culture and traditions of the host country, contribute to community organisations, identify with the community, and express satisfaction [3,61], indicating the limited "successful" integration of immigrants into the host society and culture [64]. This highlights an opportunity for improvement for immigrants, NGOs, and public policymakers to foster social connections, as pointed out by Refs. [10,64], and [32].

In the exploratory factor analysis, the new constructs or dimensions of the human capital variable were "Training" (Factor 1), " Capacities/Competencies " (Factor 2), and “Capitalization of experience” (Factor 3). Regarding integration, the new constructs were "Resilience" (Factor 1) and "Sense of belonging and social cohesion" (Factor 2). For DW conditions, the new constructs were " Respectful Treatment and freedom " (Factor 1), "Safety, health and security" (Factor 2), and "Fair remuneration" (Factor 3).

Stronger normative and organizational linkages between global migration governance and migrant rights movements are therefore crucial for advancing DW agendas [9,10], and a holistic approach to migration is needed to reduce the existing high levels of inequality [9,10,15,16].

Conclusions

The objective of this study was to investigate the challenges faced by Venezuelan migrants and refugees while integrating into Peruvian society as productive citizens from the perspective of HC, vulnerability, integration, and DW. It is concluded that despite the high educational level of the migrant population, many of them struggle to use their skills and knowledge effectively. This struggle results in a significant “brain waste” wherein migrants’ HC potential is underutilized, with the main causes including the lack of recognition of their qualifications and the inability to practise their professions in Peru. Consequently, 1) migrants often find themselves relegated to the informal sector, experiencing underemployment or even conditions resembling modern slavery, and 2) irregular or precarious employment arrangements further deprive migrants and refugees of essential occupational health and safety measures, exposing them to non-decent working conditions, thus increasing their vulnerability and insecurity.

Among the factors of DW, it is essential to highlight that achieving the conditions of freedom, respect, and dignity is not only the most relevant factor in this challenge but also the most complex. The state of non-decent work exposes migrants and refugees to a range of vulnerabilities that directly affect them, and its impact also extends to their families. Furthermore, the frustration they have from not being able to use their HC, combined with the discrimination they encounter, aggravates the challenges poses by labour precariousness, making it difficult to break the cycle of poverty and marginalisation. As a result, the potential of migrants and refugees to contribute to the sustainable development of the host society and territory is only partially utilised.

However, in terms of integration, most migrants and refugees make effective use of support networks, and they demonstrate respect for the culture of the host community, experience a sense of identification, and express overall satisfaction.

The results highlight the necessity to improve the integration of migrants and refugees by involving the host society, governments, and businesses. Public policies should therefore prioritize equity, respect, trust, support, and the improvement of living conditions, while ensuring DW. Thus, among the various implications and recommendations for public policies and practice, we suggest special attention, from a coproduction perspective [29] including governments, business, and host societies, that considers the improvement of migrants' and refugees’ living conditions and DW conditions while also considering local vulnerable society needs.

Therefore, it is imperative to establish a regulatory framework and develop and implement appropriate social policies to ensure DW [11], incorporating justice, security, freedom, and equity [10,12]. Facilitating professional practice is also crucial to prevent migrants and refugees from forgetting or losing their professional skills and abilities [19]. Three major areas of concern are identified by this study in relation to discrimination: 1. gender as the relevant issue and with women being the most affected; 2. race or skin colour, primarily affecting men; 3. sexual orientation and disability, with similar proportions of discrimination for both men and women. These findings support the notion that there are particular groups facing discrimination on the basis of nationality, gender, and class [58,59]. Special care should be given according to those groups most impacted.

This study makes several important contributions to the existing literature on migration, human capital, and labour integration. By addressing the underutilization of human capital among Venezuelan migrants and refugees in Peru, this research fills a crucial gap highlighted in studies such as [38,43] regarding "brain waste" and the mismatch between migrants’ skills and their employment opportunities. Additionally, this study expands on the discourse around DW by connecting it directly to the lived experiences of vulnerable migrant populations, as discussed in Refs. [10,11]. The findings provide empirical evidence of how gender, race, and other social determinants intersect to create specific vulnerabilities in the labour market, which complements and expands on the work in Ref. [9] on migrant precarity. Through the integration of various views, this study offers fresh insights that can guide more successful policy interventions while also validating current theories.

In terms of limitations, it ought to be mentioned that the sampling method used in this research may restrict the generalization of the findings to a broader population, as the results are specific to the sample used. Another limitation was that the respondents were subjects who attended the NGO, which meant that we did not have access to a wider range of participants of that nationality. In addition, participation was solicited by the researchers face to face, and subjects could voluntarily decide to participate or not; this is different to when the subject decides to participate in surveys out of their own motivation, such as online ones. Although the survey was initially conceived to be completed online, through Venezuelan social networks, it did not have the expected level of response. Another limitation was the spatial distribution of Venezuelans in Lima, as they have settled in all districts, making access difficult and costly, in addition to the fact that the project had a limited time frame. Although this study examined key aspects of vulnerability, such as employment conditions and discrimination, other factors like mental health, access to education for migrant children, and the impact of family separation were not explored. These factors could impact the integration and well-being of migrants.

Future research could focus on longitudinal studies to track Venezuelan migrants' integration and human capital utilization over time, offering insights into the long-term effectiveness of current policies. Comparative analyses of migration policies across South America and their impact on integration outcomes would inform best practices for policy and practice. Additionally, exploring the intersectionality of vulnerabilities such as gender and legal status, the impact of irregular migration on social networks, and the role of employers in facilitating or hindering labour market integration could guide targeted interventions. These areas of research would not only address this study's limitations but also issue a call to action for more inclusive and effective policy frameworks.
