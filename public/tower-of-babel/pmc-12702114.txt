Effects of papain enzymolysis on the functional properties, stability and sensory quality of soy milk after thermal steam treatment
Authors: Fuwei Sun, Yiying Jin, Miaomiao Liu, Linyi Zhou, Yuanyuan Sun, Cuiping Han, Zengwang Guo, Yapeng Liu, Zhongjiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of different levels of papain addition (0.5 %, 0.75 %, 1.0 % and 1.25 %) on the functional properties, stability, and sensory quality of soy milk derived from thermally steamed soybeans. Compared with untreated samples, the contents of basic components in thermally steamed soy milk, increased after enzymolysis, along with the β-turn and random coil contents of soy milk proteins. Incorporating, 1.0 % papain significantly reduced the droplet size, enhanced the apparent viscosity, and increased the absolute ζ-potential from 21.40 to 38.45 mV. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and stability analyses revealed that moderate enzymolysis reduced the size of oil droplets and protein aggregates, thereby improving the stability of soy milk. Furthermore, thermal steaming reduced the beany odor of soy milk, and enzymolysis further decreased the contents of beany flavor compounds, resulting in higher sensory scores and better flavor.Keywords: Soy milk, Papain, Enzymolysis, Stability, Flavor

Introduction

Soy milk is an ideal nutrient-rich plant-based protein beverage. Regular consumption of soy milk has been associated with a reduced risk of chronic diseases and preventive activity against certain cancers (Luo et al., 2023). Soy milk contains soy protein, lipids, soluble sugars (fructose, stachyose, and rhamnose), lecithin, vitamins, and isoflavones (Giri & Mangaraj, 2012; Navicha et al., 2018). Notably, its protein content is nearly equivalent to that of cow's milk. Moreover, soy milk does not cause lactose intolerance and can serve as a dairy alternative. Soy milk is recognized as “plant-based milk” in Europe and the United States. Traditional soy milk production typically involves soaking soybeans, grinding them with water, filtering residues, and boiling the raw soy milk (Yan, Tong, et al., 2024).

Flavor and stability are two critical indicators of soy milk quality. During production, the combined action of endogenous enzymes in soybeans and environmental factors generates small molecule compounds responsible for off-flavors, such as beany, grassy, or fatty notes (Yu et al., 2017). In particular, during the grinding of raw beans, lipoxygenase catalyzes polyunsaturated fatty acids to produce alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, amines, and other volatile compounds with different odor thresholds, resulting in the characteristic beany flavor (Feng et al., 2020). By contrast, as an oil-in-water emulsion, soy milk undergoes degradation processes such as sedimentation, creaming, coalescence, flocculation, and Ostwald ripening, which reduce its stability (Mu et al., 2022). Therefore, improving both flavor and stability of soy milk remains a primary challenge in enhancing its overall quality.

Currently, the primary methods for eliminating the beany flavor in soy milk include physical methods (heating and supercritical CO2 extraction) (Kumari et al., 2016; Lv et al., 2011), chemical methods (pH adjustment, reducing agents, and organic solvent extraction) (Wang et al., 2021), and biological methods (enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial fermentation) (Zhao et al., 2025). Among these, heating is the most commonly used method that eliminates beany flavor primarily via effective inactivation of lipoxygenase. Heating denatures lipoxygenase in soybeans, thereby irreversibly destroying its catalytic function. This prevents lipoxygenase from initiating the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, thus inhibiting the formation of beany flavors in soy milk. Navicha et al. (2018) reported that roasting soybeans at 110 °C for 40–60 min decreased the objectionable green and beany flavors while enhancing sweetness, viscosity, and roasted flavor. However, heat treatment induced protein denaturation and aggregation, which led to the formation of oil–protein coacervate clusters in soy milk, consequently increasing the particle size of proteins and oil droplets (Peng et al., 2017). Simultaneously, heat-induced denaturation of oleosin proteins on the surface of oil bodies further compromises soy milk stability, leading to droplet aggregation. Enzymatic hydrolysis is another effective approach for eliminating beany flavor from soy milk. The addition of proteases breaks down soy proteins into smaller peptides or amino acids, releasing aromatic components from the precursors, thus improving the flavor profile (Li et al., 2025a). Moreover, enzymatic treatment cleaves peptide bonds within protein molecules, generating peptides with increased hydrophobicity and ionizable residues, which enhances their ability to readily adsorb at the oil–water interface and stabilize the emulsion (Tong et al., 2021). For example, Li et al. (2025b) reported that moderate hydrolysis of soy protein increased protein surface charge density, which generated strong electrostatic repulsion forces at the water–oil interface, thus reducing droplet aggregation and improving emulsion stability. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms by which combined heat and enzymatic treatments improve both stability and flavor of soy milk remain unclear.

In this study, we prepared soy milk from thermally steamed soybeans and enzymatically hydrolyzed it using papain. Using untreated samples as a control, we investigated the effects of thermal steaming and different papain addition levels on both soy milk composition and structure of soy milk proteins. Moreover, this study provided detailed insights into the impacts of thermal steaming and different papain addition levels on the physicochemical properties (particle size, ζ-potential, and apparent viscosity), microstructure (CLSM), stability (centrifugal sedimentation rate, stability factor, and Turbiscan), sensory attributes, and flavor of soy milk. This study offers a theoretical basis for the development of soy milk with enhanced stability and reduced beany flavor.

Materials and methods

Materials

The soybeans were purchased from Yuwang Industrial Co., Ltd., Shandong, China. Papain (800 U/mg) was provided by Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, and other chemical reagents and solvents were of analytical grade.

Soy milk preparation

Thermal steam treatment of soybeans: soybeans were washed thrice with distilled water and then soaked at 4 °C for 18 h using a 3:1 (w/w) dry soybean–to–water ratio. The soybeans were then heated in a superheated steam oven (Sharp, Healsio AX-LSX3B, Japan) at 100 °C for 5 min.

Preparation of enzyme-modified soy milk: the thermal steam soybeans were ground in a mixer (JYL-C051, Joyoung Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China) with water at a 1:10 ratio (dry soybean:water) for 3 min to obtain raw soy milk. Papain was added to the raw soy milk at concentrations of 0.5 %, 0.75 %, 1.0 %, and 1.25 % (based on soybean weight), respectively, and the mixtures were stirred at 55 °C for 30 min. All enzymatic hydrolysis reactions were inactivated by heating in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 10 min. The treated soy milk was filtered through a Philips filter screen and then boiled for 10 min. Finally, the soy milk was cooled in an ice-water bath and stored, yielding the enzyme-modified soy milk samples designated as CBS, CBS0.5, CBS0.75, CBS1.0, and CBS1.25. CBS refers to soy milk prepared using thermal steam treatment but without the addition of papain, and CB refers to soy milk prepared without thermal steaming and without papain.

Determination of protein, soluble protein, fat and total carbohydrate content in soy milk

Protein content (Bradford assay), fat content (Soxhlet method), and carbohydrate content (phenol–sulfuric acid method) were determined according to the AOAC (2016) method (Hsieh et al., 2024). The reported protein contents were corrected for the protein contributed by the added papain by subtracting the mass of papain from the total measured protein.

The total soluble protein content in soy milk supernatants was quantified using the Bradford assay (Chen & One, 2014; Xing et al., 2017). Briefly, 3 mL of a 50 % (w/w) sucrose solution was injected into the bottom of a tube containing 6 mL of soy milk, followed by ultracentrifuged at 156,000 ×g for 30 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was collected and appropriately diluted. An aliquot of the diluted supernatant was then mixed with Coomassie Brilliant Blue reagent. After incubation at room temperature for 5 min, the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS plus, Molecular Devices, USA). A standard curve was prepared using bovine serum albumin, and the soluble protein content was calculated.

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

The samples were diluted with PBS buffer to five mass concentrations: 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01, 0.015, and 0.02 mg/mL. ANS solution was added to the sample, and after thorough mixing, the mixture was allowed to react in the dark for 2 h. The fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer at an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission wavelength of 470 nm. The measurements were performed three times, and the average value was taken. The fluorescence intensity was plotted against the protein mass concentration, and the slope obtained after linear fitting represented the surface hydrophobicity index H0 of the samples (Guo et al., 2025).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Several soy milk samples were freeze-dried and stored for subsequent analysis. For Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis, soy milk powders obtained from these samples were uniformly mixed with KBr at a mass ratio of 1:100 and pressed into transparent sheets. FTIR spectra (iS10, Nicolet, USA) were acquired using 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1 over the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 (Guo et al., 2025). PeakFit software was employed to analyze the amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) using second-derivative peak fitting. Additionally, the relative contents of protein secondary structures (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) were calculated.

Particle size distribution and zeta (ζ) potential

The samples were diluted 100-fold. Particle size distributions were measured by using a Nano-ZS90 zetasizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Great Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. The refractive index of soy milk particles was set to 1.57 and that of water was set to 1.33. Each measurement was performed in triplicate.

For ζ-potential analysis, the soy milk samples were diluted 100-fold, and 0.8 mL of the diluted sample was transferred to the sample cell. With the refractive index, dispersant refractive index, and absorption set to 1.57, 1.33, and 0.001, respectively, the samples were equilibrated for 2 min before the ζ-potential was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK) (Mu et al., 2022).

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

The microstructures of the samples were examined using CLSM (FV3000, Olympus, Japan). Briefly, 1 mL aliquots of each sample were mixed thoroughly with 20 μL of 0.05 % (w/v) fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) solution and 20 μL of 0.05 % (w/v) Nile red solution (dissolved in 1,2-propanediol). The stained samples were incubated for 20 min in light-protected containers. A 10 μL portion of each prepared sample was then mounted on glass slides, covered with cover slips, and sealed with nail polish. FITC fluorescence was measured at an emission wavelength of 518 nm and Nile red fluorescence at 488 nm (Kaharso et al., 2021).

Centrifugal precipitation rate of soy milk

A total of 5 g of soy milk was weighed and placed in a 10 mL centrifuge tube, with the initial weight recorded as M0. The sample was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The sample was then weighed and recorded as M1. Stability factor of soy milk

After 100-fold dilution of soy milk, the initial absorbance (A1) was measured at 550 nm using a TU-1810 ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Beijing Miki Instrument Co., Ltd). Subsequently, the soy milk samples were centrifuged at 1006.2 ×g for 10 min at 25 °C, and the absorbance of the resulting supernatant (A2) was measured under identical spectrophotometric conditions. The stability factor R was calculated as R = A2/A1 (Yan et al., 2024).

Color parameters

The color of soy milk was measured using a colorimeter (YS3060, Jiulian Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). To evaluate the color changes, an appropriate volume of soy milk was placed in a white-background container for measurement, and the L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ values were recorded directly (Jiang et al., 2018).

Turbiscan measurements

Soy milk stability was assessed using a Turbiscan analyzer (Turbiscan Tower, Formulaction, France). Briefly, 15 mL of the sample was loaded into glass measurement cells and continuously scanned for 2 h with a pulsed near-infrared light source (λ = 880 nm) (Zhao et al., 2025).

Storage stability

The morphologies of stored soy milk were evaluated by microscopy (Nikon 80i, Japan) at 40× objective.

Sensory evaluation

Ten trained participants (5 female, 5 male, 20 to 27 years of age) from Northeast Agricultural University were invited to participate in this study. All participants had 50 h of experience in food sensory evaluation and had received specialized training in soy milk flavor assessment. Using the color, beany flavor, bean aroma, organizational state, and mouthfeel of soy milk as sensory evaluation indicators, a sensory evaluation index and scoring scale for soy milk was established, as shown in Table 1. To ensure the validity of the results, participants rinsed their mouths thoroughly with water three times after evaluating each sample. Samples were then analyzed every 10 min.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

The Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was focused on identifying and quantifying key volatile compounds associated with beany off-flavors in soy milk, based on existing literature. According to the method of Feng et al. (2024) 10 mL of soy milk was pipetted into a 20 mL headspace vial. Then, 10 μL of the internal standard, benzyl alcohol, was added. The bottle mouth was sealed, and a extraction tip was inserted. Extraction was performed in a 40 °C water bath for 40 min, with the extraction tip positioned approximately 1 cm above the liquid surface. Subsequently, the extract was thermally desorbed in the GC inlet for 5 min at 250 °C for GC–MS analysis. GC conditions: the chromatographic column was a DB-WAX (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm); the injection port was 230 °C; the program temperature was: 40 °C (held for 3 min), ramped to 100 °C at 6 °C/min, then raised to 230 °C at 10 °C/min, and held for 7 min; carrier gas flow rate: 1.0 mL/min; the carrier gas was helium (99.999 %). MS Conditions: temperature of the interface was 250 °C; temperature of the ion source was 200 °C; and the mass scan range was 29–500 m/z. Component identification was achieved by automatically matching the acquired mass spectra against the NIST 2002 library using the Xcalibur workstation. Volatile components were then quantified based on the internal standard method.

Statistical analysis

All soy milk samples were prepared and analyzed in triplicate. Results were presented as means ± standard deviations. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Significant differences between groups (p < 0.05) were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's test.

Results and discussion

Contents of basic components in soy milk

Table 2 presents the protein, soluble protein, fat, and total carbohydrate contents in soy milk. Compared with those in the blank sample, the contents of protein, soluble protein, and carbohydrate in CBS decreased, whereas the fat content increased. During the filtration of thermal steam soy milk, some insoluble substances were retained in the filter, resulting in reduced protein and carbohydrate contents in the soy milk. As the addition of papain increased from 0.5 % to 1.0 %, the protein, soluble protein, fat, and total carbohydrate contents in the soy milk increased. Some insoluble protein molecules were hydrolyzed by proteases into peptides of different molecular weights, thereby enhancing protein solubility and increasing the protein content in soy milk. In addition, a significant portion of soybean fat was retained within the insoluble okara residue during traditional soy milk production. Enzymatic hydrolysis disrupted the proteinaceous and cellular matrix of the soybean slurry, releasing the encapsulated fat from the okara into the liquid phase. Consequently, more fat was retained in the final soy milk after filtration, increasing its measured fat content (Qian et al., 2019). However, as the addition of papain increased, the contents of these components decreased. This might be because of the structural damage caused by excessive enzymatic hydrolysis, which promoted the co-precipitation or embedding of carbohydrates and other soluble solids within protein aggregates or insoluble complexes.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) is an important physicochemical parameter related to protein conformation that influences its functional properties (Table 3). The H0 value of the CBS sample was lower than that of BS, which could be attributed to heat-induced aggregation that buried hydrophobic regions. As the papain concentration increased from 0.5 % to 1.0 %, H0 increased significantly, with CBS1.0 exhibiting the highest value. This indicates that moderate enzymatic hydrolysis unfolded protein molecules and exposed internal hydrophobic residues (Shuai et al., 2022). The increase in surface hydrophobicity enhances the amphiphilicity of proteins, facilitating their adsorption at the oil–water interface and contributing to the formation of a stable emulsion. Conversely, excessive enzymatic hydrolysis breaks down hydrophobic regions or leads to protein–protein aggregation, reducing the number or surface area of hydrophobic groups exposed to water (Feng et al., 2024).

Secondary structure

The main types of protein secondary structures include α-helix, β-sheet, random coil, and β-turn. To investigate changes in these secondary structures, the amide I band region of the FTIR spectrum was further analyzed. The α-helix is an ordered and highly stable protein secondary structure; disordered protein structures are primarily composed of β-turns and random coils (Sun et al., 2024). Compared with BS, CBS exhibited a significant increase in α-helix and β-sheet contents and a decrease in β-turn and random coil contents, indicating that thermal steaming induced a more compact protein structure, promoting aggregation (Table 3). The thermal steam treatment resulted in protein refolding, forming more complex cross-links that facilitated the formation of a more compact and folded structure (Mediwaththe et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023b). With increase in papain addition, α-helix and β-sheet contents in soy milk protein decreased initially and then increased, whereas the β-turn and random coil contents first increased and then decreased. These findings indicate that enzymatic hydrolysis partially disrupted the original rigid structure of soy milk protein, thereby increasing its flexibility and causing the protein conformation to transition from an ordered to a disordered state (Guo et al., 2022; Ubaid & Saini, 2025). Consequently, the secondary structure of the soy milk protein underwent transitions.

Particle size distribution

Soy milk is a common oil-in-water emulsion, wherein the size of oil droplets considerably influences system stability. The sedimentation and buoyancy of protein particles follow Stokes' law, indicating that larger protein particles settle faster (Li et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1A, compared with untreated soy milk, the CBS group exhibited a significant increase in particle size. This was likely because of protein dissociation and rearrangement during the thermal steam process, leading to the formation of protein aggregates through non-covalent interactions or disulfide bonds. Such aggregation reduced the ability of proteins to stabilize oil droplets, thereby considerably increasing the particle size of soy milk (Guo et al., 2024). With increasing papain addition, the particle size distribution of soy milk initially shifted toward the left and subsequently to the right. Moreover, enzymatic hydrolysis increased the area of the main peak but reduced the area of the shoulder peak. The CBS1.0 group exhibited the smallest particle size distribution. These results are consistent with those of previous research (Mu et al., 2022). Papain hydrolyzed some insoluble protein molecules into low-molecular-weight proteins, increasing the soluble protein content in soy milk. This enhanced the emulsifying properties of soy milk and facilitated the formation of smaller droplets. Simultaneously, enzymatic treatment further unfolded the protein structure, enhancing protein adsorption at the oil–water interface and consequently reducing droplet coalescence. However, excessive hydrolysis broke down proteins into very small and short peptides. These peptides lacked sufficient hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions to adsorb stably at the interface, resulting in an interfacial film with weak mechanical strength that could not prevent droplet aggregation caused by collisions (Zhang et al., 2024).

Zeta (ζ) potential

The stability of soy milk is correlated with intensified repulsive forces between its oil droplets. A higher absolute ζ-potential indicates greater system stability, whereas lower values promote droplet aggregation. As shown in Fig. 1B, all soy milk samples exhibited negative ζ-potential values. The absolute ζ-potential decreased after thermal steam treatment. This is likely because partial degradation or denaturation of soybean proteins during preheating induced conformational changes and reduced the number of charged groups, leading to a decrease in droplet charge in soy milk (Chen et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2020). The ζ-potential of soy milk first increased and then decreased with increase in papain addition. Enzymatic hydrolysis decomposed proteins into polypeptides of varrying molecular weights, exposing more negatively charged groups and increasing the electronegativity of soy milk (Li et al., 2025b). Another reason might be that enzymatic hydrolysis decreased the particle size of soy milk, thus decreasing the aggregation of soy milk particles. Protein aggregation has been reported to be negatively correlated with the absolute ζ-potential. However, as papain addition increased further, the absolute ζ-potential of CBS 1.25 decreased. This might be because excessive enzymatic hydrolysis disrupted the protein structure, leading to the aggregation of small molecule peptides generated during the process, which masked the charged groups. Consequently, the electrostatic repulsion between soy milk emulsion droplets weakened, leading to droplet flocculation. This finding is consistent with the particle size distribution results.

CLSM

CLSM was employed to observe the distribution of oil and protein droplets in soy milk, thus providing insights into soy milk stability. As shown in Fig. 2A, oil droplets appeared red, while the protein phase was green. Light green indicated regions where oil and protein were mixed. In the control sample (BS), oil droplets were spherical and showed minimal aggregation. In contrast, both oil droplets and proteins in soy milk prepared from thermally steamed soybeans exhibited pronounced aggregation. During the thermal steam process, heat transfer via conduction caused localized overheating of soybean components. Lower soybean moisture content increased protein concentration, thereby enhancing aggregation propensity. Moreover, thermally steamed soy milk samples showed considerably reduced soluble protein content and diminished protein emulsifying properties. Consequently, more fat was released and coalesced into large oil droplets, leading to large-scale particle aggregation in soy milk (Wang et al., 2020). As papain addition increased from 0.5 % to 1.0 %, oil droplets and protein aggregates markedly decreased. Moderate enzymatic hydrolysis unfolded the protein structures, increased the surface charge, and exposed hydrophobic groups, thereby enhancing electrostatic repulsion and interfacial adsorption (Wang et al., 2022). Nonetheless, when papain addition was increased further, proteins and droplets in soy milk began to reaggregate. Excessive hydrolysis reduced the molecular weight and charge density of soy proteins, thereby weakening electrostatic repulsion and diminishing their ability to stabilize interfaces. As a result, oil droplets coalesced, and the hydrolyzed peptides self-assembled into aggregates or micelles, leading to macroscopic instability.

CPR of soy milk

The stability of soy milk was evaluated using CPR (Fig. 1C). Compared with BS, CBS exhibited a considerably increased CPR, indicating that thermal steam treatment reduced soy milk stability. This reduction might be attributed to the aggregation of proteins and oleosomes within soy milk, leading to the formation of larger particles. After the thermal steam process of soybeans, proteins in the unheated soy milk might promote the formation of larger aggregates through hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds. Upon subsequent reheating, the altered protein structures further enhance aggregation of proteins through hydrophobic interactions, facilitating aggregation into even larger particles. These changes result in a marked decrease in soy milk stability (Peng et al., 2017; Sağlam et al., 2014). With increasing papain addition, the CPR of soy milk initially decreased and then increased, indicating that moderate hydrolysis enhanced soy milk stability. As papain concentration increased, protein aggregates in the soy milk were hydrolyzed into smaller particles, resulting in increased molecular flexibility. These smaller, more flexible protein molecules could rapidly form an interfacial layer at the oil–water interface, thereby stabilizing the soy milk. However, excessive papain addition negatively affected the CPR of soy milk. This might be attributed to the over-hydrolysis of proteins into small peptides, which likely weakened the structural integrity of the interfacial layer (Wu et al., 2024). As a result, the emulsion became more susceptible to droplet coalescence and sedimentation, leading to an increase in CPR. This interpretation is supported by the larger particle size and reduced absolute ζ-potential observed in the CBS1.25 sample. Our findings are consistent with those of Wang et al. (2025), who reported that over-hydrolysis led to a decline in the stability of flaxseed protein–stabilized emulsions.

Apparent viscosity of soy milk

Viscosity plays a crucial role in the formation and stability of soy milk. Generally, as viscosity increases, the free movement, flocculation, and phase separation of oil droplets are restricted, enhancing the stability of soy milk (Wu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1D, all soy milk samples exhibited similar shear-thinning behavior. The apparent viscosity of soy milk was highest at the initial shear rate, gradually decreased as the shear rate increased, and eventually reached a plateau. This indicated that the interactions between proteins and water molecules, as well as interactions among proteins themselves, were disrupted under the applied shear force, allowing the molecules in soy milk to rearrange and form a more stable structure. The apparent viscosity of the CBS group was considerably higher than that of the BS group. This increase could be attributed to the disruption of oleosome structure during thermal steam treatment, which caused oleosin proteins to detach from the oleosome surface and lipids to be released from the oleosomes. Consequently, aggregates formed, leading to the increased apparent viscosity of soy milk (Ding et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2020). These findings are consistent with the CLSM results. With increasing papain addition, the apparent viscosity of the soy milk initially increased and then decreased, reaching its maximum at a papain concentration of 1.0 %. This phenomenon might be attributed to the partial unfolding of soy milk proteins during enzymatic hydrolysis, allowing polypeptide chains to cross-link and form an ordered network structure (Zhang et al., 2023). Higher viscosity resulted from steric hindrance within protein chains and from intermolecular friction. Nonetheless, with further increases in papain addition, the macromolecular polymers in soy milk degraded into smaller molecules, reducing their molecular mass, disrupting the original network structure, and decreasing viscosity.

The changes in parameters obtained from fitting the apparent viscosity of soy milk using the power-law model are presented in Table 4. The flow behavior index (n) of all samples was <1, indicating that the soy milk exhibited shear-thinning behavior. The consistency coefficient (K) represents sample viscosity, with higher K values corresponding to more viscous samples. After thermal steamed treatment, the K value of soy milk initially increased and then decreased; a similar trend was observed with increasing papain addition. Among the samples, CBS1.0 showed the highest K value and a relatively low flow behavior index, consistent with the trend observed for apparent viscosity. This is because papain reduced the particle size of soy milk and increased the specific surface area of the droplets, thereby increasing the number of droplets and the interactions between them. These interactions caused droplet collisions and entanglements during shear in the rheological analysis, increasing the resistance on the rotating probe. However, excessive papain reduced the K value, suggesting that over-enzymatic hydrolysis may disrupt soy milk homogeneity, leading to system instability and a reduction in viscosity.

Color difference of soy milk

The color of soy milk is an important criteria for evaluating its quality, influencing consumer preference and acceptance of the product (Hu et al., 2022). The L⁎ value indicates brightness, with higher values indicating lightness and lower values indicating darkness. The a⁎ value represents red and green components, with higher values indicating redness and lower values indicating greenness. The b⁎ value represents yellow and blue components, with higher values indicating a yellow hue and lower values indicating a blue hue. Compared with the blank sample, the thermally steamed soy milk exhibited decreased L⁎ and a⁎ values but an increased b⁎ value (Table 4), indicating a more yellowish appearance. This change was likely because of the disruption of the soybean tissue structure during thermal steam treatment, facilitating the release of colored substances into soy milk (Zenker et al., 2020). With increasing papain addition, the L⁎ value first increased and then decreased, whereas the a⁎ and b⁎ values first decreased and then increased. The CBS1.0 group showed the highest L⁎ value and the lowest a⁎ and b⁎ values. Appropriate enzymatic hydrolysis unfolded the protein aggregates, leading to the release of more fat into soy milk (Qian et al., 2019). Because oil bodies are white spheres, their presence increase the whiteness of soy milk, and the high fat content after enzymatic hydrolysis further contributed to the whiteness. However, excessive hydrolysis led to the formation of more protein aggregates, resulting in larger fat aggregates, which subsequently caused the soy milk to turn darker.

Stability of soy milk

As shown in Fig. 1E, thermal steam treatment reduced the stability factor of soy milk, likely due to protein aggregation within soy milk. With the addition of papain, the stability factor initially increased and then decreased, with the CBS1.0 group exhibiting the highest value. Enzymatic hydrolysis expanded the protein molecular framework and increased the soluble protein content in soy milk. This facilitated the formation of a stable oil-in-water emulsion, thereby enhancing soy milk stability. A previous study showed that enzymatic hydrolysis cleaves long amino acid chains, increasing the contents of polypeptides, short peptides, and amino acids in soy milk, thereby enhancing its stability (Yan et al., 2024). However, as the papain concentration increased, the stability factor of soy milk decreased. CLSM results indicated that at excessively high papain concentrations, over-hydrolysis generated low-molecular-weight peptides, leading to severe disruption of the protein network. These peptides formed dense, amorphous aggregates that were spatially separated from coalesced oil droplets, which resulted in the loss of interfacial integrity. Additionally, these peptides could not assemble into a cohesive, viscoelastic film at the oil–water interface, leading to intensified flocculation and coalescence (Yin et al., 2024) and, consequently, reduced soy milk stability. The enzymatic approach offers faster processing times and greater control over the degree of hydrolysis than other biological methods. Nevertheless, the potential disadvantages include the additional cost of the enzyme and the need for precise process control to prevent over-hydrolysis, which can detrimentally affect both stability and flavor.

Turbiscan test of soy milk

Backscattered (ΔBS) light intensity can be used to characterize the physical stability of soy milk; greater overlap among ΔBS curves indicate better physical stability of the soy milk system (Mu et al., 2022). The ΔBS light intensity is shown in Fig. 2B, where different colored lines represent measurements taken at different time intervals over the 2 h scanning period. The right side of the x-axis represents the liquid surface of the sample, which is approximately 50 mm. The ΔBS curves for untreated and thermally steamed soy milk exhibited significant fluctuations. Specifically, BS increased markedly from 0 to 10 mm, remained largely unchanged from 10 to 40 mm, and then decreased and failed to overlap as the height continued to increase. This indicated internal instability within the soy milk and the occurrence of droplet coalescence. The thermal steamed treatment induced oleosome aggregation in soy milk, leading to oil droplet creaming. As papain concentration increased from 0.5 % to 1.0 %, the ΔBS curves of soy milk gradually overlapped, particularly in the 0–10 mm range, suggesting that enzymatic hydrolysis improved soy milk stability. Moderate enzymatic hydrolysis facilitated the adsorption of more soluble proteins onto the droplet surfaces, thereby inhibiting droplet coalescence (Ding et al., 2021). This in turn reduced the droplet size in soy milk, consequently slowing down droplet movement and enhancing soy milk stability. However, with further addition of papain, the ΔBS curves no longer overlapped, indicating that excessive enzymatic hydrolysis led to an increase in soy milk droplet size and a decline in stability.

Storage stability

To accurately investigate changes in soy milk droplet size during storage, the microstructure of soy milk stored for 14 days was observed using optical microscopy. The droplet size in the CBS group was larger than that in the BS group (Fig. 3A). Moreover, with an increase in papain addition, the droplet size of soy milk initially decreased and then increased, consistent with the droplet size distribution measurements. In addition, the droplet size of all soy milk samples increased after 14 days of storage. Severe droplet coalescence was observed in both the BS and CBS groups after 14 days of storage. However, when the papain addition was increased to 1.0 %, no significant droplet coalescence was observed in the soy milk even after 14 days of storage. The results showed that moderate enzymatic hydrolysis increased the surface charge of the droplets and enhanced the steric resistance between the soy milk droplets, thereby preventing agglomeration, flocculation, and phase separation during storage.

Sensory evaluation

The results of sensory evaluation of soy milk are shown in Fig. 3B. Compared with the untreated control, soy milk prepared from thermally steamed soybeans showed markedly enhanced sensory properties. This soy milk exhibited almost no beany flavor but possessed a rich soybean aroma and a complex taste profile. The removal of beany odor was primarily attributed to lipoxygenase inactivation during heat treatment, which inhibited the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into volatile off-flavor compounds. Alternatively, the exposure of hydrophobic groups during the boiling of raw soy milk leads to hydrophobic aggregation among protein molecules (Yan et al., 2024). The flavor compounds bound to proteins through hydrophobic interactions may also contribute to sensory enhancement (Wang & Arntfield, 2015). Moreover, the disruption of soybean tissues and oil bodies during thermal steam treatments led to greater fat release in soy milk, which helped to stabilize and retain lipid-soluble flavor compounds, resulting in a richer aroma and taste. The thermally steamed soy milk had a slight yellow color, possibly because of the dissolution of some lipid-soluble pigments in the fat. With the addition of papain, the color, beany flavor, bean aroma, organizational state, and mouthfeel of the soy milk improved further. This suggested that at a papain addition level of 1.0 %, the positive flavor improvements outweighed any potential bitterness, resulting in the overall enhancement of sensory quality. During enzymatic hydrolysis, proteins denature and expose more hydrophobic sites, enhancing their ability to bind and release volatile compounds (Yan et al., 2025). Moreover, enzymatic hydrolysis reduced the coarse particulate sensation caused by protein aggregation, resulting in a more refined texture. The moderate reduction in viscosity also contributed to the enhanced mouthfeel. Therefore, enzymatic hydrolysis of soy milk after thermal steaming further improved its sensory quality and palatability. The key advantage of this dual-treatment strategy over conventional single pretreatments (thermal treatment) lies in its synergistic effect: thermal steaming effectively inactivates lipoxygenase to reduce the initial formation of beany flavors, whereas subsequent moderate enzymolysis improves soy milk stability by modifying the protein structure and further reduces residual off-flavor compounds. The decline in sensory scores and stability at the highest papain level (CBS1.25) may be indicative of the onset of over-hydrolysis, which can lead to the production of short-chain hydrophobic peptides that impart bitterness and other undesirable sensory attributes.

GC–MS

The variations in the volatile components of soy milk were analyzed by using GC–MS. Aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, and furans are a class of odor-active compounds that are considered undesirable by consumers. Typical compounds responsible for the beany odor include hexanal, trans-2-heptenal, hexanol, and 2-pentylfuran (Yan et al., 2024). The compounds in soy milk that were not described sensorily were shown in Table S1. Four categories of compounds contributing to the beany flavor were identified from soy milk, namely, aldehyde, furans, ketones, and alcohols (Table 5). As shown in Table 5, the main compounds (hexanal and 2-pentylfuran) contributing to beany odor in untreated soy milk were present at relatively high concentrations. However, their concentrations decreased after thermal steaming, indicating that thermal steaming may effectively reduce beany odor. Thermal steamed treatment might inhibit lipoxygenase activity to reduce the generation of soy milk flavor compounds (Jiang et al., 2018). Moreover, the contents of aldehydes (hexanal, (E)-2-hexanal, octanal, (E)-2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal, (E)-2-nonenal, and (E)-2-decenal) gradually decreased with increasing papain addition, reaching a minimum at 1.0 %. Nonetheless, excessive papain addition (>1.0 %) resulted in a slight increase in aldehyde content. Similar results were observed for furans, ketones and alcohols, indicating that appropriate papain addition (<1.0 %) reduced these off-flavor compounds, whereas excessive papain addition (>1.0 %) led to their increase. The addition of papain can generate more small peptides and amino acids, thereby producing more non-beany flavor compounds. Meanwhile, substances such as hexanal can bind firmly to terminal amino and carboxyl groups in soy proteins, forming more complex compounds and facilitating the easy removal of off-flavors (Yan et al., 2024). Moreover, enzymatic hydrolysis facilitated the release of these beany flavor substances, resulting in soy milk with a milder beany off-flavor and a richer soy aroma (Zhang et al., 2022).

Conclusion

This study examined the effects of papain enzymolysis on the functional properties, stability, and sensory quality of thermally steamed soy milk. The addition of 1.0 % papain significantly increased the soluble protein content and the absolute ζ-potential of soy milk while inducing a transition in protein conformation from an ordered to a more disordered state. These changes contributed to the reduction in droplet size and the increase in apparent viscosity of soy milk, thereby enhancing its stability. Enzymolysis also altered the color parameters, increasing L⁎ while decreasing a⁎ and b⁎. Sensory evaluation results showed that thermal steaming in the early stage effectively declined the beany odor of raw soy milk, while enzymolysis further decreased the beany flavor compounds in soy milk. The GC–MS analysis results were consistent with the sensory evaluation. However, when papain addition exceeded 1.0 %, the beany odor in soy milk increased, suggesting that 1.0 % represents the optimal enzymatic hydrolysis level. Overall, papain enzymolysis following thermal steaming improved both the quality and flavor of soy milk. This approach provides a clean-label, scalable strategy to mitigate off-flavor and stability issues in soy milk, offering new theoretical insights for the design of plant-based emulsions. Future studies should focus on scaling up the process, performing a detailed cost–benefit analysis, and exploring the applicability of this combined treatment to other plant-based milk alternatives.
