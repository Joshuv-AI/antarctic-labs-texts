Weissella: A multifaceted genus making the way for next-gen biotherapeutics?
Authors: Mithil Bochgire, Samradni Pingale, Ruchika Kaul-Ghanekar, Kalpana Angadi, Ashwini Jadhav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quest for the next-generation probiotics has highlighted non-conventional lactic acid bacteria in recent decades, and among these, Weissella has been a genus exhibiting multifaceted health-benefiting characteristics. Weissella spp., such as Weissella cibaria, Weissella confusa, and Weissella koreensis, are traditionally found in fermented foods and other ecological niches, having encouraging probiotic properties such as robust survivability under gastrointestinal conditions and capability to modulate host health. This review focuses on various health-beneficial properties of Weissella strains, including their antimicrobial activities, chiefly through the production of organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, and bacteriocins, such as weissellicin, which suppress growth of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. Several studies have reported the anticancer properties of Weissella, where certain strains are known to induce apoptosis among cancer cell lines and exhibit anti-proliferative properties. Further, Weissella spp. secrete antioxidant compounds that can scavenge free radicals and inhibit oxidative stress, a crucial causal factor in chronic conditions. Weissella also exhibits hypocholesterolemic activity through the assimilation of cholesterol, resulting in the reduction of serum cholesterol, thus showing its application in cardiovascular health management as well. Looking forward, the development of Weissella-based biotherapeutic formulations offer exciting opportunities in preventive and therapeutic strategies for managing infections, metabolic disorders, inflammation, and cancer. Despite these promising attributes of Weissella genus, there are some safety concerns related to certain strains, which need to be carefully assessed before clinical usage. Furthermore, in vivo and clinical studies are warranted in future to elucidate the underlying mechanisms for establishing complete regulatory approval.Keywords: Weissella, Next-gen biotherapeutics, Antimicrobial activity, Anti-cancer activity, Cholesterol-lowering activity

Introduction

A healthy human gut is home to trillions of probiotic microorganisms that play a fundamental role in digestion, immune regulation, nutrient synthesis, and protection against pathogens. When this microbial balance is disrupted due to factors such as high antibiotic usage, poor diet, stress, illness, or environmental toxins, it can lead to the proliferation of harmful bacteria and a decline in beneficial microbes. Dysbiosis of the human gut microbiome is linked to various health-related problems, such as antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (AAD), Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), Crohn’s disease, metabolic and cardiovascular conditions and even colorectal cancer (Kim et al. 2019; Chandrasekaran et al. 2024). In the healthy women, the vaginal microbiome is dominated by Lactobacillus species (Krauss-Silva et al. 2014). Depletion of dominant vaginal LAB and an increase in pathogenic non-Lactobacillus species can trigger the development of bacterial vaginosis, vaginitis, and other chronic inflammatory and low immunomodulatory conditions, which may lead to the progression of HPV infection to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and further into invasive cervical carcinoma (ICC) (Chee et al. 2020; Kamble et al. 2022; Jahić et al. 2022).

In the recent years, probiotics have gained attention as beneficial microorganisms because of their power in modulating the gut microbiome and reducing blood cholesterol, lowering intestinal infections, competing with pathogenic organisms, strengthening the gut barrier function, improving immune responses and lactose utilization, and for the synthesis of beneficial metabolites (Amara and Shibl 2015). Probiotics have been implicated in the alleviation of several gastrointestinal disorders, including Inflammatory bowel syndrome (IBS), IBD, and diarrhoea (Rau et al. 2024). They hold key significance in the management of different disease conditions such as obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndrome (Dobriyal et al. 2024). Moreover, the probiotic microorganisms and their metabolites exhibit anti-cancer activities. Certain species of the lactic acid bacteria, including Bifidobacterium bifidum, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus casei, etc., have shown anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Choi et al. 2022; Saravanan et al. 2023). These bacteria generate short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, propionate, acetate, etc., that have exhibited antiproliferative activity against one or more of the cancers (Kechagia et al. 2013; Thananimit et al. 2022). Moreover, various bacteriocins such as salivaricin, nisin, enterocin and plantaricin have been purified from the species of genera Lactobacillus (Mekala and Hasnath-Ansari 2023). These bacteriocins have exhibited anti-microbial activity against several opportunistic pathogens, implicated in different infectious diseases (Mokoena 2017). Moreover, these probiotics were shown to exhibit anti-cancer activity. Recently, these probiotics were characterized for the purpose of increasing their effectiveness, while decreasing the side effects of chemotherapeutic drugs (Rodriguez-Arrastia et al. 2021; Waziri et al. 2022). Weissella, an unexplored genus should be investigated for probiotic and health-beneficial properties among all the other lactic acid bacteria.

Even though Weisella species exhibit the probiotic properties, all of them are not recognized under GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) (Fessard and Remize 2017). Few of the Weissella species have been reported to cause opportunistic infections in the immunocompromised patients. For instance, Weissella confusa, which has a functional role in the fermentation of food, could cause sepsis in the humans (Fusco et al. 2015, Azim et al. 2023). Thus, it is highly important consider the safety profile of Weissella, despite having probiotic or biotechnological applications. In the available National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) genome database, the genus Weissella revealed the presence of antibiotic resistant and virulent genes. For instance, tetracycline resistance genes, tet(M) and tet(S) were reported in few strains of Weissella cibaria, Weissella ceti and Weissella thailandensis (Abriouel et al. 2015). The genomic data also showed fosfomycin (MDT-FosB) and methicillin (MRP) resistance genes in some strains of Weissella koreensis and fosB gene in strains of W. ceti (Abriouel et al. 2015). On the other hand, vancomycin resistance has been reported for W. confusa LBAE C39-2, Weissella paramesenteroides ATCC 33313 and W. cibaria KACC 11862 (Abriouel et al. 2015). In some Weissella strains, adhesion-related genes (fbpA, mucBP), mucin-binding protein gene (mucBP), collagen adhesion (WS08_0071, WS08_0583) genes and Staphylococcal surface proteins were found to be responsible for the biofilm formation and virulence in the host (Abriouel et al. 2015).Therefore, it is essential to that such Weissella strains, which exhibit antimicrobial activity, should not have any virulence genes and/or horizontal resistant gene transfer. This would ensure that such Weissella strains could be suitable as probiotics for nutritional and therapeutic applications. Further research is warranted to understand the properties of different Weissella spp., so that their potential can be harnessed for probiotic application under clinical settings.

With the progression of sequencing techniques, genome sequencing data of different Weissella spp. have explored their genetic elements, potential functions, and comparative relationships with the other strains (Abriouel et al. 2015; Panthee et al. 2019; Yuan et al. 2021; Fanelli et al. 2022). The comparative genomics analysis can help to understand the diversity and ecological adaptation of Weissella. It can also demonstrate unique metabolic enzymes that increase fermentative performance, genomic stability and biosafety of the strains for future biotechnological applications. In the case of W. paramesenteroides WP12, genomic analyses has revealed its potential as a safe and effective probiotic candidate for food and probiotic applications (Chaichana et al. 2025). The enzymes (CAZymes) from WP12 were found to enhance fermentative performance of the strain. On the other hand, metabolomic profiling of postbiotics (bioactive compounds) produced by probiotic strains demonstrated presence of alkaloids, peptides, carbohydrates, and phenolic compounds of antimicrobial or other therapeutic values. In one of the studies (Tenea 2026), genomic and metabolomic analysis of UTNCys2-2 represented a strain with plasmid-free genome, enriched with functional carbohydrates, diverse biosynthetic potential and having genomic evidence for exopolysaccharide (EPS) biosynthesis. These findings have established W. confusa UTNCys2-2 as a biosafe, multifunctional strain with strong potential for applications in food fermentation and microbial biotechnology. Pan-genome analysis can further highlight the antimicrobial resistance genes, presence or absence of any virulence-associated genes, probiotic-associated traits, including stress tolerance, vitamin biosynthesis, and potential of strains as a reservoir for novel functional genes and bioactive metabolites (Chaichana et al. 2026). In a nutshell, the genome-based framework helps in screening rational and safe probiotic strains for biotechnological applications. Among different Weissella species, few have cleared all the parameters essential for becoming potential probiotic candidate. For instance, W. confusa (Lee 2004), Weissella kimchii (Lee 2005), W. thailandensis (Thamacharoensuk et al. 2017), W. cibaria (Oh and Lee 2021), and W. paramesenteroides (Amer et al. 2021) have been reported to exhibit probiotic properties, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities, reflecting their diverse therapeutic potential.

In short, this review covers the probiotic potential of different strains of Weissella species. An attempt has been made to analyze the current literature for elucidating the potential of Weissella genus in exhibiting anti-bacterial, anti-cancer, anti-oxidant and anti-cholesterol activities, along with focusing on the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic potential of the genus.

Weissella: an overview

Weissella is a genus of lactic acid bacteria that are Gram-positive, non-spore forming coccoid or short rod-shaped bacteria. This genus was earlier considered as a member of the family Leuconostocaceae, but later on reclassified into a distinguished genus ‘Weissella’ under the family Lactobacillaceae (Collins et al. 1993). Various species such as Leuconostoc paramesenteroides, Lactobacillus viridescens, Lactobacillus confusus, Lactobacillus kandleri, Lactobacillus minor, and Lactobacillus halotolerans, were reclassified under the genus Weissella. Identification of Weissella on the basis of colony morphology and biochemical test becomes difficult as it resembles other Lactobacillus or Streptococcus-like species. However,16s rRNA sequencing is the best technique to identify and distinguish Weissella from the other genera (Kamboj et al. 2015).

Recently, Weissella genus has become significant due to its asserted probiotic features and other health benefits (Fig. 1). It has found applications in food, medical and pharmaceutical industries (Kavitake et al. 2020). Certain species of Weissella have been reported to possess antimicrobial potential via production of bacteriocins, organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, and other functional compounds. (Singh et al. 2024).

On the other hand, some species such as W. confusa (Lee et al. 2012), W. cibaria (Lee et al. 2012), W. kimchii (Jin et al. 2007), W. viridescens (Silvester and Dicks 2003), and W. paramesenteroides (Rubio et al. 2014) were isolated from the humans (Fig. 2). These varied sources highlight the ecological diversity of the genus and its potential relevance in food fermentation and probiotic research.

Anti-bacterial activity of Weissella

Several studies have evaluated the impact of probiotics against distinct multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, particularly, the probiotic and anti-bacterial potential of some Lactobacillus species (Abdul-Rahim et al. 2021; Moon et al. 2022; Qian et al. 2020; Xiu et al. 2020; Layus et al. 2020). Besides this, Weissella species such as W. confusa, W. cibaria, W. parameteroides, etc., have also been evaluated for their probiotic and anti-bacterial activities. In an exclusive in vitro study, cell-free supernatant (CFS) of dairy isolate W. confusa K3 inhibited the growth of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing uropathogenic Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa by suppressing the blaTEM (β-lactamase) gene, however, the bioactive compounds in the CFS were not identified (El-Mekkawy et al. 2023). CFS of W. confusa exhibited inhibitory effects against E. coli, assessed by agar well-diffusion assay (Keeratikunakorn et al. 2023). In another study, W. confusa DD_A7, isolated from Kimchi, showed antibacterial activity against ESBL E. coli (Dey et al. 2019).

W. confusa MD1 and W. cibaria MD2, were observed to adhere to the mucosal surfaces and intestinal epithelial cells, HT-29. These strains were non-hemolytic, non-toxic and sensitive to antibiotics, hence safe for use. However, the major limitations include assessing their antibacterial activity only against the food pathogens, lack of information regarding their bioactive compounds and in vivo studies (Lakra et al. 2020). W. cibaria P-8, isolated from the pickle, exhibited significant in vitro antibacterial activity against the intestinal pathogens, with maximum efficacy against multidrug-resistant, E. coli B2. The study showed that W. cibaria P-8 reduced intestinal inflammation mediated by MDR E. coli B2 infection by regulating inflammatory cytokines (Liu et al. 2024). W. cibaria P-8 was also reported to colonize within the intestines and increase the abundance of several beneficial gut microbes, particularly Prevotella (Liu et al. 2024). W. confusa Cys2-2, isolated from ginger, produced a bacteriocin that showed activity against laboratory and American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) strains of Salmonella, Shigella, E. coli and several Gram-negative enteric bacteria. However, the study is limited only to in vitro experiments, and lacks in vivo studies and data on the effect of Cys2-2 on clinical MDR pathogens (Tenea and Lara 2019).

W. confusa DD_A7, isolated from kimchi, displayed antimicrobial activity via the induction of oxidative stress and thus, suppressed the development of pathogens (Dey et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2023). The supernatant of W. confusa UPM22MT04, isolated from a wastewater treatment plant, was used to produce zinc oxide nanoparticles, which showed broad-spectrum bactericidal and antimicrobial activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) ATCC 700699 and ATCC 33742 strains, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 0.625 mg/ml and 1.25 mg/ml, respectively (AL-Tameemi et al. 2023).

Antibacterial activity of LAB strains, W. confusa AI10, Limosilactobacillus fermentum AI2, and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum 86 were assayed against clinical pathogens, E. coli NG50212 and S. aureus AY507047, for their safety in food industries (Shah et al. 2016). Weissella spp. also exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. SNUL2, isolated from the Korean food Sikhae, displayed significant inhibitory activity against 60 different bacterial strains, mostly food-borne pathogens. The observed antimicrobial activity was potentially induced by the presence of multiple proteins of the C₃₉ family, which include peptidoglycan endopeptidases, and N-acetylmuramoyl-l-alanine amidase-type protein precursors (Han et al. 2024). Recently, W. confusa WIKIM51 was found to exhibit distinct anti-fungal activities against Candida albicans infected vaginal epithelial cells (VECs), by inhibiting the growth of C albicans and reducing anti-inflammatory cytokines, the results being comparable with the known probiotics, Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus rhamnosus (Lee et al. 2024). Besides, Weissella sp. have been proven to be useful in stimulating vaginal health by blocking the action of pathogens (including, C. albicans and E. coli), producing hydrogen peroxide, adhering to vaginal epithelial cells and creating a protective microbiological barrier (Lee 2005). W. confusa PL9001 was reported to inhibit the growth and proliferation of genitourinary pathogens such as C. albicans, E. coli, S. aureus, and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium through co-aggregation with these four pathogens, while it also inhibited the adherence of pathogens to T24 bladder epithelial cells (Lee 2004). Another strain, W. confusa GCC_19R1 displayed antibacterial activities against Gram-negative bacteria Cedecea davisae, Acinetobacter johnsonii, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Achromobacter spanius (Nath et al. 2021). Crude exopolysaccharide (EPS) derived from W. cibaria EIR/P-2, showed strong antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity against Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175 (Kibar et al. 2020). Among the known Weissella species, W. confusa produced significant amount of antimicrobial EPS (Teixeira et al. 2021). Though various studies have reported promising antibacterial activity of different Weissella strains at in vitro level, they lack major data on antibiogram of the selected strains, non-hemolytic activity, identification and characterization of bioactive compounds and animal model validation.

Besides EPS, other antimicrobial compounds such as bacteriocins are also produced by some Weissella strains. Bacteriocins are ribosomally synthesized peptides, mainly classified (Sing et al. 2024) into Class I heat stable lantibiotics (molecular weight, mw < 5 kDa), Class II (mw < 10 kDa), Class III thermolabile (mw > 30 kDa), and Class IV (mostly cyclic peptides covalently linked to N and C termini) bacteriocins. Further, Class I bacteriocins are classified into Class Ia (lantibiotics), Class Ib (labyrinthopeptins), and Class Ic (sanctibiotics). Class II are further categorised into Class IIa (pediocin-like bacteriocins), Class IIb (having two-chain peptides) and Class IIc (single-chain peptides).

Many bacteriocins such as lactacin, leucocin, pediocin, and enterocins have been reported mostly from Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus and Enterococcus species, respectively, for their different functional activities. Other than these species, bacteriocins from different Weissella species have also been reported in the literature (Singh et al. 2024). Different bacteriocins produced by LAB with their reported characteristics

Two novel purified bacteriocins, namely bacteriocin 7293A and bacteriocin 7293B, isolated from W. hellenica BCC 7293, exhibited broad spectrum antimicrobial activity against food-borne pathogens (Table 1). Both the bacteriocins significantly inhibited the growth of Gram-positive bacteria such as Bacillus circulans JCM 2504 T, Enterococcus faecalis JCM 5803 T, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 19115; and Gram-negative bacteria such as Aeromonas hydrophila B1 AhB1, E. coli ATCC 25922, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and Salmonella typhimurium DMST 0562 (Woraprayote et al. 2015). A thermostable weissellicin D bacteriocin, isolated from W. hellenica D1501, inhibited the growth of various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, such as Lactobacillus spp., E. coli OP50, B. cereus ATCC 11778, B. subtilis ATCC 6633, P. aeruginosa PAO1, Listeria monocytogenes CMCC 54004, along with fungi and molds such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae ATCC 26603, Debaryomyces hansenii ATCC 4143, and C. albicans CMCC 28001 (Chen et al. 2014a, b). Other bacteriocins produced by Weissella spp. include weissellin A from W. paramesenteroides DX (Papagianni and Papamichael 2011); weissellicin 110 from W. cibaria 110 (Srionnual et al. 2007); weissellicin M and weissellicin Y, both from W. hellenica QU13 (Masuda et al. 2012); and weissellicin L from W. hellenica 4–7 (Leong et al. 2013). Although these bacteriocins are well-characterized, thermostable and have antibacterial and antifungal activities against various food-borne pathogens, further studies are warranted in the animal models as well as against clinical pathogens to prove them as candidate biotherapeutic drugs.

The mode of action for bacteriocins is not fully-understood. However, it is anticipated that Weissella- or non-Weissella derived- bacteriocins inhibited pathogens mainly by ion-permeable pore formation in the cell wall, resulting into membrane disruption. Thus, leakage of intracellular K+ ions, DNA, and RNA results into dissipation of energy gradients, leading to the cell death (Zacharof and Lovitt 2012; Malik et al. 2021). Weissellicin LM85 induced K+ ion efflux by pore formation from M. luteus MTCC106 cells; Bacteriocins 7293A, and B recovered from Weissella strains inhibited the cell growth of pathogens by creating hydrophilic regions in cytoplasmic membrane of the target cells, and thus, essential cellular molecules get effluxed, leading to the cell death (Papagianni and Papamichael 2011, Woraprayote et al. 2015).The purified Weissellicin LM85 obtained from Weissella confusa LM85, was reported to exhibit multifaceted antibacterial activity against pathogens such as Micrococcus luteus MTCC106, and Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC13311. It inhibited the growth of the pathogens through creation of a pH gradient, disruption of cell membrane integrity, leakage of potassium ions, cytoplasmic contents, genomic DNA fragmentation, and morphological alterations in the target cells. These findings highlight weissellicin LM85 as a potential natural antimicrobial bacteriocin in controlling the pathogenic infections. Depending on the size and mode of action, all the bacteriocins can be either bactericidal (lethal), or bacteriostatic (inhibit growth) (Nes et al. 2007).

Anti-proliferative activity of Weissella

Various studies have investigated the probiotic potential and anticancer activities of different Weissella species. A pharmaceutical formulation comprising of WIKIM28 (a novel strain of W. cibaria), an active ingredient, isolated from a Korean food, Kimchi, was patented owing to a notable reduction in tumor size in bladder cancer, breast cancer, melanoma, and thyroid cancer, amongst many others (Chin 2021). It significantly reduced CT26 induced colorectal cancer in mice by 2.3 times in 16 days. Similarly, MC38-induced colorectal cancer in mice was reduced by 16.3 times in 12 days, post-treatment with W. cibaria WIKIM28 (Choi and Yun 2019).

W. cibaria and L. plantarum have been reported to prevent cancer by reducing inflammatory cytokines, inhibiting expression of carcinogenic enzymes and immune modulation (Kwak et al. 2014). EPS isolated from W. cibaria JW15 from Kimchi, enhanced Natural Killer (NK) cell activity in C57BL/6 mice by upregulating CD69 marker (Oh and Lee 2021). In the clinical study, it was reported that daily consumption of W. cibari JW15 in non-diabetic individuals, upregulated the cytotoxicity of NK cells, which is crucial for preventing cancer (Lee et al. 2018). One more study reported that the EPS isolated from W. cibaria D-2, exhibited anti-cancer activity in the colorectal cancer cells via apoptosis. EPS (from D-2) inhibited colorectal cancer cell lines, SW480 and HT26, through the cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 phase and was non-toxic to the primary cell line, NCM460, even at higher concentrations. EPS-D2 also promoted apoptosis significantly in SW480 and HT26, through Fas/FasL mediated pathway in HT26 cell line. This mechanism was further validated in vivo in HT26 xenograft nude mice, wherein EPS-D2 inhibited tumor growth with no side effects on the body weights and vital organs in the mice (Du et al. 2023). In another study, CFS from W. cibaria p3B showed anticancer activity against cervical cancer cell line, HeLa and colorectal cancer cell line, Caco-2, and was non-toxic to the primary human intestinal cell line, INT-407 (Ankaiah et al. 2021). Other species of Weissella, such as W. thailandensis PC79-5, W. thailandensis KC81-2, W. cibaria PC86-2, W. confusa MSMC57-2, and W. paramesenteroides MSMC63-2, induced interleukin-12 (IL-12) in macrophage J774A.1 cell lines, important for the killing of the cancer cells (Thamacharoensuk et al. 2017). W. cibaria MED17 exopolysaccharide (EPS) was also found to be effective in healing wounds and repairing DNA damage in Caco-2 cells (Kahraman-Ilikkan et al. 2024). W. confusa J4-1 showed anti-cancer activity against the colorectal cancer cell line, HCT-29, by causing cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase through p53-p21 axis (Liu et al. 2023). W. confusa 163.P5.8 induced apoptosis to suppress the growth of Caco-2 cells, but in-depth mechanisms at in vitro and in vivo levels need to be evaluated (Er et al. 2015). Furthermore, W. cibaria FB069, isolated from synbiotic fermented soymilk, inhibited the proliferation of Caco-2 and HCT-29 colorectal cell lines, by downregulating the transcription of essential markers associated with inflammation (Le et al. 2020). Nanoparticles isolated from EPS of W. paramesenteroides MN2C2, were also found to be effective against Caco-2 (colorectal), HepG-2 (liver), and MCF-7 (breast) cancer cell lines (Amer et al. 2021). L-asparaginase, isolated from W. paramesenteroides MN2C2, was evaluated for its anticancer and antioxidant activities against HepG-2, MCF-7, and lung cancer cell line, A549 (Amer et al. 2021). W. cibaria MW01, isolated from Chinese-sauerkraut, reduced the production of Tumour Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-6, and IL-8 due to lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory stimuli in Caco-2 cell lines. It also restored the integrity of Caco-2 cell monolayer that had been subjected to LPS treatment. It further prevented the disruption of the function of intestinal barrier epithelia by modulating inflammatory response and tight junction protein expression through NF-κB-mediated MLCK-pMLC pathway (Huang et al. 2020).

In India, there are limited studies on anti-cancer activity of Weissella species. However, one study reported upregulation of Bad, cytochrome-c, caspase-3, Bcl2 and BAX genes in colon cancer cell line, HT-29, and immortalised human embryonic kidney, HEK-293 cells, in response to the CFS from W. cibaria RK-3-1. The bioactive compounds identified in the CFS included filicinic acid, dibutyl phthalate, and 4H-pyran-4-one,2,3-dihydro-3,5-dihydroxy-6-methyl, and showed significant anti-cancer activity in silico in molecular docking studies (Manoharan et al. 2024). The CFS inhibited the proliferation of HT-29, with IC50 of 22.5 ± 1.3 μg/ml, and did not affect the proliferation of HEK-293 cell line (Manoharan et al. 2024). In another study, the probiotic potential of EPS from W. paramesenteroides MYPS5.1 and its stable interaction with cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), by computational studies (Yadav et al. 2022) was reported. A significant increase in the population of Weissella was reported in the faecal samples, during 8-weeks period of probiotic treatment in 30 different cancer (9 colorectal, 9 breast, 1 pancreatic and 1 gastric cancer) patients with post-operative stages and was correlated with before treatment (at the time of admission), showing improvement in the quality of life of the patients (Kwon et al. 2021). More recent studies have reported a 57.25% increase in apoptosis along with 16.92% necroptosis by CFS of W. confusa in HT29 cell line at a concentration of 50 µg/ml (Sreelakshmi et al. 2025). Although many studies have shown significant anti-proliferative potential of different Weissella species, they are mostly restricted to in vitro investigation, thereby highlighting its need to be further explored and validated in appropriate animal models. Moreover, few studies have reported pathogenicity of Weissella species, showing statistically increased population in the lung cancer patients (p = 0.009), suggesting their possible involvement in lung cancer progression (Zhuo et al. 2020).Thus, in-depth studies need to be conducted to elucidate the safety of Weissella spp. at in vivo and clinical level.

Anti-oxidant activity of Weissella

Antioxidants are compounds that slow down or prevent oxidation by preventing the formation of harmful free radicals, hydrogen peroxide and other peroxides. Radicals generated from oxygen are extremely reactive and can harm biomolecules, resulting in the cell damage and death (Chandimali et al. 2025). Our body is safeguarded by natural antioxidants such as glutathione, ascorbic acid, and tocopherol, as well as enzymes such as superoxide dismutase. Synthetic antioxidants, including butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and propyl gallate, are effective but may lead to adverse effects such as liver damage and potential cancer risk (Kakade et al. 2024). Consequently, natural antioxidants, including exopolysaccharides (EPS) derived from LAB, are being recognized as safer options with negligible toxicity and possible health advantages (Adebayo-Tayo et al. 2018). Several Weissella species were reported to produce EPS with antioxidant properties that could offer applications in food and therapeutic fields. Crude EPS, derived from W. cibaria EIR/P-2, exhibited anti-oxidant property by scavenging free radicals (Kibar et al. 2020).W. confusa MD1 and W. cibaria MD2 have significant 1,1-diphenyl-2- picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging activity, 69.15% and 74.34% respectively, as well as inhibition of lipid peroxidation activity, 64.14% and 70.04%, respectively (Lakra et al. 2020). W. cibaria MTCC 5948 showed significant potential in scavenging free radicals by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Unnikrishnan and Sreeja 2025). W. confusa significantly scavenged free radicals (p < 0.05), which was confirmed by DPPH and 2, 2-Azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate) (ABTS) methods, when compared to ascorbic acid that was used as a positive control (Sreelakshmi et al. 2025). W. cibaria JW15 showed radical scavenging activity between 23.53 and 60.85% along with inhibition of lipid peroxidation by 33.31% when compared to Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) (Yu et al. 2018). W. cibaria DMA 18 (isolated from tender coconut) and crude EPS extracted from it, showed promising anti-oxidant properties of 27% and 33.55%, respectively (Amrutha et al. 2019). Galactan EPS, produced by W. confusa KR780676, exhibited promising anti-oxidant and anti-ageing activities as well as decreased ROS, which was confirmed by in vitro experiments and by yeast-based in vivo model, using S. cerevisiae (wild type and antioxidant mutant model) (Kavitake et al. 2022). The study showed that the galactan protected the yeast anti-oxidant mutants against the extracellular oxidative stress, generated by the hydrogen peroxide. Nine strains of Weissella spp. were studied for anti-oxidant activity (Liu et al. 2025) using DPPH and ABTS, of which two strains of W. confusa 91–3 and W. confusa 91–5, exhibited higher radical scavenging activity than the reference strain L. rhamnosus GG (LGG) and positive control Trolox in ABTS assay. The other five strains, W. viridescens 38–3, W. paramesenteroides 16–2, and W. confusa (69–3, 92–2 and 106–5) showed nearly similar results in the ABTS assay. On the other hand, in the DPPH assay, all the strains showed more than 50% radical scavenging activity, where the positive control Trolox had activity of 42.15% at 200 μg/ml, and the activity of LGG was 67.68%, which was very close to W. paramesenteroides 16–2 (61.76%) (Liu et al. 2025).

Anti-cholesterol activity of Weissella

High cholesterol level in the blood has been linked to a number of health problems, such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease (Khil et al. 2023). Various studies have shown that consumption of food containing probiotics such as Lactobacillus or Bifidobacterium can lower the cholesterol levels (Rerksuppaphol and Rerksuppaphol 2015). Weissella spp. also exhibit anti-cholesterol activity through various mechanisms, such as assimilation or binding to cholesterol. W. cibaria MTCC 5948 was reported to assimilate cholesterol at 53.11% in vitro (Unnikrishnan and Sreeja 2025). W. confusa MD1 and W. cibaria MD2 have shown promising cholesterol-reducing activity of 67.11% and 78%, respectively (Lakra et al. 2020). W. halotolerans F99, isolated from camel faeces showed significant anti-cholesterol activity, when assessed in vitro and in Wistar rats that were fed on high-fat diet. W. halotolerans F99 significantly reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides and low-density lipoproteins in rats (Fhoula et al. 2018). In another study (Liu et al. 2025), W. viridescens 131–3 exhibited the highest cholesterol removal rate of 25.53%, compared to the other nine isolated Weissella strains, suggesting its potential as a cholesterol-lowering candidate drug. W. cibaria SCCB2306 was reported to reduce cholesterol by 78% in liquid media containing cholesterol (Daliri et al. 2022). Based upon the available studies, it can be inferred that Weissella spp. exhibited strong anti-cholesterol activity that could help in the mitigation of hypercholesterolemia.

Underexplored properties of Weissella spp.

Besides the above-mentioned properties of Weisella spp., there are some underexplored activities that need to be mentioned here. W. koreensis OK1-6 was studied for anti-obesity activity in C57BL/6 J mice, fed on different diets for 12 weeks, including high fat diet. The mice, fed on a high fat diet, containing kimchi fermented with W. koreensis OK1-6, showed reduced levels of fatty acid synthase, leptin and insulin in the serum, along with decreased mRNA expression of liver X receptor α (LXR-α), sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2), Stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD-1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ) and carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT-1) (Park et al. 2012). Four LAB from human origin were assessed for anti-obesity activity in C57BL/6 J obese mice, fed on the high fat diet for 8 weeks. Out of four, W. cibaria MG5285 was reported to possess the highest anti-obesity activity by modulating the AMPK/ACC pathway. It also prevented fatty liver disease by preventing weight gain, and enhancing tolerance to glucose and lipid metabolism (Choi et al. 2021). Soymilk yoghurt, with W. confusa TOKAI 2, was reported to exhibit the highest inhibition of α-amylase compared to other 12 LAB, and, therefore, exhibited anti-diabetic activity, however, further animal studies are required for proving the claims (Nakashima et al. 2022). W. paramesenteroides WpK4 reduced anxiety and depression-like behaviour in stressed C57BL/6 J female mice, by lowering the levels of inflammatory cytokines, IL-1b and IL-6, in the hippocampus via gut-brain axis modulation (Sandes et al. 2020). Pre-treatment of W. confusa F213, for 14 days, reduced the severity of the symptoms, such as weight loss, diarrhoea and rectal bleeding, in Wistar male rats induced with Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS). It also minimised the lesions and infiltration of pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, along with improving mucosal integrity, and hence could play a major role in mitigating colitis (Sujaya et al. 2023). In human randomised control trial, consumption of W. cibaria CMS1 for 12 days, increased salivary immunoglobulin A significantly, while decreasing TNF-α. This study also reported that W. confusa modulated beta diversity by increasing the presence of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, while decreasing the presence of Bacteroidetes and Fusobacteria. Along with this, Lactobacillales, Bacilli, and Streptococcaceae were also found to be increased significantly (Park et al. 2024).

Numerous studies have been carried out to investigate the next-generation probiotics (NGPs), such as Akkermansia muciniphila and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii species. Although many of these NGPs have been of interest lately for the treatment and prevention of many chronic diseases, human trials are still limited. Therefore, regulatory approvals of these microbes is not available . Moreover, certain limitations restrict their use in the market, such as the lack of proper procedures to store and culture such oxygen-sensitive microbes (Al-Fakhrany et al. 2024). Conversely, several Weissella species, such as W. confusa, W. cibaria, W. paramesenteroides, and W. halotolerans, are being studied as new probiotic candidates, which have shown various health benefits at pre-clinical and clinical levels. Therefore, the new strains of Weissella, could also be included in the next-generation probiotics, and the genus Weissella could be considered as the next-generation biotherapeutics after thoroughly scrutinizing its safety in animals as well as humans.

Conclusion

Weissella spp. have excellent beneficial activities such as antimicrobial, cholesterol-lowering and antiproliferative, with potential of producing therapeutic bioactive compounds. Based upon the growing scientific evidence of probiotic therapeutic potential of Weissella spp, it stands out as a promising and emerging candidate in the search for next-generation biotherapeutics. Pre-clinical studies have so far been gratifying, although further in-depth work is required to deal with the existing challenge of establishing Weissella as a safe and efficacious adjunct in probiotic therapy.

Other challenges that need to be addressed include, selecting and optimizing the potential strains, standardizing dosage regimens, and elucidating their effects on the host microbiota. Large clinical trials are warranted to prove the efficacy and safety of Weissella, and its synergistic activity should also be evaluated in combination with conventional therapies. Addressing these issues will be critical for translating Weisella-based interventions from bench to bedside.
