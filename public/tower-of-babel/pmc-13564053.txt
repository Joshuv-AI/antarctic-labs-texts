Unresolving pneumonia in type 2 diabetes, think fungus: A case report of polymicrobial lung disease
Authors: Bob Rolex Opio, Benet Tumusime Kambale, Beatrice Achan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolymicrobial pneumonia caused by multiple pathogens is increasingly recognized in immune compromised individuals with comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The immunocompromised state associated with T2DM predisposes patients to polymicrobial opportunistic infections, including multidrug-resistant bacteria and fungi. Prompt laboratory diagnosis and organism-specific treatment are critical for optimal outcomes.Case presentationWe report the case of a 53-year-old male with newly diagnosed T2DM who presented with a three-month history of left-sided chest pain, dry cough, dyspnoea, and persistent fever. Initial empirical antibiotic therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone was ineffective. Computed tomography (CT) with contrast imaging revealed a massive loculated left pleural effusion with compressive atelectasis. Analysis of microbiological culture and sensitivity results revealed a polymicrobial infection involving multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp with inducible AmpC resistance, and fluconazole-susceptible dose dependent Candida glabrata. Based on the laboratory results, intravenous meropenem, high dose antifungal therapy, and subsequent oral fluconazole were recommended and led to successful treatment. Glycaemic control was concurrently optimized with insulin therapy and initial random blood sugar levels were 16.7 mmole per l, and at discharge was 9.3 mmoles per l.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the importance of considering polymicrobial infections in diabetic patients presenting with unresolving pneumonia. Early use of imaging and microbiological analysis of the pleural fluid enabled prompt identification of the polymicrobial causative organisms, guiding tailored antimicrobial therapy. Multidisciplinary management is essential in improving outcomes in such complex cases.

Introduction

Polymicrobial pneumonia is defined as pneumonia due to more than one pathogen, such as bacterial, fungal, and viral organisms [1]. It is increasingly recognized as a cause of severe pneumonia, especially in individuals with underlying comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus [1]. Diabetes mellitus type 2 (T2DM) is associated with impaired immune function, including decreased neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis, placing patients at a higher risk of both typical and opportunistic infections [2].

Klebsiella pneumoniae and other Enterobacteriaceae are frequently implicated in hospital- and community-acquired pneumonia in diabetic patients and can cause rapidly progressing disease due to their virulence and antibiotic resistance mechanisms [3]. Similarly, Candida glabrata is an emerging pathogenic yeast commonly found in patients with diabetes mellitus associated with reduced antifungal susceptibility. C. glabrata infections are difficult to treat and are often resistant to many azole antifungal agents, especially fluconazole [4].

This case report highlights a rare presentation of polymicrobial pneumonia in a patient with newly diagnosed T2DM involving Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp., and Candida glabrata. The complexity of diagnosis and management in such settings highlights the importance of timely imaging, microbiological evaluation, and tailored antimicrobial therapy

Case presentation

Case history

A 53-year-old male newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was referred from a regional referral hospital for tertiary care with a provisional diagnosis of suspected pulmonary malignancy. He had a three-month history of left-sided chest pain and a dry cough, without haemoptysis. He was unresponsive to empirical ceftriaxone, administered before referral. His condition progressively worsened with persistent fevers and dyspnoea. He was a social drinker with no history of smoking.

Examination

On examination, he appeared ill and was receiving oxygen at 5 L/min. He was febrile (38.1 °C), tachycardic (114 bpm), normotensive (BP 132/87 mmHg), and had a respiratory rate of 24 bpm, consistent with tachypnoea. There were reduced breath sounds and stony dull percussion note over the left lung fields. There was no oral thrush, jaundice, lymphadenopathy, or peripheral oedema. Findings of other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Methods

Differential diagnosis

The patient’s presentation with chronic left-sided chest pain, dry cough, persistent fever, and progressive respiratory symptoms raised several possible differential diagnoses. Pulmonary tuberculosis was a major consideration due to the chronic cough, chest pain, fever, and poor response to routine antibiotics like ceftriaxone. Empyema thoracis or a complicated parapneumonic effusion due to fever, dyspnoea, reduced breath sounds, and stony dullness on percussion. Pulmonary fungal infections were considered, particularly in the context of newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus, which may predispose individuals to opportunistic pulmonary mycoses, and symptoms worsening despite antibiotic therapy. Lung malignancy was also considered, given Age > 50 years, chronic symptoms, chest pain, dry cough, and unresolving symptoms despite antibiotics. Dullness to percussion and decreased breath sounds raised a possibility of malignant pleural effusion, although non-smoker status made this less likely (especially adenocarcinoma).

Investigations

Random blood sugar was 16.7 mmol/L, and TB-gene Xpert results were negative. A contrasted chest CT revealed massive loculated left pleural effusion with compressive atelectasis of the left lung lobes, small right pleural effusion, patchy consolidations in the right lower and middle lobes, but no evidence of intrathoracic mass (Fig. 1). Pleural fluid aspirate microscopic analysis; revealed 4 + inflammatory cells, 3 + budding yeast cells, 3 + Gram-positive cocci in chains, and 2 + Gram-negative rods on Gram-staining (Fig. 2), no acid-fast bacilli were seen on ZN stain, and no cells suggestive of malignancy were seen on cytological analysis. Ultrasound-guided thoracentesis yielded thick foul-smelling purulent pleural fluid consistent with empyema. According to institutional laboratory standard operating procedures, differential cell counts were not performed because the specimen was grossly purulent. Pleural fluid biochemical analysis (protein, glucose and lactate dehydrogenase) was likewise unavailable because these investigations were not routinely performed. Microbiological culture was therefore prioritized to guide definitive therapy.

The culture and sensitivity results confirmed Klebsiella pneumoniae multidrug-resistant (MDR), resistant to cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones but sensitive to meropenem. Treatment

On day one, the patient had presented to the regional hospital and was initiated empirically on Intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g once daily for seven days with no improvement, and later referred for suspected lung cancer work up. Following the CT scan results at the tertiary hospital, the patient was started on IV Piperacillin and tazobactam injection 4.5 g every 8 h for 7 days and oral Azithromycin 500 mg daily for seven days before the thoracocentesis and pleural fluid analysis. Insulin was given to correct the blood sugar to normal. After the pleural fluid analysis, the recommended treatment for the patient was intravenous meropenem (extended infusion every 8 h) 1 g for 5 days, intravenous high dose fluconazole for 14 days, then transitioned to high-dose oral fluconazole for 4 weeks.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing polymicrobial pneumonia in the setting of newly diagnosed T2DM [5]. The patient presented with non-resolving pneumonia despite broad-spectrum antibiotics, raising suspicion for atypical pathogens or mixed infections, both scenarios which are likely in individuals with impaired immunity [5]. Polymicrobial pneumonia is often underdiagnosed due to the limitations of routine culture techniques and the assumption that a single pathogen is responsible for the clinical picture [6]. In this case, the finding of K. pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp and C. glabrata highlights the need for a high index of suspicion of polymicrobial infection in at-risk populations [1]. The polymicrobial infection observed in this patient was likely driven by the combined effects of poorly controlled newly diagnosed T2DM, prolonged illness, delayed diagnosis, and initial empirical antibiotic therapy [5]. Severe hyperglycaemia (random blood glucose 16.7 mmol/l) likely impaired neutrophil function and other innate immune responses, predisposing the patient to both multidrug resistant bacterial infection and opportunistic Candida glabrata [5]. Furthermore, one week of ceftriaxone therapy without antifungal coverage may have altered the respiratory microbiota and exerted selective antimicrobial pressure, favouring persistence of multidrug resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, Inducible AmpC producing Enterobacter spp and Candida glabrata [7]. The initial suspicion of lung malignancy further delayed definitive microbiological diagnosis and targeted treatment, allowing progression to empyema [8]. Clinical recovery following culture guided antimicrobial therapy and optimization of glycaemic control with insulin highlights the importance of early microbiological investigation and metabolic control in diabetic patients with non-resolving pneumonia [9]. Diabetes alters the respiratory tract microbiome, promoting colonization and infection with resistant pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterobacter spp [10]. Moreover, hyperglycaemia impairs host immune responses and promotes fungal growth, thereby facilitating the pathogenicity of Candida glabrata, which is increasingly recognized for its multidrug resistance and ability to cause invasive infections [7].

Poor glycaemic control contributed substantially to susceptibility to infection [5]. The admission random blood glucose of 16.7 mmol/L reflected severe metabolic dysregulation, which is known to impair innate and adaptive immune response [5]. Following commencement of insulin therapy alongside culture-directed antimicrobial treatment, blood glucose improved to 9.3 mmol/l at discharge with associated good clinical recovery [5]. Although causality can not be inferred from a single case, optimization of glycaemic control likely enhanced host-immune function and complemented antimicrobial therapy [5].

Imaging, particularly CT scanning, was crucial in identifying a massive loculated pleural effusion and consolidations suggestive of empyema. This guided further diagnostic steps, including thoracentesis and pleural fluid analysis. Gram staining and cultures helped identify the polymicrobial aetiology and informed the escalation to appropriate antimicrobial therapy. The identification of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Klebsiella and AmpC-producing Enterobacter spp. necessitated the use of carbapenems, in line with the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines [11].

Treatment of C. glabrata is particularly challenging due to its reduced susceptibility to fluconazole and inherent resistance to amphotericin B. Echinocandins are the first-line agents in such scenarios, especially in immunocompromised host [7]. In this case, intravenous echinocandin followed by high-dose fluconazole was used, adhering to standard antifungal stewardship principles.

Collectively, this report underscores the importance of considering polymicrobial infections in immunocompromised patients presenting with unresolving pneumonia and highlights the need for multidisciplinary approaches involving imaging, microbiology, and individualized antimicrobial regimens.

Conclusion

These findings suggest a polymicrobial pneumoniae caused by antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp and C. glabrata with reduced susceptibility to fluconazole in a patient with immunosuppressive T2DM.

Unique Clinical Challenges

This case presented several unique antimicrobial stewardship, diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that distinguish it from routine reports of polymicrobial pneumonia. Initially, the patient presented with constitutional symptoms and radiological findings suggestive of lung malignancy, prompting referral to a tertiary center for lung cancer work up. This delayed definitive microbiological investigation and targeted antimicrobial therapy. Secondly, empirical antibiotic therapy alone failed because it did not provide coverage against the underlying multidrug-resistant pathogens. Thirdly, despite marked hyperglycaemia, the patient’s diabetes was initially managed using oral hypoglycaemic agents during an active severe pulmonary infection, whereas insulin-based therapy is generally preferred in acutely ill patients because of superior glycaemic control and improved immune function. Recognition of these overlapping challenges ultimately required coordinated multidisciplinary management involving microbiology, internal medicine, radiology and pulmonology.
