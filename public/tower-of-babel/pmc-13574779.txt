Myeloid cell replacement induces intercellular mitochondrial transfer and restores metabolism in a mouse model of mitochondrial disease
Authors: Hyunmin Cho, Ruhi Sayana, Abhishek Koladiya, Pasqualina Colella, Sangkyun Cho, James W S Jahng, Juan Jose Vasquez, Jessica Arozqueta Basurto, Joseph C Wu, Natalia Gomez-Ospina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFriedreich’s ataxia (FA) is a mitochondrial disease caused by frataxin deficiency that leads to progressive neurodegeneration and cardiomyopathy. Effective disease-modifying therapies remain limited. Here we show that myeloid cell replacement promotes neurological and cardiac recovery in FA mice through intercellular mitochondrial transfer. Donor-derived mitochondria are transferred from microglia and macrophages to central nervous system cells and cardiomyocytes, increasing oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis gene expression and mitochondrial protein abundance. These molecular changes are accompanied by improved survival and growth in male and female mice and enhanced spontaneous locomotion, strength, coordination and cardiac and function in female mice. In cultured cells, mitochondrial transfer requires direct cell-cell contact and partially restores respiratory capacity in frataxin-deficient recipient cells, which exhibit enhanced mitochondrial uptake, suggesting disease-specific mechanisms that promote mitochondrial acquisition or retention. These findings identify mitochondrial transfer as a mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of myeloid cell replacement and support hematopoietic transplantation for FA and other mitochondrial disorders.Subject terms: Haematopoietic stem cells, Development of the nervous system, Regenerative medicine

Introduction

Friedreich’s ataxia (FA) is a multi-systemic disorder characterized by slowly progressive ataxia with onset usually before age 25 years. About two-thirds of people with FA develop cardiomyopathy, up to 30% diabetes, and around 25% show atypical clinical features1,2. The disease is caused by a deficiency in frataxin (FXN) due to biallelic pathogenic variants in the FXN gene. FA’s most common pathogenic variant is an expanded GAA repeat in intron 1 of FXN3. The GAA repeats lead to promoter silencing via heterochromatin formation, thereby reducing FXN expression. GAA repeat length contributes to disease severity, with lengths greater than 66 being disease-causing, and most expanded alleles in affected individuals range from 600 to 1200 repeats3–5.

FA is fundamentally a mitochondrial disease. FXN is an essential protein enriched in mitochondria with its highest expression in dorsal root ganglia (DRG), spinal cord, cerebellar dentate nuclei, cerebral cortex, pancreas, heart, liver, and skeletal muscle, reflecting the affected organs in FA6,7. FXN is involved in the assembly of mitochondrial iron-sulfur clusters, which are cofactors for Krebs cycle proteins and multiple respiratory complexes, and are also essential co-factors for cytosolic and nuclear proteins. FXN deficiency results in abnormal accumulation of intra-mitochondrial iron, defective mitochondrial respiration, and overproduction of oxygen free radicals, resulting in cellular damage8. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondria function and reducing oxidative stress have been a big focus in this disease.

The manifestations of FA in the nervous system are primarily attributed to neurodegeneration, but neuroinflammation also plays a role9. In the CNS, activated microglia are the primary producers of reactive oxygen species and appear activated in brain regions implicated in FA neuropathology10–13. Multiple studies have identified defects in FXN-deficient microglia, including increased phagocytic activity, heightened inflammatory responses, and loss of homeostatic functions, some preceding neurodegeneration14–17. Similarly, macrophage activation and resultant systemic inflammation18 potentially contribute to the development of cardiomyopathy19,20 and insulin resistance21. These findings suggest that replacing affected microglia and macrophages could mitigate the progression of the disease.

Currently, there are no disease-modifying therapies for FA. Treatment focuses on symptomatic management and addressing mitochondrial redox and iron homeostasis22. Both adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy and genome editing approaches have shown promising results in preclinical studies, demonstrating the ability to increase frataxin levels and improve cardiac and neurological function. Notably, some AAV-based strategies have advanced into clinical testing, although their therapeutic efficacy in patients remains to be fully established23–36. However, challenges remain, such as the feasibility of efficiently targeting multiple cell types, maintaining appropriate frataxin levels, and their long-term safety37,38. Bone marrow (BMT) or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) has been used for decades to treat multi-systemic diseases with neurodegeneration as a hallmark symptom39. BMT has shown encouraging therapeutic effects in YG8sR FA mice40,41. The rationale is that bone marrow-derived cells replace tissue myeloid cells, providing healthy microglia-like cells (MGLCs) in the CNS, as well as macrophages in the heart and muscle, where they cross-correct affected cells42–45. However, previous studies used high doses of whole-body irradiation for pre-transplant conditioning, achieving low MGLC replacement, which, combined with concerns about its unfavorable risk-to-benefit ratio, has tempered the enthusiasm about its application for FA. Nevertheless, a hematopoietic cell-based therapy is worth investigating as it offers several advantages. HSCT could be implemented immediately using allogeneic transplants and autologous transplantation strategies could be developed to enhance safety35,46,47. Compared to AAVs, a cell-based therapy provides an alternative for those ineligible for AAV due to the high seroprevalence of AAV-neutralizing antibodies and avoids the potential toxicity risks associated with high doses, particularly in the DRG48,49.

The mechanisms responsible for recruiting hematopoietic-derived cells to the CNS are not yet known, but myeloablative conditioning of the recipient is necessary42,50. Current conditioning methods in HSCT result in slow and low engraftment of MGLCs in the CNS51,52, significantly reducing HSCT’s therapeutic efficacy for conditions like FA53–56. To improve the success of a hematopoietic stem cell-based approach for neurological disorders, we recently developed a conditioning regimen using FDA-approved drugs that rapidly and robustly replace microglia in the CNS and macrophages in the heart57. This regimen combines busulfan myeloablation and the Colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor PLX3397. In progranulin-deficient mice, this approach successfully restored biochemical and neurological abnormalities57. Other microglia replacement protocols have also been effective in several murine models of neurological diseases52,58–60. We posited an optimized microglia/macrophage replacement protocol would enhance the efficacy of transplantation for FA. Ultimately, a clear demonstration of the benefit of this approach would support the clinical development of a hematopoietic stem cell-based therapy for FA.

To explore the potential of myeloid cell replacement therapy for FA, we utilized the YG8-800 mouse model. This model, derived from the YG8sR strain, possesses a human FXN gene with approximately 800 GAA trinucleotide repeats, making it the best existing model as it replicates critical features of the human disease17,61–63. We achieved nearly complete replacement of microglia and macrophages in the YG8-800 mice through a combination of busulfan, PLX3397, and BMT. Following microglia replacement, we observed mitochondrial transfer, increased transcription of genes involved in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation across various CNS cell types, improved synaptic markers, and increased mitochondrial protein abundance. These changes significantly improved overall health and neurobehavioral performance in the treated mice. Similarly, macrophage replacement restored cardiac function and improved the expression of genes related to mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis in the heart. Additionally, we provide compelling evidence of intercellular mitochondrial transfer from donor myeloid cells in culture, measurably enhancing mitochondrial function in FA cells.

Results

Efficient microglia replacement is accompanied by widespread mitochondrial transfer to CNS cells in YG8-800 mice

We recently described a strategy that achieves robust, rapid, and persistent myeloid cell replacement by bone marrow-derived cells throughout most tissues, including CNS and heart. This regimen combines two approved drugs: Busulfan, the myeloablative agent used in the clinic, with the FDA-approved CSF1R inhibitor PLX3397 (Pexidartinib)57. To assess whether this regimen could achieve high engraftment of bone marrow-derived microglia-like cells (MGLCs) and improve neurological manifestations in FA, we tested it in FA mice. Several mouse models of FA have been developed, each showing differences in frataxin expression, symptom severity, affected tissues, and age of onset64. One recently developed model is YG8-800, a knockout for the mouse Fxn rescued from lethality by a human FXN YAC transgene harboring ~800 GAA repeats. YG8-800 mice display more severe symptoms than other human YAC transgenic models like YG8sR, and it is currently considered the most accurate model of human FA17,61–63. Seven- to 8-week-old YG8-800 male and female mice were conditioned with Busulfan over four days (days −4 to −1, 100 mg/kg/day), transplanted (day 0), and administered PLX3397 (100 mg/kg/day) over six days by oral gavage 15 days post bone marrow transplant (BMT, Fig. 1A). Sex-matched donor bone marrow with wild-type levels of mouse FXN was derived from a double transgenic reporter mouse line that expresses a cytoplasmic enhanced green fluorescent protein (GFP) and a mitochondrial-targeted far-red fluorescent protein (mKate2)65, allowing us to track donor cells and their mitochondria in vivo and in vitro.

To assess the engraftment of MGLCs in the CNS and their therapeutic effect in YG8-800 mice, we compared three experimental conditions: YG8-800 mice receiving YG8-800 bone marrow (FA + FA), YG8-800 mice receiving GFP/mKate2 or double-positive (DP) bone marrow (DP + FA), and wild-type mice receiving GFP/mKate2 or double-positive (DP) bone marrow (DP + WT) (Fig. 1B). Engraftment rates of DP cells in peripheral blood (PB) and brain were analyzed five months post-BMT by measuring the percent of CD45 + GFP+ cells using flow cytometry. FA and WT mice showed high donor cell chimerism in the PB, 82.7% and 94%, respectively (Fig. 1C and gating strategy in Supplementary Fig. 1A). The combination of Busulfan and PLX3397 achieved a very high proportion of GFP + CD45 + CD11b+ MGLCs in the brain of FA (82.2% ± 4.03) and WT mice (94.6% ± 2.67) (Fig. 1D and E, and gating strategy in Supplementary Fig. 2). In blood and brain, the chimerism was slightly lower in YG8-800 mice compared to WT mice (p < 0.05). Histological analysis of transplanted FA and WT brains revealed a widespread and homogeneous distribution of bone marrow-derived GFP+/mKate2+ cells throughout the brain, including the cerebellar cortex and the deep cerebellar nuclei, accounting for 22–28% of all nucleated cells (Fig. 1F–H and Supplementary Fig. 3). Replacement in the DRG was rare (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of our regimen for achieving high microglia replacement in YG8-800 mouse CNS.

We previously demonstrated that in wild-type mice, the microglial niche is selectively repopulated by myeloid cells without lymphoid infiltration57. Consistent with this, flow cytometry showed similar brain immune cellularity across transplanted and untreated WT and FA groups, as measured by the percentage of CD45⁺ cells among live cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Approximately 98% of CD45⁺ CNS immune cells were myeloid (CD11b⁺), whereas B cells (CD19⁺ and/or B220⁺) and T cells (CD4⁺ or CD8a⁺) were rare and remained uniformly low across groups (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). In the brain, approximately 97% of GFP⁺ cells were CD11b⁺ and immunohistochemistry confirmed a similar fraction of GFP⁺ cells co-expressed the microglial marker IBA1 (Supplementary Fig. 3D–J). Together, these results indicate that CNS repopulation occurs through targeted recruitment of bone marrow–derived myeloid cells rather than nonspecific immune infiltration.

To investigate mitochondrial transfer, we used flow cytometry, tracking the mKate2 signal to monitor mitochondrial movement and GFP to identify transplant-derived donor cells. Putative mitochondrial acceptor cells were identified for the absence of GFP and the presence of the mKate2 (GFP-/mKate2+) (Fig. 1J). To exclude the possibility that GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells were donor-derived cells that had lost GFP, we quantified the human FXN YAC transgene relative to a mouse chromosome 9 reference gene by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). The expected 0.5 transgene-to-mouse ratio, reflecting hemizygosity in recipient YG8-800 mice, was preserved, confirming that GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells originated from the recipient mouse (Supplementary Fig. 5). Quantification of cell number and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the mKate2 signal revealed a significantly higher proportion of GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells and increased MFI in cells from FA mice compared to WT controls (12.7% vs. 4.19%, p < 0.0001 and 431 vs. 978, p < 0.0001, Fig. 1K, L) suggesting FA cells are more likely to uptake or retain mKate2+ signal.

We quantified the cellular composition of the GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ population by measuring the fraction of major CNS cell types within these cells using established surface markers. Endogenous microglia were identified as CD45⁺/CD11b⁺/GFP⁻, astrocytes as ACSA-2⁺, oligodendrocytes as O4⁺, and neurons as CD90⁺ (Thy1). The fraction of mKate2+ cells varied between 15% and 30% across all four cell types. In YG8-800 mice, the highest percentage of mKate2+ cells was observed in endogenous CD45+/CD11b+ microglia, followed by CD90+ neurons. A similar pattern was seen in WT mice, although the increase in mKate2+ cells in microglia was not statistically significant (Fig. 1M). In line with the flow cytometry results, high-resolution imaging of brain sections revealed mKate2+ puncta, often without co-localization with GFP, suggesting that the signal was transferred to other cells (Fig. 1N).

Normal hematopoietic reconstitution and enhanced mitochondria transfer in hematopoietic tissues of YG8-800 mice

To investigate differences in hematopoietic reconstitution in YG8-800 versus wild-type mice, we performed a detailed analysis of cell lineages in the bone marrow and spleen five months post-transplantation, using flow cytometry. The specific lineage markers included CD45+ for all hematopoietic cells, CD11b+/Ly6C+ for myeloid cells, CD4+/CD8a+ for T cells, and CD19+/B220+ for B cells (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the bone marrow, the proportions of myeloid cells were 76.0%, 72.3%, and 66.9% in the FA + FA, DP+FA, and DP + WT groups, respectively. The proportions of B cells were 16.8%, 21.8%, and 22.4%, while T cells represented 2.9%, 2.7%, and 2.7% of the population (Fig. 2A). None of these differences were statistically significant.

YG8-800 mice exhibited similar engraftment levels compared to wild-type mice, as measured by the frequency of GFP+ donor-derived cells across all these populations. The chimerism rates were 93.0% in the DP + FA group and 94.4% in the DP + WT group (Figs. 2B and 1C). This high level of donor cell engraftment was consistently high in the three lineages: myeloid (97% vs. 95%), T (85% vs. 83%), and B cells (86% vs. 73%) (Fig. 2B). Similar findings for cell frequencies, donor cell engraftment, and differentiation were observed in the spleen, where, as expected, there was a higher proportion of lymphocytes compared to the bone marrow (Fig. 2C, D).

To evaluate mitochondrial transfer, we quantified the fraction of mKate2+/GFP- cells in different cell populations. As observed in the CNS, the proportion of mKate2+ cells within the GFP- acceptor population in the bone marrow was higher in YG8-800 mice than in wild-type mice (21.9% vs. 11.2%, Fig. 2E, F). Reflecting the high engraftment efficiency of donor GFP+/mKate2+ cells, GFP-/mKate2+ cells comprised only a small fraction of each lineage (1–2%), and a complementary per-lineage analysis, quantifying mKate2+ cells as a fraction of GFP- cells within each lineage, agreed with the weighted-average value in Fig. 2F (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Characterization of the lineage composition of the mKate2+/GFP- cells revealed that B cells were the most enriched (59.5% and 48.8% for DP + FA and DP + WT, respectively), followed by myeloid cells (23.7% and 24.8%), and T cells (5.1% and 6.2%) (Fig. 2G). Similar findings were observed in the spleen, including an increase in the percent of mKate2+ cells in YG8-800 mice and a higher proportion of B cells, likely reflecting their higher frequency in the spleen (Fig. 2H–J). Importantly, the elevated proportion of B cells may indicate an increased capacity to uptake and/or retain the mKate2 signal, rather than a dominant contribution to the overall cellular pool or phenotypic rescue. MFI analysis of mKate2 re-demonstrated that FA cells exhibited higher mKate2 intensity compared to WT cells, with 3-fold and 2.4-fold increases in the bone marrow and spleen, respectively, consistent with the CNS findings and supporting the idea that FA cells are more likely to uptake and/or retain mKate2+ signal (Fig. 2K–M).

Microglia replacement improves growth, survival, locomotion, strength and coordination in YG8-800 mice

YG8-800 mice exhibit poor growth, alopecia, and neurological abnormalities apparent by 26 weeks of age17,61,62. To assess the impact of myeloid cell replacement on these phenotypes, female mice underwent BMT at 8 weeks of age and completed PLX3397 treatment by 10 weeks of age. Growth and survival were tracked longitudinally, behavioral testing was performed between 29 and 30 weeks, and molecular and biochemical analyses were conducted at week 30 (Fig. 3A). YG8-800 mice that received autologous cells (FA + FA) displayed more pronounced alopecia than wild-type mice transplanted with wild-type DP cells (DP + WT). In contrast, YG8-800 mice transplanted with DP cells expressing wild-type levels of frataxin (DP + FA) showed significant improvements in alopecia (Fig. 3B). To evaluated growth, we tracked body weight for approximately five months after transplantation (8–29 weeks of age) and included two additional control groups of unmanipulated FA and WT mice to account for the effects of conditioning and transplantation (Fig. 3C). In a female cohort of mice, the WT group showed the expected weight gain pattern, with the highest trajectory, closely followed by the DP + WT group (27.6 g ± 1.6 vs 26.5 g ± 1.4 at week 29 for WT and DP + WT, respectively). The slightly lower weight trajectory initially observed in the DP + WT group was not significantly different from that of WT mice by week 30, possibly indicating an initial effect of BU + PLX conditioning that did not affect the long-term weight gain. The growth patterns of unmanipulated FA and FA + FA mice were similar and both significantly depressed, demonstrating the effects of the expanded human transgene on growth in this model (18.4 g ± 0.5 for FA and 17.9 g ± 0.7 for FA + FA). In contrast, DP + FA mice exhibited a 50% recovery in weight loss, reaching approximately 22.3 ± 0.7 g (Fig. 3C). Myeloid cell replacement with DP cells improved survival, from 53% in pooled FA disease-control mice (16/30; unmanipulated FA and sham-transplanted FA + FA, which did not differ, log-rank P = 0.73) to 80% in DP + FA mice (16/20). (Fig. 3D). Similar effects were seen in male YG8-800 mice, including improved growth and alopecia and a consistent, though non-significant, trend toward improved survival, likely reflecting the small size of the male cohort (Fig. 3E–G). These improvements in growth and survival could be attributable to effects within the CNS and/or peripheral tissues like the heart.

Per previous reports, motor abnormalities in YG8-800 mice become apparent at 26 weeks. We assessed each group’s neuro-motor deficits and muscle strength between 29 and 30 weeks of age in female cohorts. At this age, female YG8-800 mice showed impairments in spontaneous locomotion, coordination, and muscle strength that improved with microglia replacement but did not completely normalize (Fig. 3H–O). Compared to WT and WT + DP mice, FA and FA + FA mice showed impaired coordination in the crossbeam test, showing increased time to cross the beam and slips (Fig. 3H, I). These differences were evident, with affected mice struggling to cross, relying heavily on the thin support of the crossbeam. FA and FA + FA mice also exhibited reduced spontaneous locomotion in the activity chamber compared to WT and DP + WT mice, as indicated by decreased ambulatory distance, vertical rearings, reduced time spent in the center zone, and increased time spent in the periphery zone. All these parameters were partially improved after transplantation in DP + FA mice (Fig. 3J–M). In the wire-hanging test, the neuromuscular function was also reduced in FA and FA + FA, who often almost fell immediately after hanging. The treated mice also improved the wire-hang test (Fig. 3O). Notably, unmanipulated WT and DP + WT mice behaved similarly in all neurobehavioral assays, supporting previous observations that microglia replacement does not affect neurobehavior57 (Fig. 3H–O).

Mitochondrial transfer is associated with transcriptional changes in oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis across CNS cell types in female mice

To explore the identity of cells acquiring the mKate2+ signal and the biochemical impact of mitochondrial uptake, we performed transcriptomic studies from dissociated brain cells from transplanted female DP + FA mice at the study endpoint (30 weeks). We used flow cytometry to delineate GFP+/mKate2+, GFP-/mKate2- and GFP-/mKate2+ populations and confirmed the accuracy of our gating strategy by image-enabled cell sorting and standard immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 6). Cells were then sorted based on mKate2 positivity (GFP-/mKate2+ vs. GFP-/mKate2-), followed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq, Fig. 4A). The scRNA-seq analysis allowed us to identify cell types without bias and measure gene expression to assess the cells’ metabolic status. We sequenced 9,151 mKate2+ cells and 3,196 mKate2- cells, combining cells from three mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that mKate2+ and mKate2- cells were distributed across most identified cell clusters, indicating that uptake of mKate2+ was not limited to a specific cell type (Fig. 4B). Using known transcriptional signatures of CNS cells (Supplementary Data 1), we categorized the cells in each sample into 12 subpopulations (clusters), including choroid plexus epithelial cells (CPC), endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, microglia, neurons, ependymal cells, astrocytes, macrophages, olfactory ensheathing glia (OEG), pericytes, arachnoid barrier cells (ABC), and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC) (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Among all populations, the CPC cluster displayed the strongest transcriptional divergence between mKate2⁺ and mKate2⁻ cells, evident in the UMAP. This difference may reflect transcriptional reprogramming after mitochondrial uptake, but could also indicate heterogeneity in CPCs with variable capacity for mitochondrial acquisition. Increasing the clustering resolution split this broad cluster into two transcriptionally distinct subgroups; however, analysis using a curated panel of 23 CPC markers66–68 confirmed that both subclusters are best classified as CPCs (Supplementary Data 1).

Although quantitative comparisons of cell abundance in scRNA-seq data have caveats69, microglia and macrophages were markedly enriched in the mKate2+ compared to mKate2- fraction, showing ~50-fold and ~8-fold increases, respectively (Fig. 4D). Neurons and OPCs were also moderately enriched (~4-fold), whereas other cell types showed similar or slightly reduced representation in the mKate2⁺ fraction. This observed enrichment may reflect higher mitochondrial uptake in these cell types, although sampling bias cannot be excluded.

To evaluate transcriptional changes in response to mitochondrial uptake and identify shared gene expression changes across all cell types, we conducted an unbiased differential gene expression (DGE) analysis between mKate2+ and mKate2- cells in the 12-cell populations. Using a significance threshold of p < 0.05, we found that 8068 out of 32,285 genes showed significant expression changes in at least one cell type after mitochondrial transfer. Of these, 7102 genes also met a 20%-fold-change threshold (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Data 2). To pinpoint genes with consistent transcriptional changes across cell types, we calculated a Common Expression Score (CES), which was determined by subtracting the number of cell types where a gene is downregulated from the number where it is upregulated. A positive CES indicates a gene is commonly upregulated, while a negative CES indicates downregulation across multiple cell types (Supplementary Data 2).

We first examined the top commonly upregulated genes with a CES of 6 or higher (indicating upregulation in at least six cell types). In mKate2+ cells, genes associated with ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g., Cox4i1, Ndufa13, Ndufa4, Atp5g1, Ndufb11, Cox7b), inflammation (e.g., Ttr, Apoe, Igfbp2, Enpp2), and cellular homeostasis (e.g., Manf, Glrx3, Hspa5, Pebp) were upregulated (Fig. 4F (Top 30 shown) and Supplementary Data 2). To further understand the biological pathways represented by the commonly upregulated genes, we performed pathway and process enrichment analysis on the top 50 genes with CES ≥ 6. The resulting network map highlighted clusters related to ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation, encompassing 24 of the 50 genes, with specific enrichments for oxidative phosphorylation (log10(P) = −17), proton transmembrane transport (log10(P) = −6), and Complex IV (log10(P) = −4), underscoring processes involved in cellular energy production (Fig. 4G, Supplementary Data 3). Other enriched processes included protein localization to the membrane, transmembrane transport regulation, and insulin-like growth factor transport. Conversely, DGE and pathway analysis for commonly downregulated genes (CES < −6) in mKate2+ cells indicated that the most frequently downregulated genes were associated with mRNA processing, RNA stabilization, protein folding, and translation (26 out of 50 genes, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Data 4). Together, these findings suggest that the uptake of mKate2+ is indicative of mitochondrial transfer, which subsequently induces beneficial biochemical changes in FA cells, particularly in processes related to oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, which are known to be impaired in FA.

Since most upregulated genes following mitochondrial uptake were involved in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), we first surveyed the nuclear-encoded subunits of the mitochondrial OXPHOS system and found that approximately half of the gene-cell pairs were differentially expressed (Supplementary Fig. 10). Several subunits from Complex I (Ndufb5, Ndufb7, Ndufc2, Ndufa11, Ndufb11, Ndufa4, Ndufa13), Complex III (Uqcrb, Uqcrh, Uqcrq, Uqcr11, Uqcr10), Complex IV (Cox5a, Cox8a, Cox7b, Cox4i1), and Complex V (Atp5j, Atp5l, Atp5h, Atp5o, Atp5d, Atp5g1) were commonly upregulated (CSE > 6) in mKate2+ cells (Fig. 4H). We also compared mitochondrial DNA transcripts that contribute to the respiratory complexes (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12).

Mitochondrial DNA encodes 13 subunits of the electron transport chain: seven belonging to complex I (ND1–ND6 and ND4L), one to complex III (CYTB), three to complex IV (COX I–III), and two to complex V (ATP6 and ATP8). Among the 12 cell types mKate2+ CPCs, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, and arachnoid barrier cells showed statically significant decreases in transcripts corresponding to all 13 mitochondrially encoded genes (100% across all complexes). Neurons displayed an intermediate profile with 8 of 13 mitochondrial genes (62% of total). MKate2+ ependymal cells showed downregulation of a few mitochondrial transcripts (23%), with only minimal significant changes in astrocytes, OEG, and macrophages (14%). Microglia, OPCs, and pericytes did not show any mtDNA transcripts that were differentially regulated (Fig. 4I, and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). Together, these results suggest that mitochondrial uptake influences mtDNA transcript abundance in a cell type–dependent manner, though biological and technical sources of variation such as cell and transcript abundance must be considered in interpreting these patterns.

We also reviewed transcriptomic data from FA cellular models to determine if the transcriptional changes following mitochondrial uptake aligned with improvements in known FA-related profiles70–76. Although no universal signature was found across all cell types, two genes—Prdx2 and Prdx5—were frequently downregulated in prior studies and showed upregulation in several cell types following mitochondrial uptake, suggesting potential improvement. Antioxidant defense genes (Prdx2, Prdx5, Sod1, and Sod2) and Krebs cycle genes (Aco2 and Mdh1) were upregulated in mKate2+ cells, indicating a partial recovery in gene expression, as these genes are downregulated in FA skeletal muscle cells76. Furthermore, several genes associated with transcriptional and translational repression, which are usually upregulated in FA (Ehmt2, Eif2ak4, Paip2b, Suds3, and Tcf25), were downregulated in at least one cell type, further supporting a partial improvement (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes increase transcription of energy production genes following mitochondrial uptake

GSEA evaluated the enrichment of 7,713 gene sets (pathways) derived from the gene ontology biological process collections in the Mouse MSigDB. This analysis ultimately identified 855 pathways with significant changes (p < 0.05 and q < 0.25) in at least one cell type (Supplementary Data 5).

The four major CNS cell types—neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes—showed substantial transcriptional changes following mitochondrial acquisition (Fig. 5A–H). In neurons, several nuclear genes involved in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation, such as Cox4i1, Ndufv1, Atp5o, and Ndufa9, along with mitochondrial DNA genes (mt-Cytb and mt-Co2), were upregulated following uptake (Fig. 5A). GSEA of all DEGs in neurons identified 17 significantly upregulated and four downregulated pathways (Supplementary Data 5). Notably, the upregulated pathways are mainly related to ATP synthesis, electron transport chain, and oxidative phosphorylation, followed by pathways involved in DNA binding regulation and proteostasis (Fig. 5B, top 10 pathways shown). Astrocytes also showed enhanced expression of genes involved in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g., Atp5g1, Atp5k, Cox4i1, Ndufa11, and Ndufa13). In these cells, upregulation of Ttr, Ptgds, Igfbp2, and Chchd10 may contribute to neuroprotection and cellular recovery (Fig. 5C). Consistent with DGE findings, pathway analysis indicated increased pathways related to ATP synthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and cytoplasmic translation (Fig. 5D). Microglia displayed similar patterns to astrocytes, with upregulation of DEGs in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation. In addition, the upregulation of Arl6ip1, Igfbp2, and Cybb suggests modulation of pathways related to cell survival and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5E and F). In oligodendrocytes, DEGs overlapped with those in other cell types; however, pathway analysis showed a higher normalized enrichment score in pathways related to sodium regulation and pyramidal neuron differentiation and function, along with other energy derivation-related processes (Fig. 5G and H).

We analyzed 855 pathways that significantly changed in at least one cell type and quantified shared responses using a Common Pathway Score (CPS), defined as the number of cell types with pathway upregulation minus those with downregulation (Supplementary Data 5). Higher absolute CPS values indicate broader concordance across cell types. Using this metric, we identified 33 commonly upregulated pathways following mitochondrial uptake, with CPS values ranging from 3 to 8 (Fig. 5I, top 20 pathways shown). Although each cell type exhibits differences in their transcriptional responses, there is strong convergence in metabolic pathways. Mitochondrial energy metabolism–related pathways were broadly represented across cell types, with proton transmembrane transport detected in 8 of 12 cell populations (Fig. 5I). Key pathways including ATP metabolic process, cellular respiration, oxidative phosphorylation, and energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds were enriched in 7 of 12 cell types. More specialized oxidative pathways such as aerobic respiration, respiratory electron transport chain, and ATP synthesis–coupled proton transport were observed in 6 of 12 cell types. Specifically for neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, all cell types that were well represented in the dataset, there was even stronger pathway convergence (Supplementary Data 6). All four cells upregulated proton transmembrane transport, ATP metabolic processes, cellular respiration, energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, oxidative phosphorylation, and the respiratory electron transport chain. Three of the four cell types showed additional upregulation of aerobic respiration, ATP synthesis–coupled proton transport, dehydrogenase complex assembly, ATP synthesis–coupled electron transport, electron transport chain, and mitochondrial electron transport from dehydrogenase to ubiquinone. In total, 29 GOBP terms related to energy metabolism were significantly altered in at least two of the four cell types (Supplementary Data 6).

Eight pathways, with CPS scores ranging from −3 to −5, showed downregulation after mitochondrial uptake (Fig. 5J). Many of these pathways are related to RNA splicing and processing (e.g., RNA processing, RNA splicing via transesterification reaction, mRNA processing) and translation (e.g., translation initiation, cytoplasmic translation, peptide biosynthetic process). Previous GSEA analyses in human FA skeletal muscle and fibroblasts have highlighted enrichment in pathways associated with RNA splicing, RNA processing, RNA binding, and translation72,76. The downregulation of these pathways in our data suggests a potential amelioration of the FA disease phenotype following mitochondrial uptake.

Microglia replacement partially rescues synaptic and mitochondrial abundance in the cerebellum of female YG8-800 mice

Transcriptomic analyses suggested that mitochondrial acquisition after microglia replacement enhances energy metabolism and cellular recovery in several CNS cell types by partially restoring mitochondrial function and promoting homeostatic pathways. To validate some of these findings, we examined synaptic density, mitochondrial protein abundance, and mitochondrial dynamics in the cerebellum, the region most affected in FA, using immunohistochemistry and immunoblotting in FA + FA, DP + FA, and DP + WT female mice. At 30 weeks of age, FA + FA mice showed cerebellar degeneration with synaptic loss and mitochondrial impairment, consistent with prior reports in the YG8-800 model17. Synaptophysin immunostaining revealed an approximate 76% reduction in the cerebellar cortex of FA + FA mice relative to controls (Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, DP + FA treated mice exhibited improved Synaptophysin signal, corresponding to a 40% decrease compared to DP + WT (Fig. 6B). Immunoblotting and quantitative densitometry further confirmed reduced Synaptophysin and PSD95 levels in FA + FA mice (50% and 30% of DP + WT controls, respectively), while DP + FA mice showed substantial restoration of both proteins (76% and 55% of DP + WT controls; Fig. 6C–E). Together, these findings indicate that donor-derived MGLCs confer measurable protection against synaptic degeneration, although not complete normalization to wild-type levels.

Assessment of mitochondrial dynamics through phosphorylated dynamin-related protein 1 (Ser616-pDRP1), a marker of mitochondrial fission, revealed a five-fold increase in p-DRP1 intensity in Purkinje cells in FA + FA mice relative to DP + WT controls consistent with excessive mitochondrial fission and/or fragmentation77 (Fig. 6F, G). In DP + FA mice, phospho-DRP1 levels were reduced by ~2.5-fold compared with FA + FA but remained approximately twice those of DP + WT, indicating partial normalization of mitochondrial fission activity or drive (Fig. 6G).

Complementary immunoblot analysis of cerebellar lysates revealed a marked depletion of mitochondrial respiratory chain subunits in FA + FA mice at 30 weeks of age, including complex I (NDUFB8), complex II (SDHB), complex III (UQCRC2), and complex V (ATP5A), consistent with severe mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction (Fig. 6H). Quantitative densitometry showed that these complexes were reduced to approximately 10–50% of levels observed in control DP + WT mice; specifically, complex I to 14%, complex II to 10%, complex III to 49%, and complex V to 51%. In contrast, DP + FA mice exhibited a partial but substantial recovery in mitochondrial protein abundance, restoring approximately 69–84% of control values for the respective complexes (Fig. 6I–L). These findings indicate that mitochondrial transfer mitigates, though does not completely normalize, the respiratory chain deficits characteristic of frataxin deficiency.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that mitochondrial stress, excessive fission/fragmentation, and synaptic loss are major features of the YG8-800 brain, and that engraftment of donor-derived cells and mitochondrial transfer in DP + FA mice confers measurable, though incomplete, rescue of mitochondrial and synaptic integrity.

Cardiomyopathy affects about two-thirds of individuals with FA, starting as hypertrophic and progressing to dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure, a common cause of death78–80. YG8-800 mice have signs of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy that emerge by six months of age61–63. We first examined the reconstitution of macrophages in the female YG8-800 mouse hearts following transplantation. Mice conditioned with Busulfan/PLX3397 and subsequently receiving BMT showed high engraftment of donor-derived GFP+/mKate2+ cells in the heart with frequent mKate2 signals that did not co-localize with GFP (Fig. 7A). Flow cytometry of cardiac immune cells showed stable immune composition characterized by selective recruitment of CD11b⁺ myeloid cells, while CD19+/B220 + B cells and CD4⁺/CD8a⁺ T cells remained low and comparable to unmanipulated controls (Supplementary Fig. 16A-C). Consistent with this, CD68 immunostaining showed that 82–86% of GFP⁺ cells co-expressed CD68, supporting their designation as macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 16D-F).

We evaluated cardiac function using two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography to measure parameters such as left ventricular dimensions (end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters), ejection fraction, fractional shortening, wall thickness, and chamber volumes. Comparisons were made across FA (unmanipulated), FA sham (FA + FA), treated (FA + DP), healthy (WT + DP), and WT (unmanipulated) control groups (Fig. 7B). FA mice transplanted with cells expressing wild-type levels of frataxin exhibited significant improvements in cardiac function, including increased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, Fig. 7C), fractional shortening (FS, Fig. 7D), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVPWd, Fig. 7E), along with reduced left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LV-vol-s, Fig. 7F).

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of macrophage replacement in the FA heart, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of whole heart tissue. This study included three groups: FA + FA (n = 3), DP + FA (n = 3), and DP + WT (n = 2). PCA of gene expression profiles revealed distinct clustering, with DP + FA and DP + WT samples grouping closely together, while FA + FA samples formed a separate cluster, indicating significant differences in gene expression (Fig. 7G). To further explore the genes and pathways involved, we compiled a set of 52 marker genes associated with cardiac phenotypes in FA murine and cellular models30,31,81–83 (Supplementary Data 7). Z-scores were computed from FPKM values across all samples for these genes. In line with our PCA findings, hierarchical clustering of the expression of these 52 genes for the three conditions clustered the DP + FA samples closer to the DP + WT samples, indicating more similar gene expression profiles than those of the affected FA + FA mice (Supplementary Fig. 17A).

A detailed analysis of gene expression profiles of these 52 genes across the experimental groups revealed significant modulation in pathways associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, fibrosis, iron metabolism, β-oxidation and glycolysis, electron transport, and the TCA cycle (Fig. 7H, I, Supplementary Data 7 and Supplementary Fig. 17A). Consistent with prior studies, the FA + FA group exhibited marked upregulation of genes linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and heart failure, such as Acta1, Actn1, and Igf1, alongside heart failure markers Nppb, Aldh1a3, and Gdf15, suggesting cardiac stress and heart failure. Importantly, most of these genes showed significant reductions in the DP + FA group, most indistinguishable from the DP + WT group (Fig. 7H, I and Supplementary Fig. 17B). Fibrosis markers, including Postn and Bag3, were significantly elevated in FA + FA, reflecting active extracellular matrix remodeling, but were reduced in DP + FA. Nrf2 activation in the heart regulates the expression of antioxidant genes like Nqo1 and Sod2, which reduce oxidative stress, protect against ischemia-reperfusion injury, mitigate cardiac remodeling, and improve cardiac function84. In the DP + FA group, the upregulation of these Nrf2 targets compared to FA + FA suggests enhanced oxidative stress defense and protection against cardiac damage (Fig. 7H and Supplementary Fig. 17B). Disruptions in iron metabolism were also evident in FA + FA mice, characterized by decreased expression of Slc40a1 and Isca1 and increased expression of Slc25a37 and Trf (Fig. 7I and Supplementary Fig. 17B). These patterns were improved in the DP + FA group, suggesting improved iron homeostasis. Furthermore, genes involved in β-oxidation and glycolysis, commonly downregulated in FA, such as Hadha and Hadhb, were upregulated in DP + FA, indicating metabolic reprogramming to meet energy demands (Fig. 7I and Supplementary Fig. 17B). Collectively, these findings underscore the cardiac dysfunction and metabolic disturbances in YG8-800 mice and indicate substantial improvements across multiple pathways important for cardiac function in the DP + FA group.

To unbiasedly assess the treatment effects, we also performed DGE analysis between FA + FA and DP + FA mice. This analysis identified 822 significantly differentially expressed genes (p < 0.05), with 349 exhibiting a fold change (FC) > 20% and 473 displaying an FC < −20% (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Fig. 17C). GSEA of all differentially expressed genes revealed consistent enrichment (FDR < 0.05) of pathways associated with aerobic respiration, ATP synthesis, mitochondrial respiration, and mitochondrial function in treated samples (Fig. 7J and Supplementary Data 9). These findings suggest a notable recovery of mitochondrial function in heart tissue upon macrophage replacement.

Direct cell–cell contact enables mitochondrial transfer and improves respiration in FA cells

To ascertain whether mitochondrial transfer is a mechanism through which tissue myeloid cells restore mitochondrial function in FA cells, we established a co-culture system consisting of donor macrophages and acceptor FA cells. Double-positive macrophages (DP-Mø) were generated by differentiating bone marrow cells from GFP/mKate2 mice using a cytokine cocktail including M-CSF and GM-CSF, and primary skin fibroblast cultures were prepared from wild-type and YG8-800 FA mice. In these co-cultures, fibroblasts and macrophages were distinguishable due to differences in cell and nuclear size and were easily separated based on their different attachment properties. After 72 h of co-culture, fluorescence microscopy revealed the presence of mKate2+ signals in fibroblasts, suggestive of mitochondrial uptake (Fig. 8A, B). Notably, WT fibroblasts exhibited a low level of mitochondrial uptake, approximately 2%, while FA fibroblasts showed a 10-fold higher uptake, around 20% (Fig. 8C). This observation suggests that FA fibroblasts have a compensatory mechanism that facilitates mitochondrial uptake or retention.

To confirm that mitochondria, rather than mRNA or protein from the mitochondrially targeted mKate2 transgene, were being transferred, donor macrophages were labeled with a mitochondrial tracker that fluoresces upon accumulating in the mitochondrial membrane. Flow cytometry was then used to assess mitochondrial transfer to mouse and human FA fibroblasts. Fibroblasts and macrophages were distinguished based on their light scattering properties (forward scatter [FSC] and side scatter [SSC]) and the expression of the myeloid marker CD11b (Fig. 8D–F). In mouse fibroblasts (mFA Fb), at 48 h, a distinct mKate2+/GFP- fibroblast population (Q1) emerged, representing ~10% of the total cells which increased to ~20% by 72 h (Fig. 8D). Flow cytometry confirmed that the vast majority of GFP-/mKate2+ cells were CD11b-negative and exhibited light-scattering properties consistent with fibroblasts, arguing against the possibility that this population arose from macrophages that had lost GFP expression (Fig. 8E). MKate2⁺ fibroblasts showed a marked rightward shift in Mitotracker fluorescence, indicative of increased mitochondrial load (Fig. 8F). Similar results were obtained in patient-derived fibroblasts (hFA Fb), distinguished by CD90 expression (Fig. 8G–I). In both mouse and human FA fibroblasts, 93–95% of the mKate2+ fibroblasts were also Mitotracker positive (Fig. 8I). Imaging of the human fibroblasts confirmed GFP-/mKate2+/Mitotracker+ cells with characteristic fibroblast morphology (Fig. 8K and Supplementary Fig. 18A)

Mitochondrial dyes are prone to leakage, which can lead to false-positive signals85. To probe for mitochondrial transfer using an orthogonal approach, we used the mouse macrophage/human FA fibroblast co-culture, exploiting sequence divergence between their mitochondrial genomes. We designed a sensitive ddPCR assay to quantify mitochondrial transfer by measuring the mitochondrial DNA to nuclear DNA ratio (mtDNA/nDNA) with species-specific fluorescent probes, using nDNA as an internal control for cellular contamination. Following co-culture, we analyzed FAC-sorted mKate2⁺ acceptor fibroblasts alongside control mouse macrophages and unexposed human FA fibroblasts. For each condition, we measured mouse mtDNA normalized to mouse nDNA, and human mtDNA normalized to human nDNA. As expected, mouse macrophages contained no human DNA and isolated human fibroblasts lacked mouse DNA (Fig. 8L and Supplementary Fig. 18B). MKate2⁺ acceptor fibroblasts exhibited a human mtDNA/nDNA ratio comparable to isolated human fibroblasts but also had a pronounced mouse mtDNA signal, indicating the coexistence of both mitochondrial genomes (Fig. 8L and Supplementary Fig. 18B). Together, imaging, flow cytometry, and ddPCR analyses provide convergent evidence of mitochondrial transfer in this macrophage–fibroblast mitochondria transfer axis.

Mitochondrial transfer between cells can occur through contact-independent and contact-dependent pathways85,86. In contact-independent transfer, mitochondria are released into the extracellular space either as free organelles or enclosed within extracellular vesicles. In contrast, contact-dependent transfer can occur through direct cytoplasmic bridges or tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), dendritic projections that connect via end-foot-like structures, and adhesion-mediated transfer involving connexin 43 (Cx43)-containing junctions85,86. To distinguish between these mechanisms in our system, we employed a non-contact Boyden chamber co-culture assay. GFP⁺/mKate2⁺ macrophages were seeded in the upper chamber and acceptor fibroblasts in the lower chamber, separated by a 0.4-µm porous membrane (Fig. 8M). Under these conditions, no mitochondrial transfer was detected, indicating that transfer requires physical interaction between donor macrophages and fibroblasts (Fig. 8N). Cytochalasin D, which blocks actin polymerization and disrupts TNTs, reduced transfer by ~70%, whereas the gap junction blocker carbenoxolone had a modest effect (~20%) (Fig. 8O, P). These results suggest that mitochondrial transfer occurs predominantly through actin-dependent structures such as TNTs.

To assess the impact of intercellular mitochondrial transfer on cellular respiration, we analyzed the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) across four different cell populations purified by FAC-sorting: WT fibroblasts (WT monoculture), mouse FA fibroblasts (FA monoculture), mKate2- FA fibroblasts (FA co-culture/mKate2-), and mKate2+ FA fibroblasts (FA co-culture/mKate2+). As expected, FA fibroblasts exhibited significantly lower OCR levels than WT fibroblasts, indicating impaired mitochondrial function (Fig. 8Q). OCR was significantly enhanced in FA mKate2+ fibroblasts, particularly during maximal respiration, as demonstrated by the response to FCCP. In contrast, FA mKate2- fibroblasts (co-cultured cells that did not receive mitochondria) showed no such improvement (Fig. 8Q). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in maximal respiration in FA mKate2+ fibroblasts compared to FA fibroblasts and FA mKate2- fibroblasts (p < 0.0001, Fig. 8R). However, WT fibroblasts retained the highest maximal respiration capacity, indicating only partial recovery. Similarly, spare respiratory capacity was significantly improved in FA mKate2+ compared to FA and FA mKate2- fibroblasts (p < 0.0001), although it remained below the levels observed in WT fibroblasts (Fig. 8S). These results demonstrate that intercellular mitochondrial transfer from macrophages partially restores mitochondrial function and respiratory capacity in FA cells. Interestingly, Seahorse metabolic analyses on the donor macrophages following co-culture revealed a modest decrease in their respiratory capacity. This finding could reflect bidirectional exchange, metabolic adaptation or stress (Supplementary Fig. 18C-F).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that replacing microglia and tissue-resident macrophages with bone marrow-derived cells expressing wild-type FXN levels leads to partial metabolic restoration and improved function in FA mice. By employing an optimized conditioning regimen that uses Busulfan and the shortest reported post-transplant course of CSF1R inhibition (CSF1Ri), we achieved high and stable microglia replacement by MGLCs throughout the CNS and macrophages in the heart. Our previous studies have demonstrated similar successes in the neuroretina and spinal cord57. This protocol has feasible clinical applicability. Busulfan is already utilized in HSCT for neurometabolic disorders. Additionally, PLX3397, administered at 1/100 to 1/200 of the human dose, is the only FDA-approved CSF1R inhibitor with established safety data87. Comparisons with unmanipulated FA and WT mice showed no treatment-related toxicities during the study period, as reflected by normal growth and neurobehavioral performance. Short-term toxicities may still occur and warrant further investigation. These findings underscore the importance of the conditioning regimen in HSCT/BMT for FA.

Previous studies evaluating the efficacy of BMT reported partial improvement in motor and mitochondrial phenotypes using YG8R mice40,41. Compared with the YG8-800 line used in this study, the YG8R model carries copies of the human FXN gene with shorter expansions (approximately 82 and 190 GAA repeats), exhibits higher levels of FXN expression, and presents a milder, less reproducible phenotype61,88,89. Furthermore, irradiation was employed as a conditioning regimen, which is not clinically applicable for FA. Aside from its associated toxicities, irradiation leads to low, variable, and slow MGLC engraftment in the CNS, even at high doses. This limitation has prompted the use of CSF1Ri to improve cell recruitment from the bone marrow52,90–94. We build upon these previous studies while using a more suitable disease model and showcasing the potential of CSF1Ri in enhancing microglial replacement. Building on this prior work and using a more faithful disease model, we demonstrate that CSF1Ri markedly improves microglial replacement, achieving ~90% turnover of endogenous microglia—near the theoretical maximum. Because microglia constitute ~10% of the ~75 million cells in the adult mouse brain95,96, this corresponds to ~7.5 million microglia, of which ~6.75 million are donor-derived and express FXN. Given that each microglial cell interacts with 10–20 neighboring cells97, such widespread replacement has the potential to influence a substantial portion of the brain’s cellular network.

Because our transplantation protocol also replaces the hematopoietic system, we found that FA mice undergo largely normal hematopoietic reconstitution. Importantly, owing to the high efficiency of engraftment, most hematopoietic cells were donor-derived leaving only a small residual host-derived (GFP⁻) compartment. Within this limited GFP⁻ population, mKate2⁺ cells represent only ~10–20%, corresponding to ~1–2% of total cells. Analysis of the lineage composition of the residual GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ compartment, showed that B cells were relatively overrepresented. This relative enrichment within a small residual population does not suggest a dominant contribution to the overall cellular compartment or to phenotypic rescue. Instead, these findings likely reflect a comparatively greater capacity of B cells to uptake and/or retain the mKate2 signal.

Several mechanisms may explain how replacing tissue myeloid cells improves symptoms in FA. Research has identified significant defects in FXN-deficient microglia, including increased phagocytic activity, heightened inflammatory responses, and a loss of homeostatic functions14–17. Collectively, these abnormalities likely drive neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in FA, suggesting that simply replacing dysfunctional microglia could slow disease progression. A similar mechanism may occur in the heart and other tissues, where macrophage activation likely contributes to systemic inflammation and the development of cardiomyopathy9,20. Indeed, blood samples from FA patients show pro-inflammatory signatures, indicating that addressing these inflammatory processes through HSCT could improve symptoms18,70. While inflammation certainly plays a role in the pathophysiology of FA, it does not completely account for it, which could explain why myeloid cell replacement therapy offers only partial benefits.

Myeloid cells likely support recovery not only through cell replacement but also predominantly through intercellular mitochondrial transfer (IMT). In our studies, IMT was supported by the detection of transfer of mitochondria labeled with a membrane-bound reporter and species-specific mitochondrial genome transfer along with transcriptomic signatures of mitochondrial recovery and restored respiratory capacity in FA fibroblasts. Several studies have shown that cells can export mitochondria to FA-relevant cells under normal and pathological conditions86,98–100. Mitochondrial transfer is critical in cardiomyocytes to maintain functional connectivity and support mitochondrial fitness101–103. IMT also promotes cardiac recovery in pathological conditions by reducing infarct size, preventing apoptosis, and enhancing cardiomyocyte function104–107. Similarly, IMT has been demonstrated between neurons and microglia and under oxygen or glucose deprivation in CNS cells, mitigating cellular damage108–112. Beyond tissue repair, IMT has been shown to impact tumor resistance, immune regulation, and inflammation control113–116. Several distinct mechanisms of contact-dependent and contact-independent mitochondria transfer have been described86,98. Here we show that transfer in the myeloid-FA cell axis occurs predominantly through actin-dependent mechanisms such as TNTs41 though other contact-dependent mechanisms such as cell fusion has also been proposed40.

Compared to wild-type cells, FA cells showed approximately greater mitochondrial uptake both in vivo and in vitro, evidenced by a higher proportion of mKate2⁺ cells and increased mKate2 MFI. This indicates that FA cells—or a subset thereof—are more likely to internalize and/or retain mitochondria. Several mechanisms may underlie this phenomenon. Frataxin deficiency disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, producing variable outcomes that range from large, hyperfused or proliferative117–119 mitochondria to fragmented networks120, and impairs mitophagy121, leading to the accumulation of damaged organelles. FA cultures also show increased senescence122,123, which could explain why a subset of cells displays heightened uptake, as senescent cells tend to favor mitochondrial fusion over fission. Additionally, compensatory mechanisms may promote mitochondrial uptake or transfer in the setting of mitochondrial stress124.

Our transcriptomic analyses of CNS single cells and bulk heart tissue revealed consistent metabolic reprogramming following IMT, with strong convergence in the pathways across diverse cell types despite differences in their abundance, particularly in the scRNA-seq dataset. These transcriptional changes corresponded to measurable, though incomplete, biochemical rescue of mitochondrial and synaptic integrity in the cerebellum. In the CNS, all major cell types, neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, showed coordinated enrichment of gene expression signatures associated with oxidative phosphorylation, electron transport chain, ATP synthesis, and cellular respiration genes, alongside cell type-specific signatures that likely reflect distinct functional cellular roles. At the gene level, many nuclear-encoded OXPHOS transcripts were upregulated, and IMT also induced cell type-specific changes in mtDNA gene expression, with all significant populations showing reduced mtDNA transcript levels. This decrease in mtDNA is hard to explain, but it may reflect normalization of mitochondrial homeostasis, consistent with prior observations that frataxin deficiency can drive compensatory mtDNA expansion in long-passaged FA cells125. In addition, mitonuclear asynchrony is a documented phenomenon in multiple biological contexts. In the specific context of FA, mitochondrial transfer may also exert non-bioenergetic effects that influence nuclear gene expression independently of mitochondrial genome activity or may trigger a nuclear-encoded compensatory response while simultaneously promoting mitophagy of damaged mitochondria. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. Nevertheless, it is important to keep in mind that the magnitude of these effects, seen in a subset of cells, may be underestimated or influenced by technical factors such as variation in total transcript counts, unequal cell representation, and pseudoreplication inherent to single-cell analyses. With deeper sampling, future studies may better resolve the extent and nature of metabolic correction achieved in each cell type.

Although FXN-deficient cells do not exhibit a universal transcriptional signature70–76, several commonly downregulated genes—such as Prdx2, Prdx5, Sod1, and Sod2—were upregulated in various CNS cells after mitochondrial uptake. Similarly, in the heart, we analyzed 52 marker genes associated with cardiac phenotypes in FA murine and cellular models30,31,81–83. This analysis revealed significant modulation of pathways linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, fibrosis, iron metabolism, β-oxidation, glycolysis, electron transport, and the TCA cycle. These findings underscore the metabolic disturbances in YG8-800 mice and demonstrate substantial improvements across multiple pathways critical for CNS and cardiac function following myeloid cell replacement.

Our findings demonstrate that myeloid cell replacement using FXN–expressing cells can restore metabolism and improve tissue function in FA, in part through sustained IMT, yet this strategy, like other investigational FA therapies, faces important challenges. The degree of rescue is substantial but incomplete, and potential risks remain, though these may be reduced through autologous transplantation approaches35,46,47 and less genotoxic conditioning regimens126. Efficacy could be further enhanced by engineering higher FXN expression or improving cross-correction capacity, and ultimately, FA treatment may require combining modalities tailored to disease stage. Notably, IMT itself represents a broadly applicable therapeutic mechanism: it has already been tested clinically in mitochondrial DNA deletion syndromes and cardiac ischemia127,128, where direct mitochondrial transplantation (administration of isolated mitochondria) offers rapid but transient metabolic support due to limited uptake and retention. In contrast, cell-based platforms like myeloid cell replacement enable persistent engraftment and provide an ongoing source of healthy mitochondria, supporting continuous transfer, tissue integration, and durable correction of mitochondrial dysfunction, an advantage for chronic or progressive mitochondrial diseases. Consistent with this, wild-type BMT improved survival, neurological function, and energy expenditure in a Leigh syndrome (Ndufs4−/−) mouse model, correlating with widespread donor-derived mitochondrial uptake129. Applying optimized microglia replacement approaches in such settings could further amplify these benefits by enhancing CNS engraftment and sustaining long-term mitochondrial delivery.

Methods

Mouse husbandry

Mice were housed in a 12:12-h dark/light cycle, temperature (20–22 °C), and humidity (30–70%)-controlled environment. Sterile food and water were provided ad libitum in the animal facilities at Stanford University. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health institutional guidelines and were approved by the Stanford University Administrative Panel on Laboratory Animal Care (IACUC 33941). Experiments examining microglia replacement, growth and survival were conducted using male and female mice. Behavioral, cardiac, histological/immunoblot and bioinformatic analyses, such as bulk and single-cell RNA-seq were conducted in female mice to minimize variability due to sex differences. At the end of each study, mice were deeply anesthetized with a Ketamine/Xylazine mixture (80 mg/kg Ketamine/16 mg/kg Xylazine, intraperitoneally) and underwent transcardial perfusion with 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS-1X, Fisher Scientific 10-010-023).

Mouse conditioning

Both male and female mice were used; sex-specific use for each downstream experiment is indicated in each method subsection and in the figure legends. Adult (8-10-week-old) C57BL/6J mice (Jax strain #000664) and YG8-800 mice: Fxnem2.1Lutzy Tg(FXN)YG8Pook/800J (Jax strain #030395) were conditioned with Busulfan (Sigma-Aldrich 14843) and administered intraperitoneally with busulfan (25 mg/kg/day) for 4 days, totaling 100 mg/kg, prior to transplant, as described in each study. PLX3397 (Pexidartib, MedChemExpress HY-16749) was administered by gavage at a dose of 100 mg/kg/day. The PLX3397 powder was dissolved in 100% DMSO and stored in aliquots at −80 °C. For administration, the PLX3397 stock was diluted in a 1:1 mixture of Polyethylene glycol Mn 400 (PEG400, Sigma-Aldrich 202398) and PBS-1X, pH 7.4, without calcium or magnesium (Fisher Scientific 10-010-023). The administration schedule and treatment period are detailed in the Fig. legend (Fig. 1A). The optimized PLX3397 conditioning regimen involved administering the drug by oral gavage for 6 days (600 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg/day), starting 15 days post-bone marrow transplantation.

Transplantation of total bone marrow

Bone marrow was harvested from adult (8–12-week-old) double-positive mice generated in our lab by crossing C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-EGFP) (Strain # 006567) and C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-mito:mKate) (Strain # 032188) mice, and from YG8-800 mice: Fxnem2.1Lutzy Tg(FXN)YG8Pook/800J (Strain # 030395). Total bone marrow cells were collected by flushing the tibiae and femurs with PBS-1X (Fisher Scientific 10-010-023) containing 4 U/mL Heparin (Sigma-Aldrich H3149-500KU). After collection, the bone marrow cells were filtered through a 30 μm cell strainer, washed twice with PBS-1X and resuspended in 100 μL of PBS-1X (1.5 × 108 cells/μL). Mice were transplanted with total bone marrow cells via intravenous injection into the retro-orbital sinus (1.5 × 107 cells/mouse) 24 h after busulfan conditioning.

Flow cytometry analyses of cells isolated from mouse hematopoietic tissues

Analyses were performed on male and female YG8-800 and wild-type mice at the indicated time points after BMT (up to 30 weeks). Mice were euthanized at designated time points for the analysis of donor chimerism in various tissues. Deep anesthesia was induced using a Ketamine/Xylazine mixture (80 mg/kg Ketamine/16 mg/kg Xylazine, intraperitoneally). Following transcardial perfusion with PBS-1X, tibiae, femurs, spleen, brain, and heart were harvested. Bone marrow cells (BM, from tibiae and femurs) and splenocytes (SP) were isolated in RPMI (Thermo Fisher Scientific 61870127) supplemented with 10% FBS, 4 U/mL Heparin (Sigma-Aldrich H3149-500KU) and 0.2 U/mL Deoxyribonuclease I (Worthington Biochemical Corporation LS002007) and filtered through a 30 μm cell strainer. Erythrocytes were lysed using the RBC lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 00-4333-57). Afterward, the cells were washed, resuspended in FACS-BL buffer, and maintained on ice until further processing. For flow cytometry staining, cells were blocked for 10 min with 10% vol/vol Mouse BD Fc Block™ (clone 2.4G2 BD Biosciences) and then stained in the dark for 30 min using the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD45.2 BV650 (clone 104 Biolegend), anti-mouse Ly6C PE-Cy7 (clone RB6-8C5 Biolegend), anti-mouse/human CD11b (clone M1/70 Biolegend), anti-mouse TER-119 AF700 (clone TER-119, Biolegend), anti-mouse CD4 APC (clone RM4-5 Biolegend), anti-mouse CD8a APC (clone 53-6.7 Biolegend), anti-mouse CD19 APC/Cy7 (clone 6D5 Biolegend), and dead cells were stained (Live/dead Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit; Invitrogen). The cells were then washed and resuspended in FACS-BL buffer. Stained cells were acquired using a BD FACSAria II cell sorter and conducted with BD FACSDiva software. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Dissociation of brain tissue and flow cytometric analysis of brain cells

Brains were collected from female YG8-800 and wild-type mice at 30 weeks after BMT and were dissociated following a modified version of the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit protocol (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-107-677). Mice were deeply anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine mixture and rapidly decapitated to minimize tissue damage and preserve cell viability. The extracted brains were washed three times with ice-cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS) containing calcium, magnesium, glucose, and pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 14287080). Tissue pieces were collected by centrifugation at 400 g for 5 min and dissociated with enzyme mixes 1 and 2 in gentleMACS C Tubes (Miltenyi Biotec 130-093-237) for 30 min using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi Biotec 130-096-427), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Digested samples were quenched with ice-cold D-PBS and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After debris and red blood cell removal, following the manufacturer’s instructions, the cell pellet was washed with FACs-BL and stored at 4 °C for further processing.

For flow cytometry staining, all brain cell pellets were resuspended in 10% vol/vol Mouse BD Fc Block™ (clone 2.4G2 BD Biosciences) for 10 min and stained in the dark for 30 min. For assessment of mKate2 uptake in different CNS cells, the cells were stained with: Anti-mouse CD45 PE-Cy7 (clone 30F11 Biolegend), anti-mouse/human CD11b-BV650 (clone M1/70 Biolegend), anti-mouse TER-119 AF700 (clone TER-119, Biolegend), anti-mouse CD90.2; Thy1.2 BV421 (clone 30-H12 Biolegend), anti-mouse ACSA-2 APC (Miltenyi Biotec 130-116-245), anti-mouse/human/rat O4 PE (Miltenyi Biotec 130-117-357), and dead cells were stained (Live/dead Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit; Invitrogen). For assessment of the immune cell composition of the brain, the cells were stained with antibody cocktail used characterize hematopoietic tissues as described above. Following staining, the cells were washed and resuspended in FACS-BL buffer. All the stained brain cells were acquired with a BD FACS Aria II cell sorter and BD FACSDiva software. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Image-enabled flow cytometry

Data were acquired using a BD FACSDiscover S8 Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences) equipped with BD CellView Image Technology and BD SpectralFX Technology. Conventional flow cytometry data and gating strategies were analyzed as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. Image-enabled events were exported as FCS files and analyzed in FlowJo software using the BD CellView Lens plugin to reconstruct and quantify cell images.

Digital Droplet PCR (ddPCR) analysis of human FXN in cells from YG8-800 mice

To determine the origin of GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells, we performed ddPCR on genomic DNA extracted from approximately 5 × 10⁴ FACS-sorted brain cells per group using QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Biosearch Technologies). A FAM-labeled probe specific to the region flanking exon 5 of the integrated human FXN YAC transgene was used to detect human FXN (hFXN) sequences, while a HEX-labeled probe targeting the murine Rosa26 locus in chromosome 9 served as an internal reference. ddPCR reactions (20 μL) contained 900 nM primers and 250 nM probe, and droplets were generated using a QX200 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad). PCR amplification was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 15 min; 45 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by 98 °C for 10 min and a 4 °C hold. Fluorescence signals (FAM and HEX) were quantified on a QX200 Droplet Reader, and data were analyzed using QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad). Primer and probe sequences were:

hFXN (FAM-labeled): F:5′-TTGTGGAATCAGTGGTTCATCT-3′; R:5′-CACTGGAAGACAAAACAAATGC-3′; Probe:5′-/56-FAM/AGGGGCTGT/ZEN/GCTGTGGAATTA/3IABkFQ/-3′. Rosa26 (HEX-labeled): F:5′-CTCAAGAGCAGAAGCATCCCT-3′; R: 5′-AAGCCACTGACTATGGTGCC-3′; Probe:5′-/5HEX/TGTCTCAA/ZEN/AGGAAGGATTGTCTGTGC/3IABkFQ/-3′.

Species-specific ddPCR quantification of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA

Approximately 50,000 co-cultured human FA fibroblasts, mouse double positive-donor macrophages, and human FA fibroblasts cultured alone were FAC-sorted and pelleted by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Total DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and quantified with a NanoDrop™ 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PrimeTime™ Std qPCR Assay probes and primer pairs (IDT), along with their amplicon lengths, are included in Supplementary Table 1.

Each 25 µL PCR reaction contained 1× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad), 1× CutSmart Buffer, 1 U AflII (NEB), 1× PrimeTime™ Std qPCR Assay mitochondrial DNA probe/primer set (IDT), 1× PrimeTime™ Std qPCR Assay genomic DNA probe/primer set (IDT), and 20 ng of total DNA. The probe/primer combinations used were Hs_MT-TL1 (FAM) with Hs_CCRL2 (HEX) and Mm_MT-TL1 (HEX) with Mm_CCRL2 (FAM). Each reaction was performed in triplicate. Droplet generation, PCR amplification, quantification and analysis were done as described for ddPCR analysis of human FXN in cells from YG8-800 mice.

Histological analyses

Brain and heart tissues were collected following transcardial perfusion with cold PBS-1X, then fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-281692). The fixed samples were washed once with PBS-1X and cryoprected by transferred to a 30% sucrose solution in PBS overnight. The tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT, Fisher Scientific 4585) and sectioned at 20 μm using a cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany, CM3050). Tissue sections were stored at −20 °C until further use. For imaging transplant-derived GFP+ cells in the brain and heart, slides were washed once with PBS-1X supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2 and 0.5 mM MgCl2 (PBS-1X++), counterstained with Hoechst 3342 (1:1000 dilution in PBS-1X, Thermo Fisher Scientific PI62249), and mounted in Aqua Poly/Mount (Polysciences 18606-20) for fluorescent microscopy. To stain microglia, we used a rabbit anti-mouse IBA-1 antibody diluted 1–100 (AB 8395040 FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and a secondary Goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 antibody diluted 1:500 (A21428 Thermo Fisher Scientific). To stain heart macrophages, we used rabbit anti-mouse CD68 antibody diluted 1–100 (InVivoPlus anti-mouse CD8α, BP0117, BioX Cell) and the same secondary.

Cell engraftment images in the brain and heart were visualized and captured using the BZ-X800 all-in-one fluorescence microscope and BZ-X800 software (Keyence, Itasca). To assess mitochondrial transfer in the brain, tissue slides were prepared for confocal imaging. Images were acquired using a 63X objective on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800). Image editing and analysis were performed using ZEN software (Zeiss). The number of GFP+ cells was quantified using 20X composite images of an entire brain sagittal section, with slides de-identified both before and after image acquisition to ensure unbiased quantification. Based on the acquired images, the engraftment rate was quantified as the ratio of GFP + /DAPI-positive cells. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software. Quantification was performed on ≥3 sections per mouse and ≥3 fields per section.

Isolation and processing of dorsal root ganglia

DRGs were isolated from 30-week-old female mice. Following euthanasia and PBS perfusion, the spinal column was excised, halved along the midline, and pinned medial side up in ice-cold PBS. The spinal cord and meninges were removed, and DRGs were gently lifted by their axon bundles, trimmed to remove residual tissue, and collected in cold HBSS for fixation. Dissections were completed within 30 min to preserve neuronal viability. DRG were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. Cryoprotection, embedding, sectioning and staining were the same as described above.

Isolation of immune cells from heart tissue

Heart tissues were minced and digested in 2 mL of 1× HBSS containing the following enzymes for 30 min at 37 °C: Liberase™ (Sigma-Aldrich, 05401119001; 0.125 mg/mL), Collagenase Type II (Sigma-Aldrich, C6885; 0.5 mg/mL), and Deoxyribonuclease I (Worthington, LS002007; 0.3 mg/mL). After digestion, the cell suspension was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer, resuspended in 33% isotonic Percoll PLUS (GE Healthcare, GE17-5445-01), and centrifuged at 700 × g for 15 min at 4 °C (brake 3) to remove fat and debris. The pellet was washed with ice-cold MACS buffer [PBS 1× with 0.5% bovine serum albumin fraction V (Sigma-Aldrich, 10735078001) and 0.002 mM EDTA pH 8.0 (Thermo Fisher, 15-575-020)], followed by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cells were kept on ice until staining. For flow cytometry, cells were incubated with 1% (v/v) Mouse BD Fc Block™ (clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences) for 10 min, then stained in the dark for 30 min with the antibody panel used for the analysis of mouse hematopoietic tissues.

Mouse survival

Survival was monitored in both male and female mice from 8 weeks of age; female mice were followed to 30 weeks and male mice to 26 weeks. Survival status was assessed weekly to daily, depending on animal status. Animals that died naturally or were euthanized at humane endpoints were recorded equivalently. Early euthanasia was performed in accordance with IACUC guidelines for ≥20% weight loss, inability to access food or water, chronic lesions, severe pain, labored breathing, paralysis, or other health complications that could negatively impact the welfare of the mice. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and groups were compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

A review of the available literature revealed that quantitative survival data for this model have not been explicitly reported. Two studies characterized YG8-800 mice up to 24–26 weeks of age61,63,109. Vicente-Acosta et al.17 documented survival up to 12 months (without specific rates) under their husbandry conditions, whereas Nishiyama et al.130 reported that up to 70% of male FRDA mice developed rectal prolapse by seven months, necessitating euthanasia. Collectively, these findings indicate that although YG8-800 mice can survive beyond 6 months, survival vary among colonies and is strongly influenced by environmental conditions such as diet, physical environment, husbandry practices, and institutional guidelines.

Mouse behavior general

Mice were housed in groups under a reversed light cycle (8:30 am Light OFF-8:30 pm Light ON), and behavior tests were conducted during the dark cycle at Stanford’s Behavioral and Functional Neuroscience Laboratory (SBFN) by an experimenter who was blinded to the experimental conditions. All behavioral testing was performed on female FA + FA, DP + FA, DP + WT, FA and WT mice between 21 and 22 weeks after bone marrow transplantation, corresponding to 29–30 weeks of age.

Cross Beam Test

The Cross Beam Test was conducted following the protocol described by Luong et al.131. The test apparatus consisted of a 1-m-long, 2 cm-wide Plexiglass beam with a central platform measuring 0.66 cm in both width and height. Mice were placed at one end of the beam and allowed to traverse to the opposite end. During the task, the time taken to cross the beam and the number of foot slips (when the mouse’s foot lost grip off the beam) were recorded. Each mouse underwent three trials, and the average values for each parameter were calculated for analysis.

Activity chamber

The locomotor assessment was conducted in an Open Field Activity Arena equipped with Activity Monitor Software-811 (Med Associates Inc., St. Albans, VT. Model ENV-515) and infrared detectors arranged in three planes, housed within a sound-attenuating chamber (Med Associates Inc., St. Albans, VT. MED-017M-027). The testing arena dimensions were 43 cm (L) × 43 cm (W) × 30 cm (H), and the sound-attenuating chamber measured 74 cm (L) × 60 cm (W) × 60 cm (H). Mice were placed in one corner of the testing arena and allowed to freely explore for 10 min, while an automated tracking system tracked their movements. Key parameter, including total distance traveled, velocity, rearing frequency, and times spent in periphery versus the center of the arena, were analyzed. The periphery was defined as the area within 5 cm of the arena wall. After each trial, the arena was cleaned with 1% Virkon solution to prevent cross-contamination.

Hanging wire test

The mice were weighed before the test. The four-limb hanging test was based on Kondziela’s inverted screen test where the mouse grasped a wire screen that was subsequently inverted132. The chronometer was started immediately after the screen was inverted over the cage, and the duration the mouse remained suspended was noted, with a maximum limit of 300 s. Three hanging trials were conducted, with at least a 2-min interval between each test. The longest duration the mouse hung from the screen was recorded for analysis.

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic assessments were conducted on female experimental cohorts between 21 and 22 weeks after bone marrow transplantation, corresponding to the age of 7–8 months for each group. Mice were anesthetized using 0.5–1% isoflurane, and heart function was assessed using the FUJIFILM VisualSonics Vevo 2100 ultrasound system equipped with the MS 550D probe. B-mode videos and M-mode images were acquired to assess systolic and diastolic functions. Key parameters, including Ejection Fraction (EF), Fractional Shortening (FS), Left Ventricular Volume in Systole (LVVsys), and Left Ventricular Posterior Wall Systolic (LVPWS) were quantified for each animal.

Primary macrophage and fibroblast cultures

Primary macrophages were isolated from the bone marrow of 10-week-old DP (GFP/mKate2+) mice. Bone marrow cells were obtained by flushing the tibias and femurs with PBS-1X (Fisher Scientific, 10-010-023) containing 4 U/mL heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3149-500KU). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 30 μm cell strainer and washed twice with PBS. The isolated cells were then cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, 10378-016) supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin-Glutamine (Gibco 10378-016), and 25 ng/mL M-CSF at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 7 days. After the incubation period, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing 2% FBS, 1% PS, 25 ng/mL M-CSF, and 50 ng/mL GM-CSF.

Mouse FA fibroblasts were isolated from the ear pinnae skin tissues of YG8-800 and WT mice (6–8 weeks old). The cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (Fisher Scientific, 10-569-044) supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% PSG at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Human FA fibroblasts were obtained from the Coriell Institute for Medical Research (Camden, NJ, USA) under catalog number GM04078. These cells carry FXN alleles with 541 and 420 repeats at the time of sampling.

Non-contact co-culture assay

Non-contact co-culture experiments were performed using Transwell inserts (24-mm diameter, polycarbonate membrane; Corning) with 0.4-µm pore size. Inserts were placed in 6-well plates containing acceptor fibroblasts cells. 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 1 mL of complete medium and cultured until cell attachment was achieved. Double-positive macrophages (mDP-Mø) were seeded in the upper compartment at 1 × 10⁵ cells per insert in 1 mL of medium. After cell attachment, both compartments were gently washed twice with PBS and switched to a co-culture medium composed of primary macrophage medium and FA fibroblast medium mixed at a 1:1 ratio. The co-cultures were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 48–72 h. At the end of the co-culture period, cells were collected for downstream analyses.

Mitochondrial staining and function in co-culture

Mitochondrial staining was performed using BioTracker 405 Blue Mitochondria Dye (Sigma-Aldrich, SCT135) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The dye was added to the respective cell culture media at a final concentration of 100 nM, and cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 8 h. After staining, excess dye was removed by washing the cells twice with 1× PBS (Fisher Scientific, 10-010-023). The washed cells were then utilized in co-culture experiments. In co-culture experiments, mouse or human fibroblasts were mixed with DP macrophages at a 1:1 ratio in their respective culture media, according to the experimental conditions. Co-cultures were maintained for 48 to 72 h to facilitate mitochondrial transfer. To confirm the presence of CD11b+ macrophages in the mouse FA fibroblast and DP macrophage co-culture, CD11b antibody was used for flow cytometry analysis. For the human FA fibroblast and DP macrophages co-culture, CD90, a fibroblast-specific marker, was used.

To assess changes in mitochondrial respiratory function, a Seahorse Mito Stress assay (Agilent, USA) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, 20,000 cells were seeded in 96-well Seahorse plates after the specified co-culture experiments, and the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured and normalized to cell counts by DAPI staining. The culture medium was replaced with Agilent Seahorse XF DMEM Basal Media, supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 10 nM glucose, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Inhibitors, prepared in the same media, were injected during the assay at the following final concentrations: oligomycin (2 μM), FCCP (2 μM), and rotenone and antimycin A (1 μM).

Confocal microscopy of cells

For fluorescence microscopy, cells were cultured on 20 nm coverslips (Cell Treat, 229173) and fixed at room temperature for 15 min using 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences). After fixation, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS for 5 min each. Nuclear staining was performed by incubating the cells with DAPI/Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62249) for 5 min, followed by three washes with 1× PBS. The coverslips were mounted on glass slides, and images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800). Image analysis and processing were conducted using ZEN imaging software.

Analysis of co-cultured cell populations by flow cytometry

For flow cytometry analysis, cell suspensions were prepared at a concentration of 1–5 million cells/ml and incubated with antibodies in the dark for 30 min. The following antibodies were used: anti-mouse/human CD11b-APC (clone M1/70, BioLegend), anti-human CD90-APC (clone 5E10, BioLegend). Viability was assessed using eFluor™780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 65-0865-18). After incubation, the cells were washed and resuspended in FACS-BL buffer. Stained cells were analyzed or sorted using a BD FACSAria II cell sorter, and all data were processed using FlowJo software. Following the analysis, the sorted cells were counted and prepared for subsequent experiments.

Single-cell RNA sequencing- Sample preparation

Brain cells were isolated from three female DP + FA mice at 30 weeks after BMT as described in the Dissociation of Brain Tissue and Flow Cytometric Analysis of Brain Cells section. To obtain sufficient numbers of GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells for scRNA-seq, samples from three mice were pooled prior to FACS sorting. Pooling was done to ensure the optimal input of ~100,000 viable cells per sorted population (mKate2⁻ and mKate2⁺ FA acceptor cells), guaranteeing successful sequencing and downstream transcriptomic analysis. In a typical brain dissociation, each half-brain yields 1.0–2.5 × 10⁶ live cells, of which ~30% are microglia and ~70% are non-microglial. The targeted GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ population represents about 10% of the non-microglial cells, corresponding to ~7 × 10⁴–1.75 × 10⁵ cells per sample. After accounting for ~50% sorting recovery, 3.5 × 10⁴–8.75 × 10⁴ viable GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells were theoretically available for downstream analysis. In a representative sample used for scRNA-seq, 623,251 viable cells were obtained in total, including 137,115 microglia and 53,475 recovered recipient GFP⁻/mKate2⁺ cells.

Dead cells were excluded using the Live/Dead Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen), and gating on a BD FACSAria II was used to remove nonviable cells. GFP⁺ donor cells were separated from GFP⁻ acceptor cells, and within the GFP⁻ population, mKate2⁺ (mitochondria-receiving) and mKate2⁻ (non-receiving) FA acceptor brain cells were identified and sorted. Dissociated single cells were washed with RNase-free PBS containing 0.1% BSA and collected into ice-cold PBS with 0.1% BSA. Sorted viable mKate2⁺ and mKate2⁻ populations were submitted to MedGenome Inc. (Foster City, CA, USA) for scRNA-seq using the Chromium Controller and Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits (10x Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 100-bp reads.

Single-cell RNA sequencing- data pre-processing and cell type annotation

The Illumina raw BCL sequencing files were processed through the CellRanger software (10x Genomics) for generating FASTQ files and count matrices (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger/latest/analysis/running-pipelines/cr-gex-count). Feature-barcode matrices obtained from Cellranger count for both samples were processed using the ‘Read10X()’ function from Seurat package (v5.1)133. Next, cell filtering was performed based on nFeature_RNA (>300), nCount_RNA (>500) and the percentage of counts from mitochondrial genes (percent mt <10). After filtering, 12,347 GFP− recipient cells (3196 mKate2− and 9151 mKate2+) from three biologically independent DP + FA recipient mice (pooled), were retained for downstream analysis; the individual cell was the unit of statistical analysis. Normalization and scaling were performed and the top 2000 genes with the highest standardized variance were used to identify significant principal components (PCs). These PCs were utilized to identify clusters using Seurat’s FindClusters() function which employs graph-based community method. The resulting clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) Dimension Reduction method (Fig. 4). To annotate these clusters, we first identified top differentially expressed genes per cluster using the FindAllMarkers() function (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with P values Bonferroni-adjusted for the number of genes tested) and then manually annotated them using curated gene lists (Supplementary Data 1).

Single-cell RNA sequencing- DGE and pathway analysis

Next, differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was conducted between mKate2+ Acceptor FA cells and mKate2- Acceptor FA cells for each of the twelve detected cell types. We used the FindMarkers() function with MAST134 where genes that were expressed in at least 25% of cells in either population were considered (min.pct set to 0.25). The MAST hurdle model was applied as a two-sided test, and the resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across genes within each cell type using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure; genes with an adjusted P < 0.05 and a fold change > 20% (|log2FC|> 0.263) were considered significantly differentially expressed (Fig. 4E–I). Volcano plots were generated via the EnhancedVolcano package in R135. Commonly differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Data 2) were identified by extracting the average log2 fold change (log2FC) values of significant differentially expressed genes (adjusted p < 0.05) in each cell type. The common expression score (CES) for each gene was calculated by subtracting the number of cell types in which the gene was upregulated (average log2FC > 0) from the number of cell types for which the gene was downregulated (average log2FC < 0).

We also conducted gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) separately for each cell type on the corresponding differentially expressed genes via FGSEA136 (v1.26) (two-sided) on all biological process gene sets from MSIGDBR (v7.5.1). Commonly upregulated and downregulated gene sets were identified by extracting the normalized enrichment score (NES) for significantly enriched gene sets (p < 0.05 and q < 0.25). The common pathway score (CPS) for each gene set was calculated by subtracting the number of cell types in which the pathway was positively enriched (NES > 0) from the number of cell types in which the pathway was negatively enriched (NES < 0). We also conducted enrichment network analysis on genes with CES > 6 and CES < −6 via Metascape137.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Whole heart tissue was collected from WT and YG8-800 mice at 20- and 22-weeks post-bone marrow transplantation (BMT), following conditioning with Busulfan (BU) and PLX3397. The time points corresponded to an age of 7–8 months for each group. Prior to tissue collection, echocardiography was performed to evaluate cardiac function. Following two washes with cold 1x PBS, the heart tissues were dissected in cold 1x PBS using a sterile knife to facilitate efficient RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from the minced heart tissue of each mouse group using the Invitrogen™ PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (#12-183-018 A), following the manufacturer’s instructions for RNA purification. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by MedGenome Inc. (Foster City, CA, USA) using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep kit and Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with paired-end 100 base pair (bp) reads.

Quality control and read alignment were performed by MedGenome. Data quality control was performed via FastQC (v0.11.9). Low-quality sequence reads were excluded, and adapter sequences were trimmed using fastq-mcf (v1.05) and cutadapt (v4.7). Removal of other unwanted sequences (mitochondrial genome sequences, ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, adapter sequences, etc.) was performed via Bowtie2 (v2.5.3). Paired-end reads were aligned to the Ensembl Mus musculus genome (GRCm39) via STAR (2.7.11b). Raw read counts were estimated from the aligned reads using HTSeq (v2.0.5). FPKM (Fragments per kilobase per million) expression values for each gene were estimated from the aligned reads using cufflinks (v2.2.1).

Principal component analysis was performed for each of the eight samples using FPKM expression values via the prcomp base function in R135. FPKM expression values for selected genes were log-transformed prior to computing Z-scores for visualizing relative gene expression across samples. Hierarchical clustering and heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap function from pheatmap (v1.0.12) with default settings.

Differential gene expression DGE analysis between DP + FA (n = 3) mice and FA + FA (n = 3) mice was conducted from the raw count data using DESeq2 (v1.40.2), applying the two-sided Wald test with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted on the resulting differential expression results (genes ranked by the DESeq2 Wald test statistic) via FGSEA136 (v1.26), two-sided, on all biological process gene sets from MSIGDBR (v7.5.1), with P values adjusted for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure.

Statistical analyses

All the data presented in this manuscript are expressed as either the mean ± standard deviation of the mean (SD) or mean ± standard error mean (SE, for behavioral analyses, n > 10). The number of samples, denoted as n, refers to the individual mouse for in vivo experiments (where n = 1 per mouse) or to the number of independent biological replicates for in vitro experiments (where one independent biological replicate corresponds to n = 1). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11 (GraphPad Software). Parametric tests were applied to data that followed a normal distribution, as assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The following statistical analyses were as follows: two-tailed unpaired t-test for comparison between two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction for comparisons involving more than two groups, or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Sidak’s post hoc tests for comparisons with multiple variables. The significance threshold for all parametric tests was set at α = 0.05, with all tests being two-sided. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific statistical tests applied to each data set are described in the The exact p-values for each relevant comparison are provided in the Source Data file.
