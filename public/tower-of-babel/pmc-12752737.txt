Gastrointestinal complications in intensive care
Authors: Liping Liu, Jiali Duan, Wangping He, Christian Waydhas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal (GI) complications are prevalent and severe clinical challenges encountered in critically ill patients. They are closely linked to disease progression, increased morbidity and mortality, and escalating healthcare expenses. This comprehensive review summarizes the epidemiology of GI complications in critically ill patients, elucidating the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms including hemodynamic alterations, inflammatory cascades, neuro-endocrine dysregulation, and gut microbiota imbalance. It confers distinctive clinical manifestations and effective diagnostic approaches. Therapeutic strategies, encompassing nutritional support, pharmacological management, and surgical or interventional procedures will be discussed. The review also briefly introduces the concept of the “gut-organ axis,” emphasizing how intestinal barrier disruption and dysbiosis can disseminate inflammatory and metabolic signals to distant organs such as the lungs, liver, kidneys, and brain, thereby underscoring the need for clinicians to recognize systemic effects. In essence, prompt identification and multimodal intervention are pivotal for optimizing outcomes in critically ill patients; judiciously addressing GI complications in clinical decision-making can mitigate morbidity and enhance both short-term and long-term prognosis.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) complications are highly prevalent among critically ill patients and have become an important focus in intensive care medicine. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 50 %–60 % of intensive care unit (ICU) patients develop at least one abdominal symptom during their hospitalization, and about one third experience two or more symptoms [1], [2], [3]. These complications may occur as primary manifestations of abdominal disease or as secondary sequelae of systemic illnesses such as sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Common clinical features include abnormal or absent bowel sounds, vomiting, diarrhea, gastric retention, bowel distension, and gastrointestinal bleeding [2], 4]. The occurrence of these complications has been closely linked to hemodynamic instability, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, and systemic inflammatory responses [5], 6]. They not only impair nutritional absorption and gastrointestinal motility but also contribute to prolonged ICU stays and significantly increased mortality [7]. Despite their clinical importance, gastrointestinal dysfunction has not yet been incorporated into most organ failure scoring systems, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), resulting in its frequent under-recognition in critical care practice.

The GI tract plays a dual role during critical illness – it is both a target organ suffering from systemic inflammatory and circulatory disturbances, and a promotor organ capable of propagating inflammatory cascades and metabolic disturbances throughout the body potentially worsening preexistent or leading to MODS [8], [9], [10], [11]. In addition, recent research has revealed that the gut is not merely an isolated organ but rather a highly integrated system linked to multiple physiological networks, including the immune, endocrine, metabolic, and nervous systems. These multidirectional communications have given rise to the concept of the “gut-organ axes,” encompassing strong interactions between the gut with other organs such as the brain, lung, liver, heart, and kidneys [12]. These emerging findings have expanded the traditional understanding of gastrointestinal pathology and provided a theoretical foundation for viewing the gut as a central immunometabolic hub in critical illness.

However, despite extensive research, several challenges persist in the assessment and management of GI dysfunction in ICU settings. Early identification remains difficult due to nonspecific symptoms, limited bedside diagnostic tools, and the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, previous studies have often focused on single complications – such as stress-related mucosal disease, gastroparesis, diarrhea, or intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) – without integrating them into a cohesive framework that reflects their interrelated mechanisms. As a result, the existing literature provides fragmented insights into the complex interactions between the gastrointestinal tract and other organ systems, and there is still a lack of comprehensive synthesis that connects traditional clinical observations with modern mechanistic evidence.

This review therefore seeks to bridge this gap by providing a multidimensional and integrative analysis of gastrointestinal complications in critically ill patients. The objective of this review is to elucidate the pathophysiology, diagnostic strategies, and therapeutic interventions related to gastrointestinal complications in critically ill patients, while highlighting recent advancements. By synthesizing evidence across molecular, physiological, and clinical domains, this work aims to construct a holistic framework for understanding gastrointestinal dysfunction and its systemic implications. Ultimately, this review underscores the importance of the gastrointestinal tract as both a sentinel and a therapeutic target in critical illness, providing new theoretical and clinical perspectives for improving patient outcomes and guiding future research in precision critical care.

Gastrointestinal physiological function and pathophysiological process of GI injury

Currently, there is no universally accepted definition of GI dysfunction. Chinese Guidelines on nutritional support define GI dysfunction as a state in which the GI tract exhibits a diminished capacity to absorb macronutrients and/or water and electrolytes, as well as impaired GI barrier function and/or motility disorders [13].

GI functions mainly encompass a range of processes, including peristalsis with protrusion of GI contents, digestion and absorption of intestinal substances, mucosal barrier function, endocrine function and immune function. Any disturbance of these functions, whether occurring alone or in combination, can adversely affect the GI system and result in dysfunction or even the failure of the GI-tract in severely ill patients [14].

The barrier against pathogenic agents, closely intertwined with most other functions of the GI-tract, plays a pivotal role in conjunction with normal peristalsis. One of its primary roles is the elimination, removing, and blocking of harmful substances, while simultaneously maintaining GI hemostasis. The barrier function can be further subdivided into four distinct aspects: chemical, mechanical, biological and immune barriers:(1)Secreted gastric acid, bile, lysozyme, proteolytic enzymes, and other chemical substances possess certain bactericidal effects, forming a chemical barrier.(2)The intact and undamaged mucosa epithelium and thick mucus covering the epithelial surface constitute a mechanical barrier.(3)The complex and stable intestinal flora, and microenvironment formed by the interaction with intestinal innate microorganisms constitute a biological barrier, that produces colonization resistance to pathogenic bacteria.(4)Secreted immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) form the immune barrier [15].

These barriers serve as the initial line of defense against the invasion of intestinal pathogenic bacteria. Damage in any one area comprises the intestinal barrier function, leading to bacterial and endotoxin translocation and the development of enterogenic sepsis, which is a significant contributor to “MODS [16], [17], [18].”

Various factors can disrupt this physiological protection. Understanding these processes is essential for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

The body’s inflammatory response and circulatory shock redistribute the blood flow throughout the body. Notably, GI blood flow is particularly reduced resulting in tissue hypoxia, insufficient energy supply, and reduced secretion of hormones and digestive substances. These factors initially damage the chemical barrier. The reduced mucosal oxygen supply leads to local acidosis and compromises the mucosal epithelium further weakening the mechanical barrier. Additionally, changes in GI motility, acid secretion, and the use of antibacterial drugs, vasopressors, hypovolemia as well as fluid overload disrupt the intestinal flora and compromise the biological barrier. The impaired immune function in sepsis facilitates the invasion of intestinal bacteria and toxins, leading to continuous activation of the liver and intestinal leukocyte systems. Further inflammatory mediators are releases, perpetuating a vicious cycle, that further exacerbates GI disfunction and further propagates the condition [19], 20].

Furthermore, an accumulating body of evidence indicates that gut microorganisms play a pivotal role in preserving intestinal health and immune function. Critically ill patients frequently encounter intestinal flora imbalance due to factors such as intestinal dysfunction,the extensive antibiotic usage, compromised immune system function, and malnutrition. The disruption of the intestinal flora’s equilibrium can result in the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria and hinder the growth of beneficial bacteria [21], 22]. The imbalance can further deteriorate the intestinal barrier function and escalate the risk of intestinal inflammation and infection [23], 24]. Studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between intestinal flora imbalance and various GI complications, including stress ulcers, GI bleeding, intestinal infections, pseudomembranous colitis, and others [25]. In recent years, a growing number of studies have proposed that the intestinal microbiota causes functional disorders in other organs outside the intestine, collectively known as the gut organ axis [8], 26]. The gut microbiota, and microbial-derived chemicals can regulate pathophysiological processes across multiple organs, including metabolic state, immune response, and more, by influencing immune cell function, intestinal permeability, and other undisclosed mechanisms [27], 28].

In addition, the enteric nervous system (ENS) is a crucial component of GI physiology, where interactions between neurons and glia regulate gut function. Enteric glia, which expresses muscarinic receptors, plays a role in monitoring acetylcholine and contributes to the physiological regulation of gut motility [29]. Furthermore, the GABAergic system within the GI tract is also involved in regulating secretion, motility, and visceral pain, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for GI disorders [30].

In critical condition, neuroendocrine disorders can affect GI function through various mechanisms. One notable mediator is Ghrelin, which is also known as growth-stimulating releasing peptide. It is a polypeptide composed of 28 amino acids, primarily produced by oxyntic gland X/A-like cells of the gastric fundus mucosa, accounting for about one-fifth of the total number of gastric mucosal cells. Ghrelin is widely distributed throughout the body, including the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, intestines, kidneys, pancreas, and placenta. As an endogenous ligand of growth hormone secretagogue receptor, Ghrelin not only stimulates the release of growth hormone from the pituitary gland but also serve as an important intermediary between the central nervous system and peripheral tissues involved in nutrient metabolism, helping to facilitate food intake, improve GI function, regulate lipid metabolism and energy balance, and more. Ghrelin regulates GI motility and mucosal protection [31]. Similarly, orexins, neuropeptides that promote arousal and are synthesized and secreted in the lateral hypothalamus region, influence feeding behavior and central regulation of gut physiology [32], 33]. Corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) can impact GI motility and permeability during stress, resulting in delayed gastric emptying and disordered bowel movement [34].

Furthermore, the extensive use of analgesic and sedative, antibiotic, and vasoactive drugs, can have direct or indirect negative impact on the GI tract. Sedatives and muscle relaxants, commonly used in critically ill patients, may exacerbate GI dysfunction by affecting the neuroendocrine system [35]. Vasoactive drugs (such as norepinephrine, epinephrine, etc.) can reduce intestinal blood flow, potentially aggravating intestinal ischemia and the occurrence of GI complications.

In conclusion, GI complications in critically ill patients arise from the combined actions of multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, involving factors such as systemic inflammatory responses, hemodynamic changes, intestinal flora imbalance, and neuroendocrine disorders. These mechanisms are intertwined with each other, leading to the occurrence of complications such as impaired GI barrier, decreased intestinal function, and increased risk of infection, thereby affecting the clinical outcome of patients. Understanding these pathophysiological mechanisms is essential for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies (Figure 1).

Assessment of GI dysfunction in intensive care

The diagnosis of GI dysfunction in critically ill patients is challenging, due to the inability of these patients to articulate their symptoms and the limited understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms. Furthermore, the absence of pathognomonic findings at the bedside exacerbates the diagnostic process [36]. Currently, the diagnosis of GI dysfunction primarily relies on a combined assessment of clinical symptoms, imaging examinations, biomarkers and scores (Figure 2). Abdominal pain, abnormal findings on percussion and palpation, diarrhea, prolonged absence of bowel movement, presence and characteristics of intestinal rumbling, bloating, vomiting and reflux, and high gastric residual volume (GRV) are among the clinical manifestations of GI dysfunction [1], 37]. However, a single symptom may not be sufficient to fully reflect its severity. Abdominal pain can be assessed and localized, providing valuable diagnostic information. However, an appraisal is not feasible in coma patients, and the incidence of abdominal pain in critically ill patients and its correlation with prognosis remains unclear. Furthermore, the increasing prevalence of obese patients poses challenges in accurately assessing abdominal distension within this population [38]. Traditionally, the presence and characteristics of intestinal rumbling sounds are being used to indicate disease such as intestinal obstruction. However, its reliability is questioned, due to the lack of a clear correlation between intestinal rumbling, recovery, and dynamic recovery. Similarly, the absence of bowel sound exhibits low sensitivity and specificity for bowel obstruction [39]. Vomiting may signify various GI disorders, but small reflux may be overlooked in mechanically ventilated patients [40].

However, the significance of GRV measurement remains a subject of debate. Some evidence suggests that routine monitoring of GRV may not enhance patient outcomes and could potentially result in unnecessary interruptions of enteral feeds. Conversely, other studies suggest that GRV measurement has a limited effect on morbidity or mortality in mechanically ventilated patients receiving enteral nutrition. Yet, high GRV has emerged as an important component in the definition of enteral feeding intolerance (EFI) [42].

Diarrhea may indicate an intestinal infection, ischemia, or malabsorption. Diarrhea is frequently observed in ICU patients and necessitates meticulous evaluation to ascertain its clinical significance [43].

IAP measurement serves as another clinical tool to indirectly assess GI function and facilitate the diagnosis of a potentially life-threatening condition. Intestinal dilation and/or intestinal edema as well as other intraabdominal fluid accumulation may lead to IAH, which is associated with a poor prognosis in critically ill patients. IAH can further progress to ACS resulting in abdominal and remote organ dysfunction. IAP should be monitored every 8 h for early detection if any of the following conditions or risk factors is present on admission to the ICU: (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 27 kg/m2; (2) positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)≥ 7 cmH2O; (3) abdominal distension; (4) the absence of bowel sounds; or (5) positive fluid balance during the ICU stay [44]. IAP can be readily estimated by measuring bladder pressure, which can be performed in any ICU that utilizes invasive vascular pressure monitoring. A comprehensive description of the procedure is provided by Sugrue et al. [45].

The development of a Core Outcome Set (COS) for daily monitoring of GI function in critically ill patients, as established by the COSMOGI project, provides a standardized framework to enhance consistency and comparability in future study [46]. This framework encompasses essential signs and symptoms such as abdominal distension, bowel dilatation, and IAP.

The intricate nature of GI dysfunction and its assessment in critical illness, coupled with the heterogeneity of the patient population, presents a challenge in determining the reliability and clinical applicability of any tool designed to evaluate GI dysfunction in intensive care settings. A combination of various signs and symptoms may be more appropriate for predicting an increase in ICU mortality, which is a common surrogate marker [7]. Key criteria for assessing acute GI dysfunction include grading systems such as the acute GI injury (AGI) scale and the GI failure (GIF) score [43] as well as serum biomarkers like d-lactate, diamine oxidase, and citrulline [47], 48].

The grading criteria for the severity of GI dysfunction can refer to the classification of AGI [49] which is divided into four grades according to the severity of clinical manifestations (Table 1). It is a commonly used clinical grading standard for GI dysfunction and is associated with the prognosis of patients [51], 52]. The GI Dysfunction Score (GIDS) is a tool for assessing GI function in ICU patients, and incorporates a variety of clinical signs and symptoms [53], 54] (Table 2). One study indicated that GIDS combined with the SOFA showed better accuracy and reliability in evaluating the mortality rate of severely ill patients at 28 and 90 days than the SOFA alone, suggesting that GIDS combined with SOFA score could improve the accuracy of mortality prediction [56], but further validation is still needed in different ICU populations.

Imaging techniques are cornerstones for the diagnosis of (anatomic) intraabdominal pathologies. While abdominal X-rays are available at the bedside, their diagnostic yield remains low and unspecific: Free intraabdominal air can only be detected in lateral views, which are difficult to obtain in most ICU patients. Typical air-fluid levels, indicative for ileus, can be missed in the X-rays taken in supine position. Abdominal X-rays may help to differentiate between distension of the large and the small intestine. Imaging techniques with a high diagnostic yield, such as computed tomography (CT), angiography (including CT-angiography), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) require the transport of the critically ill patient to the radiology suite. They are particularly valuable in the evaluation of conditions that may require surgical or radiological intervention, such as dilated bowel, intestinal gas, intestinal infarction and other vascular conditions, thickening of the bowel wall, and perforation) [57], [58], [59]. In the past, there has been concern, that the intravascular application of contrast media may further harm kidney function of already severely compromised critically ill patients. Recent studies, reviews and metanalysis have shown, however, that the application of intravascular contrast media is safe in this population and the benefits significantly outweigh the risks [60], [61], [62]. One shortcoming of the imaging tests may be the need for dynamic (and repeated) assessment, and the risk of missed diagnosis in the early stages of the disease. Nuclide scintigraphy is the gold standard for gastric emptying assessment, but it is expensive and complex and is not available in critically ill patients in the ICU [63].

Abdominal ultrasound can be performed at the bedside and repeated with ease. It is the diagnostic test of choice for detecting free abdominal fluid as well as liver and biliary tract pathologies. Its role in the diagnosis of gastric emptying, residual gastric volume, bowel diameter and peristalsis, bowel wall thickness, and mesenteric ischemia is less clear. Mihnovits et al. recently presented an up-to-date and extensive review on GI ultrasound in critically ill patients [64]. A Gastrointestinal Injury Ultrasound Score (AGIUS) of more than two points has been shown to predict feeding intolerance in critically ill patients [65]. Other gastrointestinal ultrasound protocols, such as the Gastrointestinal and Urinary Tract Sonography (GUTS) [66], the Transabdominal GastroIntestinal Ultrasound (TGIU) score [67]or the Lai’s protocol [68] have been suggested. These protocols have been used to complement the AGI score, evaluating GI function with indicators such as bowel diameter, bowel wall thickness, and motility [65], 66]. Several pitfalls and shortcomings of abdominal ultrasound in the intensive care setting must be considered. The examination is challenging and observer-dependent, and requires considerable routine and experience. It is time-consuming, requiring up to 30 min or more. Topographical variability, the dynamic nature of bowel walls, and interference with gas collection can pose significant challenges [64].

Electrogastroscopy (electrogastrography, EGG) and electrodietescopy (Electroenterography, EEnG) are new non-invasive technique for quantitatively visualizing GI motility in critically ill patients. They could provide valuable quantitative information about GI motility, potentially aiding in the identification of risk factors and prognostic factors for GI dysfunction [69].

Various biomarkers have been investigated to assess GI function and EFI in critically ill patients [70]. Enterocyte function markers such as citrulline, serum intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP), Intracellular bile acid-binding protein (I-BABP), acetylcholine heparin-binding protein (HBP) and Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), d-lactate, and smooth muscle actin (SMA) have been extensively studied, but their results have been inconclusive [47], [71], [72], [73], [74], [75].

Currently, there is no single method for continuous and accurate monitoring of GI function. Evaluation remains intermittent and largely confined to clinical assessment. Consequently, there are currently no established biomarkers of GI function and/or response to enteral nutrition.

Common GI complications in critically ill patients

Common GI complications in critically ill patients include GI bleeding, feeding intolerance, diarrhea or pseudomembranous enteritis, GI motility disorders such as bowel obstruction or Ogilvie syndrome, iIAH/ACS, and Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI) (Figure 3) and will be discussed in the following chapters.

GI bleeding

Current evidence indicates that clinically significant GI bleeding affects roughly 1 % of ICU patients, with a higher prevalence of 3 %–4 % in patients who are invasively ventilated or have coagulation abnormalities, making it both a relatively common and serious complication that substantially worsens morbidity and mortality in the critically ill [76], [77], [78]. GI bleeding can manifest either in the upper or lower GI tract. Common causes of upper GI bleeding include stress ulcers, gastric and duodenal ulcers, and esophageal varices [79]. Lower GI bleeding may be caused by diverticular disease, hemorrhoids, vascular maldevelopment, infectious colitis, ischemic bowel disease, and inflammatory bowel disease [80].

Recent data challenge the notion that mechanical ventilation is an independent risk factor. The prophylactic use of acid-suppressing drugs such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or H2-receptor antagonists is common practice [85], 86]. However, while acid-suppressing drugs are effective in preventing GI bleeding, their impact on mortality remains uncertain [84], 87], 88].

For critically ill patients experiencing upper GI bleeding, early bedside endoscopy is the method of choice both leading to diagnosis and allowing therapeutic interventions to achieve hemostasis. It has been observed that early upper GI endoscopy (EGD) within 24 h after the recognition of the bleeding increased the detection rate of bleeding lesions and the success rate of endoscopic hemostasis [89]. In contrast, the effectiveness of early colonoscopy in managing lower GI bleeding appears to be limited due to inadequate bowel preparation and interference from blood [89]. Colonoscopy performed within 24 h of admission showed no reduction of the risk of rebleeding or mortality among patients hospitalized with acute lower GI bleeding [90].

A recent guideline provides recommendations for the treatment of upper GI bleedings [82], 91]. It has to be emphasized that hemoglobin levels should not exceed 8–10 g/L during fluid and blood management. Early administration of tranexamic acid appears to be beneficial in patients with upper GI bleeding, but not with lower GI bleeding [92], 93]. Patients with coagulopathy, or antithrombotic and anticoagulant medication require specific attention [94].

Feeding intolerance

Feeding intolerance in critically ill patients is common ICU patients, resulting in inadequate nutrient intake and adverse clinical outcomes. Its incidence ranges from 29 % to 60 % and is associated with various factors, including patient age, body mass index, disease severity (as assessed by the APACHE II score), renal insufficiency, GI disorders, hypoalbuminemia, sepsis, and mechanical ventilation [95], [96], [97].

In critically ill COVID-19 patients, the incidence of feeding intolerance is also high and correlates with poorer clinical outcomes, such as longer ICU stays and higher in-hospital mortality rates [98]. Furthermore, AGI and feeding intolerance are considered independent predictors of poor prognosis in COVID-19 patients [99].

To address feeding intolerance in critically ill patients, various interventions have been explored. For instance, enteral nutrition employing peptide-based formulas has demonstrated to reduce the incidence of GI intolerance and hyperglycemia [100]. Additionally, fiber supplements have been shown to significantly lower the risk of diarrhea, reflux, vomiting, and constipation, while also improving clinical outcomes [101].

Ultrasound examination may be used to predict the occurrence of feeding intolerance. Studies have found that the cross-sectional area of the antrum and the ultrasound score for AGI can serve as independent predictors of feeding intolerance and 28-day mortality [67]. Additionally, measurements of antral echo density are utilized to assess the efficacy of prokinetic drugs, enabling clinicians to determine whether to administer prokinetic drugs or place a pyloric tube [102].

By identifying and managing relevant risk factors, as well as adopting appropriate nutritional strategies and technical means, it is feasible to improve the nutritional status and possibly clinical outcomes of patients.

Diarrhea/pseudomembranous colitis

The incidence of diarrhea is reported to vary between 37 and 74 %. This condition can result in prolonged hospital stays and increased mortality [103]. The occurrence of diarrhea is associated with multiple factors, including the use of antibiotics, enteral nutrition, and PPIs [104], 105]. Notably, antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD) is prevalent particularly when enzyme inhibitor antibiotics are used [106]. Furthermore, the use of PPIs is considered an independent risk factor for Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea (CDAD) [104].

While enteral nutrition is the preferred route of nutritional support for ICU patients, but it well-known to cause diarrhea. It has been observed that the incidence of enteral nutrition-associated diarrhea is positively correlated with feeding days and length of ICU stay [107]. The composition of enteral nutrition, particularly a high osmolarity and high fiber content, may also contribute to the risk of diarrhea [103].

For treatment, the use of probiotics has been shown to reduce the incidence of diarrhea in some mechanically ventilated critically ill patients [108]. To effectively manage diarrhea in ICU patients, it is recommended to adopt clear and specific concepts regarding the type of enteral nutrition used in general and for specific patient populations. In the event of diarrhea, a multidisciplinary approach involving dieticians, pharmacists, and microbiologists is essential to identify the potential cause and potentially adapt the type and volume of enteral nutrition.

For very severe, life-threatening AAD fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) maybe considered a rescue therapy [109].

Pseudomembranous colitis is a severe complication in critically ill patients, frequently associated with antibiotic use, particularly related to Clostridioides difficile (formerly known as Clostridium difficile) infection. However, pseudomembranous colitis is not always caused by Clostridioides difficile, as other pathogens and factors can also lead to similar intestinal damage [110]. In certain cases, pseudomembranous colitis may be caused by cytomegalovirus (CMV), especially in immunocompromised patients such as organ transplant recipients [111]. Additionally, pseudomembranous colitis may occur in patients receiving immunosuppressants or antitumor drugs, even in the absence of antibiotic use [112].

For critically ill patients, the treatment of pseudomembranous colitis may require a combination of multiple strategies. Traditional treatment methods include the use of metronidazole and vancomycin. However, metronidazole is no longer considered as first line treatment for severe, severe-complicated, fulminant, or life-threatening CDI [113], [114], [115]. Oral fidaxomicin is recommended as the primary drug, but oral vancomycin is considered equivalent. If an oral administration is not feasible, the naso-duodenal or rectal route is viable; the adjunctive intravenous application of metronidazole or tigecycline may be considered. In refractory cases tigecycline has been shown to be effective [116]. The role of bezlotoxumab in cases of severe CDI remains unclear.

Moreover, pseudomembranous colitis in critically ill patients may be associated with other GI complications such as toxic megacolon [117]. In certain cases, pseudomembranous colitis may manifest as localized lesions, mimicking other acute abdominal symptoms such as acute appendicitis, which could result in misdiagnosis and delayed treatment [118].

In severe complicated or refractory CDI, surgical consultation should be sought at an early stage.

GI dysmotility

In the ICU, common GI motility disorders include esophageal motility disorders, gastroparesis, intestinal obstruction, and Ogilvie syndrome [1]. They can lead to malnutrition, gastric bacterial colonization, pulmonary aspiration, and ileus [119].

Esophageal motility disorders are commonly observed in heavy drinkers, diabetics, and critically ill patients receiving opioids or benzodiazepines, which suppress esophageal motor activity. In ICU patients, both the amplitude and frequency of esophageal peristaltic contractions are reduced [120], 121].

Gastroparesis and constipation are among the most common GI motility disorders. Gastroparesis typically manifests as delayed gastric emptying, a widespread issue in ICUs, that affects nutrient intake and medication efficacy [122]. It may result in gastric (over)distention which increases the risk of vomiting, aspiration of large amounts of gastric contents with consecutive aspiration pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Constipation may coexist with gastroparesis, particularly in patients with severe gastroparesis, where the incidence of slow transit constipation significantly increases [123].

In terms of treatment, medications that promote GI motility (such as prokinetics) have shown some effectiveness in improving gastric emptying and increasing caloric intake, but their impact on nutrient absorption and clinical outcomes remains unclear [124]. Additionally, non-pharmacological strategies like early enteral nutrition, correcting electrolyte imbalances, and early patient mobilization are also considered key measures [125].

Small bowel obstruction is a common surgical emergency characterized by a diverse range of causes, including postoperative adhesions, tumors, intestinal volvulus, mesenteric thrombosis, and inflammatory diseases. In patients with small bowel obstruction, the neuro-sensitivity and inflammatory response of the intestine may undergo alterations. A study conducted indicated that 24 h after the onset of small bowel obstruction, the sensitivity of afferent nerves increases, concomitantly with a delayed onset of intestinal inflammation [126].

Laparoscopic surgery is gradually gaining recognition as a possible treatment modality of small bowel obstruction. While laparoscopic surgery has not yet achieved complete standardization in managing small bowel obstructions, it demonstrated the potential to enhance postoperative recovery, reduce hospital stay, and minimize wound infection rates in specific conditions, such as mild abdominal distension, proximal obstruction, and partial obstruction compared to open surgery [127]. Additionally, laparoscopic surgery has shown good results in managing small bowel obstructions accompanied by intestinal ischemia, facilitating the recovery of bowel function and shortening hospital stays [128].

Multi-slice spiral CT (MSCT) plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of small bowel obstruction, especially in evaluating intestinal ischemia. CT images can facilitate the identification of the location and cause of the obstruction, as well as exclude potential complications such as intestinal ischemia [129]. In certain instances, small bowel obstruction may be caused by Meckel’s diverticulum, and MSCT can assist in identifying these complex cases [130].

For postoperative small bowel obstruction arising from internal hernia, the single-incision surgery (SPS) strategy is regarded as an effective initial surgical approach. SPS can provide accurate diagnosis and further surgical intervention if necessary [131]. This method shows potential in reducing postoperative adhesions and accelerating recovery.

The management of small bowel obstruction requires a multi-disciplinary approach involving close collaboration between intensivists, radiologists, and surgeons to determine the most appropriate diagnostic and treatment options.

Over 60 % of intensive care patients experience delayed defecation [132], [133], [134]. Patients with constipation exhibit a prolonged duration of ventilation weaning, an extended length of ICU stays, an increased incidence of infections, and a higher mortality rate [135].

The Ogilvie syndrome, also known as acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, is a functional bowel disorder characterized by the acute dilation of the colon without mechanical obstruction. This syndrome was first described by Ogilvie in 1948 and typically manifests in hospitalized patients, especially those with multiple severe internal or surgical conditions [136]. Although considered as a rare event, the exact incidence remains unknown [137]. Given that delayed defecation and distension of the colon are common problems in critically ill patients it may be hypothesized that Ogilvie syndrome represents the most severe presentation of a continuum of mobility disorders affecting the colon. The precise cause of acute colonic pseudo-obstruction has not been fully elucidated, but it is generally assumed to be due to a functional obstruction resulting from an imbalance between the excitability of the parasympathetic nerves and the inhibitory effects of the sympathetic nerves. In certain circumstances, Ogilvie syndrome may be associated with other serious medical conditions. For instance, after heart surgery, Ogilvie syndrome is considered a rare but potentially fatal complication [138]. Additionally, the syndrome can also occur after spinal fusion surgery, although its incidence is low- It is associated with postoperative complications and prolonged hospital stays [139].

In certain cases, Ogilvie syndrome may be linked to drug use. For example, the chemotherapy drug cisplatin has been reported to be associated with autonomic neuropathy, which can lead to the development of Ogilvie syndrome [140]. Additionally, Ogilvie syndrome may also occur after cesarean section, although this is relatively rare. Nevertheless, it essential for clinicians to be aware of this possibility to facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment [141].

The clinical manifestations of Ogilvie syndrome encompass abdominal distension, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and symptoms such as inability to pass gas or stool. The diagnosis of this syndrome requires the exclusion of mechanical obstruction, which is typically accomplished through CT-imaging [142]. In some cases, Ogilvie syndrome can result in severe complications, such as colonic ischemia or perforation, with an incidence rate as high as 50 % [143].

The treatment of Ogilvie syndrome typically starts with conservative therapy, including measures such as GI decompression and electrolyte correction. In the majority of cases, these measures can effectively alleviate colonic dilation. Should conservative treatment prove ineffective, medications such as neostigmine may be used, with approximately 75 % of patients experiencing symptom relief following its administration. The prevalence of severe constipation and Ogilvie syndrome appears to exhibit variations across different cultural backgrounds and neostigmine is only occasionally required in China. For patients who remain unresponsive to treatment, endoscopic decompression or more invasive surgical interventions, such as ileostomy or colectomy, may become necessary [144].

IAH and ACS

IAH and abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS) are prevalent pathophysiological conditions in critically ill patients. IAH is observed in up to 50 % of intensive care patients, and the incidence of ACS is reported to be as high as 6.3 % [44]. According to the consensus of the World Society for the Study of Abdominal Aneurysms and Aneurysms, IAH is defined as an iIAP persistently ≥12 mmHg, and is subdivided into four grades. ACS is defined as an IAP persistently≥20 mmHg accompanied by new organ dysfunction [145], 146]. The mortality correlates with the severity of the condition [44] (Table 3).

The occurrence of IAH and ACS is associated with multiple factors, including massive fluid resuscitation, ventilation with elevated PEEP of 6 mmHg or higher, post-abdominal surgery, and an APACHE II Score of 18 or higher [38], 44], 147]. IAH and ACS appear to have a higher incidence in specific high-risk populations, such as patients with acute pancreatitis, hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery as well as liver transplantation [148], 149].

Increased abdominal pressure can negatively affect almost all major organs and organ systems potentially causing severe (multiple) organ dysfunction. The pathophysiology of IAH and ACS are described in several reviews [150], 151].

At the bedside a deterioration of pulmonary function coinciding with rising ventilation pressures, hypotension, lactic acidosis, oligo/anuria, and a stiff abdominal wall are the prominent but late sings of ACS. Only by measuring intraabdominal pressure in patients at risk (before clinical signs manifest) allow a timely diagnosis and treatment.

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia

Mesenteric ischemia is a rare but severe complication among critical care patients. Acute arterial embolism, arterial thromboses and venous thromboses account for approximately 50 %, 25 % and 10 % of causes, respectively [155], 156]. NOMI is a distinct form of acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI), characterized by intestinal ischemia caused by vascular spasm or constriction in the gut, rather than organic obstruction of the mesenteric arteries. NOMI accounts for approximately 25 % of mesenteric ischemic events. The diagnosis and treatment of NOMI are challenging, especially in critically ill patients in ICUs. Given its high mortality rate of up to 75 % [157], 158] and low rate of the return to normal social activities among survivors, early diagnosis and timely treatment are paramount [159], 160].

The pathogenesis of NOMI primarily involves vasoconstriction and reduced blood flow in the mesenteric arteries [161]. Studies have shown that the occurrence of NOMI is closely related to postoperative cardiac surgery, dialysis, hypotension, or any other low cardiac output states [162], 163]. An increased oxygen demand during the initiation of enteral nutrition may also contribute to the development of NOMI, particularly when there is already a marginal intestinal blood flow.

There are no specific signs or symptoms that suggest the presence of NOMI. However, abdominal pain accompanied by abdominal distension shortly after the commencement of enteral nutrition may provide a clue [161]. Within the 24 h preceding diagnosis, rising lactate levels and norepinephrine doses as well as a fall of pO2/FiO2-ratio have been observed. Organ dysfunction and SOFA-Score are increasing. Notably, C-reactive protein and procalcitonin levels remain stable throughout the course of the disease [158]. NOMI may be suspected in the absence of a discernible alternative causes for deteriorating organ dysfunctions or shock, when risk factors are prevalent, or when unclear abdominal signs emerge. Therefore, early imaging techniques are of paramount importance. The diagnosis of NOMI relies on enhanced CT scans and selective angiography [164], 165].

The most common therapeutic approaches to NOMI are surgical interventions alone (52 %), a combination of surgery with an endovascular intervention (2 %), or a conservative or end-of-life management in 46 % [156]. Surgical interventions include the resection of necrotic bowel and surgical revascularization. Furthermore, intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging technology can facilitate the assessment of blood flow perfusion in the intestine, thereby guiding the extent of surgical resection and minimizing unnecessary bowel resection [166], 167].

In recent years, the intraarterial administration of vasodilators, such as prostaglandin E2, papaverine or alprostadil, into the mesenteric artery has garnered significant attention and has been proposed as the first line option in the treatment of NOMI. A prospective observational study demonstrated that the intraarterial administration of a vasodilator resulted in a significant reduction of lactate levels with a survival rate of 59 % among patients whose lactate levels decreased by more than 2 mmol/L within the first 24 h. Several subsequent studies have indicated improved survival rates in patients that received selective mesenteric artery injection of vasodilators [158], 161], 168].

Although the prognosis of NOMI remains unfavorable, the identification of risk factors and timely intervention are pivotal for improving the prognosis of NOMI patients [169], 170]. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are still lacking, the early use of locally administered vasodilators into mesenteric artery appears to be a promising novel treatment approach for NOMI.

Prevention and specific management concepts of GI dysfunction

Pharmacological measures and nutritional support

The prevention and treatment of GI dysfunction encompasses a range of pharmacological and nonpharmacological measures, including correction of electrolyte imbalance, avoidance of opioid agonists and anticholinergics, early rehabilitation activities, and early initiation of enteral nutrition. Prokinetic agents, such as erythromycin, have been shown to enhance gastric emptying and improve feeding tolerance in patients with gastroparesis [171]. However, the efficacy of these agents on nutrient absorption and overall clinical outcomes remains an area of ongoing research [124]. Nutritional support is another critical aspect of managing GI dysfunction. Early enteral feeding is recommended to maintain the integrity of the GI mucosal and prevent complications associated with delayed feeding [172]. In the event of AGI, a strategy of trophic feeding, which involves providing minimal enteral nutrition to maintain gut function, may be beneficial as an organ-protective strategy [173]. Additionally, selective duodenal decontamination (SDD) has shown some promise in alleviating organ dysfunction in burn patients, suggesting its possible benefits in specific populations [174].

Advancements in deep phenotypic analysis and pharmacogenomics, hold a promise of providing more precise guidance for drug selection and the formulation of treatment plans in the future [175], 176].

Probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics

In recent years, the concepts of probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics have gained significant attention due to their contributions to GI health and overall well-being. These substances have been extensively studied for their potential to enhance digestion, bolster immune function, and even influence mental health [177], 178]. Probiotics are live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits to the host, particularly by supporting the gut’s natural balance of bacteria. Common examples of probiotics include Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, which are commonly found in fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, and kimchi [179]. Prebiotics, on the other hand, are non-digestible food components that stimulate the growth or activity of beneficial gut bacteria. They serve as a food source for probiotics, augmenting their growth and efficacy. Prebiotics are commonly found in fiber-rich foods like garlic, onions, leeks, asparagus, and bananas. One example is inulin, a type of prebiotic fiber, is found in foods such as chicory root and facilitates the growth of Bifidobacteria in the gut, thereby improving digestion and immune function [180], 181]. Postbiotics, a relatively novel concept, refer to the bioactive compounds produced by probiotics during fermentation, which can exert beneficial effects on health even after the probiotics themselves are no longer present. These metabolites include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), microbial cell components, exopolysaccharides, cell lysates, peptidoglycan, active lipids, and organic acids, which have been shown to possess anti-inflammatory properties and support gut barrier integrity. Lactobacillus, for example, strains produce lactic acid and other metabolites that contribute to a healthier gut environment by lowering the pH of the colon and inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria [182], 183]. These biological agents may play an important role in improving immune function and reducing infectious complications by modulating the gut microbiota [184]. Critically ill patients experience a significant change in the composition of the gut microbiota composition, particularly a reduction in the relative abundance of strict anaerobes such as Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. This phenomenon is associated with a poorer prognosis [25], 185].

Probiotics can offer health benefits to the host through various mechanisms. These mechanisms include enhancing GI barrier function, modulating gut microbiota by inducing antimicrobial peptides in host cells, releasing probiotic antimicrobial factors, competing for epithelial adhesion, and immune regulation [186]. In ICU patients, probiotics have demonstrated efficacy in managing AAD, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and necrotizing enterocolitis [187], 188]. Furthermore, probiotics and prebiotics (the combination of probiotics and prebiotics) therapy has been reported to mitigate systemic inflammation by maintaining the innate gut microbiota and reducing postoperative infections and ventilator-associated pneumonia [23]. In certain instances, prophylactic use of probiotics and prebiotics may constitute a therapeutic option for preventing infectious complication and modulating immunity [189]. Larger-scale randomized, multicenter trials are needed to clarify their clinical significance and specific conditions in critical care when they may be particularly beneficial [190].

Traditional Chinese medicine

In recent years, the application of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) in the management of GI dysfunction of critically ill patients has garnered increasing attention. A meta-analysis has demonstrated that TCM exhibits remarkable efficacy in treating GI dysfunction after acute stroke. It effectively accelerates the return of bowel sounds to normal, and elevated levels of gastrin and cholecystokinin [191]. In the context of postoperative rehabilitation, studies have demonstrated that the combination of rapid recovery technology with traditional Chinese and Western medicine can effectively promote the recovery of GI function. Notably, a study revealed a significant difference in the time to first postoperative defecation between the treatment and control groups (87.16 ± 32.09 h vs. 109.79 ± 40.25 h, respectively; p<0.05). The combination therapy reduced the postoperative stress response and inflammatory response, facilitating patients’ prompt return to normal life [192], 193]. In patients with sepsis, a meta-analysis of 22 RCTs indicated that intestinal Chinese medicine preparations substantially improved the GI function score (MD: −0.50, 95 % CI: −0.61 to −0.38), APACHE II score (MD: −3.30, 95 % CI: −3.73 to −2.86) and reduced the mortality rate (MD: 0.34, 95 % CI: 0.25–0.47) [194]. In a further study in patients with AGI associated with sepsis, the combined treatment with TCM reduced 28-day mortality, shortened mechanical ventilation time and ICU hospital stay, and significantly improved GI function [195].

Finally, TCM has demonstrated the potential in treating functional GI disorders and inflammatory bowel disease by regulating intestinal immunity and inflammatory response through the brain-gut axis mechanism [196].

Acupuncture, as another form of TCM treatment, has been shown to be more effective than standard prokinetic drugs in improving delayed gastric emptying in critically ill patients. It has been has demonstrated that acupuncture can significantly reduce residual gastric volume and improve feeding tolerance, thereby reducing the incidence of malnutrition [197]. Additionally, auricular point pressing combined with Western medicine treatment has also been shown to effectively alleviate postoperative GI dysfunction [198]. A study demonstrated that early application of TCM bundle therapy, including syndrome differentiation-based herbal decoction gavage, acupuncture, mirabilite application, and defecation mixture, significantly reduced the incidence of AGI and improved clinical outcomes in elderly patients with severe sepsis. The intervention group exhibited decreased AGI rates, shorter mechanical ventilation duration, reduced ICU length of stay, and lower overall 28-day as well as AGI-attributed mortality compared to controls. [199].

In summary, TCM and acupuncture demonstrate promising effects in managing GI dysfunction in critically ill patients. While existing studies support their efficacy, more high-quality randomized controlled trials are necessary to further validate the safety and efficacy of these therapies [195]. By integrating the strengths of traditional and modern medicine, a more comprehensive treatment approach may be provided for critically ill patients.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

FMT has garnered substantial attention in recent years due to its potential application in specific critically ill patients with major gut microbiota imbalances and severe intestinal infections, organ failure, and mortality [200]. Consequently, FMT is proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to restore gut microbiota balance, thereby improving clinical outcomes in such critically ill patients. Some studies suggest that FMT may improve diarrhea symptoms and enhance survival rates [109], 201]. A case study reported that a critically ill patient successfully cleared a multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae infection after receiving FMT [202]. However, despite the potential benefits of FMT in specific situations, its use in critically ill patients requires caution. Given that these patients may have compromised immune function, FMT could lead to bacterial translocation and other unforeseen complications [203]. Therefore, larger case series and randomized controlled trials are necessary to verify the safety and efficacy of FMT in critically ill patients [204]. In conclusion, the application of FMT in critically ill patients shows promising prospects, but more research is required to support its optimal application strategies in different disease entities [205]. Through in-depth study of the mechanism of FMT and optimization of its operation process, FMT is hoped to become an important tool in the management of critically ill patients.

Future direction of research: the “gut-organ axes”

Although the role of bacterial and endotoxin translocation during critical illness, trauma and shock as a promoter of multiple organ failure has been extensively studied in the past, the role of the intestinal flora and microbiota has recently achieved new attention. Inflammatory mediators and metabolites released by the intestine appear to form a bidirectional or multidirectional communication mediated by neural, endocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways between the gut with its microbiota and distant organs or organ systems. This communication forms a “gut-organ axis” that interconnects the gut with the brain, the lung, the liver, the heart, the kidneys, and the immune systems (Figure 5). The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in these axes. Most of the connections in the gut-organ axes have been demonstrated in patients with chronic or non-critical diseases, but it is hypothesized that they occur in a similar manner in critically ill patients since they often exhibit intestinal flora imbalance and barrier dysfunction. These interactions may constitute a focus of future research, and the knowledge of these axes may facilitate better understanding of the development and persistence of multiple organ dysfunction and the role of GI complications. Furthermore, it may enable the development of improved or novel therapeutic options.

Gut-brain axis

A key component of the gut-brain axis is the vagus nerve, which functions as a central conduit for transmitting signals from the gut to the brain. Serotonin, a neurotransmitter partly synthesized in the gut, plays a pivotal role in this communication process, influencing emotional regulation and stress responses. The gut microbiota generates various metabolites, including SCFAs, which can enhance serotonin synthesis and vagal activity, further shaping gut-brain communication pathway [206].

The dysbiosis or imbalance in the gut microbiota, has been implicated in several chronic neurological and psychiatric disorders. such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson diseases [207], 208]. The kynurenine pathway, which is involved in tryptophan metabolism may play a role by producing neurotoxic metabolites that contribute to neuronal damage [209]. The gut-brain axis also appears to play a significant role in cognitive health, contributing to cognitive development, stability, and impairment [210].

Moreover, it is believed to be essential in the pathogenesis of functional GI disorders (FGIDs), such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). These disorders appear to result from the interaction of altered gut physiology with psychological factors, where brain and gut symptoms reciprocally influence each other. Patients with FGIDs often exhibit altered gut microbiota composition compared to healthy controls [211].

Furthermore, the gut-brain axis is involved in the regulation of food intake and energy homeostasis. The GI tract contains various appetite-regulating signals, including gut-brain peptides and SCFAs, which the central nervous system integrates to promote or inhibit feeding behavior. [212].

In recent years, the inter-connection between the gut and the brain has been recognized to be linked with several conditions of acute critical illness. Wang et al. conducted a comprehensive review of the axis’s role in sepsis-associated encephalopathy elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this condition [213]. Other authors have explored the gut-brain axis’s involvement in severe forms of epilepsy [214].

A deeper understanding of the gut-brain axis appears to be a promising area for future research with the potential to enhance our understanding of the pathophysiology of neurologic disorders in critically ill patients. This includes the various forms of delirium and other cognitive disorders observed in the ICU, the regulation of appetite and feeding, and the interaction of the intricate interplay between the gut microbiota and gut mobility.

Gut-lung axis

Gut microbiota-derived metabolites, particularly SCFAs, have been demonstrated to modulate immune responses and maintain immune homeostasis, thereby influencing the pathogenesis of lung diseases [215], 216].

Recent studies have underscored that the gut-lung axis’s involvement in various respiratory conditions, extending beyond chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and to include the ARDS. [217], 218]. In ARDS, gut-derived immune cells and metabolites, such as alveolar macrophages and IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, can trigger lung injury through systemic circulation, [219], 220].

The inter-connection of the gut with the lungs also plays a significant role in respiratory viral infections. The gut microbiota influences antiviral immune responses in the lungs through such mechanisms as cytokine regulation and immune cell migration. Dysbiosis in the gut can exacerbate the severity of respiratory infections and diminish the efficacy of antiviral therapies [221], 222]. The gut dysbiosis may contribute to the severity of respiratory symptoms and the overall disease outcome as has been implicated in immune response to COVID-19 [223], 224].

Moderating the gut-lung axis is being investigated for its potential therapeutic applications. Modulating the gut microbiota through dietary interventions, probiotics, and prebiotics is being investigated as a strategy to prevent or alleviate lung diseases. In this respect, probiotics have demonstrated an improvement of the gut microbiota composition and reduced inflammation, in the lung [225]. Additionally, understanding the gut-lung axis can provide insights into novel treatment strategies for lung diseases, such as targeting specific microbial metabolites or pathways involved in gut-lung communication [226], 227].

Gut-liver axis

The liver, is closely interconnected with the gut through the portal vein, which transports nutrients, microbial products, and other metabolites directly from the gut to the liver. This connection enables a continuous exchange of signals and substances, influencing both gut and liver functions [228].

Like in the pathophysiological pathways in the other gut-organ interactions,the gut microbiota play a key role [229], 230]. Alterations in the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) can lead to increased intestinal permeability, allowing harmful substances such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) to enter the bloodstream and reach the liver. This can trigger inflammatory responses and contribute to the pathogenesis of liver diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), alcoholic liver disease (ALD), liver cirrhosis, and sepsis [231], [232], [233], [234].

The liver also influences the gut microbiota through the secretion of bile acids and immunoglobulins, which can shape the composition and function of the gut microbial community. Bile acids have been demonstrated to regulate gut microbiota composition and intestinal barrier function. Disruptions in bile acid metabolism are associated with liver diseases such as cholestatic liver disease and primary sclerosing cholangitis, underscoring the importance of the gut-liver axis in these conditions [235], 236].

Therapeutic strategies targeting the gut-liver axis are being explored to treat liver diseases. These include the utilization of probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics to restore a healthy gut microbiota, as well as FMT to directly alter the gut microbial composition. Additionally, interventions aimed at reducing intestinal permeability and modulating bile acid metabolism are being investigated for their potential to enhance liver health [237], [238], [239]. It may be speculated that the interactions between the gut and the liver may also play an important role during critical illness and deserves more attention in future research.

Gut-heart axis

The gut-heart axis, an emerging concept, that focusses on the intricate relationship between gut microbiota and cardiovascular health. It underscores the bidirectional communication between the gut and the heart, mediated by various microbial metabolites and immune responses. [240], 241].

One of the key mechanisms through which the gut-heart axis operates is the production of microbial metabolites such as trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) and SCFAs TMAO, derived from dietary choline and carnitine, has been associated with increased cardiovascular risk by promoting atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction [242], 243]. Conversely, SCFAs, produced by the fermentation of dietary fibers, exhibit protective effects against cardiac damage by modulating inflammation and maintaining gut barrier integrity [244], 245].

Dietary patterns play a role in shaping the gut microbiota composition and, consequently, the gut-heart axis. Diets rich in fibers, such as the Mediterranean diet, have been linked to a favorable gut microbiota profile and reduced CVD risk [246]. Additionally, interventions like probiotics and prebiotics have shown promise in modulating gut dysbiosis and improving cardiovascular outcomes by influencing blood pressure, lipid profiles, and glycemic control.

The therapeutic potential of targeting the gut-heart axis is further supported by innovative approaches such as FMT, which aims to restore a balanced gut microbiota and improve cardiometabolic parameters in individuals with CVD [244], 247], 248]. However, despite these promising findings, further research is needed to fully understand the complex interactions within the gut-heart axis and to develop effective, personalized therapies for cardiovascular health, including their potential relevance to conditions with critical illnesses.

Gut-kidney axis

The gut-kidney axis has garnered recent attention due to its implications in various renal diseases, particularly chronic kidney disease (CKD).

Some studies have underscored the importance of the gut-kidney axis in the pathogenesis and progression of CKD. Dysbiosis can lead to the production of uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, which are implicated in renal dysfunction and systemic inflammation [249], 250]. These toxins, originating from microbial metabolism, contribute to the uremic load and exacerbate kidney damage, highlighting the bidirectional nature of the gut-kidney interaction [251].

The gut-kidney axis is also involved in the modulation of immune responses and inflammation, which are critical factors in CKD progression. Alterations in gut microbiota can lead to increased intestinal permeability, enabling the translocation of bacterial products into the systemic circulation, thereby triggering inflammatory pathways [252], 253]. This inflammatory response is further compounded by the disruption of the epithelial barrier, which increases exposure to endotoxins and exacerbates renal injury.

Dietary interventions, probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT can restore gut microbiota balance, reduce the production of harmful metabolites, and ultimately slow the progression of CKD [254]. Therefore, these interventions may alleviate secondary renal dysfunction in critically ill patients through the gut-kidney axis.

Moreover, the gut-kidney axis is not isolated but interacts with other organ systems, forming complex networks such as the gut-kidney-heart axis. This interconnectedness underscores the systemic nature of CKD and its complications, including cardiovascular disease [253]. Presently, it can only be speculated whether these pathomechanisms play a role in the development of acute renal failure (ARF) during conditions of acute and severe critically illness, or in the phase of recovery from acute renal failure.

Limitation

This article reviews the gastrointestinal complications of critically ill patients in the ICU, which has the following limitations. First of all, gastrointestinal function is only one important part of the treatment process of critically ill patients. Gastrointestinal complications in critically ill patients appear in various forms, and gastrointestinal complications are easy to miss in clinical treatment until obvious clinical symptoms appear. However, the current research evidence still lacks methods and means of early identification and early intervention.

Due to space limitations, the discussion of some complications in this article is not comprehensive and we had to refer to some specific guidelines and in-depth review articles. Secondly, in the literature review of gastrointestinal complications in critically ill patients, the research on some complications is still in an early stage of clinical understanding and there still remain many open questions. Therefore, sometimes no clear conclusions and recommendations for the clinical management can be given but rather be hinted at the gaps in knowledge and understanding.

Future direction

Maintaining normal gastrointestinal function is essential for the recovery and improved prognosis of critically ill patients. At present, there are still many knowledge gaps and limitations in the research and clinical prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal complications in critically ill patients. Even the research and understanding of some complications are still incomplete in many respects. How to identify gastrointestinal dysfunction and related complications at an early stage, while administering effective treatment and intervention for different complications, remains the focus of future research. In addition, in terms of treatment, TCM (Chinese medicine, acupuncture, etc.) still deserves further research in improving gastrointestinal function and preventing complications in critically ill patients, including in combination with other treatments. Finally, the human body as a whole is interconnected and interacts with each organ, which may be more pronounced in pathological states. In recent years, the research on the gut-organ axis has become a hot topic and gradually deepened, and many studies have explored the role and potential treatment direction of the intestinal organ axis, and the interaction between the gut and other organs still deserves further attention in the treatment of critically ill patients.

Conclusions

The gut is one of the body’s major organ systems and GI dysfunction and complications play a crucial role both with respect to frequency of occurrence and to severity of their presentation and their effects on morbidity and mortality. They pose significant challenges to the diagnosis, the treatment, and prognosis of critically ill patients in intensive care units.

Gastrointestinal dysfunction and complications comprise a large variety of entities with different pathophysiologies, symptom presentations and treatment options.

At present, the diagnosis of gastrointestinal dysfunction and complications is still based on clinical symptoms and signs, is notoriously difficult, and a diagnosis can often be made at a relatively late stage of the condition. In addition to a meticulous clinical examination, imaging techniques are essential, and bedside ultrasound may offer a beneficial option for feeding and motility disorders. Routine measurement of intraabdominal pressure in all patients at risk will allow the early detection of IAH and ACS. It appears essential to routinely use validated scores of GI function that will enable a more objective, reliable, and close monitoring of the GI tract and to detect more subtle changes and deteriorations at an earlier stage. Severe gastrointestinal complications (such as gastrointestinal bleeding, feeding intolerance, diarrhea and pseudomembranous colitis, gastrointestinal motility disorders, and IAH) are common in ICU patients. If detected late they may progress to gastrointestinal failure, MODS, and mortality. The knowledge of risk factors and the adherence to guidelines are essential in preventing and treating these entities. It is worth noting that gastrointestinal complications such as NOMI and Ogilvie syndrome may be more common than anticipated. in critically ill patients. The microbiota of the gut plays a central role for regulating gastrointestinal functions. A treatment with probiotics, prebiotics and postbiotics appears to prevent and manage feeding problems, motility disorders and infectious complications as well as damage to distant organs. FMT is a relatively new and increasing attractive option. However, sound scientific data are still lacking, adverse effects are not yet fully understood and clear indications not generally consented. Therefore, it should be used carefully and preferably only in experienced centers within trials. Traditional Chinese Medicine (combined with Western medicine) appears to have a great potential and may help reducing complications and improving the prognosis of patients.

The implementation of the concept of gut-organ axes is an advancement in the understanding of the pathophysiological processes that influence GI function and the function of the distant organs. The bidirectional communication between the organs may influence major immune reactions and neuroendocrine communications. However, there is still no clear answer to how the gut-organ axis affects organ function in critically ill patients. Therefore, further research on the gut-organ axis in critically ill patients may provide new insights in the pathophysiology of GI disorders and MODS and offer innovative treatment options in the future.
