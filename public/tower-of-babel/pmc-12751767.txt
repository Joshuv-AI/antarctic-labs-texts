Determinants of dietary adherence among hypertensive patients attending outpatient treatment at public hospitals of Gedeo Zone, South Ethiopia, 2025. Unmatched case‒control mixed study
Authors: Alemayehu Tadesse Gedo, Mahlet Birhane Estifanos, Adane Tesfaye, Michael Tadesse Gadacho, Kasa Tefera Tulu, Abu Huseen Ahmed, Wagaye Alemu Zenebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsuming a healthy diet is among the essential ways to control hypertension. However, dietary adherence among patients with hypertension is low in Ethiopia, particularly in the current study area. Therefore, this study aimed to assess determinants of dietary adherence among patients with hypertension receiving outpatient treatment at public hospitals in the Gedeo Zone, southern Ethiopia, 2025.MethodsAn institution-based unmatched case‒control study supported by a phenomenological study design (convergent parallel study) was conducted among 369 (123 cases and 246 controls) patients with hypertension. A seven-item questionnaire with seven dietary components was used to assess dietary adherence among patients with hypertension. For the quantitative study, a stratified random sampling technique was used to allocate the study area, and then a simple random sampling technique was used to select the study participants. Statistical package for Social Science version 29 software was used to perform bivariable and multivariable logistic regression to test the associations between the dependent and independent variables. For the qualitative study, purposive selection was performed. After data transcription, ATLAS.ti 25 was used to code and analyse the data.ResultA total of 369 (123 cases and 246 controls) study participants were enrolled. The dietary adherence was found to be better in older patients (> 59) (AOR = 3.62; 95% CI (1.14, 11.50), those who had no comorbidities (AOR = 3.28; 95% CI (1.04, 10.36), Patients with more than 2 years duration of hypertension (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI (1.47, 7.36), and those who had good knowledge about hypertension and healthy lifestyle (AOR = 1.214; 95% CI (1.03, 4.75)). However, it was lower in those who had not received nutritional education (AOR = 0.32; 95% CI (0.13, 0.78)).ConclusionAge, not receiving nutritional education, Absence of comorbidity, Duration of hypertension, and Knowledge about hypertension and healthy lifestyles were found to be determinants of dietary adherence; hence, interventions focusing on the above determinants promoting adherence and patients’ health support should be implemented.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01214-y.Keywords: Dietary, Adherence, Hypertensive patients, Outpatient treatment, Gedeo zone

Introduction

The term hypertension refers to a blood pressure reading of 140/90 mm Hg or greater [1]. In both developed and developing nations, hypertension is currently a major public health issue that contributes to a marked rise in morbidity and mortality [2]. For individuals with hypertension, the dietary approach is the most effective nonpharmacological therapy management option and is a primary intervention [3]. Adherence to a recommended diet is the agreement between a person’s medication-related behaviors and the dietary and lifestyle modifications that medical professionals advise [4]. In a study conducted in different parts of the world, dietary adherence among patients with hypertension was 22.5% in Pakistan [5], 36.3% in America [6], 30% in Finland [7], 50% in Israel [8], 58% in Jordan [9], 11.8% in Saudi Arabia [10], 65% in Turkey [11], and 35.5% in Korea [12]. From studies carried out at public hospitals in African nations, dietary adherence among hypertensive patients was found to be 20% in Benin [13] and 22.2% in Tanzania [14].

A previous study of hypertensive patients who received outpatient treatment in various public hospitals in Ethiopia reported that dietary adherence was 32.8% in the Bahir Dar City Administration [15], 57.8% in Bishoftu General Hospital, Oromia Region [16], 61.7% at Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College [17], 35% in selected hospitals in the Central Gondar Zone [18] and 76.4% in Dessie Referral Hospital, Northeast Ethiopia [19].

According to World Health Organization (WHO) reports, sedentary lifestyles and unhealthy diets account for 90% of cases of hypertension, with only 10% of cases resulting from other causes [20]. High sodium intake accounts for one-third of hypertension cases, followed by inadequate potassium intake (20%), obesity (30%), low fitness (20%), and excessive alcohol consumption [21]. Dietary adherence to recommended diets among people with hypertension is affected by sociodemographic factors (age, sex, marital status, educational level, occupational status), clinical factors (duration of hypertension, presence of comorbidity), awareness factors (receiving nutritional education, knowledge of hypertension, knowledge of healthy lifestyles), psychosocial factors (social support, number of counselling sessions by doctors and family members), and behavioral factors (undertaking regular physical exercise, smoking, alcohol consumption) [2, 4, 17, 18, 22, 23]. The WHO Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs outlines nine global noncommunicable disease (NCD) prevention targets, including a 10% reduction in the prevalence of inadequate physical activity and a 25% reduction in the prevalence of high blood pressure by achieving a 30% reduction in the average population’s salt intake [24]. To reduce the prevalence of behavioural risk factors such as excessive alcohol consumption, smoking, inactivity, and salt intake, the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) developed and carried out initiatives. These initiatives included increasing alcohol excise taxes, promoting healthy eating habits, monitoring cigarette use and preventative measures, and increasing public awareness [25].

Although following a healthy diet can prevent hypertension and lower blood pressure and is a government priority in Ethiopia, dietary adherence has been found to vary among pastoralist, agro-pastoralist, mixed farming, and agrarian communities, and data on the determinants of dietary adherence among individuals living in mixed farming areas are lacking. Moreover, as nonadherence to the recommended diet is affected by multiple factors, determinants influencing dietary adherence among hypertensive individuals in mixed farming communities in Ethiopia, particularly in the Gedeo Zone, have not been explored. Furthermore, prior research has been limited to cross-sectional studies, which makes it impossible to draw conclusions about causality. However, the current study carefully utilized mixed study designs that combine qualitative and quantitative approaches. This helps to draw conclusions about causality and increases the validity and reliability of the findings. Examining the determinants of dietary adherence would help facilitate action for patients. Therefore, this study aimed to assess and explore determinants of dietary adherence among hypertensive patients receiving outpatient treatment at public hospitals in the Gedeo Zone, South Ethiopia, 2025.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

This study was conducted at public hospitals in the Gedeo Zone. The Gedeo is one of the zones that is found in the southern Ethiopian regional state. This zone, which occupies 1,210.89 square kilometers, is home to 847,434 people, 424,742 of whom are men and 422,692 of whom are women. Thirty-nine people live as pastoralists, while 107,781(12.72%) people live in cities. This zone has 172,782 housing units and 179,677 households, with an average of 4.72 people per household. Agriculture is the backbone of the economy in the Gedeo Zone, with coffee being the most significant cash crop grown in the region. Present-day methods of agricultural intensification tend to overlook the socioecological benefits of home garden agroforestry systems in favour of the limited goal of increasing yield [26]. The Gedeo zone has one general hospital and three primary hospitals: Dilla University General Hospital and Bule, Gedeb and Yirgacheffe primary hospitals. Currently, there are a total of 1,737 patients receiving hypertension follow-up at public hospitals, which are 964, 216, 321 and 235 patients at Dilla University General Hospital, Yirgacheffe, Bule and Gedeb Primary Hospitals, respectively. The study was conducted from January 01–March 05, 2025.

Study design

An institution-based unmatched case‒control study design supported by a phenomenological study design (convergent parallel study) was conducted. The researchers applied the phenomenological study design to explore the personal experiences, beliefs, and emotions of hypertensive patients and to uncover their hidden challenges regarding dietary adherence.

Source and study population

For the quantitative study, the source population was all hypertensive patients who were on outpatient follow-up at public hospitals in the Gedeo Zone. With respect to the study population, selected hypertensive patients who were on treatment follow-up and nonadherent to the recommended diet were the cases, whereas selected hypertensive patients who were on treatment follow-up and adherent to the recommended diet were the controls.

For the qualitative study, purposely selected hypertensive patients and physicians composed the population.

Eligibility criteria

All hypertensive patients aged ≥ 18 years and who were receiving medical treatment follow-up or antihypertensive treatment for at least one month before the beginning of the study were included, whereas hypertensive patients who were critically ill, mentally unstable, pregnant women, and whose medical cards had incomplete information were excluded from the study.

Sampling size determination

For the quantitative study, for dietary adherence among cases and controls, the sample size was computed via the double population proportion formula by considering a ratio of nonadherent cases to adherent controls of 1:2, a power of 80, a 95% confidence interval, and an adjusted odds ratio of 7.52, with a proportion among cases of 0.748 and among controls of 0.283 from a study of hypertensive patients in governmental hospitals of the Guji Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia [27]. For determinants of dietary adherence, the sample size was computed via Epi Info version 7.2.5.0 software by considering a ratio of nonadherent cases to adherent controls of 1:2, power 80, 95% confidence interval, and odds ratio varying according to the variable. The sample size formula for the method described in Fleiss w/CC was used. Considering the adequacy of the sample size, the largest sample size was 341. By adding a 10% nonresponse rate, the total sample size for this study was 375. Therefore, the total sample sizes for this study were 125 cases and 250 control hypertensive patients.

For the qualitative study, the sample size was determined on the basis of the saturation of information that comes to the discussion point during the interview. The data for qualitative study was collected by using the same or parallel variables, constructs, or concepts as of quantitative study. To resolve inequality of sample size in a convergent mixed methods design, it was considered that having unequal sample sizes was not a problem. In this study, the researchers argued that the intent of qualitative and quantitative research differ (one to gain an in-depth perspective and the other, to generalize to a population) and that each provides an adequate count. Moreover, the researchers sampled study participants purposely from the study respondents who were included in the quantitative study.

Sampling procedure

For the quantitative study, a stratified random sampling technique was used to place those public hospitals according to their geographic area. Accordingly, there are four public hospitals in the Gedeo Zone: Dilla University General Hospital (north of the zone), Bule Primary Hospital (southeast of the zone), Gedeb Primary Hospital (south of the zone), and Yirgacheffe Primary Hospital (southern center of the zone). The sample size (cases and controls) was subsequently distributed for each hospital via proportional allocation. Once the sample size was distributed for each hospital, an institution-based census was conducted on the dietary adherence status of 1643 hypertensive patients who were followed-up at public hospitals by using a seven-item dietary adherence questionnaire to select cases and controls. Then, a simple random sampling technique was applied to select cases and controls until the study sample size was completed by using the order of the medical records of the patients available at the Outpatient Department (OPD) by the time they were attended at each hospital. Once the patient completed medical treatment, the selected patient was referred to separate areas to be interviewed by data collectors.

For the qualitative study, the study participants were chosen via a purposive sampling technique on the basis of their age, sex, time of diagnosis, and advice of medical staff at the hypertensive patient’s outpatient follow-up clinic. The study participants were selected from the respondents who participated in the quantitative study.

Operational definition

Dietary adherence

Participants in this study were deemed adherent if they regularly and primarily ate a diet high in fruits, vegetables, grains, and low dairy products; occasionally or infrequently or never consumed spices and saturated fat; and infrequently or never added salt when cooking and eating over the preceding seven days [28].

Cases

Dietary nonadherent hypertensive patients who were on treatment follow-up after their dietary adherence status was assessed by seven recommended diets in the last seven days.

Control

Dietary adherent hypertensive patient respondents who were on treatment follow-up after their dietary adherence status was assessed by seven recommended diets in the last seven days.

Good knowledge

Those study participants who answered mean and above out of 15 hypertension and healthy lifestyle knowledge-based questions [29].

Poor knowledge

Those study participants who answered below the mean out of 15 hypertension and healthy lifestyle knowledge-based questions [29].

Good patient–physician relationship

Those study participants who answered mean and above out of 9 questions about the client interaction in the care and treatment of HTN patients [30].

Poor patient–physician relationship

Those study participants who answered below the mean out of 9 questions about client interaction in the care and treatment of HTN patients [30].

Social support

Support gained from family and nonfamily members. The score ranges from 3 to 14, with high values representing strong levels and low values representing poor levels of social support. The Oslo social support scale (OSSS)−3 score can be operationalized into three broad categories of social support. 3–8 poor social support, 9–11 moderate social support and 12–14 strong social support. The participants who were given scores of 3–8, 9–11 or 12–14 were considered to have poor social support, moderate social support or strong social support, respectively [31].

Self-efficacy

the patient’s belief in his or her capacity to carry out tasks [15].

Good self-efficacy

Those study participants who answered below the mean out of 5-item Health-Specific Self-Efficacy Scales (Nutrition Self-Efficacy) to cope with and manage their disease [30].

Poor self-efficacy

Those study participants who answered below the mean out of 5-item Health-Specific Self-Efficacy Scales (Nutrition Self-Efficacy) to cope with and manage their disease [30].

Alcohol consumption

Respondents reported that they either consumed alcohol or that male patients consumed more than two bottles while female patients consumed more than one bottle per day [5].

Number of people close to the patient

Any person who the patient could count on if he/she had a serious problem or in the case of an emergency.

Data collection tool and procedures

For the quantitative study, a structured questionnaire was developed to gather data on sociodemographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, clinical, psychosocial, Body Mass Index (BMI) and adherence to the recommended diet was used. Using checklists, clinically related data, including blood pressure measurements, comorbidity information, and duration of HTN since diagnosis, were collected from the client’s chart. Data were collected by two nurse professionals via the Kobo toolbox at each public hospital in two isolated chronic care units. A total of eight nurse professionals were recruited from the Public Hospitals other than the study area.

A seven-item questionnaire with seven dietary components—fruits, vegetables, grains, low-fat dairy products, saturated fat and oil, sodium salts, and spice foods—was used to gauge adherence to the recommended diet. The number of times the respondents ingested the aforementioned foods during the preceding seven days was used to gauge their intake of dietary fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products (15; 28).

Nutritional status was assessed by using BMI(kg/m2). A stadiometer was used to measure the participant’s height in centimetres (cm) while they were standing upright and barefoot, and a Seca weight scale was used to measure the participant’s body weight while they were wearing light clothing and no shoes, to the nearest 0.1 kilogramme (kg). The body mass index (BMI) was computed by dividing the square of the height in metres by the weight in kilogrammes. The BMI classification is as follows: normal weight (BMI = 18.50–24.99 kg/m2), overweight (BMI = 25.0029.99 kg/m2), underweight (BMI ≤ 18.49 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30.00 kg/m2) [32].

Hypertension knowledge-level scale (HK-LS) questionnaires were used to gauge participants’ extent of hypertension knowledge [33]. The 15 items on this scale are intended to gauge respondents’ level of knowledge. To evaluate hypertension people’s knowledge, the tool includes sections on definition, therapy, medication adherence, food, lifestyle, and complications. A yes-or-no response was required for the selected-response questions in the tool; the correct response is denoted by “1” and the incorrect answer by “0.” (15; 28).

The “Oslo 3-item social support scale (OSSS-3)” was used to measure how supportive their friends, family, and neighbours were of hypertension patients. The score was between 3 and 14 [34]. Participants with OSSS-3 scores between 3 and 8 were categorised as having low social support, those with scores between 9 and 11 as having moderate social support, and those with scores between 12 and 14 as having excellent social support. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the tool’s validity and reliability was 0.687. The scale guarantees construct validity and internal consistency in reliability [35].

The Health-Specific Self-Efficacy Scale (Nutrition Self-Efficacy) was used to measure self-efficacy [36]. The respondents were asked how frequently they took specific actions or how they overcame obstacles pertaining to their health. Participants were deemed to have excellent self-efficacy if they answered “very certain” or “rather certain,” whereas those who answered “very uncertain” or “rather uncertain” were deemed to have poor self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the tool’s validity and reliability was 0.95 for the whole scale and 0.93, 0.92, and 0.93 for the knowledge, motivation, and nutrition skills subscales, respectively [37].

A simplified 10-item version of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) was used to assess alcohol intake moderation. Three categories of questions were given to the respondents: harmful alcohol use, dependency symptoms, and hazardous alcohol use [38]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for each component of the tool was 0.80, indicating its validity and reliability. The specificity and sensitivity of the questionnaire were 0.94 and 0.98, respectively. Internal validity and reliability have improved with the AUDIT. It has a high sensitivity and specificity and can identify 97% of those with alcohol dependence [39].

Smoking status was evaluated through the WHO stepwise approach in chronic disease survey questionnaires [40].

Physical exercise status was assessed via the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-short form [41]. With respect to the reliability and validity of the tool, typical IPAQ correlations were approximately 0.80 for reliability and 0.30 for validity. These results of the IPAQ reliability and validity study show that the IPAQ has measurement properties that are as good as those of other established self-reported physical activity measures [42].

Patient–physician relationships were assessed via the Patient–Doctor Relationships Questionnaire (PDRQ-9) in primary care [43]. The respondents were asked to decide about their primary care physicians. Those participants who responded “appropriate”, “mostly appropriate” and “completely appropriate” were considered to have good patient–physician relationships, whereas those who responded “somewhat appropriate” and “not at all appropriate” were considered to have poor patient–physician relationships. With respect to the reliability and validity of the tool, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.96 for the entire scale. The scale ensures internal consistency in reliability and construct validity [44].

For the qualitative study, the data were collected via a nonstructured interview guide which was developed by the researchers, which included a set of determinants of dietary adherence for key informants and hypertensive patients. The respondents were deemed pertinent to the study on the basis of the homogeneity criteria. The data were gathered face-to-face via key informants (KIs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs) in the local language (Gedeuffa) and Amharic, and the participants were interviewed for a maximum of 30–90 min. The data were collected simultaneously during quantitative data collection via field notes and audiotaping. The field notes were captured by considering observations and assumptions about what was heard or observed and personal narratives about what was experienced by the researcher during the data collection time. Audio recordings of the voices of the participants were obtained with the interviewers’ consent. To make the interviewees feel as comfortable as possible, rapport building and any other efforts were made. The interviews were conducted by the principal investigator.

Data quality management

For the quantitative study, before data collection, the structured questionnaire prepared for data collection was pretested by data collectors in 5% of the study sample size on hypertensive patients found in public hospital other than study or which was Kebado Primary Hospital which is found in Sidama National Regional state. The questionnaire was modified on the basis of the results of the pretest. The questionnaires were developed in English, translated to Amharic, and then returned to English to maintain consistency by a well-trained language expert. Two training sessions concerning the objective of the study, the study instruments, the consent form, how to interview, and the data collection procedure were given to the data collectors. The collected data were rechecked for completeness by the principal investigator.

For qualitative study (trustworthiness), to maintain the reliability and validity of the qualitative data, the standards established by Guba and Lincoln were used as follows [45].

Credibility (related to internal validity)

How closely data collection, presentation, and interpretation adhere to the tenets of the research methodology chosen to address the research question determines credibility. To guarantee the validity of the research findings, the investigator evaluated the eligibility criteria prior to data collection. To avoid unnecessary interruptions and preserve the rights of the participants, the sorrow preparation of the entire study was prepared before the interview started. Moreover, credibility was also maintained by using triangulation and investing more time through sustained engagement of the principal investigator advisor(s) in the overall activity of the data collection process. Moreover, every step of the research, from its inception to the report writing process, was meticulously outlined and documented, including any decisions made and the reasoning behind them, for advisor(s) to follow. To prevent errors or biases as well as misinterpretation that emerged after personal experiences with the problem under study, methods and time frames for the collection of data and the entire duration of the field study were described through continuous engagement of the advisor(s) and other researchers in the overall activity of data processing and analysis.

Transferability (related to generalizability)

Transferability refers to the provision of sufficient information in comprehensible language to enable others to respond to the research question in a different context. To guarantee transferability, appropriate probes were employed to gather comprehensive data on responses to preserve the transferability of the findings. To provide detailed descriptions, extensive field notes and digital audio recordings were made for each IDI both prior to and during analysis.

Dependability (related to reliability)

The process of ensuring that the researcher’s role is clear, that the proper data collection methods are employed, and that the research questions are specific and suitable for the study design is known as reliability. To guarantee reliability, member checking was performed by giving the participants their initial results back so that they could correct any mistakes and contest any erroneous interpretations, preserving the reliability of the results. To foster the acceptance of participants with differing viewpoints, prolonged engagement was also employed.

Conformability (related to objectivity)

To guarantee that the study is confirmable, the researcher’s advisor or advisors were involved to confirm that the interview questions and research methodologies, among other aspects of the study, are appropriate. Through ongoing involvement of the principal investigator advisor(s) and other researchers in the overall activity of data processing and analysis, methods and time frames for the collection of data and the entire duration of the field study were described to prevent error or bias as well as misinterpretation that arose after personal experiences with the problem under study.

Data processing and analysis

For the quantitative study, after the data were collected via the Kobo toolbox, they were exported into SPSS software version 29 for analysis. Frequency distribution tables were used to describe some variables. A Hosmer‒Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess multicollinearity, and it was found that a variance inflation factor (VIF) was 2, which suggested that the model’s predictions were well calibrated and that the model fit the data well. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression were employed to test the associations between the dependent and independent variables. In the bivariable analysis, a P value of < 0.25 was used to select candidate variables for multivariable analysis. In multivariable analysis, a P value of less than 0.05 was used to determine the statistical significance of the association, and an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval was used to determine the presence, strength, and direction of association between covariates and the outcome variable.

For qualitative study, before data analysis, data collected via field notes and audiotaping were transcribed and translated by the researchers in transcription and translated into English, and their accuracy was reviewed by another experienced researcher who trained in qualitative research by employing intelligent transcription to input the data into word processing software. Field notes were combined with interview transcripts to provide a comprehensive dataset for analysis. By including any attributes, the structured data were placed into text groups. Before data coding, the transcripts from each data collection tool were cross-checked with 30% audio-recorded data to maintain their accuracy and consistency. Deductive coding was applied according to the research questions to identify relationships and connections with a predetermined research framework, and themes as well as subthemes were created accordingly. Processing and analysis were performed via ATLAS.ti version 25 software. Finally, the results were presented via narration (Fig. 1).

Results

Quantitative phase

Sociodemographic characteristics

In this study, 369 (123 cases and 246 controls) study participants were enrolled, for a 98.4% response rate. The majority, 89 (72.4%) and 157 (63.8%), of the respondents were male in the case and control groups, respectively. A total of 45 (36.6%) participants in the case group and 150 (61.0%) in the control group were aged 40–59 years. Among the total participants enrolled in the study, 46 (37.4%) participants were unable to read and write, whereas 110 (44.7%) of the control participants attended secondary school. Among the total study subjects, 87 (70.3%) of the cases and 165 (67.1%) of the controls were from rural areas (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

In this study, 28 (22.8%) of the respondents in the case group and 36 (14.6%) in the control group had a family history of hypertension. With respect to comorbidities among the study respondents, 20 (16.3%) of the cases and 11 (4.5%) of the controls had illnesses other than hypertension. Accordingly, 14 (11.4%), 6 (4.9%), and 7 (5.7%) of the respondents, as well as 14 (5.7%), 11 (4.5%), and 19 (7.7%) of the controls, reported diabetes mellitus, renal disease, and other comorbidities, respectively. The findings from this study also revealed that 75 (61.0%) and 108 (43.9%) of the respondents from the case and control groups, respectively, reported that it had taken more than two years since they were diagnosed with hypertension. With respect to the nutritional status of the respondents, 28 (22.8%), 56 (45.5%) and 39 (31.7%) of the respondents in the case group had normal, overweight, and obese statuses, whereas 116 (47.2%), 74 (30.1%) and 56 (22.8%) of the respondents in the control group had normal, overweight, and obese statuses, respectively (Table 3).

Awareness-related characteristics

In this study, 58 (47.2%) and 132 (53.7%) of the study subjects from the case and control groups, respectively, had good knowledge of hypertension and healthy lifestyles. Among the total sample of study respondents, 56 (45.5%) and 19 (15.4%) of the case had heard about hypertension and healthy lifestyles, whereas 192 (78.0%) and 205 (83.3%) of the control had heard about hypertension and healthy lifestyles, respectively. With respect to the sources of information about hypertension and healthy lifestyles, 58 (47.4%), 51 (41.5%), 41 (33.3%), and 15 (12.2%) of the study subjects were health professionals, media (TV, radio, newspaper), family members, and friends, respectively. A total of 139 (56.4%), 130 (52.8%), 107 (43.7%), and 8 (3.3%) of the study participants in the control group heard from health professionals, media (TV, radio, newspaper), family members, and friends, respectively. This study revealed that 25 (20.3%) of the respondents in the case group and 117 (47.6%) of the respondents in the control group reported that they had received nutritional education (Table 5).

Respondents’ level of adherence to the recommended diet

According to an institution-based census conducted on the dietary adherence status of 1643 hypertensive patients on follow-up by using a seven-item dietary adherence questionnaire, approximately 652 (39.7%) respondents were found to be adherent, whereas 991 (60.3%) were nonadherent to the recommended diet.

Determinants of dietary adherence

In the bivariable logistic regression, age, educational status, nutritional education, family history of hypertension, comorbidities, BP measurement (at any health institution), duration since hypertension diagnosis, physical activity, self-efficacy, knowledge, and patient‒physician relationships were associated with dietary adherence. However, in the multivariable logistic regression, age, nutritional education, comorbidities, duration since hypertension diagnosis, and knowledge were significantly associated with dietary adherence. The odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.62 times greater among hypertensive patients older than 59 years than among those aged 18–39 years (AOR = 3.62; 95% CI (1.14, 11.50)). The odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.28 times greater among hypertensive patients with no comorbidities than among those with comorbidities (AOR = 3.28; 95% CI (1.04, 10.36)). Additionally, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.29 times greater among hypertensive patients whose duration since diagnosis was greater than two years than among those whose duration since diagnosis was less than two years (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI (1.47, 7.36)). Furthermore, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 67.7% lower among hypertensive patients who had not received nutritional education than among those who had received nutritional education (AOR = 0.32; 95% CI (0.13, 0.78)). Moreover, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 1.21 times greater among hypertensive patients who had good knowledge about hypertension and lifestyle than among those who had poor knowledge about hypertension and lifestyle (AOR = 1.21; 95% CI (1.03, 4.75) (Table 7).

Qualitative phase

Participant description

In this study, interviews were conducted with 8 hypertensive patients and 3 physicians to explore determinants of dietary adherence. Among them, 7 were males, and 4 were females. The participants were all adult hypertensive patients who were receiving outpatient treatment. The minimum and maximum ages of the participants were 30 and 62 years, respectively. The study participant’s longest period since being diagnosed with hypertension was 12 years, while their shortest period was 1 year.

Theme 1: sociodemographic factors

Age

The study participants expressed concerns about how age affects adherence to dietary recommendations. Thus, for example, a 30-year-old female physician expressed her feelings with age as follows:

…from my previous clinical work experience, what I observed is that the “hypertensive people who are found in their older age were willing to follow the recommended things”. This is because of their attention and practice towards the advice they obtained during their follow-up sessions…. [General practitioner from the chronic OPD clinic]

Place of residence

The study participants who participated in the study expressed concerns about how their residential area affects adherence to the recommended diet. Thus, for example, a 52-year-old female patient expressed her feelings in a residential area as follows.

…”since we are living in an urban area, everything is to be consumed to prevent the illness from becoming expensive due to the farmers as well as the merchants struggling with inflation”. We do not have enough land in the town for gardening, just as the people from the rural… [From the urban area].

Theme 2: socioeconomic factors

Household monthly income

The study participants expressed concerns regarding the impact of their household monthly income on their adherence to the recommended diet. Thus, for example, a 42-year-old male patient from a rural area expressed his feelings with a household monthly income impact as follows.

…As we have many expenditures at the family level, “fulfilling all that were recommended is somewhat difficult when I compare it with our current family income and level of inflation going on in our country.” [From the low-income household participant].

Availability of diet

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of the limited presence of food items around their environment on their adherence to the recommended diet. Thus, for example, a 40-year-old male patient expressed his feelings with a limited presence of food items as follows.

…Frankly speaking, I’m poor at having fruits. This is because “here around my living area, the fruits were not available as much as we got them from the merchants who were bringing them from the neighbor”, which is also expensive to use… [From the rural area].

Accessibility of diet

The study participants who participated in the study expressed concerns about the impact of limited access to new food options. Thus, for example, a 40-year-old male patient expressed his feelings with limited access as follows.

…Most times, I had a meal three times a day. As you know, “Our major food is something from tradition or the things we get by harvesting the land, but we did not have something new to consume accordingly.” [From the rural area].

Theme 3: personal conception-related factors

Knowledge

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of a lack of awareness of following the recommended diet. Thus, for example, a 54-year-old female patient expressed her feeling of a lack of knowledge about adherence to spicy food groups as follows.

…Most times, I had a meal from the shop as it was made, and I didn’t identify which one is best to consume. “I did not hear or have an awareness about the materials we are buying from the shop, whether they are good or not, except for salt”. [From the rural area]

Individual attitudes and beliefs

The study participants who participated in the study expressed concerns about the impact of influencing one person on others. Thus, for example, a 46-year-old female patient expressed her feelings with respect to and belief in beverages as follows.

…Additionally, some of the people follow the false saying in the community rather than asking the educated people. For example, “one of the sayings currently people with the illness applying is that beverages such as ‘tonic and Coca-Cola soft drink’ and ‘nech haraqe’ control blood pressure.” [From the rural area].

Cultural beliefs

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of cultural or religious beliefs. Thus, for example, a 54-year-old female patient expressed her feelings with cultural or religious beliefs as follows.

…Some of the people behind our neighbors also told me “Hypertension is not related to food because food is the gift from God; rather, hypertension is the result of a curse from generation to generation as a result of sin, and it will be healed through praying.” [From the rural area].

Theme 4: clinical factors

Duration of time since HTN diagnosis

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of the duration of HTN diagnosis on adherence to the recommended diet. Thus, for example, a 32-year-old male physician expressed his feelings as follows.

…as far as I am concerned and from my precious clinical knowledge, “those patients with long duration of time since their diagnosis are following the recommended diet and their medication in a better way”. The possible reason for this is that as the duration of time after their diagnosis increases, patients are exposed to different information or awareness about their illness as well as the methods of self-management. [General practitioner from the chronic OPD clinic]

Comorbidity (complex health condition)

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of their complex health condition. Thus, for example, a 62-year-old male patient from an urban area expressed his feelings about the impact of complex health conditions on dietary adherence as follows.

…Truly speaking, “the major problem that prohibited me from performing the recommended diet was the nature of my current health condition”. My child, sorry for my interruption, ‘Why is it not possible to avoid or to be completely healed from these conditions?’. Now, I’m feeling confused about the things I need to have because the meals recommended for hypertension are just raising the blood sugar level… [From the patient with hypertension and diabetes mellitus].

Nutritional education

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of receiving nutritional education. Thus, for example, a 52-year-old male patient expressed his feelings about the impact of nutritional education on dietary adherence as follows.

…I am controlling hypertension by performing daily routine work, which may be at home or out of the home, which induces or increases sweating. Additionally, “I am controlling hypertension by following what I am educated on by health professionals, like ceasing alcoholic consumption, minimizing salt and saturated oil, consuming vegetables and fruits, and taking medication as I was educated by health professionals to do so”. [From the urban area]

Theme 5: psychosocial factors

Patient‒physician relationship

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of the relationship between the physicians. Thus, for example, a 36-year-old male patient from an urban area expressed his feelings about the impact of his relationship with a physician on dietary adherence as follows.

…To tell you in a short way, my blood pressure has become controlled owing to “my eating a food as I was advised by the health professionals”. Most of the time, “I ask the physicians what to do to stop hypertension”, and I am doing physical activity accordingly. Today, I come here not to take medications but to get the checkup and to hear what to do next time… [From the urban area].

Peer pressure

The study participants who participated in the study expressed concerns about the impact of the influence of one person on others. Thus, for example, a 40-year-old male patient expressed his feelings about the impact of peer pressure on adherence to salt consumption as follows.

…Truly speaking, I am not following what the health professionals told me about salt restrictions. This is because of the “influence from my friends and colleagues”. They told me I will not control hypertension by salt restriction; rather, they told me to drink the alcohol named ‘Nechi Haraqe’ and ‘whisky’ to control the hypertension… [From the rural area].

Social support

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of social support. Thus, for example, a 36-year-old male patient expressed his feelings about the impact of social support on dietary adherence as follows.

…What I like to add is that as there is a mismatch between demands and supply in our country as well as at each district level, “it is better to work with the farmers to build up the level of supply and to minimize specifically the cost of natural products like fruits.” [From the urban area].

Shared dietary habits

The study participants expressed concerns about the impact of shared dietary habits. Thus, for example, a 50-year-old male patient expressed his feelings about the impact of shared dietary habits on dietary adherence as follows.

…Most times in the morning, we had coffee with kollo or Ambasha or dabboo or sometimes kokori. Most of the time, during lunchtime, we had qocho with cabbage or unrefined milk, which was also “our major food during the nighttime for dinner”. “Here around my neighbors, it is known that our major food is this”. [From the rural area]

Reliance on others (reliance on social networks)

The study participants who participated in the study expressed concerns about the impact of reliance on others or depending on what others did or decided. Thus, for example, a 42-year-old male patient expressed his feelings of reliance on others on dietary adherence as follows.

…truly talking, I didn’t know what the meal from the shop was made of; rather, I “had it as my wife or other workers in the restaurant offered it to me”, and I didn’t consider that it may cause illness… [From the rural area].

Discussion

Ensuring patients’ adherence to recommended diets to prevent complications of HTN remains a major challenge to public health in many developing countries. Poor adherence to the recommended diet is a reason for uncontrolled HTN, serious complications, and waste of healthcare resources. Thus, this study aimed to assess and explore the determinants of dietary adherence among hypertensive patients receiving outpatient treatment at public hospitals.

The current study revealed that the majority of hypertensive patients had low levels of adherence to the recommended diet. Accordingly, 39.7% (20.3%, 48.3%) of the hypertensive patients were adherent to the recommended diet. The findings of this study are relatively lower than those of studies performed in Israel (50%) [8], Jordan (58%) [9], Turkey (65%) [11], and Addis Ababa (64.7–69.1%) [46, 47]. This discrepancy with the two local studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, may be explained by the different study settings, socioeconomic situations, and food varieties in the two cities. Other studies from Israel, Jordan, and Turkey, however, might be the result of variations in the nations’ dietary practices, research participants’ residences, measurement equipment, and sample sizes. Studies conducted in Israel and Jordan involved 1000 and 1360 participants, respectively, in terms of sample size. In a study conducted in Turkey, participants had to have a diagnosis of hypertension for at least a year to be eligible, but those who had at least two follow-up visits were also included.

In contrast, the findings of this study were much greater than those of studies conducted in Pakistan (22.5%) [5], Benin (20%) [13] and Saudi Arabia (11.8%) [48]. This disparity may result from differences in the study population, sample size, adherence assessment method, and national eating patterns. In Pakistan, most social events essentially consist of “eating out” with friends and family. Data were gathered from small sample sizes in studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and Benin, with 150 and 144 participants, respectively. This might affect adherence to the recommended diet.

The findings of this study revealed that age, nutritional education, comorbidities, duration since hypertension diagnosis, and knowledge were determinants of dietary adherence among hypertensive patients. In this study, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.62 times greater among hypertensive patients older than 59 years than among those aged 18–39 years. The participants in the qualitative phase also supported this by relating it to the increase in age, which increased the patients’ attention and practice towards the advice they obtained from the physicians. This finding is in line with findings from studies conducted in several locations in Ethiopia [27, 49, 50]. Owing to their considerable experience and the health-related advice they receive to follow the recommended diet; elderly hypertension patients may have higher adherence levels. Conversely, elderly people may become more independent, have less peer pressure, undergo cognitive changes, and be more likely to manage several health conditions that require frequent medical attention.

Quantitatively, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.28 times greater among hypertensive patients with no comorbidities than among those with comorbidities. Likewise, the participants in the qualitative phase also expressed the impact of comorbidities on their adherence to the recommended diet. They linked this to their poor dietary adherence toward the prevention and control of their hypertension. A study conducted in the United States supported this finding, showing that respondents with comorbidities had greatly reduced adherence to the recommended diet [51]. In studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and Addis Ababa, comorbidities were also significantly associated with poor dietary adherence [29, 52]. This is because hypertensive patients with comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, obesity, or chronic kidney disease) often exhibit lower adherence to recommended dietary guidelines, such as the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet. This suggests that the presence of additional health conditions may hinder patients’ ability or motivation to follow dietary restrictions. Additionally, comorbidities can amplify barriers such as a lack of dietary education, low self-efficacy, and poor social support. For example, hypertensive patients with comorbidities are not willing to follow dietary guidelines, perceiving dietary changes as “impossible”.

Statistically, the present study revealed that the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 3.29 times greater among hypertensive patients whose duration since diagnosis was greater than two years than among those whose duration since diagnosis was less than two years. Qualitatively, the participants also expressed the impact of the duration of time since diagnosis on hypertensive patients’ adherence to recommended diets by relating it to the fact that as the duration of time after diagnosis increases, patients are exposed to different information or awareness about their illness as well as the methods of self-management. This finding was supported by studies performed in the Bahir Dar City Administration, Ethiopia [15] and China [53]. This may be because people who have had hypertension for less than two years do not consider it to be a life-threatening condition, in contrast to people who have had hypertension for more than two years, who may still adhere to rigorous lifestyle modifications because of the increased risk of complications over time. In regard to medication adherence, patients who are taking their medications as prescribed may not exhibit any symptoms or difficulties, which could lead to them neglecting to change their lifestyle.

According to the findings of this study, the odds of adherence to the recommended diet were 67.7% lower among hypertensive patients who had not received nutritional education than among those who had received nutritional education. Similarly, the participants in the qualitative phase also expressed the impact of nutritional education on their dietary adherence by relating it to following the recommended diet as educated by their physicians. This finding is consistent with similar studies conducted in Korea [12], Benin [13], and Bahir Dar, Ethiopia [28]. Furthermore, the results align with the outcomes of a study carried out in the United States of America [53]. This may be because those who have received extensive dietary education are more likely to have a greater awareness of the benefits of dietary control in managing hypertension. Nutritional education helps patients understand nutritional information about the disease and have a better chance of providing considerable information about the disease from different educational sources.

Furthermore, the quantitative findings of this study revealed that the odds of adhering to the recommended diet were 1.21 times greater among hypertensive patients who had good knowledge about hypertension and lifestyle changes than among those who had poor knowledge about hypertension and lifestyle factors. Similarly, the participants in the qualitative phase also expressed the impact of a lack of knowledge on their adherence to the recommended diet. They linked this to their lack of awareness of adherence to spicy food groups. These findings were in line with findings from studies conducted in Korea [12] and Benin [13]. This is because understanding hypertension and proper nutrition can positively affect a patient’s ability to access, use, and benefit from the suggested lifestyle choices. Hypertension knowledge is an important part of the chronic care model and knowing how to prevent and control the disease is an important part of managing it. This may be because knowledgeable patients may understand the disease and how to prevent and manage it. It is important to increase patient access to information on risk factors and dietary recommendations.

Qualitatively, the finding from this study explored that availability of diet, accessibility of diet, individual attitudes and beliefs, cultural or religious beliefs, shared dietary habits, and reliance on others (reliance on social networks)) were among the determinants of dietary adherence among patients with hypertension. Availability of Diet affects the adherence level If healthy, low-sodium, and potassium-rich foods (recommended for hypertension) are not readily available in local markets, patients may struggle to adhere to dietary guidelines. In addition to this, in areas where processed, high-salt foods are more accessible than fresh produce, patients may find it harder to maintain a heart-healthy diet. Moreover, food deserts (areas with limited grocery stores) force patients to rely on convenience stores or fast food, which often lack healthy options. Regarding individual attitudes and beliefs, patients who understand how diet affects blood pressure are more likely to adhere to recommendations, and those who believe they can control their diet are more likely to stick to it, whereas feelings of helplessness lead to poor adherence. Additionally, if family members do not follow or support dietary changes, the patient is less likely to adhere (e.g., cooking separate meals is burdensome), and peer pressure to eat unhealthy foods at events can derail adherence. Regarding reliance on others, for instance elderly or disabled patients who rely on others for meal preparation may have no control over their diet if caregivers ignore guidelines, and patients in nursing homes or hospitals may have limited food choices, affecting adherence. However, this finding was not supported by the study findings in quantitative phase, and also studies done in other places were not investigated it.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The mixed-methods approach of this study will provide healthcare managers and other stakeholders with a thorough understanding of the factors that influence dietary adherence in patients with hypertension. The likelihood of recall bias could be reduced because the study participants were selected in a public hospital setting following their completion of an outpatient follow-up, and they were interviewed in a separate room. Due case-control study has limitations in establishing causality and ensuring representative samples, it is difficult to definitively prove cause-and-effect relationships. Furthermore, as case-control studies are observational, it is difficult to determine whether an exposure truly causes the problem under study or is merely associated with it. Since self-reporting was the sole technique employed to measure adherence, the level of adherence may have been overestimated. Additionally, because the data were gathered in a hospital, participants may have given information in support of hospital staff out of frustration, which could lead to social desirability bias. However, since the necessary explanations were provided and the data collection and record process were conducted in a quiet room, we did not think this could have a big effect. Moreover, some of the variables (i.e., availability of diet, accessibility of diet, individual attitudes and beliefs, cultural or religious beliefs, shared dietary habits, and reliance on others (reliance on social networks)) under qualitative study were not clearly convergent compared with the quantitative findings of this study and other studies and need further follow-up in future studies.

Conclussion

This study revealed that age, not receiving nutritional education, absence of comorbidities, duration since hypertension diagnosis, and knowledge were the determinants of dietary adherence among hypertensive patients. Moreover, place of residence, household monthly income, availability of diet, accessibility of diet, individual attitudes and beliefs, cultural or religious beliefs, patient‒physician relationships, peer pressure, social support, shared dietary habits, and reliance on others (reliance on social networks) were among the other determinants of dietary adherence according to qualitative exploration. Addressing these challenges requires multifaceted interventions. Efforts should focus on working with hospital administrations, patents, and nutritionists to improve nutritional education services for younger patients, those with comorbidities, those with poor knowledge, those who do not receive nutritional education, and those patients who are influenced by peer pressure, follow shared dietary habits, and rely on social experience or networks. Additionally, hypertension patients and the general population should be given access to health education programs that address dietary adherence to improve their awareness, specifically for younger patients, patients with comorbidities, patients who do not receive nutritional education, and patients with low awareness of hypertension and lifestyle changes, as well as patients who have been influenced by peer pressure, follow shared dietary habits, and rely on social experience or networks. Future researchers need to consider longitudinal designs to clarify temporal relationships, incorporate objective dietary assessments, and explore community-level and health systems interventions to address psychosocial barriers by including all the variables identified under qualitative study.
