Metastable dual-defect states drive deep protonation for selective CO2 photomethanation
Authors: Ye He, Jianping Sheng, Qin Ren, Yao Lv, Yanjuan Sun, Sheng Dai, Fan Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient proton and charge carrier management is crucial in sustainable catalysis but is often constrained by the trade-off between proton supply kinetics and charge recombination, which limits selectivity. Here, we propose an in situ strategy to construct metastable adjacent dual-vacancy (MADV) sites, where dynamically tuned electronic states enable rapid electron transfer and spatial proximity ensures efficient mass transport, collectively enhancing proton-coupled electron transfer. Ti3d-derived active electronic states promote H2O dissociation, supplying abundant protons and forming hydroxylated surfaces for CO2 activation. Concurrently, dual-vacancy adjacency induces bidentate coordination, lowering the CO2 reduction barrier and steering selectivity toward CH4. The engineered MADV sites achieve nearly 100% CO2-to-CH4 selectivity with a production rate of 251.85 μmol g−1 h−1, approximately 75 times higher than pristine TiO2. These findings highlight the significance of adjacent sites with active electronic states in protonation processes and provide guidance for designing selective catalytic systems.Subject terms: Materials for energy and catalysis, Catalysis

Introduction

Proton shuttling is fundamental to catalysis, particularly in proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) processes, where protonation is intricately linked with electron transfer1–4. The PCET mechanism plays a pivotal role across diverse catalytic fields, including acid-base catalysis5, organic synthesis6, hydrogenation reactions7, ammonia synthesis8, electro/photocatalysis9,10, etc. It governs key aspects such as intermediate generation, reaction rates, product selectivity, energy conversion efficiency, and reaction pathways11–13. Consequently, advancing our understanding and optimizing the proton shuttling remain critical scientific challenges, which are technological cornerstones for sustainable catalytic development.

Effective proton shuttling relies on stable proton donation and efficient transfer14. In sustainable catalysis, water is widely used as a proton donor due to its abundance and environmental compatibility15. However, the high bond dissociation energy (BDE = 118 kcal/mol) renders water thermodynamically stable, making proton dissociation challenging16,17. To address this limitation, efficient water activation is essential for facilitating proton dissociation and enhancing proton shuttling, thereby improving catalytic performance. Defect engineering offers a promising strategy to lower the energy barrier for water dissociation by introducing mid-gap states18,19, which optimize the electronic state distribution near the Fermi level and enhance the hybridization between H2O and active sites, thereby facilitating H-O bond cleavage. Increasing defect density could theoretically improve water dissociation efficiency by amplifying the concentration of active electronic states20,21. However, excessive defect concentrations risk compromising material stability. Thus, achieving a precise balance by modulating mid-gap states to maximize water dissociation while minimizing defect concentration remains a critical challenge.

Beyond water dissociation, efficient proton transfer is equally crucial for effective proton shuttling and catalysis22–24. Since proton and electron transfers are coupled processes, precise temporal and spatial coordination is required25–28. However, the large mass difference between protons and electrons, complex proton transfer pathways, high spatial hindrance, and unstable intermediates slow the proton transfer, causing mismatched proton-electron transfer, reduced reaction rates, side reactions, and thus lower selectivity and efficiency in catalysis29–31. Minimizing proton transfer distance—ideally to the atomic-scale spacing between adjacent active sites—can enable faster and more efficient proton migration. Therefore, constructing adjacent active sites with enhanced electronic states is critical to overcoming water dissociation bottlenecks while ensuring efficient proton transfer.

In this study, we developed an in situ strategy to construct metastable adjacent dual vacancy (MADV) sites during light-induced reactions. This approach dynamically regulates the generation of multiple active electronic states while enhancing proton transfer efficiency through atomic-scale adjacent active sites. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we precisely controlled the thickness of ultrathin TiO2 films to induce intrinsic surface strain, weakening the Ti-O bond strength. We preconstructed stable oxygen vacancies (VO) to optimize the surface electronic distribution. Under optical excitation, oxygen atoms near these VO selectively escape, generating excited-state VO. The excited-state VO integrates with the preconstructed adjacent VO to form MADV, exhibiting self-recovery in the absence of light, thus ensuring material stability. The dynamic MADV modulates the electronic structure to generate multiple active electronic states, potentially accelerating electron transfer and enhancing H2O hybridization, thereby lowering the energy barrier for water dissociation and providing a plentiful supply of protons. Furthermore, atomically adjacent dual defects could shorten the interaction distance between protons and reaction intermediates, lowering the energy barrier for proton migration and enhancing shuttle efficiency. The enhanced coupling between protons and intermediates improves catalytic activity and selectivity, facilitating efficient CO2 photoreduction to CH4. Operando electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was employed to monitor the real-time dynamics of MADV and elucidate their key roles in electron and proton transfer. These findings provide deeper insights into metastable active sites with active electronic states and offer valuable guidance for designing efficient and stable catalysts for PCET-related reactions.

Schematic of MADV formation.

Regulating TiO2 thickness induces surface intrinsic strain, weakening bond energy (Step 1). Then, constructing stable VO regulates the electron distribution of neighboring oxygen atoms. These factors collaboratively drive the formation of metastable adjacent dual-vacancy sites (MADV) in situ under external forces (Step 2). The multiple active electronic states on MADV ensure effective water dissociation to supply abundant protons, while the proximity of dual sites facilitates effective intermediate protonation. MADV holds the potential for achieving high activity and selectivity in the photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to CH4.

Results and discussion

Inference of dual-vacancy site formation

Reducing the thickness of two-dimensional (2D) metal oxides can induce imbalanced interactions among surface atoms, which may result in surface intrinsic strain32. Theoretical results depicted in Fig. 2a suggest that surface intrinsic strain may led to noticeable tensile strain, elongating Ti-O bonds and consequently reducing their bond energy. Additionally, as shown by the Bader results in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1, the constrained electron transport direction in ultra-thin TiO2 could cause additional charges induced by the oxygen vacancies (VO) to be conducted primarily within the surface. This behavior differs from the vertical electron redistribution observed in the Thick-VO (Supplementary Fig. 1). On the Thin-VO surface, the most pronounced electron loss appears in the adjacent oxygen atom, labeled as O1, which may weaken the covalent bond strength between this O atom and the adjacent Ti atom. Coupled with the reduced Ti-O bond energy due to tensile strain, the oxygen atom O1 may become more prone to escape upon exposure to light energy, potentially forming dual vacancy sites alongside the preconstructed VO. We note that these DFT calculations are performed under idealized conditions and may not fully capture the structural and dynamic complexity of the catalyst under real catalytic environments.

Subsequently, TiO2 nanosheets with varying thicknesses and identical preconstructed VO concentrations were synthesized to validate the prediction regarding surface intrinsic strain. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2) reveal that both Thin-VO and Thick-VO exhibit a pure anatase structure. Additionally, EPR spectra (Fig. 2d) show identical vacancy peaks (g = 2.0038) in samples of equal mass, indicating an equal VO concentration in both materials33,34. Observations from scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. 2b, c) reveal the thickness of Thick-VO is approximately 29.1 nm and Thin-VO around 7.1 nm. Analysis of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 2b, c) reveals that these materials are square-shaped nanosheets exposing (001) surfaces. Notably, the lattice spacing of Thin-VO is 3.79 Å, showing a little bit of expansion compared to that in Thick-VO (3.73 Å). This evidence indicates tensile strain within Thin-VO, which is consistent with the computational findings.

Raman characterizations show similar peaks appearing at 144, 394, 514, and 636 cm−1 for both Thin-VO and Thick-VO (Fig. 2e), signifying the typical anatase TiO2 phase and corroborating the XRD results35. Furthermore, in anatase TiO2, the intensity ratio of the Raman vibration modes A1g and Eg is proportional to the exposure ratio of the (001) plane, indicating that the Thin-VO exhibits a higher proportion of the (001) crystal planes due to a larger proportion of exposed surfaces in thinner materials36. Notably, the Raman results show a significant red shift of the principal peak in Thin-VO (144 cm−1) compared to Thick-VO (146 cm−1), reflecting a substantial decline in bond energy for Thin-VO. The above experimental findings align with previous theoretical analysis and further confirm the propensity of Ti-O bond fracture to generate new sites in Thin-VO. Therefore, both theoretical calculations and experimental results support robust evidence of the design of ultra-thin TiO2 with inherent strain, highlighting its potential for forming MADV sites (Thin-MADV) to achieve high activity and selectivity in CO2 reduction.

Characterization of metastable adjacent dual-vacancy sites

The identification of MADV relies on a combination of theoretical modeling and multiple complementary in situ characterization techniques. As an initial approach, operando EPR was employed to track the dynamic evolution of VO during photocatalysis. Figure 3a reveals a substantial VO increase in Thin-VO under CO2 and H2O atmospheres upon illumination, while only marginal growth is observed in Thick-VO (Fig. 3b). A similar trend in VO evolution can be noted under vacuum conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that light triggers the formation of new VO in Thin-VO more efficiently. Besides, introducing oxygen in dark conditions would restore the newly formed Vo to its initial state, confirming the metastability of the new VO in Thin-VO (Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, in situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements on Thin-VO and Thick-VO further demonstrate a decrease in O/Ti molar ratios on Thin-VO from 1.92 to 1.85 under illumination, confirming the escape of oxygen atoms on Thin-VO (detailed analysis seen Supplementary Discussion 1, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, investigations into new VO sites in Thin-VO (Supplementary Fig. 7) reveal a significantly lower formation energy of this newly formed VO (4.34 eV) compared to preconstructed VO (5.03 eV). The site with the lowest VO formation energy corresponds precisely to the O1 site depicted in Fig. 2a, aligning with Bader analysis findings. Therefore, under light exposure, the O1 atom tends to escape more readily, leading to the formation of new VO that combines with pre-existing stable VO to create dual-vacancy sites. Conversely, in Thick-VO (Supplementary Fig. 8), the energy required for new VO formation increases to 5.13 eV, making it more challenging and accounting for the limited VO generation under illumination.

To further confirm the adjacency of dual-vacancy sites, we employed in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) using phenol as a probe to observe changes in peaks following its adsorption onto MADV. Phenol is selected as the probe for two main reasons: first, the specific adsorption of VO to oxygen atoms allows the hydroxyl group on phenol to be readily adsorbed by VO; secondly, the physical proximity of benzene rings upon adsorption onto adjacent dual-vacancy sites results in a π-π stacking effect37,38. This π–π stacking interaction might slightly increase the conjugation length of the benzene ring, causing a redshift in the infrared peak. Thus, investigating whether phenol adsorbs onto dual-vacancy sites and exhibits π–π stacking could help determine their adjacency. The sample underwent a 60-min purge with Ar at 100 °C to eliminate surface adsorbents. After introducing phenol for 20 min, the in situ reaction chamber was sealed, and the sample was irradiated to induce MADV formation. As shown in Fig. 3c, the in situ DRIFTS data illustrates that the peaks can be observed at 1605, 1598, 1500, and 1473 cm−1 on Thin-VO and Thick-VO, corresponding to the stretching of benzene rings in adsorbed phenol39. After illumination, the intensity of the ring stretching signal decreased on Thick-VO due to phenol desorption caused by energy injected from the light. Conversely, Thin-VO exhibited a noticeable increase in phenol signal intensity, attributed to an increased number of active sites for phenol adsorption induced by newly formed MADV under light exposure. Notably, while the ring stretching on Thick-VO remained unchanged under irradiation, the vibration peak of ring stretches on Thin-VO shifts to a lower wavenumber, consistent with the influence of π–π stacking on the benzene ring vibration. Therefore, it can be inferred that the dual vacancy sites are in proximity to each other.

Additionally, an experimental quantitative TEM utilizing normalized electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) analysis was carried out to directly visualize the adjacent dual vacancy sites. The main principle and analysis details are outlined in Supplementary Discussion 2 and Supplementary Figs. 9–11. The [111] zone axis was selected for observation, enabling differentiation between pure Ti and O atomic columns (Supplementary Fig. 9). Figure 3e shows an aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) image of the Thin-VO sample along the [111] zone axis, where red and blue spheres correspond to O and Ti atomic columns, respectively, providing atomic-scale structural verification of the MADV site. The corresponding integrated differential phase contrast STEM (iDPC-STEM) image is shown in Supplementary Fig. 12 to identify the positions of oxygen atomic columns. As shown in Fig. 3f, the L-edge EELS signals for the Ti atomic column marked by the blue circle in Fig. 3e (blue line) shifts to lower energy compared to the vacancy-free TiO2 reference sample (black line), indicating the presence of VO around this Ti column. To further confirm the proximity of the VO sites, we collected the normalized EELS results of the O atomic columns (Ⅰ, Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ) near this Ti column (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 13), with the positions of the O atomic columns marked as shown in Fig. 3e. It is clear that the O intensities per unit thickness at I and IV are significantly lower than those of standard TiO2, while the intensities at II and III are comparable to the standard. Therefore, VO is present in the I and IV columns. In the theoretical calculation model from the [111] zone axis in Fig. 3f, I and IV correspond to adjacent sites of dual vacancy, consistent with the theoretical predictions (Fig. 2a), further confirming the proximity of the MADV sites.

Subsequently, in situ Raman was utilized to confirm the formation of MADV and its metastable nature. Theoretically, the presence of dual-vacancy sites would diminish vibrational modes linked to the escaped oxygen atom, resulting in a decrease in Raman peak intensity. Additionally, the formation of dual-vacancy sites would shorten Ti-O bonds and reinforce their strength (Supplementary Fig. 14). As expected, in situ Raman tests (Fig. 3d) exhibit a notable decrease in the intensity of the primary Raman peak (144 cm−1) exclusively in Thin-VO under irradiation, accompanied by a peak blue shift, which is all not observed in Thick-VO. These results align with earlier theoretical predictions, confirming the in situ formation of dual-vacancy sites in the Thin-VO. Furthermore, upon cessation of irradiation and introduction of oxygen, we observe a gradual recovery signal of Thin-VO (Fig. 3d), indicating the refilling of oxygen into the newly formed VO. Therefore, the decrease in peak intensity is ascribed to the escape of oxygen atoms, while the phenomenon of self-recovery also validates the metastability of the newly generated VO. Consequently, Raman spectra vividly illustrate the process of MADV formation, supporting earlier speculation regarding the photo-induced oxygen atom escape. When combined with our previous understanding of the metastability of photoexcited VO, the findings from in situ Raman, in situ XPS, and operando EPR all confirm the metastable nature of MADV sites. The self-recoverable metastable property reveals the intrinsic structural reversibility and resilience, supporting the durable photocatalytic performance of Thin-VO.

Active electronic states on MADV

We initially utilized density functional theory (DFT) calculations to provide insight into how MADV sites dynamically regulate the electronic structure. The partial density of states (PDOS, Fig. 4a for Thin-VO, Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Discussion 3 for Thick-VO) suggests that the in situ formation of MADV sites could alter the electronic structure, leading to the possible emergence of multiple active electronic states near the Fermi level. These modulated active electronic states, primarily from Ti 3d orbitals situated below the Fermi level, suggest a tendency for active electrons accumulation on Ti atoms, which could faciliatate electron transfer. The electron accumulation on Ti atoms is further supported by Bader analysis (inset in Fig. 4d) and in situ XPS results (Supplementary Fig. 16). Additionally, after illumination, the peaks for adsorbed oxygen species in Fig. 4b (531.35 and 532.32 eV) obviously shift to lower binding energies, demonstrating that active electronic states facilitate electron transfer to surface adsorbates. Calculated adsorption energies suggest that the dynamic generation of active electronic states may enhance the adsorption and activation of reactants. Specifically, the CO2 adsorption energy increases from −0.06 eV to −0.21 eV, and the H2O adsorption energy increases from −0.5 eV to −0.99 eV (Supplementary Fig. 17). Thus, the in situ formation of MADV sites induces the variations in electronic structure that not only enhances the electron donor potential of Ti atoms to promote reactant adsorption and activation, but also shortens the transition pathway from active electronic states under illumination, thereby promoting the generation of more charge carriers.

To explore the electronic properties of the multiple active electronic states, we employed operando EPR to monitor electron transfer dynamics before and after irradiation. As shown in Fig. 4c, the adsorption process of Thick-VO remains nearly unchanged following irradiation. In contrast, for Thin-VO, the formation of active electronic states significantly alters the adsorption behavior of CO2 or H2O (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 18). The EPR signal decay increases from 0.49 to 0.99 (Fig. 4d), indicating a higher transfer of electrons to adsorbed molecules, which reflects those active electronic states provide a more efficient pathway for electron transfer. Additionally, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) results (Supplementary Fig. 19) show that the newly formed MADV sites (Thin-MADV) enable an increased CO2 adsorption from 17.5 ml g−1 to 30.9 ml g−1, substantially improving reactant adsorption, whereas no significant change is observed with Thick-VO, aligning with previous adsorption calculations. An accelerated adsorption kinetics phenomenon is also observed in Thin-MADV.

To accurately assess the electron transfer efficiency during the adsorption kinetics, we normalized the EPR signals and fitted them using a double exponential decay function (Fig. 4c, d), with the model choice explained in detail in Supplementary Discussion 4 and Supplementary Fig. 20. The two fitted time constants for Thin-VO are approximately 3365 s, while in Thin-MADV, the fast component decay time is 1413 s, significantly shorter than that of Thin-VO. This fact implies that the generation of active electronic states allows for rapid electron transfer. A similar trend in fitting is noted during H2O adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 18). Thus, the presence of multiple active electronic states not only increases the availability of transferable electrons but also regulates and accelerates the electron transfer process.

To further verify this accelerated electron transfer behavior from a dynamic perspective, we performed femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements on Thin-VO and Thick-VO before and after irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5a, the kinetic decay of signal at 507 nm reveals a significant reduction in the decay time constant of Thin-VO from 171.5 ps to 83.9 ps after light-induced formation of MADV sites, while Thick-VO exhibits a negligible change. This indicates that MADV sites markedly accelerate the decay of excited states, consistent with enhanced electron transfer efficiency. Moreover, TRPL spectra (Fig. 5b) show a similar trend: the average photoluminescence lifetime decreases from 7.2 ns (Thin-VO) to 4.4 ns (Thin-MADV), suggesting more efficient separation and transport of photogenerated carriers.

Furthermore, the in situ UV spectra (left side of Fig. 5c) and the emission peak of the in situ PL spectra (right in Fig. 5c) both show slight redshifts, suggesting a reduction in the bandgap due to the generation of active electronic states in the bandgap. Notably, after the light is turned off, the bandgap returns to its original level, highlighting a self-recovering metastable characteristic that ensures the stability of the catalyst. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results (right side of Fig. 5d) reveal that the generation of active electronic states promotes electron transfer and reduces resistance. Consequently, under continuous Xenon lamp irradiation, the enhanced electron excitation and transfer lead to a gradual increase in photocurrent (left side of Fig. 5d), presenting a sharp contrast to the gradual decay observed in Thick-VO. In summary, the dynamic modulation of active electronic states by in situ generated MADV sites further enhances electron transfer efficiency, underscoring the potential to improve reactivity and catalytic performance.

Efficient proton production on MADV

As shown in Fig. 4d, one of the fitting components of Thin-MADV exhibits a prolonged decay time of 7895 s, compared to Thin-VO. This decelerated adsorption process suggests an inhibited molecular adsorption, possibly due to rapid initial adsorption affecting subsequent steps. In order to examine this speculation, we conducted computational adsorption tests on MADV sites preoccupied by reactant. The results suggest that the subsequent CO2 adsorption capability on MADV decreased from −0.21 to −0.04 eV after initial CO2 adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 21). Similarly, the calculated adsorption energy for subsequent H2O adsorption capability decreased from −0.99 to −0.34 eV (Supplementary Fig. 22). These results imply that subsequent adsorption on MADV could be inhibited, with subsequent adsorption energies higher than those on Thin-VO (−0.06 eV for CO2 and −0.5 eV for H2O), which may contribute to slower adsorption kinetics. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 6a, when the gas mixture of CO2 and H2O is introduced instead of individual components, the decay time constants for both components on Thin-MADV (244 s and 1884 s, respectively) were notably lower than those on Thin-VO (3570 s), indicating an enhanced mutual activation of CO2 and H2O. Operando EPR results further suggests that CO2 and H2O mutually amplify each other’s electron utilization on MADV. A critical question arises: what mechanism promotes electron utilization between CO2 and H2O molecules on MADV during photocatalysis? We initially performed operando EPR under direct illumination with CO2 and H2O, as shown in Fig. 3a. However, the resulting signal was convoluted with overlapping processes, including MADV formation and photo-induced charge transfer, making it difficult to isolate the intrinsic molecule site interactions. Therefore, to eliminate such interference, all subsequent adsorption and spectroscopy measurements were conducted after pre-generating MADV sites under illumination, followed by reaction gas introduction under dark conditions. Firstly, operando EPR was employed to track electron transfer in irradiated Thin-VO and Thick-VO during sequential introduction with H2O and CO2. As shown in Fig. 6b, upon the introduction of H2O, the electron signal is decreased for both materials, suggesting H2O is gaining the electrons accumulated at the VO sites. The midsection of Fig. 6b illustrates a more rapid decline in Thin-MADV, indicating a higher electron transfer rate and further underscoring the role of MADV in promoting rapid electron transfer. Subsequently, CO2 was introduced into the system. Interestingly, in contrast to Thick-VO, CO2 adsorbed on Thin-MADV appears to exhibit electron-donating behavior, indicating an electron-deficient CO2δ+ state. However, when the order of introduction for H2O and CO2 was reversed (bottom of Fig. 6b), CO2 reverted to displaying electron acceptor characteristics. This shift indicates that the presence of H2O modulates the CO2 activation state by influencing its electron transfer direction. To explore this, we conducted a Bader analysis on the optimized CO2 adsorption structure at MADV sites with either adsorbed water or hydroxyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 23). Bader charge analysis indicates that CO2 adsorbed on the water-covered MADV sites gains 0.01 e−, whereas on the hydroxylated MADV surfaces, CO2 is calculated to donate approximately 0.24 e−, forming CO2δ+ species with depleted electron density. This electron depletion aligns well with the operando EPR observations (midsection of Fig. 6b). Such activation facilitates the subsequent electron-gaining reduction steps during photocatalysis. Thus, it can be concluded that MADV sites inherently possess a strong capability to facilitate H2O dissociation, even in the absence of illumination.

To further explore how the MADV site activates H2O and facilitates its spontaneous dissociation, we utilized Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (COHP) analysis to examine the bonding strength and orbital hybridization of Ti-H2O and H-O40,41. As depicted in Fig. 6c, anti-bonding orbitals are calculated to exist below the Fermi level on Thick-VO, leading to electrons filling in higher energy orbitals. In contrast, on Thin-MADV, the energy of anti-bonding orbitals shifts upward, which may reduce electron occupancy in high-energy orbitals and promote more filling into low-energy bonding orbitals. This trend suggests a strong interaction between Ti atoms at the MADV site and H2O molecules. A more negative Integrated Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (−ICOHP) value also suggests a stronger bonding. Consequently, based on the proportion of −ICOHP for overlapping orbitals in Fig. 6d, the hybridization between O2p and Ti3d appears enhanced compared to Thick-VO, which can be attributed to the substantial filling of unpaired electrons into Ti3d orbitals after the in situ generation of active electronic states. Furthermore, in Fig. 6e, we observe a decrease in the bonding strength of the H-O bond when H2O adsorbs onto either Thin-MADV or Thick-VO. Particularly at the MADV sites, more pronounced anti-bonding orbitals are near the Fermi level, which may lead to electron filling into higher energy orbitals, thereby weakening the H-O bonding. The decrease in the O2p-H1s ICOHP value is likely due to the stronger tendency of O2p to hybridize with Ti3d orbitals, further promoting the breaking of the H-O bond and the formation of H protons. Then, we estimated the activation energy for H2O dissociation on Thin-MADV and Thick-VO. As shown in Fig. 6f, H2O dissociation energy is merely 0.25 eV at the MADV site, significantly lower than the 1.39 eV observed at Thick-VO. The high likelihood of spontaneous H2O dissociation at the MADV site is consistent with COHP analysis. Subsequently, in situ DRIFTS further revealed a notable increase in hydroxyl signals at 3697 cm−1, originating from H2O dissociation at the MADV site during water adsorption42,43. Therefore, the presence of active electronic states offers advantages in activating H2O molecules and facilitating their spontaneous dissociation.

Mechanism of CH4 selectivity

The above congruence between experimental and theoretical results reveal that light-induced in situ formation of MADV sites enable rapid electron transfer via active electronic states, accelerating the adsorption and direct dissociation of surface H2O molecules. We then specifically investigated the adsorption behavior on the hydroxylated MADV surface. The DFT calculations suggest that CO2 may exhibit stronger adsorption (−0.58 eV) compared to H2O (−0.40 eV), which could indicate that hydroxylation promotes CO2 uptake (Supplementary Fig. 24). Theoretically, hydroxylation derived from H2O dissociation may favor CO2 adsorption, while the concurrently generated abundant protons could potentially assist selective CH4 production through subsequent CO2 hydrogenation and deoxygenation. Protonation thus serves a dual role: activating CO2 and steering product selectivity toward CH4 by dictating hydrogenation and deoxygenation pathways.

To experimentally validate this mechanism, as shown in Fig. 7a, in situ DRIFTS was employed to characterize protonation behavior in intermediate species through analyzing the adsorption processes of mixed gas (CO2 and H2O) in Thin-VO, Thick-VO, and Thin-MADV, respectively. To ensure data comparability, Thin-VO was restored to its initial state via 100 °C heating and Ar flushing in the in situ DRIFTS reaction chamber after completing adsorption tests. Subsequently, under light exposure, Thin-VO was then in situ converted to Thin-MADV for subsequent dark-state adsorption analysis. As hypothesized, Thin-MADV exhibited significantly enhanced CO2 adsorption, evidenced by intensified adsorption peaks that confirm MADV’s activation effect. Notably, introducing a mixed gas of CO2 and H2O into MADV sites (Fig. 7a, right) induced prominent COOH− peaks at 1631 and 1717 cm−1 44, identifying a key intermediate from CO2-protons coupling. The generation of COOH− is widely recognized as a rate-limiting step in the CO2 reduction.

Theoretical optimization of adsorption structures (Fig. 7a, middle) further reveals that CO2 prefers a stable bidentate configuration on hydroxylated MADV sites. This bidentate mode has the potential to lower CO2 molecular energy and reduce reduction barrier45. In contrast, at single VO sites in Thin-VO, CO2 adopts a monodentate adsorption mode. This theoretical speculation is further corroborated by in situ DRIFTS results: CO2 adsorption profiles differ significantly between MADV and Thin-VO sites. Specifically, on Thin-VO and Thick-VO (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 25), CO2 adsorption peaks mainly appeared at 1541 and 1375 cm−1 (monodentate mode)46,47, whereas on Thin-MADV (Fig. 7a), peaks predominantly concentrated at 1620 and 1343 cm−1, indicating a shift to a bidentate mode with higher intensity48,49. Therefore, hydroxylated MADV sites fundamentally alter the CO2 adsorption geometry, thereby promoting enhanced generation of COOH− during CO2 adsorption on Thin-MADV under dark-state without additional energy input. This observation confirms that the bidentate coordination mode reduces the CO2 reduction energy barrier, enabling efficient interaction with active protons derived from H2O dissociation.

Prior studies consistently emphasized that the CO* → CHO* pathway is pivotal for CH4 selectivity in CO2 reduction50, determining whether the CO* intermediate dissociates or undergoes further protonation to form CHO* for CH4 generation. To assess this, we computed the Gibbs free energy of the CO conversion process. The results (inset in Fig. 7b, c) suggest that on Thick-VO, both CO desorption (−0.16 eV) and hydrogenation processes (−0.24 eV) may occur spontaneously, with similar energies, indicating the potential for both CO and CH4 generation, hence leading to poor selectivity. However, on Thin-MADV, the CO desorption energy is 0.89 eV, indicating a stronger association of the CO* intermediate with MADV sites, potentially reducing the likelihood of CO desorption. Simultaneously, the energy for CHO* generation is −1.69 eV, suggesting a spontaneous reaction. Consequently, CO* tends to undergo deep hydrogenation, making the photocatalytic selective reduction of CO2 to CH4 thermodynamically more feasible on Thin-MADV. Based on in situ DRIFTs data during the reaction (Fig. 7b, c), we observe a gradual accumulation of HCO3− (1436 and 1380 cm−1)51 and CO* (2170 and 2065 cm−1)52 on Thick-VO, along with minimal C-H signals around 3100–2700 cm−1 and 1383 cm−1 53, aligning with the poor selectivity for CH4 on Thick-VO. Conversely, on Thin-MADV, CO* is rapidly consumed and undergoes deep protonation. The characteristic peak at 1370 cm−1 is assigned to bidentate-adsorbed HCOO* species53, marking a key step in the methanation pathway. Signals for CH3O* (2983 and 2818 cm−1) and CH2O* (2885 cm−1)54, combined with C-H stretching and bending vibrations at 2784 cm−1 and 1382 cm−1, confirm the presence of hydrogenated alkyl-like intermediates53,55. These observations further validate the highly selective methanogenesis at MADV sites. All these advantages could enable the Thin-MADV to achieve close to 100% selectivity in photo-methanation and display notable CO2 photoreduction activity.

Photoreduction of CO2 over MADV

Photocatalytic CO2 reduction properties were evaluated on prepared samples to verify the positive role of MADV. In Fig. 8a, compared to the Thin sample without VO, which exhibited CO and CH4 production rates of 5.38 and 3.37 μmol g−1 h−1, respectively. Thin-VO showcased a substantial increase in CH4 yield, reaching 251.85 μmol g−1 h−1, representing an 75-fold enhancement. The activity of Thin-VO was repeated three times (Supplementary Fig. 26), confirming the reliability of the data. On Thick-VO with single-vacancy sites, the CO and CH4 generation rates were 111.15 and 35.49 μmol g−1 h−1, respectively, with CH4 selectivity at only 24.20%. In contrast, the presence of MADV could significantly enhance the CH4 selectivity to around 100%. The production and selectivity of CH4 in MADV have been significantly enhanced.

Control experiments (Fig. 8b) validate that the origin of CH4 is from CO2 photoreduction. Carbon isotope analysis (Fig. 8c) further reveals that CH4 primarily originated from isotopically labeled CO2. Furthermore, prolonged testing (Fig. 8c) displays the sustained stability of Thin-VO over 14 h during the CO2 photocatalytic reduction. Post-reaction characterizations further confirm that its crystal structure and chemical states remain unchanged (detailed analysis seen Supplementary Discussion 5 and Supplementary Figs. 27–30), supporting its structural robustness. Apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) measurements (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 8d and Supplementary Discussion 6) show a wavelength-dependent response matching the absorption of Thin-MADV, with the AQE value of 5.21% at 365 nm, indicating CH4 production is driven by photogenerated carriers. Supplementary Table 3 present a comparison of our data with representative recent CO2-to-CH4 photocatalysts. These findings further affirm the photocatalytic performance of CO2 on Thin-VO, consistent with the anticipated role of MADV in promoting CH4 activity and selectivity.

In conclusion, this study employs strain modulation coupled with in situ defect engineering to precisely construct MADV sites, generating active electronic states that enhance proton shuttle efficiency. A combination of advanced in situ techniques, including operando EPR, in situ DRIFTS, and HAADF-STEM, verifies the formation of MADV, its self-recovering metastability, and the close proximity of dual sites. The in situ-generated MADV dynamically alters the electronic structure, producing multiple active electronic states contributed by Ti3d. These states double the electron transfer quantity and accelerate the electron transfer rate by 2.4 times, as evidenced by operando EPR. The enhanced hybridization of the active electronic state Ti3d with O2p orbitals in H2O weakens the H-O bonds, facilitating the spontaneous H2O dissociation. This process generates hydroxylated surfaces and abundant hydrogen protons, facilitating CO2 adsorption and activation. The dual-site proximity modifies the CO2 adsorption configuration to a bidentate form, shortening the proton migration pathway and enhancing the deep hydrogenation of intermediates. Consequently, the in situ-formed MADV achieves a CH4 production rate of 251.85 μmol g−1 h−1 with nearly 100% selectivity in CO2 photoreduction. This work provides both experimental insights and innovative protocols into achieving efficient protonation processes and their precise detection in general catalytic systems.

Methods

Materials

Anatase TiO2 nanosheets were synthesized using commercially available chemicals. Tetrabutyl titanate (Ti(OBu)4, 98%) and hydrofluoric acid (47%) were obtained from Adamas-beta (Shanghai, China) and used directly without further purification.

Caution

Hydrofluoric acid solutions are stored in Teflon containers due to their corrosive nature.

Materials fabrication

300 μL of hydrofluoric acid solution [HF, 47%] was mixed with 5 mL Tetrabutyl titanate [Ti(OC4H9)4, TBT], and the resulting solution was then transferred into an 80 ml Tefron-lined autoclave. The mixture solution was heated at 180 °C for 24 h. The as-prepared powders were thoroughly rinsed with ethanol and water, freeze-dried at −80 °C overnight, and then calcined in air at 470 °C for 1 h (heating rate: 1 °C min−1) to obtain the sample denoted as Thin. The sample Thick was synthesized using the same procedure, but the amounts of HF solution and TBT were adjusted to 3 mL and 25 mL, respectively. Thin-VO and Thick-VO were synthesized by calcining Thin and Thick at 300 °C for with a heating rate of 1 h in an Ar atmosphere, with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1. The resulting powerders were colloected for further characterization.

Structure characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD, Shimadzu XRD-6100) was employed for structural analysis. The light absorption property was assessed using UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS, Shimadzu UV-2600). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were conducted with Alpha300RA. Unpaired electrons in the samples were investigated using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Bruker EMX nano). The chemical states and surface composition were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ system with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV), applying C 1 s at 284.8 eV for charge correction. To examine the surface morphology, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEM-2010) and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Zeiss Gemini SEM 300) were employed. CO2 adsorption experiments were performed on static volumetric adsorption analyzer (BSD-PS2). The analysis of isotope-labeled samples was conducted using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GCMS, QP-2020 NX, Shimadzu). The structural and compositional features of the samples were investigated using aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) on a ThermoFisher Themis Z microscope, which is equipped with dual aberration correctors. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) images were obtained under a convergence semi-angle of 25 mrad, with inner and outer detector collection angles set to 47 and 200 mrad, respectively. Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) data were recorded using a Gatan Enfinium ER (model 977) spectrometer, employing its dual-EELS capability to acquire detailed spectroscopic information. EELS spectra were acquired in STEM mode under the following conditions: 300 keV accelerating voltage, 30 pA beam current, 25.0 mrad convergence semi-angle, and a collection semi-angle of 36.8 mrad using a 5 mm aperture. Spectrum images were collected with a 0.1 nm pixel size and 0.2 s dwell time per pixel, with drift correction applied to mitigate sample movement. Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) was employed to explore ultrafast exciton dynamics, with experiments conducted using a pump wavelength of 320 nm and a pump power of 470 μW. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were conducted using a femtosecond Yb-doped fiber laser system (FemtoYL-IR-100, YSL Photonics) operating at a wavelength of 343 nm, which was obtained via third-harmonic generation. The laser pulses, with a repetition rate of 2.5 MHz, were directed onto the samples through a UV objective lens (M Plan UV50X, Mitutoyo). Photoluminescence emission from the sample was collected by the same objective and detected by an avalanche photodiode (MPD-050-CTE, Picoquant). The resulting signals were analyzed using a time-correlated single-photon counting system (MultiHarp 150, Picoquant), providing a temporal resolution of approximately 200 ps.

In situ characterizations

Operando electron paramagnetic resonances spectroscopy

Operando EPR experiments were carried out on a Bruker EMX nano spectrometer using a specially designed reaction chamber (Supplementary Fig. 31) that integrates gas system, light source, and vacuum system. The catalyst powders, weighed precisely beforehand, were carefully loaded into the quartz chamber. The sample height inside the quartz chamber was carefully adjusted with silica wool. Then, we evacuated the chamber to vacuum and then injected different atmosphere for the following adsorption or photocatalysis process. EPR signals were continuously collected under operando conditions at room temperature using a Bruker EMX nano spectrometer operating at approximately 9.58 GHz. The instrument was configured with a sweep width of 100 G, sweep time of 30 s, time constant of 20.48 ms, modulation amplitude of 2.0 G, an attenuator setting of 20 dB, and an X-axis resolution of 2000. The temporal evolution of the signal intensity was quantified by performing double integration of the spectra, enabling detailed tracking of relative variations during the different experimental stages.

Quasi In situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

Following a 15-min irradiation in a dedicated preparation chamber, the sample was immediately moved to the XPS analysis chamber under an air-free environment33.

In situ Raman measurement

In situ Raman study was conducted by High-resolution and ultra-sensitive confocal Raman spectrometer (LabRAM Soleil, HOBIBA) equipped with 532 nm laser source (Oxxius 532), Synapse Charge Coupled Device (CCD) detector. Raman spectra were collected under conditions of Ar or O2 gas flow, with or without illumination, and recorded in real-time throughout the entire reaction process.

In situ DRIFTS measurement

In situ DRIFTS spectra were recorded on a Tensor II FT-IR spectrometer (Bruker, GER). A key pretreatment step involved heating the loaded photocatalyst to 150 °C under 100 mL/min He flow for 30 min, effectively desorbing residual H2O, CO2, and hydrocarbons from the surface. After cooling to room temperature, a CO2 and H2O mixture was introduced into the reaction cell. Adsorption–desorption equilibrium was established before illumination with a 300 W Xe lamp (AM1.5 filter), during which time-resolved FT-IR spectra were recorded. To assess the effect of dual active sites, the photocatalyst was sequentially exposed to pure H2O, CO2, and H2O mixture, and phenol. Following 30 min of illumination, the light was switched off, and the adsorption process was repeated. Spectra were collected every 3 s before and after illumination to monitor dynamic changes31.

Density functional theory calculation

The TiO2 structural model used in our DFT calculations was based on experimental data. The bulk phase corresponds to JCPDS No. 21-1272, confirmed by XRD (Supplementary Fig. 2), and the exposed (001) surface was selected according to HRTEM and SEM observations (Fig. 2b, c), providing a realistic basis for the surface model. All DFT calculations were performed using the “Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP5.4 code)”. Electronic exchange and correlation effects were described by the generalized gradient approximation in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) form. To correctly represent the localized Ti 3d states, a Hubbard parameter (U = 4.0 eV) was introduced. The plane-wave basis was truncated at 400 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled with a k-point interval of about 0.04 Å−1. All atomic positions were relaxed until the residual force on each atom was less than 0.01 eV Å−1, after which the optimized structures were used for electronic structure calculations. The volume of exchanged electrons (Δq) was calculated using the Bader method. Chemical-bonding analyses were carried out with the computer program LOBSTER (Local Orbitals Basis Suite Towards Electronic Structure Reconstruction).

The adsorption energy (Eads) is defined as Eads = Etot – (Es + Emol), where Etot, Es, and Emol depict the total energy of the adsorption complex, the pure catalyst, and the isolated molecule, respectively. The Gibbs free energy variation (∆G) from the initial state (IS) and final state (FS) was given by the equation:△G=EFS−EIS+△EZPE−T△S, incorporating the differences in zero-point energy (ΔEZPE) and entropy (∆S) at room temperature (T, 298.15 K). Transition state (TS) were located via the climbing image-nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method and confirmed by a single imaginary frequency, enabling the calculation of activation energies (Ea) as Ea=ETS−EIS.

Photocatalytic activity measurements

The photocatalytic CO2 reduction activity was examined using an MC-SPB10 system (Beijing Merry Change Technology CO., Ltd.), which integrates a sealed quartz reactor, a 300 W Xe light source equipped with an AM 1.5 G filter, and online gas chromatography (GC). Cooling water circulated through the reactor to suppress thermal effects during irradiation, maintaining the system at approximately 20 °C. 10 mg of photocatalyst was dispersed in 1.0 mL of deionized water and uniformly deposited on a glass fiber membrane (diameter: 2.0 cm). The reaction system was evacuated repeatedly before introducing CO2 with water vapor until the total pressure reached 101 kPa. Gaseous products periodically monitored using gas chromatography fitted with both FID and TCD detectors. No other gaseous products beyond CH4 and CO were detected within the GC detection limit. The apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) of the photocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction was measured using a high-uniformity integrated optical fiber light source (PLS-SXE300UV, Beijing Perfectlight Technology Co., Ltd.) to ensure uniform illumination over the catalyst surface. Monochromatic LED filters were applied to isolate specific wavelengths. The amount of incident photons (N) at each wavelength was calculated using the equation: N = Eλ⁄hc, where E is the measured light intensity, λ is the incident light wavelength, h is Planck’s constant, and c is the speed of light. The AQE for CH4 generation was calculated as: AQE=(8nCH4/N)×100%, where nCH4 is the amount of CH4 produced. The factor of 8 accounts for the number of electrons required to generate one CH4 molecule from CO2. The example calculation is depicted in Supplementary Discussion 6.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical experiments were conducted using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation with Pt as the counter electrode and Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference electrode. The photocathode with the loaded sample served as the working electrode in a standard three-electrode setup. The electrolyte used was Na2SO4 (0.2 M). A 300 W xenon lamp was used as the light source. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out with a sinusoidal ac perturbation of 5 mV applied over the frequency range of 0.01–105 Hz.
