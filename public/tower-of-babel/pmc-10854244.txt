To Analyze the Efficacy of Platelet-rich Plasma in Contrast to Platelet-rich Fibrin along with Synthetic Nanocrystalline Hydroxyapatite and β-tricalcium Phosphate Bone Graft in Regeneration of Bony Defects in Children
Authors: Nonie Marianne Koksi Sangma Shadap, Gunjan Yadav, Sonali Saha, Kavita Dhinsa, Anshul Sharma, Amit Rai
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo analyze the efficacy of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in contrast to platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) along with synthetic nanocrystalline hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) bone graft in the regeneration of bone defects in children.Materials and methodsA total of 20 children in whom bone defect was present were selected and divided into two groups of 10. In group I, the bone defect was filled with PRP + nanocrystalline HA with β-TCP and group II, with PRF + nanocrystalline HA with β-TCP. Radiographs using VistaScan with a mesh grid were taken to analyze the bone density and bone regeneration. Clinical evaluation with respect to the color of overlying mucosa, sinus formation, pus discharge, and seepage of bone graft was done. The parameters used to analyze the radiographs included gray scale analysis, residual bone defect calculation in pixels both performed in CorelDraw version 13 software, and radiopaque scoring scale.ResultsThe mean bone defect density measured using gray scale was higher in the PRF than PRP group. The percentage of bone formation analyzed using residual bone defect calculation and mean radiopaque score revealed that the PRF group showed better outcomes compared to the PRP group.ConclusionBoth PRP and PRF are equally effective in promoting bone regeneration with PRF showing slight superiority over PRP.How to cite this articleKoksi Sangma Shadap NM, Yadav G, Saha S, et al. Introduction

Skeletal inadequacy in the jaw develops spontaneously as a result of disorders including cysts, tumors, and infections or physician-induced factors brought on by the surgical excision of antecedent intraosseous periradicular diseases.1 It was reported by Gokmenoglu et al. that every year, innumerable teeth were extracted due to bone defects.2 Handling of bony defects represents an immense test for doctors who are constantly seeking to enhance the efficacy of bone grafting with bone substitutes.1

Regeneration has emerged as a key goal in the field of therapy and is currently a significant focus of investigation.3 Regenerative therapy employs biomaterials such as diverse bone grafts and barrier membranes to facilitate optimal healing and regeneration of the bony defects.4

A desirable objective of surgical treatment is the reconstruction of the depleted bony tissue in children.5 The healing capacity of bone in children, especially, is good, however; the regenerative capability is restricted in cases of extensive defects, such as postenucleating cysts, substantial periapical lesions, or compromised healing ability of the host.5 In such cases as well, regenerative therapies may be used. Material to be used to recover osseous tissue should meet specific requirements of exemplary standards like biocompatibility, bioresorbability, osteoconductivity, osteoinductivity, structural similarity to bone, porosity, mechanical resilience, user-friendliness, safety, and affordability.6

Recognition and competent methods of utilizing growth factors have greatly enhanced bone regeneration procedures, in recent times. The regenerative mechanism of the human body amplified by employing patients’ blood is now substantiated in the literature. The procedure in this is to expedite and enhance the impact of growth factors found in platelets, the catalysts for bone regeneration initiation.5 Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) plays an important role as an arbiter for bone healing and remodeling as well as significant in stimulating the multiplication of nonspecialized mesenchymal cells.1 Presently, PRP and PRF are being utilized as they are abundant sources of autologous growth factors.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) have gained widespread use in different dental fields. Numerous studies have demonstrated their efficacy in enhancing soft tissue and bone regeneration for periodontal defects and postextraction sockets, either independently or in conjunction with various graft materials. Nonetheless, very scarce documentation is available regarding the use of PRP and PRF in bone defects. To this day, no research has been undertaken to compare the efficacy of PRP and PRF in combination with β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) in bone defects in children. Thus, the present in vivo study has been undertaken to evaluate and compare the efficacy of PRP and PRF with synthetic nanocrystalline hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate bone graft in the regeneration of osseous defects of jaws in children.

Materials and Methods

The research was carried out as a prospective, single-blinded clinical trial after approval of the Institutional Ethical Committee of Sardar Patel Post Graduate Institute of Dental and Medical Sciences, Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, India. Subjects, selected from the Department of Pedodontics and Preventive Dentistry, comprised 20 children between the ages of 7 and 14 years divided equally into groups of 2. Group I consisted of PRP + nanocrystalline HA with β-TCP and group II, PRF + nanocrystalline HA with β-TCP. Inclusion criteria comprised children in whom bone defect was created after any surgery for bony pathology as well as systemically healthy, cooperative children and in whom the defects were able to be closed by primary closure. Whereas, children who were medically vulnerable, uncooperative, mentally challenged, and those with bony diseases were excluded. A general assessment was carried out through their medical and dental history, clinical examination, and radiographic and routine hematological examination (CBC, complete blood count; BT, bleeding time; CT, clotting time, and Hb%, hemoglobin; viral markers) before surgery.

Platelet-rich Plasma Preparation

This was prepared according to Nagaveni et al.5

Patients had 10 mL of blood drawn from their antecubital fossa intravenously and collected before the surgical procedure in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-coated vacuum tube made of sterile plastic. The REMI R-8C automated centrifuge machine was utilized to obtain PRP by spinning at a speed of 1300 rpm for 10 minutes.

Following the centrifugation process, three distinct layers were obtained—an upper straw-colored fluid known as platelet-poor plasma (PPP), a middle layer rich in platelets called the buffy coat, and a lower layer abundant in red blood cells (RBC). The straw-colored plasma, along with the buffy coat and 1 mL of the RBC layer, was then subjected to further centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes. The resulting PRP, which appeared as a yellow coat at the bottom of the test tube, was carefully collected using a pasture pipette and transferred to a sterile tube. Conversely, the PPP was discarded.

For activation and just before its application, a PRP gel was formed by adding 6 mL of calcium chloride and thrombin to PRP, which was then mixed with Sybograf Plus bone graft [synthetic nanocrystalline HA with β-TCP composite (Eucare Pharmaceuticals, Jamshedpur, India.)] in a 1:1 volume preparation (Fig. 1).

Platelet-rich Fibrin Preparation

In compliance with Choukroun's 2001 protocol, the PRF was prepared by withdrawing 10 mL of blood intravenously from the patients’ antecubital vein and collecting it in a glass-coated plastic tube without anticoagulant. The tube was promptly subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes in an automated centrifuge machine (REMI R-8C) forming a well-organized fibrin clot in the center of the tube, between the PPP located at the top and the red corpuscles settled at the bottom. Following the extraction of PPP, PRF was meticulously extracted using sanitized tweezers while securing a small layer of RBC and deposited onto a sterilized dappen dish (Fig. 1).

Surgical Procedure

Around 5% povidone-iodine solution and 0.2% chlorhexidine digluconate rinse were used and both external and internal areas of the mouth were sanitized. To minimize discomfort, the operative area was numbed with a 2% lignocaine hydrogen chloride solution containing adrenaline which was administered through either a nerve block or infiltration technique, depending on the location. On the labial surfaces of the adjacent teeth, crevicular incisions were made using a Bard Parker blade No.15. A mucoperiosteal flap was then carefully lifted, taking care to preserve the interdental papillary tissue. Once the underlying osseous defect was exposed, a meticulous debridement of both soft and hard tissues was performed using curettes. The osseous margins were smoothened out for optimal healing. Finally, 0.9% normal saline was used for copious irrigation. The root end of the involved teeth was resected and retrograde obturation and filling were done using gutta-percha points and glass ionomer cement, respectively.

In group I, after surgical bone defect removal, PRP was mixed with nanocrystalline HA and β-TCP bone graft (Sybograf plus), and in group II, PRF was mixed with nanocrystalline HA and β-TCP bone graft (Sybograf plus) and these were used for bone regeneration. 3–0 Black Silk was utilized to suture and reposition the mucoperiosteal flaps.

Systemic antibiotics amoxicillin 20 mg/kg/day every 8 hours, analgesic ibuprofen 15–18 mg/kg/day every 8 hours, and multivitamin becosule once a day for 10 days were prescribed. Patients were provided with instructions for postoperative care and maintaining oral hygiene including the use of 0.2% chlorhexidine digluconate mouthwash twice daily. A reassessment was conducted 24 hours following the surgery to check for any acute indications of inflammation or infection. After 7 days, the sutures were taken out and the surgical area was rinsed with normal saline.

Any indications or manifestations of postoperative complications in patients were monitored through clinical evaluation based on the following parameters:

Radiographic Evaluation

The evaluation of bone density and regeneration was conducted through radiographs taken using VistaScan. Preoperative radiographs were analyzed followed by an assessment of the immediate postoperative period after the 1st, 3rd, and 5th months in both groups. CorelDraw software version 13.0 was used for radiological analysis. A gray scale of 256 tonalities was employed to measure bone density on the radiographs.

The measurement of bone formation was performed using scoring criteria given by Nakkeeran et al.1:

The residual defect size of the cavity was measured in pixels using the same software at preoperative and postoperative 1-, 3-, and 5-month periods. The residual defect size was measured using the freehand mask tool and the length and breadth measurements obtained were utilized to calculate the area and thus the scale of the defect. Finally, the percentage increase in bone formation was calculated by the given formula as similarly stated by Nakkeeran et al.1

Postoperative defect size (in pixels) × 100/preoperative defect size − 100

Statistical analysis was performed on the obtained data.

Statistical Analysis

A commercially available software program for statistical and power analysis was utilized to assess the clinical and radiological data—Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 21. The Chi-squared test and independent t-test were utilized to conduct inferential statistics. The Independent t-test was employed to compare age, bone defect density, and residual defect size between groups. On the contrary, the Chi-squared test was used to compare the remaining categorical variables between groups. A statistical significance level of 0.05 was established.

Discussion

Bone possesses the inherent ability to regenerate, whether it is in response to injury, during skeletal development, or as part of continuous remodeling throughout adult life. Regeneration is the result of a precise and coordinated biological mechanism that comprises a series of bone induction and conduction events. This intricate process involves several cell types, as well as intracellular and extracellular signaling pathways that follow a well-defined temporal and spatial sequence. Ultimately, the goal of regeneration is to facilitate optimal skeletal repair and the restoration of skeletal function.7

Repair potential is meager in larger defects thus bone requires grafting materials to stimulate its repair. A coordinated process of tissue regeneration replicates a cascade of reparative occurrences that may lead to the formation of a unified tissue structure.8

Bone grafting substances encompass autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic bone substitutes. Autologous bone grafts are widely regarded as the benchmark, but have certain stumbling blocks like their limited availability, infection and therefore alloplastic grafts, made from HA, may be a more acceptable substitute due to ideal specifications like osteoconduction, hardness, and acceptability by bone.9 TCP is also in use, especially in combination with HA displaying further ideal requirements like osteoconductivity and restorability.9 This study involved the utilization of a bioactive material consisting of nanocrystalline HA and β-TCP bone graft to repair and regenerate bone in areas with bony defects.

Kojima et al. reported that the utilization of HA/β-TCP composite biomaterials resulted in enhanced formation of cortical bone compared to other grafting biomaterials. This improved outcome can be attributed to the quick reabsorption of β-TCP, which facilitates superior vascularization and stimulates the formation of medullary bone.10 Trisi et al. have said that β-TCP has a purity phase of 99% making it entirely resorbable with concurrent new bone formation within a period of 6–12 months.11 Lu et al. further stated that the innate bioactivity of β-TCP enables its porous structure to function as carriers for proosteogenic growth factors, resulting in favorable release kinetics and minimized side effects.12

For regeneration, whole blood withdrawn from the body can be utilized to its full extent when it is centrifuged thereby obtaining a significant abundance of platelets with a reduced volume of plasma for various processes in dental surgery which determines the quality of healing. After this, active proteins from α-granules result in injury to tissues which in turn liberates cytokines and growth factors and stimulates the proliferation of cells.13

Platelet centrifuged blood products are important in releasing about 90% of growth factors (most commonly PDGF and TGF-β) which participate in collagen synthesis providing a scaffold for migration of osteoblasts.13

Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) promotes the synthesis of fibronectin, a protein that adheres cells together and plays a significant role in cell growth and movement during the healing process. This protein is crucial for the replication and differentiation of essential stem cells that support fibroblasts and endothelial cells, which are responsible for osteoconduction, hyaluronic acid production, wound contraction, and tissue remodeling.14

On the contrary, TGF-β induces fibroblast chemotaxis and stimulates the production of collagen and fibronectin in cells. Simultaneously, it restrains the degradation of collagen by reducing proteases and enhancing protease inhibitors. Collectively, these effects promote fibrogenesis. Additionally, research conducted in laboratory settings and living organisms has demonstrated that TGF stimulates the growth of mesenchymal stem cells and osteoblasts, thereby facilitating bone regeneration.15

The autologous platelet products have high remedial potential and therefore can be used in various preparations and various disciplines of medicine and tissue engineering.16

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has demonstrated its ability to accelerate both the speed and magnitude of fresh bone development. Comprising 4% RBC, 95% platelets, and 1% white blood cells, this solution has proven benefits in facilitating bone formation.17 The level of platelets and, consequently, the level of growth factors can exceed the normal concentration by 5–10 times. It is through platelets that PRP has its main mechanism which besides homeostasis also releases factors that promote angiogenesis and tissue repair.16 PRP also suppresses the cytokine liberation that limits swelling and interaction with macrophages to promote tissue healing by 2–3 times more than in normal situations and epithelialization.18 PRP possesses antimicrobial properties that are efficacious against a range of pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Cryptococcus neoformans, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, and Escherichia coli. As given by Choi et al. the credit for this activity can be attributed to a group of platelet microbicidal peptides, which are cationic peptides exhibiting microbicidal properties and are synthesized by platelets.19

Choukroun et al. in 2001 developed and introduced a novel simplified platelet gel therapeutic concept known as PRF, devoid of artificial biochemical modifications in the processing to accelerate soft and hard tissue healing.20 It serves as a self-contained source of growth factors, collecting platelets and cytokines within a natural fibrin clot and concentrating 97% of platelets and over 50% of leukocytes in a distinct three-dimensional arrangement.21

The fibrin matrix, which provides support, is the primary factor determining the actual therapeutic potential of PRF, despite the significant role played by platelets and leukocyte cytokines in its biology.20 PRF undergoes a single phase of centrifugation and is prepared without the use of any anticoagulants to eliminate the potential hazards linked with the utilization of bovine thrombin.22

What separates PRP and PRF is that the latter has circulating immune cells and antigenic plasmatic molecules encompassed within it making it wholly specific to the donor and it also has a slow gelling process discerning it from other plasma derivatives as well, as it modifies the mechanical and biological characteristics of the fibrin matrix.21,22 The efficacy of this method relies on the prompt acquisition of the blood specimen and its subsequent transfer to the centrifuge.23

Saluja et al.23 and Toffler et al.24 reported that when PRF was used concomitantly with bone grafts it offered several benefits such as bone growth, fostered wound healing, wound healing, graft stabilization, hemostasis, and improved the handling properties of graft materials.

In the current investigation, each of the groups had postoperative healing that was uneventful with a similar healing pattern. During the 4-month recall period, all sites exhibited complete closure and healing of the soft tissue with no observations of bone graft granule loss or sequestration. These findings align with the research conducted by Kavitha et al.25 and Strauss et al.26 According to Prasanthi et al.,27 fibrin bandage and growth factor release contribute to wound closure and mucosal healing by PRF.

The research conducted by Triveni et al.,28 Goyal,29 Pinipe et al.,30 and Stumbras et al.31 align with the findings that the healing of soft tissues using PRP and PRF was favorable.

The prolonged period during which growth factors are released is thought to be responsible for the positive outcomes observed when employing PRF + β-TCP instead of PRP + β-TCP in the long-term. Dohan et al.32 demonstrated that PRF exhibits a delayed release of growth factors compared to PRP and showed enhanced therapeutic capabilities in terms of healing properties.

Doiphode et al.33 and Singha et al.34 stated that PRP and PRF hold the potential to serve as supplementary aids in improving the wound healing procedure and the rejuvenation of bone tissue. The radiographic results of the present study suggested both PRP + β-TCP and PRF + β-TCP exhibit equal efficacy in facilitating bone regeneration.

However, Schlegel et al.35 suggested that only early aspects of bone regeneration were mediated using PRP and therefore its long-term potency remains questionable and expected advantages, moderate. Alternatives to the use of platelet concentrates to bring about therapeutic relief were sought out and that repose was brought about through the studies of Yelamali and Saikrishna36 and Bhujbal et al.37 who stated that PRF greatly enhances soft tissue healing significantly as well as produces a faster regeneration of bone, compared to PRP.

The findings of this study aligned with the research conducted by Doiphode et al.33 as well as research by Kiran et al.38 and Thanikasalam et al.39 who convey that PRF facilitates optimal soft tissue healing and additionally accelerates bone regeneration because of simpler preparation protocols over PRP and the ability of PRF to release growth factors in a controlled and prolonged manner. Wu et al.40 reported that PRF effectively enhances the attachment and proliferation of osteoblasts, while also boosting the production of collagen-related proteins thus, effectively, and successfully promoting bone regeneration and repair.

The study's results also indicated that there were no antigenic or unintended reactions or tissue responses observed throughout the research, thereby demonstrating the safety of PRP and PRF when combined with nano-HA material.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the present study, it can be concluded that:

During the duration of the study, the periapical tissues exhibited good tolerance toward the combination of platelet aggregate PRP and PRF with nano HA + β-TCP bone graft material.

Based on the results, observations, and statistical analysis, PRF with nano HA + β-TCP bone graft showed slightly faster and denser bone formation in bony defects in children as compared to PRP with nano-HA + β-TCP bone graft.

Clinical Significance

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and PRF prepared by separation and concentration of platelets is an autologous, safe, simple, and comprehensible source of growth factors that can be recommended as a novel biological tool for regeneration of bone and rapid soft tissue healing in the field of pediatric dentistry.
