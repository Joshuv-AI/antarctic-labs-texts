Transient reset of a ventricular leadless pacemaker after monopolar pulsed field ablation: A case report with ex vivo validation
Authors: Daiki Kumazawa, Masato Munehisa, Yohei Kikuchi, Yosuke Mizuno, Kosuke Onodera, Kennosuke Yamashita
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a novel nonthermal ablation modality for atrial fibrillation (AF) and is rapidly gaining clinical adoption. Among the PFA systems currently available, the Affera system is characterized by its monopolar energy delivery. The lattice-tip catheter (Sphere-9™, Affera™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) can deliver both pulsed field and radiofrequency (RF) energy.1 Unlike bipolar PFA, monopolar PFA delivers energy from the catheter to a dispersive surface electrode, resulting in a broader current pathway that raises concerns regarding interactions with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs).

Patients with CIEDs have been largely excluded from clinical trials, and evidence regarding the safety of PFA in this population remains limited. In bipolar PFA systems, transient sensing disturbances owing to electromagnetic interference have been reported, although the procedure is generally considered safe.2, 3, 4 In contrast, monopolar PFA has been associated with more significant device interactions, including irreversible dysfunction in implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and software resets in leadless pacemakers.5 Although previous reports have described device reset and transient threshold elevation in atrial leadless pacemakers (Aveir™ AR, Abbott, Chicago, IL) after PFA,5,6 no report has evaluated the effects of monopolar PFA on ventricular leadless pacemakers (Aveir VR, Abbott).

We report a case of transient device dysfunction, including read-only memory (ROM) mode transition and reversible battery parameter deterioration, after AF ablation using a lattice-tip monopolar PFA system in a patient with an Aveir VR, supported by ex vivo observations.

Case report

A 78-year-old man (height 163.9 cm, weight 57.5 kg, body mass index 21.4 kg/m2, without kyphosis or scoliosis) with tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome had previously undergone implantation of an Aveir VR leadless ventricular pacemaker. The device had been implanted in the apical septum of the right ventricle (RV) on August 19, 2024 (Figure 1A and 1B). Paroxysmal AF had been documented since 2011, but the patient had declined catheter ablation at that time; because of symptomatic sick sinus syndrome, leadless pacemaker implantation was prioritized. The patient was not pacemaker dependent; his underlying rhythm was sinus rhythm with intermittent sinus arrest, and the device was programmed to VVI 50 ppm as a backup. Subsequently, palpitations attributable to paroxysmal AF worsened and remained refractory to antiarrhythmic drug therapy, prompting the decision to perform AF ablation, which was undertaken on April 2, 2026, 591 days after pacemaker implantation. The ablation procedure was performed using a lattice-tip catheter (Sphere-9, Affera, Medtronic) with an integrated mapping system. 4 dispersive (patient return) electrodes (Valleylab™ Polyhesive™ electrode, Medtronic) were positioned on the patient’s back, arranged bilaterally on either side of the spine in upper and lower positions; the upper electrodes were placed at the level of the shoulders, and the lower margin extended to approximately the level of the intercristal (Jacoby) line. The procedure was conducted under general anesthesia (midazolam, propofol, and rocuronium) and guided by intracardiac echocardiography without fluoroscopy. No esophageal temperature probe was used. Activated clotting time was maintained between 350 and 400 seconds. Mapping and ablation were performed via a single transseptal approach. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) and posterior wall isolation were achieved using monopolar PFA with a total of 149 applications, whereas cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation was performed using RF energy with 7 applications (Figure 1C and 1D). The minimum distance between the Sphere-9 ablation catheter and the Aveir VR on the Affera mapping system was approximately 30 mm during left atrial PFA applications. No device interference was observed during RF ablation of the CTI under intracardiac echocardiography guidance.

Although the procedure itself was uneventful, immediate postprocedural device interrogation in the electrophysiology laboratory revealed a system reset with transition to ROM mode, accompanied by apparent battery depletion. Manufacturer-reported battery status relative to the replacement recommended time decreased from 97% to 50%, estimated longevity from 19.6 to 12.5 years, and battery voltage from 3.1 to 2.8 V. In contrast, pacing threshold (1.0 V at 0.4 ms) and sensing amplitude (11.0 mV) remained unchanged (Table 1). Surface electrocardiogram and continuous telemetry monitoring showed no loss of capture, no clinically significant bradycardia, and no hemodynamic instability. At 24-hour follow-up interrogation, device parameters demonstrated recovery of battery status to 96%, with stable pacing threshold and sensing. No reprogramming or emergency intervention was required. The patient was discharged without clinical complications. Subsequent follow-up at 1 month confirmed continued stable device parameters.

Ex vivo experiment

To investigate the mechanism of this phenomenon, we conducted an ex vivo experiment using a freshly excised porcine heart model (Figure 2A and 2B). The porcine heart was placed in a saline bath (0.9% sodium chloride, approximately 5 L, at a water temperature of 35°C–37°C) to approximate blood conductivity. A dispersive return electrode was positioned on the posterior surface of the saline bath container. An Aveir VR was positioned at the RV septum. A Micra™ AV (Medtronic) was similarly implanted in the RV as a comparator device. The Sphere-9 catheter was used for both PFA and RF applications, with the same clinical settings as the index procedure. The distance between the catheter tip and each device was measured for each application site: approximately 30 mm for right atrial (RA) applications, 20 mm for CTI applications, and 5 mm for close-proximity applications in the RV. RF applications delivered to the RA, CTI, and RV did not affect Aveir VR function. Similarly, monopolar PFA delivered to the RA, CTI, and RV did not induce any device abnormalities. In contrast, repeated monopolar PFA applications delivered either on the CTI or in the RV in close proximity to the Aveir VR induced device dysfunction. At each of these sites, after 10 consecutive applications, a system reset was observed on programmer interrogation (Abbott™ Merlin programmer), followed by spontaneous recovery within minutes; after 20 consecutive applications, the device transitioned to ROM mode. Importantly, when a 30-second interval was introduced after 10 consecutive applications, followed by an additional 10 applications at the same site, the device did not transition to ROM mode. These experiments were repeated 3 times with consistent results. Notably, no comparable phenomena were observed with the Micra AV under any application conditions, including CTI and RV monopolar PFA.

Discussion

This case suggests that monopolar PFA can induce transient functional changes in a ventricular leadless pacemaker (Aveir VR). Although a system reset and apparent battery depletion were observed immediately after the procedure, both parameters recovered within 24 hours, indicating a reversible phenomenon likely representing a power-on reset (PoR) event.

PoR is a device reboot phenomenon triggered by strong electromagnetic interference or transient voltage fluctuations. In monopolar PFA, electrical energy is delivered from the catheter to a dispersive surface electrode, resulting in a wide current pathway that may traverse intracardiac devices. In the present case, although the PFA was performed in the left atrium, the monopolar current pathway between the catheter and the posterior dispersive electrode may have included the RV septum where the Aveir VR was located. It is plausible that cumulative electrical stress from 149 monopolar PFA applications, despite the distance from the device, contributed to the PoR event.

Our ex vivo experiment demonstrated the susceptibility of the Aveir VR to reset under specific monopolar PFA conditions, supporting the biological plausibility of the observed clinical phenomenon rather than reproducing the exact clinical scenario. In the ex vivo model, device dysfunction occurred when monopolar PFA was delivered either close to the device in the RV or on the CTI, whereas RA applications did not cause abnormalities. The clinical case, in which ablation was performed in the left atrium at a greater distance from the device, suggests that factors beyond simple spatial proximity—such as cumulative energy exposure, temporal clustering of applications, and the specific monopolar current vector through the body—may also contribute to device interaction. The finding that introducing a 30-second interval between application sequences prevented ROM mode transition raises the possibility that temporal spacing of energy delivery may mitigate this risk.

In this case, the targeted arrhythmia was paroxysmal AF, and posterior wall isolation was added empirically to PVI, whereas CTI ablation was performed using RF energy. Although PVI alone is often sufficient for paroxysmal AF, empirical posterior wall isolation increased the total number of monopolar PFA applications to 149. Because neither the clinical case nor the ex vivo model demonstrated device abnormalities with RF energy, the CTI ablation is unlikely to have contributed to the device interaction; rather, the additional monopolar PFA applications required for posterior wall isolation may have increased cumulative electrical stress on the device. This interpretation is consistent with our ex vivo observation that device reset was related to the cumulative number and temporal clustering of monopolar PFA applications. Accordingly, limiting empirical lesion sets beyond PVI and minimizing the total number and temporal clustering of monopolar PFA applications, when clinically appropriate, may help reduce the risk of device interaction in patients with leadless pacemakers.

The absence of comparable abnormalities in the Micra AV under the same experimental conditions suggests potential differences in device susceptibility to electrical stress. This may relate to differences in device architecture, including housing material, electronic shielding, firmware design, or battery chemistry, although the exact mechanism remains to be elucidated.

Previous reports have described irreversible device malfunction in implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and transient software resets in atrial leadless pacemakers (Aveir AR) associated with monopolar PFA.5,6 PoR-related behavior, accompanied by transient decreases in battery voltage with subsequent spontaneous recovery, has also been reported after cardioversion.7 The ROM mode observed in the present case is consistent with such PoR-related behavior.

From a clinical perspective, device interrogation is essential both immediately and at 24 hours after PFA in patients with leadless pacemakers. Transient reductions in battery voltage immediately after the procedure should be interpreted with caution, given that they may not indicate permanent device damage. We suggest that clinicians consider the following when performing monopolar PFA in patients with an Aveir VR: (1) programming to a nontracking, asynchronous pacing mode if feasible; (2) prompt postprocedural device interrogation with repeat assessment at 24 hours; and (3) awareness that battery parameter deterioration may be transient and recoverable.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single case report, and the findings may not be generalizable to all patients or clinical scenarios. Second, the exact intracardiac current pathway during monopolar PFA was not measured, and the mechanism by which left atrial ablation caused device dysfunction in the RV remains speculative. Third, the ex vivo model, while supporting biological plausibility, did not replicate the exact clinical scenario; the ex vivo observations involved close-proximity RV applications rather than left atrial ablation at a greater distance. Fourth, only 2 leadless pacemaker models were tested, and the susceptibility of other CIEDs to monopolar PFA remains unknown. Finally, although the device parameters remained stable at 1-month follow-up, longer-term effects on device function and battery longevity cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This is the first reported case of transient device reset and battery voltage reduction after monopolar PFA in a patient with an Aveir VR leadless pacemaker. The observed phenomena were reversible and likely reflect a transient PoR response to electrical stress. Our ex vivo observations support the biological plausibility of this interaction and suggest that spatial proximity, cumulative energy exposure, and temporal clustering of applications may be contributing factors. Careful evaluation of device function after monopolar PFA is essential, and early postprocedural reductions in battery voltage should be interpreted with caution given the possibility of transient changes. These findings also raise the possibility that susceptibility to PFA-induced electrical stress may differ across leadless pacemaker platforms.
