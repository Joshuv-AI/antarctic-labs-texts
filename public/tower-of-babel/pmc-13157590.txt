Biomolecular Corona Remodelling of Aspergillus fumigatus Extracellular Vesicles Shapes Immune Recognition and Macrophage Activation Programs
Authors: Lucas Fabrício Bahia Nogueira, Bianca T. M. de Oliveira, Renan E. A. Piraine, Lucas de Faria Nogueira, Júlia L. Froldi, Henrique T. de Oliveira, Larwsk Hayann, Luiz H. S. Andrilli, Lucas A. Tavares, Pietro Ciancaglini, Fausto Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) released by Aspergillus fumigatus play key roles in fungal–host communication. Similar to synthetic nanoparticles, fungal EVs acquire a biomolecular corona composed of a tightly bound “hard” corona layer of fungal proteins and a dynamic “soft” layer of host‐derived molecules adsorbed upon contact with biological fluids. Although the corona is thought to modulate EV identity and immunological outcomes, its role in fungal EV–immune cell interactions remains poorly defined. Here, we investigated how corona removal and reconstitution alter the biophysical properties and immunomodulatory function of A. fumigatus EVs. Corona removal reduced EV‐associated protein content and impaired EV‐macrophage interaction, whereas serum exposure promoted secondary corona formation and markedly increased EV association with AMJ2‐C11 macrophages. Native and serum‐coated EVs induced robust secretion of both pro‐inflammatory (IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IFN‐γ) and anti‐inflammatory (IL‐4, IL‐10) cytokines, whereas stripped EVs elicited a markedly attenuated response. At the transcriptional level, serum‐coated EVs upregulated genes involved in innate recognition and signalling pathways (Tlr2, Tlr4, Dectin1, DC‐Sign and Nf‐κb) as well as macrophage activation programs (Nos2, Arg1, Chi3l3, Retnla and Tgfb1). These findings establish the biomolecular corona as a key determinant of fungal EV immunomodulatory activity. Beyond advancing our understanding of fungal EV biology, this work highlights corona engineering as a potential strategy to reprogram EV–host interactions, with implications for antifungal therapeutic development.

Introduction

Fungal infections represent a significant global health burden, affecting more than 1.5 billion individuals and causing approximately 1.6 million deaths annually (Brown et al. 2012; Almeida et al. 2019). Among human fungal pathogens, Aspergillus fumigatus is the leading cause of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, a disease that predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals and is associated with mortality rates as high as 40%–60% despite antifungal therapy (Brown et al. 2012; Almeida et al. 2019; Freitas et al. 2023). Recent evolutionary analyses further highlight that, within section Fumigati, pathogenicity has emerged multiple times independently, with A. fumigatus standing out as the most clinically relevant species despite several closely related taxa being non‐pathogenic or rarely implicated in disease (Rokas et al. 2020). The emergence of triazole resistance and the protective role of biofilms further complicate therapy, underscoring the need for new therapeutic (Freitas et al. 2023). Beyond drug resistance, A. fumigatus employs multiple immune‐evasion strategies, including masking of cell‐wall PAMPs, secretion of immunomodulatory polysaccharides such as GAG, and dynamic remodelling during germination and hyphal growth, which collectively hinder efficient host recognition (van de Veerdonk et al. 2017).

In this context, extracellular vesicles (EVs) represent an important class of secreted factors capable of shaping these interactions (van de Veerdonk et al. 2017). EVs have emerged as central mediators of fungal biology and host–pathogen communication (Bielska and May 2019; Rizzo et al. 2020; Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021; Bitencourt et al. 2022a). Defined by the MISEV2023 guidelines as lipid bilayer‐enclosed particles incapable of autonomous replication, EVs transport a diverse cargo of proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides across the fungal cell wall (Bielska and May 2019; Rizzo et al. 2020; Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021; Bitencourt et al. 2022a; Welsh et al. 2024). The first evidence that fungal cells could produce and secrete EVs across their rigid cell wall was reported in Cryptococcus neoformans, where vesicles containing polysaccharides and lipids contributed to capsule assembly and virulence (Rodrigues et al. 2007). This seminal discovery opened a new research field in fungal biology, later extended to other pathogenic fungi, including A. fumigatus (Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; Meng et al. 2024b), Paracoccidioides brasiliensis (da Silva et al. 2016), and Candida species (Bielska and May 2019; Rizzo et al. 2020; Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021; Bitencourt et al. 2022a, 2022b). Once thought to be mere carriers of cellular waste, fungal EVs are now recognised as versatile communication platforms that modulate biofilm formation, antifungal resistance, and host immune responses (da Silva et al. 2016; Bielska and May 2019; Brauer et al. 2020; Rizzo et al. 2020; Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021; Bitencourt et al. 2022a, 2022b). This aspect is particularly relevant given that pathogenicity in A. fumigatus does not simply reflect ecological exposure, but rather the accumulation of species‐specific traits associated with host adaptation and survival (Rokas et al. 2020). Indeed, EVs from A. fumigatus and other pathogenic fungi can influence cytokine production, macrophage activation programs, and pathogen clearance (da Silva et al. 2016; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023), and were also shown to modulate neutrophil activity and immune responses in ocular infection models (Shopova et al. 2020; Meng et al. 2024b). These findings highlight their dual role as both virulence factors and potential therapeutic targets (Bielska and May 2019; Rizzo et al. 2020; Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021; Bitencourt et al. 2022a).

A critical yet underexplored aspect of EV biology is the acquisition of a biomolecular corona (Singh et al. 2020, 2023; Tóth et al. 2021; Wolf et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). Similar to synthetic nanoparticles, EVs exposed to biological fluids become coated with adsorbed proteins and other macromolecules, creating a dynamic interface that profoundly alters their biological identity (Tóth et al. 2021; Ramos et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). This corona is typically described as compromising a tightly bound “hard” layer of vesicle‐associated proteins and a dynamic “soft” layer enriched in host‐derived molecules that rapidly exchange with the surrounding environment (Wolf et al. 2012; Tóth et al. 2021; Ramos et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). Evidence from mammalian EVs demonstrates that corona composition directly dictates biodistribution, stability, cellular uptake, and immune recognition (Tóth et al. 2021; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). However, the functional impact of the corona on fungal EV‐host interactions remains largely unexplored (Wolf et al. 2012; Tóth et al. 2021; Ramos et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). Although studies in mammalian EVs have rapidly expanded in recent years (Ramos et al. 2022; Liam‐Or et al. 2024), revealing how corona composition redirects vesicle biodistribution and immune recognition, no functional investigations have examined whether similar mechanisms operate in fungal EVs.

This knowledge gap is particularly relevant in pulmonary aspergillosis, where EVs encounter alveolar surfactant and serum proteins immediately after secretion, potentially reshaping their surface identity and immunological fate (Wolf et al. 2012; Ramos et al. 2022; Freitas et al. 2023). Given that the lung is a protein‐rich environment, corona formation on fungal EVs is not only expected but likely rapid and dynamic, immediately altering vesicle identity upon entry into the airway microenvironment (Wolf et al. 2012; Ramos et al. 2022; Freitas et al. 2023). Whether the corona masks fungal epitopes to promote immune evasion or exposes new ones that trigger inflammatory responses is unknown. Thus, dissecting the role of the biomolecular corona in fungal EV biology is essential to clarify how these vesicles influence infection outcomes (Wolf et al. 2012; Ramos et al. 2022).

Here, we provide functional evidence that the biomolecular corona is a key regulator of fungal EV–immune cell interactions. By removing the corona from A. fumigatus EVs and assessing their physicochemical properties and biological activity, we demonstrate that corona stripping significantly alters vesicle size, surface charge, and protein composition, resulting in reduced EV‐macrophage interaction, distinct cytokine secretion patterns, and altered macrophage activation programs. These findings establish the biomolecular corona as a novel regulatory layer in fungal pathogenesis and open the way for corona engineering as a potential antifungal strategy (Wolf et al. 2012; Ramos et al. 2022; Liam‐Or et al. 2024).

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Buffers

Unless otherwise stated, analytical‐grade reagents were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. RPMI‐1640, heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin/streptomycin were purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vybrant DiI Cell‐Labelling Solution, TRIzol Reagent, and the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit were from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Micro‐BCA Protein Assay Kit and the Pierce Silver Stain Kit were from Thermo Fisher. Laemmli Sample Buffer, 10% polyacrylamide gels, and the ChemiDoc MP system were from Bio‐Rad. Mouse ELISA kits (IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐4 and IL‐10) were obtained from BD Biosciences (BD OptEIA ELISA Sets). The Aspergillus trace element stock solution (1000×) contained, per litre: 10 g FeSO4·7H2O, 4.4 g ZnSO4·7H2O, 1.1 g MnCl2·4H2O, 0.32 g CuSO4·5H2O, 0.15 g Na2B4O7·10H2O, and 5 g Na2MoO4·2H2O, as described in the standard A. fumigatus EV isolation protocol. Phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was prepared by dissolving 8.0 g NaCl, 0.2 g KCl, 1.44 g Na2HPO4, and 0.24 g KH2PO4 in 1 L of ultrapure water, adjusting the pH to 7.4 with NaOH or HCl, and sterilizing through a 0.22 µm membrane filter. PBS was stored at 4°C and used for EV washing and resuspension throughout the study. All solutions were prepared using ultrapure Milli‐Q water and handled under sterile conditions.

Fungal Strain, Media, and Culture Conditions

A. fumigatus CEA17 was maintained on YAG agar containing 2% (w/v) glucose, 0.2% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) malt extract, 2% (w/v) agar, and 1% (v/v) trace elements. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 5 days (Brauer et al. 2023; Freitas et al. 2023). Conidia were harvested by gently washing colony surfaces with PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween‐20, filtered through Miracloth (MilliporeSigma) to remove hyphal fragments, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS. Conidial concentration was determined using a Neubauer counting chamber under a bright‐field optical microscope (Zeiss), ensuring accurate quantification prior to inoculation. All steps were performed without vortexing to avoid disrupting conidial integrity.

For EV production, 2 × 105 conidia/mL were inoculated into YAG or YG broth supplemented with 1% (v/v) trace elements and incubated for 72 h at 37°C, 150–180 rpm, in baffled flasks filled to one‐third their volume to ensure adequate oxygenation (Meng et al. 2024b). All cultures were handled on ice prior to EV isolation to limit proteolysis.

Isolation of EVs

EVs were isolated following established protocols for fungal vesicle purification (da Silva et al. 2016; Bitencourt et al. 2022a, 2022b; Meng et al. 2024a) Culture supernatants were first obtained by gentle filtration through Miracloth (MilliporeSigma) to remove bulk mycelia. The filtrate underwent differential centrifugation, an essential clarification step to remove progressively smaller contaminants before ultrafiltration. Samples were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove intact cells, large hyphal fragments, and heavy debris, followed by 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to eliminate smaller particulates, apoptotic bodies, and large non‐vesicular structures. This two‐step clarification minimizes membrane fouling and improves downstream vesicle recovery. The resulting supernatant was transferred to an Amicon stirred cell (Merck Millipore) equipped with a 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane and concentrated at 4°C under gentle nitrogen (N2) pressure, reducing sample volume while retaining EVs. The concentrated material was then filtered through a 0.45 µm PES syringe filter to remove residual larger particles while minimizing vesicle adsorption. Filtered samples were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C using an Optima XPN‐80 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). Pellets were gently washed with cold PBS and re‐pelleted under identical ultracentrifugation conditions to eliminate soluble protein contaminants. Final vesicle pellets were resuspended in 200 µL sterile PBS. Fresh EV preparations were used immediately or stored at –80°C for subsequent analyses.

Biomolecular Corona Removal

To remove the loosely bound (soft) biomolecular corona, freshly isolated native EVs (nEVs) were resuspended in sterile PBS and incubated with 0.5 M NaCl for 1 h at 37°C under gentle agitation, following other protocols already reported in the literature (Ramos et al. 2022; Cominal et al. 2025). High‐ionic‐strength exposure promotes the dissociation of weakly adsorbed proteins from the EV surface, enabling separation of surface‐associated components from those stably integrated during vesicle biogenesis (Ramos et al. 2022; Cominal et al. 2025). Following incubation, samples were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C using an Optima XPN‐80 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). The supernatant, which contains proteins detached from the vesicle surface, was carefully collected for downstream analysis. The resulting pellet, corresponding to shaved EVs (sEVs), was washed twice in PBS to remove residual unbound proteins and resuspended in the original volume of sterile PBS.

Reconstitution of a Serum‐Derived Secondary Corona

To reconstitute a secondary biomolecular corona originating from biological fluids, nEVs were incubated with 10% (v/v) heat‐inactivated FBS for 1 h at 37°C under gentle agitation. This procedure promotes the adsorption of serum‐derived proteins onto the vesicle surface, generating corona‐coated EVs (cEVs). After incubation, vesicles were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C to remove non‐bound or weakly interacting proteins, followed by a PBS wash under identical ultracentrifugation conditions. Final cEV pellets were gently resuspended in sterile PBS for subsequent physicochemical and functional analyses.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

Size distribution and particle concentration of nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs were determined using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical) equipped with a 488 nm laser. Samples were diluted at a 1:100 ratio in sterile PBS to achieve 20–60 particles per frame, and all measurements were performed at 25°C. For each condition, following the manufacturer's instructions, five 60‐s videos were recorded (camera level 12; syringe pump speed 50) and software parameters were fine‐tuned to ensure optimal visualization and accurate tracking of vesicles (Bitencourt et al. 2018; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023). Data were analysed using NTA software version 3.4, applying standard detection thresholds according to MISEV2023 recommendations (Welsh et al. 2024). Results of NTA were plotted using OriginPro 2024 software (OriginLab).

Zeta Potential (ζ)

Surface charge measurements were obtained using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical) equipped with Zetasizer software v. 7.1 (Malvern), employing the dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique (Sebinelli et al. 2019). EVs were diluted 1:100 in low‐conductivity PBS and equilibrated for 2 min at 25°C before analysis in a disposable DTS1070 cuvette, with a refractive index set to 1.33 (Sebinelli et al. 2019). Zeta potential values were calculated automatically by the instrument software from electrophoretic mobility using the Smoluchowski approximation (Sebinelli et al. 2019). Each sample was measured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, with 10–100 runs per measurement.

Protein Quantification

Total EV‐associated protein was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions (Cleare et al. 2020). Samples and standards were incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a SpectraMax 190 Microplate Reader spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices). Protein values were used for normalization across downstream assay.

SDS‐PAGE and Silver Staining

Protein profiles of nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs were evaluated by SDS‐PAGE followed by silver staining (Cominal et al. 2025). EV samples (20 µg total protein) were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer, heated at 95°C for 5 min, and loaded onto 10% (w/v) polyacrylamide gels. Electrophoresis was performed at 100 V for 30 min, followed by 150 V for 1 h. Gels were fixed overnight in a solution containing 50% (v/v) methanol, 10% (v/v) acetic acid, and 50 µL formaldehyde, washed sequentially in 50% (v/v) ethanol, and sensitised with 0.02% (w/v) sodium thiosulfate for 1 min. After rinsing, gels were incubated in 0.2% (w/v) silver nitrate for 30 min, and band development was performed using a freshly prepared solution containing 6 g sodium carbonate, 2 mL sodium thiosulfate, and 50 µL formaldehyde (Cominal et al. 2025). The reaction was stopped with 5% (v/v) acetic acid, and gels were imaged on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad).

Dot Blot Analysis of EV and Corona‐Associated Markers

Dot blot assays were performed to assess the relative presence of EV‐ and corona‐associated proteins in nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs, as previously described for EV protein corona studies (Liam‐Or et al. 2024). EV suspensions were normalised to 5 × 1010 vesicles/mL, as determined by NTA, and 50 µL of each sample were spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio‐Rad) and air‐dried, ensuring vesicle‐based normalization across all experimental conditions. The same sample volume (50 µL) was applied to the membrane for control conditions, with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA), and EV‐depleted fetal bovine serum (EV‐depleted FBS) spotted in distinct quadrants of the same membrane. Fungal EV preparations served as positive controls for EV‐associated markers, whereas PBS and BSA were included as negative and specificity controls to evaluate non‐specific antibody binding and protein‐derived background signals. EV‐depleted FBS was included as a procedural control for serum‐derived proteins and corresponded to the same serum preparation used for secondary corona formation of cEVs. This experimental design allowed direct comparison of EV samples and controls under identical incubation, washing, and detection conditions. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 3% (w/v) skim milk in TBS‐T (20 mM Tris‐HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% v/v Tween‐20, pH 7.4), followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against HSP70 (mouse monoclonal, clone BRM‐22, Sigma–Aldrich), GAPDH (rabbit polyclonal, Sigma–Aldrich), ALIX (goat polyclonal, clone N‐20, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), CD63 (rabbit polyclonal, clone H‐193, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and bovine serum albumin (rabbit polyclonal, Sigma–Aldrich), diluted according to the manufacturers’ recommendations (typically 1:1000 in blocking buffer) (Liam‐Or et al. 2024). HSP70, ALIX, and GAPDH were evaluated as EV‐intrinsic markers, whereas CD63 and albumin were assessed as host‐derived corona components. After three 5‐min washes with TBS‐T, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent detection was carried out using SuperSignal West Femto (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and signals were acquired on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad) (Liam‐Or et al. 2024).

A complete list of antibodies, including target proteins, clone or polyclonal nature, host species, reported species reactivity, vendor, catalogue number, RRID (when available), working dilution, incubation conditions, and validation references, is provided in Table S1, in accordance with the MISEV2023 guidelines. Given that the primary aim of this analysis was the comparative assessment of EV surface identity and corona remodeling rather than molecular‐weight discrimination, dot blot analysis was considered appropriate for the objectives of this study.

Cryogenic Transmission Electron Microscopy (Cryo‐TEM)

Morphological analysis of nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs was performed by cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo‐TEM) (Parra et al. 2024). Purified EV suspensions (101 1 vesicles/mL in Ca2 +/Mg2 +‐free PBS) were deposited (3–4 µL) onto Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 copper grids (300 mesh) previously glow‐discharged for 30 s at 15 mA using a Pelco easiGlow system (Zhang et al. 2019; de Souza Guimarães et al. 2022; Parra et al. 2024; Araujo et al. 2025). Vitrification was carried out in a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 4°C and 100% relative humidity, using a blot time of 3–4 s, blot force –2, and immediate plunge‐freezing into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen. Grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until imaging (Zhang et al. 2019; de Souza Guimarães et al. 2022; Parra et al. 2024; Araujo et al. 2025). Cryo‐TEM imaging was performed at the Brazilian Nanotechnology National Laboratory (LNNano/CNPEM, Campinas, Brazil) using a Talos Arctica G2 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 200 kV and equipped with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector (de Souza Guimarães et al. 2022; Araujo et al. 2025). Images were collected under low‐dose conditions using nominal magnification of 92,000× (0.8–1.5 Å pixel− 1), a total electron dose of 20–40 e− Å− 2, and defocus values ranging from –1.0 to –2.5 µm (de Souza Guimarães et al. 2022; Araujo et al. 2025). Automated data collection was performed using EPU software, selecting areas of thin vitreous ice containing well‐distributed vesicles (de Souza Guimarães et al. 2022; Araujo et al. 2025). Cryo‐TEM allowed direct visualization of EV morphology, membrane integrity, and structural differences among nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs (Parra et al. 2024).

AMJ2‐C11 Macrophage Culture

AMJ2‐C11 murine alveolar macrophages (ATCC CRL‐2456) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose; Sigma–Aldrich) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 2 mM L‐glutamine (Gibco), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. For subculturing, cells were detached using Trypsin‐EDTA (Sigma–Aldrich) and counted using an automated cell counter (Countess II FL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following trypan‐blue exclusion to ensure viability. Cells were used at passages ≤15 to avoid phenotypic drift. For EV stimulation assays, macrophages were seeded in 24‐well culture plates at 2 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Prior to stimulation, cells were washed twice with sterile PBS and maintained either in DMEM supplemented with 10% EV‐depleted FBS or in serum‐free DMEM containing 1% (v/v) Nutridoma‐SP (Roche). EV depletion of FBS was performed by ultrafiltration using Amicon Ultra‐15 centrifugal filter units (100 kDa MWCO; Merck Millipore) at 4°C, centrifuging at 4000 × g until the desired volume reduction was achieved. The EV‐depleted filtrate was immediately sterile‐filtered (0.22 µm) and stored at 4°C for no longer than 1 week. Before EV exposure, macrophages were equilibrated for 12–16 h in their respective media to stabilize basal immune signalling. EV preparations were normalised based on particle concentration determined by NTA, ensuring that equivalent numbers of vesicles were used across all experimental conditions.

Fluorescent Labelling of EVs and EV–Cell Association Assay

To assess EV‐macrophage association, nEVs, sEVs, and cEVs were fluorescently labelled using Vybrant DiI (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following manufacturer recommendations with optimizations for fungal EVs. Briefly, EV suspensions were adjusted to equal particle numbers (5 × 1010 particles/mL), mixed with DiI at a final concentration of 5 µM, and incubated for 20 min at 37°C protected from light.

Excess unbound dye was removed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4°C, followed by two additional washes with sterile PBS to eliminate remaining free fluorophore. Labelled EV pellets were resuspended in 100–200 µL of PBS, and particle numbers were re‐quantified by NTA prior to cell exposure.

AMJ2‐C11 macrophages were incubated with DiI‐labeled EVs under the indicated experimental conditions. Control samples consisting of macrophages incubated in the absence of EVs were included to determine background fluorescence. Fluorescence images were acquired using a widefield fluorescence microscope under identical acquisition settings across all conditions.

Quantitative image analysis was performed using FIJI/ImageJ software (version 1.54p). EV‐associated fluorescence was quantified by separating cell‐associated signal from extracellular fluorescence through image segmentation. Macrophage regions were defined based on bright‐field images and used to generate regions of interest (ROIs), allowing calculation of fluorescence intensity within cellular areas. Extracellular EV fluorescence was determined by detecting and quantifying DiI‐positive puncta (discrete fluorescent spots) outside cell ROIs after background subtraction. Both cell‐associated and extracellular measurements were obtained from the same image fields. Cell‐associated fluorescence was expressed as relative EV fluorescence normalised to control conditions, whereas extracellular EV fluorescence represents normalised absolute signal intensity per field. For each field, mean fluorescence values were extracted and used for downstream analysis. Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1).

Cytokine Production and Inflammatory Response

Cytokine secretion after EV stimulation was quantified using ELISA. AMJ2‐C11 macrophages were seeded in 48‐well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well. Cells were then stimulated with equal vesicle numbers of nEVs, sEVs, or cEVs (quantified by NTA) for 24 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. A positive control was included by stimulating cells with 1 µg/mL LPS (Sigma–Aldrich). Cytokines evaluated included key pro‐inflammatory mediators (IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IFN‐γ) and anti‐inflammatory mediators (IL‐4 and IL‐10), providing a broad profile of macrophage inflammatory responses to EV stimulation. After incubation, cell culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min). Cytokine production was quantified using commercial murine‐specific ELISA kits (BD OptEIA ELISA Sets) for IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐4, and IL‐10 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Detailed information for each ELISA kit, including catalogue number, lot number and supplier, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with wavelength correction at 570 nm using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated with recombinant murine cytokines provided in the kits. Detailed information on ELISA reagents, including manufacturer, catalogue number, lot number, and assay conditions, is provided in Table S2.

RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from AMJ2‐C11 macrophages stimulated with native EVs (nEVs), salt‐shaved EVs (sEVs), or serum‐corona EVs (cEVs) for 24 h using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Sigma‐Aldrich) to remove genomic DNA contamination, and RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nanophotometer (Implen). For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed using SyGreen Mix (PCR Biosystems) in 10 µL reactions containing 50 ng of cDNA, using the StepOne Plus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Gene expression was quantified using murine‐specific primers targeting pattern‐recognition receptors and macrophage activation‐associated genes, including Tlr2, Tlr4, Clec7a (Dectin1), Cd209 (DC‐Sign), Rela (NF‐κB p65), Nos2 (iNOS), Arg1, Chi3l3 (Ym1), Retnla (Fizz1) and Tgfb1. These genes were selected to represent pathogen‐recognition receptors, downstream inflammatory signalling pathways, and regulatory macrophage transcriptional programs potentially modulated by EV corona remodelling. Expression values were normalised to β‐actin (Actb), and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−#x00394;ΔCt method, as described previously (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 9 (Dotmatics). All reactions were performed in technical triplicates.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (Dotmatics). Each experiment was performed using at least three independent biological replicates, and each RT‐qPCR reaction was run in technical triplicates. Data distribution and variance homogeneity were evaluated prior to hypothesis testing. Comparisons among experimental groups were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons. For experiments evaluating EV‐induced responses, native EV‐treated cells (nEVs) were used as the reference condition to determine the effects of corona remodelling. For assays assessing macrophage activation relative to a maximal inflammatory stimulus, LPS+ Pam3CSK4 ‐stimulated cells were used as the reference control, as specified in the corresponding figure legends. For RT‐qPCR analyses, relative gene expression values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β‐actin (Actb) as the internal reference gene. For fluorescence microscopy quantification, multiple independent fields were analysed per condition in each biological replicate. Quantitative metrics (including cell‐associated fluorescence and extracellular fluorescence) were first obtained at the image level and subsequently averaged per biological replicate prior to statistical analysis, ensuring that each replicate represented an independent experimental unit. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05, with the following criteria used throughout: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****). No data points were excluded from the analyses unless technical failure was clearly identified prior to statistical testing.

Results

Physicochemical and Biochemical Remodelling After Corona Removal and Secondary‐Corona Acquisition

The physicochemical features of nEVs, sEVs and cEVs derived from A. fumigatus were compared. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) confirmed that all preparations contained particles within the expected size range for fungal EVs (Bitencourt et al. 2022a; Brauer et al. 2023; Freitas et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024). However, corona modulation markedly altered their distribution (Figure 1A,B). Removal of the primary corona by high‐ionic‐strength treatment led to a measurable shift in EV size distribution, with significant increases in Mean, D10, D50 and D90 values compared to nEVs, while the Mode remained unchanged, indicating a general expansion in hydrodynamic diameter rather than selective enlargement of a specific subpopulation. This remodelling became more pronounced after secondary corona acquisition in FBS, where cEVs exhibited the largest diameters across all parameters. These results are consistent with well‐established principles of biomolecular corona formation in nano‐bio interfaces, in which protein adsorption generates a thicker interfacial layer and alters colloidal behaviour (Wolf et al. 2022; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Cominal et al. 2025). These findings support the notion that corona formation is a dynamic remodelling process that redefines EV physicochemical identity and biological fate (Singh et al. 2020, Singh et al. 2023; Tóth et al. 2021; Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Liam‐Or et al. 2024).

Particle concentration significantly decreased after shaving and partially recovered following serum exposure (Figure 1C), indicating partial loss of detectable vesicles and recovery upon corona acquisition. Although some loss due to sample reprocessing is expected during sequential ultracentrifugation and buffer exchanges, the pronounced and reversible shift strongly supports a corona‐dependent effect on colloidal stability (Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025). Removal of adsorbed biomolecules likely increases interparticle interactions and aggregation‐driven sedimentation, reducing the number of vesicles within the analytical detection window. Conversely, serum‐derived proteins restore surface passivation and steric/electrostatic repulsion through secondary corona formation. Similar stability‐dependent changes in detectability have been reported for synthetic nanocarriers and fungal EVs exposed to biological fluids, underscoring the central role of the biomolecular corona in maintaining nanovesicle stability under physiological conditions (Cleare et al. 2020; Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025).

Consistent with this behaviour, zeta potential measurements revealed aligned surface remodelling. Shaving significantly increased the magnitude of the negative surface charge, likely reflecting enhanced exposure of membrane phospholipids and glycan residues once protein shielding is removed. In contrast, serum exposure shifted cEVs toward less‐negative values (Figure 1D,E), consistent with adsorption of serum proteins that screen surface charge and modify interfacial characteristics. These electrostatic shifts are known to influence nanoparticle stability and biological identity, potentially modulating EV recognition and uptake by host cells (Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Cominal et al. 2025; Musicò et al. 2025).

Biochemical profiling further corroborated corona‐dependent remodelling while confirming that EV identity was preserved throughout the treatments. Total protein content sharply decreased in sEVs as expected after removal of surface‐associated biomolecules (Cominal et al. 2025), whereas cEVs exhibited markedly increased protein abundance (Figure 1F), consistent with the acquisition of a new serum‐derived corona layer rather than restoration of native components (Dietz et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025). Silver‐stained SDS–PAGE confirmed depletion of loosely bound proteins in sEVs and the appearance of distinct serum‐associated bands in cEVs (Figure 1G), further demonstrating the formation of a secondary corona with a composition that differs from nEVs (Liam‐Or et al. 2024). Dot‐blot analysis showed conserved detection of fungal EV‐associated proteins including HSP70, ALIX and GAPDH across all conditions, indicating that corona manipulation did not compromise vesicle structure or cargo retention, in line with MISEV2023 recommendations for biochemical characterization of EVs (Rodrigues et al. 2007; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Welsh et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025). In contrast, Albumin was exclusively detected in cEVs, while PBS, 5% BSA, and EV‐depleted FBS controls confirmed antibody specificity and minimal non‐specific background. Notably, CD63, a canonical mammalian tetraspanin, was undetectable in all groups, consistent with its absence in fungal EVs and further confirming that the treatments did not introduce cross‐contamination from serum‐derived EVs. Altogether, these molecular signatures validate that high‐salt shaving efficiently strips native corona components while serum exposure rebuilds a distinct corona that reshapes EV detectability, surface charge and physicochemical identity under biologically relevant conditions.

Together, these data demonstrate that the presence and composition of the biomolecular corona are not ancillary features, but fundamental determinants of fungal EV physicochemical identity. By altering size, stability, surface charge, and accessible epitopes, corona modulation creates vesicles with distinct “biological fingerprints” that are expected to impact nanoscale structure and interface organization. Given that nanoscale morphology (including bilayer integrity, membrane contrast, and surface coating) critically governs EV interactions with biological systems (including uptake, immune recognition, and biodistribution) (Wolf et al. 2022; Dietz et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024), direct visualization by cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo‐TEM) was next employed to examine whether corona remodelling translates into detectable ultrastructural alterations.

EVs Preserve Morphology After Corona Modulation as Revealed by Cryo‐TEM

Cryo‐TEM imaging confirmed that all EV subsets displayed intact spherical structures with well‐preserved bilayer morphology, consistent with canonical descriptions of fungal EV ultrastructure (Souza et al. 2019; Dietz et al. 2023; Parra et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025). However, subtle yet reproducible differences were detected following corona modulation (Figure 2A–C). sEVs exhibited decreased membrane contrast and smoother perimeters compared to nEVs, consistent with removal of peripherally associated proteins that normally contribute to bilayer visualization and curvature stabilization (Souza et al. 2019; Dietz et al. 2023; Parra et al. 2024; Cominal et al. 2025). In contrast, serum‐coated EVs presented a visibly thickened and more diffuse outer border, suggesting formation of a secondary corona layer composed of adsorbed serum biomolecules, an ultrastructural hallmark previously observed in nanoparticle systems and more recently in EVs exposed to biological fluids (Singh et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2023; Ramos et al. 2022; Parra et al. 2024).

All EV populations displayed spherical morphology with well‐defined lipid bilayers. Subtle differences in membrane contrast and vesicle packing were observed following corona modulation, consistent with changes in surface composition. Scale bar = 100 nm. Importantly, EVs remained structurally preserved across conditions, indicating that neither salt‐based shaving nor secondary‐corona acquisition compromised membrane integrity, aligning with MISEV2023 guidelines for EV handling (Welsh et al. 2024; Cominal et al. 2025). Together, these data reinforce that corona composition contributes not only to physicochemical remodelling (size, charge, detectability) but also reshapes nanoscale architectural cues that may influence how EVs are recognised by immune cells within host environments. Since such recognition events typically represent the first step toward EV–cell interaction and downstream immune activation, the next analyses evaluated how corona modulation affects macrophage contact and immunological response to fungal EVs.

Biomolecular Corona Regulates the Interaction of Fungal EVs With Macrophages

Fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed clear qualitative differences in the interaction between DiI‐labelled EVs and AMJ2‐C11 macrophages depending on corona composition and the presence of extracellular proteins in the culture environment (Figure 3A–S). Importantly, the improved analytical approach allowed discrimination between cell‐associated fluorescence and extracellular EV distribution (Figure 3T–U).

In FBS‐supplemented medium, where the serum was previously EV‐depleted to avoid exogenous vesicle interference (Eitan et al. 2015; Lehrich et al. 2018; Welsh et al. 2024), nEVs produced intense punctate fluorescence at macrophage surfaces (Figure 3D–F), consistent with efficient contact and early recognition at the cell–vesicle interface. This pattern was further supported by quantitative analysis of cell‐associated fluorescence (Figure 3T), which showed increased EV–cell association under FBS conditions.

In contrast, sEVs exhibited reduced cell‐associated fluorescence compared to nEVs under FBS conditions (Figure 3L–N and Figure 3T), indicating that removal of primary fungal corona components impairs effective EV–cell association rather than completely abolishing EV presence in the system.

Notably, under serum‐reduced Nutridoma conditions, fluorescence signals were predominantly diffuse and distributed throughout the extracellular space (Figure 3H–J and P–R), suggesting that EVs remain largely non‐associated with macrophages in the absence of serum‐derived corona components. This observation was confirmed by increased extracellular fluorescence values (Figure 3U), indicating accumulation of non‐bound EVs rather than productive interaction with the cell surface.

These results indicate that the native fungal corona supports macrophage sensing through recognition of surface‐exposed PAMPs required for phagocyte activation (da Silva et al. 2016; Bitencourt et al. 2018; Brauer et al. 2020; Brown et al. 2024). Thus, fungal corona elements act as a functional molecular interface for PRR‐mediated detection of fungal EVs (da Silva et al. 2016; Dietz et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024).

The extracellular protein environment also significantly influenced EV–cell interaction. Under Nutridoma‐supplemented, serum‐free medium (1% v/v), both nEVs and sEVs showed limited cell‐associated fluorescence despite detectable total signal, whereas FBS supplementation promoted clear EV–cell association, indicating that host‐derived proteins enhance EV stabilization and membrane docking (Dietz et al. 2023; Musicò et al. 2025).

Such enhancement likely stems from corona‐mediated mechanisms (including opsonization, receptor clustering, and electrostatic/steric stabilization) largely driven by adsorbed serum proteins such as albumin (Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Liam‐Or et al. 2024). These proteins are known to improve EV docking dynamics and strengthen vesicle–cell contact, a phenomenon well‐established in nano–bio interfaces and recently extended to EVs (Dietz et al. 2023; Heidarzadeh et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024; Tassoni et al. 2025).

These findings reinforce that the biomolecular corona is a decisive determinant of EV–macrophage recognition. By stabilizing colloidal behaviour and facilitating receptor accessibility, the corona shapes how fungal EVs engage the immune interface (Singh et al. 2020, 2023; Wolf et al. 2022; Dietz et al. 2023). Importantly, our data demonstrate that total fluorescence detection alone may overestimate EV–cell interaction, as extracellularly dispersed EVs contribute significantly to the signal under serum‐reduced conditions.

Consistent with observations in mammalian EVs, corona‐enriched vesicles exhibit enhanced macrophage interaction and inflammatory activation (Santos et al. 2024). Altogether, the fungal‐derived and serum‐derived coronas act synergistically to define EV immune identity. When integrated with the physicochemical and ultrastructural remodelling shown in Figures 1 and 2, these findings support a model in which corona composition governs how fungal EVs are initially interpreted by innate immune sentinels, contributes to influencing downstream activation of inflammatory pathways explored in the next section.

EV Corona Composition Dictates Cytokine Secretion Profiles in Macrophages

Cytokine profiling of AMJ2‐C11 macrophages revealed that corona composition and the extracellular protein milieu jointly modulated the balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory responses (Figures 4 and 5). In 10% (v/v) EV‐depleted FBS, the LPS + Pam3CSK4 control induced the expected robust inflammatory profile (Figure 4A–D), with high levels of IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α and IFN‐γ, validating the responsiveness of the system (de Campos et al. 2020). nEVs elicited a partial pro‐inflammatory response, particularly for IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IFN‐γ, whereas sEVs tended to induce lower or comparable levels of these mediators, indicating that removal of the primary fungal corona attenuates macrophage activation (Figure 4A–D). In contrast, cEVs often restored or augmented the production of IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IFN‐γ compared with nEVs and sEVs, especially under FBS supplementation (Figure 4A–D), consistent with a secondary serum‐derived corona that potentiates EV‐driven inflammatory signalling. This pattern mirrors previous observations in which A. fumigatus EVs triggered a partial pro‐inflammatory response in macrophages, characterised by increased TNF‐α production and up‐regulation of iNOS and adhesion molecules (Souza et al. 2019; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023).

Anti‐inflammatory cytokines were also influenced by corona status. Under FBS conditions (Figure 4E,F), nEVs and particularly cEVs promoted elevated IL‐4 and, to a lesser extent, IL‐10 levels relative to control, while sEVs showed a reduced ability to sustain this anti‐inflammatory profile. Such mixed cytokine signatures, combining pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory mediators, are consistent with the dual immunomodulatory nature of fungal EVs reported for Cryptococcus spp. (Vanhoffelen et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025), Candida spp. (Ikeda et al. 2024) and Paracoccidioides spp. (da Silva et al. 2016; Ikeda et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025), which can simultaneously enhance TNF‐α/IFN‐γ and IL‐10 or TGF‐β secretion depending on context. These data suggest that, in a protein‐rich environment, the serum‐acquired corona of cEVs favours a more complex macrophage response, with concurrent activation and regulatory cues.

When macrophages were stimulated in Nutridoma (1% v/v), EV‐driven effects became more pronounced and predominantly pro‐inflammatory (Figure 5). In this setting, nEVs, sEVs and cEVs all markedly increased IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IFN‐γ (Figure 5A,C,D) compared with unstimulated controls, while IL‐6 (Figure 5B) was strongly induced mainly by nEVs. The overall pattern resembled a pro‐inflammatory activation profile, particularly for nEVs and cEVs, with robust TNF‐α and IFN‐γ secretion and relatively modest IL‐10 levels (Figure 5F). This is in line with studies showing that EVs from Paracoccidioides brasiliensis have been associated with pro‐inflammatory macrophage activation programs, characterised by high production of NO and pro‐inflammatory cytokines with limited IL‐10 induction (da Silva et al. 2016; de Rezende et al. 2025). Likewise, A. fumigatus EVs have been reported to induce TNF‐α and iNOS expression in macrophages, reinforcing their capacity to promote inflammatory activation (Souza et al. 2019; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023).

The comparison between EV‐depleted FBS and Nutridoma conditions highlights the contribution of host proteins to shaping EV‐induced cytokine profiles. In the absence of exogenous serum proteins (Figure 5), fungal corona components appear to dominate the interaction, favouring a more uniformly pro‐inflammatory output. In contrast, under FBS supplementation (Figure 4), the secondary serum‐derived corona of cEVs supports a more balanced response, with co‐induction of IL‐4 and IL‐10 alongside pro‐inflammatory mediators, reminiscent of the mixed profiles described for clinical EVs from patients with systemic mycoses (de Rezende et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings indicate that both fungal‐ and serum‐derived coronas act as decisive determinants of EV‐driven cytokine secretion, fine‐tuning the magnitude and inflammatory balance of macrophage activation in a manner that subsequently manifests as transcriptional reprogramming of innate receptors and activation‐associated markers discussed next.

EV Corona Modulates Transcriptional Responses Associated With Macrophage Activation

EV stimulation promoted marked transcriptional remodelling of innate immune pathways in AMJ2‐C11 macrophages (Figures 6 and 7). The expanded transcriptional panel revealed coordinated modulation of pathogen‐recognition receptors (Tlr2, Tlr4, Dectin1 and DC‐Sign), downstream inflammatory signalling (Nfκb), and polarization‐associated genes including Nos2, Arg1, Chi3l3 (Ym1), Retnla (Fizz1) and Tgfb1. Together, these markers provide a broader view of macrophage activation programs rather than a strict binary M1/M2 classification.

In parallel, Arg1 and Chi3l3 (Ym1) levels provided deeper insight into macrophage polarization programs, especially considering the murine nature of AMJ2‐C11 cells and the relevance of Chi3l3 (Ym1) as a hallmark of M2‐like responses in rodent pulmonary immunity (Souza et al. 2019; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025). Additionally, Retnla (Fizz1) and Tgfb1 were included as markers associated with regulatory and tissue‐remodelling macrophage programs frequently observed during fungal infections.

Under EV‐depleted FBS (Figures 6A, C–G and 7A, C–G), sEVs drove the strongest up‐regulation of Tlr2, Tlr4 and Nfκb, and were also associated with reduced expression of Chi3l3 (Ym1) and Retnla (Fizz1) compared with nEVs. This suggests that removal of the native fungal corona unmasks PAMP‐enriched ligands, which may include β‐glucans and mannoproteins, thereby improving PRR accessibility and sensing at the cell–EV interface, while diminishing transcriptional signatures associated with regulatory macrophage activation (Bitencourt et al. 2018; Brown et al. 2024; Ikeda et al. 2024). In contrast, nEVs induced the highest Chi3l3 (Ym1) expression, together with elevated Retnla expression, suggesting that the native fungal corona favours a transcriptional program combining inflammatory signalling with regulatory and tissue‐associated responses (Bitencourt et al. 2018; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; de Rezende et al. 2025). The resulting mixed inflammatory–regulatory transcriptional signature mirrors cytokine profiles detected in the same condition (Figure 4), consistent with the dual‐modulating nature of fungal EVs described across multiple pathogenic species (da Silva et al. 2016; Freitas et al. 2023; Vanhoffelen et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025).

Conversely, cEVs elicited an intermediate transcriptional response in EV‐depleted FBS, characterised by moderated induction (Figure 6AC, D and G) of Tlr2, Tlr4 and Nfκb, in line with co‐activation of IL‐4 and IL‐10 detected in cytokine assays (Figure 4). This attenuated responsiveness reflects the partial formation of a secondary serum‐derived corona, which overlays the EV surface and reduces the direct exposure of highly immunogenic PAMPs. In this configuration, receptor docking becomes less efficient and less synchronised, tempering inflammatory signalling while still permitting a limited degree of activation. Mechanistically, this phenomenon aligns with the electrostatic screening and steric masking exerted by adsorbed host proteins, ultimately generating a nuanced transcriptional signature that is neither fully inflammatory nor fully regulatory (Dietz et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025). Together, these observations position cEVs at an intermediate point along a corona‐dependent continuum of immune sensing.

Under serum‐free Nutridoma (Figure 7B, H–L), the absence of serum proteins resulted in a predominant pro‐inflammatory transcriptional program, with strong induction of Nfκb and Nos2 (iNOS), particularly in response to nEVs and cEVs. This profile is consistent with the enhanced production of IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IFN‐γ detected in Figure 5. In this protein‐poor environment, Arg1 expression remained low and Chi3l3 (Ym1) induction was attenuated, while Retnla and Tgfb1 expression patterns suggested limited activation of regulatory transcriptional programs (Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2024; Ikeda et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025). This finding demonstrates that direct exposure of fungal EV ligands promotes inflammatory signaling while restricting regulatory macrophage transcriptional signatures. Thus, Nutridoma reveals the intrinsic immunostimulatory potency of fungal EVs when host‐derived corona constraints are removed.

Altogether, these findings demonstrate that the biomolecular corona functions as a dynamic molecular switch, dictating whether fungal EVs are sensed by macrophages. In protein‐rich environments, the primary and secondary coronas dampen PAMP exposure and sustain regulatory cues, reflected by preserved Chi3l3 (Ym1) and Retnla expression, thus promoting a tempered inflammatory–regulatory transcriptional program that may favor fungal persistence. Conversely, corona stripping amplifies PRR engagement, driving maximal NF‐κB activation, reduced Chi3l3 (Ym1) and Retnla expression, and a dominant inflammatory transcriptional profile. Therefore, corona remodelling emerges as a central determinant of macrophage sensing and functional activation programs during host–pathogen interactions. These results support the concept that EV corona remodelling shapes macrophage activation programs rather than inducing discrete macrophage polarization states within the classical M1/M2 framework.

Discussion

Fungal EVs are increasingly recognised as universal mediators of intra‐ and inter‐kingdom communication, released by all domains of life and functioning as nanoscale conveyors of bioactive information that influence microbial fitness, immunity, and disease progression (Bitencourt et al. 2022a; Ikeda et al. 2024). In the context of fungal pathogens, EVs transport virulence determinants including polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and immunomodulatory molecules that can profoundly alter host responses (Brauer et al. 2020; Rizzo et al. 2020; Bitencourt et al. 2022b; Freitas et al. 2023; de Rezende et al. 2025). However, EV functionality extends beyond intrinsic cargo: their biological identity is continuously shaped by microenvironmental encounters, particularly through the dynamic acquisition or loss of a biomolecular corona (Ramos et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Tassoni et al. 2025). In this study, we provided the first demonstration that corona remodelling contributes to redefining the physicochemical features and immune recognition profiles of fungal EVs, revealing a surface‐dependent molecular switch that dictates the fate of macrophage responses.

High‐salt stripping partially removed native fungal corona components, leading to increased hydrodynamic diameter, decreased vesicle detectability, and more negative zeta potential. These effects are consistent with destabilization of interfacial layers and exposure of underlying lipids and carbohydrate structures, a phenomenon previously described for nanoparticle systems subjected to corona depletion (Wolf et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Cominal et al. 2025; Musicò et al. 2025). The decrease in particle concentration after high‐salt treatment indicates that removal of loosely bound fungal surface components contributes to affect vesicle detectability in NTA. Subsequent incubation with EV‐depleted serum increased the number of detectable particles and markedly restored surface‐associated proteins, as demonstrated by protein quantification and SDS–PAGE profiles. Consistent with these biochemical observations, dot blot analyses incorporating buffer‐only (PBS), protein‐only (5% w/v BSA), and EV‐depleted serum controls further confirmed that the detected signals reflect bona fide EV‐ and corona‐associated proteins rather than non‐specific antibody binding. Importantly, the secondary corona formed in FBS does not reproduce the composition of the original fungal corona but instead establishes a distinct host‐derived interfacial layer, defining a new EV bioidentity shaped by serum exposure. Together, these observations reinforce that the EV corona represents a highly plastic and context‐dependent interface, rapidly adapting to the extracellular environment and altering how these nanovesicles present themselves to host cells (Singh et al. 2020; Tóth et al. 2021; Dietz et al. 2023; Musicò et al. 2025).

Cryo‐TEM analysis confirmed that corona modulation did not compromise membrane integrity, reinforcing that the observed functional differences across EV subsets arise from surface‐associated remodelling rather than structural disruption. Notably, despite preserved morphology, the immunological consequences of corona remodelling were profound. Corona removal reduced cell‐associated EV fluorescence, indicating impaired EV–cell association rather than absence of EVs in the system, demonstrating that native fungal surface ligands, including β‐glucans and mannoproteins, enhance membrane docking and initiate recognition via PRRs such as Tlr2 and Dectin‐1 (da Silva et al. 2016; Bitencourt et al. 2018; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; de Rezende et al. 2025).

When host proteins adsorbed onto EVs, cell association was restored, consistent with literature showing that serum coronas provide steric and electrostatic stabilization or opsonizing cues that facilitate uptake (Heidarzadeh et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024; Tassoni et al. 2025). Importantly, our imaging analysis further demonstrated that total fluorescence signal alone does not necessarily reflect EV–cell association, as serum‐reduced conditions led to increased extracellular EV dispersion despite detectable fluorescence. Similar behaviour has been observed in the mammalian EV field, where serum coronas facilitate cellular uptake and modify immunogenicity (Dietz et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025). These findings support a model where fungal EVs, once released into host tissues, acquire a hybrid identity integrating fungal and host elements, blurring taxonomic boundaries at the nano‐bio interface.

Cytokine and transcriptional analyses revealed that native and secondary‐corona EVs induced a balanced inflammatory–regulatory program, combining Nfκb activation with Arg1, Chi3l3 (Ym1) and Retnla (Fizz1) induction under protein‐rich conditions, together with modulation of Tgfb1 expression. This dual signature aligns with prior studies describing immune modulation as a conserved property of EVs produced by pathogenic fungi (da Silva et al. 2016; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023; Ikeda et al. 2024; de Rezende et al. 2025). By contrast, sEVs strongly up‐regulated Tlr2/Tlr4/Nfκb and suppressed Chi3l3 (Ym1), highlighting that unmasking of PAMP‐enriched ligands drives a more pronounced inflammatory transcriptional program.

In serum‐reduced Nutridoma, fungal corona cues dominated recognition, with coordinated induction of PRRs including Tlr2, Tlr4, Dectin1 and DC‐Sign, together with maximal Nfκb and Nos2 expression (Souza et al. 2019; Brauer et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2023). Notably, these responses occurred despite reduced EV–cell association, suggesting that even limited or transient contact with PAMP‐exposed EV surfaces is sufficient to trigger robust innate immune signalling.

Altogether, these data support a conceptual advance: the fungal EV corona is not merely a passive accessory layer but a regulatory gatekeeper that determines whether EVs amplify or temper inflammation. From these findings, a mechanistic model can be proposed in which corona remodelling functions as a bioidentity switch controlling EV immunogenic outcomes and host–pathogen interactions. In protein‐rich niches (e.g., bloodstream, alveolar surfaces), fungal and host‐derived corona constituents may jointly limit danger signalling and sustain immunoregulatory macrophage responses, favouring fungal persistence (Rhodes et al. 2022; Brown et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). Conversely, in protein‐poor microenvironments, such as early infection sites with low exudation, PAMP‐rich surface exposure may enhance inflammatory clearance (Rhodes et al. 2022; Brown et al. 2024; Mejía et al. 2024). In this context, our data indicate that EV spatial distribution, whether cell‐associated or extracellular, constitutes an additional regulatory layer influencing immune sensing. Such microenvironmentally tuned immune outcomes underscore the strategic advantage of EV corona plasticity in fungal pathogenesis.

Beyond fungal disease, these findings have broader implications. Because corona acquisition is a conserved phenomenon across biological kingdoms, the principles uncovered here may extend to EV‐mediated communication in bacteria, protozoa, plants, and animals (Tóth et al. 2021; Wolf et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Tassoni et al. 2025). Moreover, the ability to modulate EV corona composition opens opportunities for corona engineering strategies, either to (i) potentiate antifungal immunity by preventing immune masking or (ii) enhance targeted delivery and safety of EV‐based therapeutics (Dietz et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024; Musicò et al. 2025; Tassoni et al. 2025).

Collectively, our findings support the emerging concept that EV identity is not solely defined by their cellular origin, but also by the dynamic remodelling of their surface through biomolecular corona acquisition in host environments. This concept may be particularly relevant for fungal pathogens, whose EVs encounter complex host fluids during infection. In this context, EV–host interactions should be interpreted as a composite interface formed by both vesicle‐intrinsic components and adsorbed host‐derived molecules. Our results indicate that this corona remodeling can reshape macrophage transcriptional activation programs, suggesting that EV immunological identity is fluid and context‐dependent. These observations align with the broader nano–bio interface paradigm, in which nanoparticle biological behaviour is dictated by the biomolecular corona rather than by the pristine particle surface. Extending this concept to fungal EVs provides a framework to understand how pathogen‐derived vesicles may adapt their immune signalling properties upon exposure to host fluids. Understanding how the EV surface interface dynamically evolves during infection may therefore enable us to manipulate vesicle bioidentity for therapeutic benefit, whether by blocking opsonization pathways that favour fungal persistence or by designing antifungal EVs with tailored immunomodulatory function. Collectively, our study establishes corona remodelling as a fundamental and previously unrecognised determinant of EV‐driven communication, with relevance spanning across diverse organisms and host‐microbe interactions. Although our study provides the first functional evidence linking fungal EV corona remodelling to macrophage activation, future studies should dissect the precise protein composition of the corona and its receptor‐specific interactions.

Conclusion

Altogether, this study demonstrates that the fungal EV corona acts as a dynamic and decisive regulator of immune perception. By modulating surface composition, either through selective removal of native constituents or rapid adsorption of host‐derived proteins, corona remodelling reshapes physicochemical signatures, receptor accessibility, and macrophage inflammatory programming. Importantly, our findings also reveal that corona composition influences the spatial distribution of EVs, distinguishing cell‐associated vesicles from extracellularly dispersed populations, which differentially contribute to immune sensing. As fungal EVs inevitably acquire a secondary corona upon entering host environments, this surface redefinition becomes a key determinant of immune detection, vesicle persistence, and ultimately pathogen success.

By positioning the corona as a molecular switch that toggles EVs between potent inflammatory triggers and more balanced immunomodulatory signals, our findings uncover an underappreciated but actionable layer of host–pathogen communication.
