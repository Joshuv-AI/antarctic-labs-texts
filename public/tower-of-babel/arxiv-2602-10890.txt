Hybrid methods for Friedrichs systems with application to scalar and vector diffusion-advection-reaction
Daniele A. Di Pietro, Aurelio E. Spadotto

Abstract
In this work we study arbitrary-order hybrid discretizations of Friedrichs systems. Friedrichs systems provide a framework that goes beyond the standard classification of partial differential equations into hyperbolic or elliptic, and are thus particularly suited for problems that include both diffusive and advective terms. The family of numerical schemes proposed in this work hinge on hybrid spaces with unknowns located at elements and faces. They support general meshes, are locally conservative and, compared with traditional Discontinuous Galerkin discretizations, lead to smaller algebraic systems once static condensation has been applied. We carry out a complete stability and convergence analysis, which appears to be the first of its kind. The performance of the method is illustrated on scalar and vector three-dimensional diffusion-advection-reaction problems. MSC2020: 65N30, 65N08, 35F15 Key words: Friedrichs systems, Hybrid High-Order methods, hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin methods, polytopal methods, diffusion-advection-reaction

Introduction

In this work we carry out what appears to be the first complete theoretical study of arbitrary-order hybrid discretizations of Friedrichs systems and showcase their performance on scalar and vector diffusion-advection-reaction problems.
The proposed family of methods support general meshes, are locally conservative, and, compared with classical Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods, lead to smaller algebraic problems after static condensation of the element unknowns.

Relevant partial differential equations (PDEs) can exhibit elliptic or hyperbolic behaviours depending on the value of the physical parameters.
A prototypical example is provided by diffusion-advection-reaction and its vanishing diffusion limit.
Weak formulations and robust numerical discretizations for such PDEs are often not straightforward to derive.
A significant advance in this respect was made by Friedrichs, who introduced in
[
22
]
a framework to treat in a unified way elliptic and hyperbolic problems.
The key idea consists in recasting the PDE as a system of first-order equations satisfying symmetry and positivity conditions, which is possible for a variety of models in continuum mechanics and electromagnetism; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Section 56.2]
.
Deriving weak formulations of Friedrichs systems involves, however, some technicalities.
In
[
18
]
, the authors introduce a weak formulation in graph spaces with boundary conditions enforced through (positive) boundary operators that do not necessarily admit a trace representation.
Such formulation is amenable to the analysis in the spirit of the Banach–Nečas–Babuška Lemma (see
[
21
, Chapter 56]
), and will be the starting point of the present work.

Various approaches have been considered in the literature for the numerical approximation of Friedrichs systems since the late 1990s.
A first analysis of finite volume methods for unsteady Friedrichs systems can be found in
[
31
]
, where the authors show convergence in
h^{\frac{1}{2}}
(with
h
denoting the meshsize) under a hyperbolic CFL condition; see also
[
26
]
for an extension of these results to weak solutions and a posteriori error estimates.
Discontinuous Galerkin methods were considered in
[
18
]
, using the techniques developed for the imposition of boundary conditions as a paradigm for the weak enforcement of interface conditions.
The same authors also discussed enhanced DG methods for Friedrichs systems with two- or multi-field structure in
[
19
,
20
]
.
A related contribution is
[
16
]
which, taking inspiration from the theory of two-fields Friedrichs systems, introduced a DG method for variable diffusion with advection that is fully robust for locally vanishing diffusion.
Discontinuous Galerkin methods for Friedrichs systems in graph spaces were also considered in
[
25
]
, while discontinuous Petrov–Galerkin methods were proposed and analyzed in
[
3
]
.
More recently,
hp
-adaptive hybridizable DG schemes for Friedrichs systems with one- and two-fields structure have been proposed and numerically demonstrated in
[
6
]
, which also contains a coercivity study.
A side result of our analysis are inf-sup stability and convergence results for the
h
-version of the one-field scheme.
Space-time DG methods were considered in
[
9
]
, while
[
4
]
focuses on stabilized continuous finite element approximations.
Finally, the series of papers
[
2
,
10
,
29
]
addresses various aspects related to the development of asymptotic-preserving and well-balanced schemes for unsteady Friedrichs systems.

In this work we carry out what appears to be the first comprehensive analysis of hybrid approximations of Friedrichs systems on general polytopal meshes.
Hybrid methods are based on unknowns that are local polynomials on mesh elements and faces.
Among the most prominent examples, we cite here hybridizable DG
[
8
]
methods, built around the notion of numerical flux, and Hybrid High-Order methods
[
15
,
17
]
, based on the notion of local reconstructions; see
[
7
]
and also
[
13
, Section 5.1.6]
for a study of the relations between these two methods.
The scheme we propose and study here can be regarded as a generalization of the face-element upwind discretization of advective terms considered in
[
14
]
.
Our analysis hinges on inf-sup stability, which we prove by taking a suitable projection of the directional derivative as a test function.
Denoting by
k\geq 0
the polynomial degree of the hybrid space, this stability result is instrumental in proving error estimates of order
h^{k+\frac{1}{2}}
for the error, which includes an
L^{2}
-like norm of the solution components and of the (discrete) directional derivative.
Closely following
[
21
]
, we carefully track the dependence on the model parameters, which makes it possible to uncover improved pre-asymptotic convergence in
h^{k+1}
when reaction dominates.
The performance of the proposed method is showcased on various diffusion-advection-reaction models.
We consider, in particular, the vector case corresponding to magnetic diffusion-advection-reaction, which we preliminarily show to fit within the Friedrichs framework.
Stabilized methods for this problem were developed in
[
33
,
32
,
27
]
.
A scheme supporting general polytopal meshes for magnetic advection-reaction has also been proposed in
[
5
]
.
The performance of the method is numerically demonstrated on three-dimensional tests including, in particular, a physical benchmark inspired by
[
30
]
and describing the expulsion of the magnetic field from a rotating cilinder.

The rest of this work is organized as follows.
The continuous and discrete settings are respectively discussed in Sections
2
and
3
.
Section
4
contains the statement of the discrete problem.
Complete stability and error analyses are carried out in Section
5
.
Finally, Section
6
contains the numerical validation of the method.

2
Continuous setting

2.1
Friedrichs systems

We briefly recall the notion of Friedrichs system.
The setting and notation are closely inspired by
[
21
, Chapter 1]
.
Denote by
\Omega
a bounded polytopal domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Given an integer
m>0
, let
\mathcal{K}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})

and let
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{A}^{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq d}
be a family of
(m\times m)
-matrix valued fields such that
\mathcal{A}^{i}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})\cap C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
for all
1\leq i\leq d
and
\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\mathcal{A}^{i}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
, where
\partial_{i}
denotes the partial derivative with respect to the
i
th variable.
We additionally assume that the fields
\mathcal{A}^{i}
are Hermitian, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{i}=\left(\mathcal{A}^{i}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
, and that there exists a real number
\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}>0
such that, almost everywhere in
\Omega
,

\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{K}^{\mathsf{H}}-\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}\geq 2\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}\mathcal{I}_{m},

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
denotes the identity matrix.
We define the following differential operators on
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{C}^{m})
:

A_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}^{i}\partial_{i},\qquad A\coloneqq\mathcal{K}+A_{1},\qquad\tilde{A}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}^{\mathsf{H}}-\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}-A_{1}=\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{K}^{\mathsf{H}}-\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}-A,

(2)

as well as the Hermitian field
\mathcal{N}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
such that

\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\mathcal{A}^{i}_{|\partial\Omega},

(3)

where
n
denotes the outward unit normal field on
\partial\Omega
.
In order to enforce boundary conditions, we will need another non-negative field
\mathcal{M}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
such that
\Ker(\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{N})+\Ker(\mathcal{M}+\mathcal{N})=\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})
, we consider the problem that consists in finding
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle Au

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(4)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{N})u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.

The weak formulation of problem (
4
) involves some technicalities and is discussed in detail in
[
21
, Section 56.3]
.
Denote by
V\coloneqq\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})\;:\;A_{1}v\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})\right\}
the
L^{2}
-graph space of
A_{1}
.
Both
A
and
\tilde{A}
can be extended to operators in
\mathcal{L}(V;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}))
.
To circumvent the problem of traces for functions in
V
, we introduce the boundary operator
N\in\mathcal{L}(V;V^{\prime})
(with
V^{\prime}
dual space of
V
) such that
\langle Nw,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\coloneqq(Aw,v)_{\Omega}-(w,\tilde{A}v)_{\Omega}
for all
(w,v)\in V^{2}
, where
(a,b)_{\Omega}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}(a,b)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}
with
(\alpha,\beta)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\coloneqq\beta^{\mathsf{H}}\alpha
for all
\alpha,\,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
(interpreted as column vectors).
It can be proved that
N
is a boundary operator.
The role of
\mathcal{M}
is played by the monotone operator
M\in\mathcal{L}(V;V^{\prime})
such that
\Ker(N-M)+\Ker(N+M)=V
.
Setting
V_{0}\coloneqq\Ker(M-N)
, the weak formulation consists in finding
u\in V_{0}
such that

(Au,v)_{\Omega}=(f,v)_{\Omega}\qquad\forall v\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m}).

(5)

Throughout the rest of the paper, we will bridge the strong and weak formulation by assuming that, for all
(w,v)\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})
,
s>\frac{1}{2}
, with
(a,b)_{\partial\Omega}\coloneqq\int_{\partial\Omega}(a,b)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}
usual inner product of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})
,

\langle Mw,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=(\mathcal{M}w,v)_{\partial\Omega},\qquad\langle Nw,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=(\mathcal{N}w,v)_{\partial\Omega}.

2.2
Examples

We briefly discuss two examples that will be used in the numerical experiments of Section
6
.

2.2.1
Scalar diffusion-advection-reaction

Let
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}
with
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\cap\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}=\emptyset
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
non-empty.
Consider a uniformly elliptic matrix-valued field
\kappa\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and let
\mu\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
.
Let
\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\nabla\cdot\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
assume that there exists
\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}>0
such that
\mu-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\beta\geq\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.
Given
f_{p}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we consider the problem of finding the flux
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the scalar potential
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\kappa^{-1}\sigma+\nabla p

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\sigma+\beta\cdot\nabla p+\mu p

\displaystyle=f_{p}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}$},

\displaystyle(\sigma+\beta p)\cdot n

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}$}.

It is known that this problem can be recast into the form (
4
) with
m=d+1
by setting
f\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{d}\\
f_{p}\end{bmatrix}
and letting

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\kappa^{-1}&\mathcal{O}_{d}\\
\mathcal{O}_{d}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mu\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{A}^{i}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{d,d}&e_{i}\\
e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}&\beta_{i}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{d,d}&n\\
n^{\mathsf{T}}&\beta\cdot n\end{bmatrix},\quad

where
e_{i}
is the
i
th element of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
while
\mathcal{O}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{O}_{d,d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
respectively denote the zero (column) vector and matrix.
The boundary condition can be recast into the form
u\in\Ker(\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{N})
setting

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\chi_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{D}}+\chi_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{N}},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{D}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{d,d}&-n\\
n^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{N}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{d,d}&n\\
-n^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix},

where, for any subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\chi_{Z}
denotes its indicator function.

2.2.2
Vector diffusion-advection-reaction

Assume
d=3
and let
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}
be partitioned as before.
Consider a positive diffusion coefficient
\varepsilon\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and a scalar reaction coefficient
\gamma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Let
\beta
as before and define the inflow boundary
\Gamma^{-}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\partial\Omega:\beta\cdot n(x)<0\right\}
as well as the outflow boundary
\Gamma^{+}\coloneqq\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma^{-}
.
For a given source
f_{p}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we consider the problem of finding the vector potential
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that:

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\varepsilon\nabla\times p)+L_{\beta}p+\gamma p

\displaystyle=f_{p}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(7)

\displaystyle n\times p+\chi_{\Gamma^{-}}(p\cdot n)n

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}$},

\displaystyle n\times(\varepsilon\nabla\times p)+\chi_{\Gamma^{-}}(\beta\cdot n)p

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}$},

where the Lie derivative operator
L_{\beta}
is such that, for any function
q:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
smooth enough,

L_{\beta}q\coloneqq-\beta\times(\nabla\times q)+\nabla(\beta\cdot q).

Introduce the flux
b\coloneqq\varepsilon\nabla\times p
and the global variable
z\coloneqq(b,p)
.
Consider the identities

\nabla\times s=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathcal{R}^{j}\partial_{j}s,\qquad\alpha\times s=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathcal{R}^{j}\alpha_{j}\right)s\eqqcolon\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}s,

where the family
(\mathcal{R}^{j})_{1\leq j\leq 3}
of anti-symmetric matrices
\mathcal{R}^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is such that
\mathcal{R}^{j}_{ik}=\epsilon_{ijk}
for all
1\leq i,j,k\leq 3
,
\epsilon
being the Levi–Civita symbol.
Exploiting the vector identity

\nabla(\beta\cdot p)=\beta\times(\nabla\times p)+p\times(\nabla\times\beta)+(p\cdot\nabla)\beta+(\beta\cdot\nabla)p,

the problem can be rewritten in the form (
4
) setting

f\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{3}\\
f_{p}\end{bmatrix}
and

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{3}&\mathcal{O}_{3,3}\\
\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&(\nabla\beta-\mathcal{V}_{\nabla\times\beta}+\gamma\mathcal{I}_{3})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{A}^{i}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&-\mathcal{R}^{i}\\
-(\mathcal{R}^{i})^{\mathsf{T}}&\beta_{i}\mathcal{I}_{3}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&-\mathcal{V}_{n}\\
\mathcal{V}_{n}&(\beta\cdot n)\mathcal{I}_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

The boundary condition can be recast in the form
z\in\Ker(\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M})
setting

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\chi_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{D}}+\chi_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{N}},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{D}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&\mathcal{V}_{n}\\
\mathcal{V}_{n}&|\beta\cdot n|\mathcal{I}_{3}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{N}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&-\mathcal{V}_{n}\\
-\mathcal{V}_{n}&|\beta\cdot n|\mathcal{I}_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

To guarantee the validity of (
1
),
we suppose the existence of
\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}>0
such that, almost everywhere in
\Omega
,

\left(\gamma-\frac{\nabla\cdot\beta}{2}\right)\mathcal{I}_{3}+\nabla_{\mathrm{s}}\beta\geq\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}\mathcal{I}_{3},

where
\nabla_{\rm s}
stands for the symmetric part of the gradient.

Remark 1

(Interpretation of problem (
7
))
.

In the context of magnetohydrodyamics, problem (
7
) can be used to describe the magnetic field
b
in terms of a vector potential
p
(see
[
23
, Section 5]
as well as
[
24
]
).
In Section
6.3
, the equation is adapted with minor changes to a context where the potential variable
p
has the meaning of magnetic field and the flux
b
is proportional to a current density.

3
Discrete setting

3.1
Mesh

We consider a polytopal mesh
(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathcal{F}_{h})
matching the geometrical requirements detailed in
[
13
, Definition 1.4]
, with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
set of elements and
\mathcal{F}_{h}
set of faces.
For any
Y\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we denote by
h_{Y}
its diameter, so that
h=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}>0
.
Boundary faces lying on
\partial\Omega
are collected in the set
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm b}
and we set
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm i}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm b}
.
For every mesh element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{T}
the subset of
\mathcal{F}_{h}
containing the faces that lie on the boundary
\partial T
of
T
.
For all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm b}
,
n_{F}\coloneqq n
denotes the unit normal vector to
F
pointing out of
\Omega
.
For any
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm i}
, on the other hand, we fix a unique orientation by selecting once and for all a unit normal vector
n_{F}
.
For all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
, we denote by
\omega_{TF}
the orientation of
F
relative to
T
such that
\omega_{TF}n_{F}
points out of
T
.

3.2
Polynomial spaces

Given
Y\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}
and an integer
\ell\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}(Y;\mathbb{X})
,
\mathbb{X}\in\{\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}\}
, the space spanned by the restriction to
Y
of polynomials in the space variables of total degree
\leq\ell
with
\mathbb{X}
-valued coefficients.
The
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector on
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}(Y;\mathbb{X})
is denoted by
\pi^{\ell}_{Y}
irrespectively of the value of
\mathbb{X}
, all ambiguity being removed by the context.

3.3
Trace operators on faces and local integration by parts formula

For all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, denoting by
(n_{F,i})
the Cartesian components of
n_{F}
, let

\mathcal{N}_{F}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{F,i}\mathcal{A}^{i}_{|F}.

By the assumed regularity on
\mathcal{A}
, this quantity is single-valued on
F
and it satisfies
\mathcal{N}_{F}\in L^{\infty}(F;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
.

For all
Y\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}
, denote by
(w,v)_{Y}\coloneqq\int_{Y}(w,v)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}
the inner product of
L^{2}(Y;\mathbb{C}^{m})
.
Let
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
The following integration by parts formula corresponds to the first display equation in the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 60.1]
:
For all
(w,v)\in H^{s}(T;\mathbb{C}^{m})^{2}
,
s>\frac{1}{2}
,

(Aw,v)_{T}=(w,\tilde{A}v)_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}\omega_{TF}(\mathcal{N}_{F}w,v)_{F}=(w,\tilde{A}v)_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}\omega_{TF}(w,\mathcal{N}_{F}v)_{F},

(9)

where, to obtain the second equality, we have written
(\mathcal{N}_{F}w,v)_{F}=\int_{F}(\mathcal{N}_{F}w,v)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\int_{F}v^{\mathsf{H}}\mathcal{N}_{F}w=\int_{F}v^{\mathsf{H}}\mathcal{N}_{F}^{\mathsf{H}}w=\int_{F}(w,\mathcal{N}_{F}v)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=(w,\mathcal{N}_{F}v)_{F}
(the third step being a consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{N}_{F}
is a Hermitian field).

4
A hybrid scheme for Friedrichs systems

4.1
Spaces and interpolators

Let an integer
k\geq 0
be fixed.
We consider discretizations of problem (
5
) based on the following space:

\underline{U}_{h}^{k}\coloneqq\left\{\underline{v}_{h}=((v_{T})_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},(v_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}})\;:\;\text{$v_{Y}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(Y;\mathbb{C}^{m})$ for all $Y\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}$
}\right\}.

Let
s>\frac{1}{2}
.
The meaning of the components is provided by the interpolator
\underline{I}_{h}^{k}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})\to\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
such that, for all
v\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})
,

\underline{I}_{h}^{k}v\coloneqq\big((\pi^{k}_{T}v)_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},(\pi^{k}_{F}v)_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\big).

For all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the restrictions of
\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
,
\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
, and
\underline{I}_{h}^{k}
to
T
, obtained collecting the components attached to
T
and its faces, are denoted replacing the subscript
h
with
T
.
Finally, for all
\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
, we let
v_{h}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{m})
be the piecewise polynomial field such that

(v_{h})_{|T}\coloneqq v_{T}\qquad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

(10)

4.2
Global discrete integration by parts formula

Straightforward algebraic manipulations show that, for any Hermitian matrix
H\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and vectors
\alpha_{1},\,\beta_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, it holds

(H\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}-(H\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}{2},H(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}+\left(H(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}),\frac{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}{2}\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}.

(11)

Proposition 2

(Global discrete integration by parts formula)
.

For all
(\underline{w}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k}\times\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(Aw_{T},v_{T})_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(w_{T},\tilde{A}v_{T})_{T}

(12)

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}\omega_{TF}\left(\frac{w_{F}+w_{T}}{2},\mathcal{N}_{F}(v_{F}-v_{T})\right)_{F}

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}\omega_{TF}\left(\mathcal{N}_{F}(w_{F}-w_{T}),\frac{v_{F}+v_{T}}{2}\right)_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm b}}(\mathcal{N}_{F}w_{F},v_{F})_{F}.

Proof.

By the single-valuedness of
(\mathcal{N}_{F}w_{F},v_{F})_{F}
at interfaces together with the fact that
\omega_{T_{1}F}+\omega_{T_{2}F}=0
whenever

is shared by the mesh elements

and

9

over

and adding the above expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(Aw_{T},v_{T})_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(w_{T},\tilde{A}v_{T})_{T}

(13)

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}\omega_{TF}\left[(\mathcal{N}_{F}w_{F},v_{F})_{F}-(\mathcal{N}_{F}w_{T},v_{T})_{F}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm b}}(\mathcal{N}_{F}w_{F},v_{F})_{F}.

For all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and all

(14)

The conclusion follows applying the above relation with

Remark 4

(Static condensation)
.

The algebraic problem corresponding to (

Such a strategy leads, in many cases, to algebraic problems that are significantly smaller than the ones for DG methods; see, e.g.,
[
1

on this subject, where a detailed comparison of Hybrid High-Order and DG methods for the Stokes problem in three space dimensions is provided.

Proposition 7

(Equivalent reformulation of

\displaystyle|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\underline{v}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{\flat,h}^{2}

Corollary 8

(Partial coercivity of
a_{h}
)
.

Denoting by

the real part operator, for all
\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k}
, it holds

|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\underline{v}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{\flat,h}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}((\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{K}^{\mathsf{H}}-\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A})v_{h},v_{h})_{\Omega}+\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|\underline{v}_{T}|_{0,\partial T}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\underline{v}_{h}|_{{\rm b},h}^{2}+|\underline{v}_{h}|_{j,h}^{2}=\Re(a_{h}(\underline{v}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})).

(30)

Proof.

The inequality in (
30
) is a straightforward consequence of the lower bound (
1
) for
\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{K}^{\mathsf{H}}-\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}
together with the definition (
28
) of the
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\cdot|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{\flat,h}

To prove the equality, we start by writing (
26

\underline{w}_{h}=\underline{v}_{h}
and notice that, for all

,

\displaystyle(Av_{T},v_{T})_{T}+(v_{T},\tilde{A}v_{T})_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}v_{T}^{\mathsf{H}}Av_{T}+\int_{T}(\tilde{A}v_{T})^{\mathsf{H}}v_{T}

Lemma 9

(Inf-sup condition)
.

Let Assumptions
1

Then, for all
\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k}

(31)

where

(32)

As a consequence, problem (
24
) is well-posed.

Proof.

The well-posedness of problem (

To this purpose, let

(i)
Test function for the control of the directional derivative

\displaystyle\text{

\mathfrak{t}_{T}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|z_{T}\|_{L^{2}(T;\mathbb{C}^{m})}\overset{\eqref{eq:zh}}{\leq}\mathfrak{t}_{T}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|(A_{1,T}^{0}-A_{1})v_{T}\|_{L^{2}(T;\mathbb{C}^{m})}+\mathfrak{t}_{T}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|A_{1}v_{T}\|_{L^{2}(T;\mathbb{C}^{m})}.

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bnd.pi.0.T.Ai-Ai}}{}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathfrak{t}_{T}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathfrak{a}_{T}h_{T}^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathfrak{r}_{\flat}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|v_{T}\|_{L^{2}(T;\mathbb{C}^{m})}

{(a+b)^{2}}\leq{2(a^{2}+b^{2})}
for all
a,\,b\in\mathbb{R}
in the right-hand side, and recalling the definition (
28
) of the triple norm proves (

).

We next show that

|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\underline{z}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}\lesssim|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\underline{v}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}

Remark 12

(Convergence rates)
.

The right-hand side of (

From a physical standpoint, this can be interpreted as a dominant-reaction regime.

6.3
Diffusion-advection of the magnetic field in a rotating cylinder

In the context of magnetohydrodynamics, the stationary magnetic field
B
in a medium with uniform conductivity
\sigma>0
and magnetic permeability
\mu>0
advected by a velocity field
\beta
such that
\nabla\cdot\beta=0
can be described by the (steady) induction equation:

\nabla\times(\beta\times B)+\frac{1}{\sigma\mu}\nabla^{2}B=0.

(45)

For a derivation of (
45
) starting from Maxwell equations, we refer to
[
28
, Chapter 1, Section II]
.
Assuming sufficient regularity for the following manipulations to make sense, we recast the induction equation into the framework described in Section
2.2.2
using the identities:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}B

\displaystyle=-\nabla\times(\nabla\times B)+\nabla(\nabla\cdot B),

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\beta\times B)

\displaystyle=(B\cdot\nabla)\beta-(\beta\cdot\nabla)+(\nabla\cdot B)\beta-(\nabla\cdot\beta)B.

Additionally recalling that
\nabla\cdot B=\nabla\cdot\beta=0
, we get

\nabla\times\left(\frac{1}{\sigma\mu}\nabla\times B\right)+(\beta\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)\beta=0.

(46)

Letting

p=B,\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\sigma\mu},\qquad\gamma=0,\qquad f_{p}=0,

(
46
) can be cast in the setting (
8
) by modifying the field
\mathcal{K}
as follows:

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{3}&\mathcal{O}_{3,3}\\
\mathcal{O}_{3,3}&-\nabla\beta\end{bmatrix}.

In this context,
b=\sigma^{-1}J
, where
J
has the meaning of current density.

An experimental configuration for which the magnetic field can be described in terms of the induction equation (
45
) is presented in
[
30
]
. In this work, the authors consider an infinitely long cylindrical rod of conducting material surrounded by void.
The rod is placed in a region with a uniform magnetic field parallel to te section of the cylinder and is put in circular motion around its axis with a constant angular velocity.
The magnetic field tends to be expelled from the rotating region (see
[
30
, Figure 2]
and also, for a similar configuration,
[
28
, Figure II.7]
).
The expulsion of the magnetic field outside the circulating region is more pronounced for higher values of magnetic Reynolds number
\mathfrak{Rm}\coloneqq\sigma\mu\|\beta\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}}L
, (
L
being a characteristic length of the system), corresponding to advection-dominated regimes.

To simulate the experimental setting, we consider a cubic domain
\Omega=\left(-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}\right)^{3}
centered in the origin.
The axis of the cylinder is parallel to the
z
axis and passes through the center of the domain.
Let
R
be the radius of the cylinder,
\omega
be the angular velocity and
k
a user-dependent parameter.
Introducing cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
such that
x=r\cos\theta
,
y=r\sin\theta
,
z=z
and the associated covariant basis
(\hat{e}_{r},\hat{e}_{\theta},\hat{e}_{z})
, the velocity field is defined as

\beta=\omega r\phi(r)\hat{e}_{\theta},\qquad\phi(r)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\lambda(r-R)}}.

The sigmoid function
\phi
is a smooth approximation of a step function such that
\phi\approx 1
for
r<R
and
\phi(r)\approx 0
for
r>R
.
When
\lambda
is chosen large enough, the velocity field reproduces the rigid rotation of the cylinder immersed in a void region.
The problem is solved with
\Gamma_{D}=\partial\Omega
, and the boundary data is set so that the solution matches the uniform field
B_{0}=\hat{e}_{x}
along the boundary. The numerical tests are performed setting
\mu\sigma=1
and varying the magnitude of
\mathfrak{Rm}
through
\omega
.
The results displayed in Figure
3
capture the expected behaviour: as the magnetic Reynolds number increases from
0
(unperturbed configuration) to
5\cdot 10^{-1}
, one observes that the fields line tend to avoid the region occupied by the cilinder.
This effect becomes more and more visible as
\mathfrak{Rm}
increases.
