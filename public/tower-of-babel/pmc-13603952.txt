Leading From the Precipice: How Academic Deans Are Navigating Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education
Authors: V. Dozier, Sarina C. Molina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis qualitative case study examines how associate deans at a medium‐sized Catholic university respond to the rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI). Guided by decolonial and feminist frameworks, the study analyzes survey data from seven academic leaders to understand their concerns, decision‐making processes, and emerging practices. Our findings reveal uneven adoption, limited institutional guidance, and ongoing concerns about academic integrity, equity, and bias. The study also highlights the often‐unseen relational and emotional labor involved in supporting faculty and students. We conclude by recommending flexible, collaborative approaches to AI governance that attend to context, care, and diverse ways of knowing.

Introduction

As artificial intelligence (AI) technologies rapidly reshape pedagogical practices, administrative processes, and student engagement, associate deans are uniquely positioned at the intersection of leadership, policy, and implementation. As leaders, it is important to recognize that although generative AI has the potential to level the playing field, coloniality remains deeply embedded in our educational systems, and AI and its algorithms often privilege Eurocentric onto‐epistemologies over localized, indigenous ways of knowing, being, and sensing. Winters et al. (2020) considered the exclusion of marginalized learners in the development of these systems as digital structural violence. Such violence is also perpetuated in detection tools that disproportionately flag international students and other multilingual writers with “restricted linguistic diversity and word choice” (Liang et al. 2023, 2).

The authors, both associate deans—education and the university library—grappled with the inherent tensions in these new technologies, where we described AI's potential, capability, bias, ethical concerns, as well as the many inaccuracies that exist within these systems, such as ghost references or hallucinations, or attributions to non‐existent references. Our education faculty began requesting support regarding the use of generative AI technologies, such as syllabi language and academic dishonesty guidelines. Simultaneously, the university library established an AI working group to assess campus AI research needs and to develop and facilitate.

Recognizing how other universities’ top‐down policies faced resistance, our Innovations Committee instead created guidelines for our faculty to customize their own classrooms, based on discipline needs and their comfort levels with these tools. This approach is, in a sense, a decolonial act, shifting away from universal policies toward more contextually grounded ways of knowing. The Department of Learning and Teaching faculty might allow more flexible AI tool use for student lesson plan assignments, while the Counseling faculty could prohibit such use in courses involving patient interactions due to the significant impact on human lives and confidentiality concerns. Beyond our units, the Center for Educational Excellence (CEE), Professional and Continuing Education (PCE), and our provost's office offered various professional development opportunities for our faculty. Despite unit‐level working groups and committees, a common campus concern was the lack of institutional AI guidance. As AI evolves, we wanted to understand how other associate deans are responding to AI's challenges and opportunities, with the hope of offering grounded leadership insights during rapid technological transformation.

Theoretical Framework

We engaged with decolonial theory to understand the implications of AI in higher education, particularly as we consider the diverse onto‐epistemologies of our students, faculty, and administration. Decolonial theory offers a critical framework for understanding the AI implications by situating AI within longer histories of coloniality that continue to shape how knowledge, power, and being are organized in the modern university (Grosfoguel 2013; Maldonado‐Torres 2007; Mignolo 2011; Quijano 2000). It also interrogates the underlying logics of coloniality centering and normalizing Eurocentric epistemologies, rationalist ontologies, and notions of modernity and progress. Here, decolonial onto‐epistemologies are understood as relational, embodied, spiritual, and contextually situated ways of knowing and being that resist the fragmentation of mind, body, and spirit characteristic of Western modernity (Molina, in press).

Jimenez‐Luque et al. (2023) defined onto‐epistemology as, “…a field of study that considers both ontology, the study of the nature of being, existence, and reality, and epistemology, the study of how we come to understand the nature of being, existence, and reality” (p. 9). From this perspective, AI is not neutral, but a system embedded in particular assumptions about intelligence, language, efficiency, and legitimacy. When uncritically adopted in higher education, AI risks reinforcing colonial hierarchies by privileging standardized forms of knowledge, disembodied cognition, and extractive data practices (Couldry and Mejias 2019), while marginalizing relational, multilingual, affective, and community‐based ways of knowing. Noble (2018) argued search engines reinforce racism, specifically for women of color, through what she termed algorithms of oppression, which may be applicable to generative AI models today. Using decolonial theory as an analytic lens enables leaders to examine how AI governance, policy, and everyday pedagogical and administrative practices, such as grading and academic dishonesty referrals, may reproduce epistemic violence and dehumanization of our students.

This study also engages with critical feminist theory (Harding 2004; Hartsock 1983) to analyze the affective leadership labor inherent in AI governance. While the decolonial framework identifies the potential systemic “epistemic violence,” a feminist lens illuminates the invisible, relational work performed by associate deans to mitigate these harms. By centering associate deans’ positionality, we can better understand how they navigate the tension between institutional efficiency and human‐centered ethics of care.

This framework illuminates the ethical, relational, and human consequences of AI decision‐making, positioning leadership not merely as enforcing policy compliance, but as a form of moral and onto‐epistemological stewardship. Ultimately, the onto‐epistemological stewardship exercised by these associate deans serves as a vital feminist intervention. By synthesizing a decolonial critique of data colonization with a feminist commitment to care, these leaders move beyond technical implementation toward a model of leadership that values human connection and epistemic diversity over hierarchical efficiency. As such, in our unit, the Innovations Committee, comprising students, staff, faculty, and administrators, was tasked with developing community guidelines for faculty based on their comfort levels with generative AI.

We recognized the deep, emotional labor that we have engaged in as we supported faculty, students, and our leadership in understanding the tensions inherent in AI usage, but also mediating increasing questions, requests, and concerns by faculty and students surrounding AI usage in the classroom (Hardt 1999). This led to managing levels of ethical tension and expectations of constant availability for consultation, all while operating with care and relationality. While we supported each other, we realized that other associate deans or mid‐level managers might be facing similar challenges alone; therefore, it was important for us to share our experiences and learn from colleagues across the university about how they are managing AI technologies within their units.

Literature Review

AI research in higher education has grown recently, emphasizing topics like AI‐enhanced learning, ethical issues, and institutional preparedness. However, it is not well‐known how leaders, especially at the associate dean level, engage with these advancements. Drawing from recent studies (An et al. 2025) on the use of AI in higher education, this review problematizes a “one‐size‐fits‐all” approach and identifies best practices for AI implementation, including inclusive policy development, transparent communication, and ethical training.

This study also explores different institutional approaches, including centralized versus decentralized strategies, faculty development programs, and AI policy creation, while also exploring tensions that arise, including ethical dilemmas, particularly around academic integrity policies, faculty buy‐in, and resistance. These tensions underscore the complex leadership decisions institutions must make in balancing innovation with institutional values.

The authors would be negligent if we did not address marginalization before discussing how AI is used in higher education, particularly in an American context. Marginalization undergirds much of our society and encompasses inequities of socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, disability, and language. Some refer to marginalization as a phenomenon, but we would argue that marginalization is institutional, even when unconscious or implicit. Brown et al. (2022) claimed addressing diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging are required elements for more meaningful learning and teaching experiences for faculty, staff, and students.

The authors’ own university implemented the Thriving Student Model in 2023, to address a holistic approach to undergraduate student experiences. However, the persistence of systemic inequalities demonstrate how AI's purported neutrality does little to smooth the ragged higher education landscape. Studies suggest when machine learning opportunities require marginalized female users to intentionally draw on diverse cultural experiences, they achieve task completion more successfully than their peers (Jiang et al. 2024). Jiang et al. (2024) study suggested marginalized female students may feel empowered when the AI learning environment invites diverse experiences and backgrounds for knowledge creation. Conceição and Stappen's (2025) study exposed a gap between AI and inclusive practice, saying that opportunities to explicitly design inclusive learning opportunities remain rare. Studies also note that AI's training on non‐representative or negatively biased data, as well as ethical issues in AI algorithms, present significant challenges for AI use, especially amongst marginalized students.

Potential AI Benefits for Students

Numerous studies highlight the benefits of AI for students who are multilingual, neurodiverse, or disabled. For students with a disability, studies show that AI can increase accessibility and support critical thinking and problem‐solving through adaptive learning platforms and AI‐powered tutor agents (Dumitru et al. 2025). Kohnke and Zaugg's (2025) study results demonstrated how AI tools aligned with universal design for learning (UDL) principles can personalize learning and help close achievement gaps for students with disabilities. Adeleye et al. (Adeleye et al. 2024) also emphasized AI‐powered assistive technologies' ability to support accommodations, allowing students with disabilities to participate in learning activities more fully, especially AI‐powered caption and translation tools for those with hearing impairments or those who are multilingual.

Scholars studying AI and multilingual students point to AI‐based translation tools as a key component of enhancing digital equity, accessibility, personalized learning, and cultural responsiveness for multilingual learners (Alzubi 2025; Assefa 2025). While these tools offer significant benefits for multilingual and neurodiverse learners, they also sit at the center of a decolonial tension, where AI can challenge “epistemic racism/sexism” by providing these students with tools to navigate and succeed within the rigid ‘canon of thought’ produced by the five countries Grosfoguel (2013) argued dominate Western knowledge systems, while at the same time, if these tools are used simply to force diverse ways of knowing into a standardized, ‘proper’ Eurocentric mold, they risk reinforcing colonial hierarchies.

However, these scholars also caution against using AI tools without considering UDL principles, sustainability, and ethical frameworks or addressing algorithmic bias or isolated technology design (Alzubi 2025; Assefa 2025). Natural language processing is essential for multilingual learners' use of AI to decrease language barriers (Adeleye et al. 2024; Eden et al. 2024) and support decision‐making through generated insights/summaries and personalized recommendations (Viberg et al. 2024). Viberg et al. (2024) also warned how biased training of AI tools can perpetuate or reinforce existing inequalities, and thus significant investment in inclusive multilingual AI technologies is necessary.

Overall, our literature review of AI use in higher education reveals some key student benefits: (1) personalized or adaptive learning and feedback (Adeleye et al. 2024; Adil 2025; Ayobami O. Ayeni et al. 2023; Eden et al. 2024; Toromade et al. 2024); (2) increased accessibility (Alzubi 2025; Dumitru et al. 2025; Kohnke and Zaugg 2025); and, (3) democratized access to educational resources (Bello and Aubert 2025; Guspian et al. 2024; Wenpang et al. 2025).

Potential AI Challenges for Students

Algorithmic bias is one of the most cited challenges for AI use in higher education, particularly for marginalized students (Adeleye et al. 2024; Conceição and Stappen 2025; Kohnke and Zaugg 2025; Viberg et al. 2024; Wang 2025). Other challenges include inequitable technology access and digital literacy skills training (Adil 2025; Alzubi 2025; Conceição and Stappen 2025; Kayyali 2025); data privacy (Adeleye et al. 2024; Chang et al. 2024; Wang 2025); and perpetuation of insufficient positive representation in AI training data (Assefa 2025; Conceição and Stappen 2025; Viberg et al. 2024).

Industry or practice resources approach AI challenges differently, usually addressing issues of academic integrity, critical thinking skill erosion, lack of AI use flexibility, and AI tools’ inability to adequately identify cultural nuance. The Chronicle of Higher Education, a premier higher education trade publication, has published numerous articles and interviews with faculty, staff, administrators, and students about their AI use and perspectives. McMurtrie (2025) reported, “Professors report a dramatic rise in AI‐generated writing and other forms of misuse (2025, para. 8).” However, some students, overwhelmed by multiple school, work, and personal responsibilities, see AI as, “…a godsend, helping them overcome learning deficits or poorly taught courses,” (McMurtrie 2025, para. 9).ʼʼ

It is important to note that not all students are embracing AI and are reporting similar concerns about learning and critical skill erosion as university faculty. “We are not all uniformly glomming onto AI just because we are young (McMurtrie 2025, para. 39).” Concerns about AI detection tools are causing tension amongst faculty and students as well. Universities are grappling with how much dependence to place on AI detection tools when academic integrity consequences can greatly impact students’ degree progression. Questions continue to linger as we navigate these tensions: Is it possible to enact a system of checks and balances for AI tool use? Who is responsible for doing so in higher education?

Qualitative Case Study Methodology

Associate deans are often tasked with translating institutional vision into actionable practices within their colleges or departments, often without clear roadmaps or precedent. Emerging trends like AI can seemingly change institutional priorities overnight, which then become priorities for associate deans within their respective units. Through qualitative inquiry, this study aims to explore how associate deans across divisions/schools in one institution navigate these emerging trends and changing priorities within their divisions. More specifically, we seek to understand from associate deans: (1) the concerns and inquiries raised by faculty and students within their respective units, (2) the decision‐making processes and corresponding policy responses, if any, undertaken at the unit level, and (3) successes and persisting challenges.

Data Collection Methods

In Fall 2025, we received study approval from the Internal Review Board (IRB‐2026‐33). We sent our Qualtrics survey consisting of 11 questions to 19 associate deans and assistant deans with academic responsibilities across our university. In addition to two demographic questions, the remaining questions centered around three primary areas: (1) questions and concerns raised by administration, faculty and students within their units since the emergence of ChatGPT and other AI tools; (2) the decision‐making processes and policy responses, if any, that are implemented at the unit level in response to these emerging technologies; and (3) the successes achieved and challenges that persist in addressing these issues.

We received seven responses, yielding a 36.8% response rate (see Table 1 below). Of the nine academic units with eligible survey participants, five (55.5%) responded. All three of Copley Library's associate deans responded to the survey, which saturates the unit's representation in the data analysis. Despite this saturation, the unit representation and overall participant response rates of this exploratory case study indicate a good qualitative sampling from our university.

Data Analysis Process

Both authors independently reviewed the Qualtrics survey responses, coding for thematic patterns and exceptions. Initial codes (adoption, engagement, policy, and implementation) were developed from our research questions. Additional tools, such as Dedoose and Microsoft Copilot, aided the authors’ data analysis process. Then the authors met to compare notes on the analysis. This dual framework of decolonial and feminist theories allowed for a nuanced analysis of how associate deans across multiple units in our university navigate tensions and perspectives on AI integration in higher education.

Limitations

While this study offers critical insights into the leadership experiences of associate deans, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the small sample size (n = 7) provides a snapshot of one medium‐sized, Catholic R2 university and cannot be generalized to the broader higher education landscape. Second, the study was conducted during a period of extreme AI flux, and the technological tools and institutional responses available during data collection may have shifted by publication time. Most importantly, because AI implementation is highly context‐dependent, there is no immediate solution or universal roadmap to derive from the findings.

Findings

This section is structured to address the core objectives of the study: (a) the primary concerns and inquiries raised by faculty and students regarding AI, (b) the current status of decision‐making processes and unit‐level policy responses, and (c) the successes achieved and the persistent challenges facing academic leaders in this transition. Drawing on quantitative metrics and qualitative insights, these findings illustrate how leaders are navigating generative AI's ethical, pedagogical, and administrative implications.

This study engaged nine academic leaders serving as associate deans across a variety of functional areas, including Faculty and Operations, Undergraduate Studies, Research and Instruction Engagement, Student Success, and the School of Nursing. The participant pool consists of a relatively new leadership group, with six participants having been in their current roles for five years or less, while the other three have between five and nine years of experience. Except for three associate deans participating from the Copley Library, all other units had one representative.

Several themes spanned all units (see Table 2). First, respondents consistently highlighted the absence of a clear institutional vision or policy framework. Even units with relatively advanced practices expressed uncertainty about the boundaries of ethical use. Second, academic integrity concerns were universal, though interpreted differently: Library and education leaders emphasized the difficulty of attribution and transparency, whereas arts and sciences foregrounded rising violations and pedagogical threat. Third, calls for professional development were consistent, reflecting a shared recognition that faculty and staff require structured opportunities to build AI literacy in a rapidly evolving landscape. Finally, several respondents articulated concerns about inequity, ranging from unequal student access to paid AI tools to the reproduction of racialized, colonial, or otherwise exclusionary biases in AI outputs.

Personal and Professional AI Engagement

Administrative efficiencies suggested a straightforward tool adoption, but the actual implementation reveals a complex “human element” requiring significant emotional labor from associate deans. This labor is not uniform; it shifts based on the unit's orientation toward technology. For instance, while deans in the Copley Library act as proactive stewards managing the “necessity” of early adoption, deans in the Arts and Sciences must navigate a different kind of emotional labor: mediating deep‐seated faculty resistance rooted in concerns over the “stunting” of critical thinking and liberal arts values. Participants mainly utilized AI tools to streamline administrative processes. Common uses include improving email tone, creating announcements, and summarizing technical instructions for system setups. Leaders also employ these technologies for teaching and research, such as generating rubrics, designing interactive learning activities, and finding key works for research. One leader noted their role as an “early tech adopter,” actively testing platforms like Research Rabbit, Perplexity, and various library‐specific AI features to help faculty and students navigate the changing landscape.

Copley Library appears as the highest campus adopter out of necessity. Resource vendors quickly adopted and embedded AI tools, like research assistants, in scholarly databases and publisher platforms, almost forcing early library AI adoption. Vendor contracts strongly influence the library's stewardship of subscription resources; thus, the library needed to quickly develop campus‐wide AI policies for the use of licensed materials. Historically, academic librarians are also charged with quickly understanding and implementing emerging technologies and leading training for the rest of the university.

Professional schools, such as Nursing and Business, displayed an adoption trend and policy implementation strongly influenced by their external industry peers, which normalized AI use in industry practice more quickly than those in the humanities. Business was particularly concerned with using AI to remain competitive, increase task efficiency, and reduce redundancy. Likewise, nursing cited clinical alignment as a motivation for AI adoption. The industry alignment also encouraged deans and faculty to use more permissive (low/low‐moderate) AI use policies in courses.

SOLES's interdisciplinary nature strongly influences AI adoption with varying levels amongst the departments. Like Nursing and Business, whether a SOLES department adopts AI is strongly influenced by external industry practices. Learning and Teaching's willingness to adopt AI often referenced K‐12's AI use in curriculum development. However, AI adoption was not fully embraced without concern, even amongst the earliest or highest adopters. Participants mentioned concerns about the loss of students’ critical thinking, writing proficiency, and technical skills due to overreliance or dependency on AI tools. Our counseling department's reticence stemmed from concerns about client privacy and confidentiality. SOLES's policies needed multilevel options to accommodate differing department uses beyond existing institutional guidelines (moderate‐high).

Academic Integrity, Ethics, and the Human Element

While recognizing AI's benefits, leaders expressed significant reservations regarding its long‐term impact on academic rigor. A primary concern is the potential for “hallucinations” (inaccurate AI outputs) and a general over‐reliance on technology that could erode students' critical thinking and writing skills. Specific professional anxieties include: (a) privacy and surveillance, citing concerns regarding personal writing samples being used as training data without consent, (b) academic dishonesty, citing concerns regarding increasing challenges of distinguishing between original student work and AI‐generated content in both coursework and admissions materials, (c) environmental and social impact where participants reported ethical concerns regarding the massive energy requirements of data centers and the deepening digital divide between well‐resourced and underfunded institutions, and (d) shifts in pedagogy and assessment, where the data suggests that while institutional‐level shifts are still in their infancy, individual faculty members are already pivoting their classroom strategies.

Some of the approaches used included “AI proofing” or shifting assessments to oral, in‐person activities and authentic learning, inverting where faculty use AI to generate lessons or drafts and have students, in turn, critically analyze those artifacts for accuracy and bias. Participants reported widespread adoption of formal AI usage statements in course syllabi to establish policy and clear ethical expectations.

A significant finding is the lack of a formal policy framework: Seven participants noted that neither their institution nor their specific unit has implemented official AI policies. This creation of localized unit guidelines functions, in a sense, as a feminist ethics of care (Gilligan 1982; Noddings 2013). Instead of implementing top‐down measures for rigid compliance, our leaders prioritized relationality, developing measures that protect the most vulnerable members of their academic communities from the precarity triggered by the sudden influx of generative AI.

The findings revealed AI implementation has significantly increased the invisible labor of Associate Deans. This affective dimension of institutional labor, the work of soothing faculty fears, managing ethical dilemmas, and fostering community resilience, is often gendered and undervalued in traditional administrative metrics. Viewed through a critical feminist lens, this “institutional housekeeping” (Bird et al. 2004) enables the university to continue functioning amid technological disruption, yet it remains largely unquantified in formal leadership evaluations. Policy discussions are most heavily influenced by ethical considerations and faculty concerns. Perhaps most tellingly, all participants surveyed in this study identified professional development and training as the most critical resource for navigating this transition effectively.

Reflecting a deeper concern for equity, several leaders viewed AI implementation through the lens of existing power structures. One participant noted that AI tools often “mine” content without acknowledgment, reinforcing colonial patterns of extraction. Another participant emphasized that because these technologies are often built by non‐marginalized groups, their implementation can be “weaponized” against historically excluded communities who have been largely excluded from the technology's creative and developmental phases. She wrote, My cultural values are more immediately relevant to my approach to AI. As a member of multiple marginalized and historically excluded groups, I have observed too many times in which tech or policy adoption/implementation is weaponized against my people. There are also few paths for creative participation or modification. The technology is often built by people who don't look like me nor do they have my people in mind.

This weaponization is a direct manifestation of what Ramón Grosfoguel (2013) described as the “geopolitics of knowledge,” a system where the “canon of thought” is dictated by a narrow set of Westernized perspectives while marginalizing the rest of the world. When AI models are built on data that excludes marginalized voices, they reinforce this colonial structure of knowledge. Consequently, the work of associate deans in governing these tools becomes more than administrative; it is a form of ‘moral and onto‐epistemological stewardship’ that requires active resistance against the further extraction and erasure of localized ways of knowing.

This “weaponization” is particularly evident in the use of AI detection tools, which Liang et al. (2023) showed disproportionately flag multilingual writers. From a decolonial perspective, this is a modern form of “epistemic violence” that punishes students for “restricted linguistic diversity,” effectively pathologizing any way of being or knowing that doesn't align with Eurocentric, standardized norms. For neurodiverse learners and multilingual students, the administrative decision to rely on such tools is not just a policy choice. It is an act of digital structural violence that reinforces who is seen as a legitimate ‘knower’ or knowledge holder in the academy.

The library participants also surfaced governance issues that were largely invisible to other units: (1) the risk of exporting licensed content into AI systems, (2) the emergence of AI features within publisher platforms without institutional consent, and (3) the need for additional layers of contract and privacy review. These concerns directly shaped new unit‑level policies around resource licensing and AI‑supported instructional tools. The library functions not only as an AI steward but also in copyright and privacy stewardship. Though still in litigation, copyright considerations for AI are a closely monitored issue for librarians, who are often charged with campus copyright training and course‐level guideline interpretation. The library participants were notably concerned about users unintentionally violating copyright by using AI for course assignments or research synthesis and analysis.

Recommendations: Creating “Communities of Practice”

Given the significant findings regarding the “lack of institutional guidance,” we recommend that universities move away from rigid, top‐down policies that often fail to account for the unique pedagogical needs of different disciplines. Instead, institutions should foster “communities of practice,” collaborative spaces where faculty, staff, and students can navigate AI implementation together. This approach aligns with a decolonial framework of “developing in community”. shifting the focus from universalizing mandates to contextually situated, relational ways of knowing and governing technology. Three actionable recommendations we propose include centering pedagogy, sustaining leadership through relational support, and building equitable infrastructure and policies.

Centering Pedagogy and Care Over Punitive Approaches

We must first address the space where students and faculty most frequently collide with technology: The classroom. In response to the limitations of current technological surveillance, institutions should encourage faculty to move away from standardized writing assignments easily replicated by AI and often disadvantage multilingual or neurodiverse learners. By implementing ‘authentic assessments,’ such as oral examinations or reflective journals, educators can better value localized and personal ways of knowing. AI should be reframed as a tool for critical inquiry; students can use these platforms to generate artifacts, which they can then analyze for inherent bias, hallucinations, and Eurocentric perspectives. This transition shifts the student from a passive consumer to a critical ‘knower’ capable of identifying and resisting ‘epistemic violence’ within the technology itself.

Sustaining Leadership Through Relational Support

To address the significant “emotional labor” and “moral stewardship” identified in this study, universities must establish dedicated peer‐support networks for associate deans and mid‐level leaders. These spaces allow administrators to navigate the ethical tensions of AI governance without the typical professional silos. Institutional governance should be intentionally interdisciplinary, ensuring that unit‐level working groups include voices from the library and humanities. This collaborative oversight ensures that AI implementation is not driven solely by administrative efficiency but is also informed by “copyright, privacy stewardship,” and the preservation of liberal arts values.

Building Equitable Infrastructure and Policy

Institutional commitment must also extend to providing equitable access to high‐quality, paid AI tools, ensuring that students from underfunded backgrounds are not further marginalized by a deepening digital divide or “extractive data practices.” Professional development should move beyond generic technical training and instead offer “contextually situated” guidance that respects the specific pedagogical values of diverse disciplines. Finally, rather than pursuing one‐size‐fits‐all mandates, universities should adopt “living documents” and iterative guidelines. This relational approach to policy allows the community to respond reflexively to the “rapidly changing field” of AI while remaining anchored in the institution's core mission and values.

Conclusion

We aimed to provide key insights and recommendations for institutional leaders, policymakers, and educators in higher education settings. This study's significance lies in its focus on the mid‐level leaders who are truly the “boots on the ground” in higher education. Associate deans are not just administrative liaisons; they are the essential translators who must convert abstract, high‐level AI ethics and institutional directives into actual classroom practices. By highlighting their role as “moral and onto‐epistemological stewards,” this study empowers these leaders to see their emotional and administrative labor as a vital form of advocacy for their faculty and students in the face of rapid technological change.

Future studies can further develop this initial exploratory work by including a broader variety of institutional types, like large public research universities or community colleges. We encourage scholars and practitioners to come together as a community to develop “communities of practice” that can respond reflexively to these emerging issues, ensuring that the future of AI in higher education is shaped by relationality and equity rather than just administrative efficiency and “one‐size‐fits‐all” solutions.
