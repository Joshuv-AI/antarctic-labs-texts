Expressing entropy and cross-entropy in expansions of common meadows
Jan A Bergstra

Abstract
A common meadows is an enrichment of a field with a partial division operation that is made total by assuming that division by zero takes the a default value, a special element ⊥ \bot adjoined to the field. To a common meadow of real numbers we add a binary logarithm log 2 ⁡ ( − ) \log_{2}(-) , which we also assume to be total with log 2 ( p ) = ⊥ \log_{2}(p)=\bot for p ≤ 0 p\leq 0 . With these and other auxiliary operations, such as a sign function 𝗌 ⁡ ( − ) \mathsf{s}(-) , we form algebras over which entropy and cross entropy can be defined for probability mass functions on a finite sample space by algebraic formulae that are simple terms built from the operations of the algebras and without case distinctions or conventions to avoid partiality. The discuss the advantages of algebras based on common meadows, whose theory is established, and alternate methods to define entropy and other information measures completely for all arguments using single terms.



Keywords and phrases
:
meadows, common meadows, partiality, entropy, cross entropy, terms, conditional operators, auxiliary operators

1
Introduction

1.1
Information theory.

We will investigate how entropy and cross-entropy, and other information theoretic qualities, can be defined uniformly and completely for all arguments by formulae or algebraic expressions over algebras of reals numbers. By a formula, or algebraic expression, we have in mind a single term built from names for the basic operators of an algebra.

Consider the entropy of a probability mass function
P
on a finite sample space
V
as given by

H(P)=\sum_{x\in V}(P(x)\cdot\log_{2}\frac{1}{P(x)}).

Now, in case
P(x)=0
for some
x\in V
, the expression for entropy contains a subterm
\frac{1}{P(0)}
that is not defined. Since
P(x)=0
is a reasonable value the definition is not complete. An alternate formulation for entropy is

H(P)=-\sum_{x\in S}(P(x)\cdot\log_{2}P(x)).

Here,
P(x)=0
means that the subterm
\log_{2}P(x)
is not defined.

In most practice, the facts that the formulae do not completely define the measures are not considered problematic. Rather than introduce case distinctions, these definitions are finished off – indeed, the second definition is preserved – by adopting a convention, namely that
0\cdot\log_{2}0=0
. Note that such a convention is an equation that might be true or false of some underlying algebra of reals with the operators
\cdot
and
\log_{2}
.

A closely related concept is cross entropy defined for two probability mass functions, say
P
and
Q
, by

H(P,Q)=\sum_{x\in S}(P(x)\cdot\log_{2}\frac{1}{Q(x)})

Alternately, it can be formulated as

H(P,Q)=-\sum_{x\in S}(P(x)\cdot\log_{2}Q(x)).

In most practice, conventions in use here include the use of a special element added to the real numbers, namely
\infty
.

The enforcement of conventions such as an equation
0\cdot\log_{2}0=0
and the addition of a new element
\infty
, have implications for the algebra and logic of the information measures. Here we seek answers to the question:

Question 1.1
.

What algebras of real numbers would allow entropy and cross entropy to be defined uniformly and completely for all arguments by formulae that are single terms over the operations of the algebras?

Note that since
H(P)=H(P,P)
, we expect if cross entropy is definable then so is entropy.

We have explored this question of definability elsewhere. In
[
12
]
, we developed an algebra
A
of real numbers in which the convention
0\cdot\log_{2}0=0
is an equation true of
A
; this algebra
A
we called the
entropic transreals
. The entropic transreals are a modified version of the establised transreals of
[
1
,
2
,
19
,
20
,
7
]
. Unfortunately, entropic transreals have non-associative multiplication, and lack an important algebraic property of division (
viz.
fracterm flattening), both facts giving reasons for looking for expressions of entropy and cross-entropy in other structures for real arithmetic with more conventional properties.

Here, we will examine the problem in a fundamental way, by choosing to start with common meadows, whose theory is established and
based on insights into the semantics, algebra and logic, and practical use of abstract data types for computer calculation.

1.2
Totality and term definability

Specifically, the technical problems arise because the key operations are partial:
\frac{x}{0}
is not defined and
\log_{2}{x}
is not defined for
x\leq 0
. Thus, to avoid these undefined arguments, which disturbs the formulae and suggests problem
1.1
, a conditional branching operator seems necessary.

Our previous studies of totality
v.
partiality in algebras have focussed on division
\frac{x}{y}
: by adding this partial operator to the field of reals we obtain the
meadow
of real numbers
[
6
]
. Having studied a number of semantic options for making partial functions total (see
[
10
]
), one has stood out.
We add a special element
\bot
, define
\frac{x}{0}=\bot
, and how
\bot
interacts with the operations on the reals. The standard approach to the algebra of

is to make it absorptive. This constructs a

of real numbers.
Thus, common meadows are our starting point for this problem.

Division is so basic it can be considered

among functions that need to be added to the field operations. For the purposes of our problem with information measures, we must add
\log_{2}
to the common meadow of real numbers. This produces an algebra of the form

\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}\ |\ 0,1,\bot,x+y,-x,x\cdot y,\frac{x}{y},\log_{2}(x))

Here, to make the algebra total, we set
\log_{2}(x)=\bot
for
x\leq 0
. Clearly, this algebra has the all operators that appear in the standard formulae above. We confirm there is a problem (Proposition
4.1
below):

Theorem.

The algebra

fails to allow entropy

for a sample space of size
n=2
to be defined by a term over

.

Then, to search for uniform and complete formulae for entropy and cross entropy, we will add more operations
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
to this basic algebra
\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}
and examine the problem for several algebras of the form

\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}\ |\ 0,1,\bot,x+y,-x,x\cdot y,\frac{x}{y},\log_{2}(x),f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}).

Among the additional operations, we focus on adding a conditional operator, a new multiplication operator, and a sign function, which simulates an ordering.

2
Totality and the common meadows

We summarise the algebraic ideas we need.

2.1
On the default peripheral value
\bot

Consider an algebra enlarged with a special element
\bot
, which may be read as an ‘unconventional outcome’. We will refer to it as a
default peripheral value
(DPV); ‘default’ because it can be used as an output by operators that fail to give a meaningful value for some reason, and as ‘peripheral’ as it is extraneous to the algebra. Mathematically, its primary role is to totalise functions that otherwise would be partial. However, on introducing
\bot
to an existing algebra we must define how
\bot
interacts with its operations. A general method is to make
\bot

absorptive
, adapting the operations to return
\bot
whenever
\bot
is an argument. This general technique is worked out in
[
9
]
.

The advantage of total functions over partial functions is the relative simplicity of working with the algebra and logic of total functions, in comparison with working with algebras and logics for partial functions. Appreciation of this advantage can be a controversial matter, but we hold that a first order logic of total functions is significantly simpler and applicable than any of its various counterparts involving logics of partial functions.

The general idea of a DPV is intended to be adaptable to contexts. Now, many functions that are partial acquire conventions to complete their definition that are not seen to be
ad hoc
. Certainly, the element
\infty
is commonly employed: for example, since we know
\lim\frac{1}{x}\to\infty
as
x\to 0
we have a reason to choose to totalise division by setting
\frac{1}{0}=\infty
. Further, we can have the intuition that an infinity can be absorptive: operations return
\infty
whenever
\infty
is an argument. However, adding a new element to an algebra with some desired intuition requires careful consideration of how the new element interacts with all the operations of the algebra. So, if
\infty
is a renaming of
\bot
then we define
-\infty=\infty
(and we do not have
+\infty
and
-\infty
). Using this
\infty
instead of
\bot
is just
one
form of infinity; it can be called a
pure absorptive infinity
. Infinity can interact with the operations in other ways even in a common meadow: we will return to this topic in Section
8.2
.

Clearly, in such circumstances we might choose to use the symbol
\infty
to do the algebraic work that
\bot
is designed to do,
viz.
when a natural ordinary value for a term
t
cannot be found we have
t=\bot
. To consider absorptive infinity in place of
\bot
is an option for the definitions of entropy and cross entropy in this paper, since the symbol
\infty
appears in some conventions to be found in information theory. It is not an option we will take.

Below we will use
\bot
instead of
\infty
as all arguments are best understood with the intuition that
f(a)=\bot
expresses that within
\mathbb{R}
,
f(a)
is undefined. This notational difference is strictly speaking unnecessary because as a function
f(-)
is immune for a change of name of
\infty
. We will use
H_{\bot}(P)
and
H_{\bot}(P,Q)
for entropy and cross-entropy but such that the outcome will be
\bot
rather than
\infty
if the outcome is a peripheral number.

In
8.2
, we provide a brief survey of peripherals for infinite values, with a naming convention for these peripherals.

2.2
An algebra of real numbers for entropy definitions

There are dozens of functions on the real numbers that are essential for applications and yet are naturally partial.
A most obvious case is division
\frac{x}{y}
: by adding this as a basic operation to the field we obtain the
meadow
of real numbers
[
6
]
. Since division is partial at
x=0
we add the peripheral value
\bot
to make it total, i.e.,
\frac{x}{0}=\bot
.
Assume that a field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers is given with the classic operations
[
27
]
:

x+y,\ x\cdot y,\ -x.

The peripheral
\bot
interacts with these operations and division using the general absorptive method, adapted to the operations, such that

-\bot=x+\bot=\bot+x=x\cdot\bot=\bot\cdot x=\frac{x}{\bot}=\frac{\bot}{x}=\bot

This constructs the
common meadow
of real numbers:

\mathbb{R}_{\bot}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}\ |\ 0,1,\bot,x+y,-x,x\cdot y,\frac{x}{y}).

Common meadows as a general construction for fields were introduced in
[
5
]
. The equational axiomatisations and the equational theory of common meadows is analysed in
[
8
,
10
,
11
]
.

To proceed, we now add the partial function
\log_{2}(x)
.

\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}\ |\ 0,1,\bot,x+y,-x,x\cdot y,\frac{x}{y},\log_{2}(x)).

The problem we posed in the Introduction, Question
1.1
, can be stated precisely as follows:

Question 2.1
.

Can the information-theoretic measures of entropy and cross entropy be defined uniformly for all inputs as single terms over this algebra
A=\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}
of reals, or over some of its expansions?

3
Defining information measures as terms

Here we make a first attempt at defining the measures.

3.1
Preliminaries on terms

Our work in this paper is founded upon concepts and mehods from abstract data type theory: signatures, terms, equations etc.
[
21
]
. The general question in the Introduction is a natural question to do with data types, for the technical Question
2.1
has the classic form of a hidden operator problem in data type theory.

However, in order to keep close to the familiar notions and formulae of entropy, we will work without being explicit about syntax and semantics, which are core to analysing data types. The users of information theory are comfortable with this informal way. At some places it may be helpful to remember the role of syntax in making arguments about terms more precise; we trust the step-up in formality will be easy when needed.

Now, mindful of the classic formulae, we must make a remark on general summations. Let
t
be a term over a signature with
+
; suppose
+
is both commutative and associative. The construction
\sum_{x\in V}t
is available for any finite set
V
as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

For a given finite set
V
of cardinality
n=|V|
, with enumeration
without repetitions
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, with tha
a_{i}
closed terms, the semantics of
\sum_{x\in V}t
is given by the term

\sum_{x\in V}t=[a_{1}/x]t+[a_{2}/x]t+\ldots+[a_{n}/x]t.

This construction is independent of the choice of the enumeration because addition is both commutative and associative. We will refer to this construction as a
finitary generalized sum
.

3.2
Additional operators: conditional and left-sequential multiplication

One approach to integrating the exceptional case of
P(x)=0
is to add operations to the algebra of reals that can create a larger class of terms with which case distinctions can be made.

Definition 3.2
.

A conditional operator
x\lhd y\rhd z
uses as the test
y=0
and is defined by:

if
y=0
then
x\lhd y\rhd z=z
;

if
y\neq 0
and
y\neq\bot
then

x\lhd y\rhd z=x
; and

if
y=\bot
then
x\lhd\bot\rhd z=\bot
.

Notice that the conditional operator compromises the absorptive character of
\bot
as
\bot\!\lhd 0\rhd 1=1
rather than
\bot\!\lhd 0\rhd 1=\bot
.

Using the conditional we can define a new ‘multiplication’ operation on the reals.

Definition 3.3
.

The operator
x~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}y
, which we call the left-sequential multiplication, or simply sequential multiplication, is defined by

x~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}y=(x\cdot y)\lhd x\rhd 0.

The idea is that the left (first) argument of a product is evaluated first and is used to obtain a result.
If
x=0
then
x~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}y=0
, or if
x\neq 0
then
x~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}y=x\cdot y
.

3.3
Entropy and cross-entropy terms with conditionals

Definition 3.4
.

A probability mass function on a sample space
V
is a function
P:V\to\mathbb{R}
with (i)
\forall_{x\in V}P(x)\geq 0
and (ii)
\sum_{x\in V}P(x)=1
.

With the algebraic operations of
3.2
, we construct the algebra

\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2},~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}\ |\ 0,1,\bot,x+y,-x,x\cdot y,\frac{x}{y},\log_{2}(x),x~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}y).

It is easy to check that with a conditional to hand the classical formulae can be made into terms, notably using the operator
~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}
:

Proposition 3.1
.

The Shannon entropy
H(P)
of a probability mass function
P
can be defined as terms over
A

H(P)=\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}(\log_{2}\frac{1}{P(x)})

Equivalently, Shannon entropy can be defined by the term:

H(P)=-\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}P(x)

Cross entropy is given by:

H(P,Q)=\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}\frac{1}{Q(x)}

or equivalently:

H(P,Q)=-\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}Q(x).

Proof.

We consider one of the measures for illustration, in particular the second definition of entropy:

H(P)=-(\sum_{x\in V,P(x)>0}P(x)\cdot\log_{2}P(x)~+\sum_{x\in V,P(x)=0}0)=-\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}P(x).

∎

Example 3.1
.

With
\mathbb{R}_{\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}/\bot}

.
Upon expansion with

, and with left sequential multiplication
~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}

\mathbb{R}_{\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}/\bot,\log_{2},\ ~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}}
. Assume that for

Then
~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}
works as follows:

a~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}b=0

We note that under these conditions,

Example 3.2
.

Suppose
P
and
Q
are probability mass functions with the property that for some
x\in V
,
P(x)=\frac{1}{2}
and
Q(x)=0
. Then

P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}\frac{1}{Q(x)}=\frac{1}{2}~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}\frac{1}{0}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log_{2}\frac{1}{0}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log_{2}\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}=\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}

It follows that the presence of an infinite value

,
(or

is not only relevant for totalising division and logarithm but that totalisation also

\infty_{\mathsf{cmu}}
(or
\bot
)

For a function
F:V\to\mathbb{R}
from a finite collection of atomic events

\overline{E}^{V}_{P}(F)=\sum_{x\in V}P(x)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}F(x).

(i) if for some

4
The need for auxiliary operators

A meadow extended with the addition of
\log_{2}
has all the functions needed to make the classical information formulae, but the algebra
\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}
cannot avoid the partiality problem, even with its total operations.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A=\mathbb{R}_{\bot,\log_{2}}
. Then
A
does not allow entropy
H^{V}_{\bot}(P)
of a probability mass function
P
for a sample space
V
of size
n=2
to be defined by a term over
A
.

Proof.

Let the sample space
V=\{0,1\}
. Then

H^{V}_{\bot}(P)=P(0)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}P(0)+(1-P(0))~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}(1-P(0)).

Introducing variable
p
, an expression for entropy for size
n=2
amounts to the existence of
an term
t
over the signature
\Sigma
of
A
such that

t(p)=p~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}p+(1-p)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}(1-p),

for all
p\in[0,1]
. Suppose such a term exists.

We now consider the situation in terms of conventional analysis, without the element
\bot
and where division and logarithm are partial functions on real numbers. Then, viewing
t
as a partial function on
\mathbb{R}
one notices that it is made up of a composition of functions, all of which are differentiable on all arguments in their respective domains. In particular, both for division and
\log_{2}
we notice that the domain is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}
, not including
0
, and within the respective domains both functions are continuous and derivable.

It follows that
t(-)
understood as a partial function on
\mathbb{R}
is differentiable whenever defined. In particular
t(p)
, which takes value
0
for
p=0
by definition of entropy, must be differentiable for
p=0
. The function

h(-)=(1-p)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}(1-p)
is differentiable for
p=1
so it follows that
t(-)-h(-)=p~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}p
is differentiable in
0
.

Thus, it follows that

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{(p+h)~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}(p+h)-p~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}p}{h}

must exist within
\mathbb{R}
for
p=0
so that

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{h~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}h-0~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}\log_{2}0}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{h\cdot\log_{2}h-0}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{h\cdot\log_{2}h}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\log_{2}h

exists within
\mathbb{R}
, which is not the case, thereby completing the proof.
∎

5
Expressions for entropy and cross-entropy

The algebraic property of flattening

Extending fields to common meadows, and then common meadows with further operations opens up many questions about their general algebraic properties. The case of common meadows has been analysed using axiomatisations but here we are in the business of adding several more operators, including familiar (
\log_{2},\mathsf{s}

~\mathbin{\begin{picture}(-0.3,0.0)\put(-0.6,0.0){$\times$}\put(-0.46,-0.0){\circle{0.4}}\end{picture}}

As a tool for the specification of entropy and cross-entropy this paper uses left sequential multiplication

8.1
Fields, meadows, and expansions

Most useful functions on the real numbers are approximable by polynomials, thanks to Weierstrass’s Theorem,
1
1

1

A continuous function on a compact interval can be approximated to arbitrary precision by a polynomial.
which makes the field of real numbers a truly fundamental algebraic structure for mathematics and its applications. Recall that the field of real numbers has constants
0
and
1
, and operations
x+y,-x,x\cdot y
.
and that polynomials are normal forms for terms over these operations. However, an approximation of a function is not an exact and complete characterisation (though the approximation process via power series generate one). Many functions are known not to be definable as polynomials, i.e., by terms over fields, including elementary functions such as
\frac{x}{y},\sqrt{x},\sin(x),\cos(x),\tan(x),e^{x},\log(x),\ldots
etc.
Yet, such special functions appear in important formulae everywhere in pure and applied mathematics. The point is that the neat, understandable, classic formulae and equations of mathematics and its applications are made of terms and that, in turn, they are made from the operations of algebras of reals.

To allow formulae to be constructed that specify functions exactly, special functions can be added as operators to the field operations and composed to make larger classes of terms. The algebraic and logical methods that underpin computing deal with terms made from these operators whose inter-relationships are analysed axiomatically. This is the approach of abstract data types
[
21
]
.

An early algebraic appreciation of classes of elementary functions was made by G H Hardy in
[
22
]
. Hardy defines an
elementary function
to be a member of the class of functions that comprises:

(i) rational functions

(ii) algebraical functions, explicit or implicit
2
2

2

Here explicit or implicit includes root extraction (explicit) and solutions of polynomial equations (implicit).

(iii) the exponential function
e^{x}

(iv) the logarithmic function
log\ x

(v) all functions which can be defined by means of any finite combination of the symbols proper to the preceding four classes of functions.

Hardy’s monograph addresses the questions:

If f(x) is an elementary function, how can we determine whether its integral is also an elementary function? If the integral is an elementary function, how can we find it?

Problems of these kinds for different, simpler classes of functions were studied by Richardson who proved that the integration of functions in his class
is undecidable
[
23
]
. Adding operators to fields certainly complicates the logical theories of the extended structures, as Tarski’s Exponential Problem for real fields demonstrates.

8.3
Adding signed infinities to a signed common meadow

The common meadow of reals
\mathbb{R}_{\bot}

With the understanding that addition and multiplication are commutative and associative in

\infty_{\mathsf{cmp}}+\infty_{\mathsf{cmp}}=\infty_{\mathsf{cmp}}\cdot\infty_{\mathsf{cmp}}=\infty_{\mathsf{cmp}}

∎

Finally, information theory is based on probability theory, which reminds us that elementary probability theory is itself an interesting area for applying algebras with total operators and peripherals. We briefly consider conditional probability and the Bayes-Price Theorem in

Proposition 8.1
.

In a common meadow of reals
\mathbb{R}_{\bot}
,

P(A)\neq 0\vee P(B)=0\to P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}.

Proof.

To see that the formula needs a disjunctive condition

P(A)\neq 0

P(B)\neq 0

is obvious so that
the only cases requiring attention are with

and/or

If

\frac{P(B|A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}=\frac{(\frac{0}{0})\cdot 0}{p}=\bot

Sufficiency of the condition is as follows: (i) if

then for all values of
