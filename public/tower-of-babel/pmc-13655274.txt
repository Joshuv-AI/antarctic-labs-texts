The Effect of Training Exercise on Cartilage Zonal Composition and Structure at Two Differently Loaded Sites in the Juvenile Equine Metacarpophalangeal Joint
Authors: Michael J. S. Duggan, Margot C. Labberté, René van Weeren, Reinout Stoop, Mika M. Hyttinen, Jarno Rieppo, Jeroen de Groot, Pieter. A. J. Brama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate the depth related spatial distribution of biochemical and structural components in articular cartilage at different topographical sites experiencing different loading within the juvenile equine metacarpophalangeal joint in two groups of foals subjected to different exercise conditions.MethodsSixteen Warmblood foals were included in the study and were randomly assigned into 1 of 2 exercise groups (box rest and box rest plus training) during the first 5 months of life and subsequently euthanized. Osteochondral plugs were harvested from two sites which have previously been shown to experience different load levels and sectioned into 100 μm thick slices. Cartilage slices were analysed for glycosaminoglycan, DNA, and total collagen content, as well as content of hydroxylysine residues (HYL) in collagen and the collagen cross-link Hydroxylysylpyridinoline (HP). Collagen fibril architecture (Orientation Angle and Parallelism Index) was determined using quantitative polarized light microscopy.ResultsThe constantly loaded site 2 exhibited higher glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and HP content with HYL higher in the superficial and middle layers of the trained group, while the intermittently peak loaded site 1 showed greater collagen accumulation in deeper layers but lower cross-link density. Structural analyses (Oa and PI) confirmed that collagen fibril orientation and alignment adapt regionally, with training enhancing fibril organization in superficial zones.ConclusionThis study illustrates that mechanical loading is a key driver of functional adaptation, resulting in distinct zonal and topographical differences in the biochemical and structural composition of equine cartilage at 5 months.

1. Introduction

Articular hyaline cartilage covers the surfaces of bones within synovial joints, providing a smooth, low-friction, and wear-resistant surface essential for load distribution in diarthrodial joint function. Despite its simple appearance, articular cartilage possesses a complex structure with multifaceted interactions between the chondrocytes and the extracellular matrix (ECM). 1 The ECM is composed predominantly of collagen, proteoglycans and water. Articular cartilage is arranged into four zones with variations in density and arrangement of key structural elements.2-5 These zones are referred to as; superficial, transitional (middle), deep (radial) and calcified.3,5 It is this complex composition and architecture that affords articular cartilage its functional properties.

The superficial zone constitutes the upper 10–20% of the cartilage thickness, within which, fine collagen fibrils are aligned parallel to the articular surface and densely packed.4,6,7 Chondrocytes in this portion of the cartilage are typically abundant and flat in shape. 8 The superficial zone has the highest water and lowest proteoglycan content which, combined with the low collagen fibril constraint, play a key role in the zone’s swelling potential.3,9 The middle zone makes up 40–60% of the total thickness with thicker collagen fibres that are generally arranged in a random pattern; however, a tendency to orient at 45° has been reported. 10 The chondrocytes located in the middle zone are significantly greater in volume and surface area compared with those in the superficial zone 8 but are less densely packed. Proteoglycan content tends to increase through the middle zone with some reports suggesting it has the highest level of proteoglycans11,12 whilst others suggest the highest content is within the deep zone.13,14 The deep zone represents 30% of the cartilage thickness; here collagen fibres interweave to form large bundles oriented perpendicularly to the surface, and penetrating the tidemark into the deeper calcified region. These perpendicular fibres provide anchorage to the subchondral bone 15 and, despite the osmotic potential provided by the proteoglycan content, 16 the high collagen constraint restricts the swelling potential and subsequently results in a lower water content. In general, the collagen fibril diameter increases from superficial to deep whilst the inverse is true for cell density8,17 and water, which tend to decrease.

It is well recognised that loading is a critically important aspect of cartilage development, driving the development of topographical heterogeneity.18-23 The aim of this study was to investigate the depth related spatial distribution of biochemical and structural components in articular cartilage at two separate locations within the equine metacarpophalangeal joint which have previously been shown to experience different load levels associated with constant weight bearing and intermittent peak loading during exercise 24 under different exercise conditions (box rest and box rest plus training).

2. Materials & Methods

2.1. Study Design

Sixteen Warmblood foals (8 male, 8 female) were included in the study (Figure 1). The foals were allowed paddock turnout with the mares from birth until one week of age. After which, they were randomly assigned into 1 of 2 groups (box, trained). The box group were kept with the mare in a stable of 3m x 3.5 m for 24 h per day. The trained group were stabled in the same sized stables and were subjected to a predefined training programme. The training program is described in detail by Van Weeren et al., 25 but briefly, consisted of an increasing number of gallop sprints in a 48 x 15 m paddock constructed with a concrete floor overlayed with a thick layer of sand. At day 7, the program involved 12 sprints, from days 8-24 16 sprints, from days 25-38 24 sprints, and from day 39 to the end of the study, on alternating days, 32 and 16 sprints. Training took place 6 days a week. The foals were subjected to euthanasia using intravenous administration of pentobarbital at 5 months of age. 2.2. Sample Collection

The left metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint was removed en bloc by transection through the proximal phalangeal bone and distal 3rd metacarpal bone less than 2 hours after euthanasia. The joint was wrapped immediately in cling film and stored at -20°C. After thawing, osteochondral plugs (6 mm ∅) were extracted perpendicular to the articular surface from 2 predefined locations on the lateral side of the proximal articular surface of the proximal phalanx, using a custom-built hollow drill. 26 One plug was taken from the dorsal edge of this lateral side (Site 1, Figure 2) representing cartilage exposed to peak loading during exercise. The second was harvested from the mid-region of the joint cavity (Site 2, Figure 2) representing constant weight bearing load. 27 The osteochondral plugs were sectioned into 2 equal halves perpendicular (a, Figure 2) to the articular surface using a 24mm diameter, 0.1mm thick circular blade. One of the halves was subsequently split in half again (b & c, Figure 2). One of the “quarters” (c, Figure 2) was sectioned using a microcryotome into 100 μm slices from superficial to deep and one “quarter” (b, Figure 2) was used as a full thickness cartilage specimen stored at -20oC until further biochemical analysis. Half “a”(Figure 2) was demineralised, paraffin embedded and sliced for polarised light microscopic structural analyses. 2.3. Sample Analysis

2.3.1. Biochemical Analysis

Sample analysis was performed across the full cartilage thickness of the sample with the cartilage sectioned into 100 μm cartilage slices using a microcryotome until the subchondral bone was reached.

2.3.2. Quantification of Glycosaminoglycan & DNA Content

Cartilage samples were digested using papain (1 u/ml, Sigma P3125) in 400 μl of 50 mmol/l phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), containing 2 mmol/l Na2EDTA and 2 mmol/l cysteine for 4 h, at 65°C. A dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) colorimetric assay was used to measure the polysulphated GAG content. 28 Shark chondroitin sulphate was used as a standard. DNA content was measured using the protocol report by Kim et al. 29 Hoechst 33258 was added to 80 μl of the papain-digested cartilage and fluorescence was measured immediately after mixing using a fluorimeter, with excitation at 365 nm and emission at 460 nm. Calf thymus DNA (Sigma D-4764) was used as a reference.

2.3.3. Quantification of Total Collagen, Hydroxylysine (HYL) Content and Hydroxylysylpyridinoline (HP) Crosslinks

Papain digested samples were then hydrolyzed with 6M HCl at 110°C for 20h. The amount of hydroxyproline and proline was determined by reverse-phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) of 9-fluorenylmethyl chloroformate (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland)-derivatized amino acids, as described by Bank et al. 30 Collagen content was calculated assuming 300 residues hydroxyproline per triple helix and a molecular weight of 300,000 g/mol. For the determination of the collagen cross-link hydroxylysylpyridinoline, acid-hydrolyzed samples were diluted to 50% acetic acid and injected on an HPLC system equipped with online sample purification on CC31 cellulose using a Prospekt solid-phase extractor (Separations, Jasco Benelux BV, IJsselstein, The Netherlands). The retained cross-links were eluted from the CC31 material and online chromatographed on a cation exchange column (Whatman Partisil SCX; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Eluting cross-links were detected by a Jasco fluorometer (model FP-920; Separations). The PYD/DPD HPLC calibrator (Metra, Palo Alto, CA) was used as standard. Values are expressed as total amount of residues per collagen molecule.19,26,30-32

2.4. Structural Analysis

2.4.1. Parallelism and Orientation Using Quantitative Polarized Light Microscopy (qPLM)

Collagen fibril architecture was quantified using qPLM as previously reported.23,33 The Parallelism Index (PI) was determined, which assesses the degree of alignment of the collagen fibrils in relation to each other, ranging from 0% (random organization) to 100% (perfect parallelism). The orientation angle was also calculated and expressed in degrees, this angle quantifies the direction of the fibrils in relation to the articular surface, where 0o is parallel to the articular surface and 90o is perpendicular. Measurements were obtained across the full cartilage thickness.

2.5. Statistical Analysis

A generalized linear mixed-effects model was used with horse as a random effect and regimen (box or trained), slice depth (superficial, middle, deep), site (1 or 2), and their interactions as fixed effects. Model assumptions were assessed using simulation-based residual diagnostics (DHARMa), supported by descriptive statistics (mean, median, skewness), graphical assessment (histograms, density plots, QQ plots, residual vs. fitted plots), and statistical tests (e.g., Shapiro-Wilk) where applicable. Tukey was used for pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means. Slice depth was treated as a categorical factor with three groups based on relative cartilage thickness: Superficial (0–20%), Middle (21–60%), and Deep (61–100%). All statistical tests were interpreted using a 5% level of significance (p<0.05). Depth-wise line plots were generated by normalizing the full slice thicknesses using a percentage of the total cartilage thickness to account for variations in slice number. All statistical analyses and plot generation were performed using R statistical programming language (R Core, 2023). All data were included in the analysis unless specifically noted.

3. Results

The total number of slices obtained ranged from 7 to 10 at site 1, and 6 to 10 at site 2 with the mean number of 9.38±1.02 and 8.56±1.31 respectively. Generalized linear mixed-effects modelling identified significant main effects of site, slice depth and/or regimen, no significant two- or three-way interactions were detected for GAG, DNA, collagen or HP. Glycosaminoglycan concentrations (Figure 3) were consistently higher at site 2 compared with site 1 across all slice depths and both exercise regimens (p < 0.001). Furthermore, within site 1, trained horses had higher GAG concentrations than box horses in the superficial (p = 0.004) and deep (p = 0.008) groups. Site 1 DNA content (Figure 4) exceeded site 2 across all slice groups and regimens (p<0.001) except for the superficial group in the trained regimen where there was no difference (p = 0.115). In the deep layer, total collagen content (Figure 5) was higher at site 1 for both regimens (box: p = 0.038; trained: p=0.007) and in the middle layer for the trained regimen only (p=0.004). Hydroxylysylpyridinoline (HP) content (Figure 6) showed consistent site differences, with significantly higher values at site 2 across all cartilage depths and exercise regimens (p < 0.004). Within-site comparisons revealed higher HP levels at site 1 in the superficial layer (p = 0.021) and at site 2 in the middle layer (p = 0.047) of the box horses only. Hydroxylysine (HYL) (Figure 7) showed significantly higher values at site 2 than site 1 in the superficial layer and middle layers of the trained group (p <0.001, p = 0.031). A significant two-way interaction between regimen and site was detected (p = 0.016). Orientation angle (Oa) (Figure 8) was significantly higher in the superficial group at site 1 compared to site 2 in both the box (p < 0.001) and trained (p = 0.001) groups. In the deep layers site 2 showed significantly higher values than site 1 (p = 0.007) in the trained group only. Significant two-way interactions were detected between regimen and site (p = 0.025) and slice depth and site (p < 0.001). Parallelism (PI) (Figure 9) was significantly higher in site 2 across all slice groups (p < 0.04) except for the middle group in the trained horses where no significance was detected. A regimen effect for PI was detected at site 1 only in the superficial (p = 0.008) and middle (p = 0.003) groups with trained being significantly higher than box. Significant 2-way interactions were detected between regimen and site (p = 0.003) and slice depth and site (p = 0.003). 4. Discussion

The biochemical composition of cartilage at sites 1 and 2 differed significantly in at least one regimen-site combination for all biochemical and structural components analysed. It has been previously established that the two sites experience different loading conditions at rest and exercise. 27 Sectioning the cartilage into 100 μm layers, rather than analysing full-thickness samples, enabled depth-specific biochemical assessment and accounted for the gradual compositional changes across the cartilage depth providing valuable and relatively unique insight.

The consistently higher GAG content at site 2 for both the box and trained horses is consistent with other reports showing the same finding between horses kept at pasture compared with the same training regimen used in this study. 20 It was shown in the study reported here that GAG content increased in both sites and exercise regimens from superficial to deep which is again consistent with previous reports in horses 20 and other species.13,34-36 The effects of training were evident at site 1 with significantly increased GAG content in the superficial and deep zones when compared to the horses maintained on box rest. This supports the findings of Saadat et al, who found an increase in proteoglycan content in the deep zone associated with increased loading in a rabbit model. 37 The optimal exercise loading has not yet been determined but light and moderate exercise in rodents caused an increase in femoral condyle cartilage GAG content compared to a sedentary control whereas high intensity caused a reduction. 38 In dogs, moderate exercise increased GAG content by 28% in the femoral condyles in the intermediate and deep zones but not in the superficial zones. 39 In treadmill-trained young Thoroughbreds, Murray et al (2001) found an increase in GAG-content in the cartilage from the dorsal radial and dorsal intermediate carpal bone surfaces, which are sites known to be heavily loaded during exercise, like the dorsal margin of the first phalanx examined in our study. 40 The data from the Bristol study were corroborated by Bird et al (2000) who showed that proteoglycan synthesis rates were higher in the trained animals than in the controls. 41

The collagen distribution follows a similar pattern to that of GAG with increasing levels from superficial to deep which is contrary to Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy reports in older more mature cartilage which show an apparent inverse relationship.12,20 However, FTIR presents challenges for quantification of collagen and proteoglycan content due to significant amide band overlapping which can introduce significant errors and, particularly when analysed by univariate methods which have been widely used, should be interpreted with caution.12,42 Collagen distribution does appear to be subject to changes in spatial distribution with increasing age in pigs. 23 Site 1 collagen content was higher in the deeper layers compared to site 2 in both box and trained horses which supports similar findings in adult horses where the dorsal and palmar aspects of the proximal first phalanx contain greater collagen than the central portion.22,24,26 Interestingly, there was also an increase in the middle layers at site 1 of the trained group which may be the result of increased loading at this site which is maximally loaded only during exercise. 27 This increase in collagen at site 1, which is subjected to approximately 30%-50% greater maximal load during exercise than site 2,22,27 is likely the result of functional adaptation. Differences in mechanical loading at these sites are detected by chondrocytes and through mechanotransduction pathways influence chondrocyte proliferation, collagen/proteoglycan synthesis and matrix organisation.43-45 Increases in collagen content of ∼35% have been reported at site 1 compared with only ∼10% at site 2 during the first 5 months of life when foal cartilage is experiencing a rapid transition from total lack of compressive loading in utero to full compressive and shear loading during weightbearing and exercise with topographical variations. 18 Functional adaptation is the normal collagen maturation process by which biochemical and biomechanical heterogeneity develop and is considered critical for the development of healthy cartilage able to withstand the adult forces exerted upon it whilst resisting injury. This is of particular importance, given the fact that mature cartilage has very limited reparative capacity. 46

The depth- and site-dependent variations in collagen content were accompanied by corresponding differences in HYL and HP, reflecting distinct stages of collagen network synthesis and maturation. Hydroxylysine is a post-translationally modified amino acid unique to collagen, formed by hydroxylation of lysine residues during collagen biosynthesis, and reflects hydroxylation activity and new collagen formation. 47 Hydroxylysylpyridinoline is a non-reducible cross-link formed between hydroxylysine residues during extracellular collagen maturation and reflects collagen network stabilization and tissue maturity. 48 Collagen concentrations increased with depth and were significantly higher at site 1 in the deeper layers, whereas both HYL and HP were increased at site 2 in specific depth/regimen combinations, indicating a more actively remodelled yet biochemically mature matrix at this location.

The higher HYL values at site 2 in the superficial and middle trained group suggest greater hydroxylation activity and collagen synthesis, consistent with ongoing matrix formation preceding cross-link maturation and supports previous work. 19 In contrast, consistently higher HP levels at site 2 across all depths indicate a more extensively cross-linked and stable collagen network. Previous work by this group found there was no statistically significant difference between site 1 and site 2 at 5 months when samples were harvested from the medial aspect of the joint18,19 and in adult horses, site 1 tends to be higher.19,24,49 Together, these findings suggest that site 2 exhibits both active collagen remodelling and advanced matrix maturation, whereas site 1 shows higher collagen accumulation but comparatively lower cross-link density. Loading is key to this maturation process. Site 2 is consistently loaded at all stages from birth, but site 1 is only subjected to increasing load when exercised and therefore maturation is slower to develop at this site. It is likely given the higher clinical prevalence of lateral metacarpal condylar fractures50-52 that there are differences between the loading of the medial and lateral aspects of the joint which may contribute to these findings but this has not been proven. 24 However, it has been demonstrated that cartilage degeneration starts at the dorsomedial aspect of P1 and extends to the dorsolateral aspect with further degeneration propagating from the medial to lateral fovea. 53 Overall, these results highlight a dynamic interplay between collagen synthesis, cross-link maturation, and total collagen content, governed by site-specific mechanical loading and exercise history.

Collagen fibril orientation was not associated with any exercise related differences as a main effect, but distinct site-dependent patterns were evident. In the superficial zone, site 1 exhibited higher Oa values than site 2, indicating a less horizontally aligned collagen network. In contrast, within the deep zone the Oa was higher in the trained group at site 2 compared to site 1, reflecting a more vertically oriented fibrillar structure. The differences in orientation can be explained by the differences in loading at site 1 compared with site 2 and the additional load provided by training and highlights the apparently accelerating effect loading has on the development of the final architecture as originally described by Benninghoff (1925). 2 A similar observation was made in Thoroughbred yearlings subjected to another equine exercise study 54 in which a similar depth-wise analysis of the articular cartilage was performed. 22 Equine cartilage at 5 months in this study appears to be more advanced in its development compared to porcine at 4 months 23 where collagen fibril orientation was predominantly parallel to the articular surface. This may represent differences in loading of the joints of the extremities in juvenile pigs compared with foals during the early stages of life particularly related to their housing and management, which emphasises the importance of the early life stages in cartilage development.

Parallelism, which reflects the degree of fibril alignment relative to each other, was generally higher at site 2 across most depth zones, indicating a more organized collagen network under consistent load. Localized exercise effects were observed at site 1 in the superficial and middle zones, where trained horses showed significantly higher PI than the box group, suggesting that training can enhance collagen fibril alignment in more superficially loaded regions. This data supports those reported previously from the medial aspect of the same joint 33 and adds new insight in regard to the effect of training. In summary, the structural data (Oa and PI) would suggest that collagen organization is both site- and depth-dependent, with mechanical load influential on functional adaptation of the cartilage matrix.

Only two sites were examined in this study, which limits the extent to which the observed differences can be generalised across the associated articular surfaces. Future work should therefore assess topographical variation across a greater area of the articular surface. The primary experimental comparison of this study was between training regimens, for which differences can be directly attributed to the imposed training conditions. Zonal differences whilst observed are only indirectly related to local loading conditions. Zonal variation should therefore be considered a secondary outcome and interpreted cautiously. Whilst the sample size was relatively small with 8 horse per group, the numbers are in keeping with similar studies of this nature. The harvested cores were sectioned to allow the use of multiple analytical methods as shown in Figure 2 maximising the analytic potential of the samples.

In conclusion, this study illustrates the site differences in biochemical and structural components of equine cartilage at 5 months under different training regimes. The more central consistently loaded Site 2 exhibited higher GAG and HP content with HYL higher in the superficial and middle layers of the trained group, while the during exercise intermittently loaded dorsal site 1 showed greater collagen accumulation in deeper layers but lower cross-link density. Structural analyses (Oa and PI) confirmed that collagen fibril orientation and alignment adapt regionally, with training enhancing fibril organization in superficial zones. Together, these findings highlight the dynamic interplay between collagen synthesis, maturation, and fibril organization, demonstrating that mechanical load is a key driver of cartilage biochemical composition and structure essential for functional cartilage adaptation.
