The effect of open to air frequency on diaper dermatitis recovery and maternal self-efficacy in infants aged 0–3 months: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Fatma Şule Bilgiç, Aysu Yıldız Karaahmet, Shahla Shafaati Laleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiaper dermatitis is a common condition affecting infants aged 0–3 months, often leading to physical discomfort and psychological distress for parents. Traditional management focuses on skin protection, yet the impact of open to air on recovery is under-researched. This study aims to evaluate the effects of different open to air frequencies on diaper dermatitis recovery and maternal self-efficacy.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 130 infants diagnosed with mild diaper dermatitis. Participants were assigned to two groups: Group A received 5 min of diaper-free air exposure 6 times a day, while Group B received the same duration 12 times a day. Data were collected using the Uncomplicated Diaper Dermatitis Severity Rating Scale (UDDSRSI) and the Perceived Maternal Self-Efficacy Scale (PMSS) at baseline and upon resolution of dermatitis.ResultsThe recovery duration was significantly shorter in Group B (1.98 ± 1.22 days) compared to Group A (3.23 ± 1.01 days; p = 0.002). Maternal self-efficacy scores increased significantly in both groups, with Group B showing a greater improvement (Group A: 61.51 ± 8.61 to 66.81 ± 8.74; Group B: 65.00 ± 6.92 to 75.21 ± 3.93; p < 0.001). Regression analysis indicated that the intervention in Group B significantly enhanced maternal self-efficacy (β = 8.40, t = 8.801, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that increased open to air frequency effectively shortens diaper dermatitis recovery time and enhances maternal self-efficacy. These findings suggest that simple, cost-effective interventions can significantly benefit both infant dermatological health and maternal psychological well-being, offering valuable insights for pediatric care practices.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07268352, registered on November 25, 2025. Introduction

Diaper dermatitis (DD) is the most common dermatologic condition affecting newborns and infants, presenting as inflammation, redness, and irritation in the diaper area (groin, genitals, buttocks, and lower abdomen) due to moisture, friction, and prolonged contact with urine and feces. It is particularly frequent in early infancy and a peak around 9–12 months [1]. Global prevalence estimates for diaper dermatitis vary widely, from approximately 7% to 60%, and higher rates are often observed in hospitalized or clinic-attending infants, reflecting differences in care practices and skin barrier status [2]. Friction, moisture buildup, and extended exposure to urine and feces in the diaper region are the usual triggers for diaper dermatitis, contributing to erosion of the epidermal barrier and disruption of the skin’s protective surface, thereby causing inflammation and irritation [3]. If dermatitis persists or is severe, it may lead to secondary infections such as Candida or bacterial infections, which can prolong illness, increase healthcare utilization, and may have longer-term consequences for skin integrity and barrier function [1] Beyond its physiological impact, diaper dermatitis can significantly affect the psychological and emotional well-being of both infants and their parents [4]. Infants with skin issues will exhibit longer sleep disturbances, more feeding difficulties, and higher maternal/paternal stress levels than infants without skin issues. [5, 6].

The discomfort and restlessness associated with diaper dermatitis can elevate parental anxiety and stress, potentially reducing mothers’ confidence in their caregiving abilities. This, in turn, may negatively impact maternal self-efficacy [1]. Maternal self-efficacy refers to a mother’s belief in her ability to effectively care for her child and is closely linked to her competence in managing the child’s health [7, 8].

Traditional approaches to managing diaper dermatitis focus on protecting the skin barrier and typically include frequent diaper changes, proper hygiene practices, the use of barrier creams, and ensuring diapers are not too tight [9]. In addition to these strategies, regular airing of the diaper area has been recommended as an effective method for promoting healing [10]. By reducing skin moisture, open to air may alleviate irritation and support the repair of the skin barrier. However, the impact of different durations of open to air on healing remains underexplored [11].

Recent studies suggest that exposing the skin to air for varying durations may contribute positively to skin recovery [12, 13]. Ventilation helps with two important aspects of skin care for babies: reducing moisture and preventing irritation [1].

Nevertheless, few studies have examined how such interventions influence mothers’ perceived self-efficacy. Maternal self-efficacy is not only vital for caregiving quality but also plays a significant role in maternal psychological well-being and life satisfaction [8].

In this context, the present study aims to investigate the effects of varied exposure to open air on the healing process of diaper dermatitis in infants aged 0–3 months, as well as to assess how these interventions influence maternal perceived self-efficacy. Given the limited evidence in the literature, this study seeks to propose an effective and low-cost intervention to support both dermatological recovery in infants and the enhancement of maternal caregiving confidence. The findings may contribute to the development of evidence-based practices that promote infant health and improve maternal psychosocial outcomes.

Method

Study design

This study was designed as a prospective, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) to investigate the effects of different diaper-free air exposure frequencies on the healing of diaper dermatitis and perceived maternal self-efficacy in infants aged 0–3 months. Participants were enrolled prior to the intervention, and all outcome data were collected prospectively following group allocation This study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 guidelines for reporting randomized controlled trials. A completed CONSORT checklist is included as an additional file with the submitted manuscript. The study was conducted between January 2023 and January 2024 in the Pediatric Outpatient Clinic of a private hospital. The CONSORT (2025) guidelines were followed throughout the research process [14]. The purpose of the study, its risks, and the voluntary nature of participation were explained to the mothers in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration; written and verbal consent was obtained. The data were stored anonymously.

Study participants and sample

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software [15]. The sample size was calculated for a randomized controlled trial comparing two independent groups. Based on a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), and 80% statistical power (1–β = 0.80), the minimum required sample size was determined as 61 participants per group. A 10% attrition rate was considered, and therefore, 140 participants (70 per group) were planned. The study was completed with 130 participants (Group A: 6 × 5 min/day, n = 66; Group B: 12 × 5 min/day, n = 64).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion Criteria: Infants aged 0–3 months, without any chronic health conditions, singleton and full-term births, fed exclusively with breast milk or a combination of breast milk and formula (formula-fed only infants excluded due to potential confounding effects on skin health, immunity, and the microbiota, which could interfere with isolating the effect of ventilation duration; this selection also aimed to increase sample homogeneity and internal validity), diagnosed with mild diaper dermatitis (1–3 points) according to the Non-Complicated Diaper Dermatitis Severity Assessment Scale [16]. To minimize potential diagnostic misclassification, infants with suspected Cow’s Milk Protein Allergy were excluded during the screening process. Screening was based on clinical evaluation, including the absence of key symptoms associated with CMPA such as persistent diarrhea, blood or mucus in stool, vomiting, eczema beyond the diaper area, or poor weight gain. In addition, maternal reports regarding feeding tolerance and infant gastrointestinal symptoms were considered. Infants with clinical findings suggestive of CMPA were not included in the study and were referred for further medical evaluation.

Although infants receiving mixed feeding were included, detailed quantitative data regarding formula type, amount, and feeding frequency were not systematically recorded, as the study primarily aimed to control for major feeding-related confounding by excluding exclusively formula-fed infants.

Exclusion Criteria: Infants who had received antibiotic treatment, use of any cream other than zinc-based barrier creams, moderate-to-severe diaper dermatitis (infants with moderate-to-severe DD are excluded because such cases typically require concurrent medical treatment and closer monitoring, which would confound the assessment of open-to-air healing and raise safety concerns), infants with conditions that could affect skin barrier function or diaper dermatitis risk, including dermatologic diaper-area diseases (e.g., atopic dermatitis/eczema, ichthyosis, psoriasis) and systemic disorders (e.g., cystic fibrosis, phenylketonuria), use of alternative diapering methods other than disposable diapers, infants fed exclusively with formula.

Randomization

A total of 178 infants were approached; Thirty-eight were excluded because they did not meet inclusion criteria (including formula-fed status or moderate-to-severe diaper dermatitis) or had diagnosed medical conditions that could affect skin barrier, leaving 140 mother–infant pairs who provided consent and were randomized. Before randomization, all 140 mothers completed pre-intervention data collection instruments. The 140 participants were randomly assigned to one of the two groups according to a pre-existing list generated by a computer program. Simple randomization was used for the assignment to groups. The groups were defined as the “6 times per day, 5 minutes air exposure group” (Group A) and the “12 times per day, 5 minutes air exposure group” (Group B). The group allocation for each participant was determined by a lottery method. The randomization was provided to the researcher who would apply the intervention and conduct the follow-ups in a sealed envelope. One of the researchers (A.YK) kept the allocation table hidden from the other researcher (FS.B), who would be responsible for applying the intervention and monitoring the participants after they were assigned to the study (Fig. 1). After randomization, 4 participants from Group A and 6 participants from Group B were excluded from the study because they could not be contacted and chose not to continue participating. The study was completed with 130 participants (Group A: n = 66, Group B: n = 64). Outcome assessment was conducted by independent researchers blinded to group allocation. All photographs were first evaluated for adequacy according to predefined criteria by two blinded reviewers. UDDSRSI scoring was then performed independently by two blinded raters, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third blinded adjudicator.

All data, including clinical assessments and caregiver-reported measures, were collected in real time during the study period. No retrospective data extraction from medical records was performed.

Data collection phase and scales

The data of the study were obtained using the Data Collection Form, the Uncomplicated Diaper Dermatitis Severity Rating Scale in Infants, and the Perceived Maternal Self-Efficacy Scale.

Data collection form

The form was created by the researchers through a literature review [8, 9, 11] and consists of 26 questions. Eleven questions aimed at describing the sociodemographic characteristics of the mother, and fifteen questions regarding the infant’s current condition, feeding, and diaper rash status.

Uncomplicated Diaper Dermatitis Severity Rating Scale in Infants (UDDSRSI)

This scale, developed by Buckley et al. in 2016 and adapted to Turkish by Keskin in 2019, was used to assess diaper dermatitis severity in infants. The scale evaluates four items concerning the perineal area and surrounding skin: erythema, papules, pustules, skin breakdown, and the area size. Each item can be scored between 0 and 6, with varying scoring based on severity. Erythema and irritation are scored from 0 to 3, where 0 means no erythema or irritation, and 3 means severe erythema and irritation. For the Degree of Diaper Dermatitis, Papules, Pustules, and Skin Breakdown, scores range from 0 to 1. A 0 score indicates less than 50% of the perineal area affected, a small number of papules/pustules, or skin breakdown due to friction or diaper dermatitis etiology; a 1 score means more than 50% of the perineal area is affected, numerous or uncountable papules/pustules, or skin breakdown from causes other than diaper dermatitis. The total score ranges from 0 to 6, where 0–3 indicates mild diaper dermatitis and 3–6 indicates severe diaper dermatitis [16, 17]. The UDDSRSI scores were determined by evaluating the entire diaper area, including the perianal region, buttocks, genital area, and intertriginous folds. For each infant, these anatomical regions were assessed individually for clinical signs (erythema, papules, pustules, and skin breakdown). The final score represents a composite assessment of the most severely affected areas within the perineal region, ensuring that significant irritation in the perianal zone—which often shows higher severity—was fully captured in the total score.

Perceived Maternal Self-Efficacy Scale (PMSS)

This scale, developed by Barnes and Adamson in 2007 and validated for Turkish by Kadiroğlu and Güdücü Tüfekci, assesses perceived maternal self-efficacy levels in mothers of hospitalized preterm infants. The scale is a 4-point Likert type with 20 items, rated from 1 to 4 (strongly disagree, disagree, agree, strongly agree). The scores are summed to obtain a total score ranging from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater perceived maternal self-efficacy. The internal consistency coefficient of the Turkish version of the scale was found to be 0.975. In this study, it was found to be 0.925 [18, 19].

UDDSRSI scoring was performed independently by two blinded raters, with a third blinded adjudicator resolving any discrepancies. These procedures were implemented to enhance internal validity and minimize measurement bias associated with remote visual assessment. All images were stored securely using de-identified study IDs. Prior to the intervention, caregivers in both groups received standardized training on diaper area cleansing and routine diaper care. The frequency of diaper changes and cleansing practices were comparable between groups and followed the same protocol. Therefore, hygiene-related variables were controlled. The only difference between the groups was the total scheduled diaper-free ventilation duration (30 min in Group A vs. 60 min in Group B), making ventilation time the sole independent variable.

To ensure the internal validity of the study, the frequency of diaper changes was standardized for both groups. Both Group A and Group B performed a minimum of 6 to 8 diaper changes per day, or as needed based on the infant’s elimination pattern. The intervention (air exposure) was timed to coincide with these regular changes to avoid introducing an additional variable. Furthermore, the skin cleansing protocol was identical for both groups; parents were instructed to use only lukewarm water and soft cotton wipes for cleaning the perineal area, avoiding any commercial wet wipes, soaps, or barrier creams during the study period to isolate the effect of air exposure frequency.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 24. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD and frequency (%). Independent t-tests (for continuous variables) and Chi-square tests (for categorical variables) were used for inter-group comparisons. The normality assumption was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical methods (histograms and Q–Q plots). As the distributions did not significantly deviate from normality (p > 0.05), parametric tests were deemed appropriate.The relationship between healing time, UDDSRSI, and PMSS scores was analyzed using linear regression. ANOVA was applied for repeated measures. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study protocol was approved by the Haliç University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 30.11.2022, No: 222). The purpose of the study, its risks, and the voluntary nature of participation were explained to the mothers in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration; written and verbal consent was obtained. The data were stored anonymously.

Discussion

According to the results of the study, the demographic characteristics of participants in both groups (A and B) showed no significant differences, indicating a comparable baseline between the two cohorts. In terms of maternal demographics, both groups had similar average ages, education levels, and income statuses. Specifically, the average age of mothers was approximately 27.5 years, with a majority holding university degrees. The educational attainment among fathers was also comparable, with a significant number having completed university education. Additionally, the revenue status was similar across both groups, with no notable differences in the distribution of participants in terms of income levels. When examining infant characteristics, the average age of infants was slightly higher in Group B (65.77 days) compared to Group A (56.33 days), but this difference was not statistically significant. The birth weights and heights of infants in both groups were similar, supporting the notion of homogeneity in the sample. Moreover, the gender distribution, nutritional practices, and vitamin supplementation status were consistent across groups, with no significant disparities observed. These findings suggest that the randomization process effectively balanced the demographic and clinical characteristics between the two groups, minimizing potential confounding variables that could impact the study’s outcomes. This homogeneity enhances the reliability of the results regarding the effects of varied open-air exposure on diaper dermatitis recovery and maternal self-efficacy.

Furthermore, our results indicate that higher varied open-air exposure is associated with a shorter recovery time from diaper dermatitis: Group B (12 daily exposures of 5 min) recovered in 1.98 ± 1.22 days compared with Group A (6 exposures) at 3.23 ± 1.01 days. The finding is compatible with the moisture-reduction concept described by Atherton [12].

Furthermore, while skin pH was not directly measured in the present study, the literature suggests that air exposure protocols may facilitate epidermal barrier recovery and acidify the pH of the diaper area skin [13]. Our clinical results might be associated with such physiological changes; however, further research incorporating objective pH measurements is needed to confirm this effect in our specific intervention groups. A study using baby mannequin tests examined how diaper fit affects heat and moisture transfer under dry conditions. Key findings revealed that regular pants and loose-fitting diapers provide the best comfort while maintaining consistent temperature and moisture levels. This study, like ours, emphasizes the importance of ventilation and proper fit in promoting optimal skin health and comfort for infants [22]. Another study by Wu et al. highlighted the significant impact of the air gap between the diaper and the baby’s skin on heat and moisture transfer [23]. Additionally, a study found that reducing the duration infants spend in diapers, particularly at night, is associated with a decreased incidence and severity of DD. Chinese caregivers reported the shortest overnight diaper duration (about 6 h), compared to 7.9 h in the United States and 8.5 h in Germany. This practice helps minimize excessive skin hydration and exposure to irritants, thereby improving skin barrier integrity and reducing DD. Thus, allowing infants to spend less time in diapers may effectively prevent or mitigate diaper dermatitis [2]. A systematic review proposed the ABCDE approach (Air, Barrier, Cleansing, Diapering, Education) as an evidence-based nursing intervention for the prevention and management of DD in children. The study analyzed 30 articles and found that this approach effectively reduces inflammation, repairs skin damage, and prevents the recurrence of DD [24]. A systematic review of 65 studies on three oxygen therapies for wound healing—local oxygen therapy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and supplemental inspired oxygen—found that about 77% of local oxygen trials and 63% of hyperbaric trials reported at least one significant positive wound-healing outcome [25]. Finally, another study on oxygen therapy for diaper dermatitis in infants found that oxygen significantly reduced the severity of the condition and accelerated recovery at 24, 48, and 72 h compared to usual care. The likelihood of recovery was significantly higher in the oxygen therapy group, indicating its effectiveness in promoting healing. This aligns with our research on the impact of diaper-free fresh air exposure on diaper dermatitis recovery, suggesting that increased air exposure—whether through oxygen or fresh air—can enhance skin health and reduce diaper dermatitis in infants. Both studies underscore the importance of optimizing environmental factors to support infant skin health [26]. This finding underscores the importance of environmental factors in the healing process of diaper dermatitis, suggesting that more frequent air exposure can effectively reduce the duration of the condition. However, it is important to note that there are currently no studies in the literature that directly test the efficacy of 5-minute air exposure sessions. In this regard, the protocol used in this study represents an innovative contribution to the field. Therefore, it can be concluded that further research is needed to measure the effects of open to air frequency on the treatment of dermatitis.

Additionally, the study found a significant improvement in maternal self-efficacy scores, with mothers in Group B experiencing a greater increase (from 65.00 ± 6.92 to 75.21 ± 3.93) compared to those in Group A (from 61.51 ± 8.61 to 66.81 ± 8.74). This enhancement in maternal confidence correlates with the improved health outcomes for their infants, indicating that effective interventions not only benefit the child’s dermatological health but also empower mothers in their caregiving roles. An important finding of this study is the positive impact of caregiver training on maternal self-sufficiency in diaper care. Regardless of the specific structure of the ventilation protocol, standardized education appeared to enhance mothers’ confidence and competence in managing diaper dermatitis. This finding has meaningful clinical implications, suggesting that structured caregiver education may play a critical role in improving home-based skin care practices and supporting early recovery. Integrating evidence-based diaper care training into routine postnatal education programs may therefore contribute to better infant skin health outcomes.

Bandura’s (1997) self-efficacy theory emphasizes the importance of a mother’s “sense of accomplishment,” suggesting that higher maternal self-efficacy leads to greater confidence in caregiving processes, a feeling of competence in care, and ultimately, a more psychologically healthy attitude [27, 28]. This is further supported by research indicating that maternal involvement in caregiving not only enhances the maternal role but also promotes mental health [10]. In line with these findings, the results of a study revealed that mothers who participated in the web-based program experienced a significant increase in their self-efficacy levels regarding infant care. Specifically, the scores on the Parental Self-Efficacy Scale were notably higher in the intervention group compared to the control group [29]. Similarly, the results of another study indicated that the self-efficacy of mothers of premature infants significantly improved after receiving training related to infant care, demonstrating that the training effectively enhanced mothers’ confidence in their caregiving abilities [19]. One important limitation of this study is that the sample consisted exclusively of mother–infant dyads. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other caregivers such as fathers, grandparents, or professional caregivers. Caregiving practices, responsiveness, and perceived self-efficacy may differ across caregiver types, which could influence both the implementation of the intervention and the outcomes. Future studies should include diverse caregiver groups to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Regression analysis showed that 12 × 5-minute ventilation led to an increase of 8.40 units in maternal self-efficacy (β = 8.40; p < 0.001). This effect size is rarely reported in similar studies. For example, Mörelius et al. reported a 4.2 unit increase in self-efficacy with newborn care training [30]. The higher effect in this study may be related to the protocol being practical and measurable. Furthermore, feedback mechanisms such as daily photo sharing could have increased motivation [27]. However, it is important to evaluate these results within a cultural context; for example, perceptions of maternal self-efficacy may differ in Western societies [7]. The findings of this study should be tested in other populations while considering cultural differences.

This study highlights the significance of environmental factors, like increased ventilation frequency, in managing diaper dermatitis and boosting maternal self-efficacy. The findings demonstrate the link between infant skin health and maternal confidence, suggesting that effective interventions can benefit both. Healthcare practitioners should integrate these insights into diaper dermatitis management strategies, while future research should explore innovative approaches across diverse cultural contexts.

Implications of practice and research

This study offers valuable implications for both practice and research in the management of diaper dermatitis and maternal self-efficacy. Clinically, the positive outcomes associated with increased ventilation frequency provide healthcare practitioners with a simple, cost-effective intervention that enhances infant skin health while simultaneously boosting maternal psychosocial well-being. This innovative approach encourages the integration of practical strategies into pediatric care to improve overall outcomes. From a research perspective, the study establishes a strong methodological foundation for future investigations into ventilation techniques and their long-term impacts on both infant dermatological health and maternal confidence. It emphasizes the need for further studies across diverse cultural contexts to assess the generalizability of these findings and deepen the understanding of how environmental factors can shape effective caregiving practices. In addition, research should extend beyond individual outcomes to explore the potential effects of open-to-air care on overall family well-being, including daily routines, caregiver burden, and family dynamics.

Limitations and strengths

The study presents several strengths and limitations that should be acknowledged. Among its strengths, the reliable assessment of the intervention’s effects is supported by a robust comparison between groups, pre- and post-intervention measurements, and the application of regression analysis. The homogeneity of sociodemographic characteristics among mothers and infants allowed for a focused evaluation of the intervention’s impact, reducing the potential influence of confounding variables. Additionally, the simplicity and low cost of the ventilation protocol make it an innovative approach applicable in both clinical and home settings.

However, several limitations must be noted. First, this study specifically focused on infants with mild diaper dermatitis (UDDSRSI scores ≤ 3). While this was intended to evaluate the intervention in a controlled, early-stage cohort, the exclusion of moderate-to-severe cases may limit the understanding of how air exposure impacts more advanced clinical presentations where sleep and feeding disruptions are more pronounced.

Second, the generalizability of the findings is limited by the specific characteristics of the study population. The sample consisted exclusively of biological mother–infant dyads, excluding other caregiver–infant interactions such as those involving fathers, adoptive parents, or professional caregivers. Caregiving practices, adherence to the intervention, and perceived self-efficacy may differ across caregiver types, potentially influencing the outcomes. In addition, the exclusion of formula-fed infants, although intended to ensure sample homogeneity, further constrains external validity. Differences in stool pH, consistency, and microbiota between breastfed and formula-fed infants may affect skin irritation and the effectiveness of the ventilation protocol. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to all infant populations.

Although exclusively formula-fed infants were excluded to enhance sample homogeneity, detailed data on formula feeding characteristics (e.g., type, amount, and frequency) among mixed-fed infants were not collected. Therefore, residual confounding related to feeding patterns cannot be entirely ruled out. However, the inclusion of predominantly breastfed infants may have reduced variability in exposure to potential dietary irritants.

Furthermore, the small sample size and single-center design restrict the generalizability of the findings. The absence of long-term evaluations raises questions about the sustainability of the intervention’s effects, necessitating future studies to address this gap. Methodologically, the incomplete implementation of blinding may have introduced observer and measurement biases. Additionally, parity (primiparous vs. multiparous) was not recorded or controlled for in the analyses; since parity can influence maternal self-efficacy and adherence to the home ventilation protocol, it represents a potential confounding factor.

Importantly, this study focused on a very specific population, which further limits its external validity. We exclusively included biological mother-infant dyads, thereby excluding other caregiver-infant interactions such as those involving fathers, adoptive parents, or professional caregivers. This specific pairing may influence the maternal self-efficacy outcomes and adherence to the protocol in ways that might not apply to other caregiving contexts. Lastly, while excluding formula-fed infants to enhance sample homogeneity strengthens internal validity, it constrains the generalizability of the results. Since formula-fed infants were strictly excluded, and given that differences in stool pH and consistency between breastfed and formula-fed infants can modify exposure to irritants and microbiota, the efficacy of the ventilation protocol remains to be evaluated in infants receiving formula.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant benefits of increased ventilation frequency as an effective intervention for the management of diaper dermatitis in infants aged 0–3 months. The findings demonstrate that this simple, low-cost approach not only accelerates the recovery of diaper dermatitis but also enhances maternal self-efficacy, thereby improving overall maternal psychological well-being. While the results provide valuable insights for clinical practice, the limitations regarding sample size and generalizability underscore the necessity for further research across diverse populations and settings. Long-term follow-up studies are essential to evaluate the sustainability of the intervention’s effects. Overall, the study contributes to the understanding of how environmental factors can influence infant skin health and maternal confidence, offering a pathway for future interventions and research in pediatric care.

Although recovery time was analyzed using parametric methods, it represents a time-related outcome that may not always follow a normal distribution. Therefore, alternative approaches such as non-parametric tests or time-to-event analyses could be considered in future studies to provide additional insight.
