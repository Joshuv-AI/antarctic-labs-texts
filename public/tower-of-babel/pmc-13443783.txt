Effect of pearl millet diet on immunomodulating factors, physical performance and recovery in athletes: a randomised control trial
Authors: Alisha Saifi, Kommi Kalpana, Gulshan Lal Khanna, Dayakar Rao Benhur, Gurseen Rakhra, Ekta Tanwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A pearlmilletbased diet may help modulate inflammatory cytokines and support better recovery in athletes exposed to high training stress. Its rich profile of fiber, minerals, and antioxidants suggests potential benefits for reducing inflammation and enhancing physical performance. However, evidence in athletic populations remains limited, prompting investigation into its functional impact. To explore the effect of pearl millet diet on immunomodulating factors, physical performance and recovery in athletes. A randomised controlled trial was conducted among male athletes aged 18–25 years (n = 60), who were randomly allocated into an experimental group (EG; n = 28) and a control group (CG; n = 30). The EG received a pearl millet-based diet (1/3 substitution of regular cereals with pearl millet) for 60 days, while the CG continued with their habitual cereal diet. Pre- and post-intervention assessments included body composition, immunomodulatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, Hs-CRP), and measures of physical performance and recovery.Between-group differences at post-intervention were analysed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for baseline values. Within-group changes were assessed using paired sample t-tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The experimental group showed a significant increase in muscular strength (kg) from 35.3 ± 7.59 to 42.8 ± 9.14. VO₂ max (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) increased from 47.8 ± 4.76 to 49.5 ± 4.79, while resting heart rate (beats/min) decreased from 80 ± 10.56 to 75 ± 11.16. Additionally, cumulative time (min) increased from 10.7 ± 4.12 to 12.8 ± 4.51, and distance covered in the endurance test (m) improved from 1359.0 ± 556.74 to 1565.7 ± 571.20.No significant changes were observed in serum Hs-CRP (mg/L), TNF-α (pg/mL), or IL-6 (pg/mL) levels in the EG. A 60-day pearl millet dietary intervention may enhance strength and endurance performance in the EG. However, it did not appear to produce notable changes in most immunomodulatory markers or recovery indicators.

Introduction

Pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), an ancient yet nutrient-rich crop, is dense in energy, proteins, lipids and dietary fibre along with good amounts of essential minerals such as iron, calcium, and zinc1.It also contains significant amounts of antioxidants and phenolic compounds, thus having high antioxidant activity, which plays an important role in lowering oxidative stress2. Its omega-6 fatty acids and unsaturated oils further fuel its anti-inflammatory properties. Additionally, it has high amounts of phosphorus, which aids in replenishing ATP levels, which is the primary energy source that the body utilises3.It’s complex carbohydrates with slow-digestingproperties and fats help in providing sustained energy during endurance activity as aerobic activity depends on glycogen stores in muscle and liver4–6.

Pearl millet contains 9 to 13% of protein, which can help in muscle recovery in athletes7.Dietary protein is necessary to promote growth, heal damaged cells and tissues, produce hormones, and perform a range of metabolic functions. The current evidence also indicates that consuming protein and/or amino acids before, during, and/or following exercise can improve recovery, immunological function, and growth and maintenance of lean body mass8.

Vigorous physical training and sporting events exert considerable pressure on cardiovascular, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems9.Prolonged exercise raises the amount of cortisol in the body10 and fosters attacks on the immune system along with T-cells, which results in making an athlete more prone to infections11.Intense training often leads to exercise-induced immune depression, making athletes more vulnerable to infections, particularly upper respiratory tract infections12 and slower recovery13.Exercise also promotesthe production of free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which impair immune cells and cause inflammation of both chronic and acute nature, leading to poor athletic performance11,14.Chronic and excessive inflammatory responses can disrupt recovery, reduce immune function and limit performance15.

Cytokines are a key group of immune proteins that help cells to communicate and play a crucial role in activating and controlling the immune responses16. Regular exercise alters the concentration of cytokines, which helps to prevent musculoskeletal tissue damage, but intense exercise could cause spikes in these inflammatory markers17,18. Particular inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and high sensitive C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP) reflect inflammatory responses to tissue damage19. IL-6 has dual cytokine roles; it acts as an anti-inflammatory as well as a proinflammatory marker by promoting inflammation during exercise and also suppressing TNF-α and inducing anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10, aiding recovery20. TNF-α aids in the vicious cycle of inflammation by stimulating the release of pro-inflammatorymarkers21, whileHs-CRP is an endogenous pro-inflammatory molecule that is secreted by the liver in the presence of IL-6,whichhas both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory actions11.

To reduce inflammation and improve physical performance in athletes, nutri-dense functional foods have gained attention. The well-known dry land crops harvested in the dry lands of Africa and Asia have great scope as a beneficial crop for sports persons, pearl millet1.

Furthermore, despite these potential advantages, empirical research on the direct impact of pearl millet supplementation on immunomodulating factors, physical performance and recovery is limited.Hence, the preliminary exploratory study aimed to examine the effects of pearl millet diet on immunomodulating factors and physical performance and recovery in athletes.This study intends to fill the existing voids in the literature regarding the impact of pearl millet diet as a way of diet in diminishing inflammation, enhancing immune system functionality, and improving physical performanceand recovery in athletes.

Methods

Participants

At the beginning of the study, 100 healthy male athletes aged 18–25 years undergoing training in the preparatory phasewere screened. Theathletes were recruited from Faridabad, Haryana, India. The inclusion criteria were age between 18-25years, team sport athletes, actively training in preparatory phase (Participants were classified as Tier 2 (Trained/Developmental) athletes, defined as individuals competing at the local level, training regularly (≈ 3 sessions per week) with the intention to compete in a specific sport, and demonstrating developing sport-specific skills)22, and willing to adhere to the trial guidelines, such as avoiding potentially conflicting nutritional or vitamin supplements or notifying researchers of compliance. Athletes were excluded if they consumed millets, were involved in smoking, alcohol consumption, or the use of dietary supplements or ergogenic aids, had a history of acute or chronic illness, were on medication, had physical disabilities, or suffered from sports injuries.Additionally, those who were not able to commit to availability for the study duration. Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a final sample of 60 athletes from team sports was selected.The research study was conducted in accordance with ethical considerations to ensure the right, safety and well-being of the participants. Participants were screened and recruited between [November to December 2023].Each participant was verbally briefed on the study protocol and provided informed consent in December 2023.

Sample size

Sample size calculation was based on the already existing data on the effect of whole grain diet on inflammatory markers23. The sample size was determined using G-power software (version 3.1.9.4, Germany) with an effect size of 0.855 (Mean and SD of IL-6; group 1: 0.603 ± 0.23, group 2: 0.43 ± 0.15, correlation between groups: 0.5) 5% error margin, and 95% statistical power (Fig. 1). This calculation suggests that 20 participants per group would be adequate to detect meaningful study results. Adding a 20% dropout rate in each group (Sample size = 20 per sub-group as per calculation, drop out of 20% = 4, total = calculated sample size + 20% drop out; 20 + 4 = 24 athletes in each subgroup), for the round of figure we consider it as 30 participants in each group resulted in an estimated sample size of 60 (30 athletes*2 sub-groups = 60 sample size).

Study outcome measures

The primary outcome was the change in inflammatory biomarkers (Hs-CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α) from baseline to post-intervention. Secondary outcomes included endurance performance assessed using the Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test Level 1 (total distance and estimated VO₂max), handgrip strength, body composition parameters (body fat percentage and fat-free mass), and recovery measures. All outcomes were evaluated at baseline and at the end of the intervention.

Study design

This is the randomised control study design as shown in Fig. 1. After the screening procedure, the recruited participants’ (n = 60) food intake was recorded over 3 days using 3-day diet records (two weekdays and one weekend). Before the day of laboratory testing, the participants were instructed to refrain from exercise in the previous 24 h and report to the laboratory after an overnight fast of 8–12 h on the day of the experiment. Upon arrival to the laboratory, a fasting blood sample was drawn for biochemical assessment for analysis of Immunomodulating factors (IL-6, TNF-alpha, Hs-CRP), by a trained technician. Anthropometry and body composition measurements were also done at a fasting state. Prior to 30–45 min of physical performance test, a standardised breakfast comprised 30 g Poha (an Indian Breakfast), 100 g banana and 250 ml of water24. Physical performance tests were conducted using accessible and user-friendly tools. The present study measured power, strength, agility, speed, and endurance over 2 days. On the first day, tests to assess power, strength and agility were done. On the second day, an endurance test was conducted by providing the same quantity of food to the participants. An endurance test was conducted, followed by a 5-minute warm-up. Prior to the endurance test, resting heart rate (HR) was measured, and following the endurance test, participants’ recovery HRs were also recorded at 1, 2, 3, and 15 min using the carotid pulse method25,26. The total cumulative time was also calculated. VO₂ max was estimated from the distance covered during the YYIRL1 test using a validated predictive equation and was interpreted as an indirect indicator of aerobic capacity rather than a directly measured physiological variable.

VO₂max(ml.Kg − 1.min − 1)=YYIRL1distance(m)×0.0084 + 36.427.

Following baseline (pre-test) assessments, participants were randomly allocated to either the control group (CG; n = 30) or the pearl millet experimental group (EG; n = 30) using a simple randomisation (coin toss) method. Random sequence generation and group assignment were performed by an independent data analyst who was not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment, thereby minimising selection bias. Participants were enrolled by the trained research staff. The dietary intervention was delivered and monitored by trained research staff who were not involved in statistical analysis. Outcome assessments, including body composition, physical performance, and biochemical analyses, were conducted by trained research staff following standardised protocols. Laboratory analyses were performed using coded samples to ensure assessor blinding to group allocation. Data entry and cleaning were performed by designated research personnel, and statistical analyses were conducted by a blinded data analyst using coded group identifiers.

Participant and intervention-provider blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the dietary intervention. However, objective outcome measures, standardised assessment procedures, coded group allocation during analysis, and separation of roles between recruitment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, and data analysis were implemented to minimise performance, detection, and measurement bias. Two participants from the EG withdrew due to personal reasons (final EG n = 28), whereas none in the CG dropped out (n = 30). The test was only taken at pre- and post-intervention; no follow-up period was recorded. Every procedure was carried out in compliance with the rules outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Pre-intervention data were collected in January 2024. The intervention was given to them for 2 months (mid-January to mid-March 2024). Post-intervention data (collected 60 days after the intervention) were collected in [March 2024].

Participants tolerated the pearl millet supplementation well, and no cases of gastrointestinal discomfort, allergic reactions, or withdrawal due to intolerance were observed.

Data collection techniques and tools

Anthropometric and body composition

Anthropometry and body composition assessments were conducted following the standardised ISAK guidelines29.Participants were instructed to wear minimal clothing, remove shoes, socks, and spectacles to ensure accurate measurements. Athlete height was measured using a stadiometer (Prestige height measuring scale SM-P-W-210, India) with 0.1 cm error. A bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) machine (TP-Tanita UM076, Singapore) was used to determine body weight (BW), body fat %, and fat-free mass (FFM).

Biochemical estimation

At pre-test and after 60 days of the pearl millet dietary intervention, venous blood samples (4 ml) were collected by a trained technician from the antecubital vein of the athletes following an 8–12 h overnight fast and refraining from exercise for 24 h.Blood was drawn into two separate vials withthe same puncture. One vial contained a clot activator for serum collection. After resting for 30 min these vials were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min using the Remi C-852 Clinical Centrifuge machine for the separation of serum from whole blood. The serum was transferred into Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C for further analysis of immunomodulating factor (IL-6, TNF-alpha, Hs-CRP), duplicate sample analysis by ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) methods.

Physical performance

Physical performance tests were conducted on two days. On the first day, power, strength, agility, and speed tests were conducted, and on the second day, an endurance test was conducted. The vertical jump, handgrip strength test, Illinois agility test, 20 m sprint test, and yo-yo intermittent recovery level 1 test were conducted, respectively. Between each test of “Day 1”, a minimum of 3–5 min rest was allowed for recovery of the anaerobic ATP-CP system30. Prior to performance testing, athletes were instructed on the proper techniques for each assessment. For the handgrip strength test, a handgrip dynamometer (Jamar Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer, B0B8N3THYY, India) was used; the participants were asked to grip the handgrip dynamometer firmly with their dominant hand, placing it at 90˚angle, applying maximum pressure for 3 s, with 3 trials per hand, and the maximum values were recorded31. The vertical jump test, classical also known as sargent jump test, was used according to the protocol of Sargent et (1921) to assess lower-body power32. Participants first measured their standing reach height by marking the highest point they could reach on a wall with chalk. They then performed three maximal countermovement jumps, using hip, knee, and ankle flexion, along with arm swing, to achieve maximum height. After each jump, participants marked the highest point they reached on the wall. A 45-second rest interval was provided between trials. Vertical jump height was calculated as the difference between the maximum jump height and the standing reach height. The highest jump was recorded in the three trials33. The Illinois agility test, 20 m sprint, and endurance test were conducted on flat, cleared ground. For the Illinois agility test, athletes were briefed to lie flat and then sprint through the cones as quickly as possible, completing 2 trials with the fastest time recorded34. In the 20-meter sprint, participants were instructed to sprint at maximum speed from a standing start, with 2 trials and the fastest time recorded35.

The endurance test: the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery level 1 test was conducted on day 2. As in earlier tests, clear instructions and a demo were given to the athletes. Before the test, resting heart rate (RHR) was measured27. Follow up with the 5-minute warm-up exercise. The test began as a warm-up before exercise, which is critical for achieving optimal performance36. Athletes were asked to perform until they reach their maximum heart rate or are unable to continue. Afterwards, a cool-down period was implemented, and heart rate was measured at 1, 2, 3, and 15 min post-exercise to assess recovery heart rate37. RHR and recovery HR of athletes were measured using the carotid pulse method38. The tips of the middle and index fingers are on the side of an athlete’s neck, just below their jawline, and next to the windpipe. Once the pulse was felt, it was counted for 1 min, and the heart rate was noted. The same protocol was followed after 60 days of the pearl millet diet intervention; No changes were made to the pre-specified outcomes, assessment time points, or statistical analyses after study initiation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 27.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of all variables. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean and standard deviation to summarise the data. We also compared the pre-test data between the two groups using an independent-samples t-test. This test provides insights into the differences between the two groups at the pre-test. A statistically significant baseline difference was observed for selected variables, including power and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP).

To evaluate the impact of the 60-day pearl millet intervention, a paired t-test was first utilised to analyse significant intra-group changes from pre to post-intervention. However, due to significant differences between the EG and CG at baseline, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was employed for the final inter-group comparison across all outcome variables, including anthropometric measures, body composition, physical performance, and immunomodulatory markers. In this model, post-intervention values were treated as dependent variables, group assignment was a fixed factor, and baseline values were entered as covariates to adjust for initial imbalances. This approach was preferred over change-score analysis to maximize statistical power and ensure that the interpreted effects were a direct result of the intervention rather than pre-existing differences; consequently, the ANCOVA results are considered the primary basis for the final interpretation of the study, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This exploratory study is the first to assess how a pearl millet-based diet influences immunomodulatory markers, physical performance, and recovery in athletes. Because pearl millet contains anti-inflammatory nutrients such as fiber, magnesium, and phenolic compounds, it may help modulate inflammation and support recovery.

In our pre-test findings, most parameters in the present study showed no significant differences, except power and Hs-CRP, which highlight the pre-test non-variability that would influence the study outcomes. To overcome this, an ANCOVA test was used (Table 2). After the 60-day intervention, no significant between-group differences in body weight, BF%, and muscle mass after adjusting for baseline values. These results suggest that the pearl millet intervention did not elicit measurable changes in overall body composition within the study duration. Regularly consuming millets as a dietary staple for periods ranging from three months to several years significantly enhances physical growth and body composition39. Similarly, evidence from studies on other millets, including foxtail and finger millet, indicates inconsistent effects on body composition, with several short-term interventions reporting minimal or no significant changes in body weight, fat mass, or fat-free mass despite observed metabolic benefits40.

Although slight reductions in BF% and marginal increases in muscle mass were observed in the EG, these changes were not statistically significant when compared to the CG. This may indicate that the intervention duration (60 days) was insufficient to induce substantial changes in body composition, particularly in trained athletes who typically exhibit relatively stable anthropometric profiles41. Body composition is influenced by multiple factors, including dietary intake, training load, and energy balance. In athletic populations, these variables are often tightly regulated, which may limit the observable impact of a single dietary modification. Furthermore, previous research suggests that dietary interventions alone may not produce significant changes in body composition without concurrent alterations in training intensity or total energy intake42.

Hs-CRP is a well-established biomarker of low-grade systemic inflammation and is commonly used to assess inflammatory responses associated with physiological stress and training load43. In the present study, no significant between-group differences in Hs-CRP were observed following the 60-day intervention, after adjusting for baseline values using ANCOVA. Although descriptive trends indicated a slight increase in the EG and a reduction in the CG, these changes were not statistically significant, suggesting that pearl millet supplementation did not have a measurable impact on systemic inflammation.

Similarly, IL-6 and TNF-α did not show significant between-group differences after the intervention. TNF-α is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in inflammatory signalling pathways, whereas IL-6 exhibits context-dependent behaviour, acting as a pro-inflammatory marker under chronic conditions but demonstrating transient anti-inflammatory effects when released during acute exercise. The absence of significant changes in these cytokines may reflect physiological adaptations to regular training and the relatively short duration of the intervention44.The findings of the present study differ from previous experimental research in animal models, which showed that pearl millet supplementation reduced Hs-CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels after prolonged exposure45. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in study design, duration, dosage, and population characteristics, as animal models often exhibit more pronounced metabolic and inflammatory responses compared to trained human athletes. It is also important to consider that athletes typically exhibit well-regulated inflammatory responses as a result of chronic training adaptations. As a result, baseline inflammatory markers may already be within optimal ranges, limiting the potential for further reduction through dietary interventions alone. Overall, the findings suggest that pearl millet supplementation does not significantly alter systemic inflammatory markers in trained athletes over a short-term period. Future studies with longer intervention durations, controlled training loads, and larger sample sizes are warranted to better understand the potential immunomodulatory effects of pearl millet in this population.

A significant improvement in strength was observed in the EG. No clinical trials have shown the effect of millet on handgrip strength in the athletic population. However, Nutrient strength associations reported in earlier studies support this trend; diets rich in magnesium, retinol, vitamins, and oily fish, vitamin E, iron, vitamin B12, folate and vitamin C, Omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids, have been positively linked to greater handgrip strength in the athletic population46–48. The strength gains observed in the present study may be partly explained by the nutrient composition of pearl millet. Its amino acids support muscle protein synthesis, while its micronutrient profile, particularly magnesium and phosphorus, contributes to neuromuscular function and ATP regeneration during high-intensity efforts49. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that pearl millet may enhance muscular strength in athletes.

The vertical jump, 20 m sprint, and Illinois agility test were conducted to measure the athletes’ lower-body power, speed, and agility. No significant changes were observed in either group in the post-test. 4-week high-fibre supplementation (17 g/kg) did not significantly affect vertical jump power or sprint performance in basketball players48. Improved performance skills are influenced not only by the dietary intervention but also by continuous training and practice.

The Yo-Yo intermittent recovery level 1 test was used to assess participants’ endurance. A significant improvement was observed in endurance speed, VO2Max (derived from equation), and distance covered in EG, while no effect was observed in CG, with a significant improvement in the mean difference between EG and CG. The endurance gains in our study may be linked to the nutrient composition of pearl millet. Being rich in complex carbohydrates, healthy fats, protein, and fibre, pearl millet provides sustained energy release, supporting prolonged aerobic activity49,50.The micronutrient content of pearl millet, such as B-vitamins, iron, magnesium, and omega-6 fatty acids, supports oxygen-carrying capacity in the blood, mitochondrial energy production, and neuromuscular function51,52. Additionally, the high antioxidant content of pearl millet49 may reduce oxidative stress and delay muscle fatigue, ultimately improving physical performance in athletes53,54. Recent studies suggest that specific bacterial taxa can increase in response to endurance training and produce metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which may serve as additional substrates supporting energy metabolism during prolonged exercise55. For example, Veillonella atypica has been shown to metabolise exercise-induced lactate into propionate, thereby enhancing endurance performance in experimental models55.

This decrease in RHR may reflect improved cardiovascular efficiency, possibly linked to the iron content of pearl millet, which contributes to haemoglobin synthesis and oxygen transport, as supported by recent evidence on millet consumption and improvements in iron status56. Its high fibre, mineral, and antioxidant content can help regulate cholesterol levels and reduce systemic inflammation, both of which are beneficial for heart health49. Magnesium in pearl millet is also associated with improved heart rate variability and reduced resting heart rate, both of which are markers of improved cardiovascular fitness49. No significant changes in recovery heart rate were observed at 1, 2, 3, and 15 min in either group.

Improvements in handgrip strength and endurance performance, the research suggests that pearl millet could be a valuable dietary component for athletes aiming to improve performance and recovery. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the inclusion of nutrient-dense, functional foods like pearl millet in sports nutrition. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms involved and to determine whether the effects observed in the study persist for an extended period and improve athletic performance and recovery.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Although participants were randomly allocated and most baseline characteristics were comparable between the experimental and control groups, statistically significant baseline differences were observed for certain variables, particularly power and Hs-CRP.

The study evaluated multiple physiological, performance, and biochemical outcomes without formal correction for multiple comparisons. As a result, the possibility of Type I error cannot be ruled out, and statistically significant findings should be interpreted with caution.

The sample size, although determined a priori, was relatively modest and may have limited the ability to detect small but meaningful between-group differences. Furthermore, the inclusion of only male athletes restricts the generalisability of the findings to female athletes and other populations. Several outcome measures were assessed using field-based methods, including vertical jump performance, which was assessed using the Sargent Jump Test, a widely used and reliable field-based measure of lower-body explosive power. However, some studies have suggested that the chalk-and-wall method may influence jump performance accuracy, as participants must simultaneously focus on achieving maximal jump height and marking the wall, which may alter natural jumping mechanics and slightly affect the actual performance outcome and heart rate assessment via carotid pulse palpation. While practical in applied settings, these methods are subject to observer variability and are less precise than instrumented laboratory techniques. In addition, aerobic capacity was not directly measured; VO₂ max was estimated from Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test performance using predictive equations and should therefore be interpreted as a derived indicator rather than a direct physiological measure. With respect to biochemical assessments, Hs-CRP was the primary marker of systemic inflammation evaluated. A broader panel of inflammatory cytokines, immune markers, and muscle damage indicators would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the physiological responses to the intervention. Moreover, gut microbiota composition and related metabolites were not assessed, limiting insights into the potential mechanisms underlying pearl millet’s effects. The study employed a single-blind design, in which only the data analyst was blinded to group allocation, potentially introducing bias. Overall, these methodological considerations suggest that the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Future research incorporating larger and more diverse samples, double-blind designs, direct physiological measurements, comprehensive biomarker profiling, and advanced analytical approaches is warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This exploratory study assessed the effects of a 60-day pearl millet dietary intervention in athletes. No significant between-group differences were observed in anthropometric and body composition parameters after adjusting for baseline values. However, significant improvements were found in selected performance outcomes, including handgrip strength, endurance distance, VO₂ max, and cumulative time in the EG. These findings suggest a potential role for pearl millet in enhancing physical performance, possibly due to its nutrient-rich profile, which supports energy availability and metabolic efficiency. Overall, while body composition remained unchanged, pearl millet demonstrated promising benefits for performance. Further studies with larger samples and longer durations are needed to confirm these findings.
