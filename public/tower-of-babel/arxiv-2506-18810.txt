ConciseHint: Boosting Efficient Reasoning via Continuous Concise Hints during Generation
Siao Tang, Xinyin Ma, Gongfan Fang, Xinchao Wang

Abstract
Recent advancements in large reasoning models (LRMs) like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 series have achieved notable performance enhancements on complex reasoning tasks by scaling up the generation length by Chain-of-Thought (CoT). However, an emerging issue is their inclination to produce excessively verbose reasoning processes, leading to the inefficiency problem. Existing literature on improving efficiency mainly adheres to the before-reasoning paradigms such as prompting and reasoning or fine-tuning and reasoning, but ignores the promising direction of directly encouraging the model to speak concisely by intervening during the generation of reasoning. In order to fill the blank, we propose a framework dubbed ConciseHint, which continuously encourages the reasoning model to speak concisely by injecting the textual hint (manually designed or trained on the concise data) during the token generation of the reasoning process. Besides, ConciseHint is adaptive to the complexity of the query by adaptively adjusting the hint intensity, which ensures it will not undermine model performance. Experiments on the state-of-the-art LRMs, including DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen-3 series, demonstrate that our method can effectively produce concise reasoning processes while maintaining performance well. For instance, we achieve a reduction ratio of 65% for the reasoning length on GSM8K benchmark with Qwen-3 4B with nearly no accuracy loss.

Therefore, an orthogonal and unexplored question arises: Is it possible to guide the reasoning model to speak more concisely by intervening during the generation of the intermediate reasoning steps? We point out two key points needed to answer this question: one is to design an approach to enable effective intervention during reasoning, and another is to select an optimal intensity of intervention adaptively to a given query.

To fill the blank, we propose a framework called ConciseHint, which performs intervention during the generation of reasoning, encouraging the model to speak concisely by injecting textual hints, as illustrated in
Figure

1
. Specifically, ConciseHint continuously influences the reasoning by injecting the hint that can either be a manually designed plug-and-play text (e.g., “-<|im_start|>make answer concise!<|im_end|>”) or continuous embeddings trained on a concise dataset. The hint will encourage the following token generation to be more concise. Additionally, ConciseHint adaptively adjust the injection intensity according to the complexity of the query, as easy queries can usually tolerate a larger compression ratio than complex ones. This complexity-adaptive strategy facilitates a good efficiency-accuracy balance by employing a lower hint intensity for complex queries and a higher intensity for easy ones. Moreover, ConciseHint dynamically adjusts the position of injection to ensure a good computing-accuracy balance.

Related Works

2.1
Reasoning models and their inefficiency issues

The emergence of chain-of-thought
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
)
endowed LLMs with powerful reasoning ability through explicitly generating intermediate reasoning steps. Initially, vanilla LLMs such as GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al. 2024
)
and PaLM
(
Chowdhery et al. 2023
)
can obtain the enhanced reasoning ability by few-shot
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
or zero-shot
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
prompting.
Recently, large reasoning models such as Gemini-2.5
(
Deepmind 2025
)
, OpenAI-o1
(
Jaech et al. 2024
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
have internalized the reasoning ability by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL), no longer needing manual prompting. While demonstrating superior performance compared to common models, reasoning models incur high computational costs due to the detailed and usually verbose reasoning process
(
Qu et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Chang et al. 2024
)
, leading to inefficiency in reasoning. For example, substantial works point out that reasoning models usually overthink simple queries
(
Qu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
)
, generate verbose multiple rounds of self-check
(
Qu et al. 2025
;
Fu et al. 2025
)
, and allocate a substantial proportion of tokens to support textual coherence
(
Su et al. 2025
)
rather than the core reasoning advancement. These sorts of inefficiency issues result in the waste of computational resources and energy.

2.2
Efficient methods for reasoning models

Recently, researchers have paid attention to alleviating the inefficiency of large reasoning models. Note that in this work, we only focus on the reasoning efficiency in single-sample generation rather than improving the sampling efficiency of test-time scaling methods such as Self-Consistency
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
or Best-of-N
(
Lightman et al. 2024
)
. Existing methods can be roughly divided into three groups
(
Qu et al. 2025
)
, i.e., efficient reasoning during inference, with SFT, and with RL. SFT-based methods either fine-tune the reasoning model to internalize the concise reasoning patterns on the curated concise datasets
(
Xia et al. 2025
;
Munkhbat et al. 2025
)
, or replace explicit token generation in the reasoning process by predicting answers based on internal latent representations
(
Deng et al. 2024
;
Hao et al. 2024
)
. RL-based methods
(
Shen et al. 2025
;
Luo et al. 2025
)
usually incorporate the length constraint into the reward function to encourage conciseness. In contrast, efficient reasoning during inference follows the training-free scheme, which is easy to use and more resource-friendly as a plug-and-play tool. For example, CCoT
(
Renze and Guven 2024
)
appends a prompt of “Be concise” to the input to encourage answering concisely. TALE
(
Han et al. 2024
)
uses the model itself to predict the number of tokens required to solve the query, and then prompts the model by “use less than <number> tokens”. Sketch-of-thought
(
Aytes et al. 2025
)
propose to tackle different types of tasks with different crafted system prompts. Our work also belongs to efficient reasoning during inference.

The previous literature mainly conforms to the paradigm of prompting or optimizing the model before using it to perform reasoning, but does not intervene in the model to make it speak more concisely during the actual token generation stage for reasoning. Although
Wang et al. 2025
propose to reduce the decoding probability of certain words, they mainly focus on making each candidate solution more thorough rather than reducing the overall reasoning length. In this work, we aim to explore whether we can enhance the conciseness by continuously exerting influence during the reasoning process.

3
The Proposed ConciseHint Framework

In this section, we elaborate on our proposed ConciseHint that encourages models to speak concisely by continuously and adaptively exerting influence on the reasoning process. ConciseHint injects textual hint into the reasoning process to enhance efficiency. To avoid excessive intervention in complex queries while maintaining intensive intervention for easy queries, ConciseHint scales up the injection interval adaptively, making it positively correlated with the current length. To avoid compromising accuracy in the early reasoning stage and achieve computational savings in the later stage, ConciseHint dynamically determines the injection position, from head to tail progressively. Besides, we demonstrate that even though our manually designed hint achieves good efficiency improvement in a training-free way (i.e., ConciseHint), incorporating the training of embeddings of the hint on a concise dataset can further reduce the token usage (i.e., ConciseHint-T).

ConciseHint injects the hint like “-<|im_start|>make answer concise!<|im_end|>-” in the reasoning process. For instance, if the original text is “Okay, let me try to figure out this problem. The problem says a robe takes 2 bolts of blue fiber and half that much white fiber” will be modified to “Okay,-<|im_start|>make answer concise!<|im_end|>- let me try to figure out this problem. The problem says a robe takes 2 bolts of blue fiber and half that much white fiber”. Injecting the hint can encourage the following token generation to be more concise. However, a critical problem is how to select an optimal injection intensity for a given query. An excessively high injection intensity will harm the accuracy, particularly for complex queries, while a low intensity will decrease the efficiency improvement (see
Table

3
in the ablation study). We propose to tackle this problem from a complexity-adaptive perspective. We model the control of the injection intensity as the selection of the injection interval, i.e., the number of tokens between two adjacent injections. We propose a complexity-adaptive and dynamic interval control, formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=\alpha+\beta\cdot l_{k},\ \ \alpha>0,\ \beta>0,

(1)

where
\tau_{k}
is the current injection interval,
l_{k}
denotes the current length of the reasoning process, i.e., the number of current output tokens.
\alpha
is the basic length of the injection interval, and
\beta
is a positive coefficient to control the strength of adaptivity. In all our experiments, we fixed
\alpha
to 128 (a small value) and
\beta
to
0.2
to avoid manual hyper-parameter tuning, and we find it always works well for various models and benchmarks. The injection interval
\tau_{k}
is a linear function of the current length, which indicates that the hint intensity will increase with the current reasoning length. Every time when
\tau_{k}
is computed, the model will generate the next
\tau_{k}
tokens, and then inject the hint. For example, when generating the first token, the current length is 0, then
\tau_{k}
should be
\alpha
, so the model will generate
\tau_{k}
tokens and inject the hint into the generated text.

Here, we hold a prior that the reasoning length of a query is approximately positively correlated with its complexity
(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
, and the intuitive assumption that the redundancy ratio is positively correlated to the complexity level, or in other words, easy queries can tolerate a larger compression ratio than complex ones. Therefore, when the current length
l_{k}
is small, the injection interval is set to a small value, resulting in a larger hint intensity. The reasoning of easy queries will complete in a short length, such as hundreds of tokens, so their average hint intensity is large. If the length continues to increase, it will indicate that this query should be complex rather than easy, so our
Equation

1
accordingly relieves the hint intensity by increasing the injection interval
\tau_{k}
, avoiding excessive hinting that harms the accuracy. This adaptive strategy avoids manually setting the injection interval based on prior estimation of the complexity, which is usually intractable.

Another problem is how to select the position to inject the hint text. Let
T
denote the original generated text whose length is
\tau_{k}
,
p
denote the position of injection, and
T_{hint}
denote the textual hint. Then, the modified text after injection will be:

\displaystyle T^{\prime}=T[0:p]+T_{hint}+T[p:\tau_{k}-1],\ \ p\in[0,\tau_{k}-1].

(2)

We reveal two important rules about the selection of injection position
p
:

(i) During the early reasoning,
p
should not be too close to
\tau_{k}-1
to avoid accuracy degradation. Concretely, if
p
is very close to
\tau_{k}-1
, the injected hint will approach the tail of the generated text. In this case, we observe that the subsequent generation will soon conclude the reasoning or just lazily repeat the text generated after the last hint (see case studies), which significantly undermines accuracy, as shown in
Table

4
.

(ii)
p
should not be too close to
0
. Although injecting the hint into the head solves the accuracy degradation problem, it introduces extra computing costs caused by prefilling the text between the injection position and the end, i.e.,
T[p:\tau_{k}-1]
.

Therefore, to ensure a good computing-efficiency balance, we propose a dynamic selection strategy for the position
p
, formulated as follows:

\displaystyle p=\tau_{k}*\min(\ (\tau_{k}-\alpha)/1024,\ 0.8\ ),

(3)

where
\tau_{k}
is the current injection interval and
\alpha
is the basic injection interval length, the same as those in
Equation

1
. During the early reasoning,
\tau_{k}
is small, so the injection position is close to the head, avoiding the aforementioned accuracy degradation. As the reasoning proceeds,
\tau_{k}
becomes larger, the injection position moves towards the tail to save prefilling costs.
Note that, as is well-known, prefilling is much faster than decoding, so with our strategy, the extra latency caused by prefilling is negligible.

Combination with prompt-based methods.
ConciseHint is an intervention-in-reasoning method, so it has the potential to be combined with efficiency methods which are applied before the generation of reasoning. For example, adding extra control prompts or fine-tuning a more concise model in advance. In this paper, we validate the effectiveness of combining ConciseHint and prompting-based methods. Besides the baseline of the prompting method, we also propose a stronger prompt called “AdaP”, which achieves a much better efficiency. Specifically, we append the prompt of “Please adaptively control the answer length based on the query’s complexity. The lower the complexity, the more concise your answer should be.” We also combine ConciseHint and AdaP to further improve the efficiency.

ConciseHint-T: training the embeddings of hint.
Even though the plug-and-play ConciseHint effectively improves the efficiency in a training-free way, further training the hint embeddings can bring additional token reduction. Concretely, firstly, we prepare a dataset consisting of questions and corresponding concise reasoning responses. Next, we construct modified reasoning responses by injecting hint embeddings to be trained into the original responses at a fixed interval. We initialize the hint embeddings as the embeddings of our manually designed hint (
\mathbf{E}_{ori}
) used in ConciseHint. Finally, we conduct supervised fine-tuning (like Prompt Tuning
(
Lester et al. 2021
)
) on the questions and corresponding modified responses, following the next-token prediction paradigm, and obtain the optimized hint embeddings
\mathbf{E}_{optim}
. We expect the hint embeddings to learn the inherent concise patterns in the concise reasoning responses. Then, ConciseHint-T uses the optimized hint embeddings and thus further reduces token usage. Moreover, we observe that we can control the token usage through the interpolation between the initial hint embeddings and the optimized embeddings. The interpolation embeddings can be derived from:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{interp}=\alpha*\mathbf{E}_{optim}+(1-\alpha)*\mathbf{E}_{ori},\ \alpha\in[0,1]

(4)

Controllability can be achieved by adjusting
\alpha
, where a higher value usually leads to less token usage.
\alpha=1
denotes our ConciseHint-T, while
\alpha=0
is ConciseHint.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental setup

Benchmarks.
We validate our method on commonly-used benchmarks for large reasoning models, i.e., GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
, AIME24
(
Committees 2024
)
, and GPQA-Diamond
(
Rein et al. 2024
)
.
GSM8K(Grade School Math 8K) consists of more than 8,000 high-quality quality linguistically diverse grade school math word problems. We use the test split containing 1,319 problems. AIME24 consists of 30 mathematical problems from the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME24), a renowned high school math competition recognized for its difficult and thought-provoking problems. GPQA-diamond consists of 198 high-quality and challenging multiple-choice questions written by domain experts in biology, physics, and chemistry.

Models.
We evaluate our method on the state-of-the-art open-source large reasoning models including Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-1.7B
(
Alibaba 2025
)
, and DeepSeek-R1-14B
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, which deliver remarkable advancements in tackling a wide range of reasoning tasks.

Baselines.
The basic baseline is the original reasoning model without ConciseHint and any other efficiency technique. Then, we apply our ConciseHint to the original model and compare with it. Besides, we also compare ConciseHint with a commonly-used
(
Renze and Guven 2024
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
prompting-based method called BeConcise which adds “Be concise.” to the original input, and our proposed stronger AdaP prompting. Finally, we add ConciseHint to AdaP prompting to validate the effectiveness of the combination of before-reasoning prompting and our intervention-in-reasoning method.

Evaluation configurations.
For all experiments, we set the temperature to 0.6 and top-p to 0.95. For GSM8K and GPQA-diamond benchmarks, we repeat running for 5 times and calculate the average accuracy. For AIME24, we run 10 times. We report token usage, i.e., the number of tokens to answer a query, to measure the efficiency. All input tokens and output tokens (including the injected hints) are counted. We report the average token usage across the dataset.

We thoroughly analyzed the experimental results in

and derived the following three key conclusions:

(i)
ConciseHint consistently improve the reasoning efficiency by an obvious margin, while maintaining the model performance well.
Let’s delve into the two comparisons, i.e., Ori. vs. Ori.+Ours, and AdaP vs.Adap+Ours.

On the GSM8K benchmark, compared to Ori., Ori.+Ours reduces 48.9% tokens of Qwen3-4B, from 2381 to 1213, with only an accuracy loss of 0.07. Compared to Adap, Adap+Ours reduces 33.6% tokens, from 1263 to 839, even with an accuracy rise of 0.19. This significant token reduction also appears on Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-R1-14B.

On the GPQA-Diamond benchmark, Ori.+Ours reduces 35.0% tokens of Qwen3-8B, from 8508 to 5533, with an accuracy rise from 56.87 to 57.98. Compared to Adap, Adap+Ours reduces 38.8% tokens of Qwen3-8B, from 6223 to 3808. And the reduction ratio is around 40% and 28% on Qwen3-4B and DeepSeek-R1-14B, respectively.

On the AIME24 benchmark, Ori.+Ours reduces the token usage of Qwen3-4B from 11634 to 10523, while improving the accuracy from 64.33 to 66.67, and reduces the token usage of Qwen3-8B from 11725 to 11228, while improving the accuracy from 64.67 to 67.33.
Compared to AdaP, AdaP+Ours reduces the token usage of Qwen-4B from 10755 to 9255, while improving the accuracy from 63.67 to 67.00, and reduces the token usage of Qwen-8B from 10693 to 9996, while improving the accuracy from 68.00 to 69.67. ConciseHint on Deepseek-R1-14B also obtains an obvious token reduction. Note that although a special case occurs on Deepseek-R1-14B where AdaP+Ours decreases the accuracy from 64.47 to 58.67, it can be improved by adjusting
\alpha
. For instance, the accuracy will rise to 62.33 if we set
\alpha
to 512. Although we try to fix
\alpha
to a small value in order to handle tasks with various complexity levels in general scenarios, adjusting the hyper-parameters can bring benefits in particular scenarios where we can get a rough prior about complexity. For example, we know the queries from AIME24 are usually challenging. Finally, the results indicate that the ratio of token reduction on the AIME24 benchmark is generally smaller than GSM8K and GPQA-Diamond. We attribute this to the great difficulty of AIME24, if we regard the reasoning length as a good metric
(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
to estimate a query’s complexity for a model. The average reasoning length on AIME24 is from 9K to 11K, much larger than those on GSM8K or GPQA-Diamond. And we assume the inherent redundancy ratio of difficult queries tends to be small.

(ii)
Individually applying ConciseHint also achieves a promising efficiency enhancement which is comparable to strong prompting-based methods.
Generally, our AdaP achieves a better accuracy-efficiency balance than BeConcise, indicating it is a stronger prompting-based method, so we mainly focus on the comparison between Ori.+Ours and AdaP.

On the GSM8K and AIME24 benchmark, Adap and Ori.+Ours achieve similar accuracy and token usage reduction, both obtain an obviously better accuracy-efficiency balance than the original one. For example, on GSM8K with Qwen3-4B, they reduce token usage from 2381 to 1263 (-47.0%) and 1213(-49.1%), respectively.

On the GPQA-Diamond benchmark, Ori+Ours shows a larger ratio of token reduction than Adap. For example, with Qwen3-4B, Ori+Ours and Adap reduce token usage from 7463 to 4319 (-42.1%) and 5236 (-29.8%), respectively.

(iii)
Combining ConciseHint and before-reasoning prompting methods can further enhance the efficiency.
As mentioned before, ConciseHint improves the efficiency by intervening in the reasoning process. The experimental results also demonstrate that it can be combined with before-reasoning (here we test the prompting-based) methods. Let’s focus on the comparison between Ori. and AdaP+Ours.

On the GSM8K and GPQA-Diamond benchmarks, AdaP+Ours achieves a significant token reduction ratio with minor accuracy loss. Specifically, on the GSM8K, it obtains a reduction ratio of 64.7% (2381–>839), 64.7% (2382–>935), and 45.3% (981–>537) on Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and DeepSeek-R1-14B, respectively.
On the GPQA-Diamond, it obtains a reduction ratio of 57.8% (7463–>3150), 55.2% (8508–>3808), and 40.0% (5036–>3023), respectively.

On the AIME24 benchmark, AdaP+Ours achieves a token reduction ratio of 20.4% (11634–>9255), 14.7% (11725–>9996), and 22.2% (9210–>7169), respectively. It’s worth noting that it increases the accuracy from 64.33 to 67.00 and from 64.67 to 69.97 on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, respectively.
Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of ConciseHint to improve the accuracy-efficiency balance.

4.3
Ablation studies

Through ablation studies, we demonstrate the necessity of adaptively controlling the injection intensity based on the complexity (
Equation

1
), and the necessity of dynamically determining the position of hint injection (
Equation

3
). We also present corresponding cases to make it clearer.

The necessity of adaptively controlling the injection intensity.
Recall that our method continuously scales up the injection interval to make it positively correlated with the current length. This strategy avoids excessive intervention in complex problems while ensuring a high intensity of intervention in easy problems. We use
Table

3
to quantitatively demonstrate it, where “Fixed” denotes that the injection interval is set to the fixed value, and the injection intensity is inversely proportional to the interval. We conduct experiments on AIME24 and GSM8K, as their complexity levels differ a lot. From the results, we can conclude that a high intensity of hint injection impairs the performance of complex queries, but has little effect on simple queries. For example, using the fixed interval of 64 significantly decreases the accuracy of Qwen3-4B from 67.00 to 45.33 on AIME24, but on the GSM8K, the accuracy loss is minor. And it decreases the accuracy of Qwen3-8B from 69.67 to 61.67 on AIME24, but it would even slightly improve the accuracy from 95.51 to 95.65 on GSM8K. Therefore, to avoid performance degradation, we need to relieve the injection intensity for complex queries. In the circumstances where we can know the approximate complexity of a given query in advance, we can just set a larger fixed interval for those complex queries. For example, we know the AIME24 is a challenging benchmark. However, it is usually intractable to know the complexity of a wild query, and we do not want to turn it into a hyper-parameter selection problem. Therefore, adaptively adjusting the interval using our
Equation

1
is essential.

The necessity of dynamically determining the position of hint injection.
We discuss the influence of the selection of the injection position. Recall that as the reasoning proceeds, our method dynamically moves the injection position from the head towards the tail, to avoid accuracy degradation in the early reasoning stage and save computing in the later reasoning stage. We compare our method to three fixed position selection strategies, i.e., injecting at the tail, in the middle, and at the head. The experimental results in
Table

4
indicate that the closer the fixed position is to the head, the better the accuracy it achieves. Specifically, injecting at the tail induces a significant accuracy degradation, from 55.25 to 43.03. Injecting in the middle achieves a comparable accuracy to ours, but causes the rise of token usage. Moreover, although injecting at the head slightly improves the accuracy, it increases the computing a lot due to the 100% token prefilling. Therefore, to avoid both accuracy degradation and computing increase, our dynamic position selection is essential. Note that, although in
Equation

3
we use the fixed parameters to control the speed of moving the position (1024) and the maximum position (0.8), it is possible to tune them for computing-accuracy balance.

Case studies

We present case studies in
Figure

3
to demonstrate the ablation studies more clearly, showing how the injection intensity and position affect the reasoning. The upper panel shows a sample from AIME24 benchmark with Qwen3-4B when the injection interval is fixed to 64, leading to a high hint intensity (corresponds to row 9 in
Table

3
). We can see that the model directly terminates the output after generating “Rotation by 135° (3/8 of a full rotation)” and gives no final answer under the intensive hint, resulting in a significant accuracy degradation.

The lower panel shows a sample from GPQA-Diamond benchmark with Qwen3-8B when the hint is injected at the tail (corresponds to row 3 in
Table

4
). On the first query, the model swiftly ends the thinking in the early stage after “Let me recall: benzoquinone has two carbonyl groups”, and then gives its final answer, and this sort of insufficient thinking will reduce the accuracy. On the second query, the model repeats the text between two adjacent hints, i.e., “Step-by-step explanation: step1… step2… step3”. The underlying reason lies in the fact that generating tokens immediately following the hint predisposes the model to lazily recycle textual outputs previously generated following the last hint, which will harm the performance. Therefore, moving the position forward can alleviate this problem, as it leaves enough tokens after the hint.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we propose an intervention-in-reasoning framework dubbed ConciseHint to boost the efficient reasoning of large reasoning models. Different from mainstream methods that try to enhance the efficiency before the actual reasoning, we highlight a promising paradigm of performing intervention during the generation of the reasoning process to make it more concise. ConciseHint injects the textual hints (manually designed or trained on the concise data) into the reasoning process to encourage the model to speak more concisely. To avoid accuracy degradation for complex queries due to excessive hints, ConciseHint adaptively controls the injection interval according to the complexity of the query. Besides, it dynamically adjusts the injection position, to avoid accuracy degradation that happens in the early reasoning stage and achieves a good computing-accuracy balance. We conduct experiments on GSM8K, AIME24, and GPQA-Diamond benchmarks with the state-of-the-art reasoning models DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3 series. The results demonstrate our method effectively improves the reasoning efficiency while maintaining the performance well, indicating that the intervention-in-reasoning is a promising direction for boosting reasoning efficiency. Moreover, the results also indicate that our method has the potential to be combined with before-reasoning methods to further enhance the efficiency.
