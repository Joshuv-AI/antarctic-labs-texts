Novel screening tool for psychiatric samples with major depressive disorder: a two-centre study
Authors: Nian Liu, Guangdong Hou, Xiayin Li, Bin Zhao, Zhiyong Cao, Guangyao Li, Songbai Ding, Jianping Shi, Zhongyi Bai, Xueming Xu, Dongping Liu, Zhiqin Gao, Haiying Yu, Min Cai, Hua-Ning Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) poses a significant global public health challenge. Although the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is widely used for MDD screening, its validation in psychiatric samples is limited. Additionally, variability in cut-off values across populations may lead to diagnostic inaccuracies. This study aims to develop a highly accurate nomogram tailored to improve MDD screening precision in psychiatric samples.MethodsFrom March 2024 to February 2025, participants were consecutively recruited from psychiatric outpatient clinics at Xijing Hospital and the 904 Hospital, assigned to training and validation cohorts. Using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression for dimensionality reduction and predictor identification, we then weighted key factors via multivariable logistic regression to construct a clinically interpretable nomogram. The model’s discriminative ability, calibration, clinical utility and improvement metrics underwent rigorous evaluation.FindingsIn the training cohort (n=519) and validation cohort (n=225), 53.95% and 53.33% of participants were diagnosed with MDD. We constructed a nomogram using 11 predictors selected from 20 variables. In internal validation, the nomogram’s area under the curve (AUC) (95% CI) surpassed the PHQ-9’s (0.932 (0.910–0.954) vs 0.874 (0.844–0.905), p<0.001). Similarly, in external validation, the nomogram’s AUC (95% CI) was higher than the PHQ-9’s (0.935 (0.902–0.968) vs 0.888 (0.844–0.932), p<0.001). Moreover, the nomogram demonstrated better performance than the PHQ-9 in calibration and clinical utility during both internal and external validation. These improvements suggest that the nomogram has the potential to reduce false-positive and false-negative rates in MDD screening.ConclusionThis study developed a novel tool for MDD screening in psychiatric samples by integrating weighted relevant factors, which demonstrates significantly improved accuracy in identifying MDD.Keywords: Depressive Disorder; Patient Health Questionnaire; Social Work, Psychiatric; Mental Health Services

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) constitutes a major global public health challenge due to its high prevalence, recurrence rates and associated disability. An estimated 5.0% of adults worldwide are affected by MDD, impacting over 350 million individuals1 and representing a nearly 50% increase in affected persons over the past three decades.2 MDD is also associated with a high risk of relapse, with approximately 60% of patients experiencing recurrence after initial treatment, 70% of these experiencing subsequent relapses and 90% of those with three episodes facing a fourth.3 Additionally, between 59% and 87% of patients who complete suicide have a background of MDD globally, imposing a substantial burden on individuals, families and society.4

In psychiatry, the gold standard for diagnosing MDD is via rigorously validated structured or semistructured clinical interviews.5 6 Nonetheless, considering the insufficient number of psychiatrists as well as the lack of venues and time, these interviews are not conducive to swift and efficient MDD screening, particularly within the realm of primary care. Therefore, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is extensively employed as a practical screening tool.7 While it has been validated globally across diverse countries and linguistic contexts, its validation in psychiatric populations remains relatively limited.8

Compared with primary care populations, patients with mental disorders usually present with more severe depressive symptoms and complex comorbidities.8 Research findings indicate that 41.1% of patients with MDD have at least one other co-occurring mental health disorder.9 Taking into account the distinctive traits of this population, a higher cut-off score may be essential to enhance screening precision. As indicated by research, within psychiatric samples, a PHQ-9 cut-off of ≥13 results in sensitivity and specificity of 0.83 and 0.72, respectively.8 Even though this demonstrates some screening utility of the PHQ-9, it still falls short of the desired level, thereby resulting in a relatively high incidence of misdiagnosis and missed diagnoses of MDD in clinical settings.

China’s large population and limited number of psychiatrists have significantly increased the pressure on psychiatric diagnosis and treatment. While recent advances in prediction models have improved MDD screening, practical application of rapid screening remains constrained by model complexity.10 11 Additionally, there is a notable lack of externally validated high-precision models for MDD screening in psychiatric samples. To address these challenges, this study seeks to develop a prospective nomogram by systematically analysing the dimensions and weight distribution of core depressive symptoms. The aim is to enhance the accuracy and clinical applicability of MDD screening for psychiatric samples.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from March 2024 to February 2025 from the psychiatric outpatient clinics of Xijing Hospital of the Air Force Medical University and the 904 Hospital of Joint Logistics Support Force. All participants were aged between 12 and 70 years, without gender restrictions, and were required to be capable of independently completing the questionnaire, understanding the trial and providing signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised severe psychiatric illnesses that hindered cooperation, a history of severe somatic diseases (eg, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism and tumours), severe neurological disorders, communication comprehension difficulties, language barriers, cognitive impairments or any other conditions deemed ineligible by the researchers. By February 2025, a total of 519 eligible participants from Xijing Hospital constituted the training cohort, while 225 eligible participants from the 904 Hospital constituted the validation cohort. The nomogram was developed using the internal training cohort and externally tested using the validation cohort. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital of the Air Force Medical University (number: KY20242298-C-1).

Research procedures

Demographic information of the participants was collected, including age, gender, education level, marital status and medical history. Eligible participants were required to complete the PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) questionnaires. Additionally, each subject underwent a face-to-face structured clinical interview conducted by two senior psychiatrists to confirm the diagnosis.

The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are two validated self-assessment tools used to evaluate an individual’s depressive and anxiety symptoms over the past 2 weeks, respectively. The PHQ-9 consists of 9 items, while the GAD-7 comprises seven items; both are developed based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria and are widely used for the screening and assessment of depressive and anxiety symptoms in primary care settings.12 They use a 4-level scoring system, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with a score of 10 reported as the optimal cut-off value for both tools.12

The Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview 5.0.0 (MINI), a brief structured diagnostic tool aligning with DSM-IV and International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria, is a gold standard for diagnosing mental disorders.13 In this study, the MINI depression module was used to diagnose MDD, and the interview was completed in approximately 15 min. Prior to the MINI interview, psychiatrists and participants were unaware of the PHQ-9 scores. Participants with strong suicidal ideation and those with comorbid severe mental illnesses were advised to undergo standardised psychiatric treatment.

Nomogram construction and evaluation

In this study, we employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis, combined with 10-fold cross-validation, to reduce the dimensionality of the data and select the most valuable predictors (MVPs) based on a one-standard-error rule.14 This method is capable of identifying variables that have a decisive impact on the study outcomes and reducing binomial bias among numerous potential predictors. Subsequently, we used the regression coefficients of these MVPs in a binary logistic regression model to construct a nomogram, which visually displays the relative contributions of each predictor to the outcome. By calculating the area under the curve (AUC) of the nomogram’s receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and the confusion matrix,15 we assessed its discriminative ability and employed Delong’s test to compare the differences in AUC between the two tools.16 Additionally, we identified the optimal cut-off values for the nomogram and the PHQ-9 scale by recognising the point on the ROC curve with the maximum Youden’s index (sum of specificity and sensitivity minus 1).17 To evaluate the calibration performance of the nomogram, we used the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and plotted calibration curves to compare the nomogram’s predicted probabilities with the actual observed outcomes. Furthermore, we employed the Net Reclassification Index (NRI) to intuitively reflect the improvement effects of the predictive model.18 To further determine the clinical decision-making value of the nomogram, we conducted decision curve analysis to quantify the net benefit of using the nomogram at different probability thresholds.19 This methodological framework not only enhances the accuracy of the predictive model but also strengthens its applicability and interpretability in clinical practice (see figure 1 for the flow chart).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using The SPSS V.26 software and R V.4.2.3 software. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean and SD, and comparisons were made using the independent samples t-test; data not normally distributed were expressed as median and IQR, and comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the χ2 test or Fisher’s Exact test. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics

In the training and validation cohorts, the proportion of participants with a PHQ-9 score of 10 or above was 57.03% (296 out of 519) and 56.89% (128 out of 225), respectively. Additionally, the prevalence of MDD diagnoses was 53.95% (280 out of 519) and 53.33% (120 out of 225) in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. The severity of depressive symptoms exhibited a notably similar pattern between the two groups. On conducting a meticulous comparative analysis of the baseline characteristics of the training and validation cohorts, it was discerned that no statistically significant disparities were evident in key metrics such as age, gender, educational attainment, marital status as well as PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. Moreover, an in-depth examination of the distribution of different scoring categories for PHQ-9 and GAD-7 revealed that the distribution across various strata was indistinguishable between the training and validation cohorts, with p values uniformly exceeding 0.05. These findings underscore the robust concordance in baseline characteristics between the training and validation cohorts, thereby confirming the homogeneity of the two groups at the outset of the study (see table 1 and online supplemental table S1).

Identifying optimal predictors for MDD

To ensure the generalisability and predictive accuracy of the nomogram, we used the binomial deviation as the evaluation criterion and determined the optimal parameter λ through a 10-fold cross-validation LASSO regression model. Based on the optimal λ value, we selected 11 features with non-zero coefficients (items 1 to 8 of the PHQ-9 and items 3, 4 and 6 of the GAD-7) as the MVPs for MDD (see online supplemental figure S1).

The discriminative ability of the aforementioned predictive factors was assessed based on the AUC (95% CI). In the training cohort, the AUCs for the 11 predictive factors were 0.838 (0.801–0.874), 0.841 (0.805–0.877), 0.754 (0.710–0.797), 0.809 (0.769–0.848), 0.741 (0.698–0.784), 0.767 (0.725–0.809), 0.737 (0.694–0.781), 0.730 (0.686–0.773), 0.740 (0.696–0.784), 0.765 (0.723–0.807) and 0.760 (0.718–0.803). In the validation cohort, their AUCs (95% CI) were 0.875 (0.825–0.925), 0.843 (0.789–0.898), 0.743 (0.676–0.809), 0.795 (0.734–0.857), 0.715 (0.648–0.783), 0.765 (0.701–0.829), 0.701 (0.632–0.770), 0.724 (0.657–0.791), 0.749 (0.683–0.815), 0.762 (0.698–0.826) and 0.761 (0.697–0.825).

Constructing a nomogram

Based on multivariable logistic regression, it was found that low mood, anhedonia and fatigue are independent predictive factors for MDD. A forest plot of ORs was constructed to intuitively display the results of the logistic regression analysis (see online supplemental figure S2). Using the regression coefficients of 11 characteristics (1.206, 1.329, 0.482, 0.674, 0.622, 0.390, 0.494, 0.543, 0.299, 0.116, 0.354), a nomogram was plotted using R software (see figure 2). The length of the lines in the nomogram reflects the importance of the predictive factors, intuitively providing the weight of each characteristic in MDD. Anhedonia has the greatest weight in diagnosing MDD, followed by low mood, fatigue and changes in appetite, with the smallest proportion being difficulty relaxing. In the nomogram, anhedonia was set to 100 points. Based on the ORs of the regression coefficients, the scores for the 11 symptoms are 90 722, 100 000, 36 258, 50 677, 46 820, 29 311, 37 133, 40 880, 22 491, 8697 and 26 659, respectively. For ease of operation and use, we have scaled down the scores of 90, 100, 35, 50, 45, 30, 35, 40, 20, 10 and 25 by a factor of 5 (ie, 18, 20, 7, 10, 9, 6, 7, 8, 4, 2 and 5) as the final scores for the 11 symptoms.5 When using the nomogram to predict the risk of MDD in psychiatric outpatients, the scores of each item can be added together to obtain a total score. Ultimately, the risk of MDD can be determined by drawing a vertical line downward from the total score.

Internal and external validation of the nomogram

Through Spearman’s correlation analysis, we found that there was a significant positive correlation between the total score of the nomogram and the total score of the PHQ-9 in both the training cohort (correlation coefficient r=0.916, p<0.001) and the validation cohort (correlation coefficient r=0.929, p<0.001).

In the internal validation of the training cohort, the nomogram AUC (95% CI) was significantly higher than that of the PHQ-9 (0.932 (0.910–0.954) vs 0.874 (0.844–0.905), p<0.001) (see figure 3A). Similarly, the confusion matrix data showed that the nomogram model outperformed the PHQ-9 in classifying MDD and non-MDD, with correct rates of 253/280 and 199/239, respectively, compared with 242/280 and 185/239 for the PHQ-9 (see figure 3D). This confirmed the diagnostic accuracy advantage of the nomogram. The NRI was 0.098 (z=4.092, p <0.001), indicating a significant improvement in the reclassification ability of the nomogram over the PHQ-9. Additionally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test result indicated a good model fit, with a χ² value of 7.713 and a p value of 0.359. The calibration curve further confirmed the high consistency between the predicted probabilities of the Nomogram model and the actual observed values (see figure 3B). When assessing the clinical application efficacy, with the threshold probability set between 0 and 0.8, the net benefit produced by the Nomogram model was consistently superior to the PHQ-9 scale, suggesting that this model may provide higher value in clinical decision-making (see figure 3C).

In the external validation of the validation cohort, the nomogram AUC (95% CI) was significantly higher than that of the PHQ-9 (0.935 (0.902–0.968) vs 0.888 (0.844–0.932), p<0.001), indicating its superior discriminative ability (see figure 3E). The data from the confusion matrix further supported this, with the nomogram model achieving a correct classification rate of 114/120 in the MDD group and 85/105 in the non-MDD group, both higher than the PHQ-9’s rates of 105/120 and 82/105, highlighting its high accuracy in diagnosis (see figure 3H). The NRI was 0.104 (z=2.93, p=0.003), also confirming the enhancement in reclassification efficacy of the nomogram. Additionally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test result indicated a good model fit (χ² = 9.646, p=0.291), and the calibration curve showed strong consistency between observed and predicted values (see figure 3F). Furthermore, within the threshold probability range of 0 to 0.8, the nomogram demonstrated higher net benefit compared with the PHQ-9 (See figure 3G).

The optimal cut-off values of PHQ-9 and nomogram

Using the comprehensive dataset encompassing all participants within this investigation—spanning both the training and validation cohorts—onlinesupplemental tables S2 S3 present the relevant parameters of PHQ-9 and the novel tool at different cut-off values. These parameters encompass sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), negative likelihood ratio (LR-), positive utility index (UI+), negative utility index (UI-), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) and Youden’s index. Notably, the PHQ-9 demonstrates a harmonious equilibrium between sensitivity (0.818) and specificity (0.834) when employing a cut-off value of 10, which is in line with numerous previous studies.20 Conversely, the nomogram achieves an optimal balance of sensitivity (0.859) and specificity (0.896) at a cut-off value of 35. Furthermore, the nomogram surpasses the PHQ-9 in all aforementioned parameters, including sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR+, LR-, UI+, UI-, DOR and Youden’s index. A nomogram score of 35 or above serves as a robust indicator of a heightened probability of MDD in patients, thereby mandating an exhaustive psychiatric appraisal and subsequent specialised therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

In the past thirty years, the proportion of MDD in the global disease burden has surged by 61.1%.21 Notably, in China, fewer than 1% of MDD patients currently receive adequate treatment.9 There is an urgent need to develop a simple and efficient screening tool to effectively identify and manage this increasingly serious health issue. The PHQ-9 is the most commonly used screening tool for MDD in primary care, but it is rarely validated in populations with mental disorders. A study assessed the application of the PHQ-9 in patients with psychiatric illnesses and found that when the cut-off value was set at 13 (higher than the cut-off value of 10 in primary care samples), there was a relative balance between sensitivity and specificity.8 However, the efficacy appears suboptimal, with its low specificity likely resulting in a high rate of false positives. This study constructed a predictive model for MDD screening in psychiatric samples and prospectively collected data to validate its efficacy. The results showed that the model had excellent sensitivity (0.859) and specificity (0.896), effectively reducing the likelihood of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis, providing a powerful tool to improve the accuracy of MDD screening.

In psychiatric settings, depression and anxiety often co-occur, and there is some overlap in symptoms, which makes diagnosis and treatment more challenging. Research indicates that 45.7% of patients with MDD suffer from one or more anxiety symptoms.22 This phenomenon suggests that some symptoms of anxiety disorders may also serve as potential factors for predicting MDD. LASSO regression has also confirmed that items 3, 4 and 6 of the GAD-7 are effective predictors of MDD. Item 9 of the PHQ-9 is more sensitive and may increase the psychological burden on those being screened. Some studies have confirmed that removing it has a minimal impact on overall efficacy.5 7 12 Based on the above findings, we have constructed a nomogram that takes into account the weights of various predictive factors to assess an individual’s risk of developing MDD.

The diagnosis and treatment of MDD are fraught with challenges due to its high incidence, high recurrence rate, high disability rate and high degree of heterogeneity.23 Currently, psychiatric outpatient departments in hospitals lack simple and effective predictive models for the screening and diagnosis of MDD. Although studies have been conducted to analyse databases such as Gene Expression Omnibus, exploring the relationship between genetic factors and inflammatory markers with MDD has attempted to establish predictive tools like nomograms.23,26 However, these studies are all retrospective and have not been validated with prospective external data, which limits the reliability of their results. Despite the development of some predictive models for diagnosing MDD, their operational complexity has limited their application in clinical practice. As a screening tool for MDD in psychiatric outpatient departments, it should be simple and easy to perform, avoiding cumbersome, invasive or high-cost examinations. For instance, nomograms based on optical coherence tomography angiography10 and MRI for calculating hippocampal volume,11 while suitable for precise diagnosis of MDD patients, are not suitable as tools for large-scale screening. It is commendable that Zhou et al22 combined the PHQ-9, GAD-7 and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaires to construct a risk model for predicting MDD and subthreshold MDD. Nevertheless, the small sample size and lack of external validation may result in an overestimation of the performance, which restricts its widespread application.

The strengths of this study are threefold. First, we innovatively developed an externally validated predictive model for MDD screening among patients with mental disorders, which is crucial for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and addressing strained medical resources. Second, using independent cohort data from western and eastern China, we rigorously developed and validated the predictive model, ensuring its applicability and robustness across different regions. Third, by incorporating the weight of each item, we optimised the scale’s grading system, simplified the assessment process and reduced judgement errors caused by subjective factors. Nevertheless, our study has some limitations. For instance, the new tool did not include comparisons with other commonly used depression screening tools, such as the Hamilton Depression Scale. Additionally, the sample was predominantly derived from Han populations in eastern and western China, which may restrict the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Future work will involve comparing the tool with other established scales and conducting further validation in more diverse racial populations.

In conclusion, this study presents the first externally validated screening tool for MDD specifically designed for psychiatric samples, developed through integration of weighted predictive factors from both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales. The tool demonstrates superior discriminative ability compared with PHQ-9 alone, with robust performance observed in both training and validation cohorts. Additionally, the tool’s operational efficiency and low cost make it highly suitable for widespread implementation in outpatient psychiatric settings, ultimately reducing misdiagnosis and missed cases.
