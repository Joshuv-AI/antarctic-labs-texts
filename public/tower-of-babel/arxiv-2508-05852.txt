VISTA: Vision-Language Imitation of Situational Thinking and Attention for Human-Like Driver Focus in Dynamic Environments
Kaiser Hamid, Khandakar Ashrafi Akbar, Nade Liang

Abstract
Driver visual attention prediction is a critical task in autonomous driving and human-computer interaction (HCI) research. Most prior studies focus on estimating attention allocation at a single moment in time, typically using static RGB images such as driving scene pictures. In this work, we propose a vision-language framework that models the changing landscape of drivers’ gaze through natural language, using few-shot and zero-shot learning on single RGB images. We curate and refine high-quality captions from the BDD-A dataset using human-in-the-loop feedback, then fine-tune LLaVA to align visual perception with attention-centric scene understanding. Our approach integrates both low-level cues and top-down context (e.g., route semantics, risk anticipation), enabling language-based descriptions of gaze behavior. We evaluate performance across training regimes (few shot, and one-shot) and introduce domain-specific metrics for semantic alignment and response diversity. Results show that our fine-tuned model outperforms general-purpose VLMs in attention shift detection and interpretability. To our knowledge, this is among the first attempts to generate driver visual attention allocation and shifting predictions in natural language, offering a new direction for explainable AI in autonomous driving. Our approach provides a foundation for downstream tasks such as behavior forecasting, human-AI teaming, and multi-agent coordination.



1
Introduction

Driving is a highly visual task. Drivers’ visual attention allocation is critical for sufficient situation awareness and appropriate decision-making, particularly in safety-critical situations. Less optimal time and spatial allocation of visual attention (due to distraction, drowsiness, drunk driving etc.) is among the leading causes of road accidents
[
1
]
. For example, a disproportionate number of crashes occur at intersections because drivers fail to notice crossing traffic (often due to neglect or occlusion), and many lane-change collisions stem from not checking mirrors or blind spots
[
2
]
. Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) and autonomous vehicles (AVs) need sufficient understanding of the divers’ visual attention allocation in diverse scenarios to facilitate human-AI teaming in both driver-vehicle and vehicle-vehicle interactions in mixed-traffic environments. Furthermore, an automated vehicle that understands or mimic human-like attention allocation could better anticipate hazards and improve safety. Indeed, a key open question is whether incorporating human selective attention mechanisms can improve autonomous driving (AD) performance, a topic that remains under-explored in current research
[
3
]
.

Effectively modeling driver attention is challenging with conventional supervised learning models despite its importance. Training data typically consist of driver eye-tracking or gaze fixation annotations on driving scene recordings (e.g.,
[
4
]
), which are labor-intensive to collect and often limited in scenario diversity. Fully supervised models tend to be data hungry and struggle with domain-specific or long-tail scenarios that are rare in the training distributions
[
1
]
. For instance, dangerous but infrequent events (e.g., road debris, work zones, accidents) are underrepresented in datasets, leading to biased models that perform poorly in these corner cases
[
5
]
. As a result, even state-of-the-art end-to-end driving systems can be limited when faced with novel conditions or rare events not seen during training
[
4
]
. This data inefficiency motivates new approaches that can be learned from limited examples and still handle the diverse and evolving conditions of real roads.

Moreover, accurately modeling and interpreting driver attention—including the span and focus of attention—can significantly enhance cross-verification of the vehicle’s decisions and responses across different modules, such as sensor inputs, attention prediction systems, and planning components. By understanding where and what the system is focusing on, it becomes possible to validate whether a particular maneuver (e.g., a turn or stop) aligns with the perceived scene context. This attention modeling can also support real-time interventions, such as providing haptic feedback to passengers or initiating fallback maneuvers, including remote override through teleoperation in edge-case scenarios. Integrating attention-based insights into the decision-making pipeline also supports the formation of interpretable decision chains, ultimately strengthening system transparency, and strengthen user trust in human-AI teams.

To address the current issues and to lay foundation for robust autonomous driving systems, we introduce a novel few-shot driver attention modeling framework using a pre-trained vision-language model (LLaVA). By prompting the model with a small number of annotated examples, we enable attention reasoning without extensive supervised training. Our method predicts not only where a driver would look but also why, using free-form natural language. This in-context approach introduces a cognitively inspired, interpretable, and scalable paradigm for driving attention prediction.

2
Related Works

2.1
Driver Attention Modeling

Driver attention modeling has long been studied as a visual saliency prediction task, where the objective is to estimate spatial gaze heatmaps based on dashcam images or video sequences
[
6
]
. Early works applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to this task, achieving reasonable results by learning correlations between scene features and human eye fixations. Subsequent models incorporated temporal dynamics and multi-modal inputs to improve prediction accuracy, using architectures such as CNN-LSTMs and feature fusion from RGB frames, optical flow, and semantic segmentations
[
3
,
7
]
. Existing driving attention models typically fall in one of the two categories: 1) bottom-up models that emphasize low-level visual features (e.g., motion, brightness), and 2) top-down models that integrate task-level signals such as GPS coordinates, navigation intent, or traffic semantics
[
8
,
2
]
. An established cognitive engineering framework, the Salience Effort Expectancy Value (SEEV) model
[
9
]
, outlines four integrated factors influencing overt visual attention allocation. These factors collectively fall into the bottom-up (salience, effort) and top-down (expectancy, value) categories. However, this “black box" approach provides limited insights into the underlying cause or attribution of theses four components, making it challenging to develop persuasive mitigation strategies.

2.2
Vision-Language Approaches for Interpretable Attention

To enhance explainability of existing driver attention models, emerging work has applied vision-language models (VLMs) to model driver attention in a more interpretable and semantically rich way. Rather than outputting a heatmap, these models aim to describe the driver’s focus using natural language. For instance, “Attention Neural Baby Talk”
[
10
]
generates image captions that highlight hazardous elements, such as a pedestrian or cyclist, by aligning them with attention masks. Similarly, the DRAMA dataset
[
11
]
introduces driving videos annotated with natural-language question-answer pairs that explain risk, offering a human-interpretable rationale for attentional focus (e.g.,“The bicycle ahead is swerving into traffic”). These vision-language approaches suggest that semantic understanding and risk reasoning can complement or even surpass saliency maps in capturing true driver intent and awareness.

2.3
Large Vision-Language Models and In-Context Learning

Modern VLMs such as BLIP
[
12
]
, Flamingo
[
13
]
, and LLaVA
[
14
]
have achieved impressive performance on image captioning, VQA, and multi-modal reasoning tasks with minimal supervision. BLIP used filtered web captions for pretraining, while Flamingo demonstrated few-shot capabilities via frozen LLMs and interleaved visual-text prompts. LLaVA linked a vision encoder with an LLM (Vicuna) to enable image-grounded dialogue and in-context learning
[
15
]
. These models generalize across tasks using only a few examples, making them attractive for domains where large labeled datasets are impractical.

Recognizing these advantages, early research has begun to explore the application of powerful vision-language models within the driving domain. For example, DriveVLM formulated motion planning as a text generation problem which use a pre-trained VLM to “describe” a future trajectory or driving action in words, which can then guide to an autonomous agent
[
16
]
. Similarly, some recent works use LLM to reason about driver behavior and attention. Such as, LLAda incorporates a multimodal LLM to jointly predict where a driver would look and why, providing textual justifications for the gaze allocation
[
6
]
. This represents an important step toward human-like reasoning in attention modeling. However, these approaches still depend on extensive supervised training, which necessitates a large amount of data collection and annotation. This limitation emphasizes the need for innovative strategies to connect general-purpose visual-language models (VLMs) with the specific demands of predicting attention in driving scenarios.

2.4
Contributions

Building upon prior work in vision-language modeling and driver attention prediction, this paper addresses the challenge of few-shot attention reasoning in the driving domain using a multi-modal large language model. Our approach leverages in-context learning to predict both current and future driver gaze while providing natural language rationales, all with minimal supervision. The key contributions are as follows.

•

Few-shot attention prediction with LLaVA:
We introduce the first application of a large vision-language model (LLaVA) to driver attention modeling in a few-shot setting, using only a handful of annotated exemplars.

•

Joint modeling of current and future gaze:
Our model simultaneously predicts the driver’s current gaze and plausible future attention shifts, aligning with the cognitive demands of driving.

•

Natural language rationale generation:
The model outputs interpretable scene descriptions and attention rationales, providing semantic explanations that bridge visual perception and language.

•

Efficient domain adaptation via LoRA:
We apply parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) to adapt LLaVA to the driving domain without requiring full retraining, supporting scalability and domain generalization.

3
Experimental Setup

3.1
Dataset Preparation

In this study, a vision-language dataset was constructed from the Berkeley DeepDrive-Attention (BDD-A) dataset, which consists of driving videos captured around critical braking event
[
7
]
. Each video provides a sequence of frames with corresponding driver attention annotations. To ensure that the training and evaluation samples capture meaningful shifts in visual attention, a key frame selection strategy was employed based on Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between consecutive gaze heatmaps. Each gaze map is treated as a normalized probability distribution over spatial location. The divergence between consecutive frames is computed to quantify changes in driver attention over time.
Formally, given two consecutive histograms
H_{t}
and
H_{t+1}
, the KL divergence is calculated as:

KL(H_{t}\parallel H_{t+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{t}(i)\cdot\log\left(\frac{H_{t}(i)+\epsilon}{H_{t+1}(i)+\epsilon}\right)

(1)

where
N
is the number of histogram bins, and
\epsilon
is a small constant for numerical stability.
High KL scores indicate moments of abrupt attention shift. We sort all frame pairs by their KL divergence and retain the top-k frames from each video, isolating transitions where human gaze behavior changes rapidly. This approach builds on previous work that uses KL divergence to detect saliency transitions and information gain in attention modeling and video summarization
[
17
,
18
]
. Similar KL-based techniques have also been adopted in the autonomous driving domain to analyze shifting focus in gaze behavior under varying traffic conditions
[
19
,
20
]
. Through this method, a dataset was obtained comprising 80 training, 20 validations and 81 test samples, each consisting of four aligned inputs: two RGB frames and two corresponding gaze maps (for t and t+1). These pairs represent attention transitions, making them ideal for studying and modeling few-shot temporal gaze reasoning in driving scenarios.

3.2
Caption generation

To obtain initial descriptions of the driver’s attention in each frame pair, we employed the GPT-4o vision-language model via its API. The model was provided with the four aligned inputs (the RGB images and their overlaid gaze maps for time t and t+1 along with a structured prompt. The prompt instructed GPT-4o to write exactly four sentences in one paragraph: (1) a scene description, (2) the current gaze focus, (3) a future-tense prediction of where the gaze will shift next, and (4) the reason or justification for that gaze shift. This prompting strategy is akin to a chain-of-thought guidance for the model, ensuring it covers the where, what, and why of the driver’s attention. Inspiration was drawn from
recent work (W
3
DA dataset) that uses a multi-modal large language model to generate preliminary attention explanations which are then refined by humans
[
6
]
. Similarly, after GPT-4o generated the four-sentence description, human experts reviewed and edited these outputs for accuracy and clarity. The result was a high-quality annotated caption for each frame pair, describing the scene, the driver’s current gaze target, the anticipated next gaze target, and the rationale behind the attention shift (with a concise summary). These captions (GPT-4o assisted and human-refined) serve as ground-truth descriptions for our model training.

3.3
Fine-Tuning with LLaVA

We utilize a limited set of captioned driver attention samples to fine-tune our pretrained vision-language model, employing both one-shot and few-shot learning strategies. The overall system architecture is illustrated in Figure
1
integrates a visual encoder with a language model to enable grounded, attention-aware caption generation. The base model selected was LLaVA-NeXT v1.6, which incorporates a Vicuna-7B backbone and a CLIP ViT-L visual encoder to extend a language model with a vision interface. LLaVA was chosen due to its demonstrated multi-modal understanding and strong visual instruction-following capability, including state-of-the-art performance on vision-language benchmarks
[
14
]
.

Fine-tuning such a model on a small dataset was made feasible through parameter-efficient adaptation. In particular, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
21
]
was employed, which freezes the original model weights and injects small trainable rank-decomposition matrices into each Transformer layer. The LoRA update to a weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is expressed as:

\Delta W=AB,\quad A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\quad B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}

(2)

—are provided to GPT-4o offline to produce structured draft descriptions, which are then human-refined. For training, a single RGB frame and its refined caption are used to fine-tune the LLaVA-NeXT model. The RGB image is passed through a
frozen
CLIP image encoder, and visual embeddings are projected via an MLP connector and fused with a Vicuna-7B language model; both modules (dotted box) are fine-tuned using LoRA, while CLIP remains frozen. At inference time, the model generates a four-sentence description covering the scene, current gaze focus, predicted attention shift, and its rationale.

This allows the optimization of a much smaller parameter set while preserving expressive capacity. The modified weight during training becomes:

W^{\prime}=W+\frac{\alpha}{r}\cdot AB

(3)

where
\alpha
is a scaling factor that controls the update magnitude relative to the rank
r
. In our experiments, we set
\alpha=32
for
r=64
, which offered a balanced trade-off between expressivity and stability. The query (Q), key (K), value (V), and output (O) projections within each Transformer block were adapted via LoRA, as well as the two-layer multi-modal projector that fuses vision and language features.

All LoRA weights were initialized from a Gaussian distribution and trained with a dropout rate of 0.1 to mitigate overfitting. The training objective was standard next-token prediction using the cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},\mathbf{x})

(4)

where
\mathbf{x}
is the image embedding and
y_{t}
is the
t
-th token of the target caption. This loss encourages the model to generate coherent, context-aware text grounded in visual input.

Key training parameters are summarized in Table
1
. Both few-shot and one-shot regimes employed mixed-precision (FP16) training and 4-bit NF4 quantization to reduce memory footprint. Gradient checkpointing and clipping (max norm = 1.0) ensured stable updates on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. The Vicuna tokenizer was used with left-padding and a maximum input length of 2048 tokens.

Each training sample consisted of a single RGB image and its associated caption. Images were resized to
336\times 336
pixels and normalized. A custom collate function ensured instruction-consistent formatting, matching the style used for GPT-4o-generated prompts. Optimization was performed using AdamW with cosine learning rate (LR) annealing. Early stopping based on validation loss (no improvement for 5 epochs) was used to prevent overfitting. In few-shot experiments (80 samples), convergence typically occurred around epoch 5, while in one-shot settings (8 samples), we used a reduced LoRA rank (8) and alpha (4) to minimize overfitting risk.

3.4
Zero shot

For baseline comparisons, two state-of-the-art vision-language models were evaluated in a zero-shot setting (no fine-tuning on the driver attention dataset). First, the pretrained LLaVA (Vicuna-7B) model, which had been instruction-tuned on general vision-language tasks, was used without any fine-tuning on the dataset. This model was prompted with the driving attention instruction to assess its ability to describe driver attention out-of-the-box. Second, BLIP-2
[
22
]
, a robust vision-language model known for zero-shot image captioning and VQA, was evaluated. BLIP-2 leverages a frozen image encoder and frozen language model, bridged by a trainable transformer component, and achieves high performance on generic vision-language tasks without domain-specific supervision. In this experiment, the pretrained BLIP-2 model was used to generate attention descriptions for the test without any fine-tuning. These zero-shot baselines demonstrate how a generic multi-modal model performs on the task of driver gaze explanation without exposure to driving-specific data. The value of fine-tuning approaches is also quantified through these baselines.

3.5
Evaluation criteria

Generated attention descriptions were evaluated against human-edited reference captions using a combination of standard NLP metrics and semantic alignment metrics. Conventional metrics included ROUGE-L and METEOR, which quantify lexical overlap and n-gram similarity between the generated and reference captions. To assess content grounding accuracy, we calculated the Entity Alignment (EA) F1 score. This metric evaluates whether key entities mentioned in the generated descriptions—such as traffic signs, road users, or hazards—align with those in the ground truth reference, thereby emphasizing factual and content relevance. Furthermore, one other advanced evaluation metrics were used: ParaScore. ParaScore
[
23
]
(Shen et al., 2022) is designed for paraphrase generation tasks and evaluates semantic equivalence using both reference-based and reference-free mechanisms. Its inclusion allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s ability to generate diverse yet meaning-preserving descriptions. Our approach is based on the assumption that the generated caption is a paraphrased version of the human-generated caption, which accurately describes the scenario. Finally, a small-scale human evaluation was conducted to provide an additional dimension for assessing the overall quality of the model’s output. A panel of two experts with approximately 2.5-years of driving experience, and prior experience in vision-language research evaluated the model outputs. Each evaluator independently assessed the attention descriptions based on three criteria: overall quality, informativeness, and factual correctness. Ratings were provided on a 1–5 Likert scale, and the mean of these ratings was reported as the Human Score (HS) for each model. To ensure diversity, a random subset of 30 samples from the test set was used. This subjective evaluation adds a human-centered dimension to the assessment and complements the automated metrics by capturing interpretability and perceptual grounding.

The few-shot LLaVA (baseline) consistently outperformed all other configurations across every metric. It achieved a ROUGE-L score of 0.573 and a METEOR score of 0.581, reflecting strong lexical alignment with ground truth descriptions. The EA F1 score reached 0.51, indicating that it accurately captured key entities (like traffic lights, road users, and scene elements). Upon inspection, BLIP-2 outputs resembled generic image captions, often describing central visual elements (e.g., “a car on the street”) but omitting key driver-relevant entities such as intersections, signals, or contextual intent. This highlights that while general-purpose captioning models can describe a scene, they often produce overly generic captions, failing to capture subtle but crucial visual details like a driver’s gaze or focal attention, which are vital for interpreting complex, domain-specific tasks like driving.

Overall, these findings underscore the vital importance of task-specific adaptation. They reveal that even minimal fine-tuning—when guided by representative training samples—can substantially improve a model’s ability to produce high-quality, semantically grounded visual descriptions. By learning from examples that effectively capture human expectations and attention patterns, the model becomes better equipped to align its outputs with contextual cues, leading to more accurate and meaningful interpretations of visual scenes. This suggests that strategically selected data can play a pivotal role in maximizing performance, even with limited fine-tuning efforts.

4.3
Probing-based Analysis

To obtain comprehensive and detailed descriptions of driver attention in complex scenes, this study developed a probing analysis methodology. For each image, we generated a set of five targeted questions. These questions were designed to focus on observable elements of the scene, including the most prominent object in the center view, visible traffic elements, road users ahead, environmental features, and areas of anticipated focus. Responses were generated using GPT-4o, guided by a carefully crafted prompt that instructed the model to avoid speculation and rely strictly on visible cues. This methodology ensured that the probing answer captured factual observation in a complex driving scene.

4.4
Ablation Studies

To understand the contributions of key architectural and training decisions in our few-shot learning framework, we conducted systematic ablation experiments. The baseline model employs LLaVA-NeXT with a Vicuna-7B backbone, fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with rank 64 and
\alpha=32
. Captions were refined by humans post-GPT-4o generation, and training used a maximum token length of 2048.

The study ablated the following components individually:

•

Human Refinement:
Removing human-edited captions and using GPT-4o drafts directly.

•

Future Gaze Prediction:
Retaining only scene and current gaze information.

•

LoRA Rank:
Modifying the rank to 32 (lower) and 128 (higher).

•

Input Length:
Reducing the MAX_TOKEN length to 1024 and 512.

The full model achieved the highest overall (see Figure
3
) scores across all metrics, with ROUGE-L of 0.5734 and ParaScore of 0.7810. Removing human refinement led to a significant drop in performance (ROUGE-L: 0.3549, ParaScore: 0.6322), confirming the importance of post-edited supervision. Excluding future gaze prediction modestly reduced Entity Alignment F1 and ParaScore, highlighting the relevance of modeling attention shifts.

Adjusting the LoRA rank revealed that both lower (32) and higher (128) ranks slightly underperformed compared to the baseline rank of 64, suggesting a balanced tradeoff between parameter efficiency and capacity. Reducing the token length from 2048 to 1024 or 512 sharply degraded language generation quality (e.g., ROUGE-L
<
0.35), showing that longer prompts are beneficial for vision-language reasoning.

Overall, the ablation confirms that all components: human refinement, gaze shift modeling, and appropriate LoRA/token settings—jointly contribute to the performance of our few-shot attention modeling framework.

5
Conclusions

This work introduces VISTA, a novel few-shot framework designed to model driver attention in naturalistic driving scenes. By leveraging large multi-modal models such as GPT-4o and LLaVA-NeXT, the system generates semantically rich and structured scene descriptions using only a handful of annotated examples. Unlike traditional approaches that require pixel-level gaze supervision or large-scale training datasets, our method achieves high performance through in-context learning and parameter-efficient adaptation using LoRA. Empirical results demonstrate that VISTA delivers strong accuracy across multiple metrics—ROUGE-L, METEOR, Entity Alignment F1, and ParaScore—while maintaining robustness even under low-data conditions.

Ablation studies confirm the contribution of key components such as human-refined prompts, predicted attention shifts, and sufficient LoRA capacity to performance gains. Qualitative examples highlight the model’s ability to generate interpretable, human-aligned descriptions that integrate spatial awareness, task context, and future intent. These findings establish the potential of vision-language models for interpretable and cognitively inspired driving behavior analysis.

Future work may explore incorporating risk-aware gaze priors, active learning for caption refinement, and real-time deployment on embedded platforms. Overall, this study represents a step toward human-centered, language-based driver attention modeling systems that are both scalable and generalizable across diverse driving scenarios. This modeling capability will benefit the development of adaptive driver attention guidance systems and support advanced human-AI teaming in surface transportation.
