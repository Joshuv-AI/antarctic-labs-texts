A first measurement of baryonic feedback with Fast Radio Bursts † reischke@posteo.net c rreischke@astro.uni-bonn.de
Robert Reischke †, Steffen Hagstotz

Abstract
Baryonic feedback fundamentally alters the total matter distribution on small to intermediate cosmological scales, posing a significant challenge for contemporary cosmological analyses. Direct tracers of the baryon distribution are therefore key for unearthing cosmological information buried under astrophysical effects. Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) have emerged as a novel and direct probe of baryons, tracing the integrated ionised electron density along the line-of-sight, quantified by the dispersion measure (DM). The scatter of the DM as a function of redshift provides insight into the lumpiness of the electron distribution and, consequently, baryonic feedback processes. Using a model calibrated to the BAHAMAS hydrodynamical simulation suite, we forward-model the statistical properties of the DM with redshift. Applying this model to approximately 100 localised FRBs, we constrain the governing feedback parameter, log ⁡ T AGN \log T_{\mathrm{AGN}} . Our findings represent the first measurement of baryonic feedback using FRBs, demonstrating a strong rejection of no-feedback scenarios at greater than 99.7 % 99.7\,\% confidence ( 3 ​ σ 3\sigma ), depending on the FRB sample. We find that FRBs prefer fairly strong feedback, similar to other measurements of the baryon distribution, via the thermal and kinetic Sunyaev-Zel’dovich effect. The results are robust against sightline correlations and modelling assumptions. We emphasise the importance of accurate calibration of the host galaxy and Milky Way contributions to the DM. Furthermore, we discuss implications for future FRB surveys and necessary improvements to current models to ensure accurate fitting of upcoming data, particularly that from low-redshift FRBs.

The capability to investigate cosmological small-scale structures is fundamentally limited by uncertainties associated with baryonic feedback. Dynamic processes driven by active galactic nuclei (AGN), supernovae, and stellar winds redistribute gas both within and around haloes, thereby altering the overall matter distribution in the Universe and changing its statistical properties in the non-linear regime

FRBs are short transient events lasting only a few milliseconds, with a frequency range from approximately 100 MHz to several GHz.

The key observable of FRBs of cosmological interest is therefore the DM-
z

;
Macquart et al. 2020
;
Hagstotz et al. 2022
;
James et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2022
;

, e.g.)
.
In addition, the statistical properties of the DM are an excellent probe of the statistical properties of the LSS if enough FRBs are available

;

;
Rafiei-Ravandi et al. 2021
;

;

;
Reischke et al. 2021
;
Reischke et al. 2022
;
Reischke et al. 2023
;
Reischke et al. 2024
;

;
Hussaini et al. 2025
;
Medlock et al. 2025
;
Hsu et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

In this paper, we use localised FRBs, i.e, events where a host galaxy and therefore an independent redshift estimate has been found, to constrain the scatter of the Macquart-relation. Baryonic feedback smoothes out the distribution of baryons relative to the dark matter distribution. Therefore, strong feedback will lead to less scatter in the DM-
z

(

Sharma et al. 2025

to put constraints on

Planck Collaboration et al. 2020

4

2.1
Dispersion measure

The total DM,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{tot}}

4

\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z)=\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{host}}(z)+\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}}(z,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\;.

(1)

The dispersion is associated with electrons associated with the host halo, with the Milky Way

5

As well as its halo,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}\to\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}+\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{halo}}

It should be noted that all of these contributions are random variables with their own associated probability distribution function. Fitting the Macquart relation is concerned with finding the expectation value of

1

Let us first focus on the LSS contribution

\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z)=c\int_{0}^{z}\!n^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{e}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z^{\prime})\,f^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{IGM}}(z^{\prime})\,\frac{1+z^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\;,

(3)

In this model one typically finds keep

\displaystyle n^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{e}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z)=\chi^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{e}}\frac{\rho^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{b}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z)}{m^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{p}}}=\chi^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{e}}\frac{\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{b}}}{m_{\mathrm{p}}}\big[1+\delta^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{e}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},z)\big]\;,

(4)

with the proton mass

\displaystyle=Y^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{H}}+\frac{1}{2}Y^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{He}}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}{Y}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{He}}\,,

Covariance

We assume to have a set of

host-identified FRBs
\big\{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs},i},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},z^{\mathstrut}_{i}\big\}

i=1,...,N_{\mathrm{FRB}}

{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathstrut}_{ij}=\sum_{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P^{\mathstrut}_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta^{\mathstrut}_{ij}\right)C^{\mathcal{DD}}_{ij}(\ell,z)\;,

2.3
Likelihood

The likelihood of the total DM, i.e.
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tot}}
, is given by marginalising over the host contribution and the Milky Way contribution:

\begin{split}p\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{tot}}\big|z\right)=&\;\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{host}}\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{halo}}\\
&\times p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}})p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{halo}}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{halo}})p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{host}}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{host}})\\
&\times p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{tot}}-\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{host}}}{1+z}-\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{MW}}-\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{halo}}\bigg|z^{\mathstrut}_{i}\right)\;.\end{split}

(14)

The host DM,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{host}}
, is the DM in the rest-frame of the host and therefore gets redshifted by a factor
(1+z)
when included in
p_{\mathrm{LSS}}
.
The total likelihood is then given, assuming that all FRBs are independent (we will discuss this in more detail in Section
3.2
), by:

L=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{FRB}}}p\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{obs,i}}\big|z^{\mathstrut}_{i}\right)\;.

(15)

In the remaining part of this section, we will discuss the specific ingredients entering
Equation

14
.

2.3.1
LSS dispersion measure PDF

There is strong evidence that the DM one-point probability density function (PDF) can be modelled by a log-normal distribution reasonably well
(
McQuinn 2014
;
Sharma et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. However, so far, it was common practice to use a fitting formula provided in
Miralda-Escudé et al. 2000
.
Sharma et al. 2025
discussed why this distribution has its shortcomings and is not an actual good fit to hydrodynamical simulations
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Walker et al. 2024
)
and that the PDF is indeed better described by a log-normal distribution

p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}}\left(x;\mu^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}},\sigma^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}}\right)=\frac{1}{x\sigma^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(\log x-\mu^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{ln}}}\right]\;.

(16)

The average of the log-normal,
\mu_{\mathrm{ln}}
, for an FRB
i
, can be related to the cosmological mean,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{LSS,}i}\equiv\langle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{LSS}}(z_{i})\rangle
in
Equation

9
, and its variance,
\mathrm{C}_{ii}
calculated from the diagonal elements of
Equation

10
:

\mu_{\mathrm{ln},i}=\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut 2}_{\mathrm{LSS,}i}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut 2}_{\mathrm{LSS,}i}+{\mathrm{C}^{\mathstrut}_{ii}}}}\right)\;.

(17)

Likewise, the log-normal variance is

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{ln},i}=\log\left(1+\frac{{\mathrm{C}}_{ii}}{\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut 2}_{\mathrm{LSS,}i}}\right)\;,

(18)

so that:

p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}}\big|z^{\mathstrut}_{i}\right)=p^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{LSS}};\mu^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln,}i},\sigma^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{ln},i}\right)\;.

(19)

Note that the off-diagonal elements of
Equation

10
describing correlations between different sightlines cannot be included in the case of a log-normal PDF. We address this limitation explicitly in Section
3.2
.

The results for assuming a Gaussian distribution for
p_{\mathrm{LSS}}
are shown in Appendix
A
. The inability to fit the long tail leads to an overestimation of the Gaussian variance, and therefore an underestimation of the feedback strength.

3.2
Correlations

First, we check the impact of correlations between different sightlines due to the LSS. To this end, we assume, for this test, a Gaussian likelihood with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
such that:

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{LSS}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{MW}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{host}}\;,

(23)

where the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
are given by
Equation

10
. For the MW and the host, we assume a diagonal covariance with standard deviations of
30\mathrm{\,pc\;cm}^{-3}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}
as a free parameter rescaled by the host redshift. Although this assumes the wrong shape of the likelihood, it will give us an estimate how large the effect of neglecting the correlations in the data set is. Recall that we assume all sightlines to be independent in
Equation

15
, since it is not possible to incorporate the correlations self-consistently for a log-normal distribution without running forward simulations
(
Konar et al. 2024
)
. The result of this exercise is displayed in

Figure

1
, where we can see that we get slightly larger values of
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
if we ignore the covariance between different events. Note that we only show relative values here, as the exact value of the result will not be correct in the first place due to the Gaussian assumption of the likelihood.
While the effect is not statistically significant, we investigate this result further. In
Figure

2
the correlation coefficient,
r_{ij}
, of the covariance matrix with those pairs of FRBs where it is larger than 0.3 marked in red. We opt to exclude 4 FRBs so that the correlation in the data set becomes smaller. Here, we always choose to remove the one with lower redshift to potentially keep more cosmological information. The selection is based on redshift so that we do not preferentially select objects with a larger DM.

Using
r_{ij}>0.3
is an arbitrary threshold, and there will remain a degree of correlation in the data set. In fact, using
r_{ij}>0.2
already results in 16 correlated pairs. For the currently available sample size, the effect is still small, and we choose to continue the analysis with the correlation cut outlined above. Nonetheless, this again highlights the importance of correlations between various FRBs moving forward
(see
Reischke & Hagstotz 2023b
, for more details)
.

Constraints on feedback

We first present our fiducial constraints, i.e. using the selection discussed in Section
3.1
and
3.2
and only the FRBs shown in
Table

. The resulting posterior contours are shown in
Figure

\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}=7.3

(
Dark Energy Survey and Kilo-Degree Survey Collaboration et al. 2023
)
. Anything with lower values of
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
would correspond to an increase in the power spectrum relative to a cold dark matter-only simulation.

It can be seen that the fiducial results have almost zero probability mass for vanishing feedback, rejecting the possibility of the free electrons following the dark matter distribution. This agrees with the results of

, indicating that feedback drives baryons out of galaxies and halos. Inspecting the green contours further, one can see that the host contribution compensates for the smaller clustering of electrons relative to the dark matter. Due to a larger
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
, baryons get expelled more, and the electron distribution becomes smoother, decreasing its variance. In turn, the orange contour shows smaller host variance as a larger fraction can be accommodated by the LSS, which is clumpier due to the lack of feedback. Lastly, we indicate the prior boundaries by a light grey-shaded area.

.

, together with the events of the fiducial (red points) and the full data set (orange points). We do not show error bars for the data, since observational uncertainty is always much smaller than the scatter induced by the LSS and host model. Note that the figure shows the DM after

Upon further inspection, we see that the dashed line moves closer to the peak of the likelihood with increasing redshift, indicating that the distribution becomes more symmetric and more Gaussian and higher redshifts. This is in line with what has been found in simulations
(
Walker et al. 2024
;
Konietzka et al. 2025
, e.g.)
and is as well-supported by several theoretical arguments: Let us assume for a moment that the LSS is a Gaussian random field. Then, the only non-Gaussian contribution comes from the log-normality (or rather non-Gaussianity) of the host distribution
(
McQuinn 2014
;
Theis et al. 2024
;
Medlock et al. 2024
;
Reischke et al. 2024
)
. As discussed before, the host contribution dominates the likelihood at low redshifts, thus resulting in a large degree of non-Gaussianity and therefore a mismatch between the mean and the mode of the likelihood. However, the LSS contribution itself is also not Gaussian due to structure formation. The expansion parameter controlling the perturbative treatment of structure growth is the relative fluctuation strength,
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{LSS}}/\langle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{LSS}}\rangle
.

In
Figure

5
, we show the PDF of the DM as a function of
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
(which traces redshift,
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}=0.5
corresponds to a redshift of
0.2
for our best fitting model). It is clear, that the host contribution dominates at low redshifts, as discussed before. Furthermore, at higher redshifts, the PDF will tend to become more and more Gaussian as
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}\to 0
. In this limit, one can expand the log-normal distribution (or any distribution) around its peak in an asymptotic Edgeworth expansion and recover a Gaussian to leading order. The observed trend in
Figure

4
can therefore be well understood. It is noteworthy that
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
drops below unity already at fairly low redshifts.

3.4
Goodness of fit

Next, we address the question whether the models used are actually a good description of the data. This is being addressed in a Frequentist fashion. The standard way would be a
\chi^{2}
-test. Since the likelihood is, however, non-Gaussian, we cannot interpret it as a quadratic form in the same way as the
\chi^{2}
. Alternatively, one can draw
N
random realisations of the data from the likelihood at the maximum of the posterior,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, and calculate the best-fit log-likelihood,
\log L
, for each realisation. This serves as a proxy for realisations
\chi^{2}_{i}
and provides an estimate of its distribution upon many realisations of the data
(
Tanseri et al. 2022
)
. By comparing the log-likelihood of the actual data,
\chi_{\mathrm{data}}
to these realisations, we can estimate the one-sided
p
-value, or the probability-to-exceed as:

p=P\left(\chi^{2}\geq\chi^{\mathstrut}_{\mathrm{data}}\right)\approx\sum_{\chi_{i}\leq\chi_{\mathrm{data}}}\frac{1}{N}\;.

(24)

We show the resulting distribution and the corresponding
p
-values in
Figure

6
. Here, the green histogram corresponds to our standard analysis including
T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
, while the orange histogram assumes no feedback. The coloured vertical lines indicate the actual log-likelihood of the data, and the shaded region indicates the area which contains the probability-to-exceed, which is also quoted in the colour-coded boxes.
The left plot shows the fiducial data set and the right plot corresponds to the full data set (the green and grey contours in
Figure

3
respectively). For the fiducial case, both cases with or without feedback are good fits, since the data is a very probable realisation of the respective models. The purely Frequentist point of view would therefore be that it is unnecessary to include the more complicated model, including baryonic feedback. From a Bayesian perspective, however, the situation is very different since we do have prior information that feedback exists in the Universe and that our models have to allow some modification
(see also
Gelman et al. 1996
, for a discussion in a very general setting)
. This is of course reflected in the contours of
Figure

3
and we will map those to the power spectrum suppression in the next section. It is, however, important to keep in mind that the current FRB data does not tell us whether feedback exists or not if we do not know a priori that it exists in the first place. As for every Bayesian analysis (especially in the case of limited constraining power), the results are subject to prior choices.

For the full set, i.e. the right plot of
Figure

6
, the situation is a bit different. Adding in low redshift FRBs reduces the
p
-value for both cases drastically, and the model without feedback actually falls below the commonly used (but somewhat arbitrary) boundary of
p=0.05
. While this is not statistically significant in a strict sense, it gives a hint that the modelling of FRBs at low redshifts remains challenging. This will become even important as more, especially low-redshift data, becomes available. It can potentially hint to the fact that the model of the Milky Way both for the ISM and the halo contribution is too simplistic, or that the host model is not flexible enough. Hence, future analysis might need to revise the assumptions made to integrate FRB analysis in a cosmological setting
(see e.g.
Reischke et al. 2024
, for a baryonification model applied to the host contribution)
.

3.5
Matter power spectrum suppression

As the last part of our analysis, we will translate the constraints on
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
from
Figure

3
into the resulting suppression of the matter power spectrum, which we define as

\mathcal{S}(k,z)=\frac{P_{\mathrm{hydro}}(k,z)}{P_{\mathrm{dmo}}(k,z)}\;,\quad\mathcal{S}(k)\equiv\mathcal{S}(k,0)\;,

(25)

where
P_{\mathrm{hydro}}(k,z)
is the matter power spectrum from the hydrodynamical simulation, i.e. including baryons, and
P_{\mathrm{dmo}}(k,z)
is the matter power spectrum in the same simulation but only with gravity, i.e. dark matter only (dmo). First, let us reiterate that AGN heating temperature (
T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
) correlates with feedback-driven modifications to halo profile concentration parameters for gas-poor and gas-rich haloes, halo-bound gas fractions, and the equation of state of the gas
(
Mead et al. 2020
)
. Formally, it is related to the
BAHAMAS
subgrid heating parameter
\Delta T_{\mathrm{heat}}
, which governs the occurrence of AGN feedback such that it activates only after accumulating sufficient energy to heat a fixed number of gas particles by
\Delta T_{\mathrm{heat}}
. So while there is effectively no dmo setting,
Dark Energy Survey and Kilo-Degree Survey Collaboration et al. 2023
found that
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}=7.3
reproduces a dmo power spectrum, which we will use as the reference here.

The main result of this paper can be seen in
Figure

7
. We show
\mathcal{S}(k,0)
for the fiducial and the full data set on the left and the right respectively.
Here, the black dashed line marks the median of the one-dimensional marginal posterior of
T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
(i.e. the sub panel in
Figure

3
). Since we saw earlier that the constraints on
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
are slightly prior dominated at the upper end, we will quote lower bounds. In other words, we specify the minimum feedback required by FRBs at a given level of confidence.
The solid line shows the
2\sigma
or
3\sigma
lower bound on feedback. Put in another way, any
S(k,0)
above that line and closer to unity is ruled out by
2\sigma
or
3\sigma
respectively. Including the full data set, we see that the median and the upper limit on feedback stays more or less the same, however, the lower limit on the feedback strength changes, and it can be seen that the dmo scenario is excluded by more than
3\sigma
.

To put the constraints into numbers, we calculate the 68
\,\%
confidence interval of the marginal posterior distribution and find:

\begin{split}\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}=&\;7.87^{+0.23}_{-0.27}\quad\mathrm{fiducial}\\
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}=&\;7.90^{+0.16}_{-0.22}\quad\mathrm{full~set}\;,\end{split}

(26)

very consistent with the
BAHAMAS
model. For completeness, we also show the best fit value found in
Tröster et al. 2022
(dashed red curve) who assume the same model used here and fit the kSZ and cosmic shear cross-correlations. Their result is very consistent with ours, but they find weaker constraints in general and are partially prior dominated as the marginal posterior at the edge of the prior range dropped only to a tenth of the posterior value at the marginal mode.

Additionally, we show in dashed orange recent results by
Kovač et al. 2025
using
X
-Ray and kSZ data, but with a more flexible model introduced in
Schneider et al. 2025
. Their results also exhibit fairly strong feedback, consistent with our findings and with the
f_{\mathrm{gas}}
–8
\sigma

FLAMINGO
simulation
(
Schaye et al. 2023
;
Schaller et al. 2025
)
.

3.6
Possible limitations and discussion

There are a couple of caveats to the analysis presented here, which we will discuss here.

First, there is the issue of completeness of the survey, or in other words, the luminosity function of FRBs
(
James et al. 2022
;
Hoffmann et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. This can potentially introduce selection effects in the FRB sample, especially for large DM values. Since this selection will preferentially select FRBs with low DMs, as they are more likely to be detected provided an overall sensitivity of a given telescope, the corresponding sample of FRBs will prefer FRBs which are scattered low in the DM-
z
relation, thus biasing the inferred scatter low.
Secondly, as discussed earlier, the fact that low-redshift FRBs will dominate the sample requires for a very accurate modelling of both the Milky Way and the host contribution. The presented results indicate, however, that the first point is not a dominating systematic at the moment, as splitting up the sample does not yield any systematic shift in the best-fitting values but rather changes the uncertainty of the measurements. We show an attempt of quantifying the overall scatter in
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
in
Figure

8
. To obtain this plot, we use a random sample of the full set of FRBs with the size of the fiducial set. Next, the posterior is maximised, and we report the
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
value, upon repeating this exercise many times, we arrive at
Figure

8
where
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
was shifted such that zero falls onto the best fit fiducial case,
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}=7.87
. It is clear that the fiducial data set is not a very unlikely draw and that selection effects would have biased our result. This is of course only true for the FRBs that are available, and it simply shows that the fiducial case was not a particularly special case with respect to the full data set.

At the same time, the
p
-value issue discussed in Section
3.4
suggests that the modelling of the host and the Milky Way will require some refinement in the future.

Another limitation is the model for the feedback itself. While the power spectrum suppression is representative of the
BAHAMAS
via variations of both cosmological parameters and
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
, this one-parameter family has a limited functional form. Put differently, while other emulators, e.g.
Giri & Schneider 2021
, can reproduce the
BAHAMAS
suppression, the model here does not cover all kinds of suppressions as measured in hydrodynamical simulations
(see e.g.
Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2021
, for a list of different feedback implementations)
. Nonetheless, since
\log T_{\mathrm{AGN}}
is sufficient to describe current weak lensing data
(
Wright et al. 2025
)
and the constraints presented here show that FRBs indeed require relatively strong feedback.

Very recently,
Leung et al. 2025
have investigated the relationship between the host DM and the host’s stellar mass,
M_{\star}
, for a sample of 20 FRBs. They find a negative trend of
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{host}}
with
M_{\star}
, as observed in the low-mass halos (with stellar masses well below
10^{11}\,M_{\odot}
) which is in contrast to the relationship observed in the CAMELS simulations
(
Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2021
;
Theis et al. 2024
)
. By translating this relationship to the power spectrum suppression, this suggests effectively
\mathcal{S}(k,0)=1
in contrast our analysis. However, we want to emphasise that both papers rely on different observables. We model the effect on feedback on the distribution of electrons within the LSS and therefore on the scatter of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
, while
Leung et al. 2025
focus on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{host}}
.
8
8

8

We would expect that some part of the preference of vanishing feedback in their paper arises from the assumption of a Gaussian likelihood for
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{host}}
. Even with the conservative selection employed there, one would expect
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{host}}
to follow a long-tailed distribution. Modelling such a case with a Gaussian typically requires very large variances to accommodate the tail, resulting in a possible underestimation of the overall feedback strength. How this would happen in the analysis presented here is shown in Appendix
A
.

4
Conclusions

In this work, we constrain baryonic feedback for the first time using the scatter in the dispersion measure - redshift relation of FRBs. We forward-model the power spectrum of the hot (ionised) gas using a halo model approach which has been calibrated to the
BAHAMAS
simulations, providing a one-parameter model for the strength of baryonic feedback which enables us to sweep a range of possible feedback strengths consistent with the
BAHAMAS
suite.

By assuming a prior on the cosmological parameters given by Planck
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
, we can marginalise over the host contribution of FRBs and find constraints on baryonic feedback and map it to the suppression of the matter power spectrum. Using a set of
69
FRBs (fiducial data set) and an expanded (full) set with
28
additional FRBs, with most of them at
z<0.1
, we constrain feedback and obtain the following main conclusions:

1.

A scenario with
no baryonic feedback, i.e. where the power spectrum suppression is unity, is ruled out at
99.7\,\%
confidence for the fiducial data set. In case of the extended data set, we can exclude a dark-matter-only scenario with
5\sigma
.

2.

The median of the suppression measured by our model is in line with strong-feedback predictions from the
FLAMINGO
simulations.
However, with the current data, the results are still consistent with the fiducial run of
FLAMINGO
. Nonetheless, the results point to stronger feedback and are thus aligning excellently with other works
(
Bigwood et al. 2024
;
McCarthy et al. 2024
;
Kovač et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

3.

We test the importance of the Milky Way contribution by marginalising over it and find that the influence on the constraints of feedback are negligible.

4.

With an increasing number of FRBs, correlations between different sightlines will become more and more important
(
Reischke & Hagstotz 2023b
)
. This issue is addressed by calculating the Gaussian covariance between the burst and removing the highly correlated ones with a correlation coefficient
r\geq 0.3
. In total, 4 such pairs are identified and removed. The current amount of correlation does not warrant a further investigation, however with the next jump in sensitivity this effect needs to be taken into account
(see
Reischke & Hagstotz 2023b
;
Konar et al. 2024
, for details)
to deliver unbiased results.

We thus conclude that the constraints presented here are robust given the current modelling assumptions, and that FRBs indeed prefer strong feedback in the same way as other probes of the baryon distribution. The measurement therefore provides the first step in using FRBs as a potent cosmological probe in the era of precision cosmology with the LSS.
