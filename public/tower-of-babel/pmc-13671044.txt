Effects of cellulose nanocrystals on the physicochemical and retrogradation properties of pea starch
Authors: Shaoning Cui, Shaoqian Cui, Wei Xie, Hui Li, Fang Li, Shengju Ge, Zhaoquan Fu, Han Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pea starch (PS), with its high amylose content, is prone to retrogradation, which limits the shelf life of pea-based foods. However, direct evidence linking the dispersion of cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) to water-state changes in high-amylose legume starch remains limited. The gelation and storage-induced reorganization of PS with 0–8% CNCs were investigated by multi-scale analyses. Rod-like CNCs competitively bound water and bridged starch fragments, restricting granule swelling and amylose leaching; SEM revealed a concrete-like CNCs framework that thickened the pore walls, while FTIR indicated only non-covalent hydrogen bonding. As CNCs content increased from 0% to 8%, relative crystallinity decreased from 34% to 16% and the degree of retrogradation from 14.07% to 7.23%, accompanied by a reduced free-water fraction (A₂₃, 27.79%) and a shortened T₂₂. These findings suggest that the nano-reinforcement and interfacial hydrogen-bond modulation by CNCs mitigate PS gel aging, providing a scientific basis for designing stable pea-based products.

Introduction

With growing interest in healthy and alternative foods, pea-based foods, such as vermicelli, fiber meat, and “Liang-fen”, are gaining wide global acceptance (Wu et al., 2023). As the dominant component of these products, pea starch (PS) plays a key role in determining their physicochemical and textural properties (Zheng et al., 2025). However, PS typically contains a high amylose content (17.2–43.5%), which renders it prone to retrogradation (H. Wang et al., 2027; Y. Wang et al., 2025). Such recrystallization deteriorates the texture and shortens the shelf life of pea-based products, which severely restricts their industrial application (Chen et al., 2026).

Various methods have therefore been applied to suppress starch retrogradation, including enzymatic treatments (Madhumita et al., 2024), chemical modifications (Kaushik et al., 2024), and physical modifications (Mahajan & Sharma, 2024). Among them, physical modification of native starch is considered a safer alternative, as it avoids the toxicity concerns of chemical modification and the limited controllability of enzymatic treatment (S. Zhang et al., 2025). In recent years, nanoparticles have become a focus of physical modification research due to their small psize and good biocompatibility (Dun et al., 2020). It is generally recognized that nano-sized particulate fillers impede the reassociation of starch macromolecular chains through steric hindrance and competitive water binding; for instance, hydrophilic nano-SiO₂ forms hydrogen bonds with cassava starch, restricts the mobility of molecular chains, and thereby mitigates starch retrogradation (Liu et al., 2024).

Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) are rigid rod-like (whisker-like) nano-derivatives with high crystallinity and hydrophilicity. In this regard they differ fundamentally from flexible amorphous polysaccharides, such as chitosan and alginate, and from entangled cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) (Pezeshkian et al., 2026; Rezzouq et al., 2026). Rather than acting as conventional polymeric backbones, CNCs serve as excellent nano-reinforcing agents: their short, inflexible geometry limits self-entanglement, yet their abundant surface hydroxyls forge physical linkages with the neighboring starch polymer networks. This enables CNCs to act as a concrete-like supportive framework inside gelatinized starch gels, imposing direct steric hindrance against the realignment of amylose/amylopectin chains while competitively immobilizing water—a dual attribute that makes them particularly well suited to suppressing starch recrystallization and retrogradation. Nevertheless, whether and how this structural advantage is transferred to a high-amylose legume starch system remains unclear.

Our previous work confirmed that CNCs can retard the retrogradation of maize and sweet potato starch (S. Cui et al., 2018). However, PS differs fundamentally from cereal (normal/waxy maize) and tuber (sweet potato) systems in two respects: it contains a much higher amylose level, and it possesses an inherent legume granule architecture. These differences lead to distinctly different gelatinization and recrystallization pathways, which are governed by different amylose/amylopectin ratios and granule porosities. Moreover, the earlier bulk-macro measurements did not directly quantify water-state changes, did not provide microscopic visualization of CNCs dispersion, and failed to identify the concentration threshold at which CNCs aggregation occurs.

On the basis of these considerations, we propose the following testable hypothesis: CNCs retard PS retrogradation predominantly through (i) competitive water binding, which restricts the water available for amylose leaching, and (ii) physical obstruction of amylose chain realignment through surface interactions with starch granules, rather than through a simple dilution effect or a generic polysaccharide effect. To test this hypothesis, we deployed a multi-scale analytical suite covering pasting behavior (rapid viscosity analyzer, RVA), interfacial bonding (Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, FTIR), water-state mobility (low-field nuclear magnetic resonance, LF-NMR), nanoscopic dispersion (scanning electron microscopy, SEM), texture (texture profile analysis, TPA), thermal recrystallization (differential scanning calorimetry, DSC), and crystallinity (X-ray diffraction, XRD) with the aim of providing quantitative evidence for the pea-specific inhibitory mechanism. In particular, rather than merely replacing one starch substrate with another, this study attempts to establish a structure–activity relationship in which the regulatory efficacy of CNCs is governed by their nanoscale dispersion state, and to link this relationship directly to water-mobility quantification. These insights are expected to provide guidance for the design of stable pea-based foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pea starch (purity ≥98%, molecular weight Mw: 3.081 × 107, amylose content: 43.2%) was provided by Xinxiang Liangrun Whole Grain Food Co., Ltd.; commercial microcrystalline cellulose (MCC, purity ≥95%) derived from wheat straw was obtained from Shanghai Nuoshen Food Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and analytical-grade reagents were utilized throughout the experiments. All pea starch and CNCs used in this study were taken from a single production batch to ensure uniformity and to minimize batch-to-batch variation.

Preparation of cellulose nanocrystals

CNCs were synthesized following a previously established protocol from our group (S. Cui et al., 2016), with minor adjustments. Briefly, 100 g of MCC was hydrolyzed with 1000 mL of 65 wt% H₂SO₄ under sonication at 50 °C for 120 min. The reaction was terminated by adding an excess of distilled water. The ivory slurry was repeatedly centrifuged at 10,000 r/min until the supernatant became clear. The precipitate was then transferred into a dialysis bag (8000 D) and dialyzed against deionized water until a constant pH of 7.0 was reached. Finally, the suspension was collected and freeze-dried to obtain CNCs powder for subsequent analyses.

Characterization of cellulose nanocrystals

The shape and structural characteristics of CNCs were investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following our previous method (S. Cui et al., 2016). Imaging was carried out on a JEM-2100F instrument (Jeol, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Prior to analysis, a 0.01% (w/v) aqueous dispersion of CNCs was sonicated for 60 s. The zeta potential, size distribution, and polydispersity index (PDI) were assessed at 25 °C using folded capillary cells in a Malvern Zetasizer Nano device (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). XRD profiles of both MCC and CNCs were collected on an X'TRA-055 diffractometer (ARL, Switzerland) under Cu Kα irradiation (λ = 0.154 nm) at 40 kV and 40 mA. Infrared absorbance spectra of MCC and CNCs were acquired with a NEXUS-870 spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet, Madison, WI). Samples were thoroughly mixed with KBr powder and compressed into translucent disks. All spectra were recorded over the range 4000 to 400 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution in absorption mode (64 accumulations, ambient conditions). Each specimen was measured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Starch swelling properties

A starch suspension was prepared by dispersing 0.03 g of starch in 3.0 mL of distilled water using a vortex mixer. The suspension was then incubated in a magnetic water bath at 25 °C, 45 °C, or 70 °C for 20 min. Next, the starch slurry was stained with 100 μL of 0.1 N iodine solution at 25 °C. Subsequently, 15 μL of the slurry was placed on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. The sample was observed at 200× magnification using a BK-POLR polarization microscope (Chongqing Aote Optical Instrument Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China), and images were captured under both normal and polarized light.

RVA measurements

PS–CNCs mixtures were prepared as follows: PS (1.5 g, dry basis) was weighed and the calculated amounts of CNCs (0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% on a dry starch basis, w/w) were first dispersed in 25 mL of distilled water in the RVA canister by sonication to form a uniform suspension. PS was then added to the suspensions and stirred at ambient temperature with a plastic spatula for 5 min to avoid aggregation of the starch granules. The pasting properties of the starch suspensions were measured with an RVA (Perten Instruments, Warriewood, Australia) according to a published method (Jiang et al., 2024). The RVA profiles (pasting curves) were recorded to evaluate the pasting behavior under controlled heating and cooling.

Retrogradation (syneresis %) of PS–CNCs gels

The starch gel aging test was carried out with reference to Osei Tutu et al. (2023). Dispersions containing 6% PS–CNCs were heated in a water bath at 95 °C for 15 min, cooled to 50 °C, and held at this temperature for a further 15 min. Aliquots (5 mL) were placed in tubes and incubated at 4 °C for 5 and 7 days. The water expelled during storage at 4 °C was quantified to reflect paste retrogradation. LF-NMR analysis

The mobility and distribution of water molecules within different regions of the gel structure were assessed with a low-field 1H NMR analyzer (NMI20-040 V-I, Suzhou Niu Mai Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd.), following a previously reported method (Jiang et al., 2024). The starch paste (10 mL) gelatinized by RVA was placed in transparent glass bottles (22 mm diameter × 51 mm height) and stored at 4 °C for 5 and 7 days for retrogradation. The glass bottles were then positioned inside a cylindrical glass tube with an internal diameter of 40 mm. The Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence was employed to determine the transverse relaxation time (T₂), and the relaxation times corresponding to T₂₁, T₂₂, and T₂₃ were calculated.

SEM measurements

The microscopic morphology of the freeze-dried gel specimens prepared as described in Section 2.6 was characterized with a Nova NanoSEM450 field-emission SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). Specimens were sectioned into thin flakes, mounted on stubs with conductive glue, and sputter-coated with Au. Their internal architectures were then imaged at 500× magnification under a voltage of 5 kV (W. Yu et al., 2025).

FTIR measurements

FTIR analysis was conducted on samples that had retrograded for 5 and 7 days. Prior to analysis, the freeze-dried starch powder was thoroughly mixed with dried potassium bromide (KBr) at a ratio of approximately 1:100 (starch: KBr) and pressed into a transparent pellet. The measurements were performed with a NEXUS-870 spectrophotometer (Thermo Nicolet Corporation, Madison, WI). Spectra were recorded over 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with 64 scans accumulated at room temperature.

TPA measurements

The compressive resistance of PS–CNCs gels was assessed with a TA.XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Inc., UK) according to an earlier protocol with slight modifications (Huang et al., 2026). Paste produced by the RVA procedure (Section 2.5) was transferred into cylindrical silicone molds (10 × 10 mm); after 30 min of ambient cooling, it was stored at 4 °C for 7 days to obtain the test gels. Gel firmness was then probed with a P/36R cylindrical tip at 3.0 mm/s pre-speed, 1.0 mm/s test-speed, and 3.0 mm/s post-speed, under 30% strain and a trigger force of 5 g. Triplicate determinations were performed for each blend.

DSC measurements

The gelatinization behavior of PS with varying CNCs contents was analyzed with DSC (Mettler-Toledo, Schwerzenbach, Switzerland) following our previous method (S. Cui et al., 2018). Specifically, 1 g of PS and the corresponding amounts of CNCs (0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8%, on a dry starch basis, w/w) were weighed and mixed uniformly. Then, 3 mg of the mixed powder was precisely weighed into an aluminum pan and thoroughly mixed with 6 μL of distilled water. The pan was then hermetically sealed and allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 12 h to ensure complete hydration of the starch. Subsequently, the samples were heated from 20 to 100 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min. The gelatinization enthalpy change (ΔHg) was determined by integrating the areas under the DSC curve and was expressed in J/g of dry starch. After 7 days of storage at 4 °C, DSC curves were rescanned using the same heating procedure to record the retrogradation enthalpy (ΔHr). The retrogradation rate (R) was calculated as the ratio of ∆Hr to ∆Hg (Zhu & Liu, 2020).

XRD measurements

The X-ray diffractograms of samples retrograded for 7 days were obtained with a Bruker X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS Inc., Germany), as described previously. Prior to analysis, the samples were adjusted to a moisture content of 20% and stored in a chamber containing saturated NaCl solution, which ensured a steady 75% relative humidity at 26 ± 0.4 °C for 24 h. This moisture equilibration step is essential because the X-ray diffraction intensities and the derived relative crystallinity of starch are strongly influenced by the hydration state of the sample. Standardizing the moisture content eliminates artifacts arising from differential surface evaporation and ensures that the observed XRD differences reflect CNCs-regulated retrogradation rather than uncontrolled hydration gradients. The generator was operated at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu–Kα radiation. Diffractograms were obtained over a 2θ range of 4° to 40° with a step increment of 0.01°. Relative crystallinity (RC) was determined using Jade 5.0 software (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA, USA).(2)Relative crystallinityRC=AcAc+Aa×100

Statistical analysis

The formulation without CNCs (0%) was systematically included as the native PS (blank) control in all characterization techniques, enabling direct evaluation of the CNCs-specific effects. All experiments were conducted in technical replicates (n = 3), and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Graphs were generated using Origin Pro 8.5 and Excel. Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS statistical software (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Characterization of cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs)

Fig. 1A depicts the microscopic morphology of the prepared CNCs. The CNCs showed a rod-like shape, with lengths ranging from 100 to 500 nm and diameters ranging from 10 to 50 nm, corresponding to an aspect ratio of roughly 5–25. The polydispersity index (PDI) was 0.411, indicating a moderate size distribution in the aqueous system; colloidal stability was nevertheless maintained by the negative surface charge. Specifically, CNCs displayed a negative surface charge with a ζ-potential of −36.9 mV, which mainly stems from sulfate ester groups introduced onto accessible hydroxyl sites of the cellulose backbone during sulfuric acid hydrolysis. Fig. 1B presents the corresponding plength distribution. Fig. 1C presents the X-ray diffraction patterns and the corresponding crystallinity indices of MCC and CNCs. In this plot, CNCs showed characteristic cellulose I polymorphism, evidenced by principal reflections near 2θ = 17° and 22°, consistent with earlier reports (S. Cui et al., 2018). Quantitatively, the CNCs sample attained a crystallinity index of 88.71%, higher than the 62.26% recorded for MCC. Such outcomes imply that hydrolytic processing elevates crystallinity by selectively degrading amorphous domains within the microcrystalline substrate. The FTIR spectra of MCC and CNCs were highly similar, indicating that the acid-hydrolysis procedure did not alter the molecular structure of cellulose. The absence of impurity peaks in these FTIR spectra, together with the acid-hydrolysis purification, indicates a CNC purity of >95%. Collectively, these intrinsic parameters (a defined aspect ratio, a PDI of 0.411, a high negative zeta potential, and 88.71% crystallinity) are critical to the interfacial dispersion behavior of CNCs with PS and underlie the retardation efficacy described below.Fig. 1Structural and morphological features of the prepared CNCs (A: TEM image; B: pdiameter; C: XRD spectra; D: FTIR spectra).Fig. 1

Starch swelling properties

The swelling of starch granules is initiated when they are heated under moist conditions. As water penetrates the granules, the intermolecular hydrogen bonds between starch molecules rupture. Water molecules then form new hydrogen bonds with exposed hydroxyl groups, leading to molecular separation and irreversible granule swelling. During this process, granules can expand to several times their initial volume, which can be observed by optical microscopy. At 25 °C, the starch granules maintained their intact granular morphology (Fig. 2a) and obvious birefringence was observed, with most granules showing a Maltese-cross pattern aligned along their long axis (Fig. 2b). At 45 °C, the Maltese-cross pattern of some granules became unclear, indicating that the birefringence gradually disappeared, because water absorption and swelling disrupted the ordered granule structure and caused some linear amylose chains to leach from the expanded granules into the surrounding solution. At 70 °C, the granules absorbed more water and swelled further, with a substantial increase in volume, and their birefringence almost completely disappeared, because the high temperature accelerated the migration of water into the granules and promoted the breakage of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. The structural changes that starch granules undergo during swelling are key determinants of their functional properties, such as gel strength, digestibility, and the overall quality of starch-based foods.Fig. 2Swelling behavior of pea starch at different temperatures (200×) (a: ordinary light; b: polarized light).Fig. 2

The addition of CNCs significantly (p < 0.05) altered the gelatinization properties of the PS–CNCs mixtures. Notably, at a CNCs concentration of 4%, the peak viscosity of the blends decreased from 505 to 487 Pa·s. This reduction may be attributed to CNCs surrounding and limiting the swelling of starch granules during heating, which could hinder amylose leaching and weaken the intermolecular cohesion among starch granules, thereby decreasing the peak viscosity (B. Wang et al., 2024). The reduction may also reflect competition for water between starch granules and the hydrophilic CNCs during hydration. The breakdown viscosity of the PS–CNCs mixtures increased significantly at 4% CNCs (p < 0.05). The continual thermal and mechanical treatment in the presence of CNCs may therefore have promoted the complete collapse of the starch granules.

During cooling from 95 °C to 50 °C, the movement of starch molecules slows and the molecules come closer together, causing the viscosity to increase again. An obvious reduction in the final viscosity of the starch pastes was observed in the presence of CNCs, which might be attributed to altered aggregation behavior of the starch molecules (Gao et al., 2022). Over time, starch molecules reform hydrogen bonds, leading to gel stiffening and the expulsion of water from the network—a process reflected by the setback viscosity. The setback viscosity of pure PS was 187 Pa·s, and decreased significantly with increasing CNCs concentration, suggesting that CNCs slowed down the retrogradation of the gelatinized starch during cooling. Overall, the incorporation of CNCs appears to interfere with the initial gel formation and to alter the starch granule structure, thereby affecting both gelatinization and retrogradation behavior. Notably, our experimental observations indicate that above a critical concentration (i.e., a CNCs loading of 8%), the redispersibility of CNCs deteriorates markedly and the nanoparticles become prone to agglomeration. Such particulate aggregation plausibly restricts the effective interfacial contact between CNCs and starch macromolecules, thereby attenuating the intended regulatory effect on starch retrogradation.

Retrogradation (syneresis %) of PS–CNCs gels

During storage of the starch paste at 4 °C, the reduced temperature slows molecular motion and allows the starch molecules to approach each other more closely. This proximity could facilitate the reformation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, thereby displacing the water molecules that previously coated the starch chains. Consequently, water is expelled from the gel—a process known as syneresis (Osei Tutu et al., 2023). Between day 5 and day 7 of refrigeration, the syneresis % of the PS–CNCs pastes rose steadily, ranging from about 25.55 ± 0.16% (8% CNCs) to 29.36 ± 0.10% (0% CNCs) on day 5, and from 27.49 ± 0.01% (8% CNCs) to 30.63 ± 0.19% (0% CNCs) on day 7, indicating progressive phase separation within the PS gel matrix. Fig. 3 clearly shows that the volume of water expelled from the PS gel matrix increased as the retrogradation time was extended.Fig. 3Optical images of starch gel retrogradation (A: 5 days; B: 7 days).Fig. 3

Statistical analysis revealed that on day 5, the syneresis % decreased significantly (p < 0.05) with increasing CNCs content, following the order: 29.36 ± 0.10 (0%) > 25.55 ± 0.16 (8%). On day 7, the corresponding values were 30.63 ± 0.19 (0%) and 27.49 ± 0.01 (8%); here, only additions of ≥4% CNCs yielded significantly lower syneresis than the control, while 2% CNCs showed no significant difference from 0% CNCs. It was consistently observed that, as the CNCs content increased, the amount of water separated from the starch gel decreased; for instance, the 8% CNCs sample showed syneresis values 3.81 and 3.14 percentage points lower than the 0% control on day 5 and 7, respectively. These results indicate that CNCs effectively slowed the retrogradation of PS, most likely by forming a hydrogen-bonded or entangled network that restricted starch-chain aggregation and enhanced the water-holding capacity of the gel matrix (Osei Tutu et al., 2023). To gain a clearer understanding of the water composition within the retrograded starch system at the molecular level, the mobility of water molecules was determined by LF-NMR.

Changes in water molecules in retrograded starch

Fig. 4 shows the LF-NMR spectra of starch gels with different CNCs contents after 5 and 7 days of storage. The T₂ spectrum of the PS gel showed three distinct peaks, assigned to strongly bound, weakly bound, and free water, respectively (W. Yu et al., 2025). Extending the storage from 5 to 7 days increased the free-water fraction of all samples: in the control (0% CNCs), A₂₃ rose from 29.59 ± 0.09% to 33.09 ± 0.35%, whereas A₂₁ and A₂₂ decreased from 0.99 ± 0.06% to 0.91 ± 0.05% and from 69.42 ± 0.15% to 65.99 ± 0.39%, respectively. This redistribution reflects the gradual detachment of immobilized water from the PS macromolecules and its transformation into mobile water as the amylopectin external chains reorganized into crystalline structures through hydrogen bonding during retrogradation, thereby exuding free water from the amorphous regions (M. Yu et al., 2020). With CNCs addition, however, the increase in A₂₃ during storage was markedly smaller (2.49 percentage points at 8% CNCs versus 3.50 percentage points in the control), and the day-7 A₂₃ decreased dose-dependently from 33.09 ± 0.35% (0% CNCs) to 27.79 ± 0.29% (8% CNCs), a reduction of 5.30 percentage points (p < 0.05). This trend mirrors the syneresis results (Section 3.4), indicating that the reduced free-water fraction detected by LF-NMR corresponds to attenuated macroscopic water loss in the PS gels.

The reduced water mobility can be attributed to the hydrophilicity of CNCs and to their role as a supportive framework within the gel network. Their abundant surface hydroxyls form hydrogen-bonded PS–CNCs networks that retain water while restricting its migration; consistently, the T₂₂ of the high-CNCs groups was shortened (e.g., from 43.00 ± 1.49 to 40.11 ± 1.39 ms at 8% CNCs between day 5 and day 7). This restricted water mobility is consistent with the reduced DSC retrogradation enthalpy (Section 3.9), indicating that the short-term pasting inhibition and the long-term water-state changes are two facets of the same CNCs-induced interfacial obstruction rather than independent mechanisms. Similar water-state changes have been reported for chitosan oligosaccharides in refrigerated bread (Du et al., 2026). To further verify the resulting network architecture, the microstructure of the retrograded gels was examined by SEM.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Upon cooling after gelatinization, starch macromolecules reassociate and yield a compact three-dimensional polymeric matrix. To elucidate the effect of CNCs on the structural evolution of PS gels, SEM was employed. Fig. 5 illustrates the morphology of both unmodified starch gels and CNCs-reinforced composites after storage at 4 °C for 5 and 7 days. Following lyophilization, the PS and PS–CNCs gel specimens exhibited a rough, spongy structure, with surfaces showing numerous small, irregularly shaped fragments and inhomogeneous thin-walled pores.Fig. 5Microstructure alterations of PS–CNCs gels after storage at 4°C for 5 and 7 days.Fig. 5

Microstructure alterations of PS–CNCs gels after storage at 4°C for 5 and 7 days.

Compared with the 5-day batches, the 7-day stored PS and PS–CNCs gels presented a more compact networked morphology and reduced pore dimensions, attributed to amylose and amylopectin reorganization during prolonged storage. This finding agrees with an earlier study showing that the retrogradation of corn starch involved a morphological transition from an initially fragmented structure into a relatively dense, sheet-like morphology (Z. Yu et al., 2026). Notably, at higher magnification the CNCs were clearly discernible as rigid rod-like entities uniformly distributed within the PS gel matrix, especially in the high-loading groups (e.g., 6% and 8% CNCs). These nanofillers bridged adjacent starch fragments and pore walls, visually corroborating the mechanism proposed from the LF-NMR data that CNCs act as a supportive framework within the starch gel skeleton to restrict water mobility.

In contrast to the native PS gel, the CNCs-reinforced composites exhibited thickened fragment walls, a relatively smoother surface, and a more homogeneous pore distribution, forming a better-organized cellular network. The pores reflect the internal water distribution, whereas the thickened walls suggest robust entanglement between starch macromolecules and CNCs. It can be inferred that this robust starch–CNCs framework restricts free water from escaping the polymeric substrate, thereby elevating the capacity of the PS gel matrix to retain water. The polar hydroxyls abundant on the CNCs surfaces can competitively bind water with the starch chains, thus boosting the inherent moisture-locking ability of the gel matrix. Concurrently, these same functional sites may engage with amylose and amylopectin to intensify interchain H-bonding, which further fortifies the three-dimensional starch network (W. Yu et al., 2025). Similarly, the 7-day stored composites containing 8% CNCs exhibited a markedly coalesced matrix with narrower, more homogeneous pores than the unfortified PS gels, indicating that CNCs hindered network swelling and pclumping throughout PS gel retrogradation and ultimately refined the microscopic architecture. Such microscopic evidence supports the previous LF-NMR inference that CNCs act as concrete-like reinforcement and is fully consistent with the reduced syneresis % and the lowered A₂₃ free-water fraction, collectively indicating that CNCs retard the retrogradation of PS.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis provided insights into the starch functional-group modifications and the CNCs-induced alterations during PS retrogradation. Critically, no new absorption peaks emerged in any CNCs-enriched spectrum relative to the native starch baseline, and no significant peak shifts were detected after 5 or 7 days of storage (Fig. 6), suggesting the formation of non-covalent PS–CNCs complexes, primarily through hydrogen bonds. The absorption band around 1640 cm−1 is attributed to the O—H bending vibration of strongly bound water (Akonor et al., 2023). The absorption band observed between 2800 and 3000 cm−1 corresponds to symmetric and asymmetric C—H bond stretching vibrations. In the higher wavenumber region (3000–3600 cm−1), a broad peak signifies O—H stretching, implying both inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonds involving water (Liu et al., 2023). Compared with retrograded PS alone, the O—H stretching band near 3300 cm−1 exhibited significant broadening, which was more pronounced at higher CNCs concentrations. This may suggest that CNCs coating the PS granules restrict starch gelatinization and promote hydrogen bonding between CNCs and amylose (B. Wang et al., 2024). Notably, the CNCs–amylose interactions may be relatively more prominent than the amylose–amylose bonding, thereby suppressing hydrogen-bond formation among the leached amylose chains; this mechanism effectively delays PS retrogradation. The above spectral characteristics are in strict accordance with the multi-scale findings. The absence of covalent peaks and the broadened O—H envelope are consistent with the LF-NMR results, in which the A₂₃ free-water fraction decreased and the T₂₂ relaxation time was shortened: the abundant hydroxyls on CNCs immobilize water molecules and inhibit amylopectin/amylose chain migration. This interfacial H-bond modulation contrasts sharply with molecular-level reports on flexible Chlamydomonas reinhardtii polysaccharides (CRPs, ∼505 kDa), which inhibit rice starch recrystallization through a quantitative attenuation of H-bonding intensity inside the double helices of the cereal polysaccharide (X. Our rod-like CNCs, however, are rigid particulates rather than bond-energy plasticizers; the broadened O—H envelope observed here signifies CNCs–starch surface H-bonding that, together with the SEM-evidenced steric obstruction, denies amylose/amylopectin chains the proximity required for double-helix nucleation. Thus, although soluble polysaccharides and nanocellulose operate through different mechanisms, they share the same retardation outcome.

Texture profile analysis

The TPA of the 7-day retrograded gels further corroborates the microstructural and water-mobility findings revealed by SEM and LF-NMR. Throughout storage, the firmness of PS gels conventionally reflects the extent to which PS retrogradation proceeds. As shown in Fig. 7, the native PS gel (0% CNCs) exhibited the highest hardness after 7 days at 4 °C, reflecting severe amylopectin recrystallization and dense network compaction. This concentration-dependent trend is consistent with the behavior expected for CNCs composites: the markedly low hardness at 2% CNCs most likely stems from an insufficiently entangled network in which sparse nanofillers fail to reinforce the matrix, leading to partial structural collapse, whereas the recovery at higher loadings is attributed to robust starch–CNCs entanglement and augmented hydrogen bonding (as visually evidenced by the thickened fragment walls and homogeneous pore distribution in the SEM images). Crucially, the hardness of all CNCs-enriched gels remained lower than that of the pure PS control, indicating that CNCs did not promote retrogradation-induced rigidification. Instead, consistent with LF-NMR results, the dose-dependent decrease in the A₂₃ free-water fraction and the shortened T₂₂ relaxation times imply that the hydrophilic CNCs framework immobilized water molecules and inhibited amylopectin chain migration, thereby suppressing crystalline aggregation. Therefore, the composite gels formed a hydrated, concrete-like supportive network (observed at higher magnification) that enhanced water retention and preserved elastic recovery, collectively indicating that CNCs effectively retard the retrogradation of PS rather than accelerating PS chain reassociation.

Thermal properties of retrograded starch

Consistent with the LF-NMR evidence that CNCs constrained water-mediated chain mobility, the DSC retrogradation parameters quantitatively capture the thermal consequence of this suppressed macromolecular rearrangement rather than representing an isolated observation. The thermal properties of PS with different CNCs contents were examined by DSC, and the results are presented in Table 4, Table 5. Retrogradation involves the reorganization of amylopectin molecules into double-helical structures stabilized by hydrogen bonding. The enthalpy change (ΔH) represents the energy required to dissociate these crystalline domains within the starch granules during thermal transitions, and this endothermic process reflects the collective breaking of the hydrogen bonds that maintain the ordered molecular structure. Consequently, the degree of retrogradation (R) can be quantified by comparing the retrogradation enthalpy (ΔHr) with the gelatinization enthalpy (ΔHg) (W. Yu et al., 2025).

The experimental results demonstrated a significant decrease in ΔHg of PS with increasing CNCs content (p < 0.05). This phenomenon can be attributed to the hydration capacity of CNCs, which competitively binds water molecules and consequently limits their availability for starch gelatinization. Such competitive hydration, and the resulting formation of a less-ordered amorphous matrix, is consistent with the FTIR observation of broadened O—H bands without new covalent peaks, and with LF-NMR findings in which CNCs restricted water mobility (reduced A₂₃ free-water fraction and shortened T₂₂). DSC revealed that the onset (To), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures of retrograded PS were consistently lower than those of gelatinized PS, implicating the formation of less-perfect crystalline structures than in native PS. The thermomechanical features of these composites correlate with both the altered nanostructure of PS granules and the crystalline fraction embedded in the gel matrix (Osei Tutu et al., 2023), as visually evidenced by the CNCs-reinforced cellular network (thickened fragment walls, homogeneous pores) in the SEM images.

Both ΔHr and R of PS with CNCs were significantly lower than those of native PS (p < 0.05), exhibiting a clear dose-dependent relationship. For instance, after 7 days of retrogradation, ΔHr was 1.46 J/g for pure PS and 0.60 J/g for PS with 8% CNCs, and the R values dropped from 14.07% to 7.23%. These results indicate that the addition of CNCs inhibits the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between amylopectin branch chains and effectively retards the retrogradation of PS (M. Yu et al., 2020).

XRD spectroscopy of PS–CNCs

Fig. 8 shows the XRD patterns and the calculated relative crystallinity (RC) values of the native PS gels and the PS–CNCs composite gels after one week of storage at 4 °C. The 2θ diffraction angle can be used to determine the crystal structure of starch, while the RC quantifies the extent of PS retrogradation (W. Yu et al., 2025). PS gels, both with and without CNCs, showed a typical B-type crystal structure, characterized by a prominent diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 17°, which is primarily due to the recrystallization of amylose. Furthermore, all samples displayed a broad diffraction signal centered at approximately 22° (2θ), in agreement with a previous report (W. Yu et al., 2025). Fig. 8 shows that CNCs incorporation significantly reduced the RC values of all samples (p < 0.05). The progressive decline in RC with increasing CNCs concentration indicates that CNCs effectively suppress the recrystallization of PS molecules, impeding their transition from an amorphous to an ordered polycrystalline structure during storage. Specifically, after 7 days of storage, the RC of all samples decreased continuously from 34% to 16% as the CNCs concentration increased from 0% to 8% (w/w), representing a significant 52.94% reduction in crystallinity (p < 0.05). From a practical effect-size perspective, this 52.94% reduction in relative crystallinity, together with the concomitant drop in the retrogradation degree from 14.07% to 7.23% (a ∼ 49% relative decrease), represents a large-magnitude intervention effect and indicates that CNCs induce not only a statistically significant (p < 0.05) but also a scientifically substantial retardation of pea starch recrystallization. This pronounced concentration-dependent effect demonstrates the strong inhibitory action of CNCs on PS retrogradation.

The lack of a significant difference between the 6% and 8% CNCs groups in most parameters (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5) suggests that the regulatory efficacy of CNCs approaches saturation near 6–8% loading, which is consistent with the agglomeration limit observed above 8%. Underlying mechanism of the influences of CNCs on the retrogradation of PS

CNCs inhibit PS retrogradation through a clear two-stage mechanism (Fig. 9), consistent with a previous report (Xu & Kuang, 2024). Stage 1 (gelatinization): during heating, the highly hydrophilic CNCs competitively bind water, restricting starch granule swelling and reducing amylose leaching (supported by the RVA peak- and setback-viscosity drops). Stage 2 (retrogradation): upon cooling/storage, the rod-like CNCs disperse within the gel (SEM) and form hydrogen-bonded, concrete-like networks that create steric hindrance against amylopectin recrystallization and immobilize water (supported by the LF-NMR T₂₂ shortening and the DSC decrease in R). At the molecular level, the hydroxyl-rich CNCs surface forms interfacial hydrogen bonds with starch chains and reduces the local water activity, thereby lowering the chain mobility required for double-helix nucleation and crystalline growth. This integrated competitive-hydration and physical-obstruction model quantitatively explains the observed retardation and distinguishes it from a simple dilution effect.Fig. 9Schematic diagram of CNCs regulating PS retrogradation behavior.Fig. 9

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) effectively suppress pea starch (PS) retrogradation. With 0–8% CNCs, the degree of retrogradation decreased from 14.07% to 7.23% and the relative crystallinity from 34% to 16%, accompanied by a reduced free-water fraction (A₂₃), a shortened T₂₂ relaxation time, and softer gels. Together with the reduced peak and setback viscosities, these results indicate inhibition across both the short-term pasting and the long-term storage stages.

This work establishes a dispersion-state–governed structure–activity relationship for CNCs in a high-amylose legume starch: the inhibitory efficacy rose with CNCs loading and plateaued at 6–8%, whereas higher loadings caused agglomeration and poor redispersibility. This non-linear behavior distinguishes rigid, rod-like CNCs from flexible hydrocolloids and spherical nanoparticles, indicating that interfacial dispersion, rather than concentration alone, governs the inhibitory efficacy. The 6–8% CNCs window offers a non-chemical formulation guideline for chilled pea-based foods.

Nevertheless, the study was limited to 4 °C storage (5–7 days), and starch digestibility, ultra-early (<24 h) kinetics, and real-food matrices were not evaluated. Future work should resolve the CNCs–starch interfacial hydrogen-bond network by molecular dynamics simulation, benchmark CNCs against conventional anti-staling agents (e.g., MCC), and validate scalability under room-temperature and freeze–thaw conditions with in vitro digestibility profiling.
