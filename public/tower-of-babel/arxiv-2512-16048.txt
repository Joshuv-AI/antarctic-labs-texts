Abstract
Recently, calculations of the ground states of the lightest nuclei have been performed using highly accurate data on realistic internucleon forces. In this paper, these results were used to describe the properties of nuclei with nucleon numbers A > 4 A>4 . Taking into account tensor forces leads to the conclusion that the four subsystems in the nucleus with zero nucleon orbital momenta are combined predominantly into the S 0 1 {}^{1}S_{0} cluster. Subsystems with nonzero orbital momenta also combine into clusters with lower potential energy. This approach allows us to consistently explain the lifetime of the 8 Be nucleus, the Hoyle state, the sequential mechanism of the reaction with the emission of α \alpha particles, the shift of the reaction threshold, and more. The assumption of the existence of a one-dimensional effective interaction of nucleons in the nucleus leads to the conclusion that the nucleus contains a ”power center” and, accordingly, nucleons have orbital angular momenta relative to this ”power center.” Our approach does not predict the presence of such a ”power center” in the nucleus. Discussion of the structure of nuclei with the number

Physicists often write that the interaction of nucleons in a nucleus
differs from the interaction of free nucleons
9
and, for the
convenience of calculations, introduce simpler one-dimensional
potentials. Such a simplification of realistic internucleon forces
can lead to questionable conclusions about the structure of the
nucleus. In this paper, we used the results of precise calculations
of the properties of nuclei with the number of nucleons
A\leq
4,
carried out on the basis of realistic internucleon forces, to study
the properties of nuclei with the number of nucleons
A>4
.

From Fig.1 it is evident that the interaction of two nucleons occurs
in a four-dimensional space. The first axis is the distance axis,
where the coordinate takes on a continuous series of values from 0
to
\infty
. The second axis is the orbital angular momentum axis

L
, also from 0 to
\infty
. The orbital angular momentum takes
on integer values. The third axis is the spin axis
S
, whose
magnitude can take two values: 0 or 1, and the fourth axis is the
isospin axis
T
, whose magnitude can take the values 0 or 1.
The interaction of nucleons along all four axes is different, which
leads to the contribution of tensor forces. Accordingly, the nuclear
interaction of nucleons can be described by a rank-4 tensor.

The presence of tensor forces does not change the external
characteristics of the nucleus, such as the total energy
E
,
the total angular momentum
J
, and the parity of
the nucleus. Tensor forces change the configurations of nucleons
and, accordingly, their potential and kinetic energies and mass
defect. This significantly alters the internal properties of the
nucleus. The orbital angular momentum of nucleons can take any
values satisfying the laws of conservation of total angular momentum
and parity of the nucleus, including those leading to a spin flip of
one or more nucleons simultaneously. The total orbital angular
momentum of the nucleus
L
can take values
satisfying the law of conservation of total angular momentum of the
nucleus
J = L
+
S
where the total
spin of the nucleus
S
can take values 0
\leq
S
\leq
A/2 if the number of nucleons A is even and 1/2
\leq
S

\leq
A/2 if A is odd.

Tensor forces make the smallest contribution,
\sim
5%, to the
structure of the deuteron, in which only two states,
S
and

D
, with parallel nucleon spins, are allowed. Each new nucleon
in the nucleus increases the probability of tensor forces
contributing. Thus, medium and heavy nuclei can spend most of their
time in configurations determined by tensor forces.

All nucleon states in a nucleus must satisfy the Pauli Exclusion
Principle, which can be formulated as follows: at any point in the
four-dimensional space of a system of particles with half-integer
spin, there can be no more than one particle. This law excludes the
formation of two or more identical nucleon subsystems in a nucleus.
Ignoring the Pauli Exclusion Principle can lead to erroneous
interpretations of nuclear structure and nuclear reaction mechanisms
[10].

From Fig.1 it is evident that the subsystems of nucleons with zero
orbital angular momenta have the highest (in absolute value)
potential energy. There can be four such subsystems of nucleons in
the nucleus: two
pp
and
nn
interactions with isospin

T
=1 and two
pn
interactions with
T
=1 and
T
=0. The binding energy of a system of three nucleons is

\sim

\sim

zero orbital angular momenta are combined mainly into a
{}^{1}S_{0}

cluster (Fig.2a). Intuitively, a
{}^{1}S_{0}
cluster resembles the
”S-shell” predicted by the shell model of the nucleus. However, a
significant difference between a
{}^{1}S_{0}
cluster and an ”S-shell” is
that a ”power center” is not required for its formation. It can
freely move throughout the entire volume of the nucleus, exchange
nucleons, decay according to Young’s schemes into several parts, and
even disappear altogether. Physicists have previously expressed
doubts about the existence of a ”power center” in the nucleus

11

III
Conclusions

Tensor interactions between nucleons are of great importance for
describing the structure of nuclei and the mechanisms of nuclear
reactions. As the number of nucleons in a nucleus increases, the
role of tensor forces increases. The assumption of the existence of
an averaged effective interaction between nucleons in the nucleus
leads to the conclusion that the nucleus contains a ”force center,”
and nucleons acquire orbital angular momenta relative to this ”force
center.” Our approach suggests the absence of such a ”force center”
in the nucleus.

Numerous modifications of the cluster model of the nucleus do not
take into account the Pauli Exclusion Principle. This approach is
justified by the dubious assumptions that the wave functions of
clusters in the nucleus do not overlap or that these clusters are
bosons. Our approach takes the Pauli Exclusion Principle into
account naturally.

This approach explains the lifetime of the
8
Be nucleus, the Hoyle
state, and the possible shift in the reaction threshold during
cluster emission. Similar effects can be observed in other nuclei
and in various nuclear reactions.

It can also be noted that in nuclear interaction, the direction of
acceleration does not coincide with the direction of the force. This
is the main difference between nuclear and electromagnetic
interaction. Therefore, it is appropriate to classify the atom as
belonging to the macrocosm, while the microcosm begins with the
nucleus.
