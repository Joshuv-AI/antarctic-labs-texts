Quality of life and care experience in durable responders to immune checkpoint inhibitors for advanced or metastatic NSCLC or melanoma
Authors: N Girard, J Bonastre, G Spurrier, H Lemasson, M Chartier, V Moreau Mallet, S Michelliza, N Texier, S Hervy, C Robert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed outcomes for advanced/metastatic melanoma and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), allowing for a growing population of ICI durable responders (ICI-DR). Despite improved survival, ICI-DR patients may face persistent health-related quality of life (HRQoL) challenges and these are poorly documented. The QUALICI study aimed to describe HRQoL and factors associated with better HRQoL outcomes, patients’ experience, and expectations toward HRQoL in ICI-DR patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC or melanoma.Patients and methodsQUALICI was a multicenter, cross-sectional study conducted in 16 French hospitals. All ICI-DR adults (≥2 years since ICI initiation, no subsequent cancer therapy) were screened and randomly selected to participate and complete questionnaires assessing HRQoL (EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels [EQ-5D-5L]), social difficulties [Social Difficulties Inventory (SDI)], fatigue (Chalder Fatigue Scale), and care experience.ResultsA total of 234 patients (117 with NSCLC and 117 with melanoma) completed the questionnaires. Most patients (62.0% with NSCLC and 69.7% with melanoma) reported no/slight problems on the five EQ-5D-5L dimensions. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) visual analog scale scores were 67.0 ± 19.3 in the NSCLC group and 74.8 ± 19 in the melanoma group, with higher scores in younger and professionally active patients. Most patients (78.9% with NSCLC and 95.3% with melanoma) had moderate-to-severe fatigue and social difficulties were more pronounced in patients with NSCLC (mean ± SD SDI score: NSCLC 10.4 ± 8.5, melanoma 5.9 ± 6.6). Patients with moderate-to-severe problems on EQ-5D-5L had poorer perceived general health, increased fatigue, and more social difficulties in both groups. Two-thirds of patients had discussed quality of life issues with health care professionals yet 22.2% felt unable to fully express concerns.ConclusionMost ICI-DR patients reported satisfactory HRQoL but heterogeneity and communication gaps support the need for patient-centered survivorship care.Key words: durable responders, health-related quality of life, immune checkpoint inhibitors, melanoma, NSCLC, real-world evidence

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, substantially improving outcomes for patients with advanced malignancies. ICIs have extended overall survival in various solid tumors, particularly melanoma and lung cancer, leading to the emergence of a population of ICI durable responders (ICI-DR).1, 2, 3

Although ICI-DR patients benefit from prolonged survival, they may face specific challenges related to their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and experience physical, emotional, and neurocognitive impairments stemming from the long-term effects of their disease and treatment.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 The impact extends beyond the patients themselves, often causing psychological distress among their relatives, colleagues, and friends. The financial burden due to direct care costs and employment difficulties presents another major concern.8, 9, 10

Even after achieving remission, ICI-DR patients may experience severe emotional distress and neurocognitive dysfunction, which negatively affect their HRQoL. Persistent symptoms such as fatigue, cough, and pain can remain constant or worsen over time.6 Additionally, long-term immune-related adverse events (AEs) remain insufficiently characterized. Emerging evidence suggests that chronic toxicities, often low-grade but prolonged, can affect multiple organ systems, including endocrine, rheumatologic, pulmonary, and neurological functions, and may have a sustained impact on daily functioning and well-being.11,12 These findings highlight that ICI-DR patients represent a distinct survivorship population in whom the key issue is no longer tumor control but the long-term burden of persistent symptoms and treatment-related sequelae. Despite this, real-world data on HRQoL in ICI-DR patients remain limited, particularly outside melanoma, and long-term follow-up studies in other tumor types such as non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are scarce.13 Improvement in HRQoL for these patients may be achieved through more patient-centered care, tailored to their unique needs, and enhanced interdisciplinary coordination among health care providers.14,15 This approach should integrate the specificities of ICI treatments, which implies a deeper understanding of HRQoL issues specific to ICI-DR patients.

The QUALICI study aimed to describe HRQoL and factors associated with better HRQoL outcomes, patients’ experience, and expectations toward HRQoL among ICI-DR patients with advanced or metastatic (adv/met) melanoma or NSCLC.

Material and methods

Study design

QUALICI was an observational cross-sectional study conducted across 16 public and private French hospitals. The study focused on ICI-DR patients, defined as those who had started ICIs ≥2 years before study enrollment without subsequent cancer treatment.1 Two groups were enrolled, including patients with advanced or metastatic (adv/met) NSCLC and patients with adv/met melanoma.

The study aimed to enroll 130 patients per group and to receive questionnaires from 100 per group in order to provide adequate precision to the estimated mean scores obtained using instruments of patient-reported outcomes.

The study was divided into two phases: patient identification phase and data collection phase. In the identification phase, all eligible ICI-DR patients who started treatment as of 2017 at 10 lung and six melanoma investigator sites were recorded in a nonidentifying screening registry using standardized data sources. Up to 20 (NSCLC) or 33 (melanoma) patients per site were then randomly selected from the screening registry and invited to participate in the study to reach the target of 130 patients per group. In the data collection phase, consenting patients completed a comprehensive questionnaire at home that covered sociodemographic characteristics, daily life, quality of life (QoL), health care professionals (HCPs) visited during follow-up, care experience, QoL issues, and expectations toward HRQoL. Medical data, including medical history, prior types of therapy, ICI treatment duration, and whether ICI treatment was still ongoing, were recorded by the investigational medical team using an electronic case report form.

Data collection

Participants completed a comprehensive questionnaire that included a series of validated instruments to assess generic HRQoL, social challenges, fatigue, and cancer care experience. The questionnaire was elaborated with a multidisciplinary board and tested by patients.

The EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) evaluates five dimensions of health (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression), each scored on a five-point Likert scale (1: no problems; 5: extreme problems), and includes a vertical visual analog scale (VAS) for self-rated health (0-100; higher scores indicating better perceived health).16 The EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) minimal important difference (MID) was 5 points.17 The Social Difficulties Inventory (SDI) comprises three dimensions—everyday living, self and others’ interactions, and money matters—with 21 items graded on a four-point Likert scale (0: no difficulty; 3: very much difficulty).18,19 Total SDI scores (range 0-44) and subdomain scores were calculated. A three-point variation in the total score was considered clinically significant.19 Fatigue severity was assessed with the Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFQ-11), comprising 11 items rated on a four-point scale to estimate physical and mental fatigue subscores and a total score.20 Apprehension about the future was captured using four items from the QoL Instrument for Cancer Survivors, focusing on concerns for patients’ own health and their family, uncertainty about the future, and perceived positive changes.21 Mean monthly time spent for cancer care was self-reported by patients. Cancer care burden was quantified using a Likert scale quoted from 0 (no perceived burden) to 10 (very important burden). Finally, the questionnaire included items about HCPs visited during follow-up, HRQoL integration into clinical practice (frequency of discussions about HRQoL with HCPs and HRQoL topics discussed), and patients’ expectations for HRQoL management.

Statistical analysis

Endpoint variables were summarized using descriptive statistics stratified by cancer type (NSCLC and melanoma). Continuous variables are reported as means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and ranges. Discrete variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were explicitly quantified for all endpoints. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Subgroup analyses using two-sided statistical tests (α = 0.05) were carried out to describe each outcome by cancer group. For continuous variables, two independent groups were compared using Student’s t-test (if assumptions validated) or Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney nonparametric test if not. Analysis of variance test was used to compare more than two groups. The α risk was 5%. Given the exploratory nature of these subgroup analyses, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Criteria for these analyses were age (<65 versus ≥65 years), time since ICI initiation (24-30, 30-36, and ≥36 months), ongoing/discontinued cancer treatment status, prior therapy type (chemotherapy-based versus other regimens), and EQ-5D-5L profiles. For the latter, patients were stratified into three EQ-5D-5L severity subgroups: no problems across dimensions (NO), only slight problems in at least one dimension (SLIGHT), and moderate/severe problems in at least one dimension (MODERATE+).

Factors associated with good self-rated general health (defined as EQ-5D-5L VAS score greater than cancer-specific median score) were searched using logistic regression models. In line with the exploratory objective of the analysis, variables with P values ≤0.20 from the univariate analyses were considered as candidates for inclusion in the multivariable models in order to avoid premature exclusion of potentially relevant factors. Candidate variables were also selected on the basis of clinical relevance. A backward elimination process was then applied, retaining only the variables with P values ≤0.05.

Results

Patient population

From September to November 2023, 469 patients were screened in the 16 hospitals. Among these, 372 patients were eligible and invited to participate in the study, 302 consented to participate, and 234 filled the questionnaire (completion rate of 62.9%) (117 with NSCLC and 117 with melanoma). Baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. The NSCLC cohort comprised predominantly male patients (63.2%) with a mean age of 65.8 years. The melanoma group had a balanced gender distribution (51.3% male) and higher mean age (67.5 years). Occupational activity status differed between cohorts, with 17.2% active patients in the NSCLC group and 29.9% in the melanoma group. As per the inclusion criteria, all patients had received ICI therapy. At the time of ICI initiation, 75.2% of patients with NSCLC and 74.4% of patients with melanoma had metastatic disease. The mean duration since ICI initiation was 48.2 ± 15.1 months in the NSCLC group and 49.4 ± 13.8 months in the melanoma group. The mean duration of ICI treatment differed between patients with NSCLC and those with melanoma (33.3 months in patients with NSCLC and 23.6 months in patients with melanoma). The proportion of patients still on active ICI treatment was higher among patients with NSCLC (36.8% versus 22.2% in patients with melanoma).

QoL assessment (EQ-5D-5L)

The distribution of EQ-5D-5L dimensions and utility index score are presented Figure 1. Specifically, 17.0% of patients with NSCLC and 21.9% of those with melanoma reported no problems in any dimension. Severe or very severe problems were reported by a minority of patients, ranging from 3.4% to 13.0% in the NSCLC population and from 1.7% to 8.7% in the melanoma population, depending on the dimension assessed. The mean utility index score was similar, 0.87 ± 0.2 and 0.90 ± 0.1 in the NSCLC and melanoma groups, respectively.

EQ-5D-5L profiles. EQ-5D-5L, EuroQoL 5 Dimensions 5 Levels; NSCLC, non-small-cell lung cancer.

When stratified into the three EQ-5D-5L severity subgroups, most patients with NSCLC reported MODERATE+ issues across the five dimensions (52%); 17% and 31% presented NO or SLIGHT problems, respectively. Among patients with melanoma, a large majority reported NO or SLIGHT issues (21.9% and 36.0%, respectively) and 42.1% reported at least one MODERATE+ problem.

VAS scores were 67.0 ± 19.3 and 74.8 ± 19.0 in patients with NSCLC and patients with melanoma, respectively, and showed different patterns across age and activity status: patients with NSCLC demonstrated no significant age-related differences, whereas patients with melanoma aged <65 years reported higher VAS scores than older patients (81.3 ± 12 versus 71.4 ± 21; P = 0.02). Professionally active patients had higher VAS scores in both cohorts (NSCLC: 74.4 ± 18.8 versus 65.8 ± 18.8, P = 0.073; melanoma: 82.2 ± 11.8 versus 71.6 ± 20.5, P = 0.007). EQ-5D-5L severity subgroups showed significant differences in VAS scores, with MODERATE+ patients with NSCLC reporting MODERATE+ problems scoring 56.6 compared with 76.8 (SLIGHT) and 81.3 (NO) (P < 0.0001) and patients with melanoma reporting MODERATE+ problems scoring 64.1 compared with 81.6 (SLIGHT) and 85.2 (NO) (P < 0.0001). These differences also exceeded the MID of 5 points. There was no significant difference in VAS scores according to ICI continuation status, treatment duration, or prior therapies (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101613).

Fatigue (CFQ-11 assessment)

The mean total CFQ-11 score was 15.5 ± 5.7 in patients with NSCLC and 13.8 ± 4.5 in patients with melanoma (Figure 2A). Moderate and severe fatigue were reported in 68.3% and 21.2% of patients with NSCLC and in 84.0% and 4.7% of patients with melanoma, respectively. Physical fatigue scores differed between cohorts (NSCLC 10.6 ± 4.3, melanoma 9.2 ± 3.4), whereas mental fatigue showed similar scores (NSCLC4.9 ± 2.3, melanoma 4.5 ± 1.7).

(A) Mean CFQ-11 scores. (B) Mean SDI scores. (C) Emotional state (items from the QoL CSV). CFQ-11, Chalder Fatigue Scale; NSCLC, non-small-cell lung cancer; QoL CSV, Quality of Life Instrument for Cancer Survivors; SDI, Social Difficulties Inventory.

Occupational activity was inversely associated with fatigue in patients with melanoma (active 12.5 versus inactive 14.4, P = 0.05) but not in those with NSCLC. Subgroup analyses revealed no difference according to age, treatment discontinuation, treatment duration, or prior therapies (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101613). A gradual escalation of fatigue was observed across EQ-5D-5L severity subgroups: patients reporting MODERATE+ problems reported significantly higher CFQ-11 scores than those reporting SLIGHT/NO problems in NSCLC (18.2 versus 12.7/12.4, P < 0.0001) and melanoma (15.7 versus 13.6/10.8, P < 0.0001).

Social difficulties assessment (SDI)

The mean total SDI scores were 10.4 ± 8.5 in the NSCLC cohort and 5.9 ± 6.6 in the melanoma cohort (Figure 2B), with 50% of patients with NSCLC and 25% of those with melanoma exceeding the cut point of 10 suggesting notable levels of social distress. Differences persisted between the two cohorts across all dimensions: daily life (NSCLC 4.1 ± 4.0, melanoma 2.4 ± 3.2), financial problems (NSCLC 3 ± 3.0, melanoma 1.5 ± 2.3), and personal/other domains (NSCLC 3.2 ± 3.3, melanoma 2 ± 2.6) (Figure 2B).

Age was associated with SDI burden in both cohorts: patients with NSCLC aged <65 years reported higher scores than older patients (12.7 ± 8.8 versus 8.5 ± 7.7, P = 0.02), which was paralleled in patients with melanoma (8.4 ± 7.3 versus 4.3 ± 5.6, P < 0.01); these differences surpassed the MID estimated at 3 points. Although no difference in SDI scores was observed on the basis of occupational activity status in patients with NSCLC, professionally active patients with melanoma had higher SDI scores compared with patients who were not working (8 ± 7.2 versus 4.8 ± 6, P = 0.02). SDI scores did not differ significantly by treatment discontinuation, ICI duration, or prior therapy (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101613). EQ-5D-5L severity subgroups revealed pronounced SDI gradients: patients reporting MODERATE+ problems scored significantly higher than those reporting SLIGHT/NO problems in the NSCLC group (13.0 versus 8.3/6.0 P < 0.0001) and melanoma group (8.7 versus 4.3/3.1, P < 0.01).

Emotional state

A substantial subset of patients reported concerns about their future and the impact of cancer on their lives and those of their family (Figure 2C). Moderate-to-high concern about disease progression was expressed by 33.1% of patients with NSCLC and 34.2% of patients with melanoma. Familial cancer risk anxieties affected 45.3% of patients with NSCLC and 42.6% of those with melanoma, whereas uncertainty about long-term outcomes was reported by 45.5% of the NSCLC cohort and 33.4% of the melanoma cohort. In addition, 35.6% of patients with NSCLC and 20.0% of patients with melanoma described positive life changes attributable to their cancer experience.

Time allocation and care burden

Patients with NSCLC reported allocating a mean of 7.5 ± 13.4 h/month to cancer care, whereas patients with melanoma reported allocating 7.7 ± 17.4 h/month. Significantly more time was spent on cancer care by patients continuing ICIs (NSCLC: 9.9 versus 5.9 h, P < 0.01; melanoma: 17.1 versus 4.0 h, P < 0.0001). EQ-5D-5L severity was associated with an increase in time dedicated to health care in patients reporting MODERATE+ problems (NSCLC: 9.6 versus 6.3 h/3.1 h in patients reporting NO/SLIGHT problems, P = 0.04; melanoma: 7.7 versus 4.5 h/4.2 h, P = 0.04). Care burden, assessed with a Likert scale, averaged 2.5 in NSCLC and 3.1 in melanoma and increased in MODERATE+ subgroups (NSCLC: 3.1 versus 1.5/1.1 in NO/SLIGHT subgroups, P < 0.0001; melanoma: 4.2 versus 2.9/1.2, P < 0.0001) and among patients with melanoma continuing ICIs (melanoma: 4.8 versus 2.7, P < 0.01; NSCLC: 2.9 versus 2.3, P = 0.24) (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101613).

Overall, 87.8% of patients had regular oncology visits (35.4% annually and 52.4% more frequently) and 88% general practitioners visits (NSCLC 92.6%, melanoma 83.5%). Visits with a pain specialist were limited (NSCLC 13.7%, melanoma 10.6%). Visits with a nurse decreased after ICI discontinuation (hospital-based: NSCLC 63.6% to 20.3%, melanoma 83.3% to 23.1%; private: NSCLC 82.4% to 50%, melanoma 50% to 46.8%). Supplementary care consultations occurred primarily through patient initiative. Physiotherapists/osteopaths were the most frequently consulted (NSCLC 31.9%, melanoma 37.8%), followed by psychologists (11%) and dieticians (10%) (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101613).

QoL management during care and patients’ expectations

Two-thirds of patients had already addressed QoL topics with HCPs but a substantial proportion of patients (NSCLC 22.1%, melanoma 22.8%) declared that they were unable to express themself as they wished. Among patients who reported discussing their HRQoL with HCPs, 10.6% in the NSCLC group and 10.5% in melanoma group reported feeling unable to fully express themselves. Additionally, 11.5% of patients with NSCLC and 12.3% of patients with melanoma reported no discussion about QoL with HCPs despite a wish to do so. Most-discussed topics in both the NSCLC and melanoma groups were feelings about symptoms, sleep disorders, and experience of the treatment process and subsequent management stages (Figure 3).

Factors associated with good self-rated general health

In the multivariable models, higher household income was positively associated in NSCLC with better perceived health than median, whereas patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≥1 or those treated in private hospitals or general hospitals reported lower self-rated general health (Figure 4). In patients with melanoma, moderate or intense physical activity was associated with improved perceived health, whereas patients with an ECOG performance status ≥1 had a poorer outcome for this item.

Discussion

QoL outcomes

The QUALICI study provides comprehensive insights into HRQoL among ICI-DR patients for adv/met NSCLC and melanoma, with a median response duration of 48 months, exceeding that reported in prior long-responder studies.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11,22,23 Analysis of EQ-5D-5L dimensions revealed that 62%-70% of patients reported no or minimal problems across most domains. However, patients with NSCLC exhibited a mean VAS score of 67.0, lower than population norms for comparable age groups (∼72 for individuals aged 45-75 years and 69 for those aged >75 years),24 whereas patients with melanoma reported a mean score of 74.8, closer to general population values. These findings align with melanoma survivorship studies showing preserved but suboptimal HRQoL in ICI-DR patients.6,22,23 In NSCLC, limited prior data on long-term ICI responders are available and these studies used various definitions of long-term response (e.g. survival >1-2 years after diagnosis or treatment).8,11

Despite overall satisfactory HRQoL, as in prior studies, ICI-DR patients in the QUALICI study experienced impairments in specific HRQoL domains, including physical and social roles,7 emotional and neurocognitive functioning,5 or financial conditions.23 Most patients reported moderate-to-severe fatigue, particularly patients with NSCLC (with >20% reporting severe fatigue even after ICI therapy), as well as patients with melanoma, mirroring findings in previous studies.6,22,23 Approximately one-third of patients remained worried about future test results, >40% feared familial cancer development, and 40% were uncertain about the future, reflecting emotional impairment, as seen in previous studies.25 The SDI identified financial and daily life difficulties in a subset of patients, particularly younger, professionally active patients with NSCLC. Financial toxicity is well described in non-European countries.9,23 In QUALICI, such difficulties appeared more prevalent in younger, professionally active patients struggling to return to work and in patients with NSCLC.

QoL management during follow-up

The QUALICI study uniquely examined the management of HRQoL by HCPs in ICI-DR patients and their unmet needs. These aspects remain underexplored in literature, where only small-scale qualitative studies are available.25, 26, 27, 28 Time spent on cancer care was associated with ongoing ICI treatment. After ICI therapy, patients continued frequent oncologist, general practitioner, and pharmacist visits, whereas hospital nurse consultations sharply decreased and private nurse use remained substantial in half. Access to other professionals was limited; for instance, only 11% of patients consulted psychologists despite evidence from recent studies highlighting the importance of psychological and social support in helping patients regain control over their lives.25, 26, 27, 28 Younger patients were more likely to access psychological support services.

The perceived care burden was generally low in ICI-DR patients. This burden remained stable among patients with NSCLC regardless of whether they were still receiving treatment, whereas patients with melanoma receiving treatment reported higher burden compared with those who had discontinued ICIs. These findings align with prior studies indicating that patients often view their treatment as their only viable option for survival and express gratitude for ICIs, embracing treatment as ‘an opportunity they had to seize to extend their lives'.27,28 This perspective shaped how patients perceived care burden, highlighting gratitude and a sense of luck among patients treated with ICI.28

Discussed QoL topics, unmet needs, and expectations

HRQoL discussions with HCPs primarily addressed somatic concerns related to disease symptoms, long-term effects, and treatment pathways. Topics related to broader life aspects, including family life, social interactions, financial concerns, dependence on caregivers, or physical appearance, were less frequently raised. Although most patients did not expect more discussion on these topics, 12%-20% expressed a desire for deeper conversations about these topics, particularly in the NSCLC cohort. Previous studies emphasized that long-term responders need to transition from the ‘sick role’ to a ‘healthy role’, which involves being seen as a whole person rather than just a patient with cancer.28 This transition requires HCPs to address broader life topics and provide personalized attention. The QUALICI study highlighted that disease-related topics still dominate discussions with ICI-DR patients and suggested that broader life issues could be increasingly incorporated into care.

Patients identified several priorities for improving HRQoL management during care. They appeared particularly concerned about the need for better monitoring of AEs, which can substantially affect daily life.6,11,12 Thus, this need remained ambiguous regarding whether it referred to chronic long-term AEs or those experienced throughout treatment. Previous studies have also highlighted the necessity for tailored medical information and AE management strategies that address both patient and family needs.26,27

Patients also emphasized the importance of human interaction in their care and preferred strengthened relationships with a close medical team rather than support from social assistants or psychologists or digital tools. They identified a need to improve coordination within medical teams and to train HCPs in HRQoL. In this context, such training could include several practical components: education on the interpretation and clinical use of HRQoL instruments; development of communication skills to facilitate discussions on symptoms, fatigue, and social difficulties during routine consultations; and better integration of HRQoL evaluation into multidisciplinary tumor boards and survivorship care pathways. These levers for improving care are consistent with the observations made in a previous study conducted among patients receiving ICI treatment.15

Limitations

Firstly, although the study identified some factors associated with a better perceived general health, the cross-sectional design of the study prevented causal inference. Secondly, the representativeness of ICI-DR patients may be affected by nonparticipation biases. To mitigate this, patients were randomly selected from all eligible consecutive patients screened in the participating centers. Thirdly, the study did not collect data on whether ICI treatments were administered as monotherapy or in combination; some combination therapies, such as double immunotherapy in patients with melanoma, are known to increase toxicity, likely impacting QoL.29 Lack of detailed data on chronic AEs and their management restricted assessment of their long-term impact on HRQoL. Finally, the large number of subgroup and exploratory analyses increased the risk of type I error; the findings from these analyses should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

The QUALICI study is among the first real-life assessments of HRQoL in ICI-DR patients in France and Europe. By using multiple questionnaires, it provides a comprehensive evaluation across QoL dimensions. Patients generally reported satisfying HRQoL outcomes, although scores remained below population norms, particularly in patients with NSCLC. Marked heterogeneity was noted, with some patients facing persistent physical, social, and financial impairment. The study also revealed communication gaps between patients and HCPs, with one in five patients not addressing their HRQoL concerns during consultations. Patient-prioritized measures could improve HRQoL management, in particular closer monitoring of AEs, enhanced coordination among medical teams, and HCP training in HRQoL.

These findings highlight the need for patient-centered approaches combining physical and psychosocial dimensions in long-term cancer management. These results provide a basis for optimizing care pathways and improving HRQoL outcomes for this growing population of long-term responders.
