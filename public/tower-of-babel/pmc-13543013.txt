Vascularization of Human iPSC‐Derived Kidney Organoids Using Perfusion Culture, Pre‐Vascularized Collagen Scaffolds, and Decellularized Extracellular Matrix
Authors: Helen Kearney, Andrea Mazzoleni, Ivan Martin, Lorenzo Moroni, Manuele Giuseppe Muraro, Carlos Mota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived kidney organoids represent promising in vitro models for studying kidney development and drug‐induced toxicity, yet their lack of vasculature limits maturation and translational use. Here, we present a proof‐of‐concept co‐culture platform to promote vascularization of kidney organoids by integrating stromal vascular fraction cells, collagen sponge scaffolds, porcine kidney decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), and dynamic culture in a perfusion‐based U‐CUP bioreactor. We systematically evaluated the effects of serum supplementation, scaffold pre‐vascularization, dECM encapsulation, and flow conditions on organoid–vascular integration. Low‐level serum supplementation (1.5% FBS) sustained vascular networks without compromising organoid morphology. Perfusion supported vascular expansion; however, while a low flow rate comparable to interstitial tissue flow (0.01 mL/min) preserved organoid morphology and glomerular‐like structures, a higher flow rate optimized for vascularization (0.47 mL/min) disrupted overall organoid architecture. Encapsulation within dECM preserved glomerular morphology and supported vascular infiltration, whereas co‐culture with stromal vascular fraction cells enabled putative podocyte–endothelial interactions. Finally, exposure to the calcineurin inhibitor tacrolimus revealed dose‐dependent cytotoxicity and characteristic nephron and vascular injury, demonstrating the model's utility for nephrotoxicity screening. The established modular platform enables the engineering of vascularized kidney organoids, establishing foundations for physiologically relevant models for disease modeling and pre‐clinical drug testing.Keywords: decellularized extracellular matrix, kidney organoids, perfusion‐based bioreactor, stromal vascular fraction, vascularization

Introduction

Kidney organoids derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) have emerged as promising in vitro models to study kidney development, disease, and drug‐induced toxicity. Current differentiation protocols generate 3D organoid structures that mimic the very early stages of nephrogenesis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. However, organoids lack the ability to mature beyond a certain developmental stage. This limitation is believed to arise, at least in part, from the absence of a functional vascular network to support nephron development and maintenance [6, 7]. Furthermore, once development halts, prolonged culture leads to reduced expression of terminal kidney markers and an increase in off‐target cell populations, potentially indicative of dedifferentiation.

Previous studies have shown that transplantation of kidney organoids in vivo leads to the recruitment of host vasculature, which further supports organoid development and maturation [8, 9]. However, this strategy requires the use of animals and presents ethical and technical hurdles, including reliance on non‐human physiological models, limited reproducibility, and high costs.

To overcome these limitations, several in vitro strategies have been explored to stimulate vascularization. For example, culturing organoids in a microfluidic device under flow promoted the formation of perfusable vascular networks [10, 11, 12, 13]. However, microfluidic devices require specialized microfabrication, are prone to clogging, and are technically demanding to operate. As an alternative, the U‐CUP perfusion bioreactor, combined with collagen sponge scaffold, provides a straightforward, scalable platform for dynamic culture. The U‐CUP perfusion bioreactor supports convective transport, nutrient and oxygen delivery, and cell–matrix integration within larger 3D tissue constructs, while the collagen sandwich format allows multiple organoids to be incorporated within the same perfused scaffold. Together, these features make the system more amenable to scale‐up than closed microfluidic formats. The system is also adaptable, as flow rates can be readily adjusted using standard laboratory syringe pumps, while the accessible cell chamber allows straightforward sample mounting and scaffold retrieval. Compared with closed microfluidic organ‐on‐chip systems, the U‐CUP system provides a more accessible macrofluidic format for larger scaffold‐based constructs, with reduced handling complexity and lower susceptibility to clogging. This setup, therefore supports larger tissue volumes while providing a microenvironment that closely mimics the native extracellular niche [14].

Other studies have also shown that embedding kidney organoids in porcine kidney decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) provides a supportive microenvironment that improves vascularization and enhances tissue organization [15]. Porcine kidney dECM provides both mechanical cues and biochemical signals known to enhance vascularization, thereby complementing the dynamic flow and pre‐vascularization strategy. These findings suggest that both flow and a supportive extracellular environment are key factors for the vascular and functional maturation of kidney organoids.

Another important consideration for promoting vascularization is the introduction of an appropriate cell source to drive vasculature development. Previous studies have used human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) or hiPSC‐derived endothelial cells as vascular cell sources; however, these are often difficult to obtain and require lengthy and expensive culture protocols [16]. Stromal vascular fraction (SVF) is an attractive alternative that contains a heterogeneous mix of endothelial cells, pericytes, and stromal progenitors that can rapidly self‐assemble into vessel‐like networks in perfusion‐based bioreactors [14]. Unlike single endothelial populations, SVF provide both the cellular diversity and paracrine support necessary for stable vessel formation. Moreover, SVF can be obtained from small volumes of adipose tissue through relatively non‐invasive procedures such as liposuction, making it a practical and accessible donor‐derived vascular cell source [17]. This represents a clear advantage over commonly used endothelial populations, such as HUVECs or bone marrow‐derived progenitors, which require either limited donor material or more invasive isolation procedures.

Together, these studies highlight the need for integrated platforms that combine vascular cells, matrix support, and controlled perfusion, while also evaluating whether enhanced vascularization can be achieved without loss of nephron organization. Here, we aim to enhance vascularization of hiPSC‐derived kidney organoids by combining four elements: (i) A collagen sponge scaffold to provide mechanical support, (ii) SVF to establish a pre‐vascularized niche, (iii) dynamic culture in a perfusion‐based U‐CUP bioreactor, and (iv) dECM to provide biochemical and mechanical support for kidney and vascular development. Our aim is to establish a proof‐of‐concept for this integrated strategy and to provide preliminary evidence of its capacity to enhance vascularization and support integration with developing kidney organoids. By assessing vascular network formation alongside preservation of nephron morphology, this study addresses the balance between vascular stimulation and organoid structural integrity. By enabling simultaneous assessment of nephron and vascular injury, such a system could provide a more physiologically relevant tool for preclinical drug screening. To illustrate this application, we conducted a preliminary proof‐of‐concept test with tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) known to cause glomerular and microvascular injury, thereby demonstrating the potential of the platform for evaluating nephrotoxicity.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Differentiation

Human Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell Culture Maintenance

hiPSC line (LUMC0031iCTRL08; LUMCi004‐C; RRID:CVCL_ZA01), referred to hereafter as LUMC were maintained as colonies on 1% Geltrex (Gibco) coated Nunc cell culture treated plates (ThermoFisher) with mTeSRplus medium (STEMCELL Technologies). Medium was replenished every 2–3 days or when the color change indicated acidification. Cells were passaged once the colonies reached a point where the edges were rounded. For passaging, culture medium was removed, and colonies were washed once with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Gibco). Cells were then incubated with 1 mL Gentle cell dissociation reagent (STEMCELL Technologies) for 5 min at room temperature. The reagent was removed, and colonies were washed again with PBS before the addition of 1 mL fresh mTeSR Plus medium. Colonies were gently detached from the culture surface using a sterile cell lifter and reseeded as small fragments at the desired split ratio, typically 1:10.

Nephron Progenitor Cell Differentiation

Kidney organoid differentiation was performed following the protocol by Morizane et al. [18], with modifications previously established in our laboratory to optimize nephron progenitor cell (NPC) differentiation [19]. Briefly, hiPSC colonies were dissociated using Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies) by incubating for 10 min at 37 °C. Cells were resuspended in mTeSR Plus medium supplemented with 10 µM Y‐27632 dihydrochloride (Tocris) and seeded onto cell culture–treated plates pre‐coated with 1% Geltrex (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer's guidelines, at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2. The following day, the medium was replaced with fresh mTeSR Plus and cells were cultured for a further 24 h. hiPSCs were pretreated with mTeSR Plus supplemented with 1% DMSO before initiating the differentiation protocol. On differentiation day 0, Stage 1 medium consisting of Advanced RPMI (Gibco) with 1% GlutaMax (Gibco), 8 µM CHIR99021 (Tocris), and 5 ng/mL recombinant human Noggin (Peprotech) was added. Stage 1 medium was refreshed on day 2. On day 4, cells were switched to Stage 2 medium consisting of Advanced RPMI with 1% GlutaMax and 10 ng/mL Activin A (Miltenyi Biotec) for three days. On day 7, cells were treated with Stage 3 medium consisting of Advanced RPMI with 1% GlutaMax and 10 ng/mL FGF9 (STEMCELL Technologies). By day 9, vesicle structures were visible under brightfield microscopy, indicating successful NPC differentiation. This differentiation strategy was previously validated in our laboratory by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analysis, confirming a high proportion of SIX2+ nephron progenitor cells at day 9, consistent with established criteria for successful kidney organoid differentiation [18, 19]. NPCs were dissociated by removing Stage 3 medium, washing wells with PBS, and incubating with 1 mL Accutase at 37°C for 10–15 min. No FACS‐based isolation or SIX2‐GFP reporter‐based selection was performed. Instead, day 9 cultures generated using this validated differentiation strategy were dissociated and cryopreserved as an NPC‐enriched mixed cell population containing a high proportion of SIX2+ cells. Cells were gently resuspended into a single‐cell suspension, diluted in 9 mL Advanced RPMI, and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in Advanced RPMI, and a small sample was used for cell counting with a hemocytometer. Cells were then centrifuged again at 300 g for 5 min, and the final pellet was resuspended in CryoStor CS10 (STEMCELL Technologies) at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were frozen in a controlled‐rate Mr. Frosty container (1°C/min) at −80°C for at least 2 h and subsequently transferred to liquid nitrogen for long‐term storage.

3D Kidney Organoid Reaggregation

Cryopreserved NPC vials frozen on day 9 of kidney organoid differentiation were thawed at 37°C and transferred to a 15 mL tube containing 5 mL Advanced RPMI (Gibco). A small aliquot was taken for cell counting, after which cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 3 min. The pellet was resuspended in 5 mL Advanced RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% GlutaMax, 10 ng/mL FGF9 (STEMCELL Technologies), 10 µM Y‐27632 (Tocris), and 1 µM CHIR99021 (Tocris). For reaggregation, 60,000 cells were seeded per well into U‐bottom, anti‐adherence–coated 96‐well plates (Greiner). Plates were centrifuged at 150 × g for 3 min and incubated for 1 h to allow cell aggregation. The medium was then replaced with 150 µL Advanced RPMI supplemented with 1% GlutaMax, 10 ng/mL FGF9, and 10 µM Y‐27632. After 15 h, the medium was exchanged for 150 µL Advanced RPMI supplemented with 1% GlutaMax and 10 ng/mL FGF9. Aggregates were maintained in FGF9‐supplemented medium until day 15 of differentiation, with medium exchanged every 2–3 days. On day 15, FGF9 was withdrawn, and organoids were cultured in Advanced RPMI containing 1% GlutaMax and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) until day 23 of differentiation, with medium exchanged every 2–3 days.

Decellularized Extracellular Matrix Preparation for Organoid Encapsulation

Isolation of Decellularized Extracellular Matrix

Fresh porcine kidneys were obtained from the Central Animal Testing Facilities of Maastricht University (the Netherlands) through the tissue sharing program from ethically approved studies. Immediately following extraction, kidneys were rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Gibco), and the external capsule, ureter, medulla, and pelvis were carefully removed. The remaining cortical tissue was cut into small pieces and snaps frozen at −80 °C. Decellularization was performed according to a previously established protocol [20]. Briefly, cortical tissue fragments were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles in MilliQ water to promote cell lysis, followed by sequential incubation at 4 °C in the following solutions: 0.1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; Sigma–Aldrich, Cat# 75746), 1% (w/v) SDS, 1% (v/v) Triton X‐100 (Merck, Cat# T8787), and 0.1% (v/v) peracetic acid (38%–40%, Sigma–Aldrich, Cat# 1.07222.1000). After extensive washing in MilliQ water overnight at 4°C, the decellularized matrix was lyophilized by freeze drying and stored at −30 °C.

Decellularized Extracellular Matrix Hydrogel Preparation and Organoid Encapsulation

Porcine kidney dECM hydrogel was prepared following established protocol with modifications [20]. Briefly, freeze‐dried dECM was pulverized to a fine powder using a sterile mortar and pestle. To prepare a 2% (w/v) digest, 100 mg of dECM powder was suspended in 5 mL of 0.05 M HCl containing 10 mg/mL pepsin (Sigma). The suspension was stirred at room temperature until a homogenous solution was obtained. To improve digestion efficiency, samples were subjected to mechanical disruption using a ceramic bead homogenizer (6.0 m/s for 40 s, two cycles, MP Biomedicals), with cooling on ice between runs. The digest was then centrifuged at 15,000 × g to remove air bubbles and stirred overnight at room temperature. Prior to use, the solution was neutralized to physiological pH (pH 7.2–7.4) using sterile 1 M NaOH, supplemented with 1× penicillin‐streptomycin (Gibco), and stored at 4 °C until encapsulation. Organoids were encapsulated by overlaying with 15 µL of neutralized dECM solution and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to allow gelation.

Establishment of Stromal Vascular Fraction–Kidney Organoid Co‐Culture Systems in Collagen Scaffolds and U‐CUP Bioreactors

Seeding Stromal Vascular Fraction into Collagen Sponge in Perfusion‐Based U‐CUP Bioreactors

Cryopreserved primary human stromal vascular fraction (SVF; donor‐derived) cells, isolated from human liposuctions or abdominoplasty as previously described were used [17]. Informed consent was obtained under the general consent (Forschungskonsent) of the University Hospital Basel, approved by Swissethics (Art. 29, 32 Human Research Ordinance, HFV). Cells were seeded into 12 mm diameter collagen type I scaffolds (Avitene, Ultra Foam) pre‐mounted within cell chamber of U‐CUP bioreactors (Cellec Biotek AG). Approximately 8 × 105 SVF cells were applied per scaffold in 8 mL alpha MEM medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Gibco), 5 ng/mL FGF2 (R&D system), and 1% P/S. After equilibration of the cell suspension across the scaffold (approx. 5 min), bioreactors were connected to perfusion pumps and cultured under flow (0.47 mL/min) for seven days prior to introduction of organoids. Medium was changed every 3–4 days or when medium was yellow.

Mounting Organoids in Collagen Scaffolds and U‐CUP Bioreactors

For static culture conditions, two collagen I sponges seeded with SVF were sutured together, with kidney organoids (± dECM encapsulation) manually placed between the scaffolds (“sandwich culture”). For perfusion culture, organoids were mounted between two SVF pre‐vascularized collagen sponges within U‐CUP bioreactors, as described previously. Bioreactors were filled with 8 mL of complete medium (Advanced RPMI supplemented with 1% GlutaMax and 1.5 or 10% FBS as indicated) and connected to syringe pumps. Flow rates were adjusted to achieve either low (0.01 mL/min) or high (0.47 mL/min) perfusion regimes. Culture medium was exchanged every 3–4 days over a seven days period.

Experimental Design for Kidney Organoids in Collagen Scaffolds and U‐CUP Bioreactors

To systematically assess the contribution of individual components to kidney organoid vascularization and integration, four experimental conditions were established (Table 1). Organoids were cultured in collagen sponge (CS) scaffolds with or without porcine dECM encapsulation and SVF pre‐vascularization. Each condition was evaluated under three culture modes: static culture, perfusion with low flow rate, similar to interstitial flow rate (IFR, 0.01 mL/min), and perfusion with a high flow rate optimized for vascularization of collagen sponges (VFR, 0.47 mL/min). Control groups consisted of organoids maintained in suspension without CS scaffolds in non‐adherent U‐bottom plates.

Biological and Functional Assessment

Quantitative PCR

For each condition, 10 organoids were collected into empty PRECELLYS tubes, and culture medium was completely removed. Organoids were homogenized in 1 mL TRIzol (Invitrogen) at 500 rpm for 15 s and stored at −80° C until further use. For RNA extraction, samples were thawed on ice and supplemented with 20% (v/v) chloroform (Sigma), then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate phases. The upper aqueous phase containing RNA was transferred to a fresh tube and precipitated with isopropanol (Fisher BioReagents) for 2 h at −20 °C. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4° C, washed twice with ice‐cold 75% (v/v) ethanol, and centrifuged at 7500 × g for 10 min at 4° C. The final pellet was air‐dried for 10 min at room temperature and resuspended in nuclease‐free water (Qiagen). RNA concentration was quantified using a BioDrop µLITE spectrophotometer, and samples were stored at −80° C. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of RNA was converted to cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, a reaction mixture containing 1 µg RNA, 1 µL iScript reverse transcriptase, and 1× iScript reaction mix was prepared in nuclease‐free water to a final volume of 20 µL. Reverse transcription was performed on a PeqStar thermal cycler (PeqLab) under the following conditions: 25 °C for 5 min, 46° C for 20 min, 95 °C for 1 min, and hold at 4 °C. Double‐stranded cDNA was stored at −20 °C until further use. Quantitative PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Table S2) on a BioRad CFX96 in 96‐well plates. Reactions were set up in a total volume of 10 µL containing [2 µL HOT FIREPol Probe Universal qPCR Mix, 0.5 µL 20× TaqMan Gene Expression Assay, 2 µL cDNA template, and 5.5 µL nuclease‐free water to volume]. The cycling conditions were: 50 °C for 2 min (UNG incubation), 95 °C for 10 min (polymerase activation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Target gene expression was normalized to the endogenous control gene; GAPDH, and relative expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescent Staining of Kidney Organoids

All medium was removed from each well of 96‐well plate and washed with PBS and cells/organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) at 4 °C overnight. Samples were washed with PBS and stored for up to four months at 4 °C. Fixed plates were allowed equilibrate to room temperature for 30 min, permeabilized with buffer composed of 0.1% v/v Triton‐X (Sigma) for 15 min at room temperature. Blocking buffer composed by 0.3% v/v Triton‐X, 0.05% Tween 20 (Sigma) and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma), was added to fixed cell culture for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibody solutions were prepared by diluting antibodies/stains for specific proteins at desired concentration (Table S1) in 1.5% BSA and incubated with fixed cell culture overnight at 4 °C. Plates were brough to room temp for 30 min and washed three times with permeabilization buffer for 30 min. Secondary antibody solutions were prepared by diluting fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies including DAPI at desired concentration (Table S1) in 1.5% BSA and incubated with fixed samples overnight at 4 °C. Plates were placed at room temp for 30 min and washed three times with permeabilization buffer for 30 min.

Image Acquisition and Processing

Stained samples were mounted on 10 mm multi‐well imaging slides (Ibidi) and imaged using a Nikon Ti2‐E inverted microscope with an AxR point‐scanning confocal unit. Acquisition was controlled via Nikon NIS software, acquiring in resonant scanning mode in 12‐bit format with 2048x2048 pixel resolution. Fluorescence excitation was performed with 405, 561, and 640 nm wavelength lasers, using their respective detection windows at 429–474, 571–605, and 662–737. Images were collected as Z‐stacks of varying depth depending on the thickness of each sample, with a progressively shorter step interval between singular images with increasing magnification. Acquired Z‐stacks were then processed and analyzed via ImageJ.

Vessel Network Quantification

Organoid vasculature was analyzed from confocal z‐stack images stained for CD31 and DAPI using Fiji image analysis software (ImageJ v2.16.0/1.54p) [21]. CD31 channels were saved as TIFFs, converted to maximum intensity projections, and pre‐processed by conversion to 8‐bit, background subtraction (rolling ball radius: 50 pixels), thresholding (Otsu, Huang, or manual), despeckling, outlier removal (radius 2.0, threshold 50), and hole filling. Organoid boundaries were delineated on the DAPI channel, stored as ROIs, and applied to CD31 images. Thresholded areas were measured across the full image and within the organoid ROI (Figure S1). Skeletons were generated using Skeletonize (2D/3D) and analyzed with Analyze Skeleton (2D/3D) to obtain total vessel length, junctions, and endpoints. Metrics were calculated as: Overall vessel density = (thresholded vessel area/image area) × 100; organoid vessel density = (thresholded vessel area/organoid area) × 100; vessel length density = total vessel length/image area; junctions per area = junctions/image area; endpoints per area = endpoints/image area. Values were normalized using image calibration metadata.

Tacrolimus Exposure for Toxicity Assessment

Tacrolimus (Enzo Biosciences) was dissolved in DMSO at a stock concentration of 50 mM, aliquoted (10 µL), and stored at –20 °C. On the day of treatment, aliquots were thawed on ice and serially diluted in DMSO. The diluted stocks were added to complete medium, resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 0.24%. Because DMSO at measurable levels can influence cell behavior, a vehicle control was prepared by adding 0.24% DMSO to complete medium to match the DMSO concentration in tacrolimus‐treated samples, ensuring effects were attributed to tacrolimus treatment rather than DMSO. For the drug treatment, culture medium was removed and replaced with medium supplemented with tacrolimus.

LDH Release Assay

LDH release was evaluated using the Invitrogen CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (fluorescence‐based) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence was measured at Ex/Em = 560/590 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi‐Mode plate reader. For kidney organoids, relative fluorescence units (RFU) were plotted against Tacrolimus concentration, and a non‐linear regression curve with variable slope was fitted to estimate the CC50 (cytotoxic concentration 50%). For SVF scaffolds, percentage cytotoxicity was calculated as recommended in the manufacturer protocol, by normalizing LDH release in each condition to the spontaneous LDH release control (untreated medium) and maximum LDH release control (medium with lysis buffer). Both spontaneous and maximum LDH controls were measured in triplicate, and average values were used for normalization.

Statistical Analysis

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends, where N represents the number of independent biological replicates and n represents the number of technical replicates. For qPCR data and vessel metrics, one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare the mean of each column with the mean of the control column in each graph. For pairwise comparisons, unpaired one‐tailed t‐tests were applied, as we hypothesized that specific culture conditions would promote vessel growth relative to controls, providing a directional basis for the analysis. A p‐value smaller than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (denoted with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism8 (version 8.2.0) software.

Results

Establishment of a Co‐Culture System for Vascularization

To generate vascularized kidney organoids, we first established a co‐culture workflow integrating nephron progenitor–derived organoids with pre‐vascularized collagen scaffolds under static or perfused conditions (Figure 1a).

NPC‐enriched mixed cell population containing a high proportion of SIX2+ cells were derived from the hiPSC line using a previously established differentiation protocol until day 9 [18, 19]. At this time‐point frozen cell stocks were prepared for consistency across experiments (Figure 1b). Subsequently, collagen sponges were pre‐vascularized with SVF cells in parallel with NPCs organoids. Within seven days of culture under perfusion in U‐CUP bioreactors, SVF cells self‐organized into vessel‐like structures, confirmed by immunofluorescent staining for CD31‐positive endothelial cells (Figure 1c).

On the same day that SVF seeding was performed, NPCs were reaggregated. By day 16 of differentiation, kidney organoids had reached ∼800–1000 µm in size and exhibited early tubule formation (Figure 1d) evidenced by the translucent areas. Organoids were then mounted between two collagen sponges either within U‐CUP bioreactor chambers for perfusion culture (Figure 1e–f) or between sutured sponges for static culture (Figure 1g). Together, these steps established a co‐culture protocol enabling kidney organoid culture under both static and perfused conditions.

Fetal Bovine Serum Enhances Endothelial and Pericyte Marker Expression and Promotes Vascularization in Collagen Sponges and Kidney Organoids

We next examined the effect of fetal bovine serum (FBS) on vascularization of collagen sponges seeded with SVF cells and kidney organoids. SVF cells cultured without FBS formed minimal vasculature, whereas 1.5% FBS was sufficient to sustain vascular networks comparable to those observed with 10% FBS, as shown by CD31 (endothelial cells) and NG2 (pericytes) immunostaining (Figure 2a) 15.75.

Similarly, kidney organoids supplemented with increasing concentrations of FBS displayed progressively more extensive vessel networks (CD31 staining). Organoid size (Figure S2a) and structure were preserved for all conditions, with glomerular and vascular integration becoming most developed for the 10% FBS condition (Figure 2b). qPCR analysis confirmed significantly higher expression of CD31 (endothelial cells) and CD146 (kidney pericytes) in organoids cultured with 10% FBS (Figure 2c–d). Quantitative analysis of vascular metrics further demonstrated that organoid vessel coverage fraction, total vessel length density, junction density, and endpoint density increased with higher FBS supplementation (Figure 2e–h). Although 10% FBS produced the strongest endothelial response in the initial screening, subsequent experiments were performed using 1.5% FBS to balance vascular support with preservation of kidney organoid integrity. This lower serum concentration was selected to provide sufficient support for SVF‐derived vascular growth on the collagen scaffold while reducing the confounding influence of high levels of undefined serum‐derived factors on kidney organoid differentiation.

Impact of SVF Pre‐Vascularization and Perfusion Culture on Kidney Organoid Vascularization

We next assessed the impact of SVF pre‐vascularization and a dynamic culture mode on kidney organoid vascularization. Organoids were cultured in collagen sponge scaffolds with or without SVF pre‐vascularization under static conditions, interstitial tissue flow rate (IFR, 0.01 mL/min), or a high flow rate optimized for vascularization of collagen sponges using U‐CUP bioreactor (VFR, 0.47 mL/min). Vasculature developed in all conditions, but the different culture modes affected organoid morphology. Under static conditions organoids maintained their size and shape with tubular structures intact (Figure S3a). Under IFR conditions, organoids appeared to have integrated within the collagen scaffold (Figure 3a), with dense cell population covering a larger cross‐sectional area of the scaffold surface (Figure S2b). VFR culture conditions resulted in significantly reduced organoid cross‐sectional area (Figure S2b), morphological disruption, and breakdown of glomerular‐like structures and other features indicative of kidney organoid morphology (Figure S4a‐b).

Quantification revealed no significant increase in organoid vessel coverage fraction (Figure 3b), or total vessel length density (Figure 3c) across all conditions. There was a general increase in junction density with increasing complexity of culture setup (Figure 3d). Glomerular‐like structures were preserved in kidney organoids encapsulated in dECM and cultured with 1.5% FBS from day 16 to 23; however, these encapsulated organoids were smaller than suspension controls (Figures 4b and S2c). Vessel network formation was evident, but total vessel length density and organoid vessel coverage fraction were reduced compared to suspension controls (Figure 4c–d). We also assessed organoids encapsulated in dECM within collagen sponges, with or without SVF pre‐vascularization, under static, IFR, or VFR conditions. Vasculature was observed in all cases. Organoid morphology was retained under IFR (Figure 4e), but not under VFR (Figure S5a‐b), where organoids covered less scaffold surface area (Figure S2d). Organoid vessel coverage fraction was not significantly different between groups (Figure 4f). However, vessel length density increased in all conditions compared to suspension controls, with a stepwise increase with increasing complexity of culture setup (Figure 4g). Junction density showed no significant difference across conditions (Figure 4h), whereas endpoint density increased significantly in all modes where organoids were co‐cultured within pre‐vascularized collagen sponges (Figure 4i). Similar to the non‐dECM‐encapsulated condition cultured under static conditions within SVF pre‐vascularized collagen sponges, dECM‐encapsulated organoids cultured with SVF under static conditions showed clear interactions between PODXL‐positive glomerular‐like structures and CD31‐positive vessels (Figure 4j), as further highlighted by time‐lapse imaging (Video S2). LTL‐positive tubular structures were also observed adjacent to PODXL‐positive glomerular‐like structures, supporting the presence of nephron‐associated staining patterns (Figure S3b).

To test a more streamlined co‐culture strategy, we evaluated simultaneous seeding of SVF suspension (SVFsusp) directly into the bioreactor at the time of organoid mounting (Figure S6a). In collagen sponge sandwich cultures, simultaneous SVF seeding resulted in limited vascularization, reduced organoid size (Figure S2e), vessel coverage fraction (Figure S6c), total vessel length density (Figure S6d) compared to suspension controls supplemented with 1.5% FBS (Figure S6b). In contrast, organoids encapsulated in dECM within collagen sponges developed more complex vascular networks, though organoid size was reduced (Figure S2f) and glomerular integrity was compromised (Figure S6e). Quantification revealed higher vessel coverage and vessel length density in dECM‐encapsulated organoids co‐cultured with SVF suspension compared to encapsulated controls in suspension (Figure S6f–g). We also tested embedding SVF within dECM during crosslinking. Cell adhesion and growth were assessed across different seeding densities (Figure S7a). Embedding 6 × 106 SVF cells/mL in 30 µL dECM within a collagen sponge sandwich scaffold and culturing under IFR in a U‐CUP bioreactor for seven days resulted in signs of vessel formation (Figure S7b), although this was less pronounced than when SVF was delivered as a suspension in 8 mL medium perfused through the scaffold under VFR.

Tacrolimus Induces Nephron Toxicity and Compromises Vessel Integrity in Kidney Organoids

To demonstrate the applicability of the co‐culture platform for nephrotoxicity screening, we evaluated the response of vascularized kidney organoids to tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor known to cause glomerular and microvascular injury in vivo. To evaluate drug‐induced toxicity different concentrations of tacrolimus were tested on organoids cultured in 96 well plates for 24 h exposure (Figure 5a).

We first sought to determine the concentration range over which tacrolimus induces measurable structural and functional toxicity in kidney organoids. To capture the full dynamic range of the response, we exposed suspension‐grown organoids to increasing tacrolimus concentrations spanning 0–140 µM. This range was selected to ensure that both the onset of toxicity and the maximal response could be resolved, enabling fitting of an S‐shaped dose–response curve. Morphological assessment revealed a clear dose‐dependent breakdown, characterized by loss of compact organoid architecture and accumulation of cell debris in the well (Figure 5b). Nonlinear regression analysis of this dataset yielded a CC50 of 70.87 µM (Figure 5c). Immunofluorescent staining further confirmed cytotoxicity, showing loss of vessel integrity (CD31) and strong induction of the kidney injury marker KIM‐1 at 80 µM tacrolimus (Figure 5d). KIM‐1 was selected as an early onset injury‐associated epithelial stress marker and is widely used in drug‐induced nephrotoxicity assessment [22, 23]. We next analyzed whether stromal vascular fraction (SVF)–vascularized collagen sponges exhibit a similar susceptibility profile. Because LDH assays in this system allow direct quantification of cytotoxicity against lysed controls, and preliminary tests indicated minimal toxicity even at high concentrations, we focused on a range of 0–100 µM tacrolimus. This range is sufficient to capture cytotoxicity levels relevant to the CC50 identified for kidney organoids while avoiding unnecessary escalation. In line with morphological observations (Figure 5e), LDH release remained low (3%), indicating minimal tacrolimus‐induced cytotoxicity in SVF sponges across this concentration range (Figure 5f). Finally, we evaluated kidney organoids embedded in pre‐vascularized sponges under IFR perfusion, reasoning that the presence of a perfused microvascular compartment might alter drug sensitivity. Results showed that, in these conditions, organoids displayed a marked toxicity at concentrations near the suspension‐grown CC50: treatment with 70 µM tacrolimus led to pronounced KIM‐1 induction, particularly within and around the glomerulus‐like domains, and was accompanied by loss of vascular structural integrity based on CD31 immunostaining (Figure 5g).

Discussion and Future Directions

The absence of a functional vasculature remains a critical barrier to the maturation and translational application of kidney organoids for drug toxicity testing, disease modeling, or transplantation. Here, we present a proof‐of‐concept model that integrates SVF, collagen sponge scaffolds, porcine kidney dECM, and dynamic culture in a U‐CUP perfusion bioreactor to promote vascularization of human iPSC‐derived kidney organoids. We explored how serum supplementation, scaffold pre‐vascularization, matrix encapsulation, and flow dynamics influence nephron–vascular integration and assessed the platform's potential for nephrotoxicity screening. Although this study establishes a foundation for engineering vascularized kidney organoids, additional optimization of vascular maturation and long‐term perfusion stability will further increase the physiological relevance of future models.

Low‐Serum Supplementation Sustains Vascularization Without Compromising Podocyte Identity

A key variable examined was serum supplementation. While kidney organoids are typically cultured in serum‐free conditions, SVF generally require serum for survival and network formation [14, 17] Homan et al. showed that 1.5% FBS permitted nephrogenesis and enhanced vascular network formation in developing kidney organoids compared to serum‐free medium [10] Our results are in line with this observation as we observed an improved vascular network on kidney organoids with 1.5% FBS. This concentration was also sufficient for SVF‐seeded scaffolds to maintain vascular networks, comparable to those in 10% FBS. For 0% FBS condition a poor vasculature‐like structure was observed for both organoids alone and SVF‐seeded scaffolds. In organoids, increasing FBS concentrations improved vascular metrics and upregulated endothelial and pericyte markers, while PODXL+ glomerular‐like cells structures were preserved. These findings suggest that 1.5% FBS provides sufficient support for SVF‐derived vascular organization within collagen scaffolds while limiting potential serum‐related effects on organoid morphology. This allowed the contribution of scaffold‐based perfusion and dECM supplementation to vascularization to be assessed under low‐serum conditions, rather than relying primarily on high‐serum stimulation. While this minimal amount of FBS seems essential, transitioning to xeno‐free supplements, such as human platelet lysate could be investigated in the future to improve standardization and clinical relevance while maintaining vascular support.

Flow Dynamics Dictate a Balance Between Vascular Support, Organoid Integrity, and Glomerular Preservation

Perfusion provided essential mechanical cues (shear stress and transmural pressure) that promoted endothelial growth, but the magnitude of flow critically shaped vascular network architecture and organoid integrity. We compared static culture (no flow), a interstitial flow rate (IFR; approximating interstitial flow [24]), and a high‐flow rate optimized for vascularization of collagen sponges in U‐CUP bioreactor setup (VFR; previously used to sustain vasculature in SVF pre‐vascularized collagen sponges [25]). Although the formation of a vascular‐like network was observed for all conditions, both the SVF vascular networks and kidney organoids developed with distinct morphological characteristics. Under IFR conditions, organoids appeared to integrate within the collagen scaffold while largely preserving tubular and glomerular‐like structures, although this was accompanied by a gradual loss of the original globular organoid shape as the tissue spread across the scaffold surface. By contrast, VFR conditions were associated with marked morphological disruption, including reduced organoid cross‐sectional area and breakdown of glomerular‐like structures. While vessel coverage fraction and total vessel length density were not significantly increased in non‐encapsulated organoids, junction and endpoint densities increased with greater culture complexity, indicative of a finer, more highly branched vascular plexus [26, 27], especially when organoids were cultured within SVF pre‐vascularized collagen sponges under dynamic conditions. In dECM‐encapsulated organoids, vessel length density increased across scaffold‐based culture conditions, with endpoint density significantly elevated in pre‐vascularized collagen sponges. We know that elevated shear amplifies angiogenic expansion, producing a dense, highly branched vascular network with numerous endpoints, features typically associated with immature vasculature [28, 29]. However, in this study we show excessive flow also compromises glomerular preservation highlighting the need to tune flow rates to balance vascular support and maintain kidney organoids nephron‐like structures. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing both the biological and technical aspects of the platform.

SVF Provides Cues for Spatial Proximity Between PODXL+ Glomerular‐Like Structures and CD31+ Endothelial Networks

In addition to the effects of perfusion, our findings suggest that SVF pre‐vascularization contributes to vascular network remodeling and branching in kidney organoid cultures. Most notably, we observed PODXL+ glomerular‐like structures in close association with CD31+ vascular networks in SVF‐containing conditions, with some PODXL+ domains appearing to wrap around CD31+ vessels. This spatial organization was not observed in the absence of SVF, suggesting that SVF‐derived cues may promote closer interactions between endothelial networks and glomerular‐like domains. The glomerular basement membrane (GBM) is an acellular matrix to which podocytes and endothelial cells are attached [30]. It is well known that kidney organoids grown in suspension lack of development of GBM, therefore it is not possible to study diseases affecting processes involving filtration at the slit diaphragm as it does not develop in kidney organoids in vitro under normal culture conditions [31]. Some studies have elucidated to showing development of GBM with evidence of glomerular, tubular, and vascular maturation across time between organoids after renal subcapsular transplantation [9]. In vitro models cultured under perfusion with high fluid shear stress showed glomerular vascularization and maturation in kidney organoids, mirroring early stages of development in vivo [10, 32]. In vivo, glomerular vascularization begins when a single capillary loop enters the S‐shaped body, around which podocytes assemble to form a primitive Bowman's capsule. This is followed by vascular expansion that gives rise to early glomerular tufts [33]. Interestingly, we observed evidence of PODXL+ glomerulus‐like structures wrapping around vascular structures only in conditions containing SVF. This finding leads us to hypothesize that paracrine signaling within the co‐culture system may contribute to development of glomerular‐endothelial cells reorganization [34], indicative of the formation of glomerular tuft–like structures surrounded by podocytes. Although PODXL was used to identify early podocyte‐like domains, PODXL is not exclusively podocyte‐specific and may also be expressed by endothelial cells. Therefore, PODXL staining was interpreted in combination with CD31 co‐staining and glomerular‐like morphology. Future studies should clarify whether endothelial elements arise from SVF, organoid‐derived progenitors, or both, and should include additional podocyte maturation markers, such as NPHS1, NPHS2, or SYNPO, and higher‐resolution imaging to confirm endothelial incorporation within glomerular capillary loop‐like structures. One practical limitation of the current scaffold‐based format is that organoids become embedded within a relatively thick collagen sponge and dECM, which limits direct in situ imaging of vascular remodeling and nephron–vascular interactions over time. In addition, endpoint analysis requires fixation and immunostaining, followed by microscope‐guided localization of organoids within the collagen scaffold, which can be technically challenging due to the thickness and opacity of the scaffold.

DECM Encapsulation Preserves Glomerular Domains While Enabling Vascular Infiltration

Collagen is a major component of the extracellular matrix and is frequently used to support stromal vascular fraction (SVF) and vascular network formation [14]. However, for kidney tissue specifically, the native basement membrane is enriched in collagen type IV, rather than fibrillar collagens such as type I [35]. This biochemical mismatch may contribute to reduced structural integrity of the organoids and their tendency to fragment when cultured on collagen I‐based scaffolds. For this reason, we tested the encapsulation of kidney organoids with porcine kidney‐dECM, which more closely resembles the composition of the nephrogenic niche [20]. As a bioactive scaffold, dECM provides a favorable environment for both vascular and nephron development [36]. During organ development, the extracellular matrix behaves as a natural scaffold and also provides biochemical ques to the integrating vasculature [37]. The molecules of the extracellular matrix, specifically relating to vessel formation, can signal to the surrounding cells to initiate or terminate processes involved in blood vessel formation. In control conditions encapsulation reduced organoid size, but preserved glomerular domains. When combined with SVF and perfusion, dECM promoted vascular network formation, consistent with the role of kidney‐derived matrix in providing biochemical and mechanical cues for vascularization [15].

The stiffness of the developing fetal kidney is low, around 1 kPa, similar to the in ovo chorioallantoic membrane, which has been shown to support the formation of renal vesicles and nephron structures [38]. Previous work has demonstrated that 2% porcine dECM hydrogels are softer than 1 kPa, although stiffness increases when epithelial and fibroblast cells are embedded [20]. This may help explain why we observe improved maintenance of kidney organoid morphology when a collagen sponge is included and when cells infiltrate the matrix under flow. We also tested a streamlined simultaneous seeding approach in which SVF suspension was added at the time of organoid mounting. In dECM‐encapsulated organoids, this strategy developed more complex vascular networks, albeit with compromised glomerular structure. These results suggest that future optimization of matrix stiffness, composition, and cellular integration will be key to harnessing dECM as a reproducible scaffold for kidney organoid maturation.

Tacrolimus Exposure Reveals the Capacity of the Model to Capture Both Nephron and Vascular Injury

Finally, we performed a preliminary toxicity test using tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) commonly used in combination with other immunosuppressive agents to prevent organ rejection in transplant recipients, but known to be associated with nephrotoxicity. Our findings show that tacrolimus induces dose‐dependent cytotoxicity in kidney organoids [39], with a CC50 of ∼70 µM, accompanied by KIM‐1 induction [40], and vascular destabilization, patterns consistent with known clinical manifestations of tacrolimus nephrotoxicity [41]. In contrast, SVF‐derived pre‐vascularized scaffolds without organoids were resistant, indicating that epithelial–endothelial interactions are essential for revealing tacrolimus‐mediated vasculo‐toxic effects. This aligns with prior work demonstrating that CNIs simultaneously target renal tubular cells and the microvasculature, with paracrine signaling amplifying cellular dysfunction [41, 42]. Furthermore, the renal microvasculature is particularly vulnerable due to its dependence on podocyte‐derived VEGF [43, 44].

To better recapitulate the multicellular and hemodynamic microenvironment of the glomerulus, we incorporated stromal vascular fraction (SVF) cells and perfusion flow into our model. Flow provides physiologically relevant shear stress, which is known to modulate endothelial junctional integrity, nitric oxide signaling, and susceptibility to vasoactive agents [45]. Without flow, endothelial cells often fail to develop mature barrier properties or respond appropriately to hemodynamic stressors [46]. Our findings, therefore emphasize that flow is not only supportive of vascular maturation, but also critical for capturing clinically relevant vascular toxicity. The incorporation of flow and pre‐vascularized scaffolds with kidney organoids unmasked glomerular endothelial injury. This observation mirrors the clinical pathology of tacrolimus‐induced glomerular endothelial damage and thrombotic microangiopathy [47, 48]. KIM‐1 expression has also been reported outside classical proximal tubular injury contexts, including glomerular compartments under specific injury conditions, such as diabetic kidney injury model [49]. However, in the present study, KIM‐1 was interpreted conservatively as an injury‐associated epithelial stress readout, rather than as evidence of injury to a specific nephron compartment [22]. This is particularly important because nephron structures in kidney organoids are developmentally immature and may not represent fully compartmentalized mature nephron segments. Therefore, future studies should include broader characterization of nephron segment identity and injury responses using segment‐specific markers, including proximal tubular markers such as LTL and LRP2, podocyte maturation markers, such as NPHS1, NPHS2, or SYNPO, and additional injury biomarkers, such as NGAL, to define the affected compartment more precisely. Expanding toxicity testing to additional nephrotoxic compounds and integrating functional readouts such as perfusion metrics or filtration capabilities will be important next steps in establishing the system as a predictive organoid‐based nephrotoxicity model.

In summary, this proof‐of‐concept study demonstrates how combining pre‐vascularized scaffolds, dynamic perfusion, and kidney‐derived extracellular matrix provides a viable strategy to enhance vascularization and maintain structural integrity in human kidney organoids. The co‐culture platform fosters spatial proximity between PODXL+ glomerular‐like structures and CD31+ endothelial networks and supports detection of compound‐induced injury across nephron and vascular compartments, underscoring its potential as a physiologically relevant kidney organoid model. Although kidney organoid variability remains an inherent limitation of current differentiation protocols, the modular nature of the platform described here provides opportunities for further standardization. The use of cryopreserved nephron progenitor cells, defined scaffold materials, and controlled perfusion conditions address several technical sources of experimental variability and provides a framework for improving reproducibility. Future studies should focus on further enhancing organoid structural maturation and validating the platform across multiple independent iPSC lines, differentiation batches, and biological replicates to establish its reproducibility and broader applicability. Such advances will be essential for establishing reproducible vascularized kidney organoid models suitable for preclinical drug testing. This study focused on kidney organoids, the design principles are relevant to other organoid and tissue‐engineered systems in which vascularization remains a major barrier to maturation [50]. Many organoid models require a balance between maintaining parenchymal identity and introducing vascular, stromal, matrix, and mechanical cues [51]. Our findings highlight the importance of combining a supportive extracellular matrix, a vascular/stromal cell source, and controlled perfusion, while also demonstrating that stronger vascular stimulation does not automatically improve tissue quality. This balance is likely to be relevant for other complex organoid systems, including liver, cardiac, and brain organoids, where vascular integration must be achieved without compromising tissue‐specific differentiation.

Conclusion

This study presents a vascularized kidney organoid model generated through stromal vascular fraction co‐culture, collagen sponge scaffolds, porcine kidney–derived ECM, and controlled perfusion. The combined system supports vascular network formation and maintains nephron domain organization, while promoting close spatial association between PODXL+ glomerular‐like structures and CD31+ endothelial networks compared with standard suspension cultures. dECM encapsulation preserved glomerular‐like structures while permitting vascular infiltration. Flow conditions influenced both vascular architecture and organoid integrity, demonstrating that enhanced vascularization alone does not necessarily indicate improved organoid quality. High‐flow perfusion promoted vascular network complexity, but compromised organoid morphology and glomerular‐like structures. In contrast, low‐flow perfusion, more representative of interstitial fluid flow, better supported nephron architecture while still supporting vascular development.
