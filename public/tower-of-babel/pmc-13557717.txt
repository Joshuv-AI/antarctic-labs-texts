engGNN: a dual-graph neural network for omics-based disease classification and feature selection
Authors: Tiantian Yang, Yuxuan Wang, Zhenwei Zhou, Ching-Ti Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOmics data, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, provide critical insights into disease mechanisms and clinical outcomes. However, their high dimensionality, small sample sizes, and intricate biological networks pose major challenges for reliable prediction and meaningful interpretation. Graph neural networks offer a promising way to integrate prior knowledge by encoding feature relationships as graphs. Yet, existing methods typically rely solely on either an externally curated feature graph or a data-driven generated graph, which limits their ability to capture complementary information. To address this, we propose the external and generated Graph Neural Network (engGNN), a dual-graph framework that jointly leverages both external biological networks and data-driven generated graphs. Specifically, engGNN constructs a biologically informed undirected feature graph from established network databases and complements it with a directed feature graph derived from tree-ensemble models. This dual-graph design produces more comprehensive representations, thereby improving predictive performance and interpretability. Through extensive simulation studies and real-world applications to three independent gene expression datasets, engGNN consistently demonstrates strong classification performance compared with competitive baselines. Beyond classification, engGNN provides feature- and source-level interpretability, enabling biologically meaningful analyses such as pathway enrichment analysis. Taken together, these results highlight engGNN as a robust, flexible, and interpretable framework for disease classification and biomarker discovery in high-dimensional omics contexts.Keywords: graph neural networks, omics data, biological networks, disease classification, feature selection

Introduction

The rapid growth of omics data, such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, provides unique opportunities to uncover the regulatory mechanisms of diseases and holds great promise for predicting clinical outcomes [1, 2]. For example, gene expression profiles and gene networks have been utilized to amplify signals from genomic data, elucidate disease mechanisms, facilitate diagnostic efforts, detect risk variants, and identify biomarkers [3–5]. These data capture complex relationships among biological entities and processes, yet their high dimensionality and typically limited sample sizes present substantial challenges for building robust predictive models. In addition, omics data introduce complexities, such as sparsity and uncertainty within biological networks. Unknown interactions between features, such as gene expression profiles, further complicate classification tasks. Importantly, interpretability remains a critical and unresolved issue in the development of reliable models [6, 7].

While conventional machine learning (ML) techniques excel at classification and prediction tasks, they often struggle to process natural data in raw form [8]. Deep learning (DL), as a representation learning framework, has shown great promise in automatically extracting meaningful patterns from raw data, with notable success across diverse domains ranging from image and speech recognition to natural language processing [8–11]. In biomedical research, DL has been applied to tasks such as predicting drug activity [12], assessing the effects of mutation [13], and diagnosing diseases [14]. Recent studies have further demonstrated the use of conventional ML and DL models for biomedical and omics data analysis [15–18]. However, the “large p, small n” setting, where the number of features (p) far exceeds the number of samples (n) [19], limits the effectiveness of DL models, which generally rely on large-scale training data.

Incorporating functional relationships among features through graph structures offers a natural and principled way to represent dependencies and introduce informative priors [20, 21]. Graph representation learning (GRL) leverages these structures to learn embeddings of node-level attributes and their interactions [22]. Graph neural networks (GNNs) have recently emerged as powerful tools for solving GRL problems [23–25], where feature information is propagated and aggregated along graph edges to improve predictive performance [26]. Depending on the model architectures and training strategies, state-of-the-art GNN methods can be categorized into various types, including graph recurrent neural networks, graph convolutional networks (GCN), graph autoencoders, graph reinforcement learning, and graph adversarial methods [23]. Previous studies have shown that incorporating graph-based priors improves classification performance in biomedical studies [27–29].

However, most existing GNN-based models share a persistent limitation: they rely on a single source of graph information, either an external biological network (e.g. protein–protein interaction networks) or a graph constructed from feature data (e.g. based on similarity or supervised learning). For example, the DMGCN model incorporates multiple gene networks as external priors to reduce bias from a single graph [30], but still depends entirely on external biological networks. Conversely, forgeNet builds a task-specific graph using tree ensembles [31], while MLA-GNN constructs co-expression graphs via weighted correlation network analysis from transcriptomic data [32]. Other models, such as [33], use GCNs to infer regulatory networks directly from expression data. Each of these graph-building strategies provides only a partial view: external graphs from network databases may be incomplete or misannotated, similarity-based graphs may overfit the expression data, and model-generated graphs may encode task-specific noise. Moreover, most existing approaches utilize only one type of graph, either directed or undirected, thereby limiting the scope of structural information that can be learned. Another major challenge in applying GNNs to biomedical data is interpretability. Neural networks are often criticized as “black boxes” that obscure the contribution of individual features, and this issue is further amplified in GNNs due to the complex interdependencies among graph nodes and edges [23, 24, 26, 34, 35]. This lack of transparency limits their practical utility in biomedical research, where interpretability is essential to uncover disease mechanisms and identify clinically relevant biomarkers.

To address these challenges, we propose the external and generated Graph Neural Network (engGNN). The engGNN model integrates external biological networks and data-driven feature graphs, effectively combining prior biological knowledge with interactions learned directly from data. By jointly leveraging an external undirected feature graph and a generated directed feature graph, engGNN learns a feature-centric representation that enhances modeling capacity for high-dimensional omics data. Furthermore, engGNN provides built-in feature- and source-level interpretability, enabling feature prioritization, facilitating downstream biological analysis, and quantifying the relative importance of the two graph sources.

Overview of the engGNN framework

The external and generated Graph Neural Network (engGNN) framework combines prior biological knowledge with data-driven feature relationships in a unified learning architecture. Specifically, engGNN utilizes curated network databases to construct an external undirected feature graph and employs eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [36] to generate a directed feature graph from the data. The framework consists of four main steps: (i) a graph-embedded deep feedforward network (GEDFN) [37] that learns representations from an external undirected feature graph; (ii) a second GEDFN that learns representations from a generated directed feature graph; (iii) concatenation of the representations learned from the two GEDFN branches; and (iv) a DFN [38] that operates on the concatenated representation to produce the final prediction. External and generated graph construction

To capture both complementary and hierarchical relationships among features, engGNN jointly utilizes an undirected external graph and a directed generated graph. The undirected graph encodes curated biological knowledge, while the directed graph captures task-specific dependencies inferred directly from data.

In this study, we construct the external undirected feature graph using publicly available biological network databases to incorporate domain knowledge into the learning framework. The nodes represent molecular features (e.g. genes) and edges represent known functional relationships between them. This design enables engGNN to effectively leverage curated biological knowledge while remaining robust to incomplete or sparsely connected networks. To complement the external graph, we construct a directed feature graph tailored to the same prediction task using XGBoost. Dual-graph representation learning

The core GNN component of engGNN is the GEDFN [37], which embeds a feature graph into the first hidden layer to enforce sparse and informative connections. The model starts with the original input features, represented by the feature matrix , where rows correspond to samples and columns correspond to measured molecular features (e.g. gene expression levels). Unlike a standard DFN, which fully connects the input layer to the first hidden layer, GEDFN restricts connections based on the graph structure, allowing only neighboring features to contribute to representation learning. This design encourages information sharing among biologically related features while reducing noise from unrelated ones. Implementation details

The implementation was built using TensorFlow [41] for the DL components and the XGBoost package [36] for generated graph construction. Neural networks were trained using the Adam optimizer [42], a variant of the stochastic gradient descent algorithm. To improve computational efficiency and reduce memory load, we used mini-batch training with randomly shuffled subsets of samples. Since our primary focus was binary classification, we used two softmax output neurons (C=2) to represent the two classes.

Hyperparameter tuning. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the hyperparameter search space for engGNN. For the XGBoost component, the number of trees (estimators) was set proportional to the feature size () to balance model complexity and computational cost. For neural network components, we tuned hyperparameters including learning rate, batch size, dropout proportion, number of hidden layers (network depth), and number of neurons per layer (layer width). We performed a grid search over a feasible hyperparameter space and selected values that generalized well across datasets. For both the external-graph GEDFN branch and the generated-graph GEDFN branch in engGNN, the width of the first hidden layer was fixed at the feature size p. Network depth was set to three, with the second and third layers containing 64 and 16 neurons, respectively. For engGNN, the downstream DFN component consisted of a single hidden layer with 16 neurons, followed by a softmax output layer for classification. Other training settings were as follows: learning rate = 0.0001, number of epochs = 50, dropout rate = 0.2, and batch size = 16. Early stopping was applied to minimize overfitting by stopping training when the loss failed to improve for several consecutive epochs. The data were split into 80% training and 20% testing sets using stratified sampling, and the experiments were repeated over 20 random training/testing splits with fixed random seeds for reproducibility. Within each run, all competing methods were evaluated on the same training/testing split. To ensure fair comparisons, engGNN and all DL baselines in the respective experiments were configured with the same 32D representation and identical downstream classifier architecture. This alignment reduces potential confounding from differences in embedding dimensionality or downstream classifier capacity. All experiments were conducted on Boston University’s Shared Computing Cluster using four CPUs per job.

Feature selection performance

Since the true set of important features is available in the simulated datasets, we directly evaluated the performance of each feature selection method. A binary label was assigned to each feature: 1 if it was important and 0 otherwise. For , , , and DFN, feature importance scores were computed using a procedure similar to that described in the “Interpretability” subsection. For XGBoost, we used the Gain metric (the average improvement in loss when a feature was used for splitting); for RF, we used the Gini importance measure (mean decrease in impurity). To ensure comparability across models, all importance scores were transformed into pseudo-probabilities using percentile ranks. Evaluation metrics include ROC-AUC and PR-AUC (area under the precision-recall curve) [46, 47], both of which range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better feature selection performance. Reported feature selection results represent the average across 20 random runs.

Real data applications

In this section, we evaluated the proposed engGNN framework on three independent gene expression datasets spanning different disease domains: Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Crohn’s disease (CD), and Parkinson’s disease (PD). We assessed its classification performance, feature- and source-level interpretability, and the contributions of individual model components through ablation studies. We further performed pathway enrichment analysis on the prioritized genes from the representative AD dataset to assess their biological relevance.

Data description and graph construction

Alzheimer’s disease

We applied engGNN to a real-world peripheral blood gene expression dataset for AD [48]. AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline and behavioral impairments. The goal was to classify AD cases versus controls and prioritize genes associated with the disease.

We retrieved gene expression profiles from the National Library of Medicine (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession: GSE140831) [49], which includes 204 AD patients and 530 cognitively normal controls. We standardized gene expression values using the Z-score transformation before model training. To incorporate biological prior knowledge, we obtained a brain tissue-specific functional gene network from HumanBase/GIANT [50]. This network served as the external undirected feature graph (), where nodes represent genes and edges represent known functional interactions. Only tissue-specific interactions included in the downloaded network were retained to ensure biological relevance. We harmonized gene identifiers by mapping ENTREZ IDs to gene symbols with the R/Bioconductor biomaRt package [51]. We excluded genes without gene expression profiles and retained isolated nodes to maintain full feature coverage. The resulting external network contained 22,372 nodes (genes) and 12,116,734 edges and was integrated with the data-driven, tree-generated graph () within engGNN for classification and feature selection.

Crohn’s disease

We further evaluated engGNN using a real-world intestinal mucosa gene expression dataset for CD [52]. CD is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by persistent inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract. The analysis aimed to predict postoperative endoscopic recurrence and identify genes associated with disease recurrence.

We obtained gene expression profiles from the NCBI GEO database (accession: GSE186582), which includes 464 patients with CD, of whom 326 experienced endoscopic recurrence within six months after surgery. After filtering to retain genes with valid gene symbols, 20,061 genes remained for the analysis. Gene expression values were Z-score standardized before model training. To incorporate biological prior knowledge, we retrieved an intestine tissue-specific functional gene network from HumanBase/GIANT [50]. We harmonized gene identifiers as described for the AD dataset and integrated the resulting external graph, consisting of 20,061 nodes and 6,626,967 edges, with the generated graph within engGNN for downstream classification and feature selection.

Parkinson’s disease

We also evaluated engGNN on a real-world whole-blood gene expression dataset for PD [53, 54], a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor dysfunction and heterogeneous non-motor symptoms. The goal was to distinguish PD cases from controls and to identify disease-associated genes.

We obtained gene expression profiles from the NCBI GEO database (accession: GSE99039), comprising 205 PD patients and 255 controls. After preprocessing and gene annotation, 23,520 genes were retained. Gene expression values were Z-score standardized before model training. To incorporate biological prior knowledge, we constructed a substantia nigra tissue-specific functional gene network from HumanBase/GIANT [50], harmonized gene identifiers as described for the AD dataset, and integrated the resulting external graph, consisting of 23,520 nodes and 20,571,582 edges, with the generated graph within engGNN for downstream classification and feature selection.

Pathway enrichment analysis on Alzheimer’s disease

While the preceding analyses support the predictive contributions of the model components, biological interpretation of the prioritized genes remains essential. To evaluate the biological relevance of genes identified by engGNN, we performed KEGG pathway over-representation analysis using the AD dataset as a representative case study. After model training, the importance scores for each gene were calculated using Equation (1), and genes were ranked accordingly. The top 1000 ranked genes were selected for enrichment analysis using the R/Bioconductor clusterProfiler package [57]. The list of the top 1000 genes prioritized by engGNN is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Table 7 shows the top 10 significantly enriched KEGG pathways. The calcium signaling pathway was the most enriched. This finding aligns with numerous studies showing that disruption of calcium homeostasis plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of AD, as altered neural calcium signaling may induce synaptic deficits and promote the accumulation of Amyloid beta plaques and neurofibrillary tangles [58–61]. Other top-ranked pathways, including MAPK signaling and cAMP signaling, are also well documented in the AD literature [62–65]. Supplementary Figs S7–S9 provide detailed visualizations of these three pathways, highlighting in red the genes among the top 1000 prioritized by engGNN. In addition, several less-studied pathways, such as “signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells” and “endocrine resistance”, were also significantly enriched. While these pathways are not directly linked to AD in the existing literature, their emergence underscores engGNN’s capacity to uncover potentially novel or underexplored biological mechanisms that may warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed engGNN, a dual-graph neural network framework that integrates directed and undirected feature graphs for disease classification and feature selection in high-dimensional omics data. By combining external biological knowledge with data-driven graph construction, engGNN provides an interpretable and biologically informed approach to biomedical prediction tasks. Across extensive simulation studies and three real-world gene expression datasets spanning AD, CD, and PD, engGNN demonstrated strong and competitive performance against representative baselines. Its consistent performance across multiple diseases and diverse transcriptomic datasets supports its potential for biomedical applications, where reproducibility and interpretability are as critical as predictive accuracy.

The main contributions of engGNN are three-fold. First, it jointly leverages curated external biological knowledge and data-driven feature relationships through the integration of an external undirected graph and a tree-generated directed feature graph within a feature-centric modeling framework, producing informative representations that enhance classification performance. Second, it provides interpretable feature- and source-level importance measures that enable gene prioritization, downstream biological discovery, and characterization of the contributions from different graph sources. Third, its modular design offers flexibility for incorporating additional graph sources and extending the framework to diverse omics and biomedical prediction tasks.

While this study primarily focuses on binary disease classification using single-omics data, the proposed framework lays a foundation for several natural extensions: (i) extending engGNN to multi-class disease prediction would broaden its utility, particularly for diseases with multiple subtypes; and (ii) incorporating multi-omics data, such as methylomics, proteomics, or metabolomics, could further enhance biological insight by integrating complementary layers of molecular information.

In summary, engGNN effectively balances predictive performance with biological interpretability, demonstrating strong potential for disease classification and biomarker discovery while providing a flexible foundation for future advancements in graph-based biomedical modeling.
