Machine learning methods to study disordered proteins
Sören von Bülow, Giulio Tesei, Kresten Lindorff-Larsen

Abstract
Recent years have seen tremendous developments in the use of machine learning models to link amino acid sequence, structure and function of folded proteins. These methods are, however, rarely applicable to the wide range of proteins and sequences that comprise intrinsically disordered regions. We here review developments in the study of disordered proteins that exploit or are used to train machine learning models. These include methods for generating conformational ensembles and designing new sequences, and for linking sequences to biophysical properties and biological functions. We highlight how these developments are built on a tight integration between experiment, theory and simulations, and account for evolutionary constraints, which operate on sequences of disordered regions differently than on those of folded domains.

Introduction

Intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (collectively, IDRs) are found across all domains of life, where they are involved in a wide range of cellular processes such as signaling, transcription, and sub-cellular organization
(
Holehouse and Kragelund, 2024
)
.
Many excellent reviews have discussed IDRs, their conformational characterization
(
Holehouse and Kragelund, 2024
)
, their role in physiology and disease
(
Uversky et al., 2008
;
Babu, 2016
)
, their interacting domains (short linear motifs, SLiMs)
(
Davey et al., 2023
)
, and their phase behavior
(
Shin and Brangwynne, 2017
;
Mittag and Pappu, 2022
;
Saar et al., 2023
)
. While referring the interested reader to these detailed reviews, we here focus on the use of machine learning (ML) methods to study IDRs.

IDRs defy conventional sequence–structure–function relationships and instead usually exist as an ensemble of inter-converting configurations of similar free energy
(
Lindorff-Larsen and Kragelund, 2021
;
Holehouse and Kragelund, 2024
)
. The structural diversity of the conformational ensembles and complex evolutionary signatures make IDRs particularly elusive for experimental and computational characterization. Nevertheless, experimental techniques including small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), various nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments, and different fluorescence-based experiments have been used to determine the local and global structures, interactions, and dynamics of IDRs
(
Thomasen and Lindorff-Larsen, 2022
)
. Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using physics-based models likewise shed light on the structural ensembles. In concert, experiments, theory and simulations have thus been used to connect sequence features to their ensembles, biophysical properties, and biological functions
(
Lindorff-Larsen and Kragelund, 2021
;
Holehouse and Kragelund, 2024
)
.

ML is in the process of revolutionizing how we make sense of experimental and simulation data in science, including in the study of IDRs, and how we generate new data altogether
(
Lindorff-Larsen and Kragelund, 2021
;
Ramanathan et al., 2021
;
Aupič et al., 2024
)
. Machine learning methods, in particular neural networks (NN), are now routinely used to help understand the connections between IDR sequence, ensembles, biophysical properties, and biological functions (Figure
1
). ML has been used to make molecular simulations more accurate and computationally efficient, and even enables generative modeling of novel IDRs via sequence-conditioned networks. ML can be used to predict properties and functions of IDRs from sequence, either via conformational ensembles, or with bioinformatics approaches. In the reverse direction, IDR sequences can be designed with the help of ML to match desired properties or functions. To illustrate these developments, the following sections will highlight and review examples of recent advances in the use of ML to study IDRs, recognizing that due to space constraints we cannot cover all the exciting work in this area.

Molecular simulations of IDRs

Molecular simulations have been instrumental to generate conformational ensembles and study the dynamics of biomolecules, and to link these to biological functions.
MD simulations numerically integrate equations of motion based on force fields that quantify interactions between atomistic or coarse-grained (CG) particles, depending on the desired system size, time scale, and computational costs.
ML has helped in optimizing atomistic force fields
(
Demerdash et al., 2019
)
, the development of CG models
(
Wang et al., 2018
;
Majewski et al., 2023
)
, determining reaction coordinates
(
Mehdi et al., 2024
)
, backmapping
(
Heo and Feig, 2024
)
, and trajectory analysis
(
Sisk and Robustelli, 2024
;
Viegas et al., 2024
)
for folded and disordered proteins.

Because all-atom protein force fields were originally mostly tested and developed for stably folded proteins, it was later found that simulations with these models tended to generate overly compact conformational ensembles of IDRs
(
Robustelli et al., 2018
)
. Various improvements have led to force fields that represent both folded and disordered proteins well
(
Robustelli et al., 2018
)
, and these improvements are still on-going, for example by reweighting (i.e. adjusting the contributions of individual simulation frames for the calculation of ensemble-averaged properties) against experimental or AlphaFold data
(
Köfinger et al., 2019
;
Bottaro et al., 2020
;
Brotzakis et al., 2023
)
. Implicit solvent models such as ABSINTH or CG models such as MARTINI (4-1 mapping) or hydropathy-scale models (HPS) (one-bead-per-residue representation) strike different balances between resolution and scalability
(
Vitalis and Pappu, 2009
;
Choi and Pappu, 2019
;
Dignon et al., 2018
;
Souza et al., 2021
)
and are optimized based on experimental data (top-down), high-resolution simulations (bottom-up), or both. These CG force fields were either designed or in some cases scaled to represent global IDR dimensions well, but discrepancies between experiments and simulations can persist
(
Thomasen et al., 2024
)
.

Data-driven, ML-guided approaches to parameterize force fields optimize an objective function that describes the accuracy of the model given the parameters
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Wang et al., 2013a
;
Di Pierro and Elber, 2013
)
. Different targets and functional forms have been used for the objective function, including deviations to observables from atomistic MD simulations or experimental measurements like radii of gyration or saturation concentrations
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Di Pierro and Elber, 2013
;
Wang et al., 2013b
;
Demerdash et al., 2019
;
Martin et al., 2020
;
Regy et al., 2021
;
Dannenhoffer-Lafage and Best, 2021
;
Latham and Zhang, 2021
;
Tesei et al., 2021
;
Farag et al., 2022
;
Valdes-Garcia et al., 2023
)
, the overlap between inter-particle distance distributions from model and reference data
(
Ruff et al., 2015
)
, or differences in the agreement of free energy calculations from optimized and original parameters for reweighted and full ensembles
(
Ding, 2024
)
. Other models use a combined bottom-up and top-down approach
(
Souza et al., 2021
;
Joseph et al., 2021
)
.

Minimizing the objective function with respect to model parameters is difficult and usually analytically intractable
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Ruff et al., 2015
)
. Therefore, one common strategy is to nudge parameters, perform MD simulations, and re-assess the value of the objective function
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Wang et al., 2013a
;
Tesei et al., 2021
)
. Since the simulations are computationally expensive, parameters can be optimized in micro-cycles with reweighted existing configurations
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Wang et al., 2013a
;
Tesei et al., 2021
)
, and new simulations are only performed after convergence of the micro-cycles.

To avoid over-fitting to the training data, the parameter optimization can be regularized. This may take the form of including a prior in the objective function in a Bayesian formulation
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
;
Wang et al., 2013a
)
or using so-called Bayesian optimization
(
Ruff et al., 2015
)
, which strikes a balance between exploration (sampling high uncertainty regions of parameter space) and exploitation (refine parameter search around expected optimal objective function using prior information). Another approach uses maximum entropy reweighting to optimally fit experimental data followed by potential contrasting to fit force field parameters to a resampled reweighted ensemble
(
Ding, 2024
)
.

As one example, we originally introduced a top-down Bayesian approach to optimize coarse-grained models against experimental observables
(
Norgaard et al., 2008
)
(Figure
2
A,B). As more experimental data became available, we extended and applied this approach
(
Tesei et al., 2021
)
to optimize a set of parameters for the HPS model
(
Dignon et al., 2018
)
. Here, the strength of non-electrostatic interactions between protein particles is encoded in a residue type-specific stickiness parameter
\lambda
that relates to hydrophobicity scales. We targeted experimental SAXS and NMR data to optimize
\lambda
, and used previously published hydrophobicity scales as prior knowledge to reduce overfitting
(
Tesei et al., 2021
;
Tesei and Lindorff-Larsen, 2023
)
. The resulting data-driven CALVADOS models
(
Tesei et al., 2021
;
Tesei and Lindorff-Larsen, 2023
;
Cao et al., 2024
)
thereby capture global conformational properties of IDRs and multi-domain proteins under common solution conditions across a relatively large region of sequence space
(
Tesei et al., 2024
;
Pesce et al., 2024
)
.

The interpretation of CG simulations and direct comparison to experimental data often require atomistic resolution, e.g. for calculating SAXS curves and NMR data using well-established routines
(
Thomasen et al., 2022
)
. Backmapping is used to obtain estimates of high-resolution structures from CG representations
(
Wassenaar et al., 2014
)
. Recently, a ML approach combined side-chain rotamer libraries with a transformer NN architecture to reconstruct side-chain rotamer states in the context of the full IDR
(
Chennakesavalu and Rotskoff, 2024
)
. In cg2all
(
Heo and Feig, 2024
)
, a graph NN reconstructs atomistic detail from alpha-carbon representations and residue-type information and may considerably speed up generation of realistic all-atom structures from coarse-grained representations.

Generative models for IDR ensembles

Above we described uses of ML to support or improve MD simulations for sampling conformational ensembles. In another approach, generative ML models replace the MD simulation-based structure sampling altogether.
For folded proteins, modern ML algorithms leverage the distance information encoded in evolutionary signals (e.g. via multiple-sequence alignment) to predict protein structures and thermodynamic ensembles from sequence
(
Rotskoff, 2024
)
,
or to design novel protein folds
(
Anishchenko et al., 2021
;
Lin et al., 2024
)
. Whereas a small number of structures may be representative for highly populated states in folded proteins and informative for function, highly dynamic IDR ensembles are characterized by a large set of diverse conformations
(
Lindorff-Larsen and Kragelund, 2021
;
Ruff and Pappu, 2021
)
. Moreover, the evolutionary restraints that do operate on the highly variable sequences of IDRs are not easily picked up by procedures like multiple-sequence alignment, as discussed in the following Section. Therefore, the rapid prediction of accurate conformational ensembles of IDRs is a difficult problem
(
Ruff and Pappu, 2021
)
. The last years have seen a flurry of activity to generate models of IDR ensembles from sequence. Fragment-based methods work by stitching together exhaustively sampled peptide fragments
(
Ozenne et al., 2012
;
Pietrek et al., 2020
;
Teixeira et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
)
. In contrast, ML-based methods learn the characteristics of the full IDR chain ensembles from molecular simulations and experimental data.

In one set of ML approaches, the goal is to learn parameters in a generative model that is specific for a particular IDR. Such protein-specific models generate additional structures based on a set of pre-existing structures of the same or a closely related sequence, e.g. from short MD simulations
(
Gupta et al., 2022
;
Taneja and Lasker, 2024
;
Zhu et al., 2024a
)
. These models can be seen as a form of ML-enhanced sampling of conformations. A different protein-specific approach is illustrated with the generative recurrent NN, DynamICE, that aims to generate a structural ensemble that is consistent with experimental NMR data
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. Rather than reweighting conformational ensembles to better match the experimental data, DynamICE modulates the structures of existing ensembles via iterative updates of the residue torsion angles
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. It thus biases the configurations using experimental constraints
even for scenarios with limited overlap between the original ensemble and experimental data.

Ideally, a generative ‘IDR structure prediction’ model would quickly and accurately generate structural ensembles solely from the IDR sequence given prescribed conditions such as temperature and pH value.
Recently, progress has been made in this endeavour, in part thanks to advances in the application of novel generative NN architectures.
The NNs are trained on a set of IDR simulation trajectories and are designed to be transferable to generate structures of previously unseen IDR sequences. One such model, IdpGAN, uses a generative adversarial network (GAN), whereby a generator network is trained to create realistic new IDR structures, while a competing discriminator network is trained to differentiate between training data and generated structures
(
Janson et al., 2023
)
. IdpGAN is fast and relatively accurate for CG ensembles, but with lower transferability for atomistic modelling.
A new generation of networks such as idpSAM
(
Janson and Feig, 2024
)
and IDPFold
(
Zhu et al., 2024b
)
is based on diffusion models. Diffusion models sequentially add noise to input data in a multi-step forward process and learn the applied transformations in the reverse process
(
Ho et al., 2020
)
. These models drastically improved the transferability for atomistic ensembles at the cost of reduced structure generation speed. Naturally, the accuracy of the ML-generated conformational ensemble depends on the input training data. Currently, these generative models are mostly trained on simulation data and thereby inherit the limitations and structural biases of the various molecular models used to generate the training data.

4
Predicting IDR properties and functions from sequence

A different but related task to predicting IDR ensembles is the prediction of IDR properties and functions from sequence.
We here discuss approaches that leverage either biophysical or evolutionary information to study the relationship between amino acid sequences and the properties and functions of IDRs (Figure
1
).

In biophysical ML approaches, training is performed on homogeneous datasets generated for example from CG simulations such as those described above. By capturing the physics governing for example conformational and phase separation properties of IDRs, CG molecular models can inform ML models that provide high-throughput sequence-based predictions
(
Zheng et al., 2020
;
Lotthammer et al., 2024
;
Tesei et al., 2024
;
von Bülow et al., 2024
)
(Figure
2
C,D). Proteome-wide analyses have enabled systematic investigations of sequence-ensemble-function relationships, which highlighted associations between the overall dimensions of IDRs and the function and cellular localization of the full-length proteins
(
Lotthammer et al., 2024
;
Tesei et al., 2024
)
. These large simulation datasets can then be used to train prediction models to calculate conformational properties directly from sequence, thus bypassing the need for simulations and considerably speeding up analyses, which can then scale to millions of sequences
(
Lotthammer et al., 2024
;
Tesei et al., 2024
)
(Figure
2
C).

These approaches can also be extended to studies of self-assembly and phase separation of IDRs. For example, we have trained a NN on hundreds of simulations of two-phase systems with the CALVADOS model, using an active learning framework which guides the generation of training data in the most informative regions of sequence space. This ML model provides quantitative predictions of homotypic phase separation (single IDR components) that can be directly compared to experimental results and have accuracy comparable to computationally intensive simulations
(
von Bülow et al., 2024
)
(Figure
2
D). In this way, we use experimental information (encoded in the data-driven force field) to train a quantitative model that speeds up calculations by orders of magnitude.

Bioinformatics approaches have leveraged evolutionary conservation of IDR sequences or large, and often heterogeneous, experimental datasets. While it is clear that the biophysical, functional and sequence properties of many IDRs are conserved by evolution, it can be difficult to assess this evolutionary conservation directly through multiple-sequence alignments
(
Lindorff-Larsen and Kragelund, 2021
)
. However, homologous IDRs can sometimes be identified as the regions flanked by conserved structured domains, which are instead more readily detected by alignment-based methods
(
Toth-Petroczy et al., 2016
)
or possibly by assessment with alignment free methods
(
Chow et al., 2024
)
.

With a set of homologous IDRs one can search for specific sequence properties that appear to be under selection during evolution and thus likely important for function. Statistical analyses of homologous IDRs have uncovered associations between specific biological functions and conserved sequence features, including linear motifs, net charge, and the patterning of charged and aromatic residues
(
Zarin et al., 2017
;
Zarin et al., 2019
)
. These findings prompted the development of statistical models that link sequence features with protein-level functional annotations to infer which IDRs within a protein are likely responsible for specific functions
(
Zarin et al., 2021
;
Singleton and Eisen, 2024
)
. Further developments of the accuracy and scalability of these methods were exploited to generate proteome-wide predictions for a functional map that connects sequence features of all human IDRs with their function and cellular localization
(
Pritišanac et al., 2024
)
.

The diversity of IDR sequence features highlights the importance of obtaining a comprehensive catalog based exclusively on evolutionary conservation, without biases from previous knowledge on functional annotations
(
Lu et al., 2020
;
Lu et al., 2022
;
Ho et al., 2023
)
. To achieve this goal, unsupervised contrastive learning was used to train NNs on large sets of homologous IDR sequences to learn features and specific residues that are conserved and functionally important
(
Lu et al., 2020
;
Lu et al., 2022
;
Ho et al., 2023
)
.
Additionally, protein language models trained on experimentally curated annotations enabled rapid predictions of binding regions within IDRs
(
Jahn et al., 2024
)
. However, because the molecular context and nonspecific interactions of residues surrounding linear motifs impact IDR binding affinities
(
Bugge et al., 2020
;
Langstein-Skora et al., 2022
)
, future developments may integrate bioinformatics approaches with physical models that account for 3D structure and interaction energies
(
DelRosso et al., 2024
)
.

Curated annotations and proteomics data have also been instrumental to develop ML models that link amino acid sequences with homotypic phase separation
(
Saar et al., 2021
)
and partitioning of IDRs into multi-component condensates
(
Kilgore et al., 2024
;
Saar et al., 2024
)
. These models were validated against in-cell experiments, and are beginning to provide additional insights into the complexity of the ‘molecular grammar’ of phase separation in cells
(
Kilgore et al., 2024
;
Saar et al., 2024
)
. Future work may combine such data with biophysical modelling and ML to shed additional light on the mechanisms that govern the existence of multiple independent condensates in cells.

5
Designing IDRs with ML

IDRs fall outside the scope of traditional structure-based protein design methods because their functions are not governed by single static structures. However, advances in molecular modeling of IDRs and deep-learning methods have recently opened new avenues for designing IDRs. One set of approaches leverages models that capture the physics underlying conformational and phase properties while providing computationally efficient predictions
(
Harmon et al., 2016
;
Zeng et al., 2021
;
Lichtinger et al., 2021
;
Patel and Webb, 2024
;
van Hilten et al., 2023
;
An et al., 2024
;
Pesce et al., 2024
;
Changiarath et al., 2024
;
Krueger et al., 2024
)
. In one such example, we recently combined CALVADOS simulations, alchemical free energy calculations, Monte Carlo sampling in sequence space to design sequences with targeted conformational properties under the constraint of the amino acid compositions of naturally occurring IDRs
(
Pesce et al., 2024
)
. Experimental validation of designs based on the low-complexity domain of hnRNPA1 revealed good agreement between experiments and the design targets, demonstrating the ability to extrapolate beyond the sequences CALVADOS was originally trained on
(
Pesce et al., 2024
)
(Figure
2
E).

Simulation-based design approaches may be computationally costly, but can be made more efficient using ML methods
(
Methorst et al., 2024
)
. As described above, large-scale simulations have been used to train models to predict IDR properties directly from sequence
(
Zheng et al., 2020
;
Lotthammer et al., 2024
;
Tesei et al., 2024
;
von Bülow et al., 2024
)
, and such pre-trained models may be used as surrogates to drive design of IDRs with specified conformational or phase separation properties
(
Pesce et al., 2024
;
Emenecker et al., 2023
;
von Bülow et al., 2024
)
(Figure
2
C,D). Alternatively, surrogate models may be refined iteratively during the design process through active learning frameworks
(
Patel and Webb, 2024
;
An et al., 2024
;
Changiarath et al., 2024
)
. The active learning approach has been particularly effective in designing sequences that form biomolecular condensates, where one challenge is to balance thermodynamic stability with high internal dynamics
(
An et al., 2024
)
. In this process, multi-objective Bayesian optimization was used to identify sequences that satisfy the trade-off between competing objectives, such as the strength of self-interactions and the diffusion coefficient of an IDR in the dense phase
(
Patel and Webb, 2024
;
An et al., 2024
)
.

Bayesian optimization has also been used in the design of multiphase condensates and peptides that partition into preformed condensates
(
Changiarath et al., 2024
)
. Here, the surrogate model was an ensemble of NNs, trained on data generated from CG simulations.
By balancing exploration of untested sequences with exploitation of known successful candidates, the function prioritized sequences for further simulation, improving the accuracy of the NNs while approaching the optimization target
(
Changiarath et al., 2024
)
.

In contrast to physics-based models, deep-learning generative sequence models offer a faster and potentially more versatile approach to IDR design, although they may lack the ability of physics-based models of tuning specific interactions. By focusing exclusively on sequence data, these models bypass the need for predefined structural templates, which are generally unavailable for IDRs. Sequence-based generative models, such as EvoDiff, use evolutionary-scale datasets to predict and generate sequences that fulfill specific design objectives
(
Alamdari et al., 2023
)
. In EvoDiff, a forward process incrementally modifies a protein sequence, while a convolutional NN learns the rules to undo the corruption. Although the method is generally applicable for designing proteins with folded domains, it has also been used to design proteins with certain disordered properties. Specifically, when EvoDiff is trained on a set of human IDRs and MSAs of their orthologues
(
Lu et al., 2022
)
, it may be used to generate novel and diverse sequences that preserve the predicted disorder scores of the original IDRs
(
Alamdari et al., 2023
)
.

A challenging design objective is to create sequences that remain disordered in isolation but adopt specific structures upon binding to a partner protein. GADIS leverages a genetic algorithm and implicit solvent all-atom MD simulations to optimize sequences for targeted alpha-helicity in the bound state while maintaining key interactions at protein-protein interfaces and preserving the native amino acid composition
(
Harmon et al., 2016
)
. We envisage that such approaches, combining sequence- and physics-based ML methods, will be instrumental in deepening our understanding of folding-upon-binding.

6
Perspective

ML is becoming a part of the standard computational toolbox in protein science, and is now routinely used to predict structure from sequence, design new sequences for specific structures, or to train predictive models from experimental data. These approaches have in particular leveraged large systematic databases for protein structures and the ability to extract structural and functional information from sequences and alignments. We are now beginning to see a substantial impact of these approaches in studies of sequence-ensemble-function relationships for IDRs, adopting them to the specifics of IDRs, including the need for conformational ensembles and low position-wise sequence conservation. For example, force fields can now directly be trained on even limited experimental data. We expect that ML will be useful for the reconciliation and interpretation of strongly heterogeneous data, e.g. from simulated ensembles, biophysical experiments, biochemical assays, and clinical sources. We therefore continue to need high-quality data from experiments and simulations, possibly guided by ML in data collection. In that scenario, NNs might suggest follow-up experiments/simulations that best resolve contradictions and uncertainties in the current data.
