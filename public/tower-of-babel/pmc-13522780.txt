Köhlmeier-Degos disease is an interferonopathy characterized by type I and II interferon-driven inflammatory vasculopathy
Authors: Cornelia D. Cudrici, Shubham Goel, Keiko Sakamoto, Seon-Pil Jin, Akiko Sekiguchi, Douglas R. Rosing, Rebecca Huffstutler, Dima A. Hammoud, Lee Shapiro, Daniella M. Schwartz, Sahana Manohar-Sindhu, Monica E. Taylor, Yue Zhang, Paul Schaughency, Edward W. Cowen, Sarfaraz Hasni, Mariana J. Kaplan, Heidi H. Kong, Manfred Boehm, Keisuke Nagao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKöhlmeier-Degos disease (Degos disease [DD]) is a rare vasculopathy with characteristic skin lesions and life-threatening gastrointestinal and cerebrovascular involvement. Although DD is often viewed as a thrombo-obliterative disorder, its immunopathology remains poorly defined. Here, single-cell RNA and T cell receptor sequencing of skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid from DD patients reveal pervasive type I and type II interferon activation, with an interferon-γ-biased program compared with systemic lupus erythematosus. Cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells show interferon-responsive activation and restricted clonotypic diversity, implicating cellular immunity in the DD inflammatory landscape. In a single-patient interventional study, JAK inhibition with ruxolitinib is associated with suppression of interferon-responsive programs, improvement of cutaneous inflammation, and stabilization of neurological disease. These findings support DD as an interferon-driven inflammatory vasculopathy and provide a rationale for further evaluation of interferon-JAK-STAT signaling. This study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05998395).

Introduction

Köhlmeier-Degos disease (Degos disease [DD]) is a rare vasculopathy affecting multiple organs including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and central nervous system (CNS). The disease may remain confined to the skin (benign atrophic papulosis) or progress to a systemic form (malignant atrophic papulosis) that carries a poor prognosis with 60%–70% mortality within 2 to 5 years.1,2,3,4,5 The most common causes of death are gastrointestinal perforation and cerebral infarction or hemorrhage.1,2,3,4

Diagnosis is often prompted by characteristic cutaneous lesions that begin as erythematous papules with central necrosis and evolve into atrophic porcelain-white papules with an erythematous rim.6,7 Similar lesions can be found on serosal surfaces of the gut, and pleural or pericardial involvement has been reported in autopsy studies.7,8,9,10,11 Magnetic resonance imaging frequently reveals leptomeningeal enhancement in patients with neurologic disease.12

The mechanisms driving DD remain poorly defined. Thrombo-obliterative vasculopathy is a consistent histologic feature across affected organs.13,14 Prior work using archived tissues demonstrated upregulation of the type I interferon-inducible protein MX1 (reported as MXA) and perivascular deposition of complement complex C5b-9, suggesting aberrant activation of interferon and complement pathways.14 However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms that drive this pathology have not been systematically explored.

Because of its rarity and uncertain disease pathophysiology, there is no established standard of care. Immunosuppressive therapies such as cyclosporine, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) or interleukin (IL)-6 blockade, intravenous immunoglobulin, and corticosteroids have shown limited or no benefit.3,12,15,16,17 Eculizumab, a C5 complement inhibitor, may prevent gastrointestinal perforation but lacks controlled trial validation.10,15,18

Here, we applied single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and T cell receptor sequencing to skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from patients with DD. The data revealed expansion of cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells expressing interferon-responsive programs and enriched signature of immune activation distinct from those in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). In a single-patient interventional study, JAK inhibition with ruxolitinib was associated with improvement of cutaneous inflammation, stabilization of neurological manifestations, and suppression of interferon-responsive gene programs. Together, these findings support DD as an IFN-γ-biased interferonopathy and provide a foundation for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

Results

Patient demographics

To define the clinical spectrum and molecular correlates of DD, we enrolled 14 patients representing both cutaneous and systemic forms (Table 1). Eleven patients (78.5%) were White, two (14.3%) were Asian, and one (7.1%) was of mixed racial background. Six (42.8%) had cutaneous DD, and eight (57.2%) had systemic disease. Sex distribution was balanced across groups. The median age at presentation was 34.5 years (range 12–50 years) for cutaneous DD and 46 years (range 35–68 years) for systemic DD. In all systemic cases, cutaneous lesions preceded visceral involvement by a median of two years (range 1–27 years). The gastrointestinal tract was the most commonly affected organ (75%), followed by pleural and CNS (37.5%) involvement. CNS manifestations were observed in three patients: case 3 developed sensory and motor deficits, with MRI showing multifocal brain leptomeningeal and superficial cord enhancement; case 11 presented with optic neuritis after exclusion of alternative etiologies; and case 13 exhibited leptomeningeal enhancement and experienced fulminant progression following COVID-19 infection, culminating in gastrointestinal perforation and death several months after evaluation. Two patients (25%) exhibited myositis confirmed by imaging and biopsy. All patients were screened using a comprehensive panel of clinically available autoimmune antibodies. No consistent autoimmune pattern was observed across the cohort, except for case 1, who subsequently developed Sjögren syndrome and met the 2016 ACR-EULAR classification criteria.19 One patient had a family history of DD. Cytotoxic lymphocytes and interferon signatures dominate Degos disease skin lesions

To define the immune and tissue alterations across lesion stages in DD, we examined the clinical and histologic evolution of DD papules. Grossly, lesions progressed from erythematous papules with central necrosis to atrophic papules with porcelain-white centers and telangiectatic rims (Figures 1A and 1B). Histologic sections revealed a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at the dermoepidermal junction accompanied by keratinocyte apoptosis, forming Civatte and colloid bodies—an interface dermatitis pattern characteristic of cytotoxic epithelial injury, also observed in lupus erythematosus and graft-versus-host disease.20,21 Prominent perivascular inflammation extended into the deep dermis (Figures 1C and S1A), with tight perivascular cuffs of lymphocytes, endothelial swelling, and occasional microthromboses, consistent with vascular injury secondary to immune activation. scRNA-seq of inflammatory and scarring lesions from six DD patients and healthy volunteers identified 11 major cell types across 17 clusters (Figures 1D and S1B; Table S1). Lymphocytes were proportionally expanded in both lesion phases (Figure 1E) and showed strong enrichment of type I and type II interferon signaling pathways (Figure 1F; Table S1). Upregulated genes included MX1, ISG15, OAS1, IFNG, and STAT1, together with cytotoxic mediators (GZMB and GZMK) and CD8A (Figure 1G; Table S1). Subclustering revealed expansion of interferon-activated cytotoxic CD8A+ and CD4+ T cells (Figures 1H and S1C; Table S1), and immunohistochemistry confirmed perivascular and junctional CD8+ granzyme B+ cells with nuclear phospho-STAT1, consistent with JAK-STAT activation (Figure 1I).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DD skin lesions are dominated by interferon-activated cytotoxic lymphocytes associated with concurrent epithelial and vascular inflammation, linking interface dermatitis with the vasculopathic features characteristic of systemic disease.

Keratinocyte activation and cell death

Basal keratinocytes were among the most transcriptionally altered cell types in both inflammatory and scarring lesions. Upregulated genes included antigen-presentation molecules (HLA-B and TAP1), proteasome components (PSMB9), and pro-apoptotic or stress-response mediators (RIPK2, TNFSF10, NFKB1, PYCARD, and IL18) (Figures 2D–2F; Table S2).22,23,24,25 In contrast, negative regulators of apoptosis (IER3 and MYC)26,27 were downregulated. Immunohistochemistry confirmed increased nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation, elevated surface major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I, and TUNEL-positive keratinocytes colocalizing with Civatte and colloid bodies (Figure 2G). TUNEL staining indicates DNA fragmentation consistent with apoptosis or other forms of regulated cell death. These data indicate that basal keratinocytes respond to interferon exposure by upregulating antigen-presentation machinery and undergo cell death, implicating epithelial cytotoxicity as a key effector process in DD skin.

Endothelial activation and microvascular remodeling

Vascular endothelial cells from both inflammatory and scarring lesions exhibited strong enrichment for interferon and cytokine signaling pathways (Figures 2H and 2I; Table S2). Upregulated genes included HLA-B and HLA-DRB5, consistent with class I and class II MHC induction, as well as TGFRB2, COL15A1, and THBS1, reflecting pro-fibrotic and pro-thrombotic remodeling (Figures 2J and 2K; Table S2).28,29 Conversely, transcripts critical for vascular stress tolerance and iron handling (HMOX1 and FTL)30,31 were reduced (Figures 2J and 2K). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated increased HLA-DR and TGFβR2 and loss of HMOX1 in endothelial cells, together with elevated von Willebrand factor expression (Figure 2K). These changes suggest that chronic interferon signaling promotes endothelial activation, impaired redox homeostasis, and a microenvironment prone to thrombosis and fibrosis.

Collectively, these findings reveal a tissue-wide interferon response in DD skin that reprograms both epithelial and vascular compartments toward antigen presentation, apoptosis, and pro-coagulant remodeling, linking local cytotoxic inflammation to the systemic vasculopathic phenotype of the disease.

Peripheral blood interferon signature and cytotoxic T cell activation

Differential gene expression and pathway analyses demonstrated strong enrichment of both type I and type II interferon responses in circulating cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells from systemic DD, with upregulation of ISG15, MX1, JAK1, STAT1, and IFNG (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S3). Unexpectedly, cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells from patients with cutaneous DD also exhibited transcriptional activation of interferon and cytokine signaling pathways, albeit at a lower magnitude, including increased expression of IL2RG, JAK1, STAT1, and MHC-related genes (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S3). This finding indicates that inflammatory and interferon-driven programs are not confined to lesional skin, suggesting that cutaneous and systemic DD represents points along a biological spectrum differing in the degree rather than the presence of systemic immune activation.

Cerebrospinal fluid immune activation and interferon enrichment

CSF samples from one cutaneous and two systemic DD patients revealed robust infiltration by lymphocytes, predominantly cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells (Figures 3A and 3B). Transcriptomic profiles showed heightened interferon responses relative to paired PBMCs, with increased expression of ISG15, MX1, JAK1, and STAT1 (Figure 3D; Table S3). Notably, even in a patient with cutaneous DD and no neurological symptoms, low-level activation of interferon-responsive CD8A+ T cells was detected in the CSF, suggesting possible subclinical CNS involvement (Figure 3D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DD is characterized by systemic activation of interferon and cytotoxic programs across skin, blood, and CSF. The presence of interferon-driven signatures in cutaneous DD reveals a continuum of disease activity that may underlie progression from localized to systemic forms, with implications for early therapeutic intervention.

Restricted CD8+ T cell repertoires characterize the immune landscape of Degos disease

Given the widespread interferon activation across tissues, we next examined whether cytotoxic T cells in DD exhibit features of antigen-driven expansion.

Oligoclonal CD8+ T cell expansion across skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid

Single-cell T cell receptor (scTCR) sequencing was performed on PBMCs from seven DD patients, including subsets of samples that were matched to skin (n = 5), CSF (n = 3), or both (n = 1). Analysis revealed clonally focused T cell repertoires across all compartments (Figures 3E, 3F, and S2A; Table S3). Expanded clonotypes were predominantly cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells, comprising nearly all high-frequency clones in blood and skin (95%–100%) and more than 80% in CSF (Figure 3E). Most expanded clones were unique to individual patients, yet many were shared across two or more tissues within the same patient (Figures 3F and S2B). Notably, several of the most abundant clones were restricted to skin (Figure 3F), suggesting that a subset of cytotoxic T cells undergoes local expansion or retention within lesional tissue. These findings indicate that clonally persistent cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells circulate between tissue and peripheral compartments while maintaining tissue-specific subsets, reflecting sustained activation within both cutaneous and systemic environments.

Local expansion and maturation of cytotoxic T cell clones

To determine whether clonal expansion also occurs within individual lesions, we performed scTCR-seq on two independent skin sites from three patients. Dominant T cell clones overlapped between lesions from the same individual, confirming local expansion (Figures S2C and S2D). Clones were distributed across multiple CD8+ T cell states—including STAT1high, GZMK+, and GZMB+ subsets—suggesting that cytotoxic T cells undergo progressive activation and maturation within skin tissue (Figure S2E). Pseudotime analysis supported a differentiation trajectory from interferon-responsive STAT1high cells toward terminal GZMB+ effector states (Figures S2E and 1H).

Absence of detectable viral signatures in clonal T cell populations

Because oligoclonal T cell expansion can reflect viral responses, we applied ViralTrack analysis32 to search for viral transcripts within the single-cell dataset. The analysis was performed using the ViruSite viral genome reference (Release 2023.1, containing over 11,000 viral genomes) supplemented with seven adeno-associated virus genomes from NCBI RefSeq. No consistent virus-derived reads were detected across DD samples (Table S4). Patients did not exhibit clinical features suggestive of chronic viral infection, supporting the interpretation that the cytotoxic T cell response is unlikely to be driven by active viral infection and instead favoring an autoimmune or sterile inflammatory process.

While clonal expansion alone does not prove direct pathogenicity, the convergence of these cytotoxic T cells with sites of keratinocyte apoptosis and vascular inflammation (Figures 1 and 2) strongly implicates them as key effectors in tissue injury.

Together, these results indicate that DD features clonally restricted, interferon-activated CD8+ T cells across skin, blood, and CSF, reflecting a chronic, antigen-experienced immune response within an interferon-dominated inflammatory milieu.

Comparative profiling reveals an interferon-γ-biased immune response in systemic Degos disease

To determine whether the interferon signature in DD resembles other interferonopathies, we compared peripheral blood from DD and SLE patients using spectral flow cytometry and single-cell transcriptomics.

Broad interferon activation shared between systemic Degos disease and SLE

Spectral flow cytometry of PBMCs from eight systemic DD, eight SLE (Table S5), and eight healthy volunteers revealed comparable distributions of major immune subsets, with a trend toward a reduced CD4/CD8 T cell ratio in SLE, consistent with clinical observations of altered T cell balance in lupus33 and a trend toward increased mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of MHC class II on CD14+ monocytes in DD (Figure S3A). Although neither trend reached statistical significance, both patterns align with clinical and transcriptomic observations: the former reflecting known T cell abnormalities in SLE and the latter consistent with enhanced monocyte activation observed by scRNA-seq described below.

scRNA-seq resolved conventional and unconventional T/NK, B cell, and myeloid populations (Figure 4A; Table S5). Distinct interferon-γ bias and broader cellular engagement in systemic Degos disease

While both diseases exhibited interferon pathway activation, the breadth and balance of responses differed. Genes upregulated in both diseases were mostly enriched for canonical type-I-interferon-inducible transcripts (MX1, ISG15, and IFIT3), whereas the DD-unique set contained a greater proportion of bona fide IFN-γ-stimulated genes (STAT1, IRF1, GBP1, GBP2, GBP5, PARP9, ICAM1, and HLA-DRB5)34,35 (Figures 4D and 4E; Table S5). This pattern suggests that DD retains the core type I interferon program shared with SLE but exhibits stronger IFN-γ-driven amplification, particularly within cytotoxic and myeloid subsets.

A “defense response to virus” gene module (GO:0051607), comprising both type-I- and type-II-interferon-responsive genes, was broadly enriched across DD PBMCs, with high module scores in cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells and monocytes, whereas in SLE this enrichment was restricted to narrower subsets within each cluster (Figure 4F).

Per-patient Reactome module scores corroborated these patterns (Figure 4G). In cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells (C1), both DD and SLE patients showed higher type I interferon module scores than healthy volunteers (Cliff’s δ = 0.91, p = 0.0028 for DD vs. HV; δ = 0.72, p = 0.018 for SLE vs. HV). Only DD patients, however, displayed a significant elevation in the type II interferon (IFN-γ) module score relative to healthy volunteers (δ = 1.00, p = 0.00094), whereas the SLE type II score did not differ significantly. DD also showed a trend toward higher type II interferon activity compared with SLE (δ = 0.56, p = 0.066). Although patient-level heterogeneity reduced significance in some pairwise comparisons, the direction and magnitude of these effects were consistent with pathway-level enrichment results from the DEG analysis.

Comparable TCR repertoires between systemic Degos disease and SLE

Subclustering of T cell compartments revealed detailed lineage structures for both diseases (Figure 4H; Table S5). TCR clonotype analysis showed that hyperexpanded clones were predominantly cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells in both DD and SLE (Figure 4I). Shannon diversity of the TCR repertoires did not differ significantly between the two groups (Figure 4J), indicating comparable overall clonal diversity. These results suggest that, although both diseases feature antigen-experienced cytotoxic T cells, the distinction between DD and SLE lies primarily in the nature of the interferon programs rather than in T cell clonality.

Limited humoral activation distinguishes Degos disease from SLE

B cell subclustering revealed that SLE samples were skewed toward activated and class-switched populations, whereas DD B cells remained largely naive or unswitched (Figure 4K; Table S5). Expanded BCR clonotypes were rare in both diseases and Shannon diversity of the BCR repertoire did not differ significantly between the two (Figures 4L–4M). Autoantibody microarray profiling across 128 antigens identified 26 immunoglobulin M (IgM) and 54 IgG autoantibodies that met nominal significance (p < 0.05; Figures S3B and S3C). The majority of IgM and all IgG autoantibodies were elevated in SLE compared with DD. In contrast, DD sera displayed selective enrichment of IgM antibodies targeting histones, whereas no anti-histone IgG was detected. This pattern suggests a restricted, possibly transient innate-like response in DD rather than class-switched adaptive autoimmunity. Consistent with the clinical serology, case 1—who developed Sjögren syndrome—was an outlier within the DD group, showing increased intensities against Ro/SS-A antibodies (Figure S3D). Of note, eccrine gland inflammation (Figure S1) is a common feature between DD and Sjögren syndrome.36 Overall, these findings demonstrate that B cell-mediated autoimmunity, a hallmark of SLE, is not a primary driver of DD pathogenesis.

Collectively, these findings position Degos disease within the interferonopathy spectrum but distinguish it by a pervasive interferon signature with a prominent IFN-γ bias and dominance of cytotoxic over humoral immune activation.

JAK inhibition in a single patient suppresses interferon signatures and improves inflammatory manifestations in Degos disease

Given the pervasive interferon activation and engagement of the JAK-STAT pathway in DD, we evaluated whether pharmacologic JAK inhibition could modulate disease activity in a single-patient interventional study.

Clinical course and treatment design

A 58-year-old patient (case 3) with systemic DD was treated with the JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor ruxolitinib (Figure 5A). The patient had initially presented with cutaneous lesions in 2010, and, beginning in 2018, developed progressive neurologic symptoms characterized by ascending sensory loss, limb weakness, and neurogenic bowel and bladder. MRI demonstrated multiple foci of leptomeningeal enhancement and superficial cord enhancement consistent with DD CNS involvement. CSF analysis demonstrated elevated protein and IgG, and white blood cell counts, consistent with inflammation and blood-brain barrier disruption. Corticosteroids and azathioprine were not beneficial. The disease course was further complicated by nonspecific abdominal pain, and exploratory laparoscopy revealed numerous DD lesions on the serosa of the small bowel, colon, and the peritoneum, for which eculizumab was initiated. Skin, blood, and CSF samples were collected immediately before treatment and again at 12 weeks post-ruxolitinib initiation; PBMCs were additionally analyzed at weeks 1 and 4. Ruxolitinib suppresses interferon-driven transcriptional programs

At the transcriptomic level, scRNA-seq of PBMCs and CSF revealed high baseline expression of interferon-stimulated and cytotoxic effector genes in cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells, consistent with prior cohort analyses (Figures 5B, 5C, and S4A; Table S6). Expression of IFNG in PBMCs was reduced following prior eculizumab therapy, consistent with complement-dependent regulation of interferon-γ production,37 but remained largely unchanged after ruxolitinib initiation. In contrast, JAK inhibition broadly decreased interferon-stimulated transcripts and cytotoxic mediators in circulating cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells and monocytes (Figures 5B and 5C). Within the CSF compartment, interferon-responsive genes were also reduced, although to a lesser degree; for instance, MX1 did not decrease significantly (Figure 5B; Table S6). Module score analysis confirmed that, despite such gene-level variability, pathway-level interferon signatures were significantly downregulated in PBMCs and modestly attenuated in CSF (Figures 5C, S4B, and S4C).

T cell clonal diversity increases following JAK inhibition

Single-cell TCR sequencing from PBMCs demonstrated increased clonal diversity at 12 weeks post-ruxolitinib compared with baseline (Figures 5D and 5E; Table S6). Hyperexpanded cytotoxic CD8A+ T cell clones contracted, whereas smaller clones increased proportionally, suggesting partial release from chronic activation. In contrast, clonal diversity in the CSF remained limited, indicating that CNS-resident clones may be less responsive to short-term therapy.

Suppression of interferon signaling in skin and vascular compartments

Integration of matched skin samples identified 20 cell clusters encompassing epithelial, vascular, and stromal lineages (Figures S4F and S4G; Table S6). Ruxolitinib reduced lymphocyte infiltration and suppressed interferon-inducible and cytotoxic gene programs within lymphocyte subsets (Figures 5F and S4G; Table S6). Keratinocytes exhibited decreased expression of pro-apoptotic and antigen-presentation genes, whereas vascular endothelial cells showed reduced pro-thrombotic transcripts and partial restoration of iron-handling and homeostatic pathways (Figure 5F; Table S6).

Clinical improvement following ruxolitinib

By 12 weeks of ruxolitinib treatment, the patient demonstrated near-complete resolution of erythematous skin lesions (Figure 5G) and stabilization of neurological manifestations. Brain MRI revealed a reduction in the number and intensity of enhancing foci on T1-weighted images (Figure 5H), consistent with stabilization of CNS inflammation. Corticosteroids were tapered and discontinued over the following three months. Ruxolitinib was continued for 13 months but ultimately discontinued due to worsening anemia. The anemia was considered multifactorial, reflecting both pre-existing cytopenia and contributions from chronic illness, recurrent infections, and decubitus ulcers, with ruxolitinib as a potential additional factor. After a 1-week washout, therapy was transitioned to upadacitinib, which resulted in sustained clinical stability at 45 mg/day with no recurrence of inflammatory skin or neurological lesions and partial recovery of anemia.

Collectively, these findings show that JAK inhibition is associated with suppression of interferon-responsive inflammatory programs and molecular and clinical improvement in a patient with systemic DD. Although this proof-of-concept observation is limited to a single patient, it supports the biological relevance of cytokine-receptor signaling and suggests that JAK inhibition can attenuate immune and vascular inflammatory programs in DD.

Discussion

DD was first described over 80 years ago, yet its pathogenesis has remained obscure.38,39 Through single-cell, multi-tissue profiling, our data support DD as an interferon-mediated inflammatory vasculopathy characterized by a pronounced IFN-γ bias and cytotoxic-T-cell-associated epithelial and vascular injury. Across skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid, DD lesions demonstrated pervasive type I and type II interferon activation accompanied by expansion of antigen-experienced CD8+ T cells. These findings bridge histologic observations of interface dermatitis and microvascular inflammation with molecular signatures of interferon-driven cytotoxic immunity.

Our data suggest that the vascular pathology of DD may arise, at least in part, downstream of chronic interferon exposure rather than solely as a primary thrombotic process. Endothelial cells exhibited enhanced MHC expression, reduced heme-oxygenase-1 and ferritin transcripts, and increased von Willebrand factor and thrombospondin-1, suggesting a shift toward a pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant phenotype. In parallel, keratinocytes showed interferon-induced antigen presentation and apoptosis, linking epithelial cytotoxicity to vascular inflammation. Thus, DD involves epithelial and vascular injury through a shared interferon-driven mechanism.

The comparison with SLE revealed both overlap and distinction within the interferonopathy spectrum. Like SLE, DD exhibits broad activation of type I interferon pathways; however, DD additionally displays a prominent IFN-γ-linked component that extends interferon responsiveness to a broader range of immune cell types. In this mild-to-moderate SLE cohort, interferon signatures were largely confined to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and monocytes, whereas DD demonstrated wider engagement across hematopoietic lineages. These findings suggest that DD and SLE share a common interferon-driven framework but diverge in the relative contribution and cellular distribution of interferon subtypes. B cell activation and class-switched autoantibody production, which are central to SLE, were largely absent in DD, further emphasizing that DD represents a cytotoxic, cell-dominant interferonopathy rather than a humoral autoimmune disease.

The response to JAK inhibition supports the biological relevance of interferon signaling in DD. In the treated patient, ruxolitinib suppressed interferon-responsive and cytotoxic transcriptional programs in blood and skin, paralleled by clinical improvement and radiologic stabilization. In addition to interferon signatures, longitudinal monitoring of cytotoxic T cell clonotypes may provide a means to assess disease activity and treatment response. While these findings demonstrate proof of concept that cytokine-signaling blockade can modulate disease activity, larger prospective studies are needed to validate efficacy, assess long-term outcomes, and define optimal dosing and drug selection. Given the rarity of systemic DD, such efforts will likely require multi-institutional collaboration and adaptive trial designs.

This study also highlights broader implications for interferon biology. The coexistence of type I and type II interferon activation in DD illustrates how convergent cytokine pathways can sustain tissue-specific inflammation. Similar mixed interferon signatures are increasingly recognized across diverse autoimmune and autoinflammatory syndromes. Our data position DD at this intersection, where chronic interferon signaling may engage cytotoxic lymphocytes and vascular targets, ultimately leading to tissue ischemia and fibrosis.

Future studies will be needed to delineate the persistence, trafficking, and effector mechanisms of cytotoxic T cells in DD and to determine how their activity relates to chronic interferon exposure and vascular injury over time. Importantly, the present findings identify disease-associated immune and vascular programs but do not establish the primary initiating event in DD; defining whether interferon activation, cytotoxic T cell expansion, complement activation, genetic factors, or environmental triggers act upstream will require dedicated mechanistic follow-up studies.

In summary, by integrating single-cell transcriptomics with clinical intervention, this study supports DD as an IFN-γ-biased interferonopathy characterized by cytotoxic-T-cell-associated epithelial and vascular inflammation. These findings provide a cellular and molecular framework for DD and a rationale for evaluating cytokine-receptor signaling pathways in future clinical studies.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the number of DD and SLE patients analyzed was small, reflecting the extreme rarity of DD and the challenges of acquiring skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid samples from the same individuals. Although we profiled multiple compartments, not all DD samples were matched across skin, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid, limiting direct cross-compartment comparisons and longitudinal inference. In addition, the SLE comparator cohort consisted primarily of patients with mild-to-moderate disease; therefore, the comparison may not capture the full spectrum of interferon activation present in severe SLE.

Second, the study is primarily descriptive and correlative. The single-cell transcriptomic, TCR/BCR repertoire, histologic, and clinical data identify a prominent type I and type II interferon inflammatory program, cytotoxic T cell enrichment, and endothelial and epithelial stress in DD. However, these data do not establish the initiating mechanism of disease. In particular, although clonally expanded cytotoxic CD8A+ T cells are enriched across affected compartments and are spatially associated with keratinocyte apoptosis and vascular inflammation, our data do not prove that these cells initiate tissue injury or directly cause vascular occlusion. Functional assays testing cytotoxic-T-cell-mediated endothelial or keratinocyte injury were not performed and will be required to define effector mechanisms.

Third, the genetic contribution to DD remains unresolved. Genetic analyses, including whole-genome sequencing to assess potential germline or somatic contributions to disease susceptibility, are ongoing and are not yet incorporated into the mechanistic conclusions of this study. Accordingly, the present study does not determine whether DD is initiated or modified by germline variation, somatic mosaicism, environmental triggers, infection, autoimmunity, complement activation, or convergent combinations of these mechanisms.

Fourth, although we observed transcriptional evidence of cell-death and stress-response programs in keratinocytes and vascular cells, the specific regulated cell death pathways responsible for tissue injury remain undefined. TUNEL positivity and expression of apoptosis- or stress-associated genes support cellular injury, but they do not distinguish among apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, or other regulated cell death pathways, nor do they establish whether these programs are causal drivers or downstream consequences of interferon-rich inflammation. Future studies using functional perturbation, spatial profiling, and tissue- or organoid-based models will be needed to determine which cell death pathways are central to DD lesions.

Finally, the interventional findings are based on a single patient treated with ruxolitinib. Although JAK inhibition was associated with suppression of interferon-responsive transcriptional programs, improvement of cutaneous inflammation, and stabilization of neurological disease, these observations represent proof of concept rather than therapeutic validation. The contribution of concurrent or prior therapies, disease fluctuation, tissue-specific drug exposure, and patient-specific factors cannot be excluded. Prospective follow-up studies in additional patients will be required to assess efficacy, safety, durability of response, optimal dosing, patient selection, and whether JAK inhibition or related cytokine-signaling blockade alters long-term outcomes in DD.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a multi-tissue cellular and molecular framework for DD and supports interferon-JAK-STAT signaling as a biologically relevant pathway for future mechanistic and clinical investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patients

This study used human participants and human biospecimens (Homo sapiens), including skin biopsies, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). No animals, plants, microbe strains, established or immortalized cell lines, or primary cell cultures were used in this study. The Degos disease (DD) cohort included 14 participants, allocated by clinical disease subtype into cutaneous DD (n = 6) and systemic DD (n = 8). Comparative PBMC analyses included systemic DD participants (n = 8), SLE participants (n = 8; all female), and healthy volunteers (n = 8). Skin single-cell RNA-seq analyses included DD skin samples from 6 participants (11 samples) and healthy volunteer skin samples (n = 8), and CSF analyses included samples from 3 DD participants. The single-patient interventional study included one participant with systemic DD treated with ruxolitinib. Human participants and samples were allocated to experimental groups according to diagnosis, disease subtype, biospecimen type, and tissue availability; no randomization was performed. Participant age/developmental stage, sex assigned at birth, race, and ethnicity are reported in Table 1; Table S5. Gender identity and genetic ancestry were not analyzed, and no genotype-based inclusion or allocation criteria were used. All participants provided written informed consent under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the National Institutes of Health. DD participants were recruited under NHLBI protocols NCT03538639 and NCT02543996 and NIAMS protocol NCT02471352, which enabled tissue acquisition and de-identified research use of biospecimens and clinical data. The diagnosis of DD was confirmed by skin biopsy performed either at the NIH Clinical Center or at the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine, Weill Cornell Medicine, with review by Dr. Cynthia Magro. SLE participants were recruited under NIAMS protocol NCT04690816 and fulfilled the 1997 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for SLE; lupus disease activity was quantified using the SELENA-SLEDAI score.42 The single-patient ruxolitinib interventional study, “JAK/STAT inhibition in CNS Köhlmeier–Degos Disease” (NCT05998395), was approved under the NIH Intramural Research Program. The patient was selected based on progressive cutaneous and CNS involvement refractory to prednisone and eculizumab. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and NIH human subjects research policies. The molecular analyses reported here focus on the 12-week time point; longer-term clinical follow-up is described descriptively, while full trial results including adverse events will be reported elsewhere. Because of the rarity of DD, the limited cohort size, and the comparator cohort composition, the study was not powered to determine the influence or association of sex or gender on clinical or molecular outcomes. No established or immortalized cell lines were used in this study.

The patients were evaluated by an interdisciplinary team of NIH experts, including dermatology, cardiology, rheumatology, neurology, neuroradiology and surgery tailored to their specific involvement due to Degos disease. Patient care and research were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sampling human materials

Sample collection and patient care were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards at NIAMS and NHLBI. All individuals provided written informed consent for sample acquisition and all subsequent analyses including gene expression studies. All skin samples were obtained after local anesthesia with lidocaine. Lesional skin was obtained from DD patients by two overlapping 4-mm punch biopsies or by excisional biopsies with comparable tissue volume. Skin biopsies from healthy volunteers were similarly obtained from the flank area. PBMCs were obtained from fresh blood samples from both patients and healthy controls. Autoantibody profiling included plasma samples from healthy volunteers (HV; n = 7), DD participants (n = 8), and SLE participants (n = 6). CSF was obtained from three DD patients through standard clinical procedures.

Method details

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed at Histoserv, Inc (Germantown, MD) using archived DD skin biopsy samples obtained from patients not included in the transcriptome study. Briefly, paraffin embedded, 5-μm formalin fixed skin tissue sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated with distilled water. Antigens were retrieved at pH9 and stained with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-human CD4 (clone SP35, Ventana), rabbit anti-human CD8 (clone SP57, Ventana), rabbit anti-human Phosphorylated-STAT1 (Y701) (clone 58D6, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-human GZMB (clone D6E9W, Cell Signaling), mouse anti-human Pan-keratin (clone AE1/AE3, Santa Cruz), rabbit anti-human HLA-ABC (Huabio), mouse anti-human HLA-DR (clone TAL 1B5, Santa Cruz), rabbit anti-human TGFBR2 (LS Bio), rabbit anti-human VWF (clone 111, Thermo fisher), and rabbit anti-human HMOX1 (Thermo fisher). Antigen retrieval was performed at pH 6 for rabbit anti-human NF-kB p65 (Abcam). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were incubated with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies as appropriate. Finally, signals were detected using DAB and counterstained with Hematoxylin. Histoserv, Inc performed TUNEL assay as per their standard protocol. Briefly, slides were deparaffinized in four changes of xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and brought to distilled water. Sections were pretreated with Proteinase K, followed by an EDTA wash and bovine serum albumin blocking. Next, slides were incubated in a terminal transferase buffer, rinsed with water, and finally incubated at 37°C with a TdT, dUTP, and buffer reaction mixture. Following incubation, slides were rinsed with saline sodium citrate buffer and blocked further with bovine serum albumin. Slides were then incubated with anti-digoxigenin and visualized with Vector Red (Vector Laboratories). Finally, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured using a NanoZoomer-XR (Hamamatsu) and were analyzed using NDP.view 2 software (Hamamatsu).

Analytical flow cytometry

Previously frozen Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were analyzed by spectral flow cytometry using a Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences). Prior to staining, cells were incubated with Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend) for 15 min at room temperature to block Fc receptors. All staining steps were performed in 2% FCS in PBS. Zombie Violet (BioLegend) was used to exclude non-viable cells following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against major lymphoid and myeloid lineage markers were used, including T cell markers (CD3, CD4, CD8, CCR4, CCR7, CD45RA, CD45RO, CD25), B-cell markers (CD19, IgD, CD27, HLA-DR, CD38), NK-cell markers (CD56, CD16, NCAM1), and monocyte markers (CD14, CD16, HLA-DR). Full antibody details (clone, fluorophore, vendor) are listed in the key resources table.

Cells were stained for 30 min on ice, washed twice with PBS +2% FCS, and filtered through 70 μm strainers immediately before acquisition. Spectral unmixing and compensation were performed in SpectroFlo, and downstream analyses were conducted in FlowJo v10.

PBMCs and skin sample preparation and processing

PBMCs from HVs and DD patients were isolated according to the density centrifugation method. Briefly, blood was diluted in 1:1 ratio with PBS and layered upon ficoll hypaque (Sigma) followed by centrifugation at 400g for 30 min. Buffy coat layer was retrieved and washed with PBS (Gibco). Red blood cells were removed using ACK lysis buffer (Quality biologicals) followed by filtration with a 30 μm filter (Miltenyi Biotec). Finally, cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GeminiBio). PBMCs were either processed immediately for scRNA-seq or stored at −80°C in the freezing medium until use. 10-15 ml of CSF collected from DD patients were centrifuged at 400g for 10 min. CSF cells were then resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and were immediately processed for scRNA-seq capturing.

All skin biopsy samples were immediately dissociated to be sorted and captured for scRNA-seq. Samples were rinsed briefly in PBS (Gibco) after removing excess adipose tissue. They were then minced with scissors and disposable scalpels in a sterile tissue culture dish before they were enzymatically digested in 500 μl of enzyme mix (Whole skin dissociation kit, Miltenyi Biotec) in gentleMACS C tubes (Miltenyi Biotec). Tubes were incubated in a water bath for 2 h at 37°C with manual agitation every 15 min until dissociation was complete. 1 ml of RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS was added to stop enzymatic activities at the end of the incubation. Samples were then mechanically dissociated with gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) for 30 to 60 s and were spun down to collect pellets. The cell suspensions were suspended in 10 mL of washing buffer (PBS with 5% FBS) and gently passaged using a 10 mL syringe (Covidien). Cells were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers (Miltenyi Biotec) and centrifuged at 400g for 10 min. When needed, samples underwent ACK treatment to remove red blood cells followed by washing and filtration through 40 μm cell strainers. Finally, the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of complete RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS before sorting.

Live cells sorting

Live cells from skin samples were enriched by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Cells were stained with SYTOX-Green (Invitrogen) and were sorted on a BD FACSAria Fusion cell sorter (BD Biosciences). After doublet exclusion using a conventional FSC-SSC gating strategy, SYTOX-Green-negative live cells were collected in Eppendorf tubes in 500 μL of RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS.

Single cell capturing, library preparation and sequencing

Single cell suspension prepared from skin, PBMCs and CSF were counted using an automated cell counter (Denovix) with trypan blue, and cell concentration was adjusted in the range of 700–1200 cells/ul with the viability of >70%. Droplet based single cells capturing and library preparation was done using either 10× Chromium 5′-v2 or 5′-HT kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (10× Genomics). Gene expression libraries were sequenced either on HiSeq 6000 sequencer or NovaSeqX sequencer (Illumina).

Single cell V(D)J sequencing

Single cell TCR and BCR sequencing was performed from skin, PBMCs and CSF from DD patients. Full length TCR and BCR V(D)J segments were enriched from amplified cDNA from 5′ libraries using Chromium Single Cell V(D)J enrichment Kit as per manufacturer’s protocol (10× Genomics). TCR and BCR sequencing libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 or NovaSeqX sequencer (Illumina).

Autoantibody profiling

Plasma autoantibody profiling was performed using an autoantigen microarray panel containing 128 autoantigens and internal controls at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center’s Microarray & Immune Phenotyping Core Facility. Briefly, plasma samples were processed according to the core facility/vendor standard protocol and incubated on autoantigen microarrays. Bound autoantibodies were detected using fluorescently labeled anti-human IgG and anti-human IgM secondary antibodies, and arrays were scanned using a GenePix microarray scanner. Fluorescence intensities were extracted from scanned array images, and averaged net fluorescence intensity values for each antigen were normalized to isotype-specific internal controls. Normalized IgG and IgM signal intensities were used for downstream analysis. When multiple plasma samples were available from the same DD participant, the latest available sample was selected for cross-sectional analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and reporting

Statistical analyses were performed as described below for each data type. Unless otherwise indicated, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For all figures, asterisks denote statistical significance as follows: ∗p < 0.05 and ∗∗p < 0.01. When multiple-testing correction was applied, asterisks correspond to adjusted p values. Exact p values, statistical tests, and multiple-testing correction methods are provided in the relevant supplemental tables where applicable. For figures presenting pooled or summarized data, the measure of central tendency and dispersion is defined in the corresponding figure legend.

Single cell RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (versions 6.0.0, 6.0.2, or 7.1.0; 10× Genomics) to generate FASTQ files, count matrices, and align reads to the GRCh38-2020-A human reference genome. Downstream analyses were performed in R using Seurat (v4.3.0) with default parameters unless otherwise indicated. For each sample, cells with >5–10% mitochondrial gene content, <200–800 or >2,000–5,000 detected genes (depending on tissue and sequencing depth) were excluded to remove low-quality cells and potential doublets. Data normalization and scaling were performed on each individual dataset before integration. Skin samples were integrated using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), whereas PBMC and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were integrated using reciprocal PCA (RPCA) as implemented in Seurat. Integration anchors were selected to minimize technical batch effects while preserving inter-donor biological variation.

For unsupervised clustering, dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). The number of principal components (PCs) and clustering resolutions were optimized empirically for each dataset. Skin datasets were analyzed using 20 PCs at 0.3 resolution, PBMCs using 20 PCs at 0.4 resolution, and lymphocyte subclustering using 18 PCs at 0.6 resolution. Doublet clusters and contaminating populations were identified by marker expression and removed before downstream analyses.

Cluster annotation was guided by canonical marker genes and differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindAllMarkers or FindMarkers functions in Seurat, applying the non-parametric two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. p values were adjusted for multiple testing using either Bonferroni correction or the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, as indicated in the relevant supplemental tables. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed at adjusted p < 0.05 and log2 fold-change >0.25. To control for potential confounding by sequencing depth or donor heterogeneity, all comparisons were performed within batch-corrected integrated datasets on the same normalized expression scale, and per-sample median expression was used to verify reproducibility across donors.

For pathway and module-score analyses, normalized gene expression values were used to compute per-cell enrichment of curated gene sets (e.g., GO: 0051607 “defense response to virus”) via the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. Statistical significance between groups or timepoints was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction where applicable, and effect sizes were quantified by Cliff’s δ for non-parametric comparisons.

Visualization of gene expression was performed using FeaturePlot, VlnPlot, and DotPlot functions with log-normalized counts. Pathway enrichment of DEGs was evaluated using EnrichR (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) with the Reactome 2022 or 2024 database, filtering for adjusted p < 0.05. Pseudotime and trajectory inference for cytotoxic CD8A+ T cell subsets were conducted by converting Seurat objects to cell_data_set format and analyzing with Monocle 3 (v3.0) using DDRTree dimensionality reduction.

Single cell V(D)J analysis

Single-cell V(D)J libraries were processed using Cell Ranger (versions 6.0.2 and 7.0.1; 10× Genomics) with the human reference genome vdj_GRCh38_alts_ensembl-5.0.0. Filtered contig annotation files (“filtered_contig_annotations.csv”) were used to generate integrated TCR and BCR objects for skin, PBMC, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) datasets.

TCR and BCR repertoire analyses were performed in R using the scRepertoire package (v2.2.1). Clonotype calling and integration were performed on high-confidence paired α/β or heavy/light chains, excluding nonproductive or ambiguous sequences. VDJ metadata were incorporated into the corresponding Seurat objects to enable mapping of clonotypes to transcriptional clusters and visualization on UMAPs using the highlightClones and clonalProportion functions.

Repertoire diversity was quantified using the Shannon diversity index, calculated per sample using scRepertoire. Group comparisons of repertoire diversity metrics, including Shannon diversity, were performed using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Rarefaction and extrapolation analyses were used to assess sampling depth and estimate adjusted diversity.

Viral track analysis

To detect the presence of viral elements, 10× gene expression library sequences generated from DD Skin, PBMCs and CSF were processed through Viral-Track R scripts. Briefly, raw fastq files were generated using 10× Genomics Cell Ranger mkfastq. For each sample, the raw fastq files were processed through Cell Ranger (V7.1.0) first to obtain the estimated cell number. With the fastq files of raw reads and the estimated cell numbers, we used UMI-tools (V1.1.2) to select the R2 reads associated with cells and to annotate their corresponding cell barcode and UMI sequences on the read header. The prepared R2 fastq files were then used as input to Viral_Track_Scanning.R to scan for viral elements with a pre-built reference containing human genome (GRCh38), all viral genomes available at ViruSite viral genome reference database (http://www.virusite.org/; Release 2023.1), which included 11647 viruses, 18730 genome sequences, and 690207 genes. Seven adeno-associated virus genomes retrieved from NCBI RefSeq: Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-1 (NC_002077.1), AAV-2 (NC_001401.2), AAV-4 (NC_001829.1), AAV-5 (NC_006152.1), AAV-7 (NC_006260.1), AAV-8 (NC_006261.1) and were added to the analysis.

Autoantibody profiling analysis

Autoantibody microarray data were analyzed in R using the limma package. IgG and IgM normalized signal-intensity matrices were analyzed separately. Samples were annotated as healthy volunteer, DD, or SLE based on sample metadata. When multiple samples were available from the same DD participant, the latest available sample was selected, while all available healthy volunteer and SLE samples were retained. Linear models were fit for each antigen using lmFit with a no-intercept design matrix containing group terms for healthy volunteer, DD, and SLE samples. Group contrasts were evaluated using contrasts.fit, followed by empirical Bayes moderation using eBayes. Differential autoantibody reactivity was assessed using the indicated group contrasts, including SLE versus DD where applicable. p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. For exploratory heatmap visualization, antigens with nominal p < 0.05 in the indicated contrast were displayed. Heatmaps show row-wise z-scored normalized signal intensities for each antigen.
