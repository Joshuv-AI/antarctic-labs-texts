Early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease using machine learning and blood biomarkers
Authors: Guiliang Yan, Sizhu Wu, Qing Qian, The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by insidious onset and deteriorating cognitive functions, with primary clinical manifestations of progressive cognitive impairment and memory loss. With the accelerating process of population aging, the number of Alzheimer’s disease patients in China is increasing continuously. It has become a major disease and social problem seriously endangering the health of the elderly in China, bringing a heavy burden to patients’ families and society [1]. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is a transitional state between normal aging and Alzheimer’s disease, and is regarded as the early stage of AD [2, 3]. Studies have found that comprehensive interventions such as drug treatment and psychiatric treatment at the MCI stage can effectively prevent the progression and deterioration of the disease [4]. Therefore, accurate diagnosis of patients at the MCI stage can be an important measure to overcome the challenges in the prevention and treatment of Alzheimer’s disease.

Machine learning has been extensively utilized in the domain of Alzheimer’s disease early-stage diagnostics, demonstrating significant potential in enhancing clinical decision-making processes. By enabling systematic interpretation and processing of high-dimensional biomedical datasets, these computational frameworks facilitate disease stratification, prognostic evaluation, and therapeutic decision-making, thereby advancing data-driven precision in neurodegenerative disorder management [5]. However, previous studies on the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease mostly focused on brain neuroimaging or cerebrospinal fluid biomarker data [6, 7]. Their high-cost or invasive characteristics make it difficult for them to be widely applied in the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease [8, 9].

Recent advances in the investigation of peripheral blood biomarkers associated with Alzheimer’s disease have established their potential utility as diagnostic biomarkers for early-stage detection. The measurement cost of blood biomarkers is lower and less invasive, giving them a significant advantage in the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease [10, 11]. While the integration of machine learning with blood-based biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) diagnostics has become an active and promising field of research [12], many studies focus primarily on model performance, often leaving the specific roles of individual biomarkers in the classification process unclear [13]. Furthermore, the predictive potential of combining less-conventional biomarkers, beyond the well-established core AD panel, warrants deeper investigation [14]. An interpretable machine learning framework can help bridge this gap by not only building an accurate predictive model but also elucidating the contributions of each feature, thereby enhancing the clinical relevance and trustworthiness of the findings [15]. Therefore, this study will construct a classification model for the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease based on a panel of blood biomarkers and machine learning algorithms. Crucially, we will employ an explainability framework (SHAP) to interpret the model, aiming to explore the specific impact of these blood biomarkers on the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, and thus contribute to a better understanding of their potential pathophysiological roles.

Methods

Data source

The data used in this study were from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). ADNI is an international multi-center research project launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and supported by private and non-profit institutions. The goal of this project is to provide support for the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, the tracking of disease development, and the evaluation of treatment strategies through the collection of long-term multi-modal imaging data, clinical and cognitive assessments, and biomarker data.

Data for this study were obtained from the ADNI database and comprised 275 blood biomarker measurements from 119 cognitively normal (CN) participants and 672 participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). The demographic characteristics of the samples are presented in Table 1. Participants were categorized as MCI based on the ADNI protocol criteria, which include a subjective memory concern, objective memory loss measured by education-adjusted scores on the Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II, a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0.5, a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 24–30 (inclusive), and general cognitive function and activities of daily living that do not meet the criteria for dementia. For this cross-sectional study, blood samples collected at the baseline visit for each participant were utilized. The biomarker data was obtained from a multiplex Luminex xMAP-based panel. There were no statistically significant differences between the CN and MCI samples in terms of age, sex ratio, or years of education.

Data preprocessing

Before feature selection, it is necessary to preprocess the data preliminarily to eliminate the influence of factors such as missing values and noise in the data. First, missing values were handled by deleting features with a missing value ratio greater than 20%, and filling missing values with the average value of the same type of samples for features with a missing value ratio less than 20%. A total of 149 blood biomarker features were finally retained. Next, deal with the duplicate values in the data. For each participant with multiple sets of biomarker data, the average value of their multiple data was taken as the final data. Finally, in order to eliminate the influence of the scale differences of different blood biomarker data, it is necessary to perform a standardization transformation. Here, the StandardScaler method in the sklearn.preprocessing library was used to standardize the data by feature columns.

Feature selection

Correlation-based feature selection

After completing data preprocessing, it is necessary to select features from 149 blood biomarker features. In this study, Correlation-based Feature Selection (CFS) [16] was chosen, which has significant advantages in multi-dimensional feature selection problems [17]. Its core idea is that an excellent feature subset contains features that are highly correlated with the class, but the correlations between features are low. As shown in formula (1):

the CFS algorithm uses the Merit value as the evaluation index. In the formula, S represents the feature subset, is the correlation between the features in the feature subset and the class, is the correlation between features, and k is the number of features in the feature subset. The goal of the CFS algorithm is to select the feature subset with the highest Merit value. In this study, the forward selection method was adopted. The feature set was traversed and searched by the greedy algorithm. The feature that could maximize the Merit value after being added was selected and added to the feature subset until the set condition was triggered and stopped. In this study, the condition for stopping was to select an intermediate subset of 10 features. This number was chosen as a heuristic trade-off. It aims to retain a sufficiently rich feature space for the subsequent wrapper method while ensuring computational feasibility, as the wrapper method’s exhaustive search complexity grows exponentially with the number of features. This two-step hybrid methodology, which leverages a filter method (CFS) for initial dimensionality reduction followed by a wrapper method for final feature refinement, is a well-established approach to balance computational efficiency with the performance of feature selection [18].

Support vector machine-based wrapper method

The CFS algorithm only provides a general feature selection method that is independent of the algorithm, and it is often difficult to directly select the feature panel with the optimal performance in the model. Therefore, the wrapper method [19] was used for further feature selection. It evaluates the performance of a feature subset by running an actual model on the feature subset. In this study, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) [20] was used as the embedded model of the wrapper method.

The 10 features selected by the CFS algorithm were exhaustively combined, and 1023 feature panels could be obtained. For each feature panel, the SVM algorithm was used to construct a classification model, and the model was evaluated using parameter grid search combined with stratified five-fold cross-validation. Due to the importance of sensitivity and specificity in disease diagnosis problems, the average value of sensitivity and specificity was selected as the evaluation index. That is, the feature panel with the highest average recall rate was finally selected.

Classification model construction

Construction and performance comparison of basic classification models

After completing feature selection, the study constructed classification models using seven algorithms: Adaboost [21], K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [22], Logistic Regression (LR) [23], Naive Bayes (NB) [24], Random Forest (RF) [25], Xgboost [26], and SVM. 70% of the data was used for model training, and 30% was used for model testing. In the training set, for each algorithm, the method of parameter grid search combined with stratified five-fold cross-validation was used to find the optimal parameters. The hyperparameter configurations for the grid search are detailed in Table 2. The selection of search ranges for these parameters was guided by established empirical guidelines and initial pilot experiments on our dataset, aiming to efficiently explore the most promising regions of the hyperparameter space.

Hyperparameter tuning details for classification models

A critical challenge in this dataset is the significant class imbalance between the CN (n = 119) and MCI (n = 672) groups. To mitigate the risk of the models being biased towards the majority class, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was employed. To ensure a methodologically sound application and prevent data leakage, SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training data within each fold of the cross-validation process, never to the validation or final test sets. The implementation utilized the imblearn library in Python, with the number of nearest neighbors () set to 5 for synthetic sample generation. Through this process, the minority class (CN) was oversampled to achieve a 1:1 ratio with the majority class (MCI) in each training fold. This created a balanced dataset for model fitting and hyperparameter tuning, while the original, imbalanced test set was preserved for a final, unbiased performance evaluation.

The parameter combination that yielded the highest average of sensitivity and specificity during cross-validation was selected as the optimal model. The performance of each optimal model was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, accuracy, and F1-score on the independent test set. To assess the stability and reliability of these metrics, 95% confidence intervals were also calculated.

Construction of stacked model

Model Stacking [30] is an ensemble learning method that can be used to improve the performance of a model. It trains a meta-model with the prediction results of multiple basic models as input for the final prediction. Considering the problems of computing power and time, when incorporating a single classification model into the stacked model, the optimal parameter combination selected before was used, and then corresponding parameter adjustments were made according to the performance of the model. In this study, the models with better performance in the model performance comparison were tentatively set as meta-models, and the combinations of basic classification models were adjusted and the parameters were debugged accordingly. The performance of different stacked models constructed was compared, and the classification model with the optimal performance was selected. The data used for model construction was the same as that used for the performance comparison of individual models, and 70% of the data was used for model training and 30% for model testing.

Model interpretation

The complexity of machine learning models makes them challenging for clinical decision-makers to understand, thereby hindering their trust in these models. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a model interpretation method grounded in Shapley values from game theory, which quantifies features’ impacts on predictions by calculating their marginal contributions across all possible feature subsets and aggregating them through weighted averaging. SHAP utilizes summary plots to visualize global feature effects, displaying feature importance rankings (vertical axis) and individual sample SHAP values (horizontal axis). The color gradient (red for high values, blue for low values) maps feature magnitudes, collectively offering an intuitive overview of feature-prediction relationships. Force plots reveal local interpretability by demonstrating how each feature’s SHAP value (represented by red/blue arrows proportional to contribution magnitude) cumulatively shifts the model’s output from a baseline value (typically the average prediction) to the final prediction for individual samples. This visually deciphers feature-specific contributions during classification decisions for single instances.

Stacked model results

When Adaboost was used as the meta-model, the final constructed classification model always suffered from overfitting. That is, it performed extremely well in the training set (the average recall rate of the training set was 1.0), but had poor performance in the test set (the average recall rate of the test set was 0.51). This might be because the high sensitivity of Adaboost to noise and outliers was amplified in the model stacking, ultimately leading to overfitting. When Xgboost was used as the meta-model, the final classification model had a significant increase in sensitivity (0.98), but the specificity always dropped sharply (0.67), which might indicate that the model had poor performance in dealing with class imbalance problems.

In contrast, when the SVM model was used as the meta-model and Xgboost, Adaboost, and random forest were used as the basic model combination, the constructed model had the optimal performance. The excellent generalization ability of SVM enabled it to avoid overfitting. At the same time, by adjusting the class_weight parameter, the SVM model had good robustness to class-imbalanced data, which also successfully stabilized its specificity and prevented the classification performance of disadvantaged classes from further declining. The performance of the final classification model is shown in Table 6, with observed improvements in the three indicators of sensitivity, accuracy, and F1-score. To rigorously assess whether the stacking model offered a statistically significant improvement over the best-performing individual base model (Adaboost), further statistical analyses were conducted. A McNemar’s test comparing the Stacking model and Adaboost on the test set yielded a statistic of 5.9394 and a p-value of 0.0148. This p-value, being less than the conventional alpha level of 0.05, indicates a statistically significant difference in the misclassification rates between the two models.

Performance against gold-standard biomarker

A noteworthy outcome of our automated feature selection process was the exclusion of A40, A42, and their ratio—biomarkers widely considered central to Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis. To rigorously investigate this counterintuitive result, we conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis. Following the exact same methodology used for our primary model, several benchmark models were constructed and evaluated. First, various combinations of the amyloid-related markers (i.e., panels with A40, A42, and the A42/40 ratio) were used to train the seven base algorithms and a final stacking model. Second, multiple fusion models were built by integrating these A-related panels with our 7 selected biomarkers, again following the identical training and stacking procedure. The results were unequivocally clear: none of the models incorporating the amyloid markers—neither the benchmark panels in any combination nor the fusion models—achieved a performance comparable to our original 7-feature stacking model (the best-performing of these comparison models, which combined our 7 features with A42, achieved a sensitivity of 0.89 [0.85, 0.94], a specificity of 0.92 [0.81, 0.99], and an accuracy of 0.89 [0.86, 0.93]).

This key finding leads to a significant interpretation. The superior performance of our original model validates the data-driven feature selection process and strongly suggests that biomarkers related to non-amyloid pathways, such as inflammation (e.g., Eotaxin-3, MPO) and metabolism (e.g., AgRP), hold more potent diagnostic value for MCI in this dataset. The marked performance degradation, especially in the fusion models, implies that within our predictive framework, the A-related markers do not provide synergistic or even additive information. Instead, they appear to introduce conflicting signals or noise that hinders the algorithm’s classification accuracy. This indicates that our 7-feature signature represents a robust and potentially independent diagnostic axis, highlighting the critical importance of exploring non-amyloid-centric approaches for the early diagnosis of AD.

Model interpretation: exploring the clinical significance of biomarkers

By interpreting the model, we can explore the diagnostic significance of a blood biomarker in the constructed classification model for the early stage of Alzheimer’s disease. However, this is only the mathematical and statistical significance of the model, and it is difficult to directly determine the correlation between blood biomarkers and Alzheimer’s disease independently of the classification model. Therefore, this study will combine the relevant research literature on blood biomarkers and Alzheimer’s disease to specifically explore the correlation between blood biomarkers and Alzheimer’s disease, and also use this as a verification of the model.

AgRP played the most important role in model classification. Previous studies have repeatedly proven the correlation between AgRP and Alzheimer’s disease. Lee et al. [36] proved through research that AgRP can delay the process of neurodegenerative evolution. At the same time, Tanaka et al. [28] also found in their research that the serum AgRP concentration of patients was higher than that of healthy controls. These two studies, to a certain extent, proved the scientificity of the conclusion in the model interpretation result that too high or too low AgRP values would promote the model classification result to be MCI.

Eotaxin-3 also had a great impact on model classification. Specifically, the higher the Eotaxin-3 value, the more likely the model was to classify the sample as MCI. This was consistent with the research conclusions of Huber et al. [37]. The research conclusion of Papiri et al. [38] showed that the concentrations of Calcitonin in the cerebrospinal fluid and blood of patients with brain neurological diseases increased, which was basically consistent with the model interpretation result. MPO’s impact on classification was that a low MPO value would promote the model classification result to be MCI, which was proved by the research of Davydova et al. [39]. Ruzha et al. [40] proved in their research that Vitronectin could protect the brain by interacting with integrin receptors, which, to a certain extent, proved the conclusion in the model interpretation result that a low Vitronectin value would promote the model classification result to be MCI.

For PAPPA and NGAL, our model identified them as having the weakest and most indeterminate classificatory influence, as visualized by the diffuse SHAP plots. This finding is particularly interesting when contrasted with existing literature. For instance, studies have reported elevated PAPPA levels in MCI patients [28] and implicated it as a key protease in AD pathology through the IGF system [29]. Similarly, NGAL is recognized as a significant neuroinflammatory protein [41], with its levels in cerebrospinal fluid showing potential to predict MCI-to-AD conversion, although reports on its peripheral levels have been less consistent [42]. The apparent discrepancy between these established biological links and their limited impact in our model may stem from several factors. First, the relationship between these peripheral biomarkers and MCI could be highly non-linear or dependent on complex interactions not fully captured by the model. Second, their clinical utility might be stage-dependent, with peripheral levels not yet consistently altered in the specific MCI cohort used in this study. Finally, it is plausible that the predictive information provided by PAPPA and NGAL was largely redundant, having been effectively captured by the other, more dominant features in our final panel (e.g., AgRP, Eotaxin-3). Therefore, while not primary drivers in our current model, their established biological relevance warrants their continued consideration in AD research. Future work could further elucidate their specific roles.

In summary, the roles of five blood biomarker features within the model have been largely corroborated by existing literature, with their modes of action generally consistent with those presented in the SHAP visualization results, lending credence to the model’s scientific validity. For PAPPA and NGAL, which exhibited weaker classificatory roles in our model, literature also provides evidence supporting their biological links to Alzheimer’s disease pathological processes.

Limitations and future perspectives

While our findings highlight the promising utility of a blood biomarker-based machine learning model for early MCI detection, this study possesses several inherent limitations that also delineate critical avenues for future research and development. A primary limitation stems from the dataset utilized. Although ADNI is a robust multi-center cohort, its participants (predominantly White Caucasians with higher education levels and fewer comorbidities than the general population) may not fully represent the diversity of real-world clinical populations, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting the immediate generalizability of our findings. In addition to population diversity, the scope of the available biomarker data presented its own constraints. Specifically, our benchmark model could only be constructed using the A42 and A40 value, as other key blood-based biomarkers like p-tau181 and NfL had insufficient sample sizes within the utilized ADNI cohort for robust modeling. Consequently, a crucial future direction is the rigorous external validation of our model on diverse, independent datasets that include a more complete panel of modern biomarkers to truly ascertain its real-world robustness and applicability. While the multi-center nature of ADNI provides some internal variability, it cannot substitute for this essential step.

Regarding clinical translation, the current model is envisioned as an auxiliary rather than a standalone diagnostic tool. Its minimally invasive and relatively low-cost nature positions it as a potential early screening or triage instrument within clinical pathways. However, variations across different laboratory testing platforms and biomarker measurement procedures can significantly impact the model’s performance. Therefore, the standardization of blood biomarker assays is crucial for the clinical application of the model. Efforts in this regard are currently underway, such as the Alzheimer’s Association’s CSF and Blood Biomarker Quality Control Program, aimed at monitoring and harmonizing inter-laboratory testing differences globally.

Furthermore, the clinical integration of this model necessitates careful consideration of ethical implications. Given that the model provides a risk assessment rather than a definitive diagnosis, clear communication strategies for conveying results to patients and their families are paramount to avoid undue anxiety or false reassurance. Issues of data privacy and security must be meticulously addressed, ensuring patient confidentiality.

Conclusions

This study successfully constructed and validated a classification model based on blood biomarkers and machine learning for the diagnosis of the early MCI stage of Alzheimer’s disease. Through feature selection and model optimization, the proposed stacking model demonstrated good performance in accurately identifying MCI patients. Furthermore, explainability analysis using SHAP further elucidated the association between the selected blood biomarkers and MCI status, with results largely consistent with existing literature. This work indicates that blood biomarkers, combined with advanced machine learning techniques, offer a promising avenue for developing minimally invasive and effective tools for early AD diagnosis. Despite the positive outcomes of this study, as detailed in the Discussion section, the model’s broad clinical application awaits large-scale external validation in more diverse populations and further translational research; the ethical implications should also be carefully considered.
