Effect of temperature on the wetting performance of coal powder by different surfactant solutions: experimental and molecular dynamics simulation study
Authors: Wei Fu, Jianhong Kang, Wenxu Liang, Pengjiang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current research on coal dust wettability primarily focuses on surfactant types, concentrations, and coal properties, while the role of temperature as a key parameter in practical applications has not been fully explored. This study combines experimental measurements and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to systematically investigate the influence of temperature (291.15–308.15 K) on the wetting behavior of coal dust by three surfactants: zwitterionic BS‑12, nonionic OP‑10, and anionic SDS. Macroscopic experiments indicate that increasing temperature contributes to improved wetting performance: contact angles are reduced by 45.3% (BS‑12), 50.1% (OP‑10), and 46.8% (SDS), while coal dust settling time is shortened by 33.1%, 83.62%, and 63.44%, respectively. Molecular dynamics simulations further reveal that elevated temperature enhances the interactions between surfactants and coal and promotes structural expansion of the adsorption layer. Among them, OP‑10 exhibits relatively pronounced thermal responsiveness, with its adsorption-layer thickness increasing from 5.53 Å to 14.57 Å, consistent with the wetting behavior observed in macroscopic experiments. At the molecular level, OP‑10 primarily forms a compact and stable interfacial adsorption layer through π–π stacking interactions with aromatic structures in coal, whereas SDS and BS‑12 rely mainly on electrostatic interactions. This study provides a molecular-level reference for surfactant selection in varying temperature environments and suggests that maintaining temperatures above 298.15 K (25 °C) in practical dust suppression applications can help enhance wetting effectiveness, offering certain guidance for industrial dust control practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32648-w.

Introduction

As the world’s largest consumer of energy and producer of coal, China’s coal mining and processing industry has always served as a crucial pillar of the national economy1,2. However, the underground coal mining process generates substantial amounts of coal dust, which severely reduces production efficiency and poses significant health risks to underground workers. To mitigate these negative impacts, water mist dust suppression technology is widely implemented in actual production settings to control costs and limit the spread of coal dust3. Nevertheless, due to the hydrophobic nature of coal, pure water is often ineffective in adequately wetting coal dust. The application of surfactants to enhance the wettability of coal dust has emerged as an efficient technical solution for suppressing its spread4. The wettability of coal powder is influenced by various factors, including the type and concentration of surfactants, as well as the inherent characteristics of the coal itself. Additionally, the effectiveness of surfactants is regulated by these factors. Significant progress has been achieved in related research fields5.

Surfactants, through their directional adsorption at the solid-liquid interface, can significantly reduce the surface tension of solutions, alter the hydrophilic-hydrophobic characteristics of coal powder surfaces, and enhance the interaction between coal and water. This process promotes better wetting of coal dust and facilitates its sedimentation6. Chang et al.7 designed a factorial experiment to evaluate the inhibitory effects of different types of surfactants at varying solution concentrations on coal dust with different initial concentrations. The results indicated that all three factors significantly influenced the inhibition efficiency; notably, SDS and CTAB outperformed other surfactants regardless of the initial coal dust concentration. Paria and Khilar8 conducted extensive experiments to investigate the adsorption process of surfactants at hydrophilic solid-liquid interfaces. They reviewed the significant effects of various parameters, such as molecular structure, temperature, and salt concentration, on surfactant adsorption. Furthermore, they highlighted that the kinetic and equilibrium adsorption behaviors of surfactants at the solid-liquid interface depend on both the properties of the surfactants and the nature of the solid surface. Due to the differences in the physicochemical properties of various solid surfaces, the wetting effects of different surfactants also vary. Most cationic surfactants are derivatives of organic amines. When the solution contacts the coal surface, the interface becomes acidic, leading to the precipitation of organic amines and resulting in the loss of surfactant activity in the solution9. Consequently, cationic surfactants are unsuitable for enhancing the wettability of coal powder. In contrast, nonionic, anionic, and zwitterionic surfactants have been extensively utilized in related experimental studies10,11. R.J. Crawford12 employed three different types of surfactants-anionic, cationic, and nonionic-to investigate the hydrophobicity of the surfaces of three Australian coals (Collie, Wongawilli, and Norwich Park), proposing a surfactant adsorption model that aligns with their experimental data.

Although extensive research has been conducted on the effects of surfactant types and concentrations on the wettability of coal powder, the role of temperature-a critical parameter in industrial production-has not been thoroughly investigated6. Variations in temperature can significantly alter the physicochemical interactions between surfactants and coal molecules, thereby affecting wettability. Within a certain range, increasing the temperature of the aqueous solution reduces both its surface tension and viscosity, a phenomenon that also occurs in surfactant solutions. Marsalek et al.13 found that the adsorption capacity of the cationic surfactant CTAB on coal increases with rising temperature. Furthermore, Kang et al.14 demonstrated that as temperature increases, the surface energy of coal decreases while promoting the breakage of hydrogen bonds within coal molecules. Existing studies have confirmed that temperature indeed alters the physicochemical properties of surfactant solutions and coal15. While previous studies have established the positive effect of temperature on coal wettability, the fundamental differences in how different types of surfactants respond to temperature variations at the molecular level, along with their distinct microscopic mechanisms, remain unclear. This lack of mechanistic understanding hinders the rational screening and design of high-efficiency surfactants tailored for specific temperature conditions.

Research on surfactants is predominantly conducted through experiments, which can authentically reflect wetting outcomes but are limited to macroscopic phenomena, making it challenging to delve into the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, the vast variety of surfactants means that optimizing them solely through experiments is both time-consuming and resource-intensive. Currently, molecular dynamics (MD) and density functional theory (DFT) calculations are recognized as effective methods for capturing the microscopic physicochemical properties of coal. These methods can be employed to study the diffusion behavior of different molecules during the adsorption process, offering an intuitive explanation of the wetting mechanisms of surfactants on coal dust from a microscopic perspective16. Niu et al.17 investigated the wettability of coal dust by four surfactants (OBS, SDBS, APG, and AEO-9) through MD simulations, finding that the attraction and competitive adsorption of different surfactants during the wetting process are controlled by the differences in positive and negative electrostatic potentials. The greater the difference, the stronger the interaction. Li et al.18 analyzed the wetting properties of two dust suppressants, Octylphenol Ethoxylate (OP-10) and Nonylphenol Ethoxylate (NP-10), on anthracite through a combination of experiments and simulations.The results indicated that a shorter alkyl chain on the benzene ring of the surfactant enhances the mobility and suspension stability of anthracite particles in the wetting solution. Chen et al.19 evaluated the wettability of Sodium Dodecyl Benzene Sulfonate (SDBS) and Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS) through four static experiments, revealing that the results obtained under identical conditions were inconsistent across the methods employed. Furthermore, the experimental findings suggest that these methods are not universally applicable for analyzing influencing factors such as surfactant type, coal type, and particle size. Consequently, Molecular Dynamics (MD) has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating adsorption phenomena and changes in wettability20. By integrating MD computational simulations with studies on the interactions of related molecules, complemented by appropriate experimental approaches, a more accurate analysis of the dust suppression mechanism can be achieved. This is of significant importance for selecting more efficient surfactants to enhance dust suppression effectiveness21.

This study employs a combined approach of experimental analysis and molecular dynamics simulation to investigate the effect of temperature on the wettability of coal powder by surfactant solutions. Three surfactants with different ionic types (BS-12, OP-10, SDS) were selected for this investigation, and both static and dynamic wettability experiments were conducted at four distinct temperatures: 291.15 K (18℃), 298.15 K (25℃), 303.15 K (30℃), and 308.15 K (35℃). Concurrently, a Coal-(surfactant)-H2O system was constructed at the microscopic molecular level for molecular dynamics simulation under temperature gradients. The simulation results were analyzed in terms of electrostatic potential and relative concentration, revealing the interaction mechanisms between surfactants and coal molecules. Finally, the influence mechanism of surfactants on the wettability of coal powder at different temperatures is analyzed from the perspective of water molecule movement by calculating the mean square displacement of water molecules.

Experimental materials

Reagent selection

This study selected three representative surfactants with different ionic types for experimentation: the nonionic surfactant octylphenol ethoxylate (OP-10), the anionic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and the zwitterionic surfactant dodecyl dimethyl betaine (BS-12). The objective was to systematically investigate the effect of temperature on the wetting performance of surfactant solutions on coal powder. All surfactants used in the experiments were analytical grade reagents, procured from Shandong Yousuo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Their specific physicochemical properties are detailed in Table 2.

In accordance with the principles of practicality, economy, and environmental protection, the mass of the three surfactants was uniformly set at 0.1% for the experiments. This concentration was selected based on the following scientific rationale: (1) In practical industrial applications, such as coal dust control and coal water slurry preparation, surfactant concentrations are typically maintained within the range of 0.05% to 0.2%. A concentration of 0.1% provides optimal operability and economic efficiency22; (2) This concentration approaches the critical micelle concentration (CMC) of the surfactant, thereby effectively demonstrating its dispersion performance and adsorption characteristics23; (3) A lower concentration not only ensures effective wetting but also significantly reduces reagent usage, minimizes environmental impact, and is consistent with the principles of green chemistry.

Determination of test temperature

The determination of the test temperature is based on the actual conditions present in coal mines and the relevant provisions outlined in the “Coal Mine Safety Regulations.” In China, the rock temperature at a depth of 1000 m can reach 35℃, with the underground temperature primarily influenced by the elevation relative to the ground. Typically, seasonal variations are observed in confined areas such as shafts, whereas regions with a relative elevation difference greater than 15 m do not exhibit significant seasonal fluctuations. According to the regulations, the temperature at underground work sites must not exceed 28℃, while the upper limit for transformer chambers is set at 30℃. The air temperature at a depth of 600 m in the mine generally ranges between 15 and 17℃, with an optimal labor temperature range of 15 to 20℃. Since the underground temperature is insulated from outdoor influences, it remains around 18℃ year-round, with the highest average temperature during summer months (July and August) reaching 25℃24. Considering the actual industrial conditions and the rationality of the experiments, the selection of experimental temperatures should not only reflect the real downhole environment but also encompass a typical range from room temperature to elevated temperatures. Consequently, the experiment selected four temperatures: 291.15 K (18℃), 298.15 K (25℃), 303.15 K (30℃), and 308.15 K (35℃), thereby ensuring the practicality and reliability of the experimental results to systematically investigate the influence of temperature on the wetting performance of surfactant solutions on coal powder.

Wettability test

Static contact angle measurement

The cylindrical coal specimens for contact angle measurements were prepared using a standardized procedure: 3.00 ± 0.01 g of coal powder was precisely weighed using an electronic balance (JA3003N), uniformly loaded into a specialized mold, and compacted under 16–20 MPa pressure for 3–4 min to form the test specimens25. To ensure surface consistency, all specimens were prepared using identical mold surfaces to control surface roughness, and were tested directly after compaction without additional surface treatment. The mold cavity was cleaned with anhydrous ethanol before each experiment, and all handling procedures were performed wearing clean gloves to prevent contamination. Through these strictly controlled standardized procedures for coal mass and compaction operations, macroscopic structural reproducibility across different samples was guaranteed. It should be noted that the coal powder used in this study has a broad particle size distribution (70–150 μm). This wide distribution theoretically facilitates tighter particle packing during compaction, resulting in a denser surface with lower porosity. This helps minimize instantaneous capillary absorption of the surfactant solution into the coal specimen during the initial measurement stage, allowing droplets to remain more stable on the surface, thereby enabling more accurate measurement of contact angles that better reflect the chemical interactions at the solid-liquid interface.

In the contact angle measurement section, using pure water as a control, the wettability changes of three surfactant solutions were investigated at different temperatures. The prepared coal specimens were fixed on the sample stage of an optical contact angle measuring instrument (Kruss DSA30), and the static contact angles of the solutions on the coal surface were determined using the pendant drop method. The experimental temperature range was from 291.15 K (18 °C) to 308.15 K (35 °C). During contact angle measurements, a high-precision temperature-controlled air gun (temperature control accuracy: ±0.5 K) was used for real-time temperature control of the surfactant solution in the syringe. The air gun maintained temperature stability within ± 0.3 K throughout the entire test range of 291.15–308.15 K through a PID control system. Measurements at each temperature point were conducted after reaching thermal equilibrium, and each case was tested in triplicate and averaged to ensure data reliability.

Dynamic coal dust settlement measurement

As shown in Fig. 2, this experiment was conducted using three different surfactant solutions and a clear water control, with 100 mL of each solution placed in separate test tubes. The tubes were immersed in an electric thermostatic water bath (Model: JC-HH-6) maintained at four predetermined temperatures: 291.15 K (18 °C), 298.15 K (25 °C), 303.15 K (30 °C), and 308.15 K (35 °C), simulating typical downhole thermal environments. Into each tube, 20 mg of coal powder was introduced, and a timer was started immediately to record the time taken for the powder to settle completely from the solution surface to the bottom of the tube.

To enable quantitative cross-comparison among different systems, the average settlement rate (ASR) was calculated based on the total settlement time (t) using the following Eq. (2):

ASR = H/t (2).

where H denotes the height of the test tube and t is the total settlement time. The ASR, expressed in cm/s, reflects the apparent average settling velocity of coal particles in the solution, with higher values indicating more rapid and efficient sedimentation.

Throughout the tests, the temperature variation in the water bath was rigorously controlled within ± 0.5 °C to ensure the reliability and repeatability of the experimental outcomes.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Model construction and optimization

As a complex multi-component mixture system, coal possesses abundant reserves in China. To achieve clean and efficient production within the coal chemical industry, researchers have conducted in-depth studies on the microscopic structure of coal, revealing significant differences in the physicochemical properties across various ranks. Based on these findings, corresponding chemical structure models have been proposed. Notable coal structure models in the field of coal chemistry include the Wender model29 for lignite structure, the Wiser model30,31 for bituminous coal structure, the Given model32, the Shinn model33, and the Spiro model applicable to anthracite34. This study employs a representative Wiser bituminous coal molecular structure to construct the coal model, a selection based on industrial analysis of the samples (Table 1) and balanced with the computational capacity of the workstation. The Wiser model is widely recognized as a comprehensive and chemically reasonable representation for young bituminous coal, effectively capturing key structural features crucial for interfacial interactions, such as aromatic clusters, aliphatic chains, and oxygen-containing functional groups. The use of this representative model implies that while it cannot atomically replicate the full heterogeneity of specific coal samples, it provides a well-defined and reliable framework for investigating fundamental mechanisms. The combined approach of molecular dynamics simulations and experimental methods demonstrates that the primary molecular-level trends in surfactant adsorption behavior and water penetration characteristics are robust. The strength of this model lies in its ability to accurately simulate the core physical chemistry governing wettability, thereby ensuring the validity and scientific value of conclusions regarding temperature effects and surfactant performance.

Fig. 3The optimal geometric configuration of the molecular model.

The optimal geometric configuration of the molecular model.

In the process of molecular simulation, the molecular structure models of water, surfactants (OP-10, SDS, BS-12), and coal were initially constructed using Materials Studio 2019 software. Subsequently, the geometry optimization function in the Forcite module was employed, utilizing the Compass II force field. By applying the ‘smart’ algorithm in conjunction with annealing dynamics simulation, the optimal geometric configuration of the molecular models was achieved, resulting in the minimum configuration (as illustrated in Fig. 3). Following this, the Amorphous Cell module was used to construct individual unit cells containing 10 molecules each of OP-10, SDS, and BS-12 surfactants, along with 1000 water molecules and 8 coal molecules, all sharing identical dimensions (lengths (Å): 33 × 33). Building upon this, the Build Layer tool was utilized to overlay the constructed water molecule layer and surfactant molecule layer onto the coal surface, thereby creating a Coal-(surfactant)-H2O system model. A 30 Å vacuum layer was introduced at the top of the system to mitigate the effects of periodic boundary conditions. Upon completing the system construction, the Smart algorithm was employed to conduct geometric optimization and annealing on the system, eliminating atomic overlaps and achieving the lowest energy configuration.

MD simulation of adsorption configuration establishment

Following the completion of molecular optimization and the construction of the simulation system, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted under the NVT ensemble. The NVT canonical system is a typical framework for Monte Carlo simulations, allowing for full interactions between molecules were conducted using the Dynamic tool under the Nosé-Hoover thermostat35. In this study, four temperature gradients were selected for simulation: 291.15 K (18℃), 298.15 K (25℃), 303.15 K (30℃), and 308.15 K (35℃), to investigate the effect of temperature on the behavior of water molecules within the system. The specific analysis metrics included the relative concentration distribution (RCD) of water molecules, the diffusion coefficient (D), and the mean square displacement (MSD). The total simulation duration was set to 300 ps, with a time step of 1 fs. To ensure the simulation system reached equilibrium, the total energy of the system was monitored. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, during the latter part of the simulation, the total energy fluctuated minimally and remained stable, indicating that the system had achieved an equilibrium state. All subsequent analyses were performed based on this equilibrated trajectory.

During the simulation, the Ewald algorithm was employed to calculate electrostatic interactions, with an accuracy threshold of 0.001 kcal/mol, while the Atom algorithm was utilized to compute van der Waals interactions, with a cutoff distance of 12.5 Å. Quantum mechanical calculations were performed using the DMol3 module, applying the GGA and BLYP functionals to obtain the electrostatic potential distribution of each molecule36. The construction of the molecular model and the process of molecular dynamics simulation are illustrated in Fig. 4.

Results and discussion

Analysis of experimental results of wettability tests

Fig. 5Contact angles between different surfactant solutions and briquettes at different temperatures. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 3).

Contact angles between different surfactant solutions and briquettes at different temperatures. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 3).

In the contact angle experiment, the wettability changes of pure water and three surfactant solutions-amphoteric surfactant BS-12, nonionic surfactant OP-10, and anionic surfactant SDS-were compared at different temperatures. The test results presented in Fig. 5 indicate that increasing temperature significantly enhances the wettability of the surfactant solutions. Specifically, the contact angles of the three surfactant solutions with briquettes progressively decreased as the temperature rose. Notably, the contact angle of BS-12 decreased from 52.3° to 28.6%, representing a reduction of 45.3%; the contact angle of OP-10 decreased from 45.32° to 22.6%, a reduction of 50.1%; and the contact angle of SDS decreased from 49.8° to 26.5%, a reduction of 46.8%. In contrast, the contact angle of water decreased from 88.5° to 70.5%, reflecting a reduction of only 20.3%, which indicates a weaker response of water to temperature changes. Among the surfactants, the nonionic surfactant OP-10 exhibited the most substantial decrease in contact angle (50.1%), demonstrating the most significant improvement in the wettability of briquettes within the experimental temperature range. The decreases in contact angles for BS-12 and SDS were similar, at 45.3% and 46.8%, respectively, indicating that these two surfactants had comparable effects in enhancing wettability.Although water also exhibited a decreasing trend in contact angle with increasing temperature, the degree of reduction was significantly less than that observed in surfactant solutions. This suggests that surfactants play a crucial role in enhancing wettability. An in-depth analysis of surfactant types reveals that the nonionic surfactant OP-10 provides the most substantial improvement in wettability as temperature rises, attributable to its stable molecular structure and strong temperature-responsive characteristics37. Conversely, the amphoteric surfactant BS-12 and the anionic surfactant SDS demonstrate slightly lower effectiveness compared to OP-10, which can be attributed to their charge properties and limited temperature responsiveness. In summary, an increase in temperature can significantly reduce the contact angle between surfactant solutions and briquettes, thereby enhancing wettability, with the nonionic surfactant OP-10 exhibiting the most pronounced effect.

As temperature increases, the adsorption capacity of surfactants also rises, enhancing their diffusion ability on the surface of briquette droplets. The effect of temperature on surfactants is particularly significant, primarily due to its influence on the wettability of surfactants, which is closely related to their rheological and adhesive properties. The relationship between temperature and rheological properties involves analyzing how temperature affects viscosity; this relationship conforms to the Arrhenius equation, which indicates that viscosity decreases exponentially with rising temperature38. As the temperature increases, the intermolecular attractions within surfactant solutions weaken, leading to a reduction in internal molecular forces and an increase in fluidity. Furthermore, the enhanced mobility of certain molecules and the increased free volume within the system result in surfactant molecules migrating to the bulk phase. Consequently, the diffusion and adsorption processes occurring at the interface contribute to a gradual and irreversible decrease in the surface tension of the solution over time.The adhesion process at the two-phase interface involves the formation of chemical bonds and the generation of new products. This process is characterized by the qualitative adsorption of polar molecules on the surface, which leads to adhesion, or the establishment of an adhesion system resulting from the surface energy of the two phases. These phenomena entail changes in the system’s energy39. The size of the contact angle of a liquid on a solid surface is intrinsically linked to the solid-liquid surface energy. Additionally, temperature influences the density of the two phases at the solid-liquid interface, creating an imbalance in the forces acting on surface molecules, which subsequently affects the adhesion system. Within a specific range, as temperature increases, the work of adhesion also increases, leading to a stronger interaction between the surfactant and coal. Consequently, the contact angle decreases, and the wetting effect of the surfactant is enhanced.

Fig. 6Settlement time and average settlement rate of coal powder under different temperatures. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 3).

Settlement time and average settlement rate of coal powder under different temperatures. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 3).

The penetration capability of surfactant solutions into wet coal powder was evaluated through settlement experiments, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The time required for coal powder to settle completely after contacting the solution was recorded. It should be noted that the “total settlement time” measured in this test reflects the collective behavior of the entire particle population in a specific surfactant solution, rather than that of a single particle size. According to Stokes’ Law40, the terminal settling velocity of particles is proportional to the square of their diameter. Therefore, within the coal particle size range of 70–150 μm used in this experiment, the theoretical settling velocity of the largest particles (150 μm) is approximately 4.6 times that of the smallest particles (70 μm). This inherent velocity difference is a primary source of the observed variability in the measurement results. Additionally, based on an understanding of particle-solution interactions, the broad size distribution implies a wide range of surface-area-to-volume ratios for the coal particles. Smaller particles, with their larger specific surface area, may interact more thoroughly with surfactant molecules, potentially influencing the degree of surface wetting modification and the final flocculation/settlement behavior. Despite the particle size distribution, this experimental method effectively characterizes the relative efficiency of different surfactants in promoting rapid settlement of coal powder with a broad particle size distribution.

In the settlement experiments, due to the large specific surface area of coal powder, it was not easily wetted in pure water and failed to settle consistently with temperature variations. Therefore, these data were not included in the figure. The figure only displays data for the three surfactant solutions: amphoteric BS-12, nonionic OP-10, and anionic SDS. The results indicate that for all tested surfactants, the settlement time of coal powder gradually decreased with increasing temperature. Specifically, within the studied concentration range, the OP-10 solution exhibited the most significant reduction in the settling time of coal powder, at 83.62%. The longer hydrocarbon chain length of OP-10 enhances the dispersion force between its molecules and the hydrophilic coal surface, leading to stronger adsorption of OP-10 at the solid-liquid interface. The SDS solution showed a 63.44% reduction in the wetting and settling time of coal powder, while the BS-12 solution demonstrated the least change, with only a 33.1% reduction. This limited improvement may be attributed to the relatively weaker tendency of BS-12 molecules to aggregate at the liquid interface. Furthermore, the average settlement rate data under all experimental conditions clearly indicate that both elevated temperature and the addition of surfactants significantly enhance the settlement rate. The nonionic surfactant OP-10 consistently exhibited the highest average settlement rate across all temperatures, and this quantitative trend is fully consistent with and corroborates the conclusions drawn from the total settlement time measurements.

Fig. 7(a) The mechanism of surfactant wetting coal; (b) The contact angle morphology of different surfactant solutions and briquettes at different temperatures.

(a) The mechanism of surfactant wetting coal; (b) The contact angle morphology of different surfactant solutions and briquettes at different temperatures.

In the system where surfactant droplets interact with coal (as shown in Fig. 7a), during the initial stage, surfactant molecules adsorb onto the hydrophobic sites of the coal surface via their hydrophobic tails, while the hydrophilic head groups remain oriented toward the aqueous phase. This directional arrangement is the fundamental reason for the enhanced hydrophilicity and improved wettability of the coal surface. When the surfactant solution concentration reaches the critical micelle concentration (CMC), a complete monolayer of surfactant molecules forms on the coal surface. As the concentration increases further, excess surfactant molecules self-assemble in the aqueous solution to form micellar structures. At this stage, the molecular arrangement transitions such that the hydrophilic head groups face outward, interacting with the aqueous phase, while the hydrophobic tails cluster together within the micelles. As shown in Fig. 7(b), with the increase in temperature, due to the adsorption of surfactants, the surfactants enhance the spreading of droplets on both hydrophilic and hydrophobic surfaces, accelerating the diffusion of surfactant monomers towards the opposite direction for aggregation, preventing their hydrophobic groups from contacting with water, and forming a surfactant bilayer41. The hydrophilic polar groups are oriented towards the water, forming a hydrogen bond network, which results in the coal surface becoming more wettable.

Combining the results of contact angle experiments and sedimentation tests, it is observed that, within the experimental range, the wettability of the surfactants increases progressively with rising temperature.

Analysis of electrostatic potential

Fig. 8Distribution of static potentials on different molecules. (a) BS-12, (b) H2O, (c) SDS, (d) Coal molecule, (e) OP-10.

Distribution of static potentials on different molecules. (a) BS-12, (b) H2O, (c) SDS, (d) Coal molecule, (e) OP-10.

By analyzing the electrostatic potential distribution of different molecules in Fig. 8(a)–(e), we can gain a deeper understanding of the interaction mechanisms between surfactants and coal molecules. From the perspective of the electrostatic potential distribution range, the nonionic surfactant OP-10 exhibits the narrowest range (−0.06858 to 0.37289), indicating a relatively uniform distribution of surface charges on its molecules, which facilitates the formation of a stable adsorption layer on the coal surface. In contrast, the anionic surfactant SDS exhibits the broadest electrostatic potential distribution (from − 0.1273 to 0.50336), which includes a region of strong negative charge that facilitates preferential electrostatic anchoring to localized positive sites (e.g., -NH₃⁺ groups) on the coal surface. The electrostatic potential distribution range of the zwitterionic surfactant BS-12 (−0.15543 to 0.25948) lies between that of OP-10 and SDS. Its unique charge distribution characteristics enable it to interact simultaneously with both positive and negative charge regions in coal molecules. Notably, the electrostatic potential distribution range of coal powder (−0.099251 to 0.41202) partially overlaps with that of water molecules (−0.081134 to 0.36955), indicating the possible existence of certain electrostatic interactions between water molecules and coal molecules.

From the perspective of interaction mechanisms, the degree of electrostatic potential matching between surfactant molecules and coal molecules directly affects their adsorption efficiency42. When the electrostatic potential distribution of surfactant molecules aligns with the surface charge distribution of coal molecules, a more stable adsorption layer is formed, thereby enhancing the wettability of coal. Furthermore, the electrostatic repulsion among surfactant molecules influences their arrangement on the coal surface, which subsequently affects the wettability effect. Specifically, the non-ionic surfactant OP-10, characterized by its relatively uniform charge distribution, may create a dense monolayer on coal, effectively reducing surface tension. The anionic surfactant SDS binds to these positive regions through electrostatic interactions, forming localized adsorption sites that act as nucleation points for the subsequent development of a hydrophilic surface structure. The zwitterionic surfactant BS-12 could potentially form a more complex adsorption structure on the coal surface due to its amphoteric characteristics. Thus, the degree of electrostatic potential matching between surfactants and coal molecules is a critical factor influencing coal wettability.

Dynamic simulation analysis

Analysis of system equilibrium States at four different temperatures

Figure 9 illustrates the final equilibrium state of the water-surfactant-coal system at preset temperatures. In the initial state (Fig. 4(c)), the water layer, surfactant layer, and coal layer remain separated, maintaining the system in a non-equilibrium condition. As the simulation progresses, water molecules gradually migrate toward and adsorb onto the coal surface under the mediation of surfactant molecules, effectively wetting the coal dust and eventually reaching an equilibrium state (Fig. 9).

With increasing temperature, surfactant molecules undergo rearrangement on the coal surface. Enhanced molecular kinetic energy promotes the adsorption of surfactants, driven by electrostatic interactions. Adsorption configuration analysis reveals that surfactant molecules transition from a lateral to a vertical orientation. During this process, the hydrophobic tails of the surfactants intertwine with the hydrophobic groups of the coal, while the hydrophilic head groups form a hydrogen-bonding network with water molecules43. As shown in Fig. 9(a), BS-12 molecules exhibit weak orientational order in the system. Even in the final equilibrium state, BS-12 maintains a slightly lateral distribution, resulting in a loosely structured adsorption layer between coal and water and relatively poor wettability. In contrast, Fig. 9(b) shows that OP-10 molecules contain benzene rings that engage in π-π stacking interactions with the aromatic structures in coal, which reduces the system energy and significantly enhances coal wettability44. In Fig. 9(c), SDS molecules are anchored at the interface via electrostatic interactions, with the anionic hydrophilic head groups facing the aqueous phase and the alkyl chains oriented toward the coal. Although the SDS system may facilitate rapid water transport, the resulting adsorption layer is relatively loose in structure. Moreover, this system primarily relies on electrostatic anchoring by the hydrophilic head groups, while showing weaker performance in synergistic effects such as π-π stacking. These results indicate that both BS-12 and SDS exhibit certain limitations in achieving durable and stable hydrophilic modification of coal compared to the OP-10 system.

The broader overlapping range observed for BS-12 indicates a stronger interaction of its hydrophilic groups with water molecules, while the narrower range for OP-10 is attributed to its non-ionic nature. The elevation in temperature results in a thicker adsorption layer at the coal-water interface, facilitating deeper penetration of water molecules into the coal structure, thereby enhancing its wettability. Figure 11 illustrates the thickness of the adsorption layer and its peak variations for the water-coal systems of three surfactants (BS-12, OP-10, and SDS) at different temperatures. As the temperature increases, the thickness of the BS-12 adsorption layer fluctuates between 13.98 and 16.53 g/cc. In contrast, the peak thickness of the OP-10 adsorption layer initially decreases before increasing, ranging from 12.28 to 15.91 g/cc. Meanwhile, the peak thickness of the SDS adsorption layer first increases and then decreases, varying from 12.81 to 15.26 g/cc. These observations suggest that temperature significantly influences the adsorption behavior of different surfactants. The peak thickness of the BS-12 adsorption layer exhibits slight fluctuations, indicating relatively stable adsorption behavior, which may be attributed to its zwitterionic characteristics that facilitate effective adsorption on the coal surface across varying temperatures. Conversely, the OP-10 adsorption layer’s peak thickness first decreases and then increases, likely due to its non-ionic characteristics that result in weaker adsorption at lower temperatures; however, as the temperature rises, the interaction between its hydrophobic chain and coal strengthens, leading to an increase in adsorption layer thickness. The SDS adsorption layer’s peak thickness initially increases and subsequently decreases, potentially related to its anionic characteristics, as it shows stronger adsorption at moderate temperatures. However, at elevated temperatures, increased molecular motion may induce instability within the adsorption layer. Furthermore, the rise in temperature promotes a more uniform distribution of surfactant molecules on the coal surface, optimizing the structure of the adsorption layer and enhancing the wettability of coal. The increase in the thickness of the adsorption layer facilitates deeper penetration of water into the coal, which further reduces both the surface tension of the water and the solid interfacial tension of the coal, thereby enhancing the wettability of the coal.

It is noteworthy that the diffusion coefficients of the BS-12 and SDS systems exhibit a distinct dip at 298.15 K, which deviates from the monotonically increasing trend predicted solely by enhanced molecular thermal motion. We propose that this phenomenon reveals the complex influence of temperature on the interfacial microstructure. For these two charged surfactants, at lower temperatures, their head groups may form a relatively stable and ordered network of electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions with the coal surface and surrounding water molecules. When the temperature rises to 298.15 K, the supplied thermal energy becomes sufficient to partially disrupt this ordered structure, triggering a microscopic reorganization in the interfacial region. This restructuring process temporarily increases the resistance to molecular motion, leading to a decrease in the diffusion coefficient. As the temperature further increases, the randomizing effect of the enhanced thermal energy ultimately becomes dominant, and the diffusion coefficient resumes its upward trend. n contrast, the nonionic OP-10 strongly interacts with the aromatic constituents of coal via π–π stacking, and its interfacial structure exhibits a continuous response to temperature variations, resulting in a monotonic increase in diffusion coefficient.

Reconciling macroscopic performance with molecular-scale dynamics

The apparent discrepancy between the macroscopic experiments, which identified OP-10 as the optimal surfactant, and the molecular dynamics analysis, which indicated superior water mobility in the SDS system, can be reconciled by considering the fundamental difference in what these metrics represent. The macroscopic wetting performance, as determined by contact angle and settlement time, is an integral property reflecting the final, thermodynamic equilibrium state of the coal-water interface. In contrast, the water self-diffusion coefficient derived from MSD is a local, dynamic property that captures kinetic behavior within the simulated environment.

This study demonstrates that OP-10 achieves its outstanding macroscopic performance by forming the most stable, thickest, and most coherent adsorption layer on the coal surface. This layer acts as a highly effective and durable hydrophilic barrier, fundamentally and permanently altering the coal surface wettability. While the local water dynamics within this well-structured, stable layer are not the fastest, the quality of the interface it creates is superior for dust suppression.

In comparison, although the SDS system demonstrates the ability to promote rapid water molecule transport, the resulting adsorption layer exhibits relatively limited structural compactness. This system primarily relies on electrostatic interactions of hydrophilic head groups for interfacial anchoring, while showing weaker performance in synergistic effects such as π-π stacking. These characteristics may contribute to higher dynamics and porosity in the adsorption layer. Although this structural feature facilitates enhanced water molecule mobility (manifested as higher diffusion coefficients), it shows certain limitations in achieving durable and stable hydrophilic modification compared to the OP-10 system.

Research findings indicate that the kinetic characteristics of water penetration in the SDS system do not effectively synergize with its interfacial thermodynamic stability, while interfacial thermodynamic properties appear to exert more substantial influence on actual dust suppression effectiveness. Comparatively, the advantages demonstrated by OP-10 in constructing thermodynamically stable interfaces suggest better application potential in comprehensive performance evaluation.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the mechanism by which temperature enhances coal dust wetting by surfactant solutions through integrated experimental and molecular dynamics simulations. Beyond confirming temperature’s positive role, our findings provide critical insights for practical dust suppression strategies. The simulations were conducted using idealized coal models, which may not fully represent the heterogeneity of natural coal surfaces. Future work should explore synergistic effects in multi-surfactant systems and validate these findings under real industrial conditions.
