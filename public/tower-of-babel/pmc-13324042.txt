Comprehensive behavioral profiling in male spontaneously hypertensive rats: latent trait mapping supports a valid multidomain ADHD model
Authors: Han-Byeol Kim, Yu-Jin Kim, Hyo-Min Lim, Jiseun Lim, Shinwoo Kang, Eun-Mee Kim, Gi Jeong Cheon, Yoori Choi, Ran-Sook Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder marked by inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity. Although the spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) is widely used to model ADHD, intra-strain behavioral heterogeneity and its neurobiological relevance remain insufficiently defined. We conducted a comprehensive multidomain behavioral assessment of male adolescent SHR and Wistar–Kyoto (WKY) rats spanning locomotion/exploration, anxiety- and risk-related behavior, compulsive-like activity, cognition, and sensorimotor gating. Across tasks, SHR rats exhibited increased impulsive and repetitive exploratory behaviors, context-dependent alterations in anxiety-related measures, reduced spontaneous alternation, and impaired prepulse inhibition. Exploratory factor analysis of 26 behavioral variables identified five interpretable latent dimensions, including exploratory–attentional engagement and impulsivity/disinhibition, and revealed substantial heterogeneity within the SHR population. Using WKY-referenced factor-score deviation, SHR were stratified into normative-range and combined-deviant subtypes. The combined-deviant subtype exhibited convergent circuit-level alterations, including reduced parvalbumin (PV)/glutamate decarboxylase 67 (GAD67)-associated inhibitory features and decreased synaptic marker signals in prelimbic and infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex, together with increased dopamine transporter expression (DAT) in the striatum without changes in tyrosine hydroxylase (TH). Complementary principal axis factoring, bootstrap resampling, and split-sample analyses supported the robustness of the factor structure. These findings link multidimensional behavioral subtypes in SHR to distinct prefrontal–striatal signatures, strengthening the translational utility of SHR-based ADHD research. Subject terms: Hippocampus, Long-term memory

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common and heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity. Affecting approximately 5–7% of children worldwide, ADHD frequently persists into adulthood and is associated with substantial functional impairments, including academic, occupational, and social difficulties, as well as elevated risk for psychiatric comorbidities such as anxiety, mood disorders, and substance use disorders [1, 2]. Although psychostimulant medications such as methylphenidate are widely prescribed, therapeutic response is variable and underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for translational animal models that capture the multidimensional nature of the disorder.

The spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) has been extensively used as an animal model of ADHD, as it exhibits core behavioral phenotypes relevant to the disorder, including hyperactivity, impulsivity, and attentional deficits, and shows behavioral and pharmacological responses to psychostimulants that parallel those observed in humans [3, 4]. Alterations in dopaminergic and noradrenergic signaling within prefrontal and striatal circuits have also been reported in SHR, consistent with neurobiological findings in ADHD [5, 6]. However, most SHR-based studies have relied on isolated behavioral paradigms or single-domain analyses, which may obscure broader behavioral organization and limit translational interpretability.

In the present study, only male SHR and Wistar–Kyoto (WKY) rats were examined. This decision was based on prior evidence demonstrating robust and reproducible ADHD-like phenotypes in male SHR, particularly with respect to impulsivity, locomotor activity, and dopaminergic dysregulation [3, 4]. While female SHR display behavioral alterations, these often differ in profile, with relatively greater perseverative or attentional features and lower levels of overt hyperactivity [7, 8]. Given the focus on impulsivity- and activity-related behavioral dimensions and on factor-based modeling of behavioral variance, restricting the cohort to males reduced biological variability and enhanced interpretability. Potential sex differences remain an important consideration for future studies.

Beyond the canonical triad of ADHD symptoms, accumulating clinical evidence indicates that ADHD is frequently accompanied by deficits in emotional regulation, risk evaluation, repetitive behaviors, and sensorimotor gating [9–11]. These features contribute to clinical heterogeneity and overlap with symptoms observed in comorbid conditions such as anxiety disorders and obsessive–compulsive disorder, underscoring the need for animal models that reflect a broader behavioral spectrum.

Despite consistent group-level differences between SHR and control strains such as WKY rats, substantial inter-individual variability has been reported within the SHR population, particularly in tasks related to exploration, object interaction, and cognitive engagement [12–14]. Such heterogeneity parallels the dimensional and subtype-based structure increasingly recognized in ADHD and related neurodevelopmental disorders [15, 16]. Conventional univariate analyses, however, are poorly suited to capturing shared variance across behavioral domains or identifying coherent behavioral profiles within genetically homogeneous model populations.

Factor analytic approaches have been widely applied in human ADHD research to identify latent symptom dimensions that inform subtyping, prognosis, and treatment response [17], but comparable data-driven frameworks remain underutilized in animal models. This limitation may hinder identification of biologically meaningful behavioral endophenotypes and obscure links between behavior and underlying neural circuitry.

All behavioral testing in the present study was conducted during adolescence (postnatal days 28–56), a developmental period corresponding to the emergence of ADHD symptoms in humans. Importantly, this window precedes the onset of sustained hypertension in SHR rats, minimizing potential confounding effects of vascular pathology [18–20].

To address these gaps, we performed a comprehensive multidomain behavioral assessment in adolescent male SHR and WKY rats, spanning locomotion and exploration, anxiety- and risk-related behavior, compulsive-like activity, cognition, and sensorimotor gating. Using exploratory factor analysis of 26 behavioral variables, we aimed to identify latent behavioral dimensions and characterize inter-individual heterogeneity within the SHR population. We further tested whether behaviorally defined SHR subtypes correspond to distinct neurobiological endophenotypes within prefrontal–striatal circuits implicated in ADHD-related cognitive and impulse control. By integrating dimensional behavioral profiling with targeted neurobiological validation, this study seeks to strengthen the construct and translational validity of the SHR model for ADHD research.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from Samtako Bio Korea, and male WKY and SHR were obtained from DooYeol Biotech and Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. Animals were group-housed under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. A total of 54 male rats were used (SD, n = 12; WKY, n = 11; SHR, n = 31). All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Eulji University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines (EUIACUC 25-24).

SD rats were included only in the initial phase to provide a behavioral reference from an outbred, genetically heterogeneous strain, allowing verification of baseline differences relative to inbred strains. As the primary objective was to investigate behavioral heterogeneity within a genetically homogeneous ADHD model, only WKY and SHR rats were included in clustering and factor-based analyses. A deliberately larger sample size was used for the SHR group to enable robust assessment of inter-individual variability and facilitate unsupervised identification of behavioral subgroups.

Reagents and antibodies

Primary antibodies were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (TH, sc-25269; GAD67, sc-59531; β-actin, sc-47778), Millipore (Synapsin1, AB1543P), Invitrogen (PSD95, MA1-046), Synaptic Systems (PV, 195011C3), and Abcam (DAT, ab184451; PV, ab11427).

Behavioral analysis

General testing conditions

All behavioral experiments were conducted during adolescence (4–8 weeks). Animals were tested in a fixed order to minimize order effects and handled daily for 3 days before testing. Experiments were performed during the light phase under controlled lighting (~15 lux). Behavioral data were primarily acquired using automated tracking systems, with manual scoring performed by an experimenter aware of strain identity but blind to subgroup classification. Apparatuses were cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was conducted using a computerized tracking system (SMART®, Panlab). Rats were placed in a 60 × 60 × 30 cm arena divided into inner, side, and edge zones and allowed to explore for 10 min. Distance traveled, velocity, zone occupancy, rearing, wall-leaning, and grooming were recorded. To assess initial exploratory and anxiety-related behavior, analyses focused on the first 5 min of the session.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM consisted of two open and two closed arms (10 × 100 cm), elevated 72 cm above the floor. Rats were placed in the center and allowed to explore for 5 min. Time spent in each arm and the center zone, as well as the number of head dips, were quantified. An arm entry was defined as occurring when the center of the rat’s body crossed into an arm.

Novel object recognition (NOR)

Rats were habituated to an empty arena (two 5-min sessions). During training, two identical objects were presented for 10 min. In Test 1 (NOR10), one object was replaced with a novel object, and in Test 2 (NOR24), a second novel object was introduced. Object exploration time was recorded, and preference for the novel object was used as an index of recognition memory.

Marble buried test (MBT)

Rats were placed for 30 min in a cage containing 5 cm of bedding and 20 marbles arranged in a grid. Buried marbles (>2/3 covered) and displaced marbles (moved but not buried) were counted.

Nestlet shredding test (NST)

Rats were individually housed overnight (18:00–09:00) with pre-weighed nesting material. Shredding was quantified as the difference between initial and remaining nestlet weight.

Cliff avoidance test (CAT)

Rats were placed on a circular platform (15 cm diameter, 25 cm height). Latency to fall and fall duration were recorded.

Visual cliff avoidance test (VCAT)

The VCAT was performed as previously described [21]. Rats were placed in a transparent acrylic box positioned at the edge of a table with a patterned surface below. Time spent with the forepaws over the cliff zone during a 6 min session was measured.

Factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed using principal component extraction with oblimin rotation. Factor retention was based on eigenvalues (>1) and scree plot inspection, and factor scores were computed in SAS 9.4 for group-level analyses. Robustness analyses using principal axis factoring and resampling are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Subgroup classification based on factor scores

SHR subgroups were defined relative to WKY distributions. For each factor, the WKY mean ± 1 SD was used to define the normative range. SHR rats within this range for both Factor 1 (exploratory–attentional engagement) and Factor 3 (impulsivity-related disinhibition) were classified as SHR-Norm. Rats outside the range on Factor 1 or Factor 3 were classified as SHR-Attentional or SHR-Impulsive, respectively, while those outside the range on both were classified as SHR-Comb.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [22]. Brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, sectioned at 30 μm, and processed for antigen retrieval, blocking, and antibody incubation. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 8 confocal microscope.

Western blot

Western blotting was performed as previously described [21]. Prefrontal cortex and striatal proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and detected by enhanced chemiluminescence.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral testing was conducted across independent cohorts tested at separate times. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality was assessed using standard tests. Parametric or nonparametric tests were applied as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Experimental timeline and initial exploratory behavior in SHR rats

To comprehensively characterize ADHD-relevant behavioral phenotypes, rats were subjected to a structured battery of behavioral tests across adolescence (postnatal weeks 4–8; Fig. 1A). This developmental window was selected to capture attentional and impulse-control traits prior to the onset of sustained hypertension in SHR rats. Behavioral assays were conducted in a fixed order to minimize variability related to test sequence. Weekly body weight measurements revealed no significant differences between WKY and SHR rats across the testing period (Supplementary Fig. S1B), indicating comparable growth trajectories.

Exploratory and anxiety-related behavior was first assessed using the OFT. During the initial 5 min exploration phase, SHR rats exhibited significantly fewer zone transitions compared with WKY controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 1E), whereas total distance traveled and mean velocity did not differ between groups (Fig. 1D, F). Spatial analysis showed reduced time spent in the inner zone (p < 0.01; Fig. 1G) and increased time in the side zone (p < 0.05; Fig. 1H) in SHR rats, with no difference in edge zone occupancy (Fig. 1I). In contrast, no significant group differences were observed during the full 10 min and OFT session across locomotor or spatial parameters (Supplementary Fig. S4A–F).

Repetitive and compulsive-like behaviors in MBT and NST

Repetitive and compulsive-like behaviors were evaluated using the MBT and NST (Fig. 1J–P). The number of buried marbles did not differ between WKY and SHR rats (p = 0.213; Fig. 1K). However, SHR rats displaced significantly more marbles than WKY controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 1L).

To visualize inter-individual variability, individual buried and displaced marble values were plotted and classified into four behavioral phenotypes (Normal, Apathy-like, High-displaced, and Obsessive-like; Fig. 1M, N). SHR rats were overrepresented in the Obsessive-like (48.39%) and High-displaced (9.68%) categories, whereas no WKY rats were classified as High-displaced.

Consistent with these findings, SHR rats exhibited markedly increased nestlet shredding compared with WKY controls (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1O, P), indicating elevated repetitive motor output across paradigms.

Impulsivity-related behavior in the cliff avoidance paradigm

Impulsivity and innate risk evaluation were assessed using the CAT and VCAT (Fig. 2A–F). In the CAT, SHR rats displayed significantly shorter latency to jump from the elevated platform compared with WKY rats (p < 0.05; Fig. 2B). Survival analysis revealed a trend toward faster jumping in SHR rats, although this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.060; Fig. 2C).

In contrast, no significant group differences were observed in the VCAT for jumping latency (p = 0.201; Fig. 2E) or survival analysis (p = 0.214; Fig. 2F), indicating comparable performance when visual depth cues were present.

Together, these results imply that SHR rats exhibit impulsive responses specifically in the absence of explicit visual danger cues, potentially reflecting deficiencies in inhibitory control and innate risk assessment rather than visual perceptual deficits.

Anxiety-related behavior in the EPM

Anxiety- and exploration-related behaviors were further assessed using the EPM (Fig. 2G–L). Time spent in closed and open arms did not differ between WKY and SHR rats (Fig. 2I, J). However, SHR rats spent significantly more time in the center zone (p < 0.05; Fig. 2K) and exhibited a higher number of head dips compared with WKY rats (p < 0.05; Fig. 2L). No other EPM measures differed significantly between groups (closed arms, p = 0.073; open arms, p = 0.298).

NOR reveals enhanced exploratory drive and intact atypical recognition behavior in SHR rats

Recognition memory and object-directed exploration were assessed using the NOR paradigm (Fig. 3A–N). Locomotor activity did not differ between groups across training or test sessions (Fig. 3B–D). SHR rats spent significantly more time investigating objects during the short-term test (NOR10; p < 0.05; Fig. 3E), whereas a similar trend at the long-term test (NOR24) did not reach significance (p = 0.084, Fig. 3J).

WKY rats showed a significant preference for the novel object in both NOR10 and NOR24 (p < 0.05; Fig. 3F and K). In contrast, SHR rats did not show a significant preference during NOR10 (Fig. 3G) but did show novelty preference at NOR24 (p < 0.05; Fig. 3L).

Individual-level classification revealed greater heterogeneity in SHR rats across both time points. In NOR10, SHR rats were distributed across High-Novel, High-Familiar, and Non-preference subtypes, whereas WKY rats predominantly exhibited High-Novel preference (Fig. 3H, I). Similar patterns were observed at NOR24 (Fig. 3M, N).

WKY rats exhibit significantly higher sensorimotor gating, attention-related patterns, and spontaneous alternation behaviors compared to SHR

PPI was assessed across four prepulse intensities (Fig. 4A–E). SHR rats showed significantly reduced PPI at 68, 71, and 77 dB compared with WKY rats (p < 0.001, p < 0.05, and p < 0.05; Fig. 4B–D), with no group difference at 82 dB (Fig. 4E).

During the early exploration phase, SHR rats exhibited reduced wall-leaning (WL5E; p < 0.05; Fig. 4F) and rearing behavior (R5E; p < 0.0001; Fig. 4G), whereas grooming duration (G5E) did not differ between groups (Fig. 4H). Reduced rearing persisted across the full session (Supplementary Fig. S4H, S4K). In the Y-maze, SHR rats showed significantly lower spontaneous alternation than WKY controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 4J).

Factor analysis reveals latent behavioral dimensions across strains

To identify latent behavioral dimensions underlying multivariate behavioral performance, we conducted exploratory factor analysis with oblimin rotation on 26 behavioral variables obtained from WKY and SHR rats. Based on eigenvalues and cumulative variance explained, five factors were extracted (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Tables S1–S2).

Multivariate factor analysis reveals five latent behavioral dimensions.

A Heatmap of factor loadings (|loading| ≥ 0.4) for 26 behavioral variables across five factors. B Group comparisons of factor scores between WKY and SHR rats. C Scatterplot of Factor 1 vs Factor 3 scores. D Standardized factor loadings for individual variables. E Inter-factor correlation matrix. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05.

Factor 1 accounted for 26.36% of the total variance and showed strong loadings for variables related to exploratory locomotor activity and task engagement (e.g., OFD10, R10, N10T1, OFI10, P71, WL10, P68, and P77), reflecting an exploratory–attentional engagement dimension. Factor 2 (21.35%) was defined primarily by anxiety- and risk-related measures (VCAT, EPO5, EPCE5, and EPCL5), corresponding to avoidance-related behavioral responses. Factor 3 (11.89%) was dominated by jumping latency in the cliff avoidance test, capturing a dimension related to response disinhibition and impulsivity-like behavior. Factor 4 (13.32%) showed moderate loadings from long-term object recognition measures (ND24, N24IN), indicating a memory-guided exploratory component. Factor 5 (27.08%) loaded selectively on locomotor output during the NOR task (ND10), reflecting task-specific activity levels.

Group comparisons of factor scores revealed that SHR rats exhibited significantly lower scores than WKY rats on Factors 1 and 3 (p < 0.05; Fig. 5B), whereas no significant group differences were observed for Factors 2, 4, or 5. Visualization of Factor 1 versus Factor 3 scores (Fig. 5C) demonstrated partial segregation between strains, with WKY rats clustering toward higher exploratory engagement and lower disinhibition, while SHR rats showed substantially broader dispersion across this behavioral space. The relative contribution of individual behavioral variables to each factor and inter-factor correlations are summarized in Fig. 5D, E, revealing low to moderate correlations and supporting the relative independence of the extracted dimensions.

Consistent with this pattern, Supplementary Fig. 5A illustrates constrained factor score variability in WKY rats compared with markedly broader dispersion in SHR rats. Kernel density estimates further confirmed approximately normal distributions without floor or ceiling effects (Supplementary Fig. 5B), supporting the robustness of the extracted behavioral structure.

Collectively, these findings indicate that the five-factor model captures independent behavioral dimensions, with exploratory–attentional engagement (Factor 1) and behavioral disinhibition (Factor 3) most clearly distinguishing the SHR phenotype.

Neurobiological validation of behaviorally defined SHR subtypes

To determine whether factor-defined behavioral heterogeneity corresponded to distinct neurobiological signatures, animals were classified into three groups—WKY, SHR-Norm, and SHR-Comb—based on their relative positioning along Factors 1 (exploratory–attentional engagement) and 3 (impulsivity-related behavioral disinhibition) (Fig. 6A). Based on this stratification, neurobiological analyses focused on cortico–striatal regions implicated in attentional control and impulse regulation, including the prelimbic (PrL) and infralimbic (IL) subregions of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the dorsomedial striatum (DMS) (Fig. 6B).

Immunofluorescence analyses revealed pronounced alterations in inhibitory interneuron-associated markers in the mPFC of SHR-Comb rats. Representative images from PrL and IL showed reduced parvalbumin (PV) and glutamate decarboxylase 67 (GAD67) signals and decreased PV–GAD67 colocalization in SHR-Comb animals (Fig. 6C, E). Quantitative analyses confirmed significant reductions in PV and GAD67 puncta density and colocalization in both PrL and IL compared with WKY and/or SHR-Norm controls (p < 0.01, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6D, F). Consistent with these regional changes, western blot analyses of bulk mPFC tissue demonstrated reduced PV and GAD67 protein levels with corresponding densitometric decreases (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig. 6G–J), indicating impairment of inhibitory interneuron-related features selectively in the SHR-Comb subgroup.

Given the importance of synaptic organization for prefrontal network function, synaptic markers were next examined in the mPFC. SHR-Comb rats exhibited reduced postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) and synapsin1 puncta density and decreased PSD95–synapsin1 colocalization in PrL and IL regions (p < 0.01, p < 0.001, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6K–N). In contrast, western blot analyses of PSD95 and synapsin1 in bulk mPFC homogenates did not reveal significant group differences (Fig. 6O–R), suggesting that synaptic alterations in SHR-Comb rats are spatially restricted and preferentially detected at the microcircuit level.

Finally, dopaminergic markers were assessed in the striatum. SHR-Comb rats displayed increased dopamine transporter (DAT) immunoreactivity and puncta density (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig. 6S, T), accompanied by elevated DAT protein levels on western blot analysis (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig. 6V, W). In contrast, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunoreactivity and protein expression remained unchanged across groups (Figs. 6U, X, Y). Together, these findings demonstrate that factor-defined behavioral subtypes correspond to distinct alterations in prefrontal inhibitory circuitry, synaptic microstructure, and striatal dopaminergic regulation, providing neurobiological support for the behavioral classification framework.

Contextual behavioral comparison between WKY and sprague–dawley rats

To contextualize the behavioral characteristics of the WKY control strain, selected behavioral measures were compared with those of outbred SD rats. WKY rats showed distinct behavioral features relative to SD rats, indicating that WKY does not represent a neutral behavioral baseline. Detailed results, statistical analyses, and corresponding figures are provided in the Supplementary Results (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3; Supplementary Table S6).

Validation of the behavioral factor structure

The stability and reproducibility of the five-factor solution were evaluated using multiple complementary analytical approaches. The factor structure was re-examined using principal axis factoring. Bootstrap resampling was performed to assess loading stability, and split-sample analyses were conducted to evaluate reproducibility across independent subsamples using promax rotation. Across these approaches, the overall factor architecture and behavioral interpretation were highly consistent with the primary solution. Core behavioral variables showed stable loadings, and factor correspondence across subsamples demonstrated moderate to high congruence. Detailed results of these validation analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Figs. S6–S7; Supplementary Tables S3–S5).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a multidimensional behavioral characterization of SHR in comparison with WKY controls using a comprehensive behavioral test battery combined with exploratory factor analysis. This approach captured both ADHD-relevant group differences and structured within-strain heterogeneity, supporting SHR utility for endophenotype research. As a contextual reference, WKY rats were compared with the commonly used outbred SD strain. WKY rats displayed a relatively low-anxiety and disinhibited behavioral profile, including shorter escape latencies in the cliff avoidance test and increased exploratory engagement. Although informative for strain characterization, this comparison was not a primary focus of the present work.

The central focus of this study was the behavioral comparison between SHR and WKY rats. SHR animals exhibited a distinctive pattern of repetitive and impulsive behaviors consistent with core ADHD-related features. Increased marble displacement and nestlet shredding occurred without a corresponding increase in marble burying, suggesting disinhibited exploratory motor activity rather than compulsivity per se [23, 24]. SHR rats also demonstrated context-dependent impulsivity in the cliff avoidance test, with reduced escape latency in the absence of salient visual cues, while performing comparably to WKY rats in the visual cliff avoidance test. This dissociation parallels cue-dependent inhibitory control deficits reported in ADHD patients [25, 26].

Locomotor activity in SHR rats was not uniformly elevated across contexts. Although SHR rats are often described as hyperactive [27–30], our findings align with evidence that hyperactivity is context dependent and emerges preferentially under novel or emotionally salient conditions [31–33]. Reduced inner zone exploration during the early phase of the open field test, which dissipated over time, suggests transient novelty-related behavioral inhibition that may mask latent hyperactivity.

Anxiety-related behaviors also differed across paradigms, supporting a context-dependent interpretation rather than a uniform alteration in anxiety. Increased thigmotaxis in SHR rats was confined to early open field exploration, whereas elevated plus maze performance likely reflects altered risk assessment during conflict-based decision-making, consistent with prior reports that these tasks probe partially dissociable behavioral dimensions [34, 35].

Cognitive assessments revealed pronounced inter-individual variability in SHR rats. While WKY rats consistently displayed novel object preference, SHR rats showed heterogeneous patterns ranging from novelty preference to familiarity bias or no preference, potentially reflecting deficits in attentional modulation or salience attribution [36, 37]. Consistent with this interpretation, SHR rats also exhibited greater total object investigation time during the short-term recognition test, a behavior that may reflect dysregulated exploratory drive or impulsivity rather than enhanced mnemonic performance.

Executive function impairments were further supported by reduced spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze and diminished exploratory engagement, whereas grooming behavior was unchanged. Impaired prepulse inhibition at low and moderate prepulse intensities additionally indicates deficits in attentional filtering and sensorimotor gating, consistent with ADHD-related findings [38–41].

A major strength of the present study lies in the application of exploratory factor analysis to integrate 26 behavioral measures into latent behavioral dimensions. Five interpretable domains were identified, with exploratory–attentional engagement and behavioral disinhibition most strongly altered in SHR rats. Visualization of individuals within the Factor 1 × Factor 3 space revealed strain-specific behavioral subprofiles resembling the dimensional heterogeneity observed in ADHD [42], whereas anxiety- and memory-related factors showed limited strain-level differentiation.

Multiple complementary analyses following established EFA recommendations [43]—including PAF, bootstrap resampling, and split-sample validation with promax rotation—supported stable loadings for the core dimensions, with narrow confidence intervals for salient items and high Tucker’s congruence coefficients (Supplementary Figs. S6–S7; Supplementary Tables S3–S5). Minor loading variability did not alter overall factor interpretability, supporting its use for subgroup stratification and neurobiological analyses.

Importantly, factor-based stratification enabled direct linkage between multidimensional behavioral phenotypes and neurobiological alterations. Only the combined-deviant subgroup (SHR-Comb), characterized by concurrent abnormalities in exploratory–attentional engagement and disinhibition, exhibited consistent circuit-level changes across prefrontal and striatal regions. In the medial prefrontal cortex, particularly the prelimbic and infralimbic subregions, SHR-Comb animals showed marked reductions in PV and GAD67 puncta density and colocalization, indicating selective disruption of inhibitory interneuron microcircuits. These alterations align with excitation–inhibition imbalance implicated in ADHD pathophysiology [44–48].

Parallel analyses revealed reduced PSD95 and synapsin1 puncta density and colocalization in prefrontal microcircuits of SHR-Comb animals, whereas bulk tissue analyses showed minimal group differences, suggesting spatially restricted synaptic alterations [49].

In the striatum, SHR-Comb rats exhibited increased DAT expression without changes in TH, indicating dysregulated dopamine reuptake rather than dopaminergic neuron loss. Given that DAT is the primary molecular target of first-line ADHD pharmacotherapies, these findings have direct translational relevance [50, 51], consistent with prior reports in SHR models and subsets of ADHD patients [52–55].

Several limitations should be noted. All experiments were conducted in male rats, and sex-dependent differences in SHR behavioral profiles have been reported. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be necessary to determine generalizability. In addition, although SHR rats are a model of hypertension, all assessments were performed during adolescence, prior to the development of sustained hypertension, minimizing vascular confounds.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that data-driven behavioral stratification identifies biologically meaningful SHR subgroups with distinct prefrontal and striatal circuit alterations. Integrating exploratory factor analysis with circuit-level validation enhances the translational relevance of the SHR model and provides a framework for subtype-informed mechanistic and pharmacological studies in ADHD.
