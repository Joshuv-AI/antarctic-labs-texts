Uncharted Waters: Selling a New Product Robustly
Kun Zhang

Abstract
When introducing a novel product, a seller sets a price and decides how much information to provide to a buyer, who may incur a search cost to discover an outside option. The buyer knows the outside option distribution; the seller knows only its mean and bounds. Seeking “robustness,” the seller evaluates strategies based on guaranteed profits, balancing search deterrence against surplus extraction. Providing information can deter search and boost demand but requires offering the buyer a higher payoff via a lower price. Results help explain the variations in information provision among new products and suggest that lower search costs can raise prices and lead to noisier information, potentially harming consumers.



1
Introduction

Rapid technological development has introduced an increasing number of new products. Examples include e-readers, electric vehicles, LED lights, and infrared cookers. Due to their novelty, sellers often have significant control over what buyers can learn about them. In particular, sellers can provide information by offering free trials, product samples, and product descriptions. Buyers, however, can still compare the pricing and features of related existing products, and sellers may face considerable uncertainty about buyers’ knowledge and preferences of their alternatives. For instance, sellers may know little about how buyers acquire or process information, which substitute products they have in mind, and what kinds of stores they have access to. As a result, it is natural for sellers to seek “robustness” by adopting selling strategies that perform reasonably well across all potential scenarios.

In this paper, I examine the following questions: How do robustness concerns shape the seller’s optimal pricing and information provision strategies? Would the buyer be better off if learning about her alternatives became easier? Finally, what insights do the answers to these questions offer regarding the sale of different types of new products?

To address these questions, I study a model where a seller faces a buyer whose match value with the product is either high or low. Although the match value is unknown to both parties, the prior probability of it being high is common knowledge. Along with a posted price, the seller chooses how much information to provide about the match value. The buyer has access to an outside option representing her best alternative to the seller’s new product, the distribution of which is known to her. Observing the price and the information, the buyer updates her beliefs about the match value. She then decides whether to incur a cost to discover the value of the outside option—a process I term “search”—or to buy the seller’s product directly.

The seller is partially informed about the buyer’s outside option distribution, knowing only its mean and bounds. Moreover, she faces difficulty forming beliefs over all possible distributions. To address this uncertainty, the seller adopts a selling strategy that guarantees “robustness,” ensuring a reasonable level of expected profits despite the uncertainty. Specifically, she evaluates each strategy based on its worst-case profits across all outside option distributions consistent with her information.

Two observations help clarify the seller’s robustly optimal strategy. First, since the buyer searches only when her belief about the match value falls below a certain threshold, the seller can deter search and increase the likelihood of purchase by pooling beliefs just above this threshold through information provision. I refer to an information provision policy with this feature as a
deterrence policy
. However, a deterrence policy is not always robust: it is effective only when the price is below a threshold proportional to the search cost, which may limit surplus extraction. This reveals a trade-off between search deterrence and surplus extraction. Second, an information policy that continuously and evenly disperses beliefs provides effective hedging against uncertainty. For any fixed price, it keeps the purchase probability constant across all outside option distributions with the same mean, thereby ensuring robustness.

When search costs are low, the price threshold for effective search deterrence is also low, making deterrence policies unprofitable. In this case, the optimal information provision policy spreads product impressions uniformly, providing effective hedging while allowing for the seller to charge a higher price and thus capturing more surplus. The provided information is noisy: the buyer’s impression is likely neither strongly favorable nor clearly unfavorable.

As the search cost increases, however, the price threshold rises, allowing the seller to charge more while using a deterrence policy effectively. When the search cost is sufficiently high, providing full information—revealing whether the match value is high or low—becomes optimal. The buyer buys without search if the match value is high and otherwise never buys. Intuitively, by informing the buyer of the exact match value, the seller identifies those who find the new product’s innovative features especially appealing and ensures that they buy without search. This strategy helps the seller secure sizable demand while charging a higher price.

For intermediate search costs, an information provision policy that combines the two extremes can be optimal. It sometimes reveals that the match value is high; otherwise, it disperses beliefs evenly through noisy signals. This policy is optimal when the prior probability of a high match value is sufficiently large relative to the mean of the outside option distribution, indicating strong market confidence in the seller’s new product. In such scenarios, the buyer’s expected payoff from purchasing the seller’s product often exceeds the realized outside option value. The seller thus benefits from encouraging the buyer to revisit the product if she chooses to search.

The model produces some interesting comparative statics. Conventional wisdom in search literature suggests that higher search costs make the buyer more likely to buy without search, allowing the seller to charge a higher price. In my model, however, the robust price is generally increasing in search cost but can exhibit a downward jump at a certain point (see
Figure 3
below). This feature arises from the trade-off between search deterrence and surplus extraction. As discussed above, when the search cost is low, the seller avoids deterrence policies and sets a higher price. As the search cost rises, the price threshold for effective search deterrence also increases, making deterrence policies more appealing. When the search cost becomes sufficiently large, the demand advantage makes it profitable for the seller to switch to a deterrence policy even if she must lower the price. Accordingly, the seller may charge a lower price in exchange for effective search deterrence. Additionally, I show that the seller’s information provision policy typically becomes more informative as the search cost increases. Consequently, the comparative statics results suggest that technological advancements reducing search costs could lead to higher prices and noisier information provision for certain new products, potentially harming consumers.

The results have concrete implications for the sale of different kinds of new products. Some are
revolutionary
, such as the iPhone and the 3D printer. Others are
evolutionary
—incremental improvements on existing products—like smart thermostats and energy-saving light bulbs. Still others are
alternatives to existing products
, offering revolutionary features but sacrificing some existing functionality. Portable wireless speakers, for instance, provide greater convenience but at the expense of sound quality. In this context, search cost can be interpreted as a measure of how easily a consumer can identify the best alternative available on the market. For evolutionary products, search costs are typically low because these products differ only marginally from existing ones, making comparisons straightforward. In contrast, alternatives to existing products tend to have higher search costs, as their fundamental differences make it significantly harder for consumers to determine the most suitable outside option.

Consequently, for evolutionary products, noisy information provision is optimal. This could include offering short trial periods, limiting features available in free trials, or providing succinct product descriptions. In contrast, for alternatives to existing products, it is optimal to provide full information, clearly dividing consumers into “lovers” and “haters,” and targeting only the former. Examples of such policies include extended trial periods, money-back guarantees, or hassle-free returns with long return windows. For revolutionary products, the market typically exhibits sufficient confidence. My results thus predict that an optimal information provision strategy would cultivate a group of “die-hard fans,” while other potential consumers receive only noisy signals. Hence, my findings may help explain the wide variety of information provision policies observed among new products.

The implications discussed above rely crucially on two key features of the model: search frictions and the seller’s robustness concerns. Without search frictions, the incentive to pool beliefs vanishes, leaving robustness as the dominant consideration and making it optimal to disperse beliefs evenly. Conversely, if the seller knows the exact outside option distribution—removing robustness concerns—then providing full information is always optimal. Neither of these alternative scenarios yields implications for selling new products as clear-cut as those from the main model.

3.1
Preliminary Observations

To hedge against the adversarial Nature, it is useful for the distribution over posteriors
H
to have an affine segment. To see why, let
\tilde{w}:=\sup\left\{\supp{(H)}\right\}
, and note that if
H
is affine on
\left(p,\tilde{w}\right)
, the probability of eventual purchase satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{G}[1-H(p+\min\{a,v\})]=1-H(p+\mathbb{E}_{G}[\min\{a,v\}])=1-H(p+\xi-s),

(5)

where the first equality holds by affinity, and the second equality follows from the definition of
a
in (
3
) and the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{G}[v]=\xi
:

\mathbb{E}_{G}[\min\{a,v\}]=\mathbb{E}_{G}[v+a-\max\{a,v\}]=\mathbb{E}_{G}[v]-S_{G}(a)=\xi-s.

Therefore, no matter which outside options distribution Nature chooses, the probability of eventual purchase remains the same. Put differently, when
H
is affine on
\left(p,\tilde{w}\right)
, Nature is indifferent between spreading or contracting mass when designing the outside option distribution. This guarantees that there is not a single choice of the outside option distribution that Nature can take significant advantage of, yielding the desired robustness.

An important consequence of this observation is that an optimal distribution places no mass point within the interval
\left(p,\tilde{w}\right)
. However, Seller may have an incentive to pool mass at
\tilde{w}
. Suppose Seller knows the outside option distribution
G
, and hence the reservation value
a
. As long as the price satisfies
p+a<1
, she could benefit from deterring search by putting a mass point at
p+a

w=p+a
, she would buy without search.

In contrast, being robustness-seeking, Seller can only deter search if the sum of the price and the
maximum
reservation value is below one—that is,
p+1-s/\xi\leq 1
, or equivalently,
p\leq s/\xi
. When
p>s/\xi
, any attempt by Seller to deter search by placing an atom at some
w\in[p,1]
can be frustrated by Nature. Specifically, Nature can choose
G
such that
a=w-p+\varepsilon
for an arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0

Buyer’s expected gain from search,
a

w-p
, and thus prompting her to search.

Intuitively, when the price is sufficiently high, Seller anticipates that Nature can always make the outside option attractive enough to induce search, regardless of how optimistic Buyer’s prior is or what information is provided. On the other hand, when
p\leq s/\xi
, placing a mass point at
w=p+1-s/\xi
can be helpful in deterring search. Therefore, in an optimal distribution, there is no mass point on
[p,1]
when
p>s/\xi

[p,1]
when
p\leq s/\xi
is at
w=p+1-s/\xi

3.2
Robustly Optimal Selling Strategy

As hinted previously, Seller’s robustly optimal strategy is derived in two steps. First, I identify the robust information provision policy at each fixed price by solving a zero-sum game between Seller, who chooses an information policy to maximize revenue, and Nature, who selects an outside option distribution to minimize it. 3.2.1
Two Benchmarks

The two key features of the main model are search frictions and Seller’s robustness concerns. To clarify the role each plays in the main results, I consider two variations of the model, each retaining only one of these features.

The Role of Search Frictions: Zero Search Cost.

When search frictions are absent, the search deterrence motive disappears, making a “mass point at the top” never optimal. Since the hedging motive remains, the robustly optimal selling strategy is isomorphic to the low-search-cost case in the main model; this is formalized in

4
Discussion

In this paper, I studied the strategies of a seller launching a novel product, who sets a price and chooses how much information to provide about the product, while facing uncertainty about consumers’ outside options and seeking robustness against this uncertainty. I uncovered a novel trade-off between deterring consumer search through information provision and extracting surplus. The results illuminate the variety of price–information combinations observed in new products, with clear implications for evolutionary products, revolutionary products, and alternatives to existing products. Moreover, comparative statics results suggest that reducing search costs need not benefit consumers.

I conclude by discussing a few key modeling assumptions.

Buyer’s anonymity.

Buyer’s identity is assumed to be anonymous: Seller cannot tell whether Buyer is visiting for the first time or coming back from search. As a result, the price cannot be conditioned on Buyer’s identity. This assumption serves two purposes: (a) to focus on how search frictions and Seller’s robustness concerns influence the initial “price tag” at the launch of a new product, and (b) to examine how information provision can help deter search without resorting to price-based search deterrence tactics.
Supplementary Appendix
C.1 details what happens if Buyer’s identity is recognizable to Seller, enabling Seller to make exploding offers or engage in intertemporal price discrimination.

Deterministic prices.

I assume that Seller posts a single price. While this assumption can be relaxed to allow for random pricing, I focus on deterministic prices to better isolate the roles of the two instruments—price and information—and to obtain sharper comparative statics results.
Supplementary Appendix
C.2.1 explains how random prices can be incorporated into the model without challenging the main takeaways.

“Safe” outside option.

An implicit assumption of the model is that Buyer does not have a “safe” outside option that can be consumed without incurring a search cost. Equivalently, one may interpret this as Buyer’s “safe” outside option
u_{0}
being less than or equal to zero. This assumption is introduced solely to simplify exposition. As explained in
Supplementary Appendix
C.2.2, the results remain qualitatively the same when allowing for a “safe” outside option
u_{0}>0
.

Binary match value.

Buyer’s match value with Seller’s new product,
x
, is assumed to take one of two values: 0 or 1. One can interpret
x=1
as Buyer liking the product, and
x=0
as not liking it. While this assumption may seem somewhat extreme, it provides substantial tractability without generating unrealistic results.
Supplementary Appendix
C.2.3 discusses the added complexity of assuming the match value to be continuously distributed, and shows that some key qualitative insights remain robust. For example, the tension between search deterrence and surplus extraction persists, which begets a similar nonmonotonicity of price in the search cost. Moreover, the optimal distribution is affine on a subset of its support in many cases.

Seller’s knowledge of the search cost and the outside option distribution.

I assume that Seller has precise knowledge of Buyer’s search cost; this assumption is not as stringent as it may seem. An equivalent assumption is that Seller faces unquantifiable uncertainty regarding the search cost
s
and only knows that
s\geq\underaccent{\bar}{s}
. Seller therefore acts as if
s=\underaccent{\bar}{s}
when maximizing her revenue guarantee.

Although standard in the literature, the assumption I make regarding Seller’s knowledge of Buyer’s outside-option distribution is specific: Seller knows that the distribution’s support lies within the unit interval
[0,1]
, and that its mean is
\xi
.
20
20

20

One microfoundation for this assumption is that Seller faces nonprobabilistic uncertainty regarding Buyer’s information acquisition technology; see
Wolitzky 2016
, Section 5.2 for details.
Similar to the discussion on search cost above, it is equivalent to assume that Seller only knows that the mean falls within an interval
[\underaccent{\bar}{\xi},\bar{\xi}]
, in which case Nature would always select a distribution with the highest possible mean.

Observe that the upper bound (1) and the lower bound (0) of the support of Buyer’s outside option distribution coincide with the (normalized gross) value of the new product to those who like it and those who do not, respectively. This assumption is reasonable: if a new product is a good match, it is likely to provide a higher value (gross of price) to Buyer than any alternative. It is also unlikely that Buyer’s best alternative would have a strictly negative value. That said,
Supplementary Appendix
C.2.4 shows that minor adjustments to these bounds—or replacing them with a higher-order moment—leave the qualitative insights intact.

I first establish two preliminary results,

Claim 1

Assume

.

Proof of
Claim 1
.

Consider any price
p
with
p>s/\xi
. Seller’s revenue guarantee given

can be calculated using

\Phi(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)&\text{if }p<2\mu-1,\\
p\left[1-\frac{\xi-s}{2(\mu-p)}\right]&\text{if }\mu-t>p\geq 2\mu-1,\\
\frac{p\mu}{\sqrt{(\xi-s)(\xi-s+2p)}+(\xi-s+p)}&\text{if }1-\sqrt{2\left(\xi-s\right)}>p\geq\max\{\mu-t,2\mu-1\},\\
\frac{2\mu}{1+p}\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)&\text{if }p\geq 1-\sqrt{2\left(\xi-s\right)}\text{ and }p\geq 2\mu-1,\end{array}\right.

where

t:=\xi-s+\frac{\sqrt{(\xi-s)(\xi-s+8\mu)}}{4}>0.

It can be checked that
\Phi(p)
is concave and piecewise continuously differentiable. If
\mu-t>p>2\mu-1
, or
1-\sqrt{2\left(\xi-s\right)}>p>\max\{\mu-t,2\mu-1\}
,
\Phi^{\prime}(p)
is strictly positive, which implies that these prices yield strictly less revenue guarantee than
p=1-\sqrt{2\left(\xi-s\right)}
. Consequently, an optimal price cannot lie in these regions; thus, optimal prices can be solved by maximizing

\widetilde{\Phi}(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)&\text{if }p<2\mu-1,\\
\frac{2\mu p}{1+p}\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)&\text{if }p\geq 2\mu-1;\end{array}\right.

note that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is continuous at
p=2\mu-1
. To solve this problem, I first solve

\max_{p}~p\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)\quad\text{subject to }~0\leq p\leq 2\mu-1;

standard Lagrangian approach shows that the solution is

p_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-\sqrt{\xi-s}&\text{if }\mu\geq\bar{\mu},\\
2\mu-1&\text{if }\mu<\bar{\mu}.\end{array}\right.

Similarly, the solution to the problem

\max_{p}~\frac{2\mu p}{1+p}\left(1-\frac{\xi-s}{1-p}\right)\quad\text{subject to }~2\mu-1\leq p\leq 1,

is

p_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}}}{1-\xi+s}&\text{if }\mu\leq\underaccent{\bar}{\mu},\\
2\mu-1&\text{if }\mu>\underaccent{\bar}{\mu}.\end{array}\right.

Thus, the optimal robust price when
p>s/\xi
,
p^{*}
, is given by (
6
). The other statements in
Claim 1
then follow from
Proposition 1
and (
6
).
∎

Claim 2

Assume

\Pi=\frac{s}{\xi}-\frac{2s\xi\left(1-\mu\right)}{\xi-s}.

H_{\bar{w}}(w\mid p^{**})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-\frac{2\mu}{\bar{w}+p^{**}}&w\in[0,p^{**}),\\

When

When

The objective is strictly concave in

I claim that, however,

A.2.3
Final Step

It now remains to compare Seller’s revenue guarantee from the above two cases, namely
p>s/\xi
and
p\leq s/\xi
. To that end, for fixed
\xi\in(0,1)
and
s\in(0,\xi)
, define

D=\left\{\mu\in[0,1]:\mu s/\xi\geq\Pi_{h}\right\},

and

N=\left\{\mu\geq\frac{1+s/\xi}{2}:\frac{s}{\xi}-\frac{2s\xi\left(1-\mu\right)}{\xi-s}\geq\Pi_{h}\right\},

where
\Pi_{h}
is the revenue guarantee for the case of
p>s/\xi
defined in (
13
).
D
and
N
are the sets of priors that the revenue guarantees from
H^{*}_{b}
and
H^{*}_{h}
, respectively, exceed
\Pi_{h}
.

Note that
D
is empty if and only if
\mu s/\xi\big|_{\mu=1}<\Pi_{h}\big|_{\mu=1}
, namely
s/\xi\ <(1-\sqrt{\xi-s})^{2}
; rearrange,

s<\frac{\xi\left(\xi-1\right)^{2}}{\left(\xi+1\right)^{2}}=B_{2}(\xi).

(22)

Moreover, algebra reveals that
N
is empty if and only if
D
is empty. Thus, focusing on buy-later demand is optimal if (
22
) holds. If instead (
22
) does not hold, define

\hat{\mu}=\inf D,\text{ and }\check{\mu}=\inf N.

Because
\mu s/\xi
is linear in
\mu
, and
\Pi_{h}
is also linear for low
\mu
,
\hat{\mu}=0
if and only if the slope of the linear part of
\Pi_{h}
is less than or equal to
s/\xi
, namely

\frac{s}{\xi}\geq 1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}},

(23)

or equivalently

s\geq\frac{\xi(\xi-1)^{2}}{\xi^{2}+1}=B_{1}(\xi).

(24)

If (
24
) holds,
(p^{**},H_{b}^{*})
is optimal for all
\mu\in[0,1]
; in other words, maximally deterring search is optimal. If both (
22
) and (
24
) do not hold, it must be that
B_{2}(\xi)\leq s<B_{1}(\xi)
, and
\hat{\mu},\check{\mu}\in(0,1]
.
In this case,
Claim 2
suggests that
(p^{**},H_{u}^{*})
dominates
(p^{**},H_{b}^{*})
if and only if
s<\xi-2\xi^{2}=B_{3}(\xi)
.
Thus, if further
B_{3}(\xi)\leq s<B_{1}(\xi)
, there exists a cutoff
\hat{\mu}
such that focusing on buy-later demand is optimal whenever
\mu\leq\hat{\mu}
, and maximally deter search is optimal otherwise. If instead
B_{2}(\xi)<s<B_{3}(\xi)
, there exists
\check{\mu}\in(0,1)
such that for
\mu<\check{\mu}
, Seller focuses on buy-later demand; and for
\mu\geq\check{\mu}
, Seller optimally balances between buy-now and buy-later demand.

It only remains to verify that, whenever
p^{*}
is the robust price, it indeed satisfies
p^{*}>s/\xi
. To see this, observe that for
(p^{*},H)
in
Claim 1
to be optimal for some
\mu\in(0,1)
and

, it must be that

\frac{s}{\xi}<1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}}<\frac{1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}}}{1-\xi+s}<1-\sqrt{\xi-s};

where the first inequality holds because a necessary condition for
p^{*}
to be optimal is that (

is always optimal; the second inequality follows because

, and the third follows by algebra. Consequently, both
p^{*}=(1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}})/(1-\xi+s)
and

satisfy
p^{*}>s/\xi
. Furthermore,
p^{*}=2\mu-1
is optimal only if it is strictly above
(1-\sqrt{2(\xi-s)-(\xi-s)^{2}})/(1-\xi+s)
. Therefore, whenever
p^{*}
is optimal, it must be that
p^{*}>s/\xi
. This completes the proof.
