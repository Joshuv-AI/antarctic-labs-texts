Intraoperative Mild Hypothermia Alleviate Acute Postoperative Pain Through Attenuating Sufentanil Metabolism: A Prospective Cohort Study with Experimental Validation
Authors: Yiqi Feng, Yanxia Zhang, Xu Tang, Li Zeng, Qian Fang, Jia Tang, He Huang, Guangyou Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInadvertent perioperative hypothermia is common during anesthesia, but its association with acute postoperative pain remains unclear. We investigated whether the cumulative intraoperative temperature exposure below 36°C was associated with postoperative pain after laparoscopic surgery and explored a potential mechanism involving sufentanil disposition and hepatic CYP3A activity.MethodsThis single-center prospective observational cohort, included 229 adults undergoing elective laparoscopic abdominal surgery under general anesthesia with continuous tympanic temperature monitoring. The primary clinical endpoint was moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, defined as any numerical rating scale (NRS) score ≥4 within 24 h after surgery. Temperature exposure was summarized as the percentage of the recorded intraoperative period spent below 36°C (percentage of hypothermia time, POH), with POH ≥30% evaluated as the main exposure threshold. Complementary experiments used a mouse plantar-incision model to assess mechanical withdrawal thresholds, plasma sufentanil pharmacokinetics, and hepatic CYP3A catalytic activity under controlled lower-temperature and normothermic conditions.ResultsIn the clinical cohort, 76 of 229 patients (33.2%) developed NRS ≥4 pain within 24 h. POH ≥30% was independently associated with lower odds of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain (adjusted odds ratio, 0.23; 95% confidence interval, 0.08–0.65; P=0.005), lower early postoperative NRS scores, and less rescue analgesics use. Temperatures <35°C was uncommon, and perioperative complications did not differ between groups. In mice, lower body temperature (32.26±0.48°C) prolonged sufentanil analgesia compared with normothermia (37.32±0.32°C), increased early plasma sufentanil exposure, prolonged elimination half-life, reduced clearance, and decreased hepatic CYP3A catalytic activity.ConclusionGreater cumulative exposure to mild intraoperative hypothermia was associated with less acute postoperative pain and lower rescue analgesic use after laparoscopic surgery. Experimental findings suggest that lower temperature may prolong sufentanil exposure by suppressing CYP3A-mediated metabolism. These findings do not support inducing hypothermia but indicate that intraoperative temperature may influence opioid pharmacokinetics and should be considered when integrating thermal management with perioperative analgesic strategy.

Introduction

More than 300 million surgical procedures are performed worldwide each year.1 Despite guideline-based multimodal analgesia, acute postoperative pain remains frequent and clinically consequential.2 Recent clinical studies have reinforced the importance of procedure-tailored multimodal strategies, including intrathecal opioid dosing and ERAS-based analgesia pathways in abdominal or orthopedic surgery.3,4 In a recent population-based study in China, almost half of surgical patients reported moderate-to-severe pain within the first 24 h after surgery.5 Poorly controlled acute pain delays functional recovery, increases opioid exposure, may contribute to persistent postsurgical pain, and adds to healthcare costs.6,7 Identifying perioperative factors that reproducibly modify early pain trajectories therefore remains a priority for anesthesiology and perioperative medicine.

Postoperative pain is not determined by surgical injury alone. Patient characteristics, psychological vulnerability, type of surgery, regional anesthesia, intraoperative opioid exposure, and postoperative analgesic access all contribute to pain intensity and analgesic consumption.8 Recent trials of thoracic fascial plane and serratus anterior plane techniques also illustrate the continuing shift toward opioid-sparing, procedure-tailored analgesia.9,10

Core body temperature is another universal perioperative variable, but it has rarely been examined as a dynamic modifier of analgesic response. Inadvertent perioperative hypothermia, commonly defined as a core temperature <36°C, occurs frequently during non-cardiac surgery because anesthesia impairs thermoregulatory vasoconstriction and promotes redistribution of heat from core to peripheral compartments.11,12 Hypothermia has been linked to bleeding, wound infection, shivering, delayed recovery, and other adverse outcomes.13–15 There are also plausible physiological association between body temperature and nociception. Under controlled experimental conditions, human pain sensitivity exhibits a pronounced circadian rhythm that is approximately opposite in phase to the rhythm of core body temperature.16 However, evidence relating temperature management to pain is inconsistent. Some studies evaluating perioperative warming interventions have reported lower pain scores or analgesic use, whereas meta-analyses and randomized trials have found little or no effect on pain outcomes.17–20 The large PROTECT trial also challenged the assumption that aggressive intraoperative warming necessarily improves major postoperative outcomes compared with routine thermal management.21 These discrepancies may reflect heterogeneity in surgery type, analgesic regimens, temperature monitoring sites, and the tendency to classify patients by single temperature values rather than cumulative temperature exposure. This gap is clinically relevant because perioperative temperature and analgesic requirements evolve concurrently during perioperative care and are managed simultaneously by anesthesiologists, yet the two domains are usually studied separately.

A pharmacological mechanism provides a plausible link between intraoperative temperature and postoperative pain. Fentanyl-family opioids are substantially cleared through hepatic metabolism, and human liver microsome studies identify CYP3A4 as a major enzyme responsible for fentanyl and sufentanil N-dealkylation.22 Experimental hypothermia can reduce fentanyl and midazolam clearance through altered CYP3A metabolic capacity.23,24 We therefore hypothesized that patients with greater cumulative exposure to mild intraoperative hypothermia would experience less early postoperative pain, partly because lower temperature prolongs sufentanil exposure. To test this hypothesis, we combined a prospective cohort study with continuous intraoperative temperature monitoring and complementary mouse experiments examining sufentanil pharmacokinetics and hepatic CYP3A activity.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Ethics

This single-center prospective observational study is reported with reference to the STROBE statement for cohort studies.25 The study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval ID: 2022–03). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov before patient recruitment (NCT05681091), and all participants provided written informed consent. The primary outcome was moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, defined as an NRS score ≥4, with an expected incidence of approximately 40% based on previous studies and our preliminary observations. The primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression to identify potential predictors of NRS ≥4. Assuming at least 10 outcome events per candidate predictor, eight candidate predictors required at least 80 outcome events. Accordingly, 200 evaluable patients were required (80/0.40 = 200). To account for potential dropout or incomplete data, the target sample size was increased by 20% to 240 patients. Between October 2022 and July 2023, adult patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic abdominal surgery under general anesthesia were assessed according to predefined criteria (Figure 1A). Eligible patients were 18–70 years old, had an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of I–III, and agreed to participate. Patients were excluded for infection or fever, chronic pain or preoperative opioid use, external auditory canal anatomy or previous ear surgery precluding tympanic temperature monitoring, cognitive or psychiatric conditions that could impair protocol adherence, substance abuse, or deliberate intraoperative cooling/protective hypothermia. Anesthesia and Analgesia Procedures

The clinical workflow is summarized in Figure 1B. Patients were enrolled consecutively according to the operative schedule. A standard preoperative visit was completed on the day before surgery. On arrival in the pre-anesthesia area, routine monitoring was initiated, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, heart rate, and non-invasive or invasive blood pressure as clinically indicated. Tympanic temperature was monitored continuously from operating-room entry until operating-room exit. General anesthesia was induced with midazolam (0.04–0.1 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 μg/kg), propofol (1–2.5 mg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6–1 mg/kg). After tracheal intubation or laryngeal-mask placement, anesthesia was maintained with remifentanil (0.15 μg/kg/min), sevoflurane (1%), and propofol (1–3 mg/kg/h), targeting a bispectral index of 40–60. Additional rocuronium was administered according to surgical requirements. Perioperative temperature management followed routine institutional clinical practice and was not altered by study participation. Standard warming measures were provided to all patients, with active warming initiated or intensified when clinically indicated according to core temperature and its temporal trend. No additional study-specific warming or cooling intervention was assigned.

After surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for emergence and airway-device removal. Postoperative analgesia during the first 24 h was individualized according to patient preference, analgesic requirements, and surgical indications. PCIA was optional, and rescue analgesia was provided as needed regardless of PCIA use. In the PACU, rescue analgesia included incisional infiltration or regional block with ropivacaine 150 mg; a small number of patients also received intravenous tramadol 100 mg or flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg. After transfer to the surgical ward, intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg, dezocine 20 mg, or eptazocine 15 mg was used during postoperative 0–6 h. During 6–24 h, rescue analgesia consisted of intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg or oral ibuprofen and codeine phosphate tablets, including in patients receiving PCIA. When discharge criteria were met (Steward score ≥4), the forearm pressure pain threshold (PPT-T1) was measured. Pain intensity was assessed using the NRS in the PACU and during three postoperative windows: 0–6 h, 6–12 h, and 12–24 h after surgery to capture the early postoperative pain trajectory within the prespecified 24-h endpoint window, rather than to assume persistent analgesia from a single intraoperative sufentanil dose.

Pressure Pain Threshold Measurement

Forearm pressure pain threshold was measured at two time points: after completion of invasive procedures before surgery (PPT-T0) and at PACU discharge (PPT-T1). Measurements were performed on the lateral brachioradialis of the arm contralateral to the puncture site. Three points were marked at 3.0, 4.5, and 6.0 cm from the elbow joint. The algometer probe was applied vertically, and pressure was increased at approximately 0.3 kg/s, with a maximum of 5 kg. The lowest pressure at which the patient first reported pain was recorded.

Core Temperature Measurement

Core temperature was measured continuously using a wireless tympanic temperature monitoring system (PTC-101, Harbin Boyun Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The probe was positioned in the ear canal and recorded tympanic temperature from operating-room entry to operating-room exit. Hypothermia was defined as core temperature <36°C. For each patient, duration of hypothermia (DOH) and percentage of hypothermia time (POH; cumulative time <36°C divided by the recorded intraoperative period) were calculated. Exposure to lower temperature was additionally described as time spent <35°C.

Clinical Outcomes

The primary clinical endpoint was moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, defined as any NRS score ≥4 during the first 24 h after surgery. Secondary outcomes included time-specific NRS scores, cumulative 24-h NRS score, rescue analgesic use within 24 h, PACU forearm PPT and PPT ratio (PPT-T1/PPT-T0), intraoperative analgesic and anesthetic doses, blood loss, PACU length of stay, postoperative complications, and postoperative length of hospital stay. Demographic and operative variables included age, sex, body mass index, ASA physical status, surgical category, duration of surgery, peripheral nerve block, and use of patient-controlled intravenous analgesia.

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and acclimatized for 1 week in a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. For surgical procedures, mice were anesthetized with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane by inhalation. At the designated experimental endpoints, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation. Briefly, mice were placed in a euthanasia chamber initially containing room air, and 100% CO2 was gradually introduced at a displacement rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute. Anesthesia and euthanasia procedures were performed in accordance with applicable institutional veterinary guidelines and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-SAHCQMU-2025-0079), conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and reported with reference to ARRIVE 2.0 recommendations.26

The Von Frey mechanical withdrawal threshold after surgery was defined as the primary behavioral outcome. The group size was set at n = 8 mice per group. This sample size was estimated using G Power [version 3.127], and was further determined based on pilot data from model stability, and the repeated-measures study design. The type I and type II error rates were set at 5% and 20%, respectively.

Before the start of the experiment, an investigator who was not involved in the experimental procedures or statistical analysis assigned mice to the Sham+Vehicle, PINC+Vehicle, LT+PINC+Vehicle, LT+Suf+PINC, and Suf+PINC groups using a randomization program. Investigators involved in the experiments were blinded to the allocation information until data collection and the primary analyses had been completed. None of the animals were excluded from the study.

Plantar Incision Surgery

Plantar incision surgery was performed as previously described.28 Mice were anesthetized with 1.5–2% isoflurane, and the plantar surface of the right hind paw was sterilized. A 0.5-cm longitudinal incision was made through the skin and fascia. The plantar muscle was elevated with curved forceps and incised longitudinally 10 times without damaging the nerves. The wound was closed with 7–0 nylon suture, disinfected, and the mouse was returned to its cage for recovery.

Body-Temperature Control and Monitoring

After induction of anesthesia with 1.5–2% isoflurane, mice were placed prone on a temperature-controlled blanket. Rectal temperature was monitored with a thermocouple electrode (UNI-T, China) and recorded every 10 min. Target rectal temperature was maintained at 32±0.5°C in the lower-temperature group and 37±0.5°C in the normothermia group during the 1-h temperature-control period.

Von Frey Test

Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated with von Frey filaments at 2, 6, 10, and 24 h after surgery. Mice were placed individually in transparent chambers on a wire-mesh platform and allowed to acclimatize for at least 30 min. Filaments (0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4, 2.0, and 4.0 g; Aesthesio, Danmic) were applied to the plantar surface using the up-down method. Testing started with the 0.6-g filament; a negative response led to the next higher filament and a positive response to the next lower filament. Four additional stimuli were applied after the first positive response, and the withdrawal threshold was calculated accordingly.

Measurement of Plasma Sufentanil Concentration

Tail-vein catheterization was performed before anesthesia. After rectal temperature was stabilized at approximately 32°C or 37°C, sufentanil 5 μg/kg was administered intravenously. Whole blood (80–100 μL) was collected from the tail vein before sufentanil injection and at 5 min, 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, and 4 h after injection. Samples were centrifuged at 1600 g for 15 min at 4°C, and plasma was transferred into EDTA-containing tubes. Plasma sufentanil concentrations were measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS).

Isolation of Liver Microsomes

Liver microsomes were isolated from C57 mice using a modified calcium precipitation method. The tissue was homogenized in an ice-cold homogenization buffer consisting of 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors.

The resulting homogenate underwent differential centrifugation at 4°C, starting with a low-speed run at 3,000×g for 15 min to remove cellular debris and nuclei. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min to pellet the mitochondrial and lysosomal fractions. Calcium chloride was slowly added to the final supernatant to achieve a concentration of 8 mM and the mixture was incubated on ice for 15 min. Microsomes were pelleted by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 20 min and further purified via ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 60 min. Final microsomal pellets were resuspended in homogenization buffer (without EDTA). Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method.

Hepatic CYP3A Catalytic Activity Assay

Hepatic CYP3A catalytic activity was assessed in liver microsomes using a fluorometric CYP3A4 activity assay kit (ab211076, Abcam). Because the assay uses a CYP3A substrate system but was applied to mouse liver microsomes, results are reported as CYP3A catalytic activity rather than direct human CYP3A4 expression. Parallel reactions were performed with and without 30 μM ketoconazole, a CYP3A inhibitor. Reaction mixtures were pre-incubated at 37°C for 10–15 min before addition of the substrate/NADP+ mixture. Fluorescence was monitored for 45 min at excitation/emission wavelengths of 535/587 nm. Specific activity was calculated from the linear slope after subtraction of inhibited residual activity and expressed as pmol/min/mg protein using a resorufin standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range), according to distribution; categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons used the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Repeated measurements were analyzed with repeated-measures analysis of variance or the corresponding non-parametric approach when assumptions were not met. Univariable logistic regression was used to examine candidate predictors of NRS ≥4 pain, including demographic, surgical, analgesic, and temperature-exposure variables. Variables with a univariable P value <0.05 were considered for inclusion in the initial multivariable model. POH ≥30%, the primary exposure, was included in all multivariable logistic regression models irrespective of its univariable significance, and its independent association with NRS ≥4 pain was estimated after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and are interpreted as consistency checks rather than definitive interaction tests. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

When hypothermia was evaluated by absolute duration, thresholds of 0, 15, 30, or 60 min did not clearly discriminate patients with NRS ≥4 (Figure 2G). Discussion

In this prospective observational study of adults undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery, greater cumulative exposure to mild intraoperative hypothermia, expressed as POH ≥30%, was independently associated with a lower odds of moderate-to-severe pain during the first 24 h after surgery. The association was accompanied by lower early postoperative NRS scores, less frequent rescue analgesic use, and a higher PACU forearm PPT ratio. Complementary mouse experiments showed that lower body temperature prolonged sufentanil analgesia, increased plasma sufentanil exposure, reduced clearance, and decreased hepatic CYP3A catalytic activity. Together, these findings support the concept that intraoperative temperature is not merely a safety parameter but may also modify opioid pharmacokinetics and early analgesic response (Figure 7). The present clinical findings help reconcile conflicting literature on perioperative warming and postoperative pain. Trials of active warming have varied widely in surgical population, analgesic protocol, monitoring site, and target temperature, and systematic reviews have not demonstrated a consistent analgesic benefit from maintaining normothermia.17,18 Studies in laparoscopic and gynecological surgery have similarly reported mixed effects on postoperative pain and opioid consumption.19,20 A key distinction of our study is that temperature was assessed as a cumulative exposure rather than by a single minimum value or a device-based intervention. The percentage of hypothermia time integrates both whether hypothermia occurred and how long it persisted relative to the total monitoring period, and therefore may provide a more representative measure of perioperative temperature maintenance than a single temperature value or a binary definition of hypothermia.29 This distinction may also help explain why POH ≥30% remained independently associated with postoperative pain after multivariable adjustment, whereas the occurrence of hypothermia did not. A single minimum temperature reflects only the lowest temperature reached and does not capture the duration of exposure, while a binary hypothermia variable records whether the temperature ever fell below 36°C but provides no information on the persistence of that exposure. Absolute hypothermia duration incorporates a temporal component, but it is inherently influenced by the duration of surgery and temperature monitoring. Because surgical duration itself may be associated with postoperative pain, absolute hypothermia duration may partly reflect differences in operative exposure rather than thermal exposure alone. In contrast, expressing hypothermia duration as a proportion of the total monitoring period reduces the influence of differences in observation time and may better characterize the relative burden of perioperative hypothermia. These findings suggest that the proportion of time spent in a hypothermic state may be more informative than the presence of hypothermia alone when examining the association between perioperative temperature maintenance and postoperative pain.

The experimental data provide a plausible pharmacokinetic explanation for the clinical association. Sufentanil and related fentanyl-family opioids undergo hepatic N-dealkylation mediated substantially by CYP3A4 in humans.22 A previous clinical study reported that perioperative hypothermia was associated with reduced opioid requirements.30 Hostler et al further demonstrated that reductions in human core temperature can alter CYP3A4/5-mediated drug metabolism; specifically, a 1°C decrease from 36.5°C was associated with an 11.1% reduction in CYP3A4/5-mediated midazolam clearance.31 Consistent with these observations, preclinical and translational studies have shown that hypothermia can suppress CYP3A-mediated metabolism, thereby increasing fentanyl exposure.23,24,32 In our mouse model, lower body temperature did not independently reduce incision-evoked mechanical hypersensitivity in the absence of sufentanil, but significantly prolonged sufentanil-induced analgesia. Pharmacokinetic analysis further demonstrated reduced sufentanil clearance (CL) and a prolonged elimination half-life (t1/2) at lower body temperature, accompanied by decreased hepatic CYP3A catalytic activity. These findings strengthen the interpretation that temperature-related changes in opioid disposition, rather than a direct anti-inflammatory or antinociceptive effect of cooling alone, may contribute to lower early postoperative pain.

The clinical implication is not that hypothermia should be deliberately induced. Perioperative hypothermia is a recognized risk factor for bleeding, infection, shivering, and delayed recovery, and current perioperative practice appropriately emphasizes prevention of clinically meaningful hypothermia.12–14 Rather, our findings suggest that naturally occurring mild hypothermia may modify opioid exposure and analgesic effect, especially when it occupies a substantial proportion of the operation. This has practical relevance for opioid titration, rescue analgesic planning, emergence monitoring, and the interpretation of pain outcomes in temperature-management studies. The absence of increased complications in this cohort is reassuring but cannot establish the safety of targeting POH ≥30%, particularly because exposure to temperatures <35°C was rare.

This study has several strengths. The clinical component was prospective, used continuous core-temperature monitoring, and assessed pain repeatedly during the first 24 h after surgery. The exposure metric incorporated the temporal burden of hypothermia rather than relying on a single temperature value. The translational component tested a mechanistically specific hypothesis by combining a plantar-incision pain model, serial sufentanil concentration measurement, pharmacokinetic indices, and hepatic CYP3A activity assessment.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the clinical study was single-center, observational, and restricted to laparoscopic abdominal surgery. Differences in postoperative pain between gastrointestinal and gynecological procedures may partly reflect variations in surgical manipulation, tissue injury, and visceral nociceptive input. Although surgical type and PCIA use were included in the multivariable model and were not independently associated with NRS ≥4, residual confounding related to procedural heterogeneity and individualized postoperative analgesic management cannot be completely excluded. Second, the POH ≥30% threshold was derived from the observed temperature-pain relationship and requires external validation before clinical use. Although POH ≥30% was not significantly associated with the postoperative adverse events evaluated in the univariable analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of unadjusted confounding and the limited number of adverse events. Further studies are needed to determine whether any potential analgesic benefit associated with slower opioid metabolism outweighs the risks of mild perioperative hypothermia before any change in current temperature-management practice can be considered. Third, although tympanic temperature provides a clinically practical surrogate of core body temperature, it does not directly reflect hepatic temperature. Therefore, the relationship between systemic hypothermia and hepatic thermal changes could not be determined in this study. In addition, plasma sufentanil concentrations were not measured. Fourth, the animal temperature target (approximately 32°C) was lower than the mild hypothermia range observed clinically, and mouse hepatic CYP3A activity is not identical to human CYP3A4 activity. Fifth, considering technical difficulty of repeated serial tail-vein blood sampling under strict body-temperature control and the need to minimize animal use and procedural burden, the plasma sufentanil pharmacokinetic experiment was conducted with a limited number of independent animals (n = 3 per group). Thus, this related finding should be interpreted as exploratory and supportive rather than definitive stand-alone statistical evidence. In addition, the preclinical experiments focused on sufentanil; other opioids, non-opioid analgesics, and sex-specific effects were not evaluated. Future studies should validate POH-based exposure metrics in multicenter cohorts, incorporate clinical opioid pharmacokinetic sampling, and test whether temperature modifies analgesic response across different opioid and multimodal analgesic regimens.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a greater proportion of operative time spent below 36°C was associated with less moderate-to-severe acute postoperative pain and reduced rescue analgesic use after laparoscopic surgery. Experimental data suggest that lower temperature may prolong sufentanil exposure through reduced hepatic CYP3A-mediated metabolism. These findings identify intraoperative temperature as a potential modifier of opioid pharmacokinetics and analgesic response, but they require validation before any change in temperature-management targets can be recommended.
