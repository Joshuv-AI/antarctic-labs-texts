Perspectives and practices of counselling, consent and debriefing for cesarean sections: a mixed-methods study in two Indian states
Authors: Samiksha Singh, Shamayita Das, Sharmada Sivaram, Loveday Penn-Kekana, Louise-Tina Day, Manoj Pal, Ajay Kumar Khera, Dhanyakumar BN, Arpit Sinha, Akash Porwal, Karen Levin, Renae Stafford, Vandana Tripathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective counselling, consent, and debriefing (CCD) are essential for respectful, quality cesarean section (CS) services but limited evidence exists. We explored providers and clients’ perceptions and practices regarding CCD for CS in India.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted in seven purposively selected secondary level hospitals (five public, two private) across two states. Key-informant interviews and in-depth interviews (N = 108) with providers, women and their relatives, and community workers were conducted in all seven hospitals. Focused ethnography and record reviews of case sheets (n = 797) were conducted in only four public hospitals. Focus group discussions (n = 19) were conducted in the community. Thematic analyses of qualitative data and descriptive analyses of record reviews were conducted.ResultsProviders understood the purpose of universally practiced counselling. However, this was only unidirectional information sharing of indication and necessity of CS. Clients were not actively engaged: either providers were inaccessible, or clients were hesitant to ask questions. Both providers and clients considered consent primarily useful for protection against any litigation against the provider. Consent process was limited to obtaining signatures. The primary consenters were husbands or in-laws/parents, however, husbands/male relatives were not engaged until the stage of obtaining signatures. Women’s signatures were mandatory in Karnataka. Public hospitals had standard short one-page consent forms in English, while private hospitals had detailed forms in local languages. The term debriefing was not known or used, and concerned practice was rarely observed.Variation in quality of CCD practices was observed with providers’ attitudes and communication skills, workloads, facility norms, and availability of resources. For clients, cultural context, lower socio-economic status, lack of obstetric awareness, limited understanding of their shared decision-making rights, reluctance to question providers, and women’s poor agency negatively affected the CCD process. Mutual lack of trust, poor communication, and low felt need were other barriers.ConclusionsCounselling and consent signing for CS, although widely practiced, are of suboptimal quality. Providers believe they counsel adequately within the resource and workload constraints. Clients have limited autonomy in decision making but have higher expectations for receiving detailed information. Appropriate protocols, resources and capacity building are required for both providers and clients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09150-w.Keywords: Childbirth, C-section, Counselling, Consent, Ddebriefing, Respectful maternity care, Mixed-method, India

Background

Cesarean section (CS) is an essential, lifesaving intervention required to safeguard mother and child from critical situations. In the last decade, CS rates have increased both globally (21.1%) [1] and in India (21.5%) [2]. When clinically necessary, a CS can effectively prevent maternal and newborn mortality. However, evidence suggests that CS rates greater than 10–15% are not associated with improved maternal and fetal outcomes [3]. Evidence shows that building trust and partnership between clients and providers through appropriate Counselling, Consent and Debriefing (CCD) can improve optimum decision making, quality of care, health outcomes, and client satisfaction [4]. From the Respectful maternity care (RMC) perspective, the gaps and poor client-provider communication adversely impact the perceived quality of obstetric care including obstetric surgeries for patients [5]. Poor client-provider communication and a lack of informed consent for CS, are widespread in low-and middle-income countries, including India [6]. The unavailability of standard operating protocols and resources for CCD and withholding required CS-related information from clients have also resulted in an increase in medico-legal cases [7].

Betrán et al. (2018) identified an array of clinical and psychological factors that drive decision-making for CS and the CCD practice, these are associated with women, family, service providers, and systems [8]. They found that women may request CS for reasons including fear of labor pain, possible reproductive organ damage, a reduction in quality of sexual activity, and perceiving CS to be safer, convenient, and beneficial (e.g., tubal ligation can be performed at the same time as CS). Additionally, a scheduled CS provides parents with an opportunity to choose an auspicious birth date for their offspring. Couples’ decision to undergo CS in subsequent pregnancies is also influenced by their birthing experience in previous pregnancies—the suboptimal quality of care and disrespectful experiences in previous vaginal births, a lack of trust in the health system and care providers for vaginal births, and previous CS experience. The media is another growing influence on the decision to schedule CS. Furthermore, providers may opt for CS due to presumed professional protection against litigation, reconciliation of public and private duties, and due to limited experience and skill in performing assisted vaginal delivery. Moreover, CS generates more profit than performing vaginal delivery in the private sector [4]. Several of these factors may guide content and communication in any CCD process.

Globally, there is limited evidence on CCD practices, but few frameworks on counselling and consent exist based on specific perspectives. Beauchamp and Childress’s Elements of consent framework [9] focuses on the key concepts of good quality consent while Hall’s work [10] focuses on the ethical, legal and administrative purpose of informed consent, and the WHO’s Standards [11] include attention to client-provider communication as part of RMC experience enabling informed choice, supporting companionship during labor, and ensuring respectful, non-discriminatory care. A scoping review [12] from the sub-Saharan Africa region points out the interconnectedness between counselling and consent and type and urgency of the situation. There are limited published studies on debriefing for CS. This research will contribute insights to global and regional policy and discussions in designing interventions to enhance CCD practices in the context of respectful and patient-centered care for CS.

This mixed methods study aimed to explore pathways and determinants of CCD for CS and generate evidence that could help further define essential CCD concepts, considering stakeholders’ understanding, practices, and perspectives, health system’s capability, communities’ socio-cultural norms, and their interplay.

Methods

This mixed methods study was conducted in India as part of a multi-country research initiative led by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM), in partnership with EngenderHealth, as part of the USAID-funded MOMENTUM Safe Surgery in Family Planning and Obstetrics project.

Data were collected in two states, Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh, from November 2022 to April 2023, using focused ethnographic observations, key informant interviews (KIIs), and in-depth interviews (IDIs) in selected secondary level hospitals, and focus group discussions (FGDs) were held in catchment communities. This approach allowed a range of CCD perspectives from maternity healthcare providers and clients and their families to be captured. Data were also extracted from routine record reviews to assess the CCD documentation practices.

Study setting

The two states were purposively selected by the implementation partners considering heterogeneity in their socio-economic and demographic characteristics and health system capacities (Annex 1)—these were thus included for this research. Karnataka, a south-western Indian state with a population of over 61 million with 61% of people living in rural areas [13] has better ratings in socio-economic progress, women’s literacy, smaller family norm, better public health infrastructure and service utilisation compared to India’s average [2, 14–16] These indicators were poorer in Madhya Pradesh, a central-Indian state with a population of over 72 million with a 72% rural [17] population. The institutional delivery rate in both states is more than 90%. While the CS rates in public hospitals in Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh show considerable differences, i.e., 23% and 8%, respectively, the rates in private settings were 53% and 52% respectively [2]. In consultation with the state health departments, and considering the variation in performance and resources, four districts (two per state) and seven secondary-level hospitals (five public and two private hospitals) with high delivery and CS load (more than 50 CS per month) were selected. The KIIs, IDIs, and FGDs were conducted in local languages (Kannada and Hindi) with a mix of English as per the respondents’ preferences. Data were extracted in English from routine maternal case records and operation theatre (OT) register records that were predominantly in English but included a mix of local languages.

Study participants

Participants included the clients—women who sought obstetric care and delivered by CS in the selected hospitals, their husbands, and families and relatives (the latter were considered in ethnographic interactions). Hospital staff included: hospital superintendents, and care providers—heads of obstetric department, obstetricians (consultants and residents), medical doctors (non-specialists in obstetrics), anesthesiologists, nurses, designated counsellors, ward attendants (non-clinical), and frontline workers (Accredited Social Health Activists, ASHAs [18] engaged in the care of pregnant women in the community before and after delivery, and who often accompany them to the hospital for delivery.

Study tools and research team

Available conceptual frameworks [9–11] around CCD were referenced to help define important terms based on a systematic review. First draft of tools were developed by the LSHTM team guided by the review they conducted [12]. These were then adapted by the research team in India to the Indian context based on the existing guidelines, review of literature, and researchers’ experience of the context. Qualitative study tools were developed in English, translated in local languages (Kannada and Hindi) and back translated before final use. The tools were pilot tested in local languages in non-study districts and revised as required.

KII guides inquired about the availability of existing guidelines and standard operating procedures. Ethnography guides helped capture maternity services layout and client movements for understanding local setting and routine obstetric services, and record real time CCD practices, while also capturing client-provider interactions, their reactions and perceptions. IDI and FGD guides enquired about health workers’, women’s, and partners’ demographics, their opinions and experience related to CCD, and overall obstetric care. The record review data extraction checklist included details expected to be documented regarding CCD processes including consent forms, and additional information such as socio-demographic, reason for CS, emergency or elective CS, and general obstetric services and resources in the hospital.

Data were collected by state-based trained local research teams comprised of two ethnographers with clinical and public health background and four qualitative interviewers with social work background to ensure clinical and social understanding of clinical practices and conversations. Research teams were trained for two weeks: one week training on specific obstetric topics, qualitative research, ethnographic observations, and research ethics, and one week training on record reviews.

The definitions referred to while conceptualising the research and the research tools are mentioned in Annex 2.

Data collection

Respective medical superintendents and obstetric staff were informed about the research objective and methods prior to starting data collection. All research methods were employed sequentially, including ethnographic observation and record review, in four public hospitals. In the remaining three hospitals (one public and two private hospitals), only interviews were conducted due to lack of permission to observe or access records in the private hospitals (Fig. 1). After first obtaining necessary approvals, KIIs (30–45 min) with the hospital superintendent or head of obstetric department were conducted, followed by six to eight weeks of ethnographic observations. Initial observations were broad in nature to acquaint researchers with hospital norms and foster camaraderie among study participants and researchers. After two weeks, the observations became more specific, guided by preceding insights, and included shadowing, informal interactions with providers, non-clinical providers, women, spouses, and families to acquire perspectives and case-specific reactions, along with case studies. The researchers maintained field diaries and expanded their notes to document descriptions of observations and verbal and non-verbal interactions among stakeholders. Most observations and interviews were conducted during the day. However, sometimes teams stayed overnight in the hospitals, with management permission and adequate safety precautions, to observe the context of service availability during night shifts. The research teams had regular fortnightly and monthly debriefing for simultaneous analysis, interpretation, knowledge building, and prospective planning for further observations /enquiries.

Chronology of data collection methods in the study hospitals per state

Starting in the fourth week, IDIs (15–25 min) with consenting providers and postoperative women (a mix of scheduled and emergency CS cases) were conducted in hospitals. After hospital-based perspectives were gathered, population-level FGDs (6–8 participants each) were conducted with women who had recently delivered by CS (within 6 months prior to the FGD), their spouses, and ASHAs. The eligible women and spouses were mobilised with help of identified ASHAs in one of the purposively selected catchment blocks (sub-district administrative unit). All IDIs and FGDs were conducted in the local language and audio recorded with permission. Detailed interview notes were taken when permission for audio-recording was denied. Lastly, four hospitals provided permission to extract information from maternity case records and OT registers of last 200 individual CS women entries who were discharged in the preceding three months.

Description of qualitative data collection

Ethics

Prior to data collection, all necessary ethical approval for the protocol was obtained from a reputed independent ethics review board in India, SIGMA Institutional Review Board (IRB 10062/IRB/22–23; 29.11.2022) and the LSHTM Research Ethics Committee. Permissions were also obtained from the state health departments. An overarching consent was obtained from providers for the ethnographic observations to avoid seeking specific consent during observations of routine obstetric services that would disrupt service delivery. For client and provider interviews, researchers read and explained the study purpose and processes as outlined in the information sheet and obtained signed consent from all the study participants who agreed to be interviewed. The interviews were transcribed and saved using participant codes; the stored and to-be-analysed versions were anonymised. The extracted medical record data did not contain any identifiers.

Data management and analysis

Field researchers shared audio files as and when interviews were conducted, and uniquely labelled field notes (separately for ethnography and for interviews) on a daily and weekly basis with the core research team via a secure web-based cloud platform. Two consultants (one for Kannada and other for Hindi) simultaneously transcribed and translated audio recordings and notes in English that were reviewed and finalised by the research team. The research team stored all files on a password protected central computer and deleted them from individual recorders. Researchers extracted routine medical record data on password-protected tablets/smartphones onto an Open Data Kit (ODK)-based secure server.

Researchers familiarised themselves with data from KIIs, observations, IDIs, and FGDs as they were being conducted, through regular team meetings, reading, and reviewing of transcripts. During ethnography related debriefing, the team noted and described the usual journeys of women and their opportunities for CCD interactions along with the variations noted in the hospitals. Subsequently, based on periodic inputs by lead researchers, ethnographers refined their observations and field notes to capture various perspectives around CCD untill saturation was achieved in emerging themes. After initial familiarisation, the team of four ethnographers led by the research scientist, developed a codebook using a combination of deductive and inductive coding approaches for the observations and interviews and they continually discussed the emerging codes from interviews after they were being conducted. Initial themes included stakeholders’ understanding purpose and concept of CCD; provider and client practices, experiences, and expectations; determinants such as stakeholders’ relational dynamics, communication skills, and resources; community level factors such as women’s status and family’s engagement. The research scientist analysed the content of the debriefing sessions, the ethnographer’s daily notes, and transcripts using grounded theory approach with the help of NVivo software. The emerging themes and sub-themes from all sources were collectively discussed and refined. The results were triangulated and there was consensus on the synthesis of the final themes and sub-themes for qualitative results. As there was data collection from private hospitals, only peculiar findings are indicated where appropriate. We also analysed enablers and barriers to appropriate CCD practices in the study settings.

The team analysed the extracted record review data using R software. Key findings on hospital characteristics and obstetric services, and indicators related to consent forms are included in this paper. The information on sociodemographic was rarely recorded, and documentation of relevant clinical information had huge variation and was of poor quality; thus they were not analysed.

Results

All the hospitals had maternal and child health wing with bed strength ranging between 100 and 125. Hospitals from Karnataka had a delivery load of 5,000–6,000 per year vs. 8,000–10,000 in Madhya Pradesh, and CS rates of 54–68% vs. 15–29%, respectively. Three out of seven hospitals classified all CS as emergency cases in the records, one listed a few scheduled cases as ‘elective’ along with the majority emergency CS, and the other three had no such classification. Karnataka hospitals had better 24 × 7 availability of obstetricians, anesthetists and general doctors (Annex 3), and a higher number of obstetricians per 1000 births. All the study hospitals had obtained LaQshya [19] quality certification or accreditation at the time of our assessment.

Mapping the client-provider interactions for CCD in obstetric care including CS

This section provides understanding of the hospital settings and general obstetric care including the various CCD-related interactions essential for contextual exploration of CCD practices across the continuum from ANC to delivery (including CS) services. While there was similarity in client movement patterns in study hospitals, the points (place and time) at which client-provider interactions happened and the content of interaction varied with state, hospitals, and categories of clients (person).

In both states, only women received clinical information on pregnancy, complications, or delivery options during their antenatal visits in the outpatient department (OPD) or in the labor room (LR) in presence of other clients and staff—there were no separate counselling rooms. Husbands and family were not allowed inside the OPD or LR for crowd management and to maintain women’s privacy. In the majority of observed OPD interactions, doctors counseled the women, and women in turn paraphrased the details as per their understanding with the family waiting outside the OPD—often incomplete or misinterpreted. Unless families insisted, interaction with the doctor or the nurse was not allowed. When women arrived directly in the LR beyond OPD hours or were referred from elsewhere, nurses first assessed and interacted with the client, and doctors were called only if required. Nurses did not have authority to label any complication. If any complication was detected by the obstetrician/medical doctor, they first informed the women, before informing the family separately. The researchers observed that pre-surgery interaction or counselling of the family, and consent signing occurred either in designated stations in the LR or in the adjacent LR corridors and, for a few emergency cases, in the pre-operative area. Debriefing, if any, was only observed in the post-operative area or LR corridors.

With respect to antenatal risk status, if women were recommended for elective CS by the doctor, they were counseled during ANC consultation; however, neither the women nor the families received any repeat counselling on the pre-decided day of admission. Nurses assumed the client would have been previously counseled during ANC visits in both states. For women deemed low risk during ANC visits, providers usually did not indicate the possibility of a CS in Karnataka. Consequently, clients were taken by surprise when providers declared the need for CS during delivery and felt compelled to accept. As mentioned by participants, Karnataka which has high CS rates and high community demand, has a mandate for reducing unnecessary CS thus, they refrain from suggesting CS until there is obvious clinical indication—this is to offshoot unnecessary pressure for CS from the client side.

“Whenever I went for a checkup, doctors said everything was fine and I’d have a normal delivery. But in the end, I had a CS. Why don’t they tell this earlier?”- post-operative woman (female FGD, Karnataka)

In contrast, providers in Madhya Pradesh always mentioned the possibility of CS during ANC in case any complications arose later. However, it was observed that social beliefs, perceived long-term negative impact of CS, and large family norms made families reluctant to accept CS even if needed. As informed, Madhya Pradesh state which has lower CS rates and high client resistance for CS, promotes mentioning possibility of CS early in ANC and encourages CS when clinically indicated to reduce mortality.

In Madhya Pradesh hospitals (with low obstetrician-to-client ratio compared to Karnataka), obstetricians’ availability in labor rooms was limited due to their commitment to high-load OPD and OT; their availability was even lesser at night. Here, nurses were the providers who primarily communicated with mothers and their families, with occasional involvement from a few on-duty medical doctors. Obstetricians were called when necessary. In Karnataka, obstetricians were available during day and on call during night and took primary responsibility of informing the client about CS decision.

Perspectives regarding purpose and principles of CCD for CS

Counselling

Healthcare providers believed counselling was about making the client aware of their health condition, maternal and fetal complications, sharing the provider’s decision on the mode of delivery, and explaining the reasons for and benefits of CS and the consequences of not opting for CS. During informal discussions, they mentioned that good counselling is always participatory in nature, and clients can inquire about any information for ease of decision-making.

“…telling the mother what condition, she and her baby are in, and why I’m planning a CS, such as fetal or maternal indication, what happens if I don’t do CS… and so on. It helps them to make decisions.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Karnataka)

“Time is an important factor. If we delay in making family members understand the emergency, mother’s and child’s lives will be in danger. We provide as much information as possible to allow for an early decision.”- Obstetrician (private hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Providers acknowledged clients’ right to know and decide, but also believed the client and their family did not possess the capacity to decide for themselves.

“We cannot trust women’s or family members’ judgments on whether they need a CS or not. If we wait for their approval, then no one can control MMR and IMR. They (almost) always prefer vaginal delivery…convincing them is not always possible… For such cases we just inform our decision…. It’s up to them whether they opt for it (CS) or not…” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

For clients, counselling meant receiving adequate information from a wise provider regarding the maternal-child health status, explanation of why the CS was required and what difficulties they may face if operation was not done. This helped them understand their health status and further decision-making.

“Anyone would be scared after listening about the surgery. Doctors’ and nurses’ explanation will help us know about the difficulties (complication). After proper understanding we could convince our family members for the operation.”- Husband (FGD, Karnataka)

Consent

Providers considered the consent form as a medico-legal document signed by the women and family. A mandatory practice of consent signing was observed in all the study hospitals. Only a few providers mentioned its usefulness in establishing women’s autonomy in decision-making. Both providers and clients felt that consent was useful only for providers as it could protect them from blame/litigation later. Clients and family lacked trust in the consent process and did not understand the purpose of signing the consent forms, given their prior agreement for the CS.

“The consent form is important for facility staff…if family members deny the treatment decision at any time… it will help us avoid further litigation.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Karnataka)

“Mainly it’s (consent is) for our benefit. If complications arise, attendees will blame us… So, we need to protect ourselves.” - Nurse (public hospital, Karnataka)

“Guardian’s signature on that form helps them (doctors) to avoid blame if anything wrong happens inside OT (operation theatre). They will say… it was your willingness. No one could be blamed once the guardian put his signature on that form.” - Husband (public hospital, Karnataka)

“They (nurses) take our signature to establish that the family has complete responsibility for the decision to have a C-section. If something happens, no one can question them (providers).” - Postoperative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Debriefing

None of the respondents (providers and clients) had heard of the term “debriefing”. On further probing, providers primarily related the concept with post-operative care and discharge advice which is described later.

Counselling for CS: Varied role and practices, experiences and perceptions

In all the study hospitals, some form of counselling, relevant for CS, existed at various stages of obstetric care.

Differing counselling roles by cadre

All providers noted, that as per hospital protocols (unpublished) obstetricians or medical doctors were primarily responsible for counselling clients, mainly sharing the cause and decision for CS; the research teams also observed this practice in most facilities. Women and their relatives also preferred doctors communicated clinical conditions and treatment decisions.

Nurses were the key provider group providing detailed counselling after the doctor. Nurses explained the doctors’ decision and were largely responsible for convincing clients to comply with the decision. They were also considered accessible and most sought after by clients for more granular information.

Other counsellors, such as SUMAN [20] counsellors, a cadre under the ‘Surakshit Matritwa Aashwashan’ scheme, often supported providers in counselling clients who were resistant to undergo a CS despite strong clinical indications.

Providers found that involving ASHAs in the counselling process facilitated better communication and improved client comprehension, as it leveraged ASHAs’ close ties with the community.

“If ASHA is coming with the woman, we make them understand the reason for the CS. They understand people’s mentality. With their presence, it becomes easier to explain certain things.” - Nurse (public hospital, Karnataka)

Brief and concise information sharing

While providers seemed to have good understanding of the features of counselling, only some aspects were practiced in the manner as stated. The research team observed women being told in brief about their health condition and justification for CS was either the mother or newborn or both may have adverse outcome if CS was not conducted. All clients were told to arrange for blood from the blood bank in case blood transfusion was required during surgery. Clients were not provided details about the CS procedure, need for anesthesia, risks and benefits of the procedure, or the provision of any alternative treatment choices.

“Your body cannot take the stress of normal delivery. You are very weak and have less blood. If you do opt for normal delivery, you and your baby will be in danger. So, we have to do a CS.” – Obstetrician (Public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“Amma! You need a CS to save your child. There is a cord around neck. The baby cannot breathe.”- Obstetrician (public hospital, Karnataka)

“No one talked about anesthesia. They just asked me to lie down. I was scared. I remember the needle prick on my back, and after that, I couldn’t move my legs. I couldn’t understand.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

In a few instances, providers also counseled women who demanded CS against the procedure when it was not clinically indicated.

Inconsistent providers’ tone while counselling

Providers mostly used a soft tone while counselling, however, as workload increased, and upon repeated questioning by relatives, the tone became harsher in most of the instances. Nurses were often perturbed by husbands’ questions, which they found unnecessary and out of context.

“I have told you (repeatedly) that your wife is bleeding (APH), and urgent surgery is needed to save both the mother and child. If you do not agree with our decision…then leave (go) elsewhere.” – Post-graduate obstetric resident (public hospital, Karnataka)

“I am very irritated with the attitude of male members.They keep asking questions and do not try to understand what we are saying.” - Nurse (public hospital, Karnataka)

Language, content, and tone of counselling differed with the education status of the client and family. Educated people received technical explanations while less educated clients got concise information in local generic terms.

“Educated women and men ask questions and get more information. I’m a farmer. I don’t know much. I didn’t ask any questions. And no one made me understand any detail.” - Husband (FGD, Karnataka)

Clients’ mixed perspectives about received counselling

Some clients were satisfied with the counselling and behaviour of the providers, and some had grievances.

“Since I admitted here, doctors are properly guiding and advising us, and operated with care.” – Post-operative woman (private hospital, Karnataka)

“Earlier, I was upset after knowing there were zero chances of normal delivery. But after C-section, I’m not disappointed. Doctors and nurses spoke with empathy, gave support, and told me to have courage.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

“We requested that they (doctors and nurses) wait for a while and try for a normal delivery. But they didn’t listen to us, told us we knew nothing about the situation. They didn’t make us understand properly and declared a C-section. It was like they were forcing us.” - Mother-in-law (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“We did not know anything about the child’s complication (breech presentation). This is our first baby. The doctor should convey the information to us. We even asked about the status… but she (doctor) didn’t tell us that there was a risk. If she would have told us, we would have gone to another place.” – Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

Non-participatory counselling, fear or reluctance of clients to discuss

Counselling was non-participatory. In Karnataka, information was shared primarily with the women in OPD or labor room, while in Madhya Pradesh, providers often chose to communicate health information, including details related to complications, with the woman’s relatives rather than her.

“We prefer not to disclose the alarming situation to the woman, when she is already stressed. It may increase her BP and pulse, making her anxious and further complicate her and the baby’s condition. We share any serious information with family. They take the decision and convey it to the woman.” -Nurse (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

While male partners’ engagement was limited in counselling, their consent was considered more important. However, husbands also found it challenging to comprehend the situation and the information conveyed by the doctor.

“We can’t participate in the discussion. We are not allowed anywhere in the hospital except for a paper signature. The doctor told us a CS is a must, and we agreed.” – Husband (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“We don’t understand what they (doctor) are explaining. They talk in local language. But somehow, we don’t understand.” - Husband (FGD, Karnataka)

Many clients and families mentioned they were scared or reluctant to ask questions, even though they had doubts and hardly participated in counselling process. Instead, they reached out to nurses or other non-clinical ward attendants (see sections on relational dynamics below) for information.

In both states, researchers seldom observed providers paying attention to the client’s facial expressions and cues or actively trying to assess the client’s understanding. However, in Madhya Pradesh, hospital norms mandated clients to write the reason for the CS on the case sheet, indicating their comprehension.

Providers highlighted several challenges such as the presence of multiple caregivers at the client’s side, quite a few decision influencers; repeated requests for the same information at different times; clients’ limited participation in conversation, and occasional provision of clinically inconsistent information by non-clinical providers.

Unaddressed client concerns regarding CS

Women and their families had their own separate considerations and doubts. Women and husbands were afraid of CS and its perceived short- and long-term consequences. Both believed that women would lose strength and vitality forever, while husbands also thought their sexual lives would be affected. In a majority of cases, both wanted vaginal delivery but agreed to a CS due to the circumstances. Many of these concerns were not addressed in the counselling session.

My relatives and neighbours said after a CS, I’ll get lifetime back pain, I wouldn’t lift weights anymore, there would be a scar on my abdomen, and I wouldn’t be able to work for longer time. The village doctor also told me the same.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)”

“CS causes long-term back pain, and women cannot carry heavy weight. It hampers fieldwork. We work in the field. Our sexual life is also hampered.” - Husband (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

However, a few clients demanded a CS even when it was not indicated, to avoid labor pains and to get a tubectomy along with the CS. In all observed cases, the doctors’ decision was guided by clinical indication.

“In normal delivery, it pains a lot. But it won’t hurt that much in CS. Besides, family planning operation also gets done along with the surgery, so there is no need to come twice. The village doctor and relatives told me this.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

Consent: a mandatory and universal process; limited role of client in decision

Structured consent form

All the public hospitals had a standard consent form in English as prescribed by Labour Room Quality Improvement Initiative (LaQshya) guidelines(20). It included information on maternal and fetal indications for CS, and a statement that the women and family members agreed to the procedure. Providers considered the form insufficient for use in complicated scenarios as it had no provision/requirement to record fetal risks, CS benefits, or providers’ names and signatures. The client/family often could not understand the content written in English in the form they were signing. Private hospitals had more detailed consent forms in the local language.

“Private hospitals’ consent is better than public hospitals’ consent forms. They are detailed and could be used throughout. There is a need for revision of the format (in public hospitals) and more detailing to make the current format applicable for any kind of (obstetric) surgery. The consent form must include the statement, ‘The staff explained everything (or specific information) to me, and I understand what has been said to me truthfully’.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Nurses obtained consent, signed by mother and/or decision maker

In all observed cases, nurses briefly reiterated the doctors’ information and obtained signatures from both the client and the family on the prescribed consent forms. Significant variations existed in whose signatures were considered essential. In Karnataka, women’s signatures were mandatory, and decision-making family members’ signatures were preferred and recorded as witnesses. In Madhya Pradesh, signatures from husbands (and sometimes parents or in-laws) were deemed essential instead of the women’s. In both states, women’s signatures were considered a formality, whether obtained or not. The primary consenter was typically the spouse in Karnataka and the head of the family/key decision maker in Madhya Pradesh.

Clients’ and families’ rights and role in decision and consent signing

Clients and their families were not aware of their right to counselling and consent, or their role and responsibility in decision-making for their own care. The decision or acceptance for CS was communal in nature rather than by an individual; in most cases, women did not decide for themselves, and the husbands had non-clinical concerns.

“We are a poor family; we have to work every day. Normal delivery is much better as we can work after that. If an operation happens, someone must look after the baby and my wife. It’s a burden.” – Husband (FGD, Madhya Pradesh)

The family yielded to the doctor’s decision on the basis that ‘doctors knew better’, due to fear of the death of the mother or child, or out of fear of being denied care in that hospital. While a few clients and their families understood or agreed that CS was better.

“For us, doctor and God are equal. If the doctor says, then we will go for it. Baby and mother wellness is important.” - Mother-in-law (public hospital, Karnataka)

“My husband agreed to a CS as doctors said the baby would suffer and face problems. So, who will disagree in that moment.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

“Cesarean is better…most of the time (labor) pain doesn’t come naturally. Staff induce pain with injections, cause infection. So, women become weak, some problems may arise, and the village doctor said bleeding may also happen. So, it’s better to go for an operation.” - Husband (male FGD, Karnataka)

Pregnant women’s autonomy in consent

Women had limited agency in decision-making despite having concerns and opinions of their own. Some women discussed their thoughts and apprehensions with families but ultimately deferred to their decision. Others just awaited the family’s decision, while a few were in tears and had concerns, yet did not participate in family discussions. Only a small number of educated women asked the staff questions and actively engaged in family discussions regarding CS decision. Some women mentioned that they were unable to decide for themselves due to incomplete understanding of the clinical situation and poor general awareness. We realised there was a need for information but no apparent felt need for decision autonomy.

Spouses considered themselves wiser decision-makers due to their maturity and self-claimed better intellectual capacity, while women were considered emotional and less knowledgeable.

“The woman should get suggestions from her husband before taking any decision. She is illiterate and emotional. If there is some mishap, then men will be held responsible; this won’t be okay. No one knows what decision the woman will take, and that’s harmful for the family.” - Husband (FGD, Madhya Pradesh)

“Women get nervous. Therefore, husbands should be informed. Husband should sign because women might refuse for operation which can lead to adversities.”- Husband (FGD, Madhya Pradesh)

Inconsistent documentation of consent

Karnataka had better documentation of consent in the medical records, where nurses consistently obtained completed consent forms before surgery. Conversely, in Madhya Pradesh consent forms were sometimes incomplete and signed post-surgery (Table 2). In Karnataka, a doctor who was more involved with patient care and counselling mentioned that he audio-recorded, and another mentioned that he video-recorded, the counselling and consent taking. However, no consent was obtained for these audio or video recordings.

Debriefing: an unheard-of concept, components partially practiced

There was no structured or spelled-out intervention for debriefing in any hospital. However, immediately after surgery, the OT nurses or Ward attendants informed the family about the outcome, the gender of the newborn, and sometimes gave instructions on immediate care to be taken. No mention of the surgical or anesthesia proceedings was made.

“No one told me what happened… After a long time, the ward boy told my husband and family… we had a boy and showed it to them. No doctor or nurse said anything.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

In two public hospitals in Karnataka, doctors took post-surgical rounds in the postnatal wards, and in some hospitals RMNCH + A counselors provided basic post-natal counselling using standard materials. RMNCH + A counselors had no role in CS related pre- or post-operative counselling.

“After the surgery, during the ward round, we advise the mother how to take care of herself at home. We also instruct nurses on what information they needed to give to the clients. Mostly, it was about how to take care of their wounds, drink plenty of water, get proper nutrition, and breastfeed the baby. Nurses and counsellor give family planning education to women.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Karnataka)

In two hospitals in Madhya Pradesh, no formal post-operative rounds or counselling took place in the postnatal wards. The clients reached out to nurses if they needed any information or help in post-operative care.

“Nothing is told; they (the provider) just come to tell us that the operation is done. She (the nurse) just comes in the evening to inject the bottle… we have to ask them many times what we need to do. We ask other patients because they have been here for almost 10 days, and they know what to do.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“No one tells me anything. The doctor came and said to wake up, sit, and walk slowly.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“Doctors do not come after surgery. No one came (till now). We go to nurses to ask what to do next. We also ask ASHAs” - Mother of post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Pregnant women’s expectations from CCD for CS

Irrespective of scheduled or emergency CS, women harbored several doubts, fears, and apprehensions about their experience during the CS, post-operative care, and long-term consequences.

“I was very nervous. I was scared even before the nurse told me. I knew already that I had to go through a CS, but I used to cry thinking of it; I was scared.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

It would have been nice if they gave a bit more details in normal language that we could understand; it would have reduced our fear.” – Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“I did ask the doctor why they were giving injections in my back. She (the doctor) told me to be silent, as she (doctor) knew well what was best for me.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Women expressed feeling deprived of accurate information about CS across all levels of health care. They wanted to know about the obstetric complications leading to CS to take preventive measures during their pregnancies. They expected to be informed about the possibility of a CS before delivery, not just at the last moment. They suggested involving husbands or family members during OPD consultation and desired empathetic communication, along with comprehensive information on the CS process, post-operative care, and planning for future pregnancies and care.

“I was alone when they (doctors and nurses) told me about the complication. How could I understand? I was in pain and scared for my baby. They didn’t allow anyone with me, nor my husband, nor my mother. – Post-operative woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“It’s not that we don’t want to keep ourselves safe. I am not that educated. Doctors should tell us how I could keep my baby and myself safe in subsequent pregnancies.” – Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

Factors determining quality of CCD

Participants mentioned several factors impacting the CCD process, broadly categorised as: (1) Relational dynamics among stakeholders, (2) Hospital policy and resources, (3) Workplace environment, and (4) Community-level factors.

Relational dynamics among stakeholders

Doctors believed they were providing the best treatment to the women, and that clients didn’t have the ability to make informed decisions. While some doctors seemed unapproachable, others made themselves available to address clients’ doubts. Clients mostly trusted doctors for clinical decisions, but in cases of doubt, many were reluctant to pose questions or requests. Clients perceived doctors had superior social status, leading them to believe their voices/opinions would not be heard. Some feared if they asked something considered nonsensical, they would be scolded, mistreated, or refused treatment. A few others suspected the doctor’s decision for CS was unnecessary and only to ease their own task.

“We are scared to ask doctors. They are big (superior status) people, and we don’t know much. What if we say something wrong…they may not treat us well. - Woman’s mother (public hospital, Karnataka)

“We (client) fear doctors and think what they will think about us if we ask anything to them. May the doctors remember us and not treat us in the future.” - Post-operative woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

“Yes, we are allowed to ask questions, but they weren’t really answering, as they mentioned it’s their work and we should let them do their work.” - Woman (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

However, even though nurses often snubbed relatives, overall, they were considered approachable.

“We cannot ask questions to the doctor because of hesitation, but we ask nurses and other junior staff who will explain in detail.” -Husband (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“The doctor didn’t say anything. It was the sisters (nurses) who told me everything, such as that the baby’s position was not favorable for normal delivery, and we did the right thing by choosing CS. She comforted me with the fact that both me and my baby are healthy.” - Woman (public hospital, Karnataka)

Clients also reached out to non-clinical providers such as ward assistants and ASHAs with whom clients mentioned socio-cultural proximity, as they were perceived approachable and responded warmly. However, clinicians opposed the engagement of non-clinical staff in formal CCD process because of non-clinical staff’s lack of fundamental obstetric knowledge and the potential risk of creating nuisance by asking for money in exchange of information.

Hospital policy and resources

Providing counselling and obtaining consent was mandated under hospital policy and LaQshya quality standards. Few RMC principles (such as information sharing, privacy, birth companion, and respectful conduct) were practiced in labor rooms. Providers mentioned that unavailability of structured protocols and resources related to the CCD process, insufficient information in the standard consent form, absence of a dedicated counselling room and counsellor, and lack of training for effective communication made the CCD process substandard. Some doctors suggested client-provider trust and relationships needed improvement.

“For good-quality counselling we need a supportive atmosphere. How can we counsel a woman and her family in the middle of a crowd? Before conducting counselling all of them must be mentally calm and comfortable. We need dedicated space, person, and standard resources for good-quality counselling.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Karnataka)

“There must be some intervention regarding behaviour modification by care providers. They must be empathetic toward clients. Effective communication can solve any problem. People are ready to cooperate. Care providers should behave well with clients…The higher authority must recognise the communication issues and must take initiatives on soft skill training. Through reinforcement, one can cultivate the culture. It’s time to think about that.” - Obstetrician (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Other doctors mentioned CCD protocols cannot be standardised as every case was unique and various provider and client factors play a role in what and how information is provided to the client. A few doctors were willing to incorporate debriefing if they were provided with guidance.

Workplace environment

Where doctors and nurses seemed to work as a team, they were more available for client interactions. In instances where doctors were less available (majority time spent in OT or on call only at night), nurses with lesser decision autonomy (for diagnosis and decision of CS) had to deal with clients, constraining the CCD process. Providers, specifically nurses, mentioned workplace challenges such as overwhelming caseloads, unending documentation, understaffing, and safety concerns, leading to a perceived lack of time, stress, burnout, and suboptimal performance. The frequent transfer of trained and skilled nurses to non-obstetric departments acted as an additional barrier in the CCD process.

“Most of the time, doctors are not here when we need them most. At night, doctors do not receive calls. If received, they give instructions over the phone and ask us to prescribe USG or medication, which we are not authorised to do. If we deny, they scold us. They don’t understand that patients would not listen to everything we said. And if something happened, we would be blamed.” - Nurse (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

Community level factors

We explored clients’ and families’ general obstetric understanding, socio-behavioural attributes, and cultural norms that may influence their role in the CCD process.

Lack of obstetric knowledge

Clients and families had a poor understanding of general obstetric care, obstetric complication management, and the risks and benefits of CS despite probing for common conditions. These contributed to unfounded apprehensions and fears among clients, leading to either unrealistic preference for vaginal delivery while ignoring complications or demand for non-clinically indicated CS.

“Here elderly women believe that routine checkups during pregnancy lead to operations. They say to us that earlier there were no checkups and people had vaginal deliveries”. - ASHA (FGD, Madhya Pradesh)

Doctors and also ASHAs mentioned that local priests and other religious advisors/leaders influenced acceptance of diagnosis of complications and decisions for CS, including auspicious dates for birth.

“We (doctors) diagnosed fetal uterine death. But Pujari [Hindu priest] declared the baby was healthy and a boy. After that, we faced huge difficulties in making the family understand the situation.” – Obstetrician (private hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

ASHAs mentioned that they inform women and families on pregnancy related complications and the possibility of CS.

“We tell them about complications… we motivate people to feel free to ask questions to doctors, nurses, or any medical staff in order to feel comforted and understand the necessities of operations.” - ASHA (FGD, Madhya Pradesh)

A few obstetricians noted the value of the roles of Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANM), Anganwadi Workers (AWW), and ASHAs in establishing general obstetric, high-risk, and C-section related awareness in communities.

“….need to increase awareness among ASHAs, Anganwadi workers, subcentre staff, and then upper levels. ASHA is the first point of contact for raising basic awareness about obstetrics and gynaecology. Once the concept gets introduced at that level, the situation will surely evolve.” - Obstetrician (private hospital, Madhya Pradesh)”

Preference of boy child

Clients were aware that CS increase the risk to mother and child in subsequent pregnancies. In some families, with a strong desire for a boy child, if the couple had a previous CS and a girl child, they were reluctant to opt for a second CS in current pregnancy as protection against future risk, in case they may have to go for another pregnancy if a girl child was born again in the current pregnancy. This was observed and also mentioned by families frequently in Madhya Pradesh, and seldom in Karnataka. Providers stated that post-operative recovery of women was slow, and care of the mother and the girl child was neglected by the family.

“If a woman delivers a male baby…the mother’s mental status will be calm and stress-free. Even families do not ask any other questions after the delivery.” – Nurse (public hospital, Madhya Pradesh)

“If it’s a girl child…. it’s like a funeral. Especially the third gravidas…From the moment of delivery, they start preparing for another pregnancy. The woman is stressed, eventually, complications arise, and PPH (postpartum hemorrhage) can be precipitated. Gender preference has a huge impact on the outcome… Even the care of the girl baby and mother is poor…” – Obstetrician (private hospital, Madhya Pradesh).

Reliance on social media

Doctors and nurses stated that clients often sought information from online sources, had pre-formed opinions, and it was difficult to counsel them. On the other hand, clients posting negative reviews and videos about the hospital or staff in the media adversely impacted client-provider relationships and trust, and providers’ rational decision making.

Discussion

The findings highlight that the counselling and consent process are a continuum with multiple layers of complexities including the states and health facilities policy, program, and organisational cultural context, community-level attributes and clients perspectives. Although counselling for CS was being conducted, it was not very structured; the consent-taking process and forms existed but their purpose was perceived for legal safeguarding of the providers. There were variations between the two study states. Overall, the findings are consistent with other global studies related to counselling and consent practices showing that providers overestimate its real-time practice and there exists a “know-do-gap” [8, 21]. Providers cited poor counseling practice primarily due to workplace constraints and overburden, illiteracy of clients and their ‘irrelevant questioning’, and language barriers [8, 21, 22] There is no relevant evidence on the client side contribution to counselling and consent practices. Debriefing is ill-defined in global literature [12], and in our study as well, the postnatal counselling is mentioned as debriefing.

We further critique the existing conceptual frameworks based on our findings. As in Hall’s approach [10] to counselling and consent, the study found considerable understanding of ethical, legal, and administrative purposes of taking consent among the care providers, but these were practiced with constraint. Ethically, providers were aware of offering justified, best possible quality of care and respect clients’ rights to decide. But they also felt clients couldn’t decide for themselves in clinically complicated situations, thus ethically counselling also meant convincing the client. Legally, providers saw the utility of signed consent in protecting themselves while clients did not find the consent signing useful for them. Administratively, there were no standard protocols for CCD, the consent forms were inadequate, and there was no proper documentation which also restricted quality audits.

Beauchamp and Childress’s elements of informed consent [9] suggest that preparedness and information communication are essential implementation steps before consent signing. We found that both providers and women (including husband and family) were underprepared. Women had low competence to understand the given information regarding obstetric complications (lack of obstetric literacy) and lacked power (socio-culturally) to make decisions for themselves while they had their apprehensions, preferences, and emotional requirements—which were rarely addressed. Although the rights perspective places women at the onus of providing consent, when women prefer to have their families provide consent, the significance of counselling with women alone and emphasis on women’s signatures on consent forms is debatable. At the time of obtaining consent, men’s or elders’ consent preceded the women’s, yet men were not engaged and not allowed access to obstetric information earlier in pregnancy and childbirth. Often clients did not recognise their right to information, to decide or refuse treatment, and that they co-owned the decision. Thus, preparedness of empowered clients and community that contributes to and trusts the CCD process is essential. In the meanwhile, the frameworks should consider complex socio-cultural dynamics and the role of men and family based on the local context.

We noted that while the study hospitals aspired to incorporate RMC principles [11] in labor rooms and CS care, there were substantial gaps in approach and practices. The rights-based approach was acknowledged but not practiced, confidentiality was not maintained, the counselling was majorly discriminated against based on the social class and education of the clients, had strong gender-biased CCD practices, and unnecessary CS may have induced more harm than benefits. The CCD practices for respectful and quality obstetric and CS care require positioning under broader RMC agenda while adopting the frameworks for CCD to the local socio-cultural and health policy and system context [11].

Strengths and limitations of the study

The mixed-methods design allowed for exploration of the extent and quality of CCD-related practices; six-week long observations went beyond the initial socially desirable provider behaviours and captured actual practice. However, both clients and hospitals did not represent the general population or hospitals in the state; the clients in this study were from low- to middle- socio-economic classes attending secondary-level public hospitals. Although private hospitals were included to capture variability, we were constrained to only conduct interviews, as permissions for observations or record reviews were not granted. Thus, we couldn’t separately present findings from the private sector. Besides, the study private hospitals did not represent the private corporate hospitals. The study had limited data collection from the practices at grassroots level institutions and community.

Implications for policy and practices

The study emphasises that mere knowledge of the importance of CCD and effective communication doesn’t automatically foster providers’ engagement in women-centered respectful CS care. To bridge the existing gap, health system stakeholders need guidelines, protocols, resources, and audio-visual aids in local language for CCD in obstetric care, including surgeries, across all levels of the health care delivery system. Pre-service and in-service trainings for clinical and non-clinical providers must also address subjective assumptions, misconceptions, biases, and discrimination towards clients. Trainings should develop effective communication skills, promote practice in real-time, and respect women’s decisions under time pressure. Public health facility consent forms should be standardised, indicating the need for CS, the risks and benefits of CS and anesthesia, a proclamation on client counselling, clarification of doubts, and voluntariness of the client’s decision, as well as the consent taker’s signature. With better understanding of the policy, program, and individual hospitals context, the implementing partners need to support the state in improving the facility norms and cultures, and work environment (case load, documentation, lack of motivation, and larger system level gaps) to enable providers to practice their skills.

Community and hospital-based interventions are required to raise communities’ and clients’ awareness about obstetric complication management, medically indicated CS, CS processes and implications, patients’ rights and responsibilities, and the importance of CCD. In addition, men’s roles and engagement in obstetric care needs to be expanded by involving them in routine ANC and peri-partum discussions, including pre- and post-natal counselling sessions and consent signing.

Globally, the frameworks for CCD need to acknowledge that women alone are not the only relevant actor for CCD; spouses and families should be considered as per their power and roles. Such frameworks need to account for communities’ poor awareness of basic obstetric care, their rights and roles in CCD process; and how to balance appropriate clinical management and inappropriate client decisions. Even if there are better designed consent forms, the adequacy of counselling and consenting cannot be ascertained, thus there is a need to devise methods for assessing quality. Debriefing is a new domain to be discussed. The concept, content, and need for debriefing related to surgeries, post-operative care, future health, and obstetric considerations need to be introduced by developing protocols, audio-visual aids, and individual and group counselling.

Conclusion

This research from India highlights the multi-dimensional attributes, and variations in perceptions, practices, and expectations of the wide range of stakeholders from a quality CCD process, which is an essential aspect of RMC for CS. The findings, particularly on the limited role of the pregnant women in the CCD process, poorer agency in the decision, yet desire for information, should guide in refining existing frameworks and developing guidelines on CCD. Several gaps and several challenges for quality CCD exist, both at the provider and client side, which require policy makers and program managers to acknowledge, understand, and address the complex issue within the local policy, health systems, and cultural context.
