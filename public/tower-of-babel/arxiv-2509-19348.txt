Conic optimization techniques yield sufficient conditions for set-completely positive matrix completion under arrowhead specification pattern Thanks: Supported by the Austrian Science Fund (project ESP 486-N).
Markus Gabl

Abstract
Matrix completion results deal with the question of when a partially specified symmetric matrix can be completed to a member of certain matrix cones. Results from positive semidefinite matrix completion and completely positive matrix completion have been successfully applied in optimization to greatly reduce the number of variables in conic optimization problems in the space of symmetric matrices. In this text, we go the other direction and show that we can use tools from conic optimization (more precisely: from copositive optimization) to establish a new completion result that complements the existing literature in two regards: firstly, we consider set-completely positive matrix completion, which generalizes completion with respect to the traditional completely positive matrix cone. Secondly, we consider a specification pattern that is not in the scope of classical results for completely positive matrix completion. Namely, we consider arrow-head specification patterns where the width is equal to one. Our theory is applied to a class of quadratic optimization problems.

Introduction

This text is concerned with the theory of set-completely positive matrix completion. Given a matrix, whose entries are partially unspecified, the question is whether there are values for the unspecified entries such that the matrix can be completed to a member of set-completely positive matrix cones, such as the positive semidefinite matrix cone or the completely positive matrix cone.

This branch of linear algebra has been hugely influential in the field of sparse reformulations of conic optimization problems over the space of symmetric matrices, which are optimization problems that are linear except for a single, nonlinear, conic constraint. Such problems often arise from convex reformulations and relaxations of nonconvex optimization problems. Results involving the positive semidefinite matrix are plentiful and include
[
18
,
25
,
29
]
to name a few; a more exhaustive (but by no means complete) survey is given in
[
28
]
. Another strain of literature is concerned with reformulations based on the completely positive matrix cone (which contains all matrices of the form
{\mathsf{B}}{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
{\mathsf{B}}
is nonnegative) and its generalizations (see
[
3
,
8
,
13
]
as well as the surveys
[
4
,
6
,
12
]
). In many interesting cases, the linear constraints and objective of these problems exhibit sparsity patterns, i.e. patterns of zeros, so that certain entries of the matrix variable appear only in the conic constraint.

In such cases, sparse relaxations allow replacing the computationally costly conic constraint by a collection of such constraints on certain submatrices of the matrix variable (ideally of small order), thereby eliminating spurious entries of the matrix variable from the problem description entirely. Such reformulations can provide substantial computational benefits and have therefore been studied extensively.

Results regarding the positive semidefinite matrix cone were first studied in
[
14
]
, which sparked a plethora of investigations, an excellent survey of which is given in
[
27
]
.
Reformulations involving the completely positive matrix cone were discussed for example in
[
20
,
15
]
.
To ensure that a sparse relaxation is exact, one typically employs matrix completion results, most importantly
[
19
]
for psd
(positive semi-definite)
completion and
[
11
]
for completely positive completion. Suppose the submatrices present in the sparse relaxation can be completed to a matrix that fulfills the original conic constraint. In that case, the relaxation gap can be closed and the computational benefits can be reaped at no additional cost.

Unfortunately, said results on matrix completion impose a lot of restrictions on the sparsity pattern and the type of conic constraint, which we will discuss in greater detail later in the text. In this note, we want to improve upon existing results in this regard. To this end, we will consider sparsity patterns with a certain type of arrowhead structure. Matrices with this sparsity pattern are not within the domain of classical matrix completion results as far as the completely positive matrices are concerned. Our main result is a new sufficient condition for completability of matrices with arrow-head specification pattern, where the
width of the diagonal is equal to one
, and where the completion is with respect to certain set-completely positive matrix cones that are more general than the ’traditional’ completely positive matrix cone. Set-completely positive completion has been considered before in
[
15
]
, but results on sufficient conditions therein were very limited. We arrive at these results by employing copositive optimization techniques. This is interesting because, rather than using linear algebra results for the benefit of optimization, we use techniques from optimization in order to tackle a problem in linear algebra. However, optimization also benefits from our result since it can potentially be used to strengthen conservative approximations of sparse reformulations discussed in
[
15
]
, but we also use it
in
the present text to derive new ex-post certificates of exactness for sparse relaxations of a certain type of completely positive optimization problem
, i.e.,
certificates deduced
from the optimal solution of the relaxation
rather than from the problem structure. We hope that our approach inspires further investigation into the subject. Before laying out our derivation in greater detail, we will briefly review some important concepts regarding conic reformulations, sparse relaxations, and matrix completion and introduce some notation and preliminaries.

1.2
Conic and copositive optimization

By conic optimization problems over the space of symmetric matrices, we mean problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\right\},

(2)

where
{\mathcal{A}}\colon{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear map, the vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
is a nonempty, closed, convex matrix cone. We will be concerned with the case where the latter cone is given by the set-completely positive matrix cone, which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\coloneqq\conv\left\{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}\right\}=\left\{{\mathsf{X}}{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathsf{T}}\colon{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{r},\ r\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n}_{+},

for a nonempty closed convex cone
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
called the ground cone and
{\mathcal{K}}^{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is the cone of
n\times r
matrices whose columns are vectors in
{\mathcal{K}}
. For example,
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={\mathcal{S}}^{n}_{+}
, and
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
is the ’traditional’ completely positive matrix cone introduced in
[
23
]
and extensively studied in
[
2
,
10
]
. Since they are subsets of the psd cone, the
\mathcal{CPP}
cones are
pointed, i.e.,
they do not contain a line. Also, by
[
4
, Proposition 11]
, they are closed if the respective ground cone is closed.
All ground cones in this article are henceforth assumed to be nonempty, closed, and convex, so that respective set-completely positive matrix cones are nonempty and closed as well.

Another useful fact is that for
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},\ {\mathcal{K}}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
and
{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{1}},\ {\mathsf{Y}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{2}},\ {\mathsf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
we have that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{X}}&{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}&{\mathsf{Y}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{1}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{2})\mbox{ implies }{\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{1}),\ {\mathsf{Y}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{2}),

(3)

which is immediate from the definition of set-completely positive matrix cones.
Throughout the paper, we will write
\mathcal{CPP}
instead of
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
, whenever the ground cone is not important to the discussion or clear from the context, and we do the same with similar cones discussed shortly.

Note, that if in the description of the feasible set of (
2
) we replace
{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
by
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, then it represents a generic quadratically constrained conic quadratic problem (QCCQP). Indeed, obtaining relaxations for this difficult class of problems is one of the major motivations for studying (
2
) with
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{CPP}
. A central object in this regard is the set:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq\mathrm{cl}\conv\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1},\mbox{ where }{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\mbox{ (an arbitrary feasible set)},

and its set-completely positive characterizations, which feature prominently in the literature on copositive reformulations of nonconvex QCCQPs (see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
13
]
). For a quadratic optimization problem over an arbitrary set
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we always have:

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182pt\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{{\mathsf{Q}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+2{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\colon\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\right\}\,,

(4)

which is proved for example in
[
4

but earlier treatments are found in
[

,

,

]
.
Most importantly, we will make use of the following characterization derived in

:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}})\colon\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}=&\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbf{b},\\
\Diag\left({\mathsf{A}}{\mathsf{X}}{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=&\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbf{b}\circ\mathbf{b}\end{array}\right\},\mbox{ for }{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}\colon{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\right\},

where the
ground cone

and the

We also want to highlight the fact that a matrix in

Proposition 2
.

) and let

Proof.

From the containment of the respective matrix in

and the definition of that cone, we get

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{l}\\

\mathbf{x}_{l}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mu_{l}^{2}&\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mu_{l}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}\\

so that

since

is closed and convex and hence closed under addition and nonnegative scalar multiplication (only positive scalar multiplication would be allowed if the cone did not contain the origin, but all closed cones do). Since

1.3
Sparse counterparts in conic optimization

Next, we discuss the sparse counterparts of symmetric matrix cones. For a graph
G\coloneqq\left\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\right\}

with vertices in
{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq[1\!:\!n]
and undirected edges in
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\left\{\left\{i,j\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\right\}
we define a symmetric partial matrix
{\mathsf{M}}_{G}
to be a matrix whose entry
({\mathsf{M}}_{G})_{ij}
is specified if and only if either
\left\{i,j\right\}\in{\mathcal{E}}
or
i=j
and unspecified otherwise. Such entries will be denoted by "
*
". We call
G
the specification graph of the partial matrix
{\mathsf{M}}_{G}
. The space of symmetric partial matrices with specifications graph
G
is denoted as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(G)\coloneqq\left\{{\mathsf{M}}_{G}\colon{\mathsf{M}}_{G}\mbox{ is a partial matrix with specification graph }G\right\},

so that
{\mathcal{S}}(G)={\mathcal{S}}^{n}
exactly if
G
is a complete graph. As in this definition, we will
notationally
distinguish elements in
{\mathcal{S}}(G)
from elements of such spaces generated from other graphs, in particular
{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
, by by indexing the partial matrix with the name of the specification graph of the space it belongs to. For example, if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are both specification graphs, then elements of
{\mathcal{S}}(G_{1})
will be indexed by
G_{1}
, e.g.
{\mathsf{M}}_{G_{1}}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G_{1})
and elements of
{\mathcal{S}}(G_{2})
will be indexed by
G_{2}
, e.g.
{\mathsf{M}}_{G_{2}}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G_{2})
. This should help readers keep track of what space we operate in at any given point in the text. We will also continue to denote a generic specification graph by
G
.

If for such a partial matrix we can assign values to the unspecified entries such that we obtain a fully specified matrix in
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
, we say that the partial matrix is completable with respect to that matrix cone. Accordingly, we define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\coloneqq\left\{{\mathsf{M}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon{\mathsf{M}}_{G}\mbox{ is completable with respect to }\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}.

Note that this set is nonempty, since one can take any element of
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
and declare entries nonspecified according to
G
in order to obtain an element in
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})
.

This set can now be used to derive sparse reformulations of (
2
). Firstly, note that any linear map
{\mathcal{A}}\colon{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{A}}_{1}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathsf{A}}_{m}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ for some appropriate }{\mathsf{A}}_{l}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n},\ l\in[1\!:\!m].

Secondly, for two symmetric partial matrices
\left\{{\mathsf{A}}_{G},{\mathsf{B}}_{G}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}(G)
there is a natural analog of the Frobenius product in the space of partial matrices given by
{\mathsf{A}}_{G}\bullet{\mathsf{B}}_{G}=2\sum_{\left\{i,j\right\}\in{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathsf{A}}_{G})_{ij}({\mathsf{B}}_{G})_{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\mathsf{A}}_{G})_{ii}({\mathsf{B}}_{G})_{ii}
, i.e. the Frobenius product of the two partial matrices after replacing the unspecified entries with zeros. We will not distinguish the two Frobenius products notationally, since it will always be clear from the inputs which version is in order.
Now assume that
{\mathcal{A}}(.)
is such that for any
\left\{i,j\right\}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\notin}{\mathcal{E}}
the entries
({\mathsf{A}}_{1})_{ij},\dots,({\mathsf{A}}_{m})_{ij}
are zero so that their Frobenius products with
{\mathsf{X}}
are constant in
({\mathsf{X}})_{ij}
, in other words
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}})
is independent of those entries. Then we can replace
{\mathsf{A}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}},\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l}\in[1\!:\!m]
with partial matrices
{\mathsf{A}}^{l}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G),\ l\in[1\!:\!m]
such that
({\mathsf{A}}^{l}_{G})_{ij}=({\mathsf{A}}_{l})_{ij}
if
\left\{i,j\right\}\in{\mathcal{E}}
or
i=j
to obtain a natural
restriction
of
{\mathcal{A}}(.)
to the space of symmetric partial matrices, say
{\mathcal{A}}_{G}\colon{\mathcal{S}}(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, so that
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}})={\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})
whenever
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}
is a partial matrix with specification graph
G
obtained from the (fully specified) matrix
{\mathsf{X}}
by declaring entries to be unspecified according to
G
. An analogous projection
{\mathsf{C}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)
of a symmetric matrix
{\mathsf{C}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
, whose entries with index
\left\{i,j\right\}\in{\mathcal{E}}
are again assumed to be zero, provides
{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{C}}_{G}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{G}
. With this in place, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\min_{{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}=\hskip-5.69046pt\min_{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)}\left\{{\mathsf{C}}_{G}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}G}}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\right\},

(7)

since any feasible solution of the latter problem can be completed to an equivalent solution to the former while the converse can be obtained by declaring the respective entries of a feasible solution
{\mathsf{X}}
to be unspecified.

The sparse reformulation has fewer variables, but the set
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})
is not workable in general, in the sense that we do not have algorithms to optimize over this set directly. Thus, the standard approach is to replace this matrix cone with an outer approximation given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{PCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\coloneqq\left\{{\mathsf{M}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{ Every fully specified principal submatrix of ${\mathsf{M}}_{G}$}\\
\mbox{is the principal submatrix of a matrix in }\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\\
\mbox{ induced by the same rows and columns. }\end{array}\right\}.

Note that this cone would not change if the condition was instead stated in terms of the maximal fully specified submatrices, i.e. fully specified submatrices that are not submatrices of other fully specified submatrices. Thus, we only consider maximal fully specified submatrices henceforth, but refer to those as fully specified submatrices for convenience.

In many interesting cases, this set can be characterized by using conic constraints on each of the fully specified submatrices. For example,
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of partial matrices, such that every fully specified principal submatrix is positive semidefinite, so that the
\mathcal{PCP}
-relaxation is a
semidefinite optimization problem
, which can be solved, for example, via interior point methods.
On the other hand,
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
contains all the partial matrices where the respective submatrices are completely positive (in the classical sense), so that the
\mathcal{PCP}
-relaxation is a completely positive optimization problem. For these types of problems, practical approaches exist, see for example the respective sections in
[
9
,
4
]
.

The intersection
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}\cap\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
is never empty, since both contain the origin of
{\mathcal{S}}(G)
as
{\mathsf{O}}\in\mathcal{CPP}
whenever the ground cone is closed. In fact, we always have
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}\supseteq\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
, since a completable matrix necessarily fulfills the condition stated in
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}
.
From this, we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hskip-28.45274pt\min_{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}}\left\{{\mathsf{C}}_{G}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\\
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\end{array}\right\}\geq\min_{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}}\left\{{\mathsf{C}}_{G}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\\
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in\mathcal{PCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\end{array}\right\},\end{split}

(8)

and the majority of approaches to sparse reformulations are concerned with closing the relaxation gap in (
8
) by identifying conditions on
G
under which
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}=\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
. The classical results are summarized as follows:

Proposition 3
.

The following statements hold:

•

\mathcal{PCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{CCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
G
is a chordal graph.

•

\mathcal{PCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})=\mathcal{CCP}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
if and only if
G
is a block-clique graph.

Proof.

This proposition merely summarizes the main results in
[
11
,
19
]
.
∎

Graphs are chordal if every induced cycle is a triangle. A graph is a block-clique if every block is a clique and a block is a subgraph that is connected, has no subgraph that can become disconnected by removing just one vertex and its adjacent edges (i.e. a cut vertex), and is not contained in any other subgraph with these two properties. For a detailed explanation of chordal graphs and block clique graphs, the reader is referred to the references given in the proof above; a concise summary of these definitions and the theory of matrix completion can be found in
[
2
]
. We will not delve into the details here, as they are not important for the rest of the text.

1.4
From sparse reformulations to matrix completion

It was noted in
[
15
]
that requiring
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}=\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
is excessively strong since all that is needed to close the gap in (
8
) is that the optimal set of the relaxation contains a single completable matrix. Hence, alternative conditions for the completablility of a matrix in
\mathcal{PCP}_{G}
are desirable. The main objective of this text is to identify such conditions by working in the opposite direction. Focusing on sparse matrices with arrow-head sparsity patterns with thin diagonals, we will prove sufficient conditions under which the gap in (
8
) vanishes. This will then be used to derive a new sufficient condition for the completability of such matrices with respect to certain set-completely positive matrices cones. The bridge between closing the relaxation gap in (
8
) and obtaining a completability result is established in the following theorem.

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
be two convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}
for all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{1})=\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{2})
.

Proof.

Assume there was a point
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{1})\setminus\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{2})
. Then by strong convex separation there are
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{o}\right\},\ \mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}<\mu\leq\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x},\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
which implies the contradiction
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}>\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}
. The analogous argument applies if
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{2})\setminus\mathrm{cl}({\mathcal{C}}_{1})
.
∎

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}_{G},\ \mathbf{b}
and
G
are such that the inequality in (
8
) holds with equality for all
{\mathsf{C}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in\mathcal{PCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\implies{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})
.

Proof.

Under our assumptions, the closures of the feasible sets of the two optimization problems in (
8
) have to be identical, by
4
. Thus, the theorem will follow if we can show that the set
\left\{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}
is closed, since then we get

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\left\{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{PCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathrm{cl}\left\{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{PCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cl}\left\{{\mathsf{X}}_{G}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G)\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathsf{X}}_{G})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})\right\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}}).

It suffices to show that
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed. But this follows immediately from the fact that
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed (see for example
[
4
, Proposition 11]
or
[
26
, Lemma 1]
). To see this consider a sequence
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}^{k}\in\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}}),\ k\in\mathbb{N}
with
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\mathsf{X}}_{G}^{k}={\mathsf{X}}_{G}
. Then there is a sequence of completions
{\mathsf{X}}^{k}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
for which we have
({\mathsf{X}}^{k})_{ij}\rightarrow({\mathsf{X}}_{G})_{ij}
whenever
(i,j)
is specified. In particular,
\diag({\mathsf{X}}^{k})\rightarrow\diag({\mathsf{X}}_{G})
since all the diagonal entries are specified. Hence, the diagonal entries of the series of completions can eventually be bounded, which, from the fact that
{\mathsf{X}}^{k}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n}_{+}
, implies that all the entries are bounded eventually. Thus, we see that, perhaps after switching to a subsequence, we have
{\mathsf{X}}^{k}\rightarrow{\mathsf{X}}
with
({\mathsf{X}})_{ij}=({\mathsf{X}}_{G})_{ij}
for all
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
and
\diag({\mathsf{X}})=\diag({\mathsf{X}}_{G})
. But since
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed,
{\mathsf{X}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
is a set-completely positive completion of
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}
so that
{\mathsf{X}}_{G}
is an element of
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}({\mathcal{K}})
, which completes the proof
∎

This theorem plays a central role in the derivation of our new completion result, as we will identify conditions on the problem data under which the gap in (
8
) can be closed (see
9
), which
5
then translates into sufficient conditions for completability (see
10
).

Remark
.

In
[
27
]
the closedness of
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
was proved for the special case
{\mathcal{K}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}

and the fact that
\mathcal{CCP}_{G}
can be regarded as a type of projection of
\mathcal{CPP}
. A similar strategy could have been used for the above theorem, but a direct proof is more accessible.

2
Partial matrices with arrow-head specification pattern

Partial matrices with arrowhead specification patterns can be written as

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{X}}&{\mathsf{Z}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}&\dots&{\mathsf{Z}}_{S-1}^{\mathsf{T}}&{\mathsf{Z}}_{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{1}&{\mathsf{Y}}_{1}&\mathbf{*}&\dots&\mathbf{*}&\mathbf{*}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}&\mathbf{*}&{\mathsf{Y}}_{2}&\dots&\mathbf{*}&\mathbf{*}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{S-1}&\mathbf{*}&\mathbf{*}&\dots&{\mathsf{Y}}_{S-1}&\mathbf{*}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{S}&\mathbf{*}&\mathbf{*}&\dots&\mathbf{*}&{\mathsf{Y}}_{S}\end{bmatrix},\

where
{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{1}},\ {\mathsf{Y}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{2}},\ {\mathsf{Z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}},\ i\in[1\!:\!S].
The fully specified submatrices are given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{X}}&{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}&{\mathsf{Y}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{1}+n_{2}},\ i\in[1\!:\!S].

Such sparsity patterns may arise in conic reformulations of two-stage stochastic quadratic optimization problems as studied in
[
5
,
15
]
. The specification graph of such a partial matrix, say
G
, consists of
S+1
cliques, where the first one encompasses
n_{1}
nodes, each of which is connected to all other nodes of
G
. The other
S
cliques are of size
n_{2}
and are not connected to each other. Henceforth, we will indicate the specification graph partial matrices with arrowhead sparsity as
G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
.

If we relabel the nodes in such a graph its structure does not change, but the specification pattern of the associated partial matrices does. The effect on those matrices would be akin to permuting rows and columns in a symmetric fashion so that this pattern would no longer resemble an arrowhead. On the other hand, if the specification graph
G
of a partial matrix
{\mathsf{A}}_{G}
has a structure like
G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
then we can find a relabeling such that the specification pattern of the associated partial matrix
{\mathsf{B}}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}
has arrowhead structure. If we find completion of the latter matrix with respect to
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
, we also get a completion of
{\mathsf{A}}_{G}
with respect to
\mathcal{CPP}(\bar{{\mathcal{K}}})
where
{\mathcal{K}}
is obtained from
\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}
via the appropriate permutation of coordinates. Hence, our discussion applies more broadly to partial matrices whose specification graph has the same structure as
G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
. However, the simplicity of our presentation would suffer greatly if we abandoned the arrowhead pattern as our point of reference. Hence, we focus on matrices with such patterns and trust that the reader can envision the aforementioned generalization themselves.

We have the following characterization of
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{P}}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}
in case the ground cone decomposes into smaller cones in a convenient manner, and it will be instrumental in the succeeding sections.

Proposition 6
.

Define
{\mathcal{K}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{K}}_{S}
for some
ground
cones
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{P}}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}({\mathcal{K}})=\left\{{\mathsf{M}}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}(G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S})\colon\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{X}}&{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}&{\mathsf{Y}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{i}),\ i\in[1\!:\!S]\right\}.

Proof.

Let
G\coloneqq G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
for notational convenience.
For any partial matrix
{\mathsf{M}}_{G}
in the right hand side set, take the
i
-th fully specified principle submatrix
{\mathsf{M}}_{i}={\mathsf{X}}_{i}{\mathsf{X}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where the factor
{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\in({\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{i})^{r}
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
certifies
{\mathsf{M}}_{i}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{i})
. Let
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
be the row and column indices of that submatrix relative to
{\mathsf{M}}_{G}
. We can augment
{\mathsf{X}}_{i}
by adding rows of zeros in order to get a matrix in
\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{r}
. More precisely, we can add
(i-1)n_{2}
rows of zeros after the first
n_{1}
rows of
{\mathsf{X}}_{i}
and also add
(S-i)n_{2}
such rows at the end of
{\mathsf{X}}_{i}
. For the so constructed matrix we have
\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}_{i}\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
since
\mathbf{o}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
as they are closed cones. The submatrix induced by
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
is
{\mathsf{M}}_{i}
as desired. The converse follows from the fact that for any matrix
{\mathsf{M}}\in\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})
with certificate
{\mathsf{M}}={\mathsf{X}}{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathsf{T}},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{r}
we may delete the aforementioned rows from
{\mathsf{X}}
to obtain an
{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
which certifies that the submatrix
{\mathsf{M}}_{i}={\mathsf{X}}_{i}{\mathsf{X}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
of
{\mathsf{M}}_{G}
induced by
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
is a member of
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{i})
.
∎

Note that
{\mathcal{K}}\in\left\{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{1}+n_{2}S},\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}S}\right\}
can always be cast as a Cartesian product so that in the positive semidefinite/completely positive case the characterization requires that the fully specified submatrices are positive semidefinite/completely positive as per usual. The following lemma was proved in
[
15
]
:

Lemma 7
.

The specification graph
G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
is chordal. If
S>1
, then
G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}
is a block-clique graph if and only if
n_{1}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
.

The important take away from this result is that in light of

\mathcal{PCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{CCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
but
\mathcal{PCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\supset\mathcal{CCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
unless
n_{1}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
; we used the abbreviation

. The main contribution of our paper will be a result on sufficient conditions for matrices with arrow-head specification pattern to be completable with respect to a cone that is more general than just
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
but where

.

As discussed above, not all partial matrices of this kind are completable. In fact the classical example of a partial matrix in
\mathcal{PCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\setminus\mathcal{CCP}_{G_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})

is given by an arrow-head partial matrix with

. For the reader’s convenience, we repeat this example here:

Consider the following partial matrix and its fully specified principal submatrices

\end{bmatrix},\ {\mathsf{A}}_{1}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}6&3&0\\

A conditionally exact sparse relaxation

Our goal in this section is to provide a
\mathcal{PCP}
-based, conditionally tight, relaxation of the following quadratic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\inf_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{S}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{S}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}+\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}+\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}_{i}\right)}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{f}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}=d_{0},\ \mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{0},}\\

\mathbf{y}_{i}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S],\end{array}\right\},\end{split}

(9)

where

are

{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{Y}}_{i,i},\\

Lemma 8

Let
{\mathcal{K}}

\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{K}}^{*})

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}

\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}

First, note that
\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{K}}^{*})

Theorem 9

Conclusion and outlook

In this text, we derived a new set-completely positive matrix completion result, which gives sufficient conditions for a partial matrix with an arrowhead specification pattern and width equal to one to be completable with respect to certain set-completely positive matrix cones, including the classical completely positive cone. While usually such completion results are used to close sparse relaxation gaps, we went the opposite direction and used an exactness result in order to infer our completion result. We showed how this completion result can be applied to an instance of a sparse reformulation that, in general, does not lie within the regime of our exactness result.

The caveat is that our techniques require the sparse completely positive reformulation to be solved exactly, i.e. without replacing the
\mathcal{CPP}
-constraint with one of its many approximations, which is possible for example via the techniques in
[
7
,
16
]
. However, approximations of the complicated conic constraints are also popular, and at this point, it is not clear how these relaxations behave when applied to the sparse completely positive relaxation vs. the full completely positive reformulation. It stands to reason that any approximation scheme incurs a smaller loss if it is applied to the smaller
\mathcal{CPP}
constraints of the sparse reformulation, so that in case the sparse relaxation is tight, the respective approximation performs at least as good as if the same approximation is applied to the full reformulation. However, it seems difficult to quantify these losses caused by approximations in relation to the order of the matrix block. But we hope that we can investigate this relation in future research.
