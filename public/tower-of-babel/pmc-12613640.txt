How do basic numerical skills matter in the association of phonological processing with arithmetic?
Authors: Ningxin Zhao, Zimo Wang, Minqi Gao, Xin Chen, Xiujie Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing body of research has demonstrated that phonological processing skills are associated with arithmetic performance. The present study intended to further investigate which among the numerical abilities might explain the underlying mechanism of the relationships between phonological processing skills and arithmetic.MethodsWe examined Grade 3–5 elementary students (N = 230; aged 9 to 12 years). Data were collected in March 2021. Measures included phonological awareness, rapid automatized naming (RAN), two symbolic numerical skills (i.e., number ordering and number comparison), two nonsymbolic numerical abilities (i.e., dot comparison and counting), and simple arithmetic tasks. Structural equation modeling was employed, with age and gender controlled as covariates.ResultsPhonological awareness and RAN showed direct effects on arithmetic skills. Among the four basic number skills, only number ordering significantly mediated the association between phonological awareness and arithmetic.ConclusionsSymbolic numerical skills (i.e., number ordering ability) rather than nonsymbolic numerical skills are important for explaining the relationship between phonological processing and arithmetic in primary school children.Keywords: Arithmetic, Number Ordering, Phonological Awareness, Rapid Automatized Naming

Background

Phonological processing is a crucial aspect of language development and involves the ability to manipulate and process sounds in spoken and written language [82, 89]. Phonological awareness, phonological memory, and rapid automatized naming (RAN) are three key components of phonological processing [36, 82, 89]. Previous research has suggested that phonological awareness and RAN are more important predictors of arithmetic performance than phonological memory [65, 89]. The present study mainly explored the underlying mechanisms between phonological awareness, RAN, and arithmetic, that is, how do phonological awareness and RAN contribute to arithmetic? In this study, we hypothesized that some of the basic numerical skills might help to explain the relationship between phonological processing and arithmetic.

The relationship between phonological processing and arithmetic

Phonological awareness refers to the ability to recognize and manipulate the sound structure of spoken language [57]. This skill can be assessed by tasks that involve identifying rhythms or manipulating syllables in words [88]. Research has demonstrated that phonological awareness is highly predictive of reading ability [4, 30, 61]. Children with better phonological awareness tend to acquire decoding skills more rapidly, which facilitates fluent reading acquisition. Building on its well-established role in reading, recent research has also begun to highlight the predictive power of phonological awareness for arithmetic performance [8, 55, 89]. For example, Yang & McBride [87] identified a specific association of phonological awareness with arithmetic, even when reading was statistically controlled. Krajewski and Schneider [47] have suggested that phonological awareness plays a key role in manipulating and using verbal representations of numbers, which in turn facilitates the linkage between quantity and number words, and further supports arithmetic ability. Simmons et al. [77] found that phonological awareness measured in 5-year-olds was independently associated with arithmetic attainment 12 months later. Similarly, the weak phonological representations hypothesis [76] proposes that poorly specified phonological representations may result in poor performance on numerical tasks, particularly those that involve manipulating verbal codes.

Rapid automatized naming (RAN) is another crucial component of phonological processing and is often referred to as the rate of accessto phonological information stored in long-term memory [82]. It is commonly measured by asking participants to quickly name arrays of familiar stimuli such as objects, digits, letters, and colors [16, 62, 75]. The relationship between RAN and arithmetic performance has been supported by numerous cross-sectional studies [43, 83], longitudinal studies [86], and meta-analyses [44, 89]. There are differing views on why RAN plays a significant role in complex arithmetic tasks. One view suggests that RAN measures the capacity to establish associations between visual symbols and phonological representations, which is also essential for arithmetic [46]. Another view supports that RAN reflects global processing speed when executing multiple cognitive processes [39].

Taken together, the studies above suggest that phonological awareness and RAN are important predictors of arithmetic performance in children. However, the relationship between phonological processing and arithmetic may not be direct. The underlying mechanism of how phonological processing components influence arithmetic outcomes remains unclear.

Phonological processing, symbolic number processing, and arithmetic

Since children's mathematical competence emerges from two systems, the nonsymbolic system and the symbolic representation system [35], basic numerical processing, including nonsymbolic and symbolic processing, might be the key to understanding the relationship between phonological skills and arithmetic. Moreover, jointly considering the two systems is essential, as their interplay can offer deeper insights into how children acquire arithmetic skills [54, 74, 79] and how phonological awareness, contribute to this developmental process.

Symbolic processing refers to the ability to represent numbers visually as number symbols. Mastery of symbolic numbers is a prerequisite for successful arithmetic [42]. To examine the different types of representation in Arabic numbers, number comparison and number ordering tasks are commonly used [70]. In number comparison tasks, children are presented with pairs of numbers and must decide which number is numerically larger. Several studies have shown the role of symbolic magnitude comparison in explaining individual differences in children’s arithmetic abilities [21, 73]. This finding is supported by studies involving children with mathematics learning disabilities in which they exhibited impairment only in Arabic number comparison tasks but not in dot comparison tasks [69], suggesting that number comparison reflects the ability to access numerical magnitudes through Arabic numbers [72]. Based on the triple-code model, the speed of retrieving verbal code and magnitude information from a visual Arabic number can interact [22]. When comparing numbers, children's verbal or semantic information may be aroused first, and then translated from verbal to magnitude information. As phonological skills involve the ability to map and manipulate verbal codes [45, 47], we hypothesize that phonological skills might also contribute to number comparison tasks.

In the number ordering task, participants are instructed to discriminate whether the number triad is ‘in-order’ (all increasing, e.g., 1–2–3) or ‘not-in-order’ (decreasing or random, e.g., 2–1–3; [51]). Studies have shown that the ability to process ordinal relationships of symbolic numbers is strongly linked to arithmetic fluency in first-grade children [80]. Even when controlling for other factors such as number comparison and recognition, symbolic number ordering ability still strongly predicts scores on arithmetic tasks [51]. Importantly, Knops & Willmes [41] showed that when participants were conducting either numerical ordering or arithmetic tasks, there were overlapping regions in both the right Brodmann area 44 and the right-hemisphere homologue of Broca's area in the left hemisphere. Symbolic numerical ordering is considered a key to understanding how we represent symbolic numbers [50], and it was rather separated from the representation of the magnitude information of symbolic numbers [24, 70].

Similar to number comparison tasks, semantic translation of symbolic numbers is frequently used in number ordering task, which is related to phonological components [45, 47]. Recent research has shown that phonological processing may mediate the relationship between number ordering and arithmetic in the first three years of formal education [53]. Therefore, it is plausible that phonological awareness and rapid automatized naming (RAN) may also contribute to accurate performance in number ordering tasks.

Phonological processing, nonymbolic number processing, and arithmetic

Nonsymbolic understandings of numerical quantities have been shown to exist in infants [85] and are considered as the basis of arithmetic skills [7, 47]. For example, children can perform abstract comparisons, additions, and subtractions using nonsymbolic cues such as dot collections or sequences of sounds before formal arithmetic training [7]. In counting tasks, participants must count objects as quickly and accurately as possible [29, 78]. In the dot comparison task, children are presented with two collections of dots side by side, and they must decide which collection contains more dots (e.g., [5, 69]).

Counting is one of the early Basic Numerical Skills that children must master before learning calculations. To count dot collections accurately, children need other skills, such as phonological processing. Before counting, they must skillfully recite the number word sequence, and then they become aware that number words are linked to quantities, and counting skills develop over time [47]. This process involves phonological skills that facilitate the acquisition and application of number words. Moreover, studies have shown that RAN can indirectly explain variance in calculation fluency through counting [45]. Additionally, counting has been identified as a strong predictor of arithmetic skills [13, 28, 45, 60]. For example, in Koponen et al.’s [45] study, counting ability at the beginning of 1 st grade strongly predicted calculation fluency assessed in 2nd and 3rd grade, and more than 60% of the variance in arithmetic was predicted by counting. This suggests that accurate and fluent retrieval of the number word sequence is required for more efficient arithmetic [45].

Dot comparison taps a mental system of magnitude representation [56], which is highly linked to arithmetic performance [9, 21, 42]. Indeed, Halberda et al. [35] found that individual differences in dot comparison performance were associated with individual differences in arithmetic achievement, even after controlling for other cognitive and performance factors. This suggests that good performance on the dot comparison task may support children in selecting appropriate calculation strategies and evaluating the plausibility of their answers [58]. It is important to note that counting or counting-related strategies are often used in dot comparison, especially for long-present-duration and small-set-size comparison tasks, which are applied in the current study [9, 25]. This suggests that phonological skills may also play a role in children's performance on dot comparison. While nonsymbolic comparison is an innate ability [85], phonological skills are learned through environmental exposure [34]. Although nonsymbolic comparison skills can improve through development and training [63], there is currently limited knowledge on whether dot comparison takes advantage of phonological skills.

The current study

Altogether, previous studies have shown that phonological processing plays an important role in arithmetic performance. However, we have little knowledge about the underlying mechanism. Here, we propose a plausible mechanism that both symbolic and nonsymbolic numerical skills might play as mediators between phonological and arithmetic skills. In Grades 3–5, it is difficult to identify a basic numerical skill that significantly supersedes the other skills [52]. It might be the case that these skills are undergoing an integration process during this period [42]. Thus, we consider four basic numerical skills together as parallel relations and systematically investigate the relations between phonological processing, symbolic and nonsymbolic numerical skills, and arithmetic performance.

We mainly propose two research questions and corresponding hypotheses as follows. Our first question concerns the role of phonological processing in arithmetic. Based on previous literature, we assume that phonological awareness and RAN would strongly predict arithmetic performance after controlling for age and gender. For the second question, we focus on how phonological awareness and RAN contribute to arithmetic. More specifically, the level of one’s nonsymbolic numerical abilities (including counting and dot comparison) and symbolic numerical abilities (number comparison and number ordering) are key mechanisms that might mediate the relation between phonological components and arithmetic ability. Although each skill has been identified as an important predictor [42, 52], we assume a larger contribution of symbolic number processing than nonsymbolic number processing for the following reasons. First, previous studies suggested that symbolic processing skills are more directly linked to complex math abilities than nonsymbolic processing skills. For example, Lyons et al. [52] demonstrated that although nonsymbolic processing was significantly correlated with arithmetic, it was nonsignificant after controlling for other basic numerical skills, including symbolic tasks. Second, symbolic processing contains more linguistic components because understanding the number system is assumed to be similar to mastering any other symbolic system, such as written language [49]. Therefore, symbolic number processing could be more essential when linking phonological processing skills with children’s arithmetic performance.

Methods

Participants

Two hundred thirty elementary students (104 females, 126 males) were recruited from a primary school in HanDan city in Hebei Province from Grade 3 to Grade 5 in the study, including 71 students (34 females, 37 males) in Grade 3, 84 students (33 females, 51 males) in Grade 4 and 75 students (37 females, 38 males) in Grade 5. Their ages ranged from 9 to 12 years (M = 10.1 years, SD = 0.9). All children were native Mandarin speakers. No teachers or parents reported any intellectual disabilities, specific learning difficulties, other neurodevelopmental disorders, or visual and/or hearing impairments in the participating children. The average income of the families in Hebei in our study is at the middle level according to the national statistics data of the National Bureau of Statistics of the People's Republic of China (website: http://www.stats.gov.cn).

Measures

Phonological awareness

Syllable deletion was used to test children’s phonological awareness. In this 22-item task, children first were orally presented with a target multisyllable Chinese word on an A4 sheet of paper and then asked to tell what was left after deleting the first, middle or last syllable of the word (e.g., what is left of “知道”/zhi1dao4/without “知”/zhi1/). The number of syllables was increased, and two-syllable words were presented from Item 1 to Item 6, three-syllable words were presented from Item 7 to Item 14, and four-syllable words were presented from Item 15 to Item 22. The children received 1 point for each correct answer, and testing was terminated when children made five consecutive errors [86]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.86.

Rapid automatized naming

In this task, the children were asked to name five numbers (2, 4, 6, 7 and 9) as quickly as possible that were repeated 3 times each in random order in five rows of three on an A4 sheet of paper. Each child named the matrix twice, and the number of items per second across the two matrices was used as the participants’ score. The number of naming errors was negligible (mean number of errors was less than 1), and for this reason, it was not considered further [20, 88]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

Number ordering

In the ordinal processing task of symbolic numbers, the children were presented three numbers in three different orders (from largest to smallest, from smallest to largest, and mixed order) and were asked to judge what order the three numbers were in. There were 30 items in total: 10 items with numbers up to 20, 10 items with numbers up to 100 and 10 items with numbers up to 1,000 [84]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.90.

Number comparison

In the comparable task of symbolic numbers, children were presented with two single-digit numbers on a screen and asked to determine which number was larger in numerical magnitude, while disregarding physical size differences. The task consisted of 84 trials, divided into three conditions: neutral, congruent, and incongruent. Each condition included 28 trials. In the neutral condition, both numbers were of the same physical size. In the congruent condition, the numerically smaller number was also physically smaller, and in the incongruent condition, the numerically smaller number was physically larger. Children responded by pressing the ‘Q’ key if the number on the left side was larger or the ‘P’ key if the number on the right side was larger [33, 81], Zhou et al. 2007; [84]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.88.

Dot comparison

In this task, two arrays of dots were presented sequentially on the screen, each lasting for 200 ms and varying from 7 to 14. The children were asked to judge which dot array contained more dots by pressing ‘Q’ if the first array contained more dots or ‘P’ if the second array contained more dots. The interval between participants’ response to the last dot array and the onset of the next dot array was 1,000 ms. The dots were spread out and occupied the same area regardless of the number of dots [32, 84]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.96.

Dot counting

In this 56-trial task, one array of dots from 11 to 99 was presented on the screen for 200 ms. The children were asked to count how many dots there were. All the items needed to be completed. The participants entered their estimate for each array into the computer using the number keypad. After each trial, the participants received feedback on whether their estimate was correct. They were asked to adjust the next estimation according to the feedback. The formal test had two sessions, each with 28 trials. The dependent variable was the deviation of the estimated quantity from the correct quantity [11, 48, 84]. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.81.

Simple arithmetic

The simple arithmetic task was used to test children’s arithmetic skills. Multiple-digit addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division problems were presented in the middle of the upper part of the screen, and four candidate answers having the same number of digits were presented in the lower part of the screen (two on the left side and the other two on the right side). Only one of the four answers was correct, and the other three incorrect answers were constrained to be the correct answer plus or minus 1 or 10 [84]. Two two-digit numbers were involved in the addition tasks with the answer as a three-digit number; two two-digit numbers were involved in the subtraction tasks with the answer as a two-digit number; one two-digit number and one single-digit number were involved in the multiplication tasks with the answer as a three-digit number; and one three-digit number divided by one single-digit number were involved in the division tasks with the result as a two-digit number [84]. The participants were asked to solve the problem and judge whether the correct answer was on the left or the right side by pressing the corresponding key on the keyboard. The children received 1 point for each correct answer. The reliability of Cronbach’s α was 0.75.

Analytic approach

Data analysis progressed in three stages. First, the missing data were defined as 2.5 SDs above/below the mean of the sample, were removed. A similar approach was utilized by previous research [66]. As a result, 32 outliers in each task were identified and deleted, and a total of 198 subjects were included in the model. Second, we examined the distribution of each variable and the correlations among all variables. These two stages were performed in SPSS software version 26. Third, mediation analyses were conducted using the Mplus software version 8.0 [59]. Participants’ age and gender were included as control variables. Mplus calculated a bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrapped confidence interval (5,000 resamples) for the size of each indirect effect, with significant mediation indicated by a confidence interval that does not contain zero. Missing data were handled with Mplus 8 using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML; [3]) estimation. This procedure has been found to outperform other ad hoc procedures, such as listwise and pairwise deletion, when handling missing data [26].

The measurement model was assessed using multiple fit indices, including the chi-square value (), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker‒Lewis index (TLI), and the standardized root mean square residuals (SRMR). The guidelines for evaluating the adequacy of fit as proposed are as follows: CFI and TLI values equal to or greater than 0.95, RMSEA values with the upper bound at or less than 0.08, and SRMR values equal to or less than 0.06 [10, 38, 40].

Procedures

Before the commencement of data collection, this study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the faculty of psychology at Beijing Normal University. Children’s parents or guardians agreed with and signed the consent forms before any testing was conducted. The tests were conducted in small groups of one to four participants at school in March 2021 by trained graduate students who had received extensive training on how to implement the tasks. All the tasks were administered on the computer and were administered on the same day, lasting approximately one hour. All participants followed the same order of tasks. Before each task, the participants were given instructions and practice trials, and there was sufficient rest between tasks to reduce fatigue. They responded by pressing ‘P’ or ‘Q’ on the keyboard, clicking on the right answer on the screen, or entering a numerical value into a blank. All the tasks are available on the website ‘http://www.dweipsy.com/’ [84].

Mediation analyses

Mediation was analyzed using Mplus 8.0 based on children who had complete data points. All variables were raw data for analysis. The parameter estimates of the structural equation modeling are presented in Fig. 1. The final model fit the data very well ((30) = 145.177, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.991, RMSEA = 0.013, SRMR = 0.021). The results showed that after controlling for age and gender, both phonological awareness and RAN significantly predicted arithmetic skills (β = 0.268, p < 0.001; β = −0.325, p < 0.001). Phonological awareness also significantly predicted number ordering (β = 0.384, p < 0.001), while number ordering had a significant influence on arithmetic skills (β = 0.152, p < 0.01).

Discussion

The current study examined how different phonological processing components were associated with arithmetic skills. The findings revealed the association between phonological processing and arithmetic in students in grades 3–5. The strong correlation between phonological awareness and RAN is in line with previous studies (e.g., [89]). This suggests that phonological representations are frequently utilized in both phonological awareness and arithmetic tasks [47, 76], and that RAN may reflect an ability to link visual numbers and phonological information [44], or general processing speed [39], both of which are crucial in arithmetic.

Our second goal was to investigate the mechanism underlying the relationship between phonological components and arithmetic. We proposed that symbolic and nonsymbolic numerical skills may act as mediators between phonological processing and arithmetic skills, with symbolic numerical skills playing a stronger mediating role. Our results supported this hypothesis, with symbolic numerical skills, specifically number ordering, acting as significant mediators between phonological processing and arithmetic skills. Why might this be the case? Number ordering tasks require children to represent and manipulate symbolic numbers, relying on phonological representation and semantic translation. In contrast, number comparison tasks reflect a cardinal understanding of symbolic numbers, whereas number ordering taps into the ability to encode ordinal and declarative information in a numerical context [24, 51]. Skillful arithmetic increasingly relies on accessing declarative information in 3rd-5th graders rather than cardinal information.

Likewise, Lyons et al. [52] demonstrated that number comparison was predictive only of arithmetic in junior grades, but it became less important in senior grades. In contrast, there was a growing role of number ordering in the development of arithmetic. Similarly, Sasanguie & Vos [71] suggested that the predictive value of arithmetic shifted from cardinal processing in Grade 1 to ordinal processing in Grade 2. Moreover, an overlapping network in the frontoparietal regions was found when adults were conducting both number ordering and arithmetic tasks [41]. This might be the reason why ordinal understanding, rather than cardinality, is more crucial for a grown arithmetic performer.

The lack of association between phonological components and nonsymbolic processing in our study may be due to several factors. Firstly, as children grow older, they rely less on counting and more on fact retrieval in arithmetic tasks [18], and therefore, counting ability may become less relevant as a predictor of nonsymbolic processing. Secondly, in our dot comparison task, the time limit of 5000 ms per trial may not have allowed enough time for participants to use counting strategies effectively. Additionally, the relationship between phonological and nonsymbolic numerical skills is debated in the literature, with some suggesting that nonsymbolic processing is an innate ability that is distinct from phonological skills [47, 85]. While the nonsymbolic system becomes more accurate with age, its contribution to arithmetic skills is limited. Indeed, the underlying processes of the nonsymbolic system are partially involved in symbolic numerical processing, especially in children over 8 years old [52], and a strong correlation between symbolic and nonsymbolic processing skills in our study).

Unexpectedly, we did not find a mediating effect between RAN and arithmetic in the current research. Previous research suggests that RAN may reflect a domain-general retrieval ability, which is utilized no matter what information needs to be retrieved from long-term memory. In line with these findings, recent research suggests that the neural mechanisms underlying RAN directly support arithmetic fluency, highlighting its immediate role in arithmetic development [90]. Evidence emerged from findings that in the relation between arithmetic and RAN, the fluency score in arithmetic tasks produced a stronger association than accuracy [44], and single-digit arithmetic tasks produced a higher correlation than that in multidigit tasks [43, 45, 46]. Importantly, studies also found an equally strong correlation among RAN tasks using different materials, including naming digits, letters, colors, and other objects [44]. Indeed, RAN taps the ability to retrieve phonological information from visual cues, but we assume that it fails to predict the ability to access either magnitude and ordinal representation from visual number symbols or nonsymbolic information. Another possibility, in our arithmetic task, was that participants were asked to solve problems as quickly as they could, and processing speed was highly needed. Thus, it might be the case that while RAN reflects general processing speed [39], the fast process shapes arithmetic fluency.

Finally, we noted that some research linked RAN to working memory [2, 31], and RAN tasks also required the selection and inhibition of activated verbal responses [31]. Interestingly, both working memory and response inhibition are critical in math development [17, 31]. Thus, future studies might involve more cognitive abilities, such as speed of processing, response inhibition, and/or working memory, while investigating the relationship between RAN and arithmetic.

Furthermore, our findings indicated different effect mechanisms of phonological awareness and RAN. Although LeFevre et al.’s [49] Pathways to Mathematics model hypothesized that linguistic skills supported further arithmetic through symbolic number system, they assessed linguistic skills with the composite score ofphonological awareness and receptive vocabulary. However, considering that participants should convert symbolic digits to sound when solving arithmetic problems, RAN should have been assessed along with phonological awareness, and may directly support arithmetic [88]. In comparison, the symbolic system is not involved in completing phonological awareness task,rather, developing the symbolic system requires the assistance of phonological awareness. For example, children should first know that 13 (/shi2san1) was composed of two syllables with 10(/shi2/and 3(/san1/). This might explain why the relationship between phonological awareness and arithmetic was mediated by the symbolic system.

Limitations and future directions

With these important findings, the present study still has several limitations. One of the limitations is our sample selection. We only recruited students in Grades 3–5, as in this period, each numerical skill contributed to arithmetic in a relatively equal way [52]. Although the findings revealed only the mediating effect of number ordering, the results could be different for students in other grades. For example, Malone et al. [53] found a different mediating pathway among students in junior grades, showing that number ordering mediated the association between RAN and arithmetic. Future studies should expand the age span and compare different mediating effects among grades. Additionally, our study did not specifically examine children with mathematical learning difficulties. Prior research indicates that children with mathematical learning difficulties may follow different developmental trajectories (e.g., deficits in both symbolic and nonsymbolic number sense, [19]). Therefore, future research should include both typically developing children and those with mathematical learning difficulties to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these mediating pathways.

Moreover, all participants in this study came from a single school, which may limit the generalizability of our results. It is possible that factors such as school curriculum or teaching methods could have influenced the outcomes. Therefore, future studies should recruit participants from diverse schools to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Second, our RAN measurement focused solely on digit RAN. Although digit RAN, letter RAN, and object RAN are highly correlated [6, 12, 37] and recruit similar brain regions [14, 15], future studies should in of RAN tasks to examine whether their unique contributions to arithmetic are comparable, and to explore any potential differences in their underlying mechanisms. Third, our study did not control for other domain-general abilities (such as attention, working memory, and IQ), which also has been shown to play important roles in arithmetic learning (e.g., [1, 23, 68]). Although the current study mainly focused on the role of phonological processing in arithmetic, we were unable to determine the extent to which other cognitive abilities contribute to the observed effects. Future research could incorporate these additional cognitive variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of arithmetic development, as well as to explore the interactions between phonological processing and other cognitive abilities. Finally, our study is a cross-sectional study, which is incapable of explaining causality. For example, although the association between phonological skills and arithmetic is confirmed to be unidirectional, arithmetic could have an impact on basic numerical skills in turn. For instance, a study demonstrated that early math ability could predict children's later dot comparison performance in kindergarteners [27]. Future research should consider a longitudinal study with multiple time-point measures to unpack causality and facilitate a developmental understanding of arithmetic and its influencing factors.

Conclusions and educational implications

In light of the findings in our study, we have several important educational implications. First, we showed that phonological processing was closely associated with arithmetic. In addition to previous studies that showed that phonological processing facilitates number words and quantity linking in kindergarteners who have little formal math experience [47, 64, 87], we also suggest that the roles of phonological processing remain in 3rd-5th graders, even if children have mastered some arithmetic facts and can automatically apply fact-retrieval skills [67]. This result suggested that we might utilize phonological measures or training to identify or intervene in children with math learning difficulties. Second, our study revealed that among four basic numerical skills, number ordering was more specifically crucial to arithmetic skills. On the one hand, it addressed that the role of ordinal understanding in arithmetic might supersede that of cardinality for students above Grade 3 and, on the other hand, it indicated that number ordering training may have a potential transfer effect and further promote arithmetic efficiency. Practically, teachers in primary school could introduce more number ordering practice in mathematics teaching, and parents might assist children’s formal and informal learning of number ordering skills to optimize their arithmetic learning.
