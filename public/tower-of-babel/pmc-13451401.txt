Meditation and breathwork on anxiety in Parkinson's disease: A pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Akila Rayapuraju, Preeti U Reed, Jennifer Liu, Mei Chen, Morgan Pratt, Tulsi Chase, Victor Novack, Daniel Weintraub, David K Simon, Balachundhar Subramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and other non-motor symptoms can greatly diminish quality of life in people with Parkinson's Disease (PD). Yet, few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have assessed accessible, non-pharmacological mental health treatments for people with PD.ObjectiveTo assess the feasibility and impact of brief, remotely-delivered meditation and breathwork on neuropsychiatric symptoms in people with PD.MethodsIn this 18-week RCT, intervention participants attended weekly webinars for six weeks, while waitlisted controls maintained their usual routine before crossing over at six weeks. Participants were assessed for weekly compliance trends (primary outcome) and changes in anxiety (primary outcome), depression, perceived stress, and other non-motor symptoms at 6-week intervals: baseline and weeks 6 (T2), 12 (T3), and 18 (T4). Weekly compliance was collected in each group for 12 weeks. Anxiety was measured using the Parkinson's Anxiety Scale.ResultsThere was a significant decrease in the primary outcome, anxiety, in the intervention group (n = 17) compared to the waitlist control (n = 21) after 6 weeks of meditation and breathwork practice (mean difference (MD)= −5.0, 95% CI [−9.5, −0.49], P = .03). Within-group analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in anxiety (MD = −4.7, 95% CI [2.10,7.33], P < .001) in the intervention group (n = 28). Thirty-one percent of participants met the compliance definition.ConclusionRemotely-delivered meditation and breathwork through the BSM program significantly reduced anxiety in individuals with PD in our study and may be a promising non-pharmacological therapy. A larger RCT with continuous wellness coaching is warranted to more rigorously assess efficacy and optimize protocols.Clinical trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05335850, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05335850

Introduction

Despite growing awareness of the complex symptomatology of Parkinson's Disease (PD), clinical management continues to prioritize motor symptoms, often at the expense of non-motor aspects such as anxiety and depression. This is particularly concerning given PD's rising global burden: in 2020, 9.4 million people were affected worldwide (up from 6 million in 2016), accounting for 7.47 million disability adjusted life years in 2021, with increasing age-standardized prevalence, mortality, and disability since 1990.1,2The non-motor symptoms are now recognized as part of the disease pathology rather than merely psychological reactions to diagnosis.3,4 In fact, about one third of diagnosed PD patients present with two or more anxiety disorders.5,6 Large-scale longitudinal studies, including findings from the Parkinson's Outcomes Project, have identified anxiety and depression as among the strongest predictors of diminished quality of life and well-being in people with PD - yet they remain under-treated in routine care.7–11

Part of this persistent under-treatment stems from the constraints of existing therapies. There are no pharmacologic agents that are specifically indicated for anxiety in PD and guideline recommendations are limited. 12 Moreover,13 commonly used antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), have shown mixed results in this population, with some randomized trials reporting limited efficacy and poor tolerability — including agents such as buspirone.13–16 While existing non-pharmacological treatments such as cognitive behavioral therapy, acupuncture, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, and structured exercise have demonstrated promise for anxiety management in PD, their accessibility is often limited by logistical and financial barriers.17,18 For example, transportation difficulties such as limited access to transportation services or the ability to drive can be a major reason for not accessing mental health and other health care services among people with PD. 19 This highlights the need for scalable, low-cost, and self-administered interventions that are feasible for this population.

Even when psychological support is available, many individuals with PD find it misaligned with their needs — particularly in addressing anxiety. Interventions are often experienced as too generic, overlooking the persistent and identity-shaping nature of non-motor symptoms. 20 Traditional cognitive behavioral therapy's emphasis on correcting thought patterns can feel incompatible with patients’ lived reality, where the goal is not always symptom elimination but learning to coexist with them. This has fueled growing interest in mindfulness-based and acceptance-focused approaches, which may better reflect patients’ values and priorities. 20 Meditative techniques such as guided meditation and breathwork have emerged as potentially effective strategies to reduce stress and improve mood.21–24 Recent studies in both healthy individuals and those with chronic conditions suggest these techniques are feasible and beneficial.23–27 Yet their utility among people with PD remains underexplored — particularly in self-administered and fully remote formats.

To address this gap, we conducted an 18-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the Breath, Sound, and Meditation program (BSM), a home-based digital intervention incorporating a yogic breathing practice (Nadi Shuddhi) and two guided meditations (Isha Kriya and Nada Yoga). The primary outcomes were anxiety as measured by the Parkinson's Anxiety Scale (PAS) and compliance to the intervention, with secondary measures including depression, stress, and motor and non-motor symptoms.28–31 We hypothesized that compliance to the BSM program would lead to improvements in anxiety, motor and non-motor symptom burden.

Methods

Study design

This 18-week waitlisted RCT consisted of an intervention and control arm with four major study time-points spaced six weeks apart and referred to as Baseline, T2 (6 weeks after baseline), T3 (12 weeks after baseline), and T4 (18 weeks after baseline). This study is reported following the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. Ethics approval was conducted by the Institutional Review Board at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts (2022P000212).

Participants

Eligible participants were English-proficient Massachusetts residents aged 18–65 with a clinical diagnosis of PD referred through the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center's Movement Disorder Center or self-referrals from PD support groups, and willing to join the Breath, Sound, and Meditation (BSM) webinars. To ensure participants with at least mild to moderate anxiety were captured in the study, participants had to score a Parkinson's Anxiety Scale (PAS) total score of ≥13, or subscale threshold scores for persistent anxiety ≥10, episodic anxiety ≥5 or avoidant anxiety ≥4 on the screening questionnaire. The cut-off scores for the subscales were based on optimal cut-off points determined during the PAS scale validation. 28 Although a total PAS score of 14 has been identified as the optimal cut-off point indicating presence of an anxiety disorder, a threshold of 13 or higher was used for eligibility in the present study to facilitate recruitment. Those with severe Parkinson's or under medication for medical conditions that could impair participation such as major depression, schizophrenia, or bipolar disorder, were excluded from the study based on the investigators’ judgement.

Gender (male, female, or other) was collected through self-report. All participants provided electronic informed consent through ‘REDCap’, a HIPAA-compliant electronic data collection platform.

Randomization and masking

Randomization followed informed consent and baseline survey completion. The randomization schedule was prepared by a blinded statistician. Participants were randomized (1:1) by a centralized study team member into the intervention or waitlisted control group using the block randomization technique with a block size of 4. Webinar instructors were blinded to whether participants were part of the research study or attending the program independently of the study. However, masking the subjects from group allocations was not possible due to the nature of the study. Randomization was conducted via REDCap.

Procedures

Participants were asked to complete study surveys at baseline, T2, T3, and T4. At these time points, the Hamilton Depression Scale (GRID HAMD-17) and MDS-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part II (MDS-UPDRS II) were administered by a trained clinical fellow or clinician from the BIDMC Parkinson's Disease and Movement Disorders Center. Data was both self-reported and collected by a clinical fellow or attending through REDCap. All surveys and survey reminders were sent via REDCap.

After Baseline, the intervention group was introduced to the BSM webinars and asked to complete weekly activity logs reporting their frequency of breath work and meditation practice for six weeks until T2. The waitlisted control group was asked to continue their typical daily routine for six weeks from baseline to T2. T2 marked the crossover point for both groups. After T2, the intervention group was asked to not attend the webinars but to continue completing weekly activity logs for six weeks until T3. No activity logs were sent to the intervention group from T3 to T4. The waitlisted control group attended the BSM webinars and learned the practices from T2 to T3 and completed weekly activity logs for six weeks. Following T3, the waitlisted control group did not attend weekly webinars but completed weekly activity logs (Figure 1).

Flow of study procedures.

Participants were required to attend at least one session of the BSM webinars to learn the three practices. Weekly review webinars were not mandatory but were offered to provide additional guidance and time to present questions. The study team followed up with participants about their reasons for non-attendance or reasons for withdrawal.

Intervention

The BSM program consisted of three practices introduced in the following order: Isha Kriya, Nadi Shuddhi, and Nada Yoga. Participants were encouraged to continue all three practices daily and recommended to follow the aforementioned order to the best of their ability. However, if participants faced time constraints, they could split the practices throughout the day. Isha Kriya is a 15-min simple guided meditation practice that incorporates breathing awareness and mindful thinking. Nadi Shuddhi is a four-minute breathing practice to bring about mental balance and relaxation. Nada Yoga is a six-minute sound meditation creating balance and stability both physically and mentally. These practices have been shown to enhance overall mental health and regulate physical and emotional strain; thus, we believe the combination of this triad of practices could result in a compounding effect.32–34

The BSM program was conducted virtually weekly via Zoom. A virtual platform was initially selected to facilitate participation in the setting of the COVID pandemic and address any transportation-related barriers to participation. The webinars began by ensuring proper posture, sitting in a cross-legged position or sitting on a chair, in a private space with their full body in view of the Hatha Yoga teacher. Video guides were provided for daily self-practice by email post-webinar. All webinar instructors received certification from Isha Hatha Yoga School upon completion of 1750 hours of training. In addition, to ensure consistent delivery of the webinar in a population of PD patients, all instructors underwent a two-month training program informed by individuals with PD and medical experts well-versed in the disease. Hatha yoga instructors also were trained in common PD symptoms and provided alternative support instructions for participants experiencing challenges.

Outcomes

There were two primary outcomes in this study: (1) the impact of the BSM program on participants’ anxiety levels based on the change from baseline in the Parkinson's Anxiety Scale (PAS) score over twelve weeks in comparison to the waitlisted control and (2) feasibility of performing the intervention practices regularly for six weeks. The secondary aim of this study was to assess the impact of the BSM program on participants’ stress, depression, motor and non-motor symptoms based on the change from baseline scores in the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), GRID Hamilton Depression Scale (GRID-HAMD-17), and the Part I and II of the MDS-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS).

Primary outcomes

Parkinson anxiety scale (PAS)

The PAS, designed to measure Parkinson's related anxiety, was implemented as the primary outcome measure and as a screening measure to assess severity of anxiety. The 12-item scale consists of three subscales; persistent anxiety, episodic anxiety, and avoidance behavior. To calculate total scores, values across the three subscales are summed up. Total scores range from 0 to 48 with an optimal cut-off score of 14 identifying presence of any anxiety disorder. 28 Cronbach's alpha is 0.71 in this study population, representing acceptable internal consistency.

Compliance questionnaire (activity log)

The activity log acted as a measure of compliance, collecting information on participants’ normal weekly activity frequency and duration. Compliance was defined as completing 60% of activity or completing at least one practice a day for a minimum of four days a week. This threshold was selected based on prior research examining adherence to online mindfulness-based interventions in healthy populations, which reports adherence rates ranging from 35% to 92%. A 60% cutoff falls near the midpoint of this observed range and represents a pragmatic and achievable level of engagement. We therefore determined that this threshold would reflect meaningful participation while remaining realistic, and would be sufficient to detect potential intervention-related changes. 35 However, the original compliance definition was ambiguous as it had been defined per week rather than for the duration of the study. Therefore, two operational definitions were used to identify overall compliance. The restrictive definition of compliance was meeting the pre-specified practice frequency for all 12 weeks of intervention practice (6 weeks of webinar support and 6 weeks of self-practice). In contrast, the lenient definition considered participants compliant if they met the pre-specified practice frequency for at least 60% of the intervention period (7 out of 12 weeks). Participants missing 6 or more weeks of compliance data were labeled as missing.

Secondary outcomes

Perceived stress scale (PSS)

Perceived stress was measured using the PSS, a scale designed to quantify the degree individuals categorize life situations as unpredictable over the past month. Responses are scored on a 0–4 scale for 10 items and summed to create a total PSS score. Higher PSS scores are associated with elevated perceptions of stress. 30

Movement disorder society unified Parkinson's disease rating scale (MDS-UPDRS)

To assess motor and non-motor elements, the study incorporated the MDS-UPDRS which scores 65 items, divided across four parts. Part I collects non-motor experiences of daily living, and Part II collects motor experiences. Total scores were calculated by summing the scores for each part. Increased scores clinically indicate a heightened impact of PD symptoms on patient function. 29 Only parts I and II were assessed since parts III and IV would have required in-person exams, and this was an online intervention.

GRID Hamilton depression rating scale (GRID-HAMD-17)

The GRID-HAMD-17 scale was used to measure depression symptoms. Participants were evaluated by a trained administrator and assessed over 17 items. Total scores are calculated by summing the 17 items of scores. The highest possible score is 51 and the lowest score is 0. 31 Higher scores indicate more severe depression symptoms. Suicidal ideation was monitored throughout the study and verified in real-time during survey administration by a trained clinician. Participants with scores greater than 0 on the suicidality question were assessed further by the clinician for active suicidal thoughts. If any indication of serious risk to participant safety was identified, including suicidality, appropriate referrals to emergency services were made. No adverse events are reported for this study.

Statistical analysis

A sample size of 48 participants was determined a priori using G*Power (version 3.1) based on existing PAS data in the literature (mean [SD] = 19.2 [4.5]). 14 Calculations assumed a repeated-measures ANOVA with a within–between interaction and modeled PAS reductions of 12%, 25%, and 33% from baseline, alpha = 0.05, power = 0.95, and a correlation of 0.50 among repeated measures. The sample size was inflated to account for an anticipated 50% dropout/loss to follow-up. Data were analyzed following the intention-to-treat principle. Multiple imputation methods were applied to address missing data. The normality of anxiety scores was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which confirmed a normal distribution. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant demographics (reported as counts and percentages), baseline clinical characteristics, and study outcomes (reported as mean ± standard deviation).

Three primary analytical strategies were employed to evaluate the change in neuropsychological outcomes. First, Welch's two-sample t-tests were used to compare group differences (intergroup comparison) in total anxiety, stress, depression, and non-motor symptoms at T2. Second, to evaluate the overall effect of the intervention on total anxiety, paired t-tests were conducted using pre- and post-intervention data pooled across intervention and waitlist groups. In this scenario, “pre-intervention” was a combination of the intervention group's baseline scores and the waitlist's T2 scores while “post-intervention” combined the intervention group's T2 scores and the waitlist's T3 scores. Third, to assess if the intervention effects were sustained over time, a comparison was conducted between pooled post-intervention data (intervention group at T2 and waitlist group at T3) and pooled six-week follow-up data (intervention group at T3 and waitlist group at T4). Analyses were reported as mean differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals and effect sizes. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d, where d = 0.2 is a small effect, d = 0.5 is a medium effect, and d = 0.8 is a large effect. 36 Compliance analysis was summarized using descriptive statistics (counts and percentages) for each definition. Since missing data primarily reflected insufficient follow-up data rather than sporadic item nonresponse, sensitivity analyses were conducted under two extreme assumptions, where participants with missing compliance were classified as either non-compliant (worst-case) or compliant (best-case) via Fisher's exact test. Multivariable logistic regression models were also used for both assumptions to adjust for baseline covariates including prior yoga and meditation experience, age, gender, race, and baseline medical history. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted to assess whether the chosen cut-off point of ≥ 13 for total PAS influenced findings by restricting the analysis to only those who had a total PAS score of ≥ 14. Sensitivity analyses can be found in the Supplemental Material. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Between October 2022 and September 2024, 197 participants were referred to the study. Of these, 117 were excluded due to general ineligibility, duplicate records, or loss to follow-up. Eighty participants proceeded to formal eligibility assessment and were screened by the PAS questionnaire. Of those assessed, 32 participants were excluded (28 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 4 declined to participate). Ultimately, 48 participants were enrolled and randomized into either the intervention group (n = 24) or the waitlist control group (n = 24). Sixteen participants in the intervention group and 12 in the waitlist group completed the study. All 48 participants were included in the primary endpoint analysis, following the intention-to-treat principle (Figure 2). Demographic characteristics for the study groups are presented in Table 1. The groups were similarly matched in age, race, and ethnicity. Some variation was observed in gender and previous yoga and breathing experience, where the intervention group had a higher proportion of males and participants with previous experience with yoga and breathing practices.

Primary outcome: compliance

Program attendance records show that 41 of the 48 (∼85%) enrolled participants attended at least one of the BSM webinars, with about 19 (40%) attending 4 or more optional review sessions. Practice frequency changed by week, with most participants completing all 3 practices at a similar frequency each week (Figure 3).

As defined by the operational definitions, 7 (14.6%) participants (3 intervention, 4 waitlist control) met restrictive compliance criteria, and 15 (31.3%) participants (9 intervention, 6 waitlist control) met lenient compliance criteria. When looking at only participants who completed the study (n = 28), 25% met restrictive compliance and 53.6% met lenient compliance. Within the intervention group, 7 participants reported meeting compliance criteria when asked about average practice frequency during the last six weeks of the study, during which they did not receive any activity logs. For both definitions of compliance, 20 participants (9 intervention, 11 waitlist control) were excluded due to missing compliance data (eTable 3). Primary outcome: Parkinson anxiety scale (PAS)

Intergroup comparisons between the intervention and waitlist control groups using the Welch's two-sample t-tests revealed that the PAS total anxiety score (mean difference: −5.0, 95% CI [−9.5, −0.49], P = .03) and the avoidant anxiety subscale (mean difference: −1.8, 95% CI [−3.4, −0.26], P = .02) scores significantly decreased compared to baseline for the intervention group (n = 17), in comparison with the waitlist control group (n = 21) at T2. In addition, the 30% reduction in the mean total anxiety score by T2 in the intervention group (11.4 ± 5) fell below the clinically relevant cut-off point of <14 whereas the control group remained relatively high (16.4 ± 8.5) (Table 3, Figure 5).

Following the establishment of intervention effectiveness, the study further aimed to assess the durability (or stability) of its effects over time using intragroup comparisons. A paired t-test comparing pooled pre- and post-intervention (n = 28) scores revealed a significant reduction in the total anxiety score (mean difference = 4.7, 95% CI: [2.10, 7.30], P < .001) (Table 4). This overall reduction was primarily driven by significant decreases in both the persistent anxiety subscale (mean difference = 2.6, 95% CI: [0.99, 4.29], P = .003) and the episodic anxiety subscale (mean difference =1.6, 95% CI: [0.75, 2.32], P < .001). These findings confirmed the effectiveness of the BSM program shown in the first analysis with a larger sample.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary aim of this study was to assess the impact of the BSM program on participants’ stress, depression symptoms, and daily function using the PSS, HAM-D, and the MDS-UPDRS Parts I and II scales. Results were reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. The Welch two-sample t-test was used to compare the intervention and waitlist groups between Baseline to T2. No significant changes were noted for the PSS, HAM-D, and MDS-UPDRS Parts I and II scales (eTable 2–4). This indicates that the intervention did not result in a significant reduction in stress, depression, or measures of daily function as measured by these scales.

Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to address missing compliance data, where all participants with missing compliance data were treated as non-compliant (worst-case scenario) or as compliant (best-case scenario). Under both assumptions, the between-group conclusions were unchanged (p = 0.40; eTable 5), indicating that results were not sensitive to how missing compliance was handled. When examining baseline predictors of adherence under both assumptions, gender, race, prior yoga and meditation experience, and medical history were not significantly associated with adherence. However, younger participants (<60 years) showed lower odds of adherence under the best-case scenario (eTable 6). In addition, analyses including only participants with a baseline PAS score of ≥14 were conducted to ensure that the eligibility total PAS cut-off score of ≥13 did not influence overall findings. Results were consistent with the primary analysis, with PAS scores still demonstrating significant improvement at the end of the 6-week webinar period (eTable 7).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that meditation and breathwork practices taught in the Breath, Sound, and Meditation (BSM) program was associated with significant reduction in anxiety symptoms for the intervention group, compared with the waitlist group after the 6-week intervention period. The ∼30% decrease in total anxiety by six weeks in the intervention group suggests a positive treatment effect that is broadly consistent with the existing literature.12,17,37 Although a minimal clinically important difference has not yet been established for the Parkinson Anxiety Scale, benchmarks from related depression research suggest reductions of ≥25% to <50% from baseline may indicate partial response 38 (noted here for contextual framing not as an inferential standard for interpreting PAS change). In addition, prior studies have reported moderate-to-high correlations between changes in self-rated PAS scores and both clinician- and participant-rated measures (e.g., HAM-A, CGI-I, and HADS-A), suggesting that these changes may reflect clinically meaningful improvement. 14

In our study, total anxiety scores decreased over the course of the intervention and fell below the threshold of 14, which has been used to indicate clinically significant anxiety (DSM-IV criteria).28,39 Among participants who received the intervention, anxiety scores declined from pre- to post-intervention and remained below baseline at the 12-week follow-up. Given that this is a feasibility study, we do not take this as definitive evidence of treatment effectiveness. While these results suggest a potential benefit of the intervention for anxiety, no meaningful or statistically significant improvements were observed in stress, depression, or motor and non-motor experiences of daily living. The contrast between non-significant between-group findings and statistically significant within-group changes for episodic anxiety likely reflects differences in statistical power, as the within-group analysis (n = 28) included a larger sample than the between-group comparison (n = 17). Importantly, the study was powered to detect changes in total PAS rather than subscale outcomes, so within-group subscale findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Taken together, these findings do not support definitive conclusions regarding differential effects on acute versus episodic anxiety. Future studies should further examine the role of meditation and breathwork practices in both situational and persistent anxiety to better understand their impact.

In general, the broader literature shows that adherence and attrition with virtually delivered mindfulness-based interventions is under-reported or when it is reported, has high variability. The present study had a compliance rate of 31% and an attrition rate of 58%, falling within reported ranges for adherence (31% to 100%) and attrition (0% to 90%). 40 Engagement and adherence to the program were encouraging, where most participants (85%) attended at least one BSM webinar, and nearly 40% optional review sessions, indicating interest in continued participation. While high compliance was observed in the intervention phase, practice compliance declined when participants were phased into the independent practice period (with video guides). This suggests that the weekly support sessions may have promoted engagement with the intervention. As social-reinforcement and connectedness has been observed to play a critical role in facilitating adherence in the past, it is possible that participants were motivated by the presence of live teachers and other participants in their classes. 41 these findings provide an initial benchmark for feasibility in this group and highlight the need for strategies to enhance sustained engagement in future trials. Ongoing accountability mechanisms such as continued group-based virtual instruction, structured follow-up, and app-based tracking may be necessary in future trials for sustained engagement with the daily practices.

Prior literature shows mixed findings on the effects of various non-pharmacological therapies such as mindfulness-based programs on anxiety and other non-motor outcomes.12,25,37 The mixed findings can often be attributed to differences in study design or intervention duration and a focus on somatic symptoms which can be less specific and sensitive to change in PD since some anxiety symptoms can mimic those of PD.25,37 In contrast, cognitive behavioral therapy has consistently been shown to have a significant effect on anxiety. For instance, A 10-week cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) program conducted in a sample with similar size and demographic characteristics reported a significant between-group difference on the Parkinson Anxiety Scale, with a 9.9-point reduction in the CBT group post-treatment. 42 In our study, we observed an 8.4-point reduction in the intervention group after six weeks of meditation and breathwork practice. While a direct comparison cannot be made to due differences in study design, sample size, and intervention type, these findings suggest that meditation-based approaches warrant further investigation as a potentially accessible adjunct to CBT for addressing anxiety in individuals with PD.

Among mindfulness-based therapies, the largest RCT (n = 159) was conducted in Hong Kong by Kwok et al. (2025), where investigators explored the effects of an adapted version of the 8-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program conceptualized by Jon Kabat-Zin. 27 The in-person program included mindful breathing, body scan, mindful movement, and mindful walking, whereas our virtual program focused on seated practices involving specific breathing and sound patterns. That study reported a significant improvement in anxiety symptoms using the Hospital and Anxiety Depression Scale (HADS) at two months post-intervention.

In our study, we also observed a reduction in anxiety over time using the Parkinson Anxiety Scale (PAS), based on within- and between-group analyses. While HADS-A and PAS assess related constructs, 43 differences in study design, populations, interventions, and outcome measures limit direct comparison. We therefore interpret these findings as providing contextual alignment in direction rather than evidence of comparable effects. At four months, the prior study did not observe sustained effects, whereas anxiety levels in our sample remained below baseline. Given the feasibility design and small sample size, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that virtually delivered interventions may offer a promising and accessible approach for individuals with Parkinson's disease.

The absence of change in other non-motor symptoms in our study should be interpreted in the context of its feasibility design. Our study was powered to detect changes in total anxiety and not specifically powered for the secondary outcomes. The lack of statistically significant findings could therefore reflect limited statistical power rather than the absence of a true effect. Further, both the intervention and waitlist control groups scored only slightly above the normal depression range (0–7) at baseline, reducing the likelihood of detecting significant changes over time.

The MDS-UPDRS provides only a snapshot of disease severity rather than capturing daily fluctuations, it may have been less sensitive to subtle changes across the study period. 44 Lastly, the lack of significant or meaningful change in stress levels despite the decrease in anxiety may reflect differences in how stress and anxiety are experienced and measured for people with PD.45,46 Overall, the observed improvement in anxiety in the absence of consistent changes in other non-motor symptoms suggests that mental health outcomes in Parkinson's disease may respond differently to behavioral interventions. Further research is needed to clarify these patterns and to better understand the mechanisms underlying differential symptom responsiveness in this population.

Strengths & limitations

This study had several notable strengths that enhance the rigor and relevance of its findings. The randomized controlled trial (RCT) design strengthened internal validity by minimizing selection and allocation biases, enabling a more rigorous assessment of the intervention's effectiveness and supporting cautious causal inference. In addition, the inclusion of a follow-up period that extended beyond what is typically reported in the meditation intervention literature enabled the exploration of sustained treatment effects over time. Another strength was the use of the Parkinson Anxiety Scale (PAS) to measure anxiety as the primary outcome, allowing us to address the intervention's impact on PD-specific anxiety symptoms which are not always reflected in common anxiety measures. 43 Furthermore, the digitally delivered meditation program was administered using a standardized protocol, which ensured uniformity in content and delivery across participants. This methodological consistency enhances the study's reproducibility and facilitates future implementation in similar clinical populations.

The study also had limitations. The study did not exclude participants engaging in other programs or lifestyle activities such as physical activity, physical therapy, or other mindfulness or meditation-based programs, which may have introduced confounding effects on the results. Missing data in both study groups 6 weeks after starting the intervention may also indicate issues with intervention adherence for some participants. Our compliance results suggest that maintenance of weekly wellness coaching offered in a larger virtual or in-person group settings could increase motivation and promote intervention adherence in future studies. Regardless, the sensitivity analyses addressing missing data reinforced the significant decrease in anxiety seen after the intervention period, suggesting that the intervention still shows promise. Another limitation was that patients were not blinded to group allocations since the intervention required active learning of meditation and breathwork techniques. This may have introduced expectancy and performance bias, a common limitation associated with behavioral trials. Future studies may increase study design rigor by incorporating active control conditions. Lastly, the small sample size limits the generalizability of results. However, the intention for this study was to explore associations between the intervention and improved anxiety outcomes in PD. A larger RCT would be better suited to assessing generalizability.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the meditation and breathwork practices taught in the Breath, Sound, and Meditation program can significantly reduce anxiety symptoms in individuals PD. These practices could offer a valuable approach for patients seeking to manage PD-related anxiety without increasing reliance on pharmacological treatments. Given the modest time requirement, digital accessibility, and feasibility for at home practices, these practices may be promising candidates for scalable interventions. Incorporating meditation and breathwork practices into comprehensive care strategies may enhance symptom management and improve quality of life for individuals with PD.
