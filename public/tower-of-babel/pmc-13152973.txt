Multivariable genome-wide analysis elucidates the shared genetic architecture, immunosenescence features, and gut-origin therapeutic targets of ulcerative colitis-associated multisystem inflammation
Authors: Shanneng Tang, Xin Yao, Shanshan Wang, Fanfan Zhu, Wenbin Zeng, Dongping Lai, Xiaobing Meng, Xinyue Zhang, Ziming Zhu, Tao Zhang, Ri’an Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver 25% of patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) develop extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs), resulting in significant systemic morbidity. We define the shared genetic foundation of these manifestations as the UC-associated Multisystem Inflammatory Genetic Architecture (UC-MIGA). This study aims to identify shared genomic drivers and actionable immunosenescence therapeutic targets across the UC-EIM spectrum.MethodsWe applied genomic structural equation modeling (SEM) to seven European-ancestry GWAS datasets (UC, deep vein thrombosis, ankylosing spondylitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, pyoderma gangrenosum, interstitial lung disease, and erythema nodosum) to identify a shared latent genetic factor (F1). Post-SEM analyses included FUMA mapping, SuSIE/FINEMAP fine-mapping, FUSION/FOCUS transcriptome-wide studies, MAGMA enrichment, CELLECT deconvolution, LDSC partitioned heritability, and single-cell eQTL Mendelian randomization (MR). UC exhibited the highest standardized factor loading (0.9801) on F1, justifying its use as a representative proxy for UC-MIGA in downstream analyses. UC-telomere relationships were assessed via tissue-specific eQTL/sQTL enrichment across 49 GTEx tissues, spatial transcriptomics (gsMap), single-cell profiling (GSE214695, GSE163974), hdWGCNA, and colocalization analyses (eCAVIAR, fastENLOC).ResultsSEM identified substantial genetic overlap (CFI = 1.0, SRMR = 0.17). Super-enhancer regions showed exceptional heritability enrichment (80.16%, fold = 4.79, p = 0.0007). MR identified 35 causal immune cell-gene associations. UC-telomere analyses revealed convergence in colon-specific DNA repair-mitochondrial energetics-telomere maintenance pathways, with B cells prioritized as the core cell type. Colocalization identified NKX2-3 and LINC01475 as high-confidence shared candidates. Embryonic intestinal enrichment supported the developmental origins of this systemic axis.ConclusionUC-MIGA represents a genetically coherent architecture driven by super-enhancer-mediated epigenetic dysregulation, Th17/Treg imbalance, and immunosenescence features, including telomere dysfunction and B-cell exhaustion. The ‘developmental vulnerability-environmental trigger’ model explains the gut-origin inflammatory cascade underlying extraintestinal manifestations, with UC-telomere analysis providing a genomic foundation for systemic therapeutic strategies targeting the inflammation-aging nexus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-026-02257-y.

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease where ~ 25% of patients develop extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) involving the musculoskeletal, mucocutaneous, hepatobiliary, pulmonary, and vascular systems [1]. While extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) are clinically more prevalent in Crohn’s disease (CD) [2], systemic complications associated with ulcerative colitis (UC) remain a globally critical yet understudied domain [3, 4]. Global meta-analytical data indicate that approximately 10–20% of incident IBD cases are classified as elderly-onset (age ≥ 60); within this cohort, the incidence rate of UC (11.6 per 100,000) significantly exceeds that of CD (4.5 per 100,000) [5]. Research indicates that while the genetic mechanisms of UC and CD overlap significantly [6], their clinical manifestations and downstream signaling pathways are not identical: in UC, IL-23 primarily drives Th17-mediated IL-17A production, whereas in CD, it typically triggers a Th1-like IFNγ signaling pathway [7]. Clinically, UC presents as continuous mucosal inflammation restricted to the colon, while CD is characterized by transmural skip-lesions. Consequently, we selected UC as the entry point to deeply dissect the “gut-origin” systemic inflammatory cascade and further explore the shared genetic mechanisms associated with aging. The empirical robustness of this selection was further validated through comparative Genomic SEM; while the UC-inclusive model yielded a perfect fit (CFI = 1.000), substituting UC with CD significantly impaired model stability (CFI = 0.902), which failed to meet the standard threshold (> 0.95) for an optimal model.

Because traditional single-trait genome-wide association studies (GWAS) often fail to capture the latent genetic architectures underlying multi-organ involvement, we propose the UC-associated Multisystem Inflammatory Genetic Architecture (UC-MIGA) as an analytical framework to identify a shared latent genetic factor (F1) representing the common polygenic predisposition to multisystemic inflammation[8–11]. Based on established clinical associations with UC, broad organ system representation, and the availability of high-quality GWAS datasets from premier journals or large-scale databases, six extraintestinal phenotypes—deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [12, 13], ankylosing spondylitis (AS) [14], primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) [15], pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) [16], interstitial lung disease (ILD) [17, 18], and erythema nodosum (EN) [19]—were incorporated into this multivariable model to ensure statistical robustness.

Emerging evidence suggests that accelerated biological aging, specifically telomere attrition, is a key driver of chronic inflammation [20]. Although telomere shortening in UC patients correlates with disease severity, there is currently no systematic genome-wide approach to explore potential immunosenescence-related targets in UC—targets that could play a pivotal role in blocking the systemic dissemination of intestinal inflammation. To address these gaps, we employed Genomic Structural Equation Modeling (Genomic SEM) to identify the shared genetic susceptibility axis of UC-MIGA. Given that UC serves as the primary inductive anchor of the F1 factor (standardized estimate = 0.9801), we subsequently utilized UC as a representative proxy to conduct targeted UC-telomere comorbidity analysis to validate the “gut-origin” hypothesis. This approach integrates multiple colocalization methods, single-cell transcriptomics, and spatial profiling to identify high-confidence, actionable targets, providing a genomic foundation for therapeutic strategies targeting the multisystemic inflammation-aging nexus.

Methods

Study design

We performed separate-variable input GWAS for UC and six associated EIMs using data from European-ancestry populations. The EIMs analyzed were DVT, AS, PSC, PG, ILD and EN. All input GWAS datasets received ethical approval from their respective Institutional Review Boards. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the data underwent rigorous quality control (QC).

Zhou et al.’s UC [21] GWAS utilized UK Biobank data, defining cases through International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes extracted from electronic health records (EHR) and questionnaires, while controls were participants without related conditions. For DVT [22], Loya et al. analyzed a predominantly White British cohort from UK Biobank. After QC filtering of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) array data, 458,620 markers were retained for modeling. Association testing incorporated approximately 13.3 million imputed variants. Phenotypic measures included hematologic, anthropometric, and other quantitative traits alongside binary disease statuses—all demonstrating high call rates (> 80%) and meeting predefined heritability significance thresholds. Jiang et al.’s AS [23] GWAS employed UK Biobank data from 456,348 European-ancestry individuals, examining 11,842,647 variants. Ji et al.’s PSC [24] study combined multiple European-ancestry cohorts totaling 2,871 cases and 12,019 population controls. Genotyping was conducted using Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0 and Illumina HumanOmni2.5–8 BeadChip platforms. PG, ILD, and EN datasets originated from the FinnGen database [25], comprising 385,677, 412,181, and 399,108 participants respectively.

In addition, during subsequent genetic association analyses, GWAS summary statistics for UC were obtained from the Finnish database, including 482,657 individuals and 19,172,704 SNPs. Telomere length summary data were derived from Kessler et al. [26], who analyzed exome sequencing from the UK Biobank (UKB) and Geisinger Health System (GHS) cohorts. Somatic mutations were identified using Mutect2, followed by stringent QC, fine-mapping of significant loci, and integration of common and rare variant signals with confounder adjustment, yielding 8,599,274 effective loci and novel rare variants associated with telomere length.

Single-cell transcriptomic data

scRNA-seq was performed on colonic biopsies from healthy controls and patients with active UC or Crohn’s disease (CD), generating 46,700 cell transcriptomes(GSE214695). After tissue digestion, filtering, and QC, cells were captured using the 10 × Genomics platform and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500. Data were processed with Seurat for doublet removal, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, with batch correction via Harmony. CosMx Spatial Molecular Imaging (SMI) was applied to validate cellular localization. Trajectory analysis and public dataset comparison revealed tissue-resident and inflammation-induced subpopulations with distinct molecular features [27]. In addition, After enriching potential CSCs via FACS, the authors used scT&R-seq to simultaneously measure single-cell transcriptomes and telomere length, performing QC to obtain high-quality data. Seurat was employed for dimensionality reduction and clustering to identify cell subpopulations and marker genes. Data were validated through the 10 × Genomics platform and deposited in the public database (GSE163974) [28].

Single-cell multi-omics (assay for transposable-accessible chromatin + gene expression) data of PBMCs

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a 25-year-old healthy female donor were analyzed using the 10 × Genomics Chromium Multiome platform. After granulocyte removal, 11,909 high-quality single cells were obtained following standard protocols. Parallel Assay for Transposable-Accessible Chromatin Sequencing (ATAC-seq) and RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (RNA: 28–10–10–90 cycles; ATAC: 50–8–16–49 cycles). After QC, scRNA-seq data showed a median of 1,826 genes and 3,776 UMIs per cell, while ATAC-seq data contained 13,486 fragments per cell. In total, 108,377 open chromatin regions and 15,494 genes were identified, establishing 85,468 peak–gene associations that form a multi-omics foundation for studying immune cell heterogeneity and epigenetic regulation.

Quality control

To ensure data integrity within our study, several QC adjustments were implemented. Samples demonstrating low quality, specifically those with missing rates exceeding 5%, were excluded. Special attention was required for the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) region on chromosome 6 (approximately positions 25,000,000 to 35,000,000 bp) due to its elevated genetic diversity, structural complexity, and polymorphism within immune-related genes. In preparation for generating the shared latent factor (F1) summary statistics within the UC-MIGA framework, genotype imputation was performed using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 EUR reference panel [29]. To minimize analytical error, SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.01 were removed. Such variants are often poorly called due to insufficient sample numbers within genotype clusters and typically exhibit high standard errors during LD Score regression analysis. Furthermore, SNPs displaying an estimated effect size of zero were excluded to prevent potential numerical instability issues in matrix calculations. SNPs exhibiting allele mismatches or discrepancies with the reference panel were also screened out during QC processing.

The scRNA-seq QC pipeline was implemented using Seurat (v5.2.1). Mitochondrial genes (prefixes: “^MT-”, “^mt-”, “^Mt-”) and hemoglobin genes (“HBA1”, “HBA2”, “HBB”, etc.) were dynamically detected. Filtering criteria retained high-quality cells with UMI count ≥ 1,000, detected genes between 200 and 5,000, mitochondrial gene proportion ≤ 15%, and hemoglobin gene proportion ≤ 3%. Violin plots visualized quality metrics before and after filtering. For dimensionality reduction, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-SNE were performed and evaluated using heatmaps and elbow plots. The Harmony algorithm was applied for batch correction (theta = 2, lambda = 1) to minimize sample-specific biases. Post-correction, highly variable genes were reanalyzed to ensure robust downstream clustering and cell-type annotation.

Sample overlap

Our integrated analysis incorporated individual GWAS datasets from distinct genomic repositories, each comprising unique participant cohorts. Our methodology explicitly accounts for cohort overlap across these datasets during GWAS processing. This intentional integration enhances result robustness and generalizability while addressing potential confounding effects from sample overlap.

Genomic structural equation modeling

We employed Genomic SEM via the GenomicSEM R package (v.0.0.5) in a genome-wide analysis investigating the shared genetic architecture underlying UC and six associated EIMs. This method facilitates exploration of potential genetic architectures through multivariate models while inherently accounting for biases arising from GWAS sample overlap or unbalanced sample sizes. It also discriminates between variants influencing specific subsets of traits and those contributing to broader cross-trait susceptibility [30–32].

The Genomic SEM analysis followed a two-stage procedure. First, the Estimation Stage utilized summary statistics from the relevant GWAS to derive an empirical genetic covariance matrix and its sampling covariance matrix using a multivariate extension of cross-trait LD Score regression [33]. This matrix served as the input for the subsequent modeling phase. Second, the Factor Modeling Stage involved specifying and fitting a single common factor model (F1) within the SEM framework. This model aimed to minimize the discrepancy between its predicted covariance structure and the empirically derived genetic covariance matrix obtained in stage one. Model fit was rigorously assessed using multiple indices: Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), model chi-square (χ2), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI).

Using this statistically validated common factor model, multi-GWAS summary statistics for individual autosomal SNPs were generated by integrating their association statistics with the genetic and sampling covariance matrices. This effectively modeled the shared covariance structure inherent in the underlying UC-MIGA-related GWAS datasets.

SNP heterogeneity in genomic SEM

To assess whether the SNP associations in the shared F1 factor of UC-MIGA were fully captured by the SEM framework, we conducted SNP-heterogeneity testing. This analysis evaluates the null hypothesis that SNP associations in individual single-trait GWAS are entirely accounted for by the included statistical adjustments. A significant QSNP result suggests contributions through pathways distinct from the core shared genetic architecture of UC-MIGA.

Multilevel assessment of genomic structural equation models

Analysis proceeded using a two-step LD Score regression framework without filtering out ambiguous-strand SNPs, non-SNP variants, SNPs with INFO scores < 0.9, or SNPs with MAF < 0.01. Variants with missing data were also retained, and no SNPs were excluded based on test statistic P-values outside typical effective ranges. Partitioned LD scores exhibiting zero variance were excluded. A two-step estimator, implementing a cutoff at 30, was employed. Subsequent refinement utilized a multi-tiered strategy for genome structural equation modeling: first, correlational test P-values underwent stringent adjustment at two distinct levels (P < 1 × 10–150 and P < = 1 × 10–200) to pinpoint high-confidence SNPs at each tier; then, the biological insights derived from SNP identification across these tiers were enhanced by characterizing known and novel sequencing variants through annotation via SNPnexus [34].

Defining genomic loci and identifying novel variants

To identify genomic loci and lead SNPs associated with UC-MIGA, we implemented the Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) [35]. This analysis utilized summary statistics from the shared latent factor (F1), employing a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ and defining lead SNPs as those with low LD (r2 < 0.1) to other significant variants. We assessed the relationship of these lead loci to findings from the original single-input GWAS and cross-referenced them with established associations in the GWAS Catalog (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) to investigate potential pleiotropy. Further risk locus analysis within the UC-MIGA framework was conducted using FUMA’s built-in functionalities under the same significance threshold. Subsequently, we performed gene-level analysis leveraging MAGMA (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation), which aggregates SNP signals per gene and evaluates gene-phenotype associations. MAGMA analyzed relevant output files, identified genes proximal to the lead SNPs, and applied a significance threshold of FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. Additionally, we devised an innovative analytical approach, termed the “GWAS-minus-loci” method. This technique facilitates the discovery of novel, potentially valuable loci by systematically comparing the lead SNPs identified via structural equation modeling with those attaining genome-wide significance in the standard single-input GWAS.

Fine-mapping

To finely map causal variants associated with UC-MIGA phenotypes, this study employed two complementary methods, SuSIE (Sum of Single Effects) and FINEMAP [36], for parallel analysis within the echolocatoR R package (v.2.0.3). Analyses focused on genomic windows defined by extending 250 kb upstream and downstream from each lead SNP. Within each window, both SuSIE and FINEMAP were used to estimate the posterior probability of each SNP being causal. Variants were considered candidate causal variants for a specific method only if their posterior probability met or exceeded a threshold of 0.75, thereby defining a credible set per method. To enhance reliability and integrate findings, consensus SNPs—variants identified by both SuSIE and FINEMAP—were determined. For these shared variants, their posterior probabilities from the two methods were averaged to create an average credible set. Within this integrated set, a binary confidence flag (1 or 0) was assigned to each consensus SNP: a flag of 1 was assigned only if the SNP’s posterior probability independently reached the 0.75 threshold in both individual SuSIE and FINEMAP analyses; otherwise, it was flagged as 0.

Transcriptome-wide association study

Following the initial identification of potential causal variants, Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS) methodology [37] was employed to prioritize genes, leveraging associations between gene expression and the shared latent factor (F1) of UC-MIGA. This analysis was executed using the FUSION framework, which incorporated 37,920 pre-computed expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) features (representing unique gene/tissue combinations) from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v8 database to estimate expression-associated weights across genes and tissues. Notably, the high coverage within the UC-MIGA dataset – encompassing variants matching 33,092 of these features – indicated robust data quality and enabled analysis. Genes surpassing the significance threshold in TWAS were advanced for further investigation. Subsequently, the FOCUS method, specifically designed for fine-mapping TWAS associations to pinpoint causal gene-trait links, was applied to these TWAS-significant genes. FOCUS quantifies the probability of a gene’s causal role using its Posterior Inclusion Probability (PIP). Ultimately, genes demonstrating both TWAS significance and strong causal evidence across the integrated evidence streams were designated as potential causal effector genes for UC-MIGA.

Gene set enrichment and cellular annotation analysis

Genes annotated using MAGMA [38] underwent gene enrichment and pathway analysis via FUMA’s GSEA implementation. For identification of UC-MIGA-associated etiological cell types, we further integrated the CELLECT framework [39], leveraging single-cell transcriptomics to elucidate cellular bases of complex traits. Pivotal to this approach was the Tabula Muris dataset [40], comprising single-cell RNA-seq data from approximately 100,000 cells across 20 murine organs and tissues. Following preprocessing and normalization with the CELLEX tool to compute gene expression specificity scores, cell type enrichment was assessed using ldsc. Significantly enriched cell types were ultimately defined by a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted threshold of P < 0.05.

Heritability contributions across genomic regions

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression(LDSC) serves as a pivotal methodology for estimating partitioned heritability [41]. Its approach decomposes the aggregate heritability of a target phenotype into distinct functional genomic categories (e.g., gene bodies, enhancers, promoters), quantifying each category’s contribution to phenotypic variation. The method computationally integrates GWAS summary statistics with linkage disequilibrium (LD) weighting patterns and population allele frequency data to yield comprehensive quantitative estimates of specific functional elements’ heritability contributions.

Polygenic score construction from summary statistics

This study employed PRS-CS (Polygenic Risk Score with Continuous Shrinkage) [42], a Bayesian method, to quantify region-specific genetic contributions to disease susceptibility using GWAS summary statistics. By synthesizing external LD reference panels with GWAS data, the approach calculates posterior effect estimates for individual SNPs. These estimates subsequently enabled individual-level polygenic risk score (PRS) computation, thereby enabling systematic evaluation of genomic risk contributions across multiple chromosomal regions.

Analysis of immune cell-type specific expression biomarkers

To evaluate the association between the shared latent factor (F1) of UC-MIGA and immune cell-specific single-cell expression quantitative trait loci (sceQTL) [43] without direct measurements, we obtained sceQTL expression profiles for 126 peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples. These were matched using UC-MIGA-associated genes prioritized through lead SNPs and proximal genes, finemapped genes, and TWAS-derived genes. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed using the MendelianRandomization R package (v0.10.0). The primary association was assessed using inverse-variance weighting (IVW, significance threshold P < 0.05) [44], while robustness was enhanced through supplementary methods: weighted median estimation mitigated invalid instrument bias, MR-Egger regression (weighted by inverse outcome variance) corrected for horizontal pleiotropy [45, 46], and simple-mode and weighted-mode analyses provided additional validation. Sensitivity testing included MR-PRESSO outlier detection and correction for pleiotropic bias [47], alongside Cochran’s Q heterogeneity testing to exclude instruments exhibiting significant heterogeneity [48].

Tissue-specific e/sQTL enrichment analysis using QTLEnrich

Tissue-specific associations between UC and telomere length were evaluated using expression/splicing quantitative trait loci (e/sQTLs) data from 49 tissues in the GTEx v8 database. The QTLEnrich rank-based permutation algorithm was employed to detect enrichment levels and statistical significance of phenotype-associated variants within tissue-specific e/sQTLs. This algorithm corrects for confounding factors including MAF, distance to transcription start sites, and local LD. Enrichment significance was assessed via Adjusted_Fold_Enrichment and Enrichment_P_value parameters.

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis using MAGMA

To validate (expand upon) the QTLEnrich findings, MAGMA enrichment analysis was performed to explore genomic features associated with UC and telomere length. First, data for UC and telomere length were formatted for MAGMA analysis. Subsequently, gene-level enrichment analysis was conducted using the MAGMA tool, with P < 0.01 considered as the threshold for credible tissue identification.

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis based on single-cell spatial transcriptomics

This study integrated single-cell spatial transcriptomics with GWAS data to systematically analyze the spatial distribution characteristics of UC and telomere length at single-cell resolution. The genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) algorithm was employed, integrating cross-species data from mouse embryonic/brain tissues, macaque cerebral cortex, and human GWAS to identify spatial distribution patterns of disease-associated cell populations. This algorithm leverages GWAS-derived trait-associated genes, mapping their expression patterns onto spatially resolved cells to evaluate cellular associations between specific anatomical regions and complex traits. Utilizing spatial transcriptomic data from 25 organs of E16.5 mouse embryos, we constructed enrichment specificity and gene spatial expression atlases for UC and telomere length, establishing a single-cell resolution spatial pathogenic mechanism map [49].

Tissue-specific biological functional processes based on GeneEnrich

We conducted systematic gene set enrichment analysis on genetic variants and expression profile data associated with UC and telomere length. Using the GeneEnrich tool, hypergeometric tests and permutation tests were performed to evaluate the enrichment of candidate gene sets within intestinal system-related biological functions or phenotypic gene sets. To minimize tissue-specific bias, intestinal genes were used as the background set, and empirical P-values were calculated through permutation testing. Additionally, to exclude common confounding factors in genetic analysis, genes within the MHC region were removed. Functional gene sets were derived from biological databases including Gene Ontology, Reactome, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, Molecular Signatures Database, and Mouse Genome Informatics. Statistical significance was determined within each database, with empirical P < 0.05 considered nominally significant.

Cell signaling annotated from single-cell transcriptomic atlas

In-depth analysis was performed on preprocessed single-cell data. Based on Harmony-corrected dimensionality reduction results, principal component heatmaps, elbow plots, t-SNE, and Harmony projection visualizations were constructed. Cell cycle distribution was quantified through S-phase and G2M-phase scoring. Cell neighborhoods were constructed using Harmony-reduced dimensions, with multi-resolution clustering performed across 13 resolution parameters ranging from 0.01 to 3.0. Clustering tree analysis identified the optimal resolution of 0.1, yielding stable cell subpopulations. The FindAllMarkers function (minimum expression proportion 25%, log2FC ≥ 0.25) was employed to screen cluster-specific expressed genes, and marker gene heatmaps were generated. Automated cell-type annotation was achieved using the SingleR algorithm.

Cell signaling identified using ECLIPSER

ECLIPSER employed Bayesian Fisher’s exact test to assess cell type-specific enrichment, using background GWAS loci as controls to calculate enrichment fold changes and p-values for each trait-tissue-cell type combination [50]. The cell-specific threshold was set at the 95th percentile of background scores. Bayesian methods were used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for enrichment fold changes, based on multi-level functional genomics annotation data. The analysis workflow identified significantly associated loci for target traits based on GWAS results, expanding original signals through LD proxies (r2 > 0.8) to cover potential functional variants. Wilcoxon tests were performed for differential expression analysis within cell types, with parameters set as minimum 3 cells per group, minimum gene expression proportion of 10%, and log2FC threshold of 0.5. Genes meeting differential expression criteria (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC|> 0.5) were selected. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied to control significance, with cell types showing BH ≤ 0.05 designated as significantly enriched.

Cell signaling identified by CELLECT analysis

To evaluate the contribution of cell type-specific expression to disease heritability, two complementary methods were employed under the CELLECT (CELLtype-specific Expression Integration and Complex Traits) framework: stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC) and MAGMA gene set analysis [39]. S-LDSC analyzed GWAS summary statistics for UC and telomere length, defining cell type baselines based on tabula_muris-test and mousebrain-test datasets, with 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European samples serving as the LD score reference panel. MAGMA gene set analysis tested the correlation between gene-level association statistics and average expression levels of specific cell types, identifying characteristic association signals independent of other prominent cell types through pairwise combinations. Both methods adopted p < 0.05 as the threshold for significant enrichment.

Single-cell integration of multi-dimensional evidence for cell prioritization

To determine priority cell types for UC and telomere length, results from three analytical methods were integrated: single-cell annotated atlas, ECLIPSER, and CELLECT. An equal-weight scoring strategy was adopted, assigning 1 point per method, and calculating the weighted total score for each cell type. Higher total scores indicate greater importance of the cell type in disease pathogenesis [51].

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identifies core module genes in prioritized cells

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was performed using hdWGCNA on specific cell types identified through integrated multi-dimensional evidence. The annotated Seurat object was loaded, the WGCNA environment initialized, and candidate genes expressed in ≥ 5% of cells were filtered. The MetacellsByGroups function constructed metacells (k = 25) based on cell type and sample to reduce data sparsity. Following normalization of expression data, PCA was applied for dimensionality reduction, with Harmony correction for batch effects. The SetDatExpr function extracted target cell expression matrices, soft-thresholding power was tested via TestSoftPowers, and a signed topological overlap matrix network was constructed. The dynamic tree-cutting algorithm identified co-expression modules, module eigengene values were calculated, and associations between modules and cell types were analyzed along with connectivity assessment.

Genomic risk loci analysis and conditional analysis

By implementing a more stringent genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−1⁰), we utilized the FUMA platform to identify and annotate genomic risk loci. Uploaded result files contained SNP identifiers and genomic LD-formatted reference information values. The FUMA platform performed initial QC on these GWAS results, removing missing values and low-quality SNPs, ultimately identifying genomic risk loci associated with UC and telomere length.

Genomic eCAVIAR analysis

To identify high-confidence genes and regulatory mechanisms at UC and telomere length risk loci, the Bayesian-based eCAVIAR colocalization method was employed [52]. This method evaluates whether GWAS and e/sQTL signals tag the same causal variant, accounting for the effects of local LD and allelic heterogeneity. eCAVIAR possesses fine-mapping capabilities and can process large-scale GWAS summary statistics without requiring genotype data. A maximum of two independent causal variants per locus was specified, with input consisting of Z-scores (effect size/standard error) for each variant from GWAS and GTEx studies. LD windows were defined around lead variants to encompass regions with r2 > 0.1 (calculated based on 1000 Genomes Phase 3), extending 50 kb bilaterally. GWAS-e/sQTL-tissue combinations with colocalization posterior probability (CLPP) > 0.01 were considered significant.

Genomic fastenloc analysis

To finely map functional genes and regulatory mechanisms at UC and telomere length risk loci, the Bayesian colocalization method fastenloc was further employed [53]. This method performs fine-mapping of GWAS and e/sQTL loci based on the embedded DAP-G algorithm, estimating posterior probabilities of each variant being causal and evaluating colocalization probabilities of shared causal variants between two signals, without requiring a preset upper limit on the number of independent causal variants. Input consisted of Z-scores (effect size/standard error) for each variant from GWAS and GTEx e/sQTL summary data. LD windows around lead variants were defined as chromosomal regions containing variants with r2 > 0.1 (calculated based on 1000 Genomes Phase 3), extending 50 kb bilaterally. Colocalization between UC and telomere length GWAS loci and 49 GTEx tissues was tested, with results expressed as regional colocalization probability (RCP). GWAS-e/sQTL-tissue combinations with RCP > 0.1 were considered significantly colocalized.

Open chromatin to gene expression analysis

Open chromatin to gene expression analysis aimed to explore immune cell-expressed genes in normal tissues and investigate potential associations between stably expressed immune cell genes and annotated cells in the single-cell transcriptomic atlases of UC and telomere length. Open4Gene [54] employs a Hurdle model-based statistical method to address zero-inflation characteristics in single-cell data. The analytical workflow utilized Seurat and Signac packages for normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data. RNA expression matrices, ATAC peak matrices, and cell metadata (including cell type annotations and technical covariates) were extracted. Peak-gene pairs were defined by associating ATAC peaks with gene promoter regions within 100 kb windows. A two-component Hurdle model was employed to test peak-gene associations: the zero component modeled the relationship between ATAC open chromatin status and zero gene expression using a binomial distribution (logit link function), while the count component modeled the relationship between ATAC signal and gene expression levels using a zero-truncated negative binomial distribution (log link function).

Exon expression analysis of prioritized genes in cells

Functional annotation of exon expression from the GTEx project was performed for specific prioritized genes (derived from fastenloc, eCAVIAR, Open4Gene, gsMap, and prioritized cell-weighted co-expression network analysis), analyzing transcript diversity, structural features, and differential expression distribution across human tissues. LD score regression-based construction of statistical metrics for structural equation modeling in genomic control

LDSC regression analysis yielded single-variate heritability (h2) estimates for the seven input GWAS phenotypes (UC, DVT, AS, PSC, PG, ILD and EN), with h2 Z-scores of UC = 4.63, DVT = 7.06, AS = 3.17, PSC = 4.54, PG = 1.08, ILD = 2.67, and EN = 0.473 (full details in STable 1). Analysis of their pairwise genetic covariance matrix (STable 2, visualized in Fig. 2A) supported the construction of a common factor model. SEM validation confirmed that this model effectively fit both the genetic covariance structure and the empirical covariance matrix, achieving key fit indices including a CFI of 1.0 and a SRMR of 0.17 (model stability in STable 3a). The extracted latent factor F1 loadings and SEM parameters detailing its association with each phenotype are presented in STable 3b. We attribute the observed latent model inflation primarily to polygenic heritability signals within the SEM framework, rather than population stratification or pleiotropic bias. This conclusion is robustly supported by genetic heritability estimates: regression analyses yielded an intercept of 0.8761 (SE = 0.0067) and an observed-scale total heritability estimate (h2) of 0.2849 (SE = 0.0114). A total of 5,214 SNPs reached genome-wide significance.

Stratified evaluation of genomic structural equation modeling in GWAS

This study implemented an indirectly measured GWAS by extending SEM to incorporate individual variation data. Approximately 4.67 million SNPs were evaluated for association with UC-MIGA. The analysis revealed 18,935 genomic loci reaching genome-wide significance (P < 1 × 10⁻15⁰), among which 17,005 lead SNPs met the more stringent threshold of P ≤ 1 × 10⁻2⁰⁰ (see STable 4). Of these 17,005 UC-MIGA-associated lead SNPs, 13,510 have been previously reported. These SNPs pinpoint core pathological features including digestive inflammation (primarily ulcerative colitis), hepatobiliary disease (primarily primary sclerosing cholangitis), cutaneous inflammation (e.g., PG / EN), and cancer susceptibility (particularly colorectal, prostate, and breast malignancies), also linking to broader autoimmunity/inflammation, thrombosis/vascular, and metabolic/endocrine traits (detailed in STable 5a). This reflects the shared immunodysregulatory genetic basis underlying the multi-organ pathology defined by UC-MIGA. Key enriched pathways encompassed: cancer pathogenesis (e.g., PI3K/AKT and WNT oncogenic signaling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition [EMT], apoptosis escape, and metastasis via ECM degradation); immune/inflammatory regulation (e.g., TLR/innate immunity, cytokine signaling, inflammasome/NF-κB pathways); cellular stress/death (e.g., endoplasmic reticulum stress [ERS], mitochondrial apoptosis, autophagy/lysosomal degradation); metabolic/microenvironment dysregulation (e.g., bile acid/lipid metabolism disruption, angiogenesis); and tissue repair/fibrosis (e.g., TGF-β/SMAD signaling, extracellular matrix [ECM] degradation/remodeling) (see Stable 5b for detailed pathway annotations). Collectively, these findings delineate the unique pathology of UC-MIGA, characterized by gut-originated inflammatory dissemination leading to multi-organ stress and fibrosis.

FUMA-based evaluation of UC-MIGA structural equation models

Genomic architecture analysis using FUMA identified 2,622 risk loci (see STable 6). At the genome-wide significance threshold (sig.thres = 5e-8, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2B, STable 14), 1,982 potential UC-MIGA-associated genes were delineated. Functional annotation of 7,069 lead SNPs within the 2,622 loci revealed their predominant location in non-coding regions (83%: intergenic + intronic), with only 3% residing in functional regions (exonic + UTR). Non-coding RNA-related regions (ncRNA_intronic + ncRNA_exonic) accounted for 10%, while regulatory regions (upstream + downstream) were relatively scarce (2%). Heterogeneity testing was passed by only 120 lead SNPs. Cross-referencing with prior GWAS traits identified 114 novel SNPs among these (STable 7). Analysis of the GWAS-minus approach pinpointed 6 novel lead SNPs (rs7077040, rs11581607, rs7539625, rs1801274, rs67178545, rs9977672; STable 8). These novel SNPs show significant enrichment in key biological domains: autoimmune regulation (thyroid/intestinal/skin-joint diseases), immune cell homeostasis (lymphocyte/monocyte subsets and surface markers) and the inflammation-coagulation-metabolism network axis. Importantly, the enriched pathways implicated hallmark aging features characterized by chronic, low-grade inflammation, including accelerated epigenetic clocks (DNA methylation PhenoAge/GrimAge) and telomere attrition (leukocyte telomere length). Simultaneously, we observed SNP-mediated regulation of both immune cell subset frequencies (e.g., lymphocyte, monocyte, neutrophil counts) and functional receptor expression (e.g., CX3CR1, CD11b, CD11c, CD40, CD64, BAFF-R, CD27 levels across monocyte/macrophage subsets and B cells). These findings collectively reveal the molecular basis underlying impaired immune surveillance and sustained pro-inflammatory milieus during immunosenescence (see STable 9).

Fine-mapping

Significant fine-mapping analysis identified strong association signals (average posterior probability ≥ 0.75) across 14 genomic regions on multiple chromosomes. Associated loci include the AC018890.6_WIPF1 gene on chromosome 2 (rs78231297, rs118881878); the NUP210 gene (rs6810137, rs4684900, rs360899) and ADAMTS9 gene (rs12630431, rs4688196, rs4688197, rs894741, rs2371526) on chromosome 3; RP11-93O17.1 on chromosome 5 (rs6884259, rs17166644, rs75586944); the EYS gene (rs16895190) and COX6A1P3 gene (rs35254019, rs9387837, rs4543410, rs13193256) on chromosome 6; RP4-668E10.2 (rs9986798) and AC027269.2 (rs61620033) on chromosome 7; RP11-15B24.5 on chromosome 9 (rs7874021, rs4601413); DSCAML1 on chromosome 11 (rs678468); RP11-210L7.1 on chromosome 12 (rs78301341, rs978458); RP11-101O21.1 (rs75117199, rs17249768, rs73343491) and COX10-AS1 (rs9916623, rs79999810, rs76423718, rs35402358, rs11078163) on chromosome 17; and AL109618.1 on chromosome 20 (rs11700084, rs4419295, rs6516120, rs62200478, rs6117127). Regional association plots showed prominent peaks at these loci, with additional supporting evidence for association provided by other credible set variants (refer to Fig. 2D and STable 10).

Transcriptome-wide expression prediction

Transcriptome-wide association analysis (TWAS) performed via FUSION revealed 1,014 genes significantly associated with UC-MIGA traits after multiple testing correction (P < 1.51 × 10⁻⁶; Extended Data STable11). Subsequent fine-mapping using FOCUS with genomic structural equation modeling identified 1,049 genes exhibiting high disease causality probability (PIP > 0.99; STable 12). To establish robust genetic evidence, we applied a convergence approach identifying 152 high-confidence genes meeting all stringent criteria: significant TWAS association (P < 1.51 × 10⁻⁶), strong colocalization support (COLOC PP > 0.99), and high fine-mapping probability (FOCUS PIP > 0.99; STable 13). The directionality of TWAS Z-scores for these genes indicates differential expression associations with UC-MIGA pathogenesis: positive Z-scores (Z > 0) suggest increased expression potentially contributing to UC-MIGA susceptibility, whereas negative Z-scores (Z < 0) correlate with decreased expression linked to elevated UC-MIGA risk(see Fig. 2C).

Pathway and cell type enrichment

Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) analysis mapped 1,982 genes, which underwent gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (STable 15). This revealed significant enrichment primarily in key immune pathways: autoimmune disease mechanisms involving Th17 cell differentiation and interleukin-21 signaling; innate immunity and pathogen responses related to phagocytosis and viral GPCR signaling; dysregulation of immune cell function, evidenced by genes involved in regulatory T cells and macrophage polarization; inflammatory mediator control through prostaglandin synthesis regulation and suppression of inflammatory response. Additional enriched sets formed an immune microenvironment network: TGF-β/epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Rho GTPase modulation, and interleukin-6 response. Cell adhesion molecule binding and adaptive immune dimensions like the smallpox vaccine response further implicated cellular interactions. Collectively, the results highlight core immune phenotypes centered on Th17/Treg imbalance, autoimmune susceptibility, and chronic inflammation.

Cell type enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment within colon tissue, spleen, and various immune cells (including lymphocytes, natural killer cells, and macrophages), directly reflecting the core feature of chronic immune-inflammatory infiltration in the colonic mucosa, which involves abnormal activation of both the innate and adaptive immune systems (collectively, the global immune system). Notably, the enrichment of Th17 cells and peripheral blood B cells further underscores the critical roles of the Th17 pathway and B cell-mediated humoral immunity in the pathogenesis of UC-MIGA. Additionally, significant enrichment at the epigenetic level (involving histone modifications H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, and open chromatin regions marked by DNase) was observed in lung, brain, skin keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and mammary epithelial cells. This indicates that the systemic inflammatory effects of UC may influence multi-organ function through epigenetic regulation, thereby providing novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying its extraintestinal manifestations (as detailed in STables 16a, b, c, d, and e).

Heritability contributions across genomic regions

Based on genome-wide partitioned heritability enrichment analysis, we identified exceptionally high heritability enrichment in super-enhancer regions (SuperEnhancer_Hnisz.bedL2_0), accounting for 80.16% of the heritability (Prop._h2 = 0.802) with an enrichment fold of 4.79 (p = 0.0007). This robust significance persisted even after 500 bp extension (SuperEnhancer_Hnisz.extend.500.bedL2_0; enrichment fold = 4.58, p = 0.002), strongly suggesting that super-enhancer-driven epigenetic regulation constitutes a core mechanism in UC-MIGA [56], potentially influencing disease pathogenesis through modulation of immune-related genes (e.g., pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways). In contrast, intronic regions (Intron_UCSC.bedL2_0) exhibited moderate enrichment (enrichment fold = 2.31, p = 0.041), indicating that introns may contribute to UC-MIGA through secondary mechanisms such as alternative splicing rather than acting as primary drivers (see STable 17).

Chromosomal-level findings

This study utilized PRS analysis to delineate the genetic susceptibility profile of UC-MIGA at the chromosomal level. The results demonstrated significant heterogeneity in the contribution of different chromosomes to disease risk. Chromosomes 17 (β = 1.43) and 20 (β = 1.33) exhibited the strongest positive effects, suggesting these regions may harbor key genetic variants that increase UC-MIGA susceptibility. Chromosome 17 contains multiple genes implicated in innate immunity and inflammatory regulation (e.g., STAT3 [57], NOS2 [58]), while genes on chromosome 20, such as NFATC2 [59], likely contribute to UC-MIGA pathology by modulating T-cell differentiation and immune responses. Conversely, chromosomes 9 (β = -1.54) and 3 (β = -1.33) showed pronounced negative effects, indicating these loci potentially contain protective genetic variants. For instance, CARD9 [60] on chromosome 9 plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis by mediating antifungal immune responses, and PPARG [61] on chromosome 3 reduces disease risk by suppressing excessive inflammatory responses through its regulation of macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype (see Stables 14 and 18).

MAGMA enrichment analysis and spatial mapping reveal tissue-specific foundations of ulcerative colitis and telomere length

To further explore the genetic basis of UC and telomere length at the tissue-specific level, we performed MAGMA gene set enrichment analysis. Results revealed enrichment of genetic signals for both UC and telomere length in the terminal ileum and colonic tissues (Figs. 3E, 4E). Notably, for telomere length enrichment, the transverse colon (Colon_Transverse) exhibited higher gene set enrichment than the sigmoid colon (Colon_Sigmoid). Although both belong to the colon, they differ in embryonic origin, cellular composition, and function. For instance, the transverse colon primarily participates in water and electrolyte absorption with higher cellular proliferation and turnover rates (requiring stronger telomere maintenance), while the sigmoid colon is responsible for fecal storage and experiences greater mechanical stress but has lower cellular proliferation demands. These findings further support the critical role of cellular proliferation and telomere maintenance mechanisms in the pathophysiology of UC. Through gsMap, we successfully mapped GWAS genetic signals for UC and telomere length onto the spatial transcriptomic atlas of E16.5 mouse embryonic tissues, with robust support observed in intestinal regions (Figs. 3D, 4D), providing a cellular-resolution spatial perspective for understanding their pathological mechanisms.

Gene functional enrichment analysis reveals colon tissue-specific biological processes

To elucidate the biological functions underlying genetic signals associated with UC and telomere length, we employed the gene set enrichment analysis tool GeneEnrich to systematically evaluate functional enrichment of genes significantly associated with these traits in specific regions. The analysis focused on the transverse colon and sigmoid colon, using intestinal gene sets as background. Results from hypergeometric tests combined with permutation tests revealed significant enrichment of genes associated with UC and telomere length across multiple gene sets in these colonic regions (Figs. 3F–I, 4F-I). Specifically, within DNA repair and genome stability pathways, telomere length showed significant enrichment in DNA repair, homologous recombination (HRR & SSA), replication fork, telomere maintenance, telomeric DNA binding, and DNA replication initiation pathways, while UC exhibited significant enrichment in DNA repair and p53 pathway. Chronic intestinal inflammatory environments generate substantial genotoxic agents such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to DNA damage. Effective DNA repair mechanisms are therefore crucial for maintaining genomic stability of intestinal epithelial cells and preventing malignant transformation. Similarly, cellular energy metabolism pathways were commonly enriched in both traits. Telomere length demonstrated high enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial matrix, respiratory electron transport chain, and ATP synthesis coupled electron transport pathways. UC showed significant enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial matrix, respirasome, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and electron transfer activity, indicating that efficient energy production, particularly ATP generation via mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, constitutes a shared energetic foundation supporting telomere maintenance/replication and intestinal inflammatory response/repair. Additionally, both UC and telomere length were co-enriched in nucleotide metabolism and supply, cell cycle regulation, and stress response (p53 pathway) pathways. These findings suggest that shared genetic mechanisms underlying chronic inflammation and telomeric damage may operate through diverse biological pathways across different colonic regions.

Candidate gene networks

Based on the aforementioned multi-dimensional integrated evidence, we identified B cells and monocytes as potentially critical cell types involved in UC. To deeply dissect the transcriptional regulatory programs of these cells in disease, we employed the hdWGCNA method for cell-specific analysis. To overcome the inherent sparsity of single-cell data, metacells (k = 25) were constructed. Discussion

This study successfully delineated the shared genetic architecture underlying UC-associated multisystem inflammation (UC-MIGA) by integrating the genetic foundations of UC and its diverse extraintestinal manifestations (DVT, AS, PSC, PG, ILD, and EN). Rather than proposing a new clinical diagnosis, UC-MIGA serves as a genomic framework centered on a shared latent genetic factor (F1), which captures the pleiotropic risk linking primary intestinal inflammation to systemic complications. Employing a multi-tiered analytical approach—including genomic structural equation modeling, fine-mapping, TWAS, and cell-type-specific MR—we demonstrate that the pathogenesis of UC-MIGA is primarily driven by a “gut-origin” inflammatory cascade. This process initiates from the intestinal mucosa and leads to multi-organ stress and fibrosis, with core features involving Th17/Treg imbalance, super-enhancer-driven epigenetic regulation, and chronic low-grade inflammation associated with immunosenescence. This framework offers a robust genomic foundation to support the implementation of systemic targeted therapeutic strategies over isolated disease management in clinical practice.

Specifically, genomic SEM analysis of approximately 4.67 million SNPs identified 18,935 genome-wide significant loci at P < 1 × 10⁻15⁰, with 17,005 meeting stricter stratification (P ≤ 1 × 10⁻2⁰⁰). These risk loci implicate four core pathological networks: immune-inflammatory regulation (TLR/innate immunity, cytokine signaling, inflammasome/NF-κB pathways), cellular stress/death (ER stress, mitochondrial apoptosis, autophagy/lysosomal degradation), metabolic microenvironment dysregulation (bile acid/lipid metabolism, angiogenesis), and tissue repair/fibrosis (TGF-β/SMAD signaling, ECM remodeling). FUMA analysis further pinpointed 2,622 risk loci and 1,982 candidate UC-MIGA-associated genes, while fine-mapping (SuSIE/FINEMAP) identified high-confidence causal variants (mean posterior probability ≥ 0.75) across 14 genomic regions. Transcriptome-wide analysis (TWAS) revealed 1,014 significantly associated genes, with FOCUS fine-mapping prioritizing 1,049 genes exhibiting high disease causality probability (PIP > 0.99), ultimately yielding 152 high-confidence pathogenic genes through integrative evidence synthesis.

While the shared genetic signatures between UC and specific EIMs have been partially identified in previous disease-pair studies, the UC-MIGA framework provides a critical advancement by moving from bivariate correlations to a coherent latent genetic architecture (F1 factor). Compared to conventional single-trait GWAS studies, the heterogeneity testing (Q_SNP test) in this research successfully distinguished broad pathogenic pathways from UC-MIGA-specific ones, demonstrating that 120 tested lead SNPs exert their effects through intestinal-immune genetic mechanisms shared across UC-MIGA, while non-significant SNPs represent disease-specific pathogenic routes, thereby achieving precise separation of shared and disorder-specific pathways and providing genetic evidence for understanding UC-MIGA as a unified disease spectrum rather than a collection of independent entities. More significantly, the innovative “GWAS-minus-loci” approach identified six entirely novel UC-associated lead SNPs (rs7077040, rs11581607, rs9977672, rs7539625, rs1801274, rs67178545), whose enrichment analysis revealed a three-dimensional expansion of the UC-MIGA genetic landscape: first, these new loci showed significant enrichment in trans-organ autoimmune regulatory networks, encompassing pathways related to thyroid, intestinal, and skin-joint diseases, indicating UC-MIGA possesses a broad-spectrum autoimmune genetic basis extending beyond traditional inflammatory bowel disease; second, they precisely regulate the homeostatic maintenance of key immune cell subsets (lymphocyte, monocyte, neutrophil counts) and functional receptor expression profiles (levels of CX3CR1, CD11b, CD11c, CD40, CD64, BAFF-R, CD27 across monocyte/macrophage and B cell subsets), uncovering the fine-grained molecular regulatory mechanisms by which UC-MIGA influences disease susceptibility through immune cell phenotypic remodeling; furthermore, these loci directly linked UC-MIGA for the first time to core features of immune aging, including accelerated epigenetic clock progression (DNA methylation PhenoAge/GrimAge acceleration) and telomere system dysfunction (leukocyte telomere length shortening), thereby elevating the pathological framework of UC-MIGA from localized organ inflammation to the higher-order disease spectrum of systemic immune aging.

Building upon this foundation, our study employed a multi-tiered functional validation strategy to dissect the molecular mechanisms of UC-MIGA: fine-mapping analyses utilizing both SuSIE and FINEMAP algorithms pinpointed high-confidence causal variants with an average PIP ≥ 0.75 across 14 genomic loci, including key gene regions such as AC018890.6_WIPF1, NUP210 [62, 63], ADAMTS9 [64, 65], EYS [66, 67], and COX6A1P3, fundamentally shifting from statistical association to causal inference by precisely mapping GWAS signals to likely functional variants, thereby providing precise molecular coordinates for subsequent drug target development and gene therapy; TWAS coupled with FOCUS fine-mapping identified 152 high-confidence effector genes meeting simultaneous criteria of TWAS significance, strong colocalization support, and high fine-mapping probability, uniquely bridging the genotype–phenotype gap at the transcriptional regulation level by revealing the mediating mechanism whereby genetic variants influence UC-MIGA susceptibility through modulating gene expression, providing direct evidence for the “gene-transcription-disease” molecular pathway; MAGMA gene-set enrichment analysis, through pathway annotation of 1,982 genes, systematically uncovered four core pathological axes of UC-MIGA: an autoimmune disease mechanism dominated by Th17 cell differentiation and interleukin-21 signaling, innate immune pathogen responses involving phagocytosis and viral GPCR signaling, immune cell dysfunction reflected by regulatory T-cell and macrophage polarization imbalance, and inflammatory mediator control impairment due to defective prostaglandin synthesis regulation and inflammation response suppression, elucidating the systemic immune dysregulation mechanism from molecules to pathways in UC-MIGA; furthermore, cell-type enrichment analysis using the CELLECT framework integrated with single-cell transcriptomic data revealed significant enrichment of UC-MIGA heritability in colon tissue, spleen, and various immune cells (lymphocytes, natural killer cells, macrophages), with specific enrichment in Th17 cells and peripheral blood B cells further confirming the critical role of humoral immunity in UC-MIGA pathogenesis, while also observing significant enrichment at the epigenetic level (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K36me3 histone modifications and DNase hypersensitive sites) in lung, brain, skin keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and mammary epithelial cells, indicating that the systemic inflammatory effects of UC may influence multi-organ function through epigenetic regulation.

This study further revealed the deep regulatory mechanisms of UC-MIGA genetic susceptibility through the following methods: partitioned heritability analysis based on functional annotation of genomic regions demonstrated that super-enhancer regions (specifically SuperEnhancer_Hnisz) exhibit extremely significant heritability enrichment, accounting for 80.16% of the total heritability (enrichment fold 4.79, P = 0.0007), with highly significant results even after a 500 bp extension (enrichment fold 4.58, P = 0.002), a core insight indicating that UC-MIGA genetic susceptibility primarily operates through super-enhancer-mediated epigenetic regulation rather than direct effects of coding region variants as traditionally assumed; PRS analysis, using the PRS-CS Bayesian method, quantified heterogeneity in genetic contributions at the chromosomal level, identifying chromosome 17 (β = 1.43) and chromosome 20 (β = 1.33) with the strongest positive effects and chromosome 9 (β = -1.54) and chromosome 3 (β = -1.33) with significant protective effects, revealing a dynamic balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory gene networks in risk stratification; MR analysis uncovered that systemic inflammation in UC-MIGA arises from synergistic dysregulation of multi-immune cell networks, specifically where immune cell-specific gene expression drives gut barrier disruption and systemic inflammation spread through mechanisms including energy metabolism (DLD in CD4 NC cells mediating mitochondrial oxidative stress [68]), epigenetic modifications (SETD4 in CD4 NC cells upregulating lysine methylation [69] and KAT2B in CD4 ET cells enhancing histone acetylation [70]), cell–cell communication (IL23R in CD8 S100B cells activating the IL-23/Th17 axis [71–73] and FCGR2A in NK cells mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [74]), and inflammatory cell death (PLAC8 in CD4 SOX4 cells regulating programmed cell death [75, 76]); conversely, protective mechanisms involve YWHAB in DC/monocyte/NK cells delaying disease progression via anti-apoptosis [77], HYAL3 in CD4 NC cells and monocytes blocking inflammatory cascades by degrading pro-inflammatory hyaluronic acid fragments [78, 79], PDXDC1 in CD4 ET cells mediating pyridoxal metabolism [80], SLC2A9 in CD4 NC cells reducing ROS-related oxidative stress damage through urate transport [81, 82], ATPIF1 in CD4 NC cells maintaining energy homeostasis [83], TMBIM1 in plasma cells regulating calcium homeostasis [84], and AHSA1 in Mono NC cells preserving protein folding [85], collectively highlighting that disease progression stems from an imbalance between risk genes driving inflammatory storms and protective genes providing defense; additionally, lncRNA regulatory networks (such as AC009948.5 in B cells acting as a potential regulator of Th17 cell differentiation [86], SNHG9 in plasma cells driving lipid metabolism reprogramming [87], and NNT-AS1 in CD8 NC cells promoting cellular proliferation and invasion [88, 89]) contribute to cross-organ cascades in the extracolonic manifestations of UC.

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG repeats) at chromosome ends that protect chromosomes from degradation and fusion. Their length shortens with cell division and aging, correlating with cellular senescence, disease, and oxidative stress. Multiple studies demonstrate that UC patients exhibit universally shortened telomeres associated with aging, disease duration, and severe clinical phenotypes (including inflammation severity and tumor progression) [90, 91]. Our preceding analyses indicate that genetic risk for immunosenescence plays a critical role within the UC-MIGA framework. Considering that UC exhibited the maximal standardized factor loading (0.9801, Table S3b) on the shared F1 factor—representing the core genetic landscape of UC-MIGA—and serves as the primary intestinal origin site for systemic inflammatory dissemination, we utilized UC GWAS data and colonic tissue sequencing as representative proxies for downstream analysis. This approach aimed to systematically explore the mechanisms linking UC immunosenescence to telomere length and to identify potential targets for early intervention at the local gut level, ideally before inflammation spreads systemically. Importantly, these specific association results derived from colonic analysis should not be directly generalized to the other extraintestinal phenotypes within the UC-MIGA spectrum.

By constructing a macro-to-micro disease mechanism framework, we discovered that the genetic architecture of UC and telomere shortening is not randomly distributed but specifically converges on particular cell types, molecular pathways, and developmental time windows: (1) Colon tissue-specific genetic risk convergence is fundamentally rooted in DNA repair and genome stability, cellular energy metabolism, and related processes; (2) B cells serve as core integrators, connecting regulatory networks of immunosenescence and inflammatory dissemination through antigen-driven clonal expansion and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion; (3) The developmental origin hypothesis gains genetic support, proposing a spatiotemporal dynamic disease model; (4) High-confidence candidate genes and risk loci provide novel targets for precision therapy.

We found that genetic risks for UC and telomere length exhibit distinct tissue-spatial specificity—not coincidental but suggestive of a shared intestinal origin for the initial triggers underlying different phenotypes. Genetic risk signals for both traits showed varying degrees of significant enrichment in colonic tissues (including transverse and sigmoid colon). As the central hub for intestinal homeostasis maintenance and immune surveillance, the colon encompasses mucosal barrier defense and inflammatory-repair dynamic balance, where initial local dysregulation may act as the primary inductive environment for subsequent systemic dissemination. Specifically, risk variants likely exert initial effects by influencing regulatory programs of key genes (e.g., eQTL/sQTL) within intestinal regions, disrupting critical molecular pathways active in these areas. Enrichment analysis revealed significant clustering of DNA damage repair-mitochondrial energy metabolism-telomere maintenance signaling pathways in colonic genetic risk, suggesting that inflammation-driven oxidative stress and energy metabolism reprogramming-mediated telomere attrition may constitute key localized triggers for the broader systemic cascade. Focusing on the primary site of induction, this organ-level molecular dysfunction ultimately impairs proliferative repair capacity and genome stability maintenance in colonic epithelial cells, manifesting histopathologically as mucosal ulceration, crypt abscesses, and barrier disruption in UC patients. Notably, the differential enrichment patterns between the transverse colon and sigmoid colon reveal the importance of functional compartmentalization within the intestinal origin of this systemic axis: the transverse colon, as a primary site for water absorption, exhibits higher enrichment of telomere length-related genetic signals, potentially associated with its specific physiological functions and metabolic demands; whereas the sigmoid colon, as part of the distal colon, shows genetic risk more oriented toward inflammatory response pathways, consistent with the high susceptibility of the distal colon to inflammatory stimuli and the elevated incidence of diseases such as UC in this region [92–94]. In summary, impaired mucosal repair and inflammation clearance in UC couple with cellular senescence and regenerative dysfunction from telomere shortening. This complex functional imbalance and intersecting effects may represent the initial trigger phase that exacerbates disease severity and potentially fuels the systemic inflammatory dissemination across the UC-MIGA spectrum.

B cells were identified as the top-priority cell type shared between UC and telomere length within our framework. A critical function of B cells is producing secretory IgA, fundamental for intestinal immune tolerance and mucosal barrier integrity [95]. In UC, persistent antigen stimulation drives B cell clonal expansion [96]. AID-mediated DNA breaks during CSR/SHM can aberrantly target telomeres [97]. Although germinal center B cells upregulate telomerase to compensate for telomere attrition [98], chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in UC disrupt telomere maintenance[99], ultimately causing telomere shortening and clonal expansion that increase neoplastic risk [100]. Our B cell co-expression network analysis revealed coordinated enrichment of DNA repair, oxidative phosphorylation, and telomere maintenance pathways, indicating these cells face dual metabolic-genomic pressures: requiring high energy to support antibody synthesis while simultaneously repairing inflammation-induced DNA damage, with telomere shortening becoming the ultimate cost of this resource competition. Our analysis provides supportive evidence for a potential developmental origin component of the disease. Using the gsMap algorithm, which has been validated to localize IQ to the embryonic brain and MCHC to the embryonic liver, we found that shared genetic risk signals between ulcerative colitis and telomere length significantly converge on the intestinal regions of E16.5 mouse embryos (Figs. 3D, 4D). We postulate that genetic risk variants exert effects during critical embryonic intestinal development windows, potentially influencing proliferation and differentiation of intestinal epithelial cells and immune progenitor cells, as well as establishment of the initial immune microenvironment—for example, setting potential intestinal mucosal barrier integrity thresholds, B cell subset differentiation bias [101], or initial telomere length thresholds in B cell precursor pools [98]. This prompted us to propose a “two-hit” spatiotemporal dynamic model: The first hit occurs during development, where genetic risk establishes inherent vulnerability in the intestinal epithelial-immune network, including increased mucosal barrier permeability, excessive activation tendency of lamina propria B cells, and baseline defects in telomere maintenance mechanisms. The second hit occurs later in life when encountering environmental stressors (such as microbiota dysbiosis, infection, or dietary factors); the pre-existing vulnerable circuitry becomes an amplifier of pathological processes (chronic inflammation mediated by B cells and monocytes, accompanied by epithelial renewal dysfunction from accelerated telomere shortening), potentially initiating intestinal mucosal barrier failure, which represents the early trigger of systemic immune homeostasis collapse associated with relapsing–remitting intestinal and systemic inflammatory clinical manifestations. We hypothesize that telomere-dysfunctional B cells may excessively secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17) and diverse autoantibodies [102]. These inflammatory mediators could disseminate through the circulatory system, participating in the activation of tissue-resident monocytes, macrophages, and other immune cells in distal organs, potentially contributing to the onset of multi-organ autoimmune responses and priming the development of systemic inflammatory cascades. This model refines traditional gene-environment interaction concepts by conferring explicit temporal sequence and biological content, providing theoretical foundations for early identification and intervention in high-risk populations during the initial trigger stage.

Through multi-method integrated analysis (including fastenloc, eCAVIAR, Open4Gene, etc.), this study positioned NKX2-3, LINC01475, P4HTM, GNAI2, and RHOA as core hub nodes potentially initiating the shared genomic risks associated with the UC-MIGA axis. These genes function as potential cross-scale pathological bridges in UC-telomere shortening comorbidity mechanisms by respectively driving intestinal immune imbalance [103–105] (NKX2-3), epigenetic regulation [106, 107] (LINC01475), hypoxic stress response[108] (P4HTM), immune signal dampening [109, 110] (GNAI2), and epithelial dynamics remodeling [111, 112] (RHOA). Notably, risk variants in NKX2-3 and RHOA may operate through “pleiotropic” mechanisms, simultaneously dismantling both the physical-immune dual defense of the intestinal epithelial barrier and disrupting the coordinated telomere homeostasis maintenance network. NKX2-3 mutations may skew the balance between mitochondrial autophagy and inflammatory pathways such as IL-6 and TNF-α [113–116], potentially promoting intestinal epithelial cell senescence. RHOA dysfunction, while disrupting tight junctions, induces cytoskeletal remodeling and barrier dysfunction through actin tension dysregulation, potentially causing cellular morphological changes and cell cycle arrest, thereby promoting cellular senescence [117]. Our findings provide theoretical support for developing preventive treatment strategies based on early genetic risk identification and intervention at the initial trigger stage to potentially mitigate the systemic dissemination of inflammation across the UC-MIGA spectrum.

Despite significant advances in understanding UC and extra-intestinal manifestations, several limitations remain, which also represent future research directions. Portions of the eQTL/sQTL data utilized in this study were derived from post-mortem healthy tissues, failing to capture dynamic regulatory changes under disease states. Future studies should collect and validate analyses using tissues from UC patients. Second, while our study integrates systemic GWAS data, the downstream functional validation was primarily focused on colonic tissues based on the “gut-origin” hypothesis. Future investigations incorporating extraintestinal tissues (e.g., skin, joint, or lung biopsies) are necessary to characterize site-specific effector mechanisms in other organs of the UC-MIGA spectrum. Third, while the exceptional model fit (CFI = 1.0) and stable quality control metrics (λGC = 1.115, intercept = 0.8761) demonstrate that the shared genetic architecture (F1) is relatively minimally impacted by population stratification or confounding bias, the generalizability of our findings within the broader European population still warrants cautious interpretation. Further validation in even larger-scale European cohorts is necessary to ensure the universal applicability and robustness of the UC-MIGA framework. Finally, this analysis did not effectively incorporate or distinguish key demographic stratification variables such as sex and age, thereby limiting the depth of understanding regarding how these factors influence the genetic basis of complex traits.

Conclusion

In summary, by employing Genomic SEM, this study successfully extracted a shared latent genetic factor (F1) underlying ulcerative colitis (UC) and its associated extra-intestinal manifestations. Post-SEM analyses reveal that the genetic risk landscape of this architecture—termed UC-MIGA—is characterized by a gut-origin induction that triggers multi-organ stress and fibrosis. Its core features include Th17/Treg imbalance, super-enhancer-driven epigenetic regulation, and chronic low-grade inflammation linked to immunosenescence. By conducting targeted UC-telomere analysis focused on this intestinal origin, we constructed a comprehensive genomic framework spanning system to organ, cell to gene, and development to aging. We anchored these shared genetic risks to specific cell types—primarily B cells and monocytes as core immune effectors, and epithelial cells as barrier maintenance cells—and prioritized molecular pathways involving DNA repair, mitochondrial energetics, and telomere maintenance. Our proposed “intestinal origin-B cell mediated-systemic dissemination” cascade and “developmental priming-stress-triggered two-hit” model provide a novel framework for understanding the systemic inflammatory trajectory initiated within the gut.

This study offers, to some extent, an evolving perspective on ulcerative colitis (UC), shifting from a single-organ focus toward a systemic understanding of UC-MIGA as a potentially coherent immunosenescence architecture. Our findings preliminarily suggest that future therapeutic strategies might benefit from moving beyond a strictly “organ-by-organ” symptomatic approach; instead, while continuing to manage specific extraintestinal manifestations, such strategies could also aim to restore systemic balance by targeting the “gut-origin” mechanisms within the local colonic environment to potentially prevent further systemic dissemination. By translating these genomic discoveries into a preliminary mechanistic framework, we provide a foundation for exploring individualized prevention and treatment strategies based on genetic risk stratification across the UC-MIGA spectrum.
