Characterization of incident heart failure after ischaemic stroke/transient ischaemic attack: a UK Biobank study
Authors: Rahul G Muthalaly, Nitesh Nerlekar, Sean Tan, Andrew Lin, Adam J Nelson, Thanh Phan, Henry Ma, Stephen J Nicholls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo define incident heart failure (HF) risk in ischaemic stroke/transient ischaemic attack (TIA) survivors. The secondary aims were to define the association of HF with all-cause mortality in stroke survivors and to describe their cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) findings.MethodsThis was a prospective cohort study using the UK Biobank (UKBB) cohort of individuals aged 40 to 69 years old. We excluded individuals with prior HF and stratified them by history of ischaemic stroke/TIA. The main outcome was incident HF as defined by hospital admissions coded for heart failure. Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and CMR findings.ResultsWe included 405 406 individuals (age 56.5 years, 45.6% males). Over 13.7 years, 15 565 individuals experienced incident HF. Stroke survivors had an overall HR of 3.6 [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.3–3.8, P < .0001] for HF hospitalization and an adjusted HR of 1.4 (95% CI 1.3–1.5, P < .0001). The risk of HF hospitalization was greater than the risk of myocardial infarction (12.6% vs 5.4%). Stroke survivors with HF had a lower LVEF and higher LV mass than those without HF. Incident HF in stroke survivors was associated with a HR of 1.8 (95% CI 1.6–1.9, P < .0001) for mortality.ConclusionIncident HF is common in stroke survivors and strongly associates with mortality. The risk of HF varies greatly depending on underlying risk factors. Exploratory analyses suggest that stroke survivors with HF may have a lower ejection fraction phenotype. Future trials of HF preventive therapy in high-risk stroke survivors are warranted.Keywords: Ischaemic stroke, Heart failure, Cardiomyopathies

Introduction

Ischaemic stroke and heart failure (HF) are two of the highest burden diseases, with 5.31 million healthy life years lost due to HF and 11.3 million lost due to ischaemic stroke in 2021.1 Mortality rates due to acute ischaemic stroke have decreased by 16% since 2010, resulting in an increasing prevalence of individuals living with a history of ischaemic stroke. This has implications for management of stroke survivorship.

There is a well-established link between ischaemic stroke and subsequent coronary atherosclerotic events and revascularization.2,3 However, there has been little data published about the risk of incident HF after ischaemic stroke. Only one study, a retrospective population-based cohort, has addressed this question.2 They found a hazard ratio for incident HF of 2.2 at 3 years with an absolute incidence higher than that of myocardial infarction (MI). No study to date has investigated imaging characteristics of stroke survivors with HF vs those without HF.

HF and ischaemic stroke share risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and physical inactivity.4 Additionally, there is evidence of direct myocardial injury after stroke through neurogenically stunned myocardium (including Takotsubo syndrome), troponin elevations in the absence of culprit coronary artery disease and asymptomatic electrocardiogram changes.5 Neurogenically mediated direct injury may increase the likelihood of future cardiomyopathy and incident HF independent of traditional risk factors.

The aims of this study were to define the risk of incident HF after ischaemic stroke in individuals without a history of HF and to assess the association between HF after ischaemic stroke and all-cause mortality. We also sought to compare cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) characteristics in the stroke/TIA cohort vs non-stroke cohort and, furthermore, in stroke survivors who developed HF vs those who did not.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective cohort study of individuals without a history of HF recruited as part of the UK Biobank study (UKBB). The UKBB include participants from the general UK population aged between 40 and 69 years at the time of recruitment between 2006 and 2010. This cohort underwent baseline assessment with interviews, physical measures and blood testing. Interviews were conducted with trained staff asking a range of questions regarding medical history and sociodemographic information. Subsequent outcomes are determined through linkage with national death records and Hospital Episode Statistics data classified using the International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10). We received approval from the UKBB for the use of the data for this project (application number 22076520765).

Baseline variable and outcome assessments

Baseline variables included age, sex, medical history, and blood testing results. We determined history for a range of traditional HF risk factors using the structured medical interview, operation records, and hospital diagnostic records prior to the date of assessment (as shown in the Supplementary information). Laboratory test results were assessed at baseline and included low-density lipoprotein (LDL), cystatin-C and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). We also used physical assessments taken at the time of recruitment including body mass index and systolic blood pressure recordings.

Incident heart failure was defined as hospital records with ICD-10 codes relating to I50 (heart failure) or I42 (cardiomyopathy) subsequent to the recruitment date using previously published codes.6 For individuals with both outcomes, the date of incident heart failure was defined as the earlier of the two. Myocardial infarction was defined using the ICD-10 codes for first myocardial infarction (I21), subsequent myocardial infarction (I22) or other acute ischaemic heart disease (I24). All-cause mortality was determined using the national death record. Censor dates were set as the last date of follow-up for hospital records and for national death records.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) analysis

We used CMR data collected at the imaging visit of the UKBB cohort which occurred after 2014 in a sub-set of individuals. The full imaging protocol has been described previously.7 In brief, CMR was performed in a 1.5 Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Syngo Platform, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with ECG gating. For cardiac function, three long-axis cines and a complete short axis stack of balanced steady state free precession cines were acquired. Atrial volumes were calculated using long-axis cines. Global longitudinal strain was derived using feature-tracking on long-axis cines. No gadolinium contrast was administered for these examinations.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are expressed as per cent and numbers for categorical data and mean and standard deviation. Normality of continuous variables was assessed visually using histograms. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the ischaemic stroke and non-ischaemic stroke cohorts were undertaken using chi-squared testing or t-testing for categorical and continuous variables respectively.

Survival analysis was undertaken using the Kaplan–Meier method with Cox proportional hazard regression models used to assess hazard ratios for univariable and multi-variable models to predict heart failure based on ischaemic stroke status. Proportional hazards were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Discrimination of the models was assessed using the c-statistic with a c-statistic of 0.5 demonstrating no discrimination ability and 1.0 demonstrating perfect discrimination.

To investigate the association between incident heart failure and all-cause mortality we modelled incident heart failure as a time-dependent covariate in a Cox proportional hazards regression. To visualize the range of incident heart failure risk across different combinations of the baseline variables, we created a graphic user interface using the shiny library to model survival probabilities.

We compared CMR characteristics in the stroke/TIA vs non-stroke cohort. We also compared these parameters in the stroke/TIA cohort who had developed heart failure by the time of imaging assessment and in those who had not. These comparisons were performed using linear regression.

We performed sensitivity analysis examining the association between stroke and incident HF with the entire cohort before exclusions for missing data to ascertain whether bias was introduced by those with missing baseline variable data. We also used Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard modelling to estimate the incidence of HF in stroke survivors when treating mortality as a competing risk.

An alpha level of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the Table 1, survminer, tidycmprsk, gtsummary, ggsurvfit, survival, and shiny packages.

Results

Participant characteristics

After excluding 3420 participants with a history of HF at the time of recruitment and 93 734 participants with missing data, there were 405 406 individuals included in this study as shown in the flowchart in Figure 1 and the Central Illustration. Across all participants, the mean age was 56.5 ± 8.1 years and 45.6% (n = 185 084) were male. Ischaemic heart disease was present in 2.8% (n = 11 373) individuals. There were 6183 participants (1.5%) with a history of ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA). The ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort had a higher burden of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including being older, more male, more smoking and a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, atrial fibrillation, ischaemic heart disease, and valvular heart disease as shown in Table 1. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were lower in the ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort. Additionally, the ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort had lower levels of current alcohol use.

Incident heart failure

During a median follow-up of 13.7 years, 15 565 individuals experienced an incident HF hospitalization. This occurred in 12.6% (n = 777) individuals with a history of ischaemic stroke compared to 3.7% (n = 14 788) individuals without a history of ischaemic stroke as shown in Figure 2. In univariable cox regression, ischaemic stroke/TIA history had a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.6 [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.3–3.8, P < .0001] for incident HF hospitalization. The 10-year risk of incident HF hospitalization was 8.8% in the ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort vs 2.1% in the non-stroke/TIA cohort.

Kaplan–Meier time-to-event curve for incident heart failure by ischaemic stroke/TIA Status. Time to first heart failure event stratified by ischaemic stroke/TIA status. Log-rank test demonstrated a significant difference in the probability of incident heart failure hospitalization (P < .0001). At 10-years the risk of incident heart failure in the ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort was 8.2% vs 2.1% in the non-stroke cohort

After adjustment for a broad range of heart failure risk factors, ischaemic stroke/TIA history still conferred a HR of 1.4 (95% CI 1.3–1.5, P < .0001) for incident HF as shown in Table 2. The c-statistic for this model was 0.78 representing good discrimination. There was a wide range in the 10-year risk of incident HF dependent on the number of risk factors present as shown in the examples in Supplementary Figure S1.

Comparison with myocardial infarction

A separate cohort of ischaemic stroke/TIA survivors without a history of MI was generated to compare rates of incident MI to incident HF. There were 394 033 individuals in this cohort, of which 1.4% (n = 5486) had a history of ischaemic stroke/TIA. There were 11 585 MIs during follow-up, occurring in 5.4% of ischaemic stroke/TIA survivors and 3.0% of the non-stroke cohort. The 10-year risk of MI was 3.4% in the ischaemic stroke/TIA cohort and 1.9% in the non-stroke cohort, with a univariable hazard ratio of 1.9 (95% CI 1.7–2.1, P < .0001) and multivariate hazard ratio of 1.2 (1.1–1.4, P < .0001) after adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, AF, valvular disease, OSA, peripheral arterial disease, carotid disease, LDL, body mass index, HbA1c, and a history of dyslipidaemia as shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Association of incident heart failure with all-cause mortality

Incident heart failure in ischaemic stroke/TIA survivors conferred a hazard ratio of 1.9 (95% CI 1.7–2.0, P < .0001) for all-cause mortality when modelled as a time-dependent covariate as shown in Table 3. After adjustment for risk factors in a multivariable model, incident heart failure remained a substantial and statistically significant predictor of all-cause mortality in ischaemic stroke/TIA survivors with an HR of 1.8 (95% CI 1.6–1.9, P < .0001).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging parameters

There were 32 265 participants with CMR data available at a mean follow-up of 10.7 ± 2.8 years after initial assessment. Of these, 257 participants were stroke/TIA survivors and 14 of those suffered incident HF prior to CMR assessment. LV ejection fraction was statistically significantly lower in stroke/TIA survivors but the difference was not clinically meaningful (54.5% vs 55.5%, P = .04) as shown in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figure S3. LV mass index was slightly higher in stroke/TIA survivors (90.4 g/m2 vs 86.0 g/m2, P = .003). LV end diastolic volume (LVEDV) and LV global longitudinal strain were also similar in stroke/TIA survivors and the non-stroke cohort.

Comparing stroke survivors who developed HF to those who did not, LV ejection fraction was lower (39.7% vs 54.6%, P = .002) and LV mass index was higher (131.8 g/m2 vs 90.1 g/m2, P = .01). There was no significant difference in LV global longitudinal strain or LVEDV, though power was limited due to the lower number of stroke survivors with incident HF and CMR data available. There were trends towards larger LVEDV and reduced LV global longitudinal strain as shown in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figure S3. Stroke survivors who developed heart failure had a higher burden of comorbidities including ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and valvular disease as shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Sensitivity analyses

Using the entire cohort before exclusions for missing data, ischaemic stroke history had a HR of 4.1 (95% CI 3.9–4.1, P < .0001) which was of greater magnitude than the association after exclusions for missing data.

Using Fine-Gray sub-distribution analysis to adjust for mortality as a competing event for incident HF, we found no significant change in the association between ischaemic stroke and incident HF (sHR 3.7, 95% CI 3.6–3.9, P < .0001).

Discussion

Our study has several important new findings. Firstly, we found that ischaemic/stroke TIA is a risk factor for the development of incident HF in HF naïve stroke survivors, even after adjusting for multiple cardiovascular risk factors (HR 3.6 unadjusted and HR 1.4 adjusted). Furthermore, we found that the risk of incident HF is greater than that of MI in MI-naïve stroke survivors (HR 3.6 vs HR 1.9 unadjusted and 10-year risk of 8.2% vs 3.4%). The risk of incident HF varies greatly in stroke survivors dependent on the presence or absence of underlying risk factors, such as atrial fibrillation, ischaemic heart disease and hypertension. This reflects the heterogeneity of an ischaemic stroke population. Additionally, the development of incident HF in stroke survivors confers an almost two-fold risk (HR 1.8) for all-cause mortality after adjustment for other factors. Lastly, we found that stroke survivors overall have similar CMR characteristics to a non-stroke cohort, but that stroke survivors who develop HF have left ventricles with a higher mass and a reduced ejection fraction. There was a trend towards larger LV end-diastolic volumes though this was not statistically significant.

Few studies have investigated the risk of incident HF in stroke survivors. Sposato et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of older adult (>66 years old) in Ontario between 2002 and 2012.2 They found that stroke survivors had a 7.5% 3-year risk of incident HF compared to 4.2% in a propensity matched non-stroke cohort (HR 2.19, P < .0001). The relative risk agrees with our findings, though the absolute risk was higher in that study likely relating to the older and more comorbid population (the UKBB recruited people aged 40–69 years old). Our study confirms those findings and goes further to demonstrate a strong relationship between incident HF and mortality. We also used imaging data to identify an HFrEF phenotype in these individuals. Interestingly, Sposato et al. also found a lower rate of MI when compared to incident HF in stroke survivors (7.5% vs 4.8%), as demonstrated in this UKBB analysis. We found a relatively low 10-year risk of MI, which agrees with a previously performed meta-analysis, though there were few long-term studies with large populations in that meta-analysis.3

A plausible link exists between ischaemic stroke and incident HF based on several observations. Firstly, there is evidence of myocardial injury in the setting of stroke reflected in the 30–60% of individuals with elevated troponin and/or B-type natriuretic peptide levels in the absence of culprit coronary disease.8 Furthermore, there a number of ‘stroke heart syndromes’, including Takotsubo syndrome, which are likely triggered by systemic inflammation, catecholamine surges, and increased sympathetic tone.9–11 These syndromes suggest a mechanism for myocardial injury that may form the substrate for future incident HF. Lastly, in rat models of MCA occlusion, there is evidence of cell death signals on immunohistopathology of the myocardium, including caspase 3, Fas ligand, and MAP1LC3A.12 Thus, myocardial damage at the time of ischaemic stroke mediated through inflammation and neurogenic perturbations may contribute to the observed independent risk of future HF in our study. Such injury may result in downstream development of myocardial fibrosis, which has been demonstrated in ischaemic stroke survivors on CMR previously.13

The finding of frequent incident HF in the stroke survivor population presents clinical implications for post-stroke management. Survival after stroke is increasing owing to developments in acute stroke care, such as mechanical thrombectomy, and improvements in anti-atherosclerotic and anticoagulant therapy. Thus, the long-term trajectory of stroke survivors is increasingly important. Given the prevalence of cardiovascular disease including rhythm abnormalities, coronary artery disease, and incident HF as demonstrated here, we advocate for the routine involvement of cardiologists in post-stroke care. The findings presented here support investigation of HF screening in stroke survivors which could be performed using B-type natriuretic peptides or echocardiography. These findings also provide rationale for the trial of HF primary prevention interventions, spanning from lifestyle modification to sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and beta blockers.

Our study does have limitations. Firstly, the UKBB relies on nationalized hospital and death records for outcome assessment. Importantly, the validity of diagnoses based on hospital and primary care coding is always an issue in registry studies, with previous studies suggesting a positive predictive value of 81% for heart failure in a Danish registry.14 This necessarily introduces bias in the assessment of HF, ischaemic stroke, and TIA. It is likely that our numbers underrepresent the true incidence of HF as some individuals may not present to hospital for their incident HF episode. Secondly, CMR data were only available in a small subset of individuals who had incident HF with a history of stroke. Thus, our findings of HFrEF with a higher LV mass should be treated as exploratory only. It is likely that there is under-reporting of HFpEF given relative difficulty in making the diagnosis when compared with HFrEF. Furthermore, we cannot comment on the incidence of heart failure with a mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) but believe that our findings provide grounds for further investigation of HF phenotypes post-ischaemic stroke. Additionally, the UKBB likely selects for a younger and healthier cohort given the age limits (40–69 years old) and the need to present for assessment. This would likely bias our results by under-representing sicker individuals who may have a higher risk of HF than those in the UKBB population. Lastly, we did not have data on time since ischaemic stroke/TIA. Previous studies have shown a time-dependent relationship for the risk of myocardial infarction, where the risk is highest in the early period after a stroke, and we cannot exclude such a relationship for incident HF.

Future studies should aim to better explore the characteristics of heart failure episodes after stroke, particularly the frequency of HFpEF vs HFrEF syndromes. Additionally, our study provides a rationale for the investigation of biochemical and imaging-based biomarkers of HF (such as B-type natriuretic peptide, myocardial fibrosis, and left ventricular strain) which may help predict HF in stroke survivors. Such biomarkers, in combination with risk models such as ours, may help to identify high risk individuals who would be suitable for trials of HF preventative therapy such as SGLT-2 inhibitors. Lastly, the manifold risks of cardiac disease in stroke survivors (including ASCVD, arrhythmia and heart failure) forms a basis for a collaborative model of care that routinely involves cardiologists in the care of ischaemic stroke survivors.

Conclusion

Ischaemic stroke is a risk factor for future incident HF even after adjusting for multiple risk factors. The risk of HF after ischaemic stroke is likely higher than that of myocardial infarction and varies widely depending on a patient’s risk factors. Incident HF in stroke survivors portends a poor prognosis, conferring almost twice the risk of mortality. CMR data suggests that HFrEF may be the dominant phenotype of HF after ischaemic stroke, but further data are needed to elucidate this pattern.
