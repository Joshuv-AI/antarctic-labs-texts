Equitable digital health systems begin before technology is deployed
Authors: Gen Li
Published: 2026

Digital technology can expand access and strengthen health systems, but it may also reproduce existing inequities. Drawing on research from BMC Global and Public Health, we argue that equitable digital health innovation begins with access, literacy, trust, workforce capacity, governance, and robust health-system foundations, instead of technology alone.

Digital technologies are reshaping global health, from mobile health to artificial intelligence (AI) and data-driven care. These tools are often presented as ways to expand access, improve efficiency, save costs, and reach communities that health systems have historically underserved [1]. However, the digital transformation of healthcare also raises a critical question: who benefits from these innovations? This question matters because technology does not enter health systems neutrally but is introduced into existing conditions of inequality.

Three papers we published in BMC Global and Public Health offer timely perspectives on what it takes to build equitable digital health systems. A study on women’s cellphone access and ownership in rural Uganda highlights both opportunity and caution [2]. Many women had access to mobile phones and were willing to receive healthcare information by Short Messaging Service (SMS), suggesting real potential for mobile health approaches to support self-care and prevention. Yet access was not evenly distributed: only 82.4% of the participants had daily cellphone access, and the women without cellphones were often those already facing greater barriers to healthcare. Digital tools can improve access, but only if designed with inclusion in mind.

Infrastructure access, however, is only the first step. Another article we published highlighted digital health literacy as a structural determinant of health [3]. In the era of AI, automation, and digital platforms, literacy is no longer only about being able to search for health information online. It also means being able to understand, question, and engage with digital systems [4], including systems that increasingly shape health advice, clinical decisions, and access to services. This broader understanding shifts the onus away from individuals. If people struggle to use digital health tools, the problem may not be a lack of personal motivation or skill, but instead may reflect systems that are poorly designed, inaccessible, or insufficiently trusted by the communities they are intended to serve.

This broader view of capability connects directly to the challenge of AI in global health. By applying Paul Farmer’s “Staff, Stuff, Space, Systems, and Support” (5 S) framework, Dr. Liam McCoy and colleagues argue that AI cannot substitute for strong health systems [5]. Algorithms cannot deliver equitable care where there are shortages of health workers, medicines, infrastructure, governance, or social support. This is an important caution against technology-first thinking. AI may help identify risks, support diagnosis, or improve decision-making, but these benefits depend on the systems around it. Patients still need trained professionals, reliable referral pathways, safe facilities, essential medicines, data governance, and trust in care. Without these foundations, advanced technologies may divert attention and resources from the basic investments required to improve health outcomes.

Taken together, these papers point to a clear message: equitable digital health systems begin before technology is deployed. They begin with asking who has access, who is excluded, who can meaningfully use digital tools, and whether health systems are prepared to respond to the needs that technologies can help identify.

Technology is not a silver bullet for today’s global health challenges. Instead of asking only whether technology is ready, we should also ask whether people, institutions, and health systems are ready. Digital health preparedness begins with access, literacy, trust, acceptance of technology, relevance to care, and a workforce equipped with the skills to use digital tools well [6]. What is also needed are governance structures that ensure innovation is aligned with public health priorities rather than technology for its own sake. The goal is not simply more digital health, but digital innovation that strengthens health systems, supports meaningful participation, and improves outcomes for those most at risk of being left behind. If designed and implemented equitably, digital health systems can do more than deliver information or automate care. They can support inclusion, strengthen social connections [7], and help create health systems that serve everyone more fairly.
