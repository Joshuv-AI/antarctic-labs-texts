The health consequences of antisemitism (Does antisemitism get under the skin?): A systematic review protocol
Authors: Talia Morstead, Colleen Pawliuk, Enrica Alati, Mor Cohen-Eilig, Anita DeLongis, Tim Oberlander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposure to prejudice, discrimination, and bigotry has long been associated with adverse psychological and physiological health. Such effects are not confined to targeted individuals and can have widespread implications for the health and well-being of entire communities. Antisemitism is a form of prejudice that has the potential to function as an adverse environmental exposure capable of inflicting both direct and indirect harm. Despite its potential as a public health threat, antisemitism remains markedly understudied. The global surge in antisemitism in the wake of October 7th, 2023, reinforces the need for examination of the potential health effects. This systematic review aims to synthesize studies exploring the association between exposure to antisemitism and indicators of psychological and physiological health. By operationalizing antisemitism as a measurable environmental exposure, this systematic review applies etiology-focused methods to synthesize heterogeneous health outcomes across study designs to examine whether there is evidence that exposure to antisemitism is associated with adverse health outcomes. We will search Embase, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, Google Scholar and grey literature sources. Our narrative synthesis will situate findings within the public health literature and identify gaps that must be addressed to inform future research, intervention, and policy.

Background

The psychological and physiological toll of racism and bigotry on individuals and communities is well-established. Decades of research have demonstrated that both overt acts of hatred and more covert, ambiguous, or systemic forms of prejudice are associated with a range of adverse health outcomes—including heightened anxiety, depression, and physiological dysregulation [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. These impacts extend beyond those directly targeted, affecting individuals who witness or learn about the experiences of others in their community [[5], [6], [7]]. Evidence of intergenerational transmission further underscores the cumulative and enduring nature of this harm [[8], [9], [10]]. In response, researchers and policymakers have increasingly framed racism as a critical public health concern [11,12], and some have invoked infectious disease modelling to understand its spread and impact—reinforcing the view of racism and bigotry as noxious social exposures that demand population-level intervention [13].

Much like racism, antisemitism can function as an adverse environmental exposure capable of inflicting both direct and indirect harm [8,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Despite being described as “the oldest hatred” and persisting for millennia as Jews have remained a minority group across the diaspora [18], the health consequences of antisemitism remain markedly underexamined [17,19,20]. The global surge in antisemitism following the October 7th, 2023, terrorist attacks in Israel and the ensuing war has spurred research that is increasingly revealing evidence of the psychosocial and physiological consequences of exposure to antisemitism in all its forms [[14], [15], [16],21,22]. The adverse health impacts combined with the scale and speed of the resurgence underscore the need to move beyond traditional understandings of antisemitism as a problem confined to individual attitudes or behaviors—a view historically emphasized by some scholars in the field [23,24]. Instead, it necessitates a population-level perspective that accounts for how antisemitism circulates, replicates, and inflicts harm within and across communities over time.

Framing antisemitism through a public health lens allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of hate —not only as a direct stressor for Jews, but also as a broader societal hazard with cascading effects on community resilience, social cohesion, and public well-being. Defining antisemitism (or hate more broadly defined) as a public health threat may enable the identification of modifiable factors that might reduce its health impact. In this paper, we plan to synthesize the emerging literature reporting the health consequences of antisemitism, situate these findings within the public health literature, and identify critical gaps that must be addressed to inform future research, intervention, and policy.

While prior reviews have examined the direct and inter-generational health effects of the Holocaust [[8], [25], [26], [27]], or have taken broader approaches to trauma, genocide, or racial discrimination [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]], to our knowledge this is the first systematic review to specifically focus on antisemitism as a distinct exposure and apply public health methods to examine its health effects. Specifically, we operationalize antisemitism as a distinct exposure reflecting its unique historical and sociopolitical dimensions. To accommodate the heterogeneity of available evidence, we employ narrative synthesis methods that can integrate diverse study designs and outcome measures—an approach that meta-analytic synthesis would exclude. Crucially, our examination of health effects among targets, bystanders, and perpetrators reflects the systemic, community-level nature of antisemitism rather than framing it solely as individual-level harm, thereby capturing potential broader impacts.

Description of protocol

Review question

Is there evidence that exposure to antisemitism is associated with adverse health outcomes?

Eligibility criteria

Participants

To examine antisemitism as a population-level exposure rather than solely an individual victimization event, we will include any study of individuals or groups exposed to antisemitism—whether as victims, perpetrators, or bystanders—regardless of demographic characteristics and study context. This is consistent with environmental exposure models in public health and is consistent with the finding that exposure to discrimination and hate can have adverse effects that extend beyond members of the targeted group (e.g., 5,33). This inclusive approach will allow us to capture the full scope of antisemitism’s effects as a potential public health threat.

Exposure

Studies will be eligible if they explicitly define their exposure as antisemitism (or a near synonym such as “Jew hatred”). This approach avoids imposing a prescribed and invariably contested definition of the term [34], and guards against retroactively classifying or declassifying exposures as antisemitic outside the sociocultural and historical context of the original study (misclassification). We will include studies that examine antisemitism after the holocaust, as the association between the holocaust and health outcomes has already been examined through multiple reviews.

Outcomes

To be included in the review, studies must report at least one outcome related to physical and/or psychological health. Eligible outcomes may include a broad range of indicators, from self-reported emotional states (e.g., distress) to clinically assessed or physician-confirmed diagnoses.

Types of studies

We will include both published and unpublished studies. Any empirical work will be eligible, regardless of methodological approach; this includes qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research. Eligible studies may be observational or experimental and may report either longitudinal or cross-sectional findings.

Methods

This review will be conducted in accordance with the Johanna Briggs Institute methodology for systematic reviews of etiology and risk [35]. This protocol was developed using the PRISMA-P guidelines [36].

Search strategy

We used the PEO (participants, exposure and outcomes) framework to develop our research question and eligibility for this review. Before beginning this review, we searched PROSPERO, Open Science Framework, MEDLINE (Ovid) and Google Scholar for other systematic reviews on the topic. We did not locate a similar review on this topic.

The search strategy will aim to locate both published and unpublished studies. An initial limited search of Embase (Ovid), Web of Science Core Collection and Google Scholar was conducted to identify articles on the topic. Additionally, citation searching was conducted to identify other relevant articles. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy for Embase (Ovid; See Supplementary File 2). Additionally, lists of antisemitic events were consulted to capture studies that include events that may not be classified or focused on as antisemitic by the study authors in searchable fields (e.g. Pittsburg synagogue shooting). For each listed event, the specific names were added into the search if it was not already covered by the more general terms included in the search. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for each included information source. For databases with a health focus, the search will include only a concept of “antisemitism,” while databases without a health focus will include a concept for “health” (see Supplementary File 2 for an example search in Web of Science Core Collection). We will conduct backward and forward citation searching of all included studies using Web of Science Core Collection.

We will search Embase (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Web of Science Core Collection and Google Scholar (Publish or Perish; the first 200 results will be retrieved). Sources of unpublished studies and grey literature to be searched include Papers First (WorldCat First Search), Proceedings (WorldCat First Search), ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Citation Index (Web of Science), Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD), Open Science Framework Preprints (https://osf.io/preprints), Preprint Citation Index (Web of Science) and Google. We will not limit our search by language or by publication date.

Study selection

After the search, all identified citations will be collected and uploaded into Covidence and duplicates will be removed. After a pilot test of 100 references, titles and abstracts will be screened by 2 independent team members. The full text of potentially relevant studies will then be retrieved and assessed against the eligibility criteria by 2 independent team members. Reasons for exclusion of full-text studies will be recorded and reported in the systematic reviews. Disagreements at any stage of study selection will be resolved through consensus, or if consensus cannot be reached, through involvement of a third team member. The results of the search and study selection and inclusion process will be reported in full in the final systematic review and presented in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram.

Critical appraisal

Eligible studies will be critically appraised by 2 independent reviewers at the study level for methodological quality in the review using standardized critical appraisal instruments from JBI for experimental, quasi-experimental, and observational studies. A pilot test of 10 studies will be conducted to ensure consistency before appraisal. If a study authored by one or more of the review authors is eligible for this review, the author(s) will abstain from study selection and critical appraisal for that particular study. Authors of papers will be contacted to request missing or additional data for clarification, where required. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer. The results of critical appraisal will be reported in a table with an accompanying narrative synthesis. All studies, regardless of the results of their methodological quality, will undergo data extraction and synthesis (where possible). Although studies will not be excluded on the basis of quality, critical appraisal results will be used to inform the interpretation of findings and conclusions will be interpreted in light of this assessment. Studies assessed as lower quality will be noted as such, and the rationale for this assessment will be explicated. Where findings are drawn predominantly from lower-quality studies, this will be highlighted and conclusions will be qualified accordingly.

Data extraction

After a pilot test of 5 studies, two independent reviewers will extract data in Covidence using a tool developed by the team (S3 File). Disagreements that arise during data extraction will be resolved through consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be involved. The data to be extracted include: study information, study location, details of exposure, findings.

Data synthesis

We will narratively synthesize the data and present a summary of results in one or more tables. We will not synthesize the data quantitatively in a meta-analysis due to the anticipated heterogeneity of eligible studies. Narrative synthesis will be organized by outcome, grouping findings according to health outcome examined, and further organized by study characteristics. Where studies report conflicting findings, we will examine whether discrepancies can be explained by differences in study population, setting, methodology, or methodological quality. We will report and discuss these conflicts, including as they relate to existing uncertainty in the evidence base. Thematic patterns emerging across studies will also be explored in the discussion.

Assessing certainty in the findings

We will not assess certainty in findings, as the Grading of Recommendations Development, Assessment and Evaluation (GRADE) Working Group does not currently have guidance for systematic reviews of etiology and recommends against modifying the GRADE tool [37].

Novelty of this study

This systematic review will address a critical gap in the public health literature by examining antisemitism as a distinct form of prejudice with measurable impacts on psychological and physical health. While existing research has documented the health consequences of various forms of discrimination, antisemitism has received limited attention despite its pervasive nature and diverse manifestations. Further, by framing antisemitism through a public health lens and examining impacts beyond Jewish communities alone, this review will illuminate how prejudice against one group generates cascading effects on population health more broadly.

Beyond its substantive findings, this review advances methodological approaches for studying discrimination as a health exposure. Our framework of operationalizing prejudice as a measurable environmental exposure and synthesizing heterogeneous health outcomes across diverse study designs provides a transferable template for examining other forms of hate and discrimination. Additionally, by identifying patterns in how antisemitism exposure is measured and linked to health outcomes, this review may inform the development of public health systems that frame discrimination as a population health threat, with implications for surveillance and monitoring approaches that respect individual privacy and autonomy while protecting communities from harm.

Protocol validation

Not applicable.

Limitations

Our inclusion criteria encompass a broad range of methodologies and as such, may limit the comparability of studies due to their heterogeneity. Our decision to limit our review to studies wherein the study authors explicitly identify the event(s) as antisemitic may exclude studies that examine community impacts of relevant incidents without defining them as such.
