Noninvasive Assessment of Right Ventricular‐Pulmonary Arterial Coupling in Repaired Tetralogy of Fallot by Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Authors: Gilles Mets, Yuval Bitterman, Laura Mercer‐Rosa, Rachel Wald, Mark K. Friedberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight ventricular (RV) to pulmonary arterial coupling (RVPAc) quantifies RV contractility in relation to its afterload but traditionally requires high‐fidelity catheter derived pressure‐volume loops. We sought to evaluate a noninvasive volume‐based RVPAc parameter in children and adults with repaired tetralogy of Fallot in relation to pulmonary regurgitation (PR), RV outflow tract obstruction (RVOTO), and exercise capacity.MethodsWe retrospectively studied 92 pediatric and 105 adult patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot who had cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging and cardiopulmonary exercise testing within a 1‐year interval. RVPAc was calculated as the ratio of RV end‐systolic volume over stroke volume. RVOTO gradient was obtained by echocardiography; RV ejection fraction, and PR fraction by cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging. Exercise capacity was measured as the percentage of predicted peak oxygen consumption during cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Subgroups were established depending on the combination of PR‐RVOTO (cutoff defined as PR >30% and RVOTO >25 mm Hg).ResultsRVPAc was significantly higher in adult versus pediatric patients (1.23 [1.03–1.48] versus 1.00 [0.88–1.15]; P<0.001). RVPAc was comparable in the 4 pediatric hemodynamic subgroups (P=0.38) but tended to be higher in adults with either RVOTO, PR, or RVOTO+PR (P=0.05). RVPAc highly correlated with RVEF (r=−0.991, P<0.001) but was not associated with percentage of predicted peak oxygen consumption.ConclusionsWorse RVPAc in adult versus pediatric patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot may signify progressive RV‐PA uncoupling with age‐ or era‐related effects. However, the clinical use of volumetric RVPAc in repaired tetralogy of Fallot appears limited, as it does not provide additional information over RVEF and is not associated with exercise capacity.

Ventriculoarterial coupling captures the relationship of ventricular to arterial elastance, thereby characterizing matching of ventricular contractility to its afterload. 1 , 2 Right ventricular (RV) to pulmonary artery coupling (RVPAc) is associated with outcomes in pulmonary hypertension and left heart failure, highlighting the importance of efficient energy transfer from the RV to the pulmonary vasculature. 3 Although valuable, this invasive technique is limited in clinical practice. Noninvasive assessment of RVPAc by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) has been shown to correlate well with invasive measurements, disease severity, and outcomes in pulmonary hypertension and left heart failure and may therefore be useful in clinical practice. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Following surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF), residual pulmonary regurgitation (PR) and RV outflow tract obstruction (RVOTO) are long‐term residual lesions in addition to intrinsically abnormal pulmonary vasculature with increased stiffness and often small branches. 8 , 9 Longer term development of RV dysfunction and reduced exercise capacity is common. 10 , 11 Hence, assessment of RVPAc is relevant to assess in rTOF to quantify RV efficiency in relation to the RVOT and pulmonary vascular properties. RVPAc was previously found to be impaired in rTOF patients with chronic RV volume overload. 12 However, noninvasive RVPAc in relation to clinical outcomes has not been well studied in rTOF and previous studies have yielded discrepant findings. 13 , 14 , 15 Consequently, we sought to assess RVPAc using CMR in a contemporary cohort of children and adults with rTOF and to investigate its association with both abnormal loading conditions and with exercise capacity. We hypothesized that in rTOF, RVPAc is associated with the hemodynamic load and correlates better with exercise capacity than RVEF.

METHODS

Study Population

Two cohorts with rTOF (1 pediatric, 1 adult) and 1 pediatric control cohort were retrospectively evaluated in this cross‐sectional study. Data were obtained from retrospective chart review for the pediatric cohort and from a review of data from a prospectively collected study for adult rTOF. The pediatric cohort consisted of patients with rTOF born after 2000, who underwent TOF repair at the Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto before their second birthday and underwent CMR and cardiopulmonary exercise testing at ages 8 to 18 years. Adult patients with rTOF were recruited at the Toronto General Hospital as part of the prospectively enrolled CORRELATE (Comprehensive Outcomes Registry Late After Tetralogy of Fallot Repair) study. 16 In both cohorts, patients were excluded if echocardiography, CMR, and metabolic exercise testing were not available within a 1‐year interval or if patients underwent cardiac intervention between these tests. Patients who received an RV‐PA conduit at initial repair or had additional cardiac lesions (except for a small atrial communication) or genetic syndrome (except 22q11 deletion) were also excluded to minimize potential confounders. Likewise, patients with more than mild tricuspid regurgitation were excluded, as the CMR calculated stroke volume (SV) may be inaccurate. Data were obtained before pulmonary valve replacement in patients who underwent this procedure. If repeated tests were available, the latest eligible CMR and exercise test were selected. CMR data from children aged 8 to 18 years undergoing screening for family history of cardiomyopathy with normal results served as a control. No additional clinical data were analyzed for controls. Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional research ethics boards at the Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, with waiver of informed consent. Adult patients consented for prospective data collection at time of enrolment in the CORRELATE study at the Toronto General Hospital. The data that support the findings of this study are available upon reasonable request.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed using a standardized clinical imaging protocol with a Vivid 7, 9 or E95 ultrasound platform (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI) or Philips IE33 (Philips Medical System, Andover, MA). We used qualitative grading of tricuspid regurgitation and measured Doppler‐velocity derived RVOT gradient (mm Hg). RV systolic pressure was estimated from tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity with addition of estimated right atrial pressure (5 mm Hg in pediatric cohort as per our laboratory’s clinical routine and according to inferior caval vein appearance in the adult cohort).

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance

The CMR clinical protocol included short‐axis cine imaging to quantify ventricular volumes indexed to body surface area; ejection fraction (EF), and phase‐contrast flow velocity mapping of the main pulmonary artery to estimate PR volume and fraction. CMR imaging was performed with a 1.5‐T scanner (Avanto; Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany). Control data were obtained from studies performed on the same scanner and protocol. We used Sanz’s method to assess RV‐PA coupling as the ratio of CMR‐derived RV end‐systolic volume (ESV) index to RV indexed SV. 4 SV was calculated as RV end‐diastolic volume (EDV) − RV ESV. In accordance to invasively measured arterial elastance/ventricular elastance, higher values (especially >1) would then indicate less favorable coupling or uncoupling. To account for the hemodynamic inefficiency induced by PR, we additionally calculated an indexed effective SV, to account for the effective antegrade flow by subtracting the PR volume from the measured SV and then calculated effective RVPAc (eRVPAc) as [RV ESV index/effective SV index]. To relate RVPAc to PA size, the Nakata index was calculated as the sum of the proximal right pulmonary area and left pulmonary artery area divided by body surface area. 17

Metabolic Exercise Testing

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing was performed on a bicycle ergometer with a ramp protocol. Peak oxygen consumption was recorded and presented as a percentage of predicted peak oxygen consumption (%VO2) for age, sex, and weight. 18 , 19

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with commercially available software (SPSS V.29, Armonk, NY). Normality of data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean values with SD (mean±SD); nonnormally distributed data as median values with interquartile ranges (median [Q1–Q3]). Comparison of 2 independent groups was performed by Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Fisher’s exact test; comparison of >2 groups was performed by ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis, based on data distribution. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to measure the strength of correlations. Statistical significance was determined at P<0.05.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to identify independent predictors of RVPAc and eRVPAc (dependent variables). Independent variables were selected based on their known association with RV function from prior literature or based on hypothesis in the specific context of pressure and volume load and included QRS duration, left ventricular EF (LVEF), RVOT gradient, PR fraction, and Nakata index. To evaluate the relationship of RVPAc, eRVPAc, and RVEF with exercise performance, separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed with %VO2max as the dependent variable and adjusted for age and body surface area. Predictive capacity was compared using adjusted R2 values.

We further analyzed 4 subgroups of patients based on loading conditions. Using cutoffs corresponding to mild RVOTO >25 mm Hg 20 and moderate PR >30% 21 within range of previously published cutoff values, 12 , 13 , 15 , 22 4 loading groups were established: (1) minimal loading (no RVOTO—no PR), (2) pressure loading (RVOTO—no PR), (3) volume loading (no RVOTO—PR), and (4) combined loading (RVOTO—PR).

DISCUSSION

The RVOT and pulmonary vasculature are intrinsically abnormal in TOF and development of RV dysfunction is common in these patients, affecting their exercise capacity and outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 We evaluated a CMR‐derived volume‐based RVPAc index in a contemporary cohort of children and adults with rTOF to investigate the association of PR and RVOTO with RVPAc and assess its relation to exercise capacity. We found that RVPAc was worse in adults compared with pediatric patients with rTOF, suggesting a greater degree of RV‐PA uncoupling. However, there was no clear correlation between RVPAc and severity of RVOTO or PR. Moreover, RVPAc closely correlated inversely with RVEF and did not predict exercise capacity better than RVEF.

The median RVPAc ratio was >1 in the adult patients indicating more RV‐PA uncoupling versus children who had, on average, a matched RVPAc ratio of 1. For invasive measurements, an arterial elastance/ventricular elastance (RVPAc) ratio of 0.5 indicates efficient flow generation with minimal energy expenditure, whereas an optimal RVPAc ratio of 1 reflects matched ventricular and arterial elastance, maximizing energy transfer and stroke work. 4 , 12 As CMR appears to overestimate arterial elastance/ventricular elastance by right heart catheterization data, it remains unclear what value can be used as threshold for defining uncoupling in the volumetric method. 4

Higher RVPAc in patient cohorts versus controls aligns with previous research where patients with rTOF with significant PR had impaired RVPAc. 13 , 15 , 23 , 24 Impaired RVPAc in rTOF may result from the interplay of abnormal pulmonary vasculature, residual RVOTO, and inefficiency in effective antegrade pulmonary flow because of PR. Higher RVPAc in adult versus pediatric patients with rTOF could signify progressive RV‐PA uncoupling with age from longer exposure to abnormal loading conditions. This is supported by the moderate positive bivariate correlation between age and RVPAc. However, as the correlation is moderate other factors likely also have an impact. An era effect with a higher proportion of palliation and older age at repair as well as longer QRS signifying more electrical dyssynchrony in the adult cohort may also be important.

There was a trend for adult patients with either type of abnormal loading (RVOTO, PR, or combined) to have a higher RVPAc than those without. This subgroup analysis did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously; but could quantify that chronic residual RV pressure and volume loading negatively affect RV performance over time. This is supported by the finding that adult patients without RVOTO or PR had similar RVPAc values to pediatric patients that were close to 1, whereas adult patients with any type of abnormal loading had higher RVPAc values. Nonetheless, there was no linear association between RVPAc and severity of loading parameters such as RVOTO or PR in either pediatric or adult cohorts. In contrast to the RV hemodynamic lesions, we did note a significant association of RVPAc with LVEF and QRS duration. This result aligns with our previous work, where RV function measured by RVEF was as, or more closely, inversely associated with QRS duration than with PR, consistent with the importance of electromechanical dyssynchrony to RV function. 25 , 26

To mitigate the effect of PR volume on a volumetric coupling ratio, we calculated an effective RVPAc that includes only the effective antegrade SV. The related eRVPAc had an overall higher value than RVPAc and was also higher in patients with a PR fraction >30%, irrespective of RVOTO severity. In contrast to the conventional RVPAc, there was also a moderate linear correlation of eRVPAc with PR. This may signify the effect of PR on RV‐PA coupling that is not reflected by the standard (SV/ESV), which was developed in pulmonary hypertension where pulmonary insufficiency is usually less frequent and less severe. 4 Given that PR is common in rTOF, the effective RVPAc may better reflect RV inefficient work. 27 However, neither RVPAc nor effective RVPAc correlated with exercise capacity in either the pediatric or adult cohorts or in the hemodynamic subgroups. These findings align with a previous study of >200 patients with rTOF where CMR‐derived RVPAc did not correlate with exercise capacity. 14 Moreover, as RVEF and RVPAc are highly correlated; and our current results, and those of other studies, show that RVEF correlates relatively poorly with exercise capacity in patients with rTOF, it is perhaps not surprising that RVPAc did not confer additional benefit to RVEF in terms of predicting exercise capacity. 28 , 29 , 30 Likewise, using effective RVPAc did not significantly improve correlation with exercise capacity and did not offer added benefit. These results contrast those of Egbe et al. where echocardiography‐derived RV‐PA coupling measures in patients with chronic PR were moderately associated with exercise capacity and outperformed conventional measures of RV function alone. 13 As echocardiography derived RVPAc retains RV pressure estimation in the denominator, it may represent a different measure of RV performance or ventriculoarterial coupling than the volume‐based RVPAc.

As can be expected, RVPAc correlates very closely with RVEF in the whole group and in all subgroups (Figure 4), resulting from the relation of ESV/SV (ventriculoarterial coupling) to SV/EDV (EF). Others previously reported moderate to strong correlations between noninvasive RVPAc and RVEF, with correlation coefficients ranging from −0.57 to −0.78. 14 , 31 , 32 The differences in correlation between studies may stem, in part, from the different methods used to calculate SV: by 3‐dimensional echocardiography, 31 phase contrast in the main PA, 14 or ventricular volume analysis. 32 The relation between RVPAc and RVEF was further examined by plotting the measured RVPAc to a calculated RVPAc defined as [1/RVEF − 1], 33 , 34 showing near perfect correlation (Figure 5). This further underscores the intrinsic relationship between RVPAc and RVEF. 35 To our knowledge, this has not yet been shown for the RV and applied to both controls and subjects with rTOF, with or without residual pressure and volume loading. Thus, it seems that volumetric RVPAc by CMR is likely to be affected by volume loading similar to RVEF 36 rather than truly reflecting the ratio of ventricular to arterial elastance independent of preload. Although the application of volumetric RVPAc may be different in conditions without PR or RVOTO such as pulmonary hypertension and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, in rTOF it does not appear to add clinical value beyond RVEF.

Limitations

We analyzed data from 2 different patient populations, requiring caution when interpreting comparisons between these cohorts. Serial evaluation may be valuable to understand the association of RVPAc in relation to clinical status and exercise capacity over time, as well as the occurrence of life‐threatening arrhythmias and cardiac death. Invasive hemodynamic data were not available for gold standard comparison. In the pediatric cohort, CMR was analyzed according to clinical institutional protocols whereas in the adult cohort there is a central reader according to the CORRELATE protocol, which may vary leading to different volumes. Exercise capacity was represented solely as %VO2, which does not encompass all aspects of exercise capacity and we did not assess maximal effort.

Conclusions

CMR‐derived RVPAc is mildly elevated in patients with rTOF and higher in adult versus pediatric patients potentially signifying progressive uncoupling over time. Although RVPAc is highly associated with RVEF and to a lesser degree with RV volumes, LVEF, and QRS duration; it is not, however, associated with RVOTO, PR fraction, or exercise capacity. This shows that measuring volume‐based RVPAc does not provide improved quantification of RV efficiency compared with RVEF in rTOF.
