Multiple-microarray analysis for identification of key genes involved in diabetic nephropathy
Authors: Hui Bi, Liang Ma, Xu Zhong, Gang Long
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The purpose of our study was to discover genes with significantly aberrant expression in diabetic nephropathy (DN) and to determine their potential mechanism. We acquired renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples data from DN patients and controls from the GEO database. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples between DN patients and controls were studied. Based on these DEGs, we carried out the functional annotation and constructed protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. By comparing DN patients and controls of DEGs, we acquired the shared DGEs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls. DN patients compared to controls, we obtained 3000 DEGs, 3064 DEGs, and 2296 DEGs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples, respectively. The PPI networks of top 40 DEGs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples was consisted of 229 nodes and 229 edges, 540 nodes and 606 edges, and 132 nodes and 124 edges, respectively. In total, 21 shared genes were finally found, including CASP3, DHCR24, CXCL1, GYPC, INHBA, LTF, MT1G, MUC1, NINJ1, PFKFB3, PPP1R3C, CCL5, SRSF7, PHLDA2, RBM39, WTAP, BASP1, PLK2, PDK2, PNPLA4, and SNED1. These genes may be associated with the DN process. Our study provides a basis to explore the potential mechanism and identify novel therapeutic targets for DN.

1. Introduction

Diabetic Nephropathy (DN) is one of the most common microvascular diseases caused by diabetes, and also one of the most typical and serious complications of diabetes.[1] DN has become the most common cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD).[2] Epidemiological survey predicts that diabetic nephropathy will become the seventh leading cause of death in the world by 2030.[3] The typical pathological changes of DN include expansion of glomerular mesangium, glomerular basement membrane thickness, and podocytes loss. Currently, drugs that control blood sugar levels, lower blood pressure or inhibit the renin-angiotensin system can delay the progression of diabetic nephropathy, but there is no treatment available to repair kidney damage.[4,5] Despite advances in the etiology and treatment of DN, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms of DN are still not fully unknown. Therefore, it is of great significance value to further study the pathogenesis of DN and find novel therapeutic targets and strategies for DN.

It has been found that the progression of DN is closely related to changes in genetic factors.[6] With the development of gene expression profiles, bioinformatics have become most common used strategies to screen key biomarkers in a variety of diseases including DN.[1,7,8] TGFβ1 is reported to be differentially expressed in DN and may participate in the development of DN.[9] Xu et al find hub genes associated with DN using Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, including C1QA, C1QB, C3, CFB, ITGB2, VSIG4, and CLU.[10] Li et al find 14 DEGs (CTGF, ALB, PDPN, FLT1, IGF1, WT1, GJA1, IGFBP2, FGF9, BMP2, FGF1, BMP7, VEGFA, and TGFBR3) play important roles in the progression of DN.[11] Tang et al acquire glomerulus and renal tubule tissue gene expression data, and discover that NTNG1 and HGF are potential DN biomarkers.[12] Yao et al analyze glomerulus tissue gene expression data, and identify 13 hub genes including C3, FN1, COL1A2, LUM, THBS2, CD44, LYZ, FOS, EGR1, ALB, PLG, EGF, and DUSP1.[1] However, the common functional genes involved in DN pathology in renal tubules, glomeruli and blood samples need further identification.

In this study, we retrieved the gene expression data from the GEO database and acquired renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples data from DN patients and controls. Through comparing DN patients and controls of DEGs, we acquired and processed the shared DGEs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls. Hopefully, this study is expected to provide a deeper insight into for the uncovering pathogenesis of DN and finding potential therapeutic targets of DN.

2. Materials & methods

2.1. Raw data downloaded

The gene expression data of patients with diabetic nephropathies (DN) were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The datasets were further chose based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria: The data types were expression profiling by array. The data were obtained from renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls. Subsequently, a total of 11 datasets incorporated our study, including 5 datasets of renal tubules samples (GSE30122, GSE104954, GSE104954, GSE47184 and GSE99340), 5 datasets of glomerulus samples (GSE1009, GSE104948, GSE104948, GSE30122 and GSE96804) and 1 dataset of blood samples (GSE142153). Based on all the above datasets, renal tubules samples of 56 DN and 53 healthy control and glomerulus samples 65 DN and 70 samples, and blood samples of 23 DN and 10 healthy control were included in our study (see Table 1 for details).

2.2. Identification of differentially expressed genes (DGEs)

The different datasets of renal tubule and glomerulus were combined using MetaMA package in R language. False discovery rate (FDR) was applied to regulate the P values. The criteria for DEGs in renal tubule and glomerulus were FDR < 0.01. In blood samples, T test was applied to screen the DEGs, and the selection criteria were P < .01. In addition, heat map of these DEGs was produced with pheatmap package in R language (https://cran.r-project.org/package=pheatmap).

2.3. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

A PPI network of DEGs was constructed based on the Biological General Repository for Interactionh Datasets (https://thebiogrid.org). The network was visualized using Cytoscape3.3.0. Edges represent the number of protein pairs in the PPI network, and note represents the number of protein pairs in the PPI network. Ellipses were used to represent nodes and lines were used to represent edges.

2.4. Functional annotation analysis of DGEs

To better uncover the potential biological function of DEGs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were executed using clusterProfiler package in R language. The selection criteria for significant enrichment was FDR < 0.05.

2.5. Shared DGEs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls

Venny 2.1.0 (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) was utilized to acquire the shared DGEs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls. Subsequently, these shared DGEs were applied to establish the PPI network.

2.6. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

We used the ROC curve to determine the accuracy of the real hub genes in distinguishing DN from healthy control. The “pROC” package was utilized to produce ROC curve, and the area under the ROC curve represents the diagnostic value of shared DGEs.

3. Results

For DEGs in blood samples, we selected out 2296 DEGs between DN patients and controls, among which, 918 genes was upregulated and 1378 genes was downregulated. 4. Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is the third largest global epidemic disease in the world after cancer and cardiovascular diseases, and it poses a serious threat to human health.[13] The main clinical features of DN include glomerular injury, glomerular hypertrophy, and thickening of glomerular basement membrane.[14] At present, the treatment strategies of DN mainly include drugs to control blood glucose, blood pressure and urinary protein, but the therapeutic effect is still not significant. Nevertheless, due to the complexity of etiology, we have not fully understood the pathogenesis of DN. Therefore, it is urgently needed to clarify the pathogenesis of DN and find therapeutic targets for DN.

In our study, we retrieved gene expression data from the GEO database and acquired renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples data from DN patients and controls. We utilized bioinformatics methods to obtain DEGs in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples data from DN patients and controls, and tried to uncover the potential pathogenesis of the DN. A total of 3000 DEGs were identified in renal tubules samples between DN and normal samples, including 1446 upregulated and 1544 downregulated genes. A total of 3064 DEGs were found between glomerulus samples between DN patients and controls, of which, 1614 genes was upregulated and 1450 genes was downregulated. A total of 2296 DEGs were identified in blood sample between DN patients and controls, among which, 918 genes was upregulated and 1378 genes was downregulated. In total, 21 shared genes were finally found, including CASP3, DHCR24, CXCL1, GYPC, INHBA, LTF, MT1G, MUC1, NINJ1, PFKFB3, PPP1R3C, CCL5, SRSF7, PHLDA2, RBM39, WTAP, BASP1, PLK2, PDK2, PNPLA4, and SNED1.

Among these 21 shared genes, some have been found to play an important role in the progression of DN, including CASP3, DHCR24, CXCL1, MUC1, FKFB3, CCL5, WTAP, and BASP1. Caspase 3 (CASP3) is a cysteine-aspartic acid protease that plays a central role in the execution-phase of cell apoptosis. In streptozotocin-induced DN rats, apigenin improves renal damage due to DN via regulating CASP3.[15] Silibinin suppresses progression of DN via activating the AKT signaling pathway and decreasing cleaved caspase-3.[16] In Obese diabetic ZSF1 rats, the renal protection of whole grape powder diet is associated with up-regulation of DHCR24 in the kidneys.[17] In a diabetic animal model, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) has been identified as a possible pro-inflammatory modulator.[18] CXCL1 is reported to be upregulate in tubule samples of DN and may be involved in DN progression.[19] The expression of CXCL1 is increased in podocytes of OVE, db/db and STZ diabetic mouse models.[20] Mucin 1 (MUC1) is a heterodimeric oncoprotein that is aberrant expressed in many diseases. MUC1 accelerates mesangial cells proliferation and kidney fibrosis in DN by regulating STAT and β-catenin signal pathway, providing a new therapeutic target for DN.[21] Classification based on 5 urinary proteins including MUC1 may better predict the prognosis of DN patients than urinary albumin concentration or estimated glomerular filtration rate.[22]

The protein 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) has been associated with diabetes.[23] Silencing of PFKFB3 protects MPC5 mouse podocyte cells from high glucose-induced injury through inducing autophagy, and PFKFB3 is considered as a potential therapeutic target for DN.[24] Chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 5 (CCL5), a member of C–C chemokine family, is produced by various cell types including renal mesangial cells.[25] The level of serum CCL5 is high in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated with hypertriglyceridemia.[26] CCL5 has received considerable attention as key candidate gene for DN. ALPK1 elevates CCL5 expression via regulation of NF-κB activation in hyperglycemic conditions in renal tubular cells.[27] It is reported that Tang-Shen-Ning decoction reduces renal fibrosis and ameliorates kidney injure in DN by regulating expression of CCL5.[28] WT1 associated protein (WTAP) is a transcription factor containing zinc finger, which is involved in nephrogenesis and podocytes differentiation. In mature glomerulus, WTAP is expressed only in podocytes, and the continued expression of WTAP in the adult kidney shows a role in homeostasis of the podocyte.[29] WTAP levels in podocytes have also been suggested in the literature as a valuable alternative technique for studying podocyte damage in DN.[30] Brain acid soluble protein 1 (BASP1) is a member of family of growth-associated proteins and originally isolated from brain extracts. In the adult kidney the main site of BASP1 expression is the podocyte, where it behaves as a transcriptional suppressor of WTAP.[31] BASP1 is upregulated in human DN tubule interstitium and tubule and regulated apoptosis in DN.[31] In this study, CASP3, DHCR24, CXCL1, MUC1, FKFB3, CCL5, WTAP, and BASP1 were upregulated in renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples of DN patients and controls. These genes are involved in the development of DN and play an important role.

Among these 21 shared genes, little is known about the roles GYPC, INHBA, LTF, MT1G, NINJ1, PPP1R3C, SRSF7, PHLDA2, RBM39, PLK2, PDK2, PNPLA4, and SNED1 in the progression of DN. In this study, it was reported for the first time that the expressions levels of GYPC, INHBA, LTF, MT1G, NINJ1, PPP1R3C, SRSF7, PHLDA2, RBM39 and PLK2 were upregulated, while those of PDK2, PNPLA4 and SNED1 were downregulated in the renal tubules, glomerulus and blood samples from DN patients and controls. Therefore, more experiments are necessary to explain the role and mechanism of these genes in DN development, which will be the focus of our next study.
