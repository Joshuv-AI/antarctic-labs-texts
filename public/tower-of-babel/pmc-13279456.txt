Latest in Resuscitation Research: Highlights from the 2025 American Heart Association's Resuscitation Science Symposium

The American Heart Association's 2025 Resuscitation Science Symposium was held in New Orleans, Louisiana, from November 7th‐9th, 2025. This report summarizes the awards, scientific content, and special sessions presented during this academic meeting.

Goal Directed CARDIOPULMONARY RESUSCITATION—What's the Target?

Xavier Jouven, MD, PhD (Université Paris Cité, Paris, France) showed that although changes in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) guidelines increased the estimated chest compression fraction from roughly 59% in 1998 to 68% to 69% under the 2010 to 2020 guidelines, an unintended result was more prolonged untreated ventricular fibrillation (VF) per cycle, increasing from about 11 seconds to 72 seconds. Because most VF recurrences occur within a minute post shock, this fixed 2‐minute cycle can delay needed defibrillation. Continuous rhythm analysis during CPR, using detectors that distinguish ECG rhythms despite compressions, could allow earlier shocks and shorten VF duration to 7 to 17 seconds while also increasing chest compression fraction to ∼75%.

Daniel M. Rolston, MD, MSHPM (Northwell, New York, NY) presented on Doppler ultrasound guided CPR and highlighted its potential as a physiologic monitor in the future. He reviewed his team's prior work revealing Doppler ultrasound was more accurate than manual palpation and EtCO2 for pulse detection, 1 , 2 especially when using peak systolic velocity measurements; and others' work showing it did not prolong interruptions. 3 Additionally, Dr Rolston presented their recently published study identifying intracompression Doppler ultrasound waveforms, which also demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (88.9%) for subsequent pulse detection. 4 He closed with limitations and technologic advancements needed to expand use of Doppler ultrasound in diverse cardiac arrest settings.

Sam Parnia, MD, PhD (NYU Langone Health, New York, NY) detailed the use of electroencephalography and cerebral oximetry to investigate cortical activation and recall of cognitive experiences that occur during resuscitation of in‐hospital cardiac arrest. His multicenter work in AWARE II (Awareness During Resuscitation II) showing intra‐arrest bursts of organized brain activity on electroencephalography and patient‐reported memories of consciousness/awareness challenged the assumption that the brain is entirely inactive in low‐flow cardiac arrest states. 5 These findings aligned with the clinical phenomenon of CPR‐induced consciousness, in which patients display outward signs of awareness, such as eye opening or purposeful movement, despite the absence of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). Together, AWARE II and CPR‐induced consciousness provide preliminary evidence that the brain may not be entirely quiescent during CPR, underscoring the need for further research into the role of cerebral perfusion optimization during resuscitation.

Hal Harry Shaffner Jr, MD (Johns Hopkins Medicine, Baltimore, MD) highlighted how patient‐specific physiology determines where an individual lies along the cardiac–thoracic pump continuum and how this should inform a personalized approach to chest compressions. He reviewed experimental and translational work comparing different physiologic targets for CPR optimization, including end‐tidal CO2–guided versus diastolic blood pressure–guided strategies, 6 and presented data from a neonatal and juvenile piglet model demonstrating that individualized, EtCO2‐optimized compressions outperformed fixed‐depth standard CPR. 7 He also described emerging methods for dynamically adjusting hand position based on real‐time EtCO2 responses to improve forward blood flow, underscoring the broader concept that chest compressions should be physiologically titrated rather than delivered as a one‐size‐fits‐all intervention.

Late‐Breaking Resuscitation Science Abstracts

This session was moderated by Anne Grossestreuer, PhD (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) and Ari Moskowitz, MD, MPH (Montefiore Einstein).

Robert Sutton, MD, MSCE (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) presented the first pediatric description of airway opening index, a metric to infer airway patency during CPR. Airway opening index was calculated from the oscillations in the end‐tidal waveform associated with each ventilation during chest compressions in a prospective multicenter observational study of 99 pediatric patients with in‐hospital cardiac arrest. A target airway opening index of 30.35 (range 0 [closed] to 1 [open]) was identified, which was associated with improved survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurologic outcome and improved immediate postarrest physiology (lower lactates) despite similar CPR durations. 8

Paul Chan, MD, MS (Saint Luke's Mid America Heart Institute and the University of Missouri‐Kansas City) presented his work on the association between the Resuscitation Quality Improvement program for CPR training and cardiac arrest survival in hospitals. Using the Get With the Guidelines—Resuscitation registry they matched hospitals that implemented Resuscitation Quality Improvement, which uses high frequency (every 3 month) rather than every 2‐year CPR training, with those that did not. They then conducted a difference‐in‐difference analysis to compare the 2‐year postintervention versus 2‐year preintervention in‐hospital risk adjusted cardiac arrest survival and ROSC rates at Resuscitation Quality Improvement versus control hospitals. There was no difference in survival or ROSC in the hospitals implementing Resuscitation Quality Improvement. 9

Sam Parnia, MD, PhD (New York University Langone Hospital, New York, NY) presented his work on goal‐directed CPR using cerebral oximetry as a novel physiological target. Patients were randomized to usual care or cerebral oximetry where those with saturations <60% received physiologic brain directed resuscitation including changing chest compression characteristics (rate, depth, mechanical) or administering higher/additional vasopressors, steroids, or red blood cells. They included 93 patients and found no difference in sustained ROSC between the physiologic CPR group (58.7%) and the control group (68.1%). 10

Lars Wik, MD, PhD (Oslo University Hospital) presented a block‐randomized out‐of‐hospital clinical study evaluating the hemodynamic effects of adding active decompression with a piston device to standard mechanical CPR. Use of the device, which lifts the chest 3 cm above neutral position during decompression, compared with standard mechanical CPR, was associated with higher end tidal and systolic and mean arterial blood pressure at 5, 10, and 30 minutes of CPR and higher cerebral oximetry at 5 and 10 minutes of CPR. 11

American Heart Association/Japanese Circulation Society Joint Session

The American Heart Association and Japanese Circulation Society Joint Session highlighted complementary perspectives from the United States and Japan on cardiac arrest prevention, population outcomes, and pharmacologic strategies, with a particular focus on young patients and registry‐based analyses.

Dianne L. Atkins, MD (University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA) opened the session by emphasizing the importance of primary prevention of sudden cardiac arrest in children and young adults. She highlighted that many arrests in this population are attributable to inherited arrhythmia syndromes or structural heart disease with recognizable ECG abnormalities. Dr Atkins contrasted Japan's long‐standing school‐based ECG screening programs, implemented nationally since the 1990s, with screening practices in the United States, which remain largely limited to preparticipation athletic evaluations. She noted that broader screening efforts would require standardized ECG interpretation, stakeholder engagement, and scalable data infrastructure, with emerging machine learning tools offering potential future solutions.

Tsukasa Yagi, MD, PhD (Nihon University Hospital) presented updated national data from the All‐Japan Utstein Registry, describing long‐term trends in out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest outcomes. He demonstrated steady increases in bystander CPR and public access defibrillation rates over time, accompanied by improvements in survival, particularly among adults. Outcome gains among pediatric and adolescent patients were more modest, especially for nonshockable rhythms. Dr Yagi also highlighted sex‐based differences in bystander response among adolescents and young adults, with lower rates of bystander CPR and defibrillator use for female patients, underscoring the need for targeted public education.

Mitsuaki Nishikimi, MD (Hiroshima University Hospital, Japan) focused on the role of epinephrine in nonshockable out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest, emphasizing heterogeneity within this group, particularly between pulseless electrical activity and asystole. Using a registry‐based analysis with time‐dependent propensity score matching, he demonstrated that earlier epinephrine administration was more strongly associated with return of spontaneous circulation and survival in patients with asystole compared with those with pulseless electrical activity. Neurologic outcome differences were less consistent. These findings supported the concept that rhythm‐specific and time‐sensitive pharmacologic strategies may be important, while highlighting the need for prospective validation.

Catherine Ross, MD (Boston Children's Hospital, Boston, MA) concluded the session by presenting the EpiDose trial, a randomized dose‐effect study evaluating periarrest epinephrine bolus dosing for hypotension in the pediatric intensive care unit. The trial compared 1.0 microgram per kilogram versus 0.5 microgram per kilogram dosing, with the primary outcome defined as short‐term systolic blood pressure response. Enrollment was limited by feasibility and funding constraints, resulting in a small sample size. Although higher‐dose epinephrine showed nonsignificant trends toward greater blood pressure increases and fewer repeat doses, substantial interpatient variability was observed. Dr Ross emphasized the operational challenges of conducting exception‐from‐informed‐consent trials in inpatient pediatric settings and highlighted important lessons for future physiologically targeted resuscitation studies.

Artificial Intelligence‐Powered Life Saving: Revolutionizing Resuscitation Science

This session was moderated by Dana Edelson, MD, MS (University of Chicago, Chicago, IL) and Michael Sayre, MD (University of Washington, Seattle, WA) and discussed new artificial intelligence (AI) approaches that could revolutionize resuscitation science and improve cardiac arrest outcomes.

Natalia Trayanova, PhD (Johns Hopkins University) opened the session with an overview of advanced AI approaches for predicting cardiac arrest in high‐risk populations, focusing on deep learning models that integrate raw cardiac magnetic resonance imaging data with clinical variables. Dr Trayanova emphasized that multimodal deep learning substantially outperformed current guideline‐based approaches for stratifying the risk of lethal arrhythmias. Importantly, deep learning applied to raw magnetic resonance imaging scans could extract latent features not captured in routine clinical image interpretation, offering a path toward more precise and individualized risk assessment.

Jacob Hutton, PhD (University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC) presented the use of AI on wearable device data to improve outcomes through the early detection of unwitnessed out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests (OHCAs). His group trained their algorithms on physiological data from several patient cohorts including those in hospice, undergoing medically assisted death or withdrawal of life support in the intensive care unit, and healthy volunteers. Dr Hutton's work illustrated both the promise and the challenges of wearable‐based cardiac arrest detection: although machine learning approaches show clear potential, achieving high‐trust, clinically deployable systems will require richer data sets, improved modeling strategies, and careful attention to performance in real‐world conditions.

Cindy Hsu, MD, PhD, MS (University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI) presented an update from her AI in CPR Optimization Grant funded by the American Heart Association. She described one of the primary barriers to the personalization of OHCA care as an inability to measure coronary perfusion pressure during CPR. Dr Hsu then provided a road map for INSIGHT‐CPR, a noninvasive sensor that uses an AI algorithm to provide real‐time blood pressure feedback during CPR.

Lars Wik, MD, PhD (Oslo University Hospital, Norway) described one of the main challenges of OHCA care as identifying when ROSC occurs and introduced the idea that ballistocardiography signals could be used to develop an algorithm for automating this process. Sixty data segments from six patients who experienced OHCA were collected from both monitor/defibrillator files as well as ballistocardiography signals, which measure mechanical cardiac activity through body surface vibrations, on the carotid artery and chest. They successfully extracted fluctuations from the ballistocardiography signal representative of effective heartbeats and subsequently developed an AI algorithm that could detect ROSC.

“Shock”‐ing Debates in Cardiac Arrest Resuscitation

This session comprised 2 debates surrounding the “2025 American Heart Association Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care”. 12 The first was titled “Resuscitation Guidelines: take ‘em’ or leave ‘em?’” with Michael Donnino, MD (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA) arguing the “con” position of the guidelines and Ashish Panchal, MD (The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH) arguing the “pro” position.

Dr Donnino discussed how certain therapies dictated by advanced cardiovascular life support algorithms were not supported by high‐quality evidence, for example the 2‐minute interval of chest compressions between pulse checks. He also included clinical scenarios in which there were differences in practice and nuances not captured by the guidelines. He argued for the inclusion of differing viewpoints and historical changes in the guidelines and for a more open discussion of controversies, practice variation, and knowledge gaps.

Dr Panchal followed with a discussion on the merits of the guidelines, including standardizing care, improving patient outcomes, and helping identify knowledge gaps. He emphasized that hospitals and emergency medical services agencies with the best outcomes had systems of quality assurance and standardized guidelines in place. 13 , 14 He agreed that although some variation of practice exists and there is room for furthering knowledge, having an established guideline‐driven standard of care protects patients and provides a frame of reference for medical decision making.

In the second debate, “An Electrical Brawl: Double Sequential External Defibrillation vs Standard Defibrillation,” Sheldon Cheskes, MD (University of Toronto, Toronto, ON) argued for the use of double sequential external defibrillation and Charles Deakin, MD (University of Southampton, UK) argued in favor of standard defibrillation.

Dr Cheskes, first author of the DOSE‐VF (Double Sequential External Defibrillation for Refractory Ventricular Fibrillation) trial, discussed the study's findings that patients randomized to double sequential external defibrillation had improved neurologic outcomes and higher percentage of patients surviving to hospital discharge compared with the standard group. 15 He also mentioned the promising results of the Double‐D pilot data using early double sequential external defibrillation (before 3 standard defibrillation attempts) showing improved survival (pilot data presented by Akil Awad, PhD, Karolinska Institutet, Solna, Sweden) at the European Resuscitation Council Congress in Rotterdam, October 2025. Dr Deakin rebutted with the argument that pad placement and the resultant vector may have explained the results of the DOSE‐VF trial. He also raised concerns regarding treatment compliance, misclassification of refractory versus recurrent ventricular fibrillation, and early closure of the trial, which may have biased results.

Best of the Best Abstract Oral Session

Roos Edgar, MSc (Radboud University Medical Center, Netherlands) presented “Towards Smartwatch‐Based Cardiac Arrest Detection: Validation in Shockable Cardiac Arrest,” the validation work of a wearable smartwatch to detect cardiac arrest using a photoplethysmography algorithm. 16 Fifty‐six patients from 4 university hospitals, who underwent induced cardiac arrest during implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implantation (14 patients) or ventricular tachycardia ablation (42 patients), had the wristband applied during the procedure. Sensitivity of the device was 100% for VF and 90% for pulseless ventricular tachycardia detection, with 9 false positive alerts in the latter group.

Patty Gessner, DNP (Ascension Illinois Alexian Brothers Medical Center, Elk Grove Village, IL) presented “The Benefits of Proactive versus Reactive Rapid Response Activations” in which she described rapid response activations using an AI early warning tool Electronic Cardiac Arrest Risk Triage. These ‘proactive’ rapid response activations were compared with standard ‘reactive’ protocols. Of a total of 16 589 rapid response activations, 39% were “proactive” based on Electronic Cardiac Arrest Risk Triage, representing about 6 activations per day. 17 One out of 6 patients received an intervention and 1 out of 36 was transferred to higher level of care.

Jacob Hutton, PhD (University of British Columbia) in his talk “If It Can Help Someone, Then They Want to Do It: End of Life Studies for Wearable Cardiac Arrest Detection,” discussed feasibility and implementation of the use of wearable technology for cardiac arrest detection and data collection in patients at end‐of‐life for device validation. Patients undergoing Medical Assistance in Dying procedures and patients in hospice were prospectively enrolled. Data from 64 patients were collected, representing about 28% of patients who were approached for consent. The study found that wearable‐based research in end‐of‐life settings was feasible, yielded useful data, and participant feedback was positive. 18

So‐Young Jeon, MD (Chungnam National University Hospital, Republic of Korea) presented “Beyond Blood‐Brain Barrier Disruption and Molecular Weight: Compartmental Kinetics of S100B and NSE [Neuron‐Specific Enolase] for Neurological Prognostication After Cardiac Arrest.” In this retrospective observational study of registry data from 111 survivors of OHCA treated with targeted temperature management who had a lumbar catheter in place, the levels of S100B and neuron‐specific enolase were investigated for use in neuroprognostication using serum and cerebrospinal fluid levels. 19 The investigators found that serum S100B peaked early after ROSC and then declined despite persistently elevated levels in the cerebrospinal fluid, compared with neuron‐specific enolase, which peaked at 72 hours and remained elevated in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid. The findings suggested that S100B may have utility in the early phase rather than the late phase for neuroprognostication, whereas elevated neuron‐specific enolase remained a more consistent predictor of poor neurological outcome over time.

Beating the Unexpected: Predicting and Preventing Sudden Cardiac Death in Young People

This session was moderated by Sana Al‐Khatib, MD, MHS (Duke University) and Sara Stephens, PhD, MPH (Baylor University).

Enrico Baldi, MD, PhD (Fondazione IRCCS Policlinico San Matteo di Pavia, Italy) presented “A Tale of Mandatory Screening in Pediatric Non‐Professional Athletes,” his team's research on screening nonprofessional athletes for cardiac causes of potential sudden cardiac death in Italy. He compared the cost of screening with the potential monetary benefit of implementing such screening for hospital systems. He reinforced the value of presports screening with impressive data demonstrating improved cardiovascular outcomes for children whose pathologies were identified at presports screenings.

Gregory Webster, MD, MPH (Northwestern University, Chicago, IL) presented his team's work on evaluating families referred after a child had a cardiac arrest to determine potentially heritable causes of the sudden cardiac death. He described the importance of autopsy reports on screening guidance and future care for patients affected by identifiable causes of sudden cardiac death. Importantly, follow up screening in children alone yielded an identifiable diagnosis in only 10% of cases. Dr Webster highlighted that thorough evaluation of both parents markedly improved the likelihood of finding a potentially heritable cause.

Bjorn Knollman, MD, PhD (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) shared his team's work on targeted therapies for arrhythmogenic sudden cardiac arrest. He explored the genetic mechanisms involved in sudden cardiac death outlining the key role of calcium channelopathies in many of these arrests and highlighted the importance of screening patients for specific variants of long QT syndrome. He noted that children with structural heart defects are at especially high risk of sudden cardiac death and that dantrolene is an exciting, effective treatment to decrease ventricular arrhythmias in children whose ventricular arrhythmias are associated with structural heart defects.

Targeting Inflammation to Improve Cardiac Arrest Survival

This session was moderated by Teresa May, DO (Maine Medical Center, Portland, ME), Jing Li, MD (University of Illinois Chicago), and Tiffany Ko, PhD (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, PA).

Edy Kim, MD, PhD (Brigham and Women's Hospital, Boston, MA) opened the session by presenting mechanistic insights into early innate immune activation after cardiac arrest. His team showed that by 6 hours after ROSC, immune cell transcriptomes differed markedly between patients who later achieved good versus poor neurologic outcomes, but these differences disappeared by 48 hours, highlighting initial hours after cardiac arrest may represent a window for therapeutic intervention in the resolution of inflammation. 20 He noted that targeting single inflammatory pathways such as IL‐6 (interleukin‐6) was unlikely to be effective given the complexity of postarrest immune responses. 21 Instead, he described approaches aimed at enhancing endogenous protective mechanisms, including activation of the Nectin‐2/TIGIT (T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains) immune checkpoint pathway, stimulation of sulfatide‐responsive natural killer T cells, and delivery of IL‐10 messenger RNA via myeloid‐targeted nanoparticles, all of which have improved inflammatory profiles and neurologic outcomes in preclinical models.

David Seder, MD (Maine Medical Center) discussed adaptive immunity and its protective role after cardiac arrest. He presented compelling evidence that CD73‐expressing lymphocytes, which generate immunoregulatory adenosine (a key anti‐inflammatory mediator), are associated with survival and reduced inflammatory injury. 22 Conversely, he showed that immature CD10‐negative neutrophils were disproportionately elevated in nonsurvivors and migrated into the brain, contributing to delayed neuroinflammation. 23 He concluded that CD73‐positive lymphocytes promote adenosine generation, enhance microcirculatory perfusion, and suppress inflammatory myeloid activity, whereas immature CD10‐negative neutrophils drive delayed tissue injury. Based on these findings, he proposed future therapeutic strategies aimed at augmenting CD73 lymphocytes, strengthening adenosinergic pathways, modulating immature neutrophil activity, and ultimately exploring immunomodulatory approaches in human trials.

Wei Yang, PhD (Duke University, Durham, NC) then shifted attention to the subacute phase after cardiac arrest which is marked by profound immunosuppression, characterized by severe lymphopenia and significant thymic and splenic atrophy. He demonstrated that this immune collapse is driven by activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and heightened glucocorticoid signaling, triggered in part by increased hypothalamic cytokines such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor alpha). 24 Using T‐ and B‐cell specific glucocorticoid receptor knockouts, as well as pharmacologic blockade with the glucocorticoid receptor antagonist, his team showed that preserving lymphocyte development reduces lymphoid organ atrophy, lowers bacterial infection burden, and improves neurologic recovery. He concluded that postcardiac arrest immunity progresses through 2 distinct phases, an early inflammatory surge followed by a delayed glucocorticoid mediated immunosuppressive state, which may contribute to postarrest infections and represents a promising therapeutic target.

Laust Obling, MD, PhD (Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark) presented results from the STEROHCA (Steroid Treatment After Resuscitated Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest) randomized controlled trial, which evaluated a single prehospital dose of high‐dose methylprednisolone administered immediately after ROSC. 25 The intervention produced a marked early anti‐inflammatory response, with significantly lower IL‐6 and several other proinflammatory cytokines at 24 hours, alongside higher early IL‐10 levels. Patients receiving steroids exhibited more stable hemodynamics, requiring less norepinephrine and overall vasoactive support during the first 48 hours. Although biomarkers of brain injury did not differ between groups, adjusted analyses indicated a possible survival advantage. He emphasized that the trial established the feasibility and safety of early glucocorticoid therapy in the prehospital setting and noted that these findings set the stage for larger outcome‐powered trials currently underway.

Basic/Translational Abstract Oral Session

Jun Hagiwara, MD, PhD (Nippon Medical School, Tokyo, Japan/Becker Laboratory, Feinstein Institute, Manhasset, NY) presented delayed mitochondrial transplantation in a 10‐minute rat cardiac arrest model. 26 Animals received intravenous mitochondria at 30 and 60 minutes post ROSC; mitochondrial transplantation was associated with improved neurological function at later time points (48–72 hours) and reduced histologic evidence of neuronal degeneration and microgliosis (IBA‐1 [ionized calcium‐binding adaptor molecule 1]), supporting a delayed therapeutic window and implicating postresuscitation neuroinflammation as a potential modifiable pathway.

Clark G. Owyang, MD (Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, NY) described the establishment of a swine extracorporeal CPR (ECPR) platform integrating biventricular pressure–volume loop assessment during venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) flow ramping. 27 Following 10 minutes of no‐flow VF and a 10‐minute low‐flow CPR period, animals were cannulated for fem‐fem ECMO, then ramped across stepwise flows (15 → 30 → 45 → 60 mL/kg/min). With increasing ECMO flow, both ventricles showed reductions in stroke volume and progressive ventriculoarterial uncoupling, with more pronounced uncoupling in the right ventricle, highlighting the importance of chamber‐specific physiology during eCPR and providing a translational platform for studying personalized resuscitation and ECMOmanagement.

Louis Silva, MD (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) presented a pediatric swine asphyxial arrest study linking noninvasive cerebral optical monitoring to an invasive marker of cerebral metabolic injury. 28 Using hybrid diffuse optical spectroscopy to measure cerebral oxygenation metrics during CPR and cortical microdialysis to quantify lactate–pyruvate ratio after ROSC, the group trained machine‐learning models showing that mean cerebral tissue oxygen saturation and oxyhemoglobin features during early CPR segments explained a meaningful portion of subsequent peak lactate–pyruvate ratio variance. These data supported the feasibility of using optical signals obtained during CPR as real‐time correlates of cerebral metabolic stress.

Laura Faiver, MD (University of Pittsburgh, PA) presented a clinical translational study evaluating cerebral physiology in comatose patients after cardiac arrest monitored with jugular bulb catheters. 29 She focused on the jugular venous–arterial CO2 gap as a pragmatic adjunct to jugular venous oxygen saturation interpretation, hypothesizing that CO2 gap could distinguish diffusion‐limited hypoxia (eg, perivascular edema/microvascular dysfunction) from impaired oxygen use in patients with elevated jugular venous oxygen saturation. In a single‐center retrospective cohort, jugular venous oxygen saturation decreased after hyperosmolar therapy primarily among patients with an elevated cerebral CO2 gap, suggesting the CO2 gap may help identify a treatment‐responsive subgroup and motivating prospective protocolized validation.

The final abstract, presented by Darci Anderson (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) used hybrid diffuse optical methods (frequency‐domain diffuse optical spectroscopy plus diffuse correlation spectroscopy) to compare cerebral hemodynamics across CPR strategies in a pediatric swine asphyxia arrest model. 30 Piglets were randomized to hemodynamic‐directed CPR targeting higher versus lower systolic/diastolic pressure goals, or to depth‐directed CPR. In intention‐to‐treat analyses, targeting higher blood pressure did not yield clear improvements in cerebral oxygenation or blood flow, largely because many animals could not sustain the higher systolic target over the CPR epoch; in a per‐protocol analysis restricted to animals meeting targets, higher pressure targeting increased cerebral oxygenation without a corresponding increase in cerebral blood flow, underscoring both the physiologic appeal and the practical challenges of consistently achieving aggressive hemodynamic targets during resuscitation.

Clinical Abstract Oral Session

This session was moderated by Audrey Blewer, PhD, MPH (Duke University) and Ryan Coute, DO (University of Alabama at Birmingham).

Alexander Presciutti, PhD, MSCS (Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, MA) began the session by presenting his work on a resilience intervention titled Recovering Together after Cardiac Arrest, designed for survivors of cardiac arrest and their caregivers. Using a mixed‐methods design, his team confirmed the feasibility of the Recovering Together after Cardiac Arrest intervention and observed clinically meaningful reductions in emotional distress among participants. 31

Kunihiko Maekawa, MD (Hokkaido University Hospital, Japan) then presented his research examining the association between bystander CPR type and outcomes following drowning‐related cardiac arrest. 32 Using national data from the All‐Japan Utstein OHCA registry, his team found that both compression‐only CPR and conventional CPR with rescue breathing were associated with improved survival, with conventional CPR resulting in the highest likelihood of a neurologically favorable outcome.

David Seder, MD (Maine Health, Portland, ME) presented his work titled “Brain Oxygen and ICP [Intracranial Pressure] Optimization in Severe Post‐Cardiac Arrest (BOOSCA).” He outlined a novel clinical protocol for invasive intracranial pressure and brain tissue oxygenation monitoring and management in selected patients after cardiac arrest. 33 In a cohort of 10 patients, his team found that intracranial hypertension and brain hypoxia were treatable, and that targeted management may reduce secondary neurological injury during the postcardiac arrest period.

Guro Kruger, MD (Trondheim University Hospital, Norway) introduced the audience to her team's efforts to identify ROSC after defibrillation using a hands‐free carotid Doppler ultrasound system (RescueDoppler). 34 The team analyzed 36 shocks across 13 patients with various causes. Twelve patients had some form of spontaneous circulation present on the Doppler demonstrating that this technology could identify a successful defibrillation in real time.

Tom Aufderheide, MD, MS (Medical College of Wisconsin, Milwaukee, WI) shared results from a real‐world implementation of the Zoll Real BVM technology to improve ventilations during OHCA within the target rate (8–12 breaths/min) and volume (500–600 mL/breath). 35 Two hundred seventy‐eight patients were enrolled over a 15‐month period. The Real BVM technology was activated after 3 months. There was a statistically significant change in the compliance of ventilation parameters after the activation without any change in the chest compression performance measures.

Max Harry Weil Early Career Award Competition for Resuscitation Science/3CPR Mid‐Career Achievement Award and Lecture

Three finalists, Mohammad Abdel Jawad, MD, MS (St. Luke's Hospital Kansas City, MO), Lindsay Shepard, MD, MSCE (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia), and Sara Stephens, PhD, MPH (Baylor University, Waco, TX) competed in the Max Harry Weil Award Competition for Resuscitation Science. Dr Jawad presented a study from CARES (Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival) that found a graded inverse relationship between time to bystander CPR and survival outcomes in pediatric OHCA. 36 Dr Shepard presented her work identifying novel ventilation rate thresholds associated with improved survival outcomes in children with in‐hospital cardiac arrest. 37 Dr Stephens' presentation also focused on pediatric OHCA, outlining differences in characteristics between children of different races and ethnicities. 38 Following the presentations, an international panel of expert judges awarded the 2025 Max Harry Weil Early Career Investigator Award to Dr Shepard.

The 3CPR Mid‐Career Award recognizes a midcareer investigator whose endeavors and achievements have made a significant impact on cardiopulmonary care and resuscitation science. This year's awardee was Jonathan Elmer, MD, MS (University of Pittsburgh). Dr Elmer presented his vision for the future of resuscitation science, with an emphasis on safeguarding scientific integrity against censorship and other external threats.

Funding Opportunities

Glenn Dillon, PhD (American Heart Association) and Norman Paradis, MD (Zoll Foundation, Chelmsford, MA) provided an overview of current and forthcoming funding opportunities for investigators at all career stages. The session opened with Dr Dillon announcing that the American Heart Association, the largest nongovernmental funder of cardiovascular research in the United States, 39 had allocated a record level of research funds for FY2025 to 2026. These funds will be used to expand existing mechanisms across career stages, increase support for early‐career investigators, provide 1‐year extensions for projects originally scheduled to conclude in 2025, and launch several new funding initiatives.

Dr Paradis then highlighted funding opportunities available through the Zoll Foundation for early‐stage investigators. He emphasized that “early‐stage” is defined not by the investigator's age but by their level of research experience. Eligible applicants should be nonindependent investigators (ie, those without R‐level awards as principal investigator) working under the mentorship of an established investigator with relevant scientific expertise. The Zoll Foundation supports a broad range of applicants, including paramedics, nurses, PhD scientists, and physician–scientists.

Pumped Up: Unveiling the Opportunities of ECPR

This session was moderated by Remle Crowe, PhD (ESO Solutions, Inc., Austin, TX), Adam Gottula, MD (The University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN), Katrina Augustin, MD (Mayo Clinic, Rochester. MN), and Anna Condella, MD (University of Washington). The discussion centered on emerging strategies, implementation challenges, and system‐level considerations surrounding ECPR for refractory OHCA.

Georg Trummer, MD (University of Freiburg, Breisgau, Germany) opened the session with a presentation on controlled automated reperfusion of the whole body, a next‐generation approach to ECPR designed not only to provide circulatory support but also to actively counteract whole‐body ischemia–reperfusion injury. 40 He described controlled automated reperfusion of the whole body as a therapeutic platform rather than a support device, engineered to restore perfusion with precise pressure, flow, and metabolic control. He highlighted historical limitations of conventional ECMO, the physiological challenges of prolonged CPR, and preclinical evidence showing that controlled reperfusion can markedly reduce myocardial edema and improve organ recovery after prolonged cardiac arrest.

Next, Andrea Elliott, MD (University of Minnesota) addressed real‐world implementation challenges in ECPR. She emphasized that the success of an ECPR program depends on 3 key components: patient selection, timing, and cannulation strategy. She noted that refractory VF/ventricular tachycardia with witnessed arrest and immediate bystander CPR remains the most promising target population, although survival declines substantially with each minute of delay. She discussed barriers to effective ECPR implementation, including transport logistics, early identification of eligible patients, rapid emergency department activation, trained teams, and readiness, which implies continuous availability. Her presentation underscored that ECPR outcomes depend as much on system design as on the procedure itself.

The session then shifted to a dynamic debate examining whether field‐initiated ECPR should be adopted. Melissa Vogelsong, MD (Stanford University, Stanford, CA) argued in favor of prehospital initiation, presenting a scenario of refractory VF where intra‐arrest transport and early cannulation could offer the only pathway to survival. She highlighted the substantial proportion of patients who remain in shockable rhythms long enough to benefit from ECPR and summarized emerging data suggesting that reducing low‐flow time, even by minutes, could be lifesaving. Her argument framed field ECPR as a logical extension of high‐performance emergency medical services care in select systems capable of rapid response and coordinated workflow.

In contrast, Jose Cabanas, MD, MPH (The University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC) articulated the challenges, risks, and system‐level implications of attempting ECPR in the field. He mentioned that geography, transport times, staffing, and overall emergency medical services resource constraints fundamentally limit scalability. He argued that ECPR is not primarily a scientific challenge but an infrastructure challenge that demands multidisciplinary coordination among emergency medical services, cardiology, surgery, critical care, and hospital leadership. He raised safety concerns related to intra‐arrest transport, lack of universal clinical consensus, and the risk of diverting resources from interventions with broader population impact, such as improving on‐scene CPR and defibrillation. He underscored that only systems that consistently achieve survival with good neurologic function through conventional care should consider expanding into ECPR.

Conclusions

The 2025 American Heart Association Resuscitation Science Symposium brought together investigators from across disciplines to present advances spanning basic, translational, clinical, and implementation science in resuscitation. Across sessions, recurring themes included increasing emphasis on physiologically guided CPR, refinement of monitoring strategies during and after cardiac arrest, and growing attention to survivorship, equity, and system‐level performance. Novel technologies, including Doppler‐based hemodynamic assessment, cerebral monitoring, andAI–enabled tools, highlighted the field's continued shift toward more individualized and data‐informed resuscitation care. At the same time, debates surrounding guideline implementation, defibrillation strategies, and ECPR underscored persistent knowledge gaps and the importance of rigorous evaluation before widespread adoption. Collectively, the work presented at Resuscitation Science Symposium 2025 reflected a maturing resuscitation science ecosystem that increasingly integrates mechanistic insight, clinical trial data, and health systems research to improve outcomes after cardiac arrest. Continued collaboration, thoughtful translation of emerging evidence, and attention to implementation and equity will be essential to realizing the full impact of these advances.
