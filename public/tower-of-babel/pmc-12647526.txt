National Experience With Tracheostomy in Neonates Undergoing Congenital Heart Surgery: A Multicenter Analysis
Authors: Hosam F. Ahmed, Muhammad Faateh, Kevin Kulshrestha, Spencer Hogue, David Cooper, Sara Zak, Awais Ashfaq, David Lehenbauer, David L. S. Morales, Alexis L. Benscoter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Our aim was to describe trends in tracheostomy utilization in infants requiring congenital heart surgery (CHS) during their index admission with specific focus on clinical and financial outcomes.DESIGN:A retrospective cohort study.SETTING:Data were obtained from the Pediatric Health Information System database.PATIENTS:Patients admitted as neonates (≤ 28 d) undergoing CHS with the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) during admission from 2004 to 2022 were identified. The cohort was divided into patients with vs. without tracheostomy.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:We identified 13,415 neonatal admissions who underwent CHS with use of CPB, of which 391 (3%) underwent tracheostomy. Tracheostomy patients, compared with those without, were more likely to be female (46.8% vs. 40.0%; p = 0.007), of Black race (17.1% vs. 10.6%), preterm (29.2% vs. 14.1%), low birthweight (29.4% vs. 14.1%), had a higher frequency of chromosomal defects (23.5% vs. 8%), congenital airway (24% vs. 3.3%), and pulmonary (19.7% vs. 1.7%) abnormalities (all p < 0.001). Tracheostomy was associated with higher in-hospital mortality (23.8% vs. 8.6%), longer length of stay (183 vs. 26 d), higher cost of hospitalization ($1.2 vs. $0.2 million), and discharge to a location other than home (35.1% vs. 6.3%; all p < 0.001). Tracheostomy rates increased from 1.9% in 2004–2010 to 3% in 2017–2022 (p = 0.002), while the in-hospital mortality in these patients was similar (p = 0.72).CONCLUSIONS:The rate of tracheostomy placement in complex neonates and infants requiring CHS has increased in recent years. Patients with congenital airway or pulmonary abnormalities, cleft lip and/or palate, chromosomal disorders, and those requiring more than one surgery requiring CPB during admission were at greatest risk for tracheostomy placement. Tracheostomy is associated with longer ICU and hospital length of stay, six-fold increase in hospitalization cost, and higher rate of in-hospital mortality in our study population.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the clinical impact, financial cost, and trend in tracheostomy utilization in neonates undergoing congenital heart surgery?

Findings: We identified 13,415 patients across multiple institutions. Tracheostomy patients had significantly higher mortality, spent more than half their first year in the hospital, and had six times higher hospitalization costs.

Meaning: Tracheostomy likely serves as a surrogate for medical complexity in neonates undergoing congenital heart surgery. While tracheostomy rates have increased over time, mortality remains high compared with other patient populations. These findings should set realistic expectations for medical teams and families when making informed decisions about tracheostomy.

Improvement in operative therapies and advances in medical care have resulted in improved survival for infants with complex congenital heart disease (CHD). Increasingly, infants with CHD with significant co-existing morbidities including those with a perceived need for long-term ventilator use, who were once not considered to be operative candidates are now offered corrective or palliative procedures (1–3). Reported occurrence rates of tracheostomy in children undergoing CHD surgery are 0.8–3.5% (4–7).

There are a few reports, primarily from single centers that have examined outcomes in children with tracheostomy (1–3). Larger multicenter analyses have often included older children or are focused on isolated CHD defects and do not report recent data (4, 5). Contemporary outcomes are important to examine as it is anticipated that the rates of tracheostomy are increasing in this subset. Follow-up outcomes, financial costs, and utilization of healthcare resources in neonates and infants undergoing congenital heart surgery (CHS) and who require tracheostomy have not been extensively investigated. Furthermore, limited data exists regarding outcomes of CHS in patients who had a tracheostomy placed before surgery. Our aim was to report trends in tracheostomy utilization in neonates who underwent CHS from a large pediatric multicenter database. We also aimed to report the clinical characteristics of these patients and financial costs experienced during their hospitalization.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population and Data Source

A retrospective analysis of the Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) database was performed from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2022. All patients admitted at age 28 days young or younger and undergoing cardiac surgery with the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) at any time during their admission were included. PHIS contains data from greater than 40 major pediatric hospitals across the United States that are participants of Children’s Hospital Association. Available data includes demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnosis, procedures, medications, supplies, and hospital costs. Longitudinal data regarding inpatient, outpatient, and emergency department visits is available as long as the patient continues to follow-up at the same center. Further details regarding PHIS can be accessed at: www.childrenshospitals.org. Tracheostomies were identified using procedure (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]) codes. The timing of tracheostomy in relation to CHS was calculated by the difference of days from the index CHS, defined as any cardiac procedure performed with the use of CPB. To obtain a large cohort of patients from PHIS, we used a single all-encompassing ICD code for CPB. All non-CPB procedures were excluded from our analysis. In patients undergoing multiple cardiac surgeries during admission, the first case (index CHS) was used as a reference for these calculations and for postoperative length of stay (LOS). The Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board (CCHMC IRB No. 2018-6837; date of approval: October 29, 2018) approved this study and the requirement for individual patient consent was waived as this was an analysis of de-identified data. All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of our institution on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Covariates

CHD diagnoses and CHS were identified by ICD codes using the methodologies described previously. They were reported as nonmutually exclusive (Supplemental Tables 1–3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B583) (6). Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery (RACHS)-1 category was reported for patients discharged between January 1, 2004, and September 30, 2015, and RACHS-2 for discharges from January 1, 2015, onward. Gestational age and birthweight were obtained as reported in PHIS and derived from ICD codes in patients with missing information. Preterm indicates a gestational age of younger than 37 weeks and low birthweight less than 2.5 kg. Median household income was derived from zip codes and adjusted for inflation using urban consumer price index (CPI) and reported in 2022 U.S. dollars. The variable chromosomal disorders includes Trisomy 13/18/21, Turner, DiGeorge, Klinefelter, Fragile X, and Cri-du-chat syndromes. In addition, diagnosis/complications occurring at any time during the admission (pre- or post-index CHS) were reported including stroke, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), hepatic or renal failure, cleft lip/palate, tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF), esophageal atresia (EA), or tracheal stenosis, neonatal chronic respiratory diseases (bronchopulmonary dysplasia [BPD], interstitial pulmonary fibrosis of prematurity, Wilson-Mikity syndrome), and congenital airway abnormalities (of trachea, larynx, bronchus). Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) use includes all types including pre- or post-CHS.

Outcomes

Short-term (index admission) outcomes included in-hospital mortality (death or hospice status), nonhome discharge, total and postoperative LOS, and ventilator duration. LOS was compared both as a continuous variable as well as a binary variable defined as total LOS greater than 90th percentile. Costs were reported as adjusted estimated costs of entire hospitalization derived from charge-to-cost ratio for each hospital. All costs are reported in 2022 U.S. dollars adjusted for inflation using medical CPI. Discharged on ventilator was identified by presence use of ventilator up to at least 1 day before date of discharge. Follow-up outcomes included 1- and 5-year mortality. Days in-hospital during the first year of life represent the total number of days a patient was hospitalized within their first year. This includes the LOS from both the index hospitalization and any subsequent admissions during that period. This was computed only for patients who survived and were followed-up for at least 1 year old. To assess potential differences between patients who underwent tracheostomy before vs. after cardiac surgery, a subanalysis was conducted comparing these two groups.

Statistical Analysis

The dataset was dichotomized into two groups: patients with tracheostomy (pre- or post-CHS) and patients without tracheostomy. Baseline characteristics and outcomes were compared between the two groups using chi-square test, Fisher exact test, Student t test, or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests as appropriate. In addition, a subanalysis of tracheostomy patients was performed, comparing patients with pre- CHS vs. post-CHS tracheostomy. Also, a stratified analysis to compare tracheostomy vs. nontracheostomy outcomes in single ventricle patients with hypoplastic left heart syndrome was conducted. The trends of post-CHS tracheostomy utilization were analyzed, after excluding pre-CHS tracheostomy patients. Mortality over time was computed by Kaplan-Meier failure function. Finally, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with post-CHS tracheostomy. For this, preoperative variables were selected based on their clinical significance and by backward stepwise elimination using a p values of less than 0.1. Final model discrimination and calibration were tested by receiver operating curves and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests, respectively. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analysis was performed using Stata 18.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics Comparing Tracheostomy Vs. Nontracheostomy Patients

A total of 13,415 patients were identified, of which 391 (2.9%) underwent tracheostomy. Tracheostomy was performed before CHS in 0.5%, and after CHS in 2.4%. The median age at index CHS was significantly higher in patients with tracheostomy (20 vs. 7 d; p < 0.001). Patients who underwent tracheostomy were more likely to be female (46.8% vs. 40.0%; p = 0.007), of Black race (17.1% vs. 10.6%; p < 0.001), preterm (29.2% vs. 14.1%; p < 0.001), and low birthweight (29.4% vs. 14.1%; p < 0.001). Tracheostomy patients had a lower median household income ($48,472 vs. $51,712; p = 0.004) and were more likely to have public/state insurance (56.4% vs. 47.7%; p = 0.002). They had a higher frequency of chromosomal defects (23.5% vs. 8%), cleft lip/palate (7.2% vs. 1.5%), TEF/EA tracheal stenosis (6.7% vs. 0.9%), chronic respiratory conditions (19.7% vs. 1.7%), and congenital airway defects (24.0% vs. 3.3%; all p < 0.001). Patients who received a tracheostomy experienced a higher frequency of complications, which included stroke, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, NEC, ARDS, hepatic failure, renal failure, and sepsis during their hospitalization compared with patients without tracheostomy (all p < 0.05). Similarly, patients who underwent tracheostomy were more likely to require ECMO (24.6% vs. 11.0%; p < 0.001) and greater than 1 cardiac surgery with the use of CPB (37.6% vs. 11.3%; p < 0.001) during index hospitalization. Association of Tracheostomy With Surgical Outcome

Tracheostomy placement was associated with higher in-hospital mortality(23.8% vs. 8.6%; p < 0.001), discharge to a location other than home (35.1% vs. 6.3%; p < 0.001), and longer both hospital and ICU LOS (total: 183 vs. 26 d; ICU: 154 vs. 21 d; p < 0.001). Comparing patients who had ventilator data available (75% of the overall cohort, 79% of patients with tracheostomy), patients with tracheostomies had significantly longer total ventilator duration (132 vs. 7 d; p < 0.001). The majority of these patients (78%) were on ventilator 24 hours before tracheostomy placement. Of the tracheostomy patients who survived hospitalization, 68% were discharged on ventilator. Of those who died, 85% were on ventilator at the time of death. There was a six-fold difference in median hospitalization costs between patients with vs. without tracheostomy ($1.2 vs. $0.2 million; p < 0.001). Similar outcomes were observed in a subanalysis of single ventricle patients who underwent tracheostomy (Supplemental Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B583). On follow-up, the overall 1- and 5-year mortality in patients with tracheostomy was 29.1% and 37.7% vs. 12.5% and 15.0% in patients without tracheostomy. For those who survived the index hospitalization, 1- and 5-year survival was 6.9% and 16.4% vs. 3.3% and 6.1%, respectively (p < 0.001). Patients with tracheostomy spent significantly more days in the hospital during their first year of life compared with patients without tracheostomy (229 vs. 42 d; Table 2).

Comparison of Pre- Vs. Post-CHS Tracheostomy

Comparing patients with pre-CHS tracheostomy vs. post-CHS tracheostomy, there was no difference in the in-hospital mortality (24.6% vs. 23.6%; p = 0.86). Similarly, no differences were identified in total ventilator duration (148 vs. 128 d; p = 0.51), frequency of nonhome discharge (24.5% vs. 37.3%; p = 0.09), hospitalization costs ($1.1 vs. $1.2 million; p = 0.46), days spent alive outside hospital (136 vs. 137 d; p = 0.76), or 1-year mortality for index admission survivors (4.4% vs. 7.3%; p = 0.24). Patients with post-CHS tracheostomy had a longer postoperative LOS (146 vs. 60 d; p < 0.001), but there was no significant difference in total hospital LOS (pre-CHS 204 d [151–246 d] vs. post-CHS 181 d [116–261 d]; p = 0.30). Tracheostomy was performed after a median of 60 ventilator days in patients undergoing post-CHS tracheostomy and 29 days in pre-CHS tracheostomy group (Table 3). Tracheostomy before CHS was associated with prematurity (gestational age < 37 wk) and low birth weight (weight < 2.5 kg) when compared with those patients undergoing tracheostomy after CHS. Although there was no difference in chromosomal disorders between those patients undergoing tracheostomy before CHS vs. after CHS, patients requiring tracheostomy before CHS were more likely to have congenital airway abnormalities, chronic respiratory conditions, and TEF/EA/tracheal stenosis (Table 3).

Factors Independently Associated With Post-CHS Tracheostomy

Independent factors associated with post-CHS tracheostomy, identified using multivariable analysis, included Black race (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.53; 95% CI, 1.09–2.15), chromosomal disorders (aOR, 3.05; 95% CI, 2.24–4.15), cleft lip/palate (aOR, 3.02; 95% CI, 1.73–5.26), TEF/EA/tracheal stenosis (aOR, 3.79; 95% CI, 1.97–7.28), congenital pulmonary abnormalities (aOR, 7.36; 95% CI, 4.71–11.51), congenital airway abnormalities (aOR, 6.08; 95% CI, 4.34–8.54), era 3 (2017–2022: aOR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.14–2.18), and greater than 1 CHS with CPB during admission (aOR, 4.35; 95% CI, 3.32–5.71). Trends of Post-CHS Tracheostomy

There was a statistically significant increase in the frequency of post-CHS tracheostomy during the study period: era 1 (2004–2010), 1.9%; era 2 (2011–2016), 2.6%; and era 3 (2017–2022), 3.0% (p = 0.002). The median number ventilator days pre-tracheostomy from eras 1–3 were 49, 59, and 64 days, respectively. In-hospital mortality rates across different eras in this population were not significantly different: era 1 at 21.7%, era 2 at 22.7%, and era 3 at 26.1% (p = 0.72), whereas for the overall cohort, there was a significant decrease in mortality in the latest eras (era 1 vs. 2 vs. 3: 10.1% vs. 8.4% vs. 8.3%; p = 0.002; and Supplemental Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B583). DISCUSSION

In this retrospective analysis of over 13,000 neonates undergoing CHS that required CPB across multiple institutions, 2.9% underwent tracheostomy placement during the same admission with an in-hospital mortality rate of 23.8%. Congenital pulmonary or airway abnormalities, cleft lip and/or palate, genetic defects, Black race, and undergoing more than one CHS during primary admission were associated with an increased likelihood of receiving a tracheostomy. The rate of tracheostomy placement in children after cardiac surgery has been reported between 0.8% and 3.5%, with an increase in this rate over the past 20 years (4, 7–11). Similarly, we report an increase in rate of tracheostomy in neonatal admissions that require CHS with use of CPB during admission from 1.9% during the 2004–2010 era to 3.0% in the era from 2017 to 2022.

Not surprisingly, the rate of tracheostomy placement in our study population is on the higher end of the reported range for the pediatric CHS population. Need for tracheostomy placement was associated with congenital airway and pulmonary abnormalities, which include cleft lip and/or palate, BPD and chronic lung disease, and TEF/EA, all of which are indications for tracheostomy and/or need for long-term mechanical ventilation that are specific to the neonatal population. Also consistent with previous studies, despite increased rate of tracheostomy placement in this medically complex population, we show there has been no significant change in mortality over the past 2 decades (4, 7–9). The increasing rate of tracheostomy in the pediatric CHS population reported in previous studies and demonstrated in our study over the past decade may indicate an increased willingness of the medical care teams to take on increased surgical risk in hopes of providing substantial benefit and chance at survival in medically complex patients who would not have been considered candidates for surgery 20 years ago. This shift speaks to the advances in medical care and surgical techniques that have led to improved short- and long-outcomes in a population that was once considered too high risk for surgery. Although there has not yet been a change in mortality over time, as more higher risk cases are taken on, it will be important to study the subset of patients who require tracheostomy both with and without long-term mechanical ventilatory dependence to determine if there are additional factors predictive of short- and long-term poor outcome and mortality. Similarly, these same factors will need to be evaluated for neonatal patients undergoing alternative neonatal palliations, such as placement of internal flow restrictors and patent ductus arteriosus stents, so the medical care team can provide families with data that may aid in medical decision-making.

The current study has identified several risk factors associated with the increased likelihood of post-CHS tracheostomy, some of which have not been reported previously. Among these factors, Black race, public insurance, and lower household income were found to have a higher rate of tracheostomy indicating the role of health disparities. Additionally, patients were at least three times likely to require tracheostomy after CHS if they were born with a cleft lip/palate, had TEF/EA, chromosomal/genetic defects, or had more than one bypass operation during hospital admission and at least six times if they had congenital pulmonary or airway abnormalities.

It is not a surprise that patients with congenital pulmonary or airway abnormalities would require tracheostomy either before or after CHS, as these populations are at high risk for requiring tracheostomy, either with or without need for long-term mechanical ventilation, when CHD is absent. Although not evaluated in regression analysis or separated into pre-CHS and post-CHS analyses, patients requiring tracheostomy had much higher frequency of complications, including other organ system dysfunctions, that may contribute to need for long-term mechanical ventilation with need for tracheostomy. These same complications may also have important impact on outcomes including mortality and hospital and ICU LOS. Taken together, need for tracheostomy most likely reflects a more medically complex and critically ill patient population when compared with all infants in our study population. Although not modifiable, identification of these factors may facilitate a better understanding of the added risks faced by these vulnerable patients and may help improve preoperative counseling.

Compared with patients without tracheostomy, the tracheostomy patients had substantially higher in-hospital mortality (24% vs. 8%) rate. Excluding in-hospital mortalities, the tracheostomy patients also had significantly higher 1-year mortality (7% vs. 3%) and 5-year mortality (16% vs. 6%) rates. These rates are in line with a previous multicenter analysis of CHD patients with tracheostomy patients from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database (5). In contrast to our results and the aforementioned studies, Berry et al (12) reported a lower in-hospital mortality rate (~8%) in 917 children with tracheostomy but only included a minority (< 5%) of patients admitted with a cardiac diagnosis. Of the patients surviving to discharge, patients with tracheotomy had much longer hospital and ICU LOS, much lower days spent alive and out of the hospital during the first year of life (229; 95% CI, 166–309 d vs. 42; 95% CI, 23–83 d) and 35% were discharged to skilled nursing/rehabilitation facilities or short- and long-term care facilities. Beyond medical need, at many institutions, tracheostomy patients remain on step-down units while awaiting caregiver education and discharge planning including coordination for home health services, which contributes to hospital LOS and costs.

It’s worth mentioning that for patients with single ventricle, the outcomes of tracheostomy were significantly worse, showing double mortality, five-fold LOS of 6 months, ICU and ventilator duration of almost 5 months, almost half were discharged to a rehabilitation facility, and costs were 1 million dollars higher. These findings are in line with previous investigations that reported 52% mortality in single ventricle patients who underwent tracheostomy before Fontan operation (13, 14). Therefore, the significant impact of tracheostomy, serving as a surrogate for medical complexity, is compounded with risks of CHS in neonatal and infant cardiac surgery. It will be important in future studies to try to stratify patients that require tracheostomy based on immediate and long-term outcomes and disposition. Additionally, future studies looking into life post-discharge focusing outcomes other than mortality, such as disability scores, need for long-term ventilation, need for long-term skilled nursing, and any associations or risk factors correlating with these outcomes are needed to provide patient guardians with the most comprehensive data available to inform the sometimes very difficult decision-making regarding tracheostomy for their child.

Most of the published literature investigating outcomes related to tracheostomy in CHD reports limit their analysis to patients who underwent tracheostomy after cardiac surgery (2, 3, 5). However, we intended to report the clinical impact of perioperative tracheostomy in CHS patients admitted as neonates, regardless of the timing of tracheostomy placement. Although a smaller proportion of tracheostomies were performed pre-CHS (65/391), which have entirely different indications, we found no significant differences in hospital total LOS, total ventilator duration, frequencies of nonhome discharge, and hospitalization costs when compared with those who had post-CHS tracheostomy. These findings can be helpful for neonatologists, intensivists, and surgeons who may be hesitant to proceed with tracheostomy before cardiac surgery.

The current study has some limitations that need to be acknowledged. As this was a retrospective analysis of administrative data, there is a possibility of coding errors and missed diagnostic codes, which may underscore the prevalence of some of the risk factors and comorbidities we examined. Unfortunately, we were unable to determine the exact reasons why patients received tracheostomy or granular details such as the size of the tracheostomy tube or decannulation or data regarding prior endotracheal intubations, which may have contributed to the need for tracheostomy. Patients with pre-CHS vs. post-CHS tracheostomy have entirely different indications and represent distinct populations. Therefore, this study should be interpreted as a broad overview of outcomes and costs associated with tracheostomy in this population rather than analysis of indication-specific subgroups. This also limited our ability to identify which patients needed long-term tracheostomy without ventilation. Additionally, we were unable to establish the timing of certain complications, such as stroke or ARDS, in relation to CHS or tracheostomy, or determine their impact on clinical outcomes, including mortality and hospital and ICU LOS. This limits our ability to identify their role as important predictors of tracheostomy placement. Also, while it is well established that the need for tracheostomy, especially with need for long-term mechanical ventilation, leads to increased LOS and hospital resource utilization, we are unable to determine how these complications contribute to both in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality. Ventilator use data was not available for 25% of patients overall (missing in 21% of tracheostomy patients). CHS diagnoses and procedures are reported as nonmutually exclusive due to limited ability to accurately isolate them using ICD codes. The median follow-up duration was ~ 2 years, and therefore, we only report mid-term outcomes. Last, the PHIS does not include all pediatric hospitals in the United States, which limits the generalizability of this study to participating institutions. Despite these limitations, we were able to highlight the impact of tracheostomy in neonates undergoing CHS through detailed analysis and to underline the need for future research to optimize practice and outcomes in this patient population.

In summary, neonates undergoing CHS who required tracheostomy placement had significantly higher mortality. Tracheostomy likely serves as a surrogate for medical complexity in patients undergoing CHS with distinct indications among patients undergoing tracheostomy before or after cardiac surgery. These patients spend more than half of their first year of life in the hospital and have a six-fold higher hospitalization cost. Patients belonging to marginalized communities had increased rates of tracheostomy, highlighting the need for a proactive approach to mitigate these heath disparities. The frequency of tracheostomy use in patients undergoing CHS is increasing and mortality remains high relative to other patient populations. These observations should yield realistic expectations when considering tracheostomy and offer insights to guide medical teams and families in making informed decisions.
