Products of Idempotents in Banach Algebras of Operators
S. K. Jain, Andre Leroy, Ajit Iqbal Singh

Abstract
. Let X X be a Banach space and 𝒜 \mathcal{A} be the Banach algebra B ⁡ ( X ) B(X) of bounded (i.e. continuous) linear transformations (to be called operators) on X X to itself. Let ℰ \mathcal{E} be the set of idempotents in 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and 𝒮 \mathcal{S} be the semigroup generated by ℰ \mathcal{E} under composition as multiplication. If T ∈ 𝒮 T\in\mathcal{S} with 0 ≠ T ≠ I X 0\neq T\neq I_{X} then T T has a local block representation of the form ( T 1 T 2 0 0 ) \begin{pmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\ 0&0\end{pmatrix} on X = Y ⊕ Z X=Y\oplus Z , a topological sum of non-zero closed subspaces Y Y and Z Z of X X , and any A ∈ 𝒜 A\in\mathcal{A} has the form ( A 1 A 2 A 3 A 4 ) \begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\ A_{3}&A_{4}\end{pmatrix} with T 1 , A 1 ∈ ℬ ⁡ ( Y ) T_{1},A_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(Y) , T 2 , A 2 ∈ B ⁡ ( Z , Y ) , A 3 ∈ B ⁡ ( Y , Z ) T_{2},A_{2}\in B(Z,Y),A_{3}\in B(Y,Z) , and A 4 ∈ ℬ ⁡ ( Z ) A_{4}\in\mathcal{B}(Z) . The purpose of this paper is to study conditions for T T to be in 𝒮 \mathcal{S} .

1.
Introduction

This paper is addressed to a general mathematician interested in Algebra, Linear Algebra and Operator theory.
The problem of expressing a non-invertible (i.e. singular) function
f
on a non-empty set
X
to itself as a (finite) product of idempotent functions on
X
to itself was started by Howie
[
6
]
in 1960’s. He proved it for finite sets
X
and studied the situation for infinite sets in detail. The fact that taking
X
as a basis of a linear space
\tilde{X}
with
dim(\tilde{X})=\#X
and function
f
on
X
to
X
giving rise to linear transformation
T
on
\tilde{X}
to itself led Erdos
[
5
]
to study the problem for rings of
n\times n
matrices over fields
Howie’s student Dawlings
[
3
]
studied the question for Banach algebras of bounded (i.e. continuous) linear transformations (to be called operators) on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to itself. He also analyzed Banach spaces
X
with (or without) Schauder basis, considering the (topological) dimension to be smallest cardinality of a dense subset of
X
, and therefore, the cardinality of a Schauder basis, if it exists. Several authors studied this further like Wu
[
10
]
, Arias, Corach, and Maestripieri
[
2
]
. Arias, Corach, and Maestripieri
[
2
]
concentrated on products of two idempotents of
\mathcal{A}
. We find that the technique of local block representation gives several new results related to the expression of operators on certain classes as finite product of idempotents in
\mathcal{A}
in infinitely many ways. At times, we get a better deal when
X
is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. This constitutes the subject matter of this paper.

The paper is divided in four sections. Section 2 is concerned with basics and notation. Section 3 deals with properties of the set
\mathcal{E}
of idempotents in
\mathcal{A}
and the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
generated by
\mathcal{E}
with composition as multiplication. This includes the basic concept of local block representation for members of
\mathcal{S}
, other than
0
and
I_{X}
, which coincides with the set
\mathcal{F}
of finite products of members of
\mathcal{E}
as well, other than
0
and
I_{X}
. Section 4 develops the technique further to obtain more classes of operators expressible as products of two idempotents in infinitely many ways.

2.1.
Preliminaries

(i)

Let
X
be a complex Banach space with
\lVert.\rVert
. In case
X
is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
<.,.>
we will usually write
\mathcal{H}
in place of
X
.

(a)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the Banach algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
of bounded (i.e., continuous) linear transformations on
X
to itself (to be called operators in this paper) with point wise addition and scalar multiplication and composition as multiplication and for
T\in\mathcal{A}
,
\lVert T\rVert=\sup\{\lVert Tx\rVert:\lVert x\rVert\leq 1\}
.

Let
I_{X}
be the identity operator on
X
to itself.

(b)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of idempotents in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
the semigroup generated by
\mathcal{E}
with composition as multiplication. Then
\mathcal{S}
coincides with the set of (finite) products of members of
\mathcal{E}
.

We denote the set of such products other than
0
and
I_{X}
by
\mathcal{F}
.

(c)

For Banach spaces
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
,
\mathcal{B}(X_{1},X_{2})
will denote the Banach space of bounded (i.e., continuous) linear transformations (to be called operators) on
X_{1}
to
X_{2}
.

Then
\mathcal{B}(X_{1},X_{2})
is a left
\mathcal{B}(X_{1})
Banach module and right
\mathcal{B}(X_{2})
Banach module.

We shall use the same symbol
0
for different
0
’s in respective spaces.

(ii)

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
be the set of closed (linear) subspaces of
X
.

(a)

Suppose
{\rm dim}X\geq 2
. We write
X=Y\oplus Z
, a topological sum to mean that
Y,Z\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
,
\{0\}\neq Y\neq X
,
\{0\}\neq Z\neq X
,
Y\cap Z=\{0\},Y+Z=X,
the idempotent surjective linear maps
p_{Y}:X\to Y
and
p_{Z}=I_{X}-p_{Y}:X\to Z
given by
p_{Y}(x)=y
,
p_{Z}(x)=z
for
x=y+z
with
x\in X
,
y\in Y
,
z\in Z
are both continuous (i.e., bounded). In this case,
Y
will be said to be topologically complemented (or in short complemented) in
X
and
Z
will be called a topological complement (in short, a complement) of
Y
in
X
.
The inclusion maps
i_{Y}:Y\to X
and
i_{Z}:Z\to X
are in
\mathcal{B}(Y,X)
and
\mathcal{B}(Z,X)
respectively and indeed isometric and satisfy
p_{Y}\circ i_{Y}=I_{Y}
and
p_{Z}\circ i_{Z}=I_{Z}
.

(b)

Situation in (a) permits us to treat
T\in\mathcal{A}
as a block matrix

T=\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\
T_{3}&T_{4}\end{bmatrix}

in
X=Y\oplus Z
, a topological sum with
T_{1}=p_{Y}\circ T\circ i_{Y}\in\mathcal{B}(Y)=\mathcal{A}_{1}
, say,
T_{2}=p_{Y}\circ T\circ i_{Z}\in\mathcal{B}(Z,Y)=\mathcal{A}_{2}
, say,

T_{3}=p_{Z}\circ T\circ i_{Y}\in\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)=\mathcal{A}_{3}
, say and
T_{4}=p_{Z}\circ T\circ i_{Z}\in\mathcal{B}(Z)=\mathcal{A}_{4}
, say. We call it a block representation of
T
in
X=Y\oplus Z
. We just mention that it is an example of a Morita context
[
9
]
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
be the subset of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
consisting of
Y
’s that possess complements in
X
.

(c)

If
X=\mathcal{H}
, a Hilbert space then every closed subspace
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathcal{H}
is complemented and we may even have its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
. We write
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}\oplus^{\perp}\mathcal{L}
or simply
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}
.

(d)

An old result of Lindenstrauss and Tzafriri
[
8
]
says that a Banach space
X
in which linear closed subspace
Y
is topologically complemented is isomorphic to a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. But in spite of that we may really not worry about such uncomplemented subspaces in
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
in our paper because of the special basic properties of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
.

(e)

For a closed subspace
Y
of
X
with
\{0\}\neq Y\neq X
, the (topological) dimension of the quotient Banach space
X/Y
will be called the codimension of
Y
in
X
. If
Y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is finite dimensional or has finite codimension, then
Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
. This gives us the next interspersing proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
{\rm dim}X\geq 2
. If
Y
is a closed subspace of
X
with
\{0\}\neq Y\neq X
, then there exist
Y_{0}
and
\widehat{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
that satisfy

\{0\}\neq Y_{0}\subset Y\subset\widehat{Y}\neq X.

Further, if
Y\not\in\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
, then there exist infinitely many choices for
Y_{0}
as well as
\hat{Y}
; indeed, there are a strictly increasing sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
and a strictly decreasing sequence
(\hat{Y}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
that satisfy for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
Y_{n}\subset Y\subset\hat{Y}_{n}
,
Y_{n}
is complemented in
Y_{n+1}
and
\hat{Y}_{n+1}
is complemented in
\hat{Y}_{n}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the last part. We have
Y
is neither finite dimensional nor finite codimensional. We instantly have a desired sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite dimensional spaces contained in
Y
which is strictly increasing. By Hahn Banach Theorem, there is a continuous linear functional
f\neq 0
which is zero on
Y
. We let
\hat{Y}_{1}=\mathcal{N}(f)
, the nullspace of
f
. We can proceed by induction by taking
X=Y^{n}
and obtain
Y^{n+1}
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be as in Theorem
2.1
above. Then we have the following.

(i)

There exists a
0\neq A\in\mathcal{E}
, which is zero on
Y
.

(ii)

There exist
0\neq T_{0}
and
I_{X}\neq T_{1}\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{R}(T_{0})\subset Y\subset\mathcal{R}(T_{1})
, where
\mathcal{R}
stands for the range of an element of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

(i)

We may take
A=I_{X}-p_{\widehat{Y}}
, coming from some complement of
\widehat{Y}
in
X
.

(ii)

We may take
T_{0}=p_{Y_{0}}
and
T_{1}=p_{\hat{Y}}
coming from some complements of
Y_{0}
and
\hat{Y}
in
X
.

∎

2.2.
Basic properties of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
and examples

Consider
p_{Y}
as in
2.1
(ii) (a) above. Then,
p_{Y}
is an idempotent in
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{R}(p_{Y})=Y
and
\mathcal{N}(p_{Y})=Z=\mathcal{R}(I_{X}-p_{Y})
. Geometrically speaking,
p_{Y}
is a projection on
Y
along
Z
. They constitute the base for Cartesian co-ordinate system. As already noted in Section
1
, Erdos
[
5
]
showed that they are the building blocks for singular linear transformations of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to itself. But it is not so for infinite-dimensional Banach spaces or even Hilbert spaces. The following lemma and remarks thereafter explain it to begin with.

Lemma 2.3
.

(i)

For
Q\in\mathcal{E}
with
0\neq Q\neq I_{X}
.
\mathcal{N}(Q)
, the null space of
Q
and
\mathcal{R}(Q)
, the
range of
Q
, are both closed subspaces of
X
that are neither
\{0\}
nor
X
. Indeed,
X=\mathcal{R}(Q)\oplus\mathcal{N}(Q)
, a topological sum.

(ii)

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}
, other than
0
and
I_{X}
, say
F=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{p}
with
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{E}
,
0\neq Q_{j}\neq I_{X}
for
1\leq j\leq p
. Then
\mathcal{N}(Q_{p})\subset\mathcal{N}(F)
and
\{0\}\neq\mathcal{R}(F)\subset\mathcal{R}(Q_{1})
. Also,
0\neq\mathcal{N}(F)\neq\{0\}
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}(F)}
, the closure of
\mathcal{R}(F)
, is
\neq X
.

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\mathcal{R}(T)
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}
its closure in
X
.

(a)

Let
0\neq T\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
T
has a non-zero left annihilator if and only if
T
has a non-zero idempotent left annihilator if and only if
\mathcal{K}_{1}\neq X
.

(b)

Moreover,
T
has a non-zero right annihilator if and only if
T
has a non-zero idempotent right annihilator if and only if
\mathcal{N}(T)\neq\{0\}
.

(c)

As a consequence, any
F\in\mathcal{F}
has non-zero idempotent left and right annihilators.

(iv)

For
X=\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
,
T\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{N}(T)\neq\{0\}
if and only if
T
is singular, i.e., not invertible in
\mathcal{A}
if and only if
\mathcal{R}(T)\neq X
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{R}(T)
is closed. Also, the concepts of
T
having a non-zero left annihilator coincides with that of
T
having a non-zero right annihilator.

Proof.

Part (i) above and (ii) are immediate from discussion in this section. For (iii), we appeal to Corollary
2.2
. To give an idea, we elaborate for (iii) (a). Suppose
T
has a non-zero left annihilator
A
in
\mathcal{A}
. Then,
AT=0
. So,
A
is zero on
\mathcal{K}_{0}
. But
A
is continuous, so,
A
is zero on
\mathcal{K}_{1}
. Because
A\neq 0
, we have
\mathcal{K}_{1}\neq X
. On the other hand, suppose
\mathcal{K}_{1}\neq X
. We know
\mathcal{K}_{1}\neq X
. We know
\mathcal{K}_{1}\neq\{0\}
. So, by Corollary 2.2 (i), there exists
0\neq B\in\mathcal{E}
, which is zero on
\mathcal{K}_{1}
. So,
B
is a non-zero idempotent left annihilator of
T
.

Finally (iv) comes from basic Linear Algebra.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The statements in Lemma 2.3 (iv) above are not true for the space
\mathcal{H}=l_{2}=l_{2}(\mathbb{N})=\{x=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathcal{N}},\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{n}|^{2}<\infty\}
with inner product
<x,y>=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x_{n}\bar{y_{n}}
for
x,y\in l_{2}(\mathbb{N})
as shown below. The discussion can be adapted to Banach spaces
X=l_{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
as well.

(i)

Let
T\in\mathcal{A}
be defined by

(Tx)_{j}=\begin{cases}0,&j=1\\
x_{j-1}&j\geq 2\end{cases}.

(a)

Then
\mathcal{N}(T)=\{0\}
and
\mathcal{R}(T)=\{x\in l_{2}:x_{1}=0\}
, which is closed but
\neq\mathcal{H}
. So,
T
has a non-zero left annihilator, viz., the orthogonal projection
P_{1}
given by
P_{1}(x)=x_{1}e_{1}
,
x\in l_{2}
and its non-zero scalar multiples as well. But
T
has no non-zero right annihilators.

(b)

As a consequence,
T
is not a finite product of idempotents in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
T\not\in\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

Let
T\in\mathcal{A}
be defined by
(Tx)_{j}=x_{j+1}
, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathcal{H}
.

(a)

Then
Te_{1}=0
. So,
\mathcal{N}(T)\neq\{0\}
and
T
has a non-zero right annihilator viz.,
P_{1}
as in (i) (a) above together with its non-zero scalar multiples. But
\mathcal{R}(T)=\mathcal{H}
. So,
T
has no non-zero left annihilators.

(b)

As a consequence,
T
is not a finite product of idempotents in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
T\not\in\mathcal{F}
.

(iii)

Let
T\in\mathcal{A}
be given by
(Tx)_{j}=\frac{1}{j}x_{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in l_{2}
.

(a)

Then
\mathcal{N}(T)=\{0\}
and therefore,
T
has no non-zero right annihilators. Moreover, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
e_{j}\in\mathcal{R}(T)
simply because
T(je_{j})=e_{j}
. So,
\mathcal{R}(T)
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
\overline{\mathcal{R}(T)}=\mathcal{H}
. So,
T
has no non-zero left annihilators either.

(b)

Further,
\mathcal{R}(T)\neq\mathcal{H}
because
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
given by
x_{j}=\frac{1}{j}
is in
\mathcal{H}
but not in
\mathcal{R}(T)
. This gives another instance of
T\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{N}(T)=\{0\}
, but
\mathcal{R}(T)\neq\mathcal{H}
in contrast to the situation in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(c)

T
is not a regular element of
\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, let, if possible,
G\in\mathcal{A}
be such that
TGT=T
. Then
T(GT-I_{\mathcal{H}})=0
. But
\mathcal{N}(T)=\{0\}
. So,
GT=I_{\mathcal{H}}
. So, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
je_{j}=GT(je_{j})
. So,
\lVert G(e_{j})\rVert=j
whereas
\lVert e_{j}\rVert=1
. So,
G
is not bounded, a contradiction.

Hence
\mathcal{A}
is not von Neumann regular. This demands individual study of properties of
\mathcal{F}
as we cannot use the literature on von Neumann regular rings.

We do that in the next section.

The equivalence of statements in the last part of (iii) (a) and (b) may not hold for all closed subalgebras of
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}(X)
.

Examples 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be the Banach algebra
C[0,1]
of continuous scalar valued functions on the closed interval
[0,1]
with the sup norm. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}
be given by
f(t)=2t-1
for
0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
f(t)=0
for
\frac{1}{2}\leq t\leq 1
, whereas
g(t)=0
for
0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
2t-1
for
\frac{1}{2}\leq t\leq 1
. Then
fg=0
. Now, let
X=\mathfrak{C}^{2}
. Then
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}(X)
can be identified with
M_{2}(\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{C}))
. We consider the closed subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{1}=M_{2}(\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{C}))
, where
\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{C})
the algebra of endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
identified with
\mathfrak{C}
itself. Then
T=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&f\end{bmatrix}
has
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&g\end{bmatrix}
as a non-zero left annihilator but
T
has no non-zero idempotent left annihilator. To elaborate, the left annihilators of
T
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
are given by
\begin{bmatrix}0&b\\
0&d\end{bmatrix}
with
bf=0=df
. But
\begin{bmatrix}0&b\\
0&d\end{bmatrix}
is an idempotent if and only if
bd=b
and
d^{2}=d
. The only idempotents in
\mathfrak{C}
are
0
and
1
. Now,
d=0
forces
b=0
whereas
d=1
does not satisfy
df=0
. So,
T
has no non-zero idempotent left annihilators in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Examples 2.6
.

(i)

We consider the algebra
M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and express some nonzero singular matrices

in

For

(ii)

For any ring

(iv)

For a Hilbert space

with

,

. Then

. So, we may have the following analogue of (i).

(a)

\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{K}}&(a-I_{\mathcal{K}})\mathcal{J}^{*}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{K}}&0\\
\mathcal{J}&0\end{bmatrix},

for

.

(b)

For
0\neq b\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{L,K})
,
0\neq c\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L})
,

\begin{bmatrix}bc&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{K}}&b\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{L}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{K}}&0\\
c&0\end{bmatrix}.

(c)

For
0\neq a\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}),b\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K})
