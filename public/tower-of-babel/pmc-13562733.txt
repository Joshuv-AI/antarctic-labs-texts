A chip-level MEMS short-period seismometer for distributed planetary seismic networks
Authors: Xingyu Wei, Chenhao Du, Shaolin Zhang, Qi Wang, Shimin Jiao, Qiu Wang, Hao Ouyang, Huafeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise measurement of seismic activity is fundamental to planetary seismology. However, existing planetary microelectromechanical system (MEMS) seismometers encounter a critical bottleneck in balancing compact chip footprint, a low noise floor, and a wide measurement range. This paper presents a miniaturized MEMS seismometer, which has a compact chip footprint of only 5.2 mm × 6.5 mm. The device utilizes corrugated-gap capacitive sensing comb fingers, which significantly reduce sensing nonlinearity while maintaining high displacement sensitivity, effectively extending the linear measurement range and elevating the pull-in acceleration threshold. Furthermore, the system’s effective resonant frequency is reduced by exploiting the electrostatic negative stiffness mechanism, which aims to suppress the seismometer’s noise floor. Experimental results validate the seismometer’s comprehensive performance, demonstrating a noise floor of 5.5 ng/Hz1/2 (closely approaching the mechanical thermal noise limit), a measurement range of ±34 mg, and a bandwidth of 70 Hz. Under continuous operation, the instrument successfully captured natural seismic signals from a magnitude 7.0 earthquake 3682 km away and a magnitude 5.5 event 1240 km away, achieving a 99.1% waveform correlation coefficient when compared against a commercial reference seismometer. Ultimately, this work presents a novel chip-level MEMS seismometer for distributed seismic networks and planetary seismic observation.

Introduction

The precise measurement of planetary seismic activity is not only fundamental to scientific research, such as revealing planetary internal structures1–4, clarifying the laws of planetary formation5,6, and inverting the thermal states of extraterrestrial bodies7, but also provides essential core data for engineering applications, for instance, extraterrestrial resource exploration8–13, extraterrestrial habitat safety assessment14, and planetary geological disaster early warning15–19. The Apollo Passive Seismic Experiment (APSE) pioneered this field. Deployed on the lunar surface between 1969 and 1972, APSE acquired continuous lunar seismic data until 1977. However, its Short Period (SP) seismometer relied on a traditional macroscopic moving-coil design. The sensitive element was massive (23 cm × 29 cm). The entire system weighed 11.5 kg and consumed up to 7.4 W of power. It even required an additional 2.5 W heater to maintain thermal stability20. Although groundbreaking, APSE’s performance was heavily reliant on this bulky, power-hungry architecture, which has driven the evolution of planetary seismometers toward miniaturized, highly integrated, and low-power silicon-based MEMS.

In 2018, NASA’s InSight Mars lander deployed the Seismic Experiment for Interior Structure (SEIS). It integrated a MEMS SP seismometer developed by Imperial College London21. Operating in its high-gain mode, the noise floor of this instrument drops to an impressive 0.25 ng/Hz1/2. This currently represents the absolute peak precision of planetary MEMS seismometers. However, achieving this sensitivity comes with strict functional trade-offs. Its immense gain of 27,000 V/(m/s) strictly limits the output range to just 0.3 mm/s, making it susceptible to saturation during larger seismic events. Furthermore, the core sensor chip has a dimension of 25 mm × 25 mm and employs a highly complex electromagnetic force-balanced closed-loop architecture22,23. This design necessitates the installation of bulky permanent magnets on the housing frame to provide a constant magnetic field, which severely restricts further size and weight reductions. Building on this established foundation, the same research team developed a lunar MEMS SP seismometer capable of achieving a 0.5 ng/Hz1/2 noise floor and a 100 Hz bandwidth. The Farside Seismic Suite (FSS) will soon carry this advanced payload to the lunar Schrödinger basin24,25. Other institutions have also made significant strides. In 2020, wang et al. developed the MEMS-M³ microgravity module, operating in a low Earth orbit environment. This closed-loop device successfully suppresses noise to between 2 ng/Hz1/2 and 5 ng/Hz1/2 in the 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz band, but consumes 3 W of power26. In 2023, the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) deployed the ILSA payload during the Chandrayaan-3 mission. This marked the first stable lunar operation of a fully MEMS-based scientific payload27. To accommodate different signal strengths, the instrument adopts a fine- and coarse-range dual-chip fusion strategy, integrating two distinct sets of MEMS sensing units. It utilizes an open-loop architecture with gap-varying capacitive plates to measure displacement. Specifically, its fine-range seismometer features a compact 20 mm chip, with noise floor of 30 ng/Hz1/2, making it exceptionally difficult to detect the weak, deep geological signals that are crucial for understanding planetary interiors28,29.

This study introduces a novel miniaturized MEMS SP seismometer with an ultra-compact footprint of merely 5.2 mm × 6.5 mm. To decrease the mechanical thermal noise without increasing the chip area, an extra mass is appended vertically. Crucially, we propose a corrugated-gap sensing comb finger. This unique geometry reduces sensing nonlinearity by a factor of three while maintaining high displacement sensitivity, effectively extending the linear measurement range and elevating the pull-in threshold. Furthermore, by exploiting the electrostatic negative stiffness effect derived from the capacitive electrodes, adjusting the AC carrier voltage actively reduces the system’s effective resonant frequency. Reducing the effective resonant frequency suppresses the overall noise floor to 5.5 ng/Hz1/2, closely approaching the mechanical thermal noise limit. The proposed device is fabricated by a simple four-mask process, demonstrating excellent comprehensive performance and successfully capturing multiple natural earthquake signals with high fidelity during continuous operation. Subsequent sections discuss the structural design, microfabrication process, and test results of the MEMS seismometer in detail.

Results

Device design and working principles

The mechanical structure of the designed MEMS seismometer (as shown in Fig. 1a) consists of a sensor outer frame, a spring system composed of four cantilever beams, fixed electrodes, a proof mass, and an extra mass. The fixed electrodes and the movable electrodes on the proof mass interleave to form six sets of capacitive comb electrodes, creating differential capacitors Cs1 and Cs2. The extra mass is connected to the proof mass via the silicon oxide layer, which can increase the effective mass without expanding the footprint area. The MEMS seismometer first realizes the mechanical conversion of input acceleration into displacement through its sensitive element. This displacement signal is then converted into a capacitance signal via the differential capacitive comb electrodes. With the subsequent detection and readout circuit, the capacitance signal is ultimately converted into a voltage signal. Considering the displacement detection characteristics of the differential capacitors, the input acceleration induces a change in the gap between the electrodes, which in turn alters the capacitance value. The sensitivity of traditional parallel sensing gaps is inversely proportional to the square of the gap distance. While decreasing the gap distance can yield ultra-high sensitivity, it drastically deteriorates linearity, severely impacting the linear measurement range and inducing prominent pull-in effects. This work adopts corrugated-gap comb fingers as shown in Fig. 1c. By altering the electric field distribution along the sensitive axis, the sensing nonlinearity coefficient is reduced while maintaining constant sensitivity. To identify the optimal angle that yields the best linearity, the theoretical and simulated capacitance-displacement curves for various θθ were compared. (Fig. 1f). At θθ = 90°, nonlinearity peaked and was tightly aligned with theoretical values. As θθ decreases, the second-order coefficient reduces accordingly. For θθ < 30°, edge effects cause the simulation results to diverge from theoretical predictions. Similarly, the third-order coefficient aligned with theory at 50° and 45°, but increased to 1.2, 1.3, and 2.5 times the theoretical values at 38°, 31°, and 22°, respectively. Balancing these characteristics and laboratory fabrication capabilities, the corrugation angle was ultimately set to 31°.

Setup

The fabricated MEMS chip is encapsulated in a ceramic package, as shown in Fig. 3a. The packaged chip was subsequently integrated onto a customized printed circuit board (PCB) and tested inside a vacuum chamber. The test instruments utilized in this study are shown in Fig. 3b. A power supply (KEYSIGHT E3631A) supplies power to the PCB, and the AC carrier voltage was supplied by a waveform generator (KEYSIGHT 33600A). Performance testing

To characterize the device’s amplitude-frequency response, the waveform generator applied a 0.5–500 Hz sinusoidal drive voltage to fixed electrodes, electrostatically actuating the proof mass along its sensitive axis. Fitting this experimental data with Eq. (2) yielded an R2 value of 0.9962, establishing a +3 dB bandwidth of 70 Hz and a −3 dB bandwidth of 180 Hz .

Next, we evaluated the device’s self-noise. The MEMS seismometer was placed adjacent to the commercial reference seismometer, and measurements were started simultaneously over the same time period. The raw measured signal represents a superposition of the sensor’s self-noise and Earth’s micro-tremors34. Power Spectral Density (PSD) analysis confirmed that both devices successfully captured these micro-tremors signals, as shown in Fig. 5b. Since the background noise behaves as a common-mode signal in both sensors, we adopt coherence analysis to remove the correlated ambient noise and extract the self-noise of the MEMS seismometer35. In contrast, the miniaturized MEMS seismometer proposed in this paper achieves a balanced, high-performance breakthrough on a miniaturized foundation. The device chip size is merely 5.2 mm × 6.5 mm, making it the smallest in area among all benchmarked solutions—only 24% of the SEIS, FSS, and MEMS-M³ footprints, and 30% of the ILSA footprint. Its noise floor is as low as 5.5 ng/Hz1/2, approaching the mechanical thermal noise limit of 4.9 ng/Hz1/2. Concurrently, it realizes a wide linear measurement range of ±34 mg and a high dynamic range of 136 dB, leading peer open-loop solutions by 22 dB and performing comparably to the closed-loop NASA InSight SEIS. The bandwidth of the device is 70 Hz, which is higher than that of currently on-orbit MEMS seismometers. In summary, compared to existing MEMS seismometers, the proposed seismometer achieves a 4.5-fold improvement in the FOM, demonstrating superior overall performance.

Through the innovative design of corrugated-gap capacitive sensing comb fingers, this work reduces the second-order and third-order nonlinearity coefficients by 2-fold and 3-fold, respectively, greatly broadening the linear range, increasing the pull-in voltage by a factor of 1.4, and resolving the sensitivity-linearity contradiction in traditional architectures. By leveraging the electrostatic negative stiffness mechanism and adjusting the carrier voltage to regulate the effective resonant frequency, it suppresses the noise floor to the mechanical thermal noise limit.

Future work will focus on novel packaging methods and improved interface circuits. Specifically, utilizing an Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) for capacitive signal detection is expected to yield higher resolution and greater integration. The low manufacturing cost, compact size, and mass-production scalability of the proposed MEMS seismometer highlight its potential for broader applications, enhanced flexibility, and ease of integration. It is conceivable that these devices could be deployed as distributed planetary seismic networks in practical scenarios, serving purposes such as extraterrestrial resource exploration, safety assessments of extraterrestrial habitats, and infrastructure health monitoring.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the MEMS chip

The MEMS seismometer is fabricated on a standard silicon on insulator (SOI) wafer using an efficient four-mask process (Fig. 6). The specific workflow is as follows: (a) The wafer surface is cleaned with a buffered HF solution (1:5) to remove native oxide. (b) A 30 nm Cr and 200 nm Au layer are deposited on the handle layer. The first mask defines the deposited gold through lithography and etching. (c) Using the second mask, thick photoresist is patterned. Deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) on the handle layer forms the release cavity and extra mass. (d) The SOI is rinsed with buffered HF. A 350 nm Al layer is deposited on the device layer, where the third mask patterns the pads. (e) The fourth mask and DRIE etch on the device layer. This precisely defines the movable structures and fixed electrodes. (f) Finally, dry etching removes the buried oxide (BOX) sacrificial layer. This releses the micromechanical structures, finalizing the chip fabrication.Fig. 6Microfabrication process flow of the MEMS seismometer chip.a Cleaning the SOI wafer and removing the native surface oxide. b Depositing Cr/Au layers and patterning the substrate grounding electrodes. c Etching the handle layer via DRIE to form the release cavity and extra mass block. d Depositing an Al layer and patterning the interconnection pads on the device layer. e DRIE on the device layer to define the mass-spring structures and comb fingers. f Dry etching the buried oxide (BOX) sacrificial layer to release the micromechanical structures

Interface circuit

The capacitance detection interface circuit relies on a differential transformer bridge and amplitude modulation to mitigate electronic 1/f noise, as shown in Fig. 7. Initially, minute low-frequency capacitance variations are modulated by a 50 kHz AC carrier. A transformer-based charge amplifier then converts the resulting current into an AC voltage. This signal passes through a band-pass filter to reject out-of-band noise, significantly improving the signal-to-noise ratio. Next, a lock-in amplifier performs coherent demodulation to down-convert the high-frequency signal back to the baseband. To ensure optimal phase coherence, a phase conditioning circuit precisely aligns the demodulation reference with the original carrier source. Finally, a low-pass filter eliminates residual high-frequency carrier and mixing components, delivering the extracted low-frequency voltage signal.Fig. 7Architecture of the capacitance readout system.Schematic diagram of the interface circuit based on a differential transformer bridge and modulation-demodulation technology
