CooT : Learning to Coordinate In-Context with Coordination Transformers
Huai-Chih Wang, Hsiang-Chun Chuang, Hsi-Chun Cheng, Dai-Jie Wu, Shao-Hua Sun

Abstract
Effective coordination among artificial agents in dynamic and uncertain environments remains a significant challenge in multi-agent systems. Existing approaches, such as self-play and population-based methods, either generalize poorly to unseen partners or require impractically extensive training. To overcome these limitations, we propose Coordination Transformers ( CooT ), a novel in-context coordination framework that uses recent interaction histories to rapidly adapt to unseen partners. Unlike previous approaches that primarily aim to increase the diversity of training partners, CooT explicitly focuses on adapting to new partner behaviors by predicting actions aligned with observed partner interactions. Trained on interaction trajectories collected from diverse pairs of agents with complementary behaviors, CooT quickly learns effective coordination strategies without explicit supervision or fine-tuning. Evaluations on the Overcooked benchmark demonstrate that CooT significantly outperforms baseline methods in coordination tasks involving previously unseen partners. Human evaluations further confirm CooT as the most effective collaborative partner, while extensive ablations highlight its robustness, flexibility, and sensitivity to context in multi-agent scenarios.

Introduction

Coordination is fundamental to intelligent behavior, enabling individuals to achieve shared goals through joint effort. In dynamic and uncertain environments, such as team sports or traffic navigation, humans adjust their actions based on others’ behaviors and intentions, facilitating effective collaboration. Replicating this adaptive coordination in artificial agents remains a core challenge in multi-agent systems
(
Cao et al. 2012
)
, especially in domains like robotics
(
Yan et al. 2013
)
, gaming
(
Matignon et al. 2012
)
, and human-AI interaction
(
Carroll et al. 2019
)
, where an agent’s success often depends on effectively responding to its partners.

Existing methods for developing agents capable of coordinating with unseen partners have pursued various strategies. Self-play
(
Tesauro 1994
;
Yu et al. 2022a
;
Hu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
trains agents by having them repeatedly interact with copies of themselves. Although this is effective in coordinating with known partners, it frequently leads to conventions that fail when interacting with unfamiliar collaborators. Population-based methods
(
Jaderberg et al. 2017
)
seek to address this by training agents within diverse populations using strategies such as partner randomization
(
Hu et al. 2020
;
Lucas & Allen 2022
)
, reward shaping
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, explicit modeling of partner behaviors
(
Lou et al. 2023
)
, and enhancing behavioral diversity
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Lupu et al. 2021
;
Xue et al. 2024
)
. However, these approaches rely heavily on extensive training and exposure to diverse partners and generally do not support efficient online adaptation, limiting their applicability in open-ended or real-world scenarios. Directly framing coordination as a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problem also proves inefficient, often requiring millions or even billions of interaction trajectories to learn effective coordination strategies
(
Nekoei et al. 2023
)
, thereby severely limiting rapid adaptation to new partners.

Recent advances in in-context learning (ICL)
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023a
)
offer a promising alternative by enabling models to adapt their behavior based solely on contextual examples, without additional training or fine-tuning. Originally demonstrated in language modeling
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, ICL has recently been adapted to sequential decision-making domains, including offline reinforcement learning
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2023
)
and distillation of reinforcement learning policies
(
Laskin et al. 2023
;
Kirsch et al. 2023
)
. However, applying ICL to multi-agent coordination introduces distinct challenges. Unlike task-focused settings with clearly defined rewards, coordination tasks require aligning with the diverse behaviors of various partners, often without explicit feedback or well-defined success metrics. Thus, the primary challenge shifts from generalizing across tasks to effectively interpreting and adapting to the behaviors of new partners purely based on observed interactions.

To address this challenge, we introduce Coordination Transformers (
CooT
), a framework designed specifically for in-context partner adaptation. Unlike existing methods
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Laskin et al. 2023
)
that focus on generalizing across tasks,
CooT
explicitly aims to generalize across diverse partner behaviors. Specifically,
CooT
predicts actions that best align with the observed partner’s behavior to maximize collaboration effectiveness. To achieve this, we train
CooT
on trajectories collected from interactions between pairs of agents whose behaviors reflect distinct underlying preferences. Such agents, which we term behavior-preferring agents, operate based on event-based hidden reward functions that guide their actions but are hidden from their partners. These event-based preferences yield diverse and potentially unpredictable coordination behaviors, even within identical environments. By observing interactions between these agents and their complementary counterparts,
CooT
learns context-driven strategies that enable effective coordination with previously unseen partners.

We extensively evaluated
CooT
against existing methods
(
Yu et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
using the Overcooked environment
(
Carroll et al. 2019
)
, a popular multi-agent benchmark requiring precise and adaptive coordination. The results show that
CooT
consistently outperforms baselines across a wide range of coordination tasks involving unseen agents. Furthermore, human evaluations consistently rank
CooT
as the most effective partner in interactive collaboration. Ablation studies provide additional insights, revealing that
CooT
continuously improves coordination by observing more interactions with novel partners, achieving effective coordination with fewer than ten observed trajectories. These studies also highlight the importance of temporal structure in interaction contexts; specifically, preserving longer-range temporal relationships through chunk-wise augmentation significantly enhances generalization compared to methods that disrupt temporal continuity.

Related work

In-Context Reinforcement Learning.
In-context learning (ICL)
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023a
)
refers to the ability of a model to adapt its behavior by conditioning on examples presented at inference time, without any gradient updates.
While ICL demonstrates most prominently in text generation
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
and text-conditioned image synthesis
(
Yu et al. 2022b
)
, recent researches extend the application to reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Laskin et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2023
)
to solve novel tasks.
In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) provides promising frameworks for offline reinforcement learning
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2022
)
, online decision-making
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
, learning reinforcement learning algorithms
(
Laskin et al. 2023
)
, exploration and exploitation
(
Dai et al. 2024
)
, and meta reinforcement learning
(
Kirsch et al. 2024
)
.
Theoretical analyses further suggest that such models may implicitly perform optimization-like computations
(
Laskin et al. 2023
;
Von Oswald et al. 2023
)
.
Some ICRL utilize this ability by viewing previous trajectories as examples, and show impressive results in learning RL algorithms
(
Laskin et al. 2023
)
, posterior sampling
(
Lee et al. 2023
)
, and balancing exploration and exploitation
(
Dai et al. 2024
)
, suggesting that ICRL can act adaptively conditioned on previous trajectories. Unlike previous research that focuses on generalizing to novel tasks,
CooT
generalizes to unseen partners by framing previous episodes as few-shot context.

Learning to Coordinate.

Learning to coordinate in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) involves designing agents that can develop effective collaboration strategies, often under varying assumptions about their partners. One key challenge in coordination is zero-shot coordination (ZSC)
(
Stone et al. 2010
)
. Without further training, ZSC requires an ego agent to cooperate with previously unseen partners at test time.
Self-play (SP)
(
Tesauro 1994
;
Yu et al. 2022a
;
Hu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
trains agents by interacting with copies of themselves, but they often struggle to generalize to novel partners
(
Carroll et al. 2019
)
, due to overfitting to a fixed partner policy. To improve robustness, population-based methods
(
Jaderberg et al. 2017
;
Long* et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2020
;
Strouse et al. 2021
)
expose the ego agent to a range of partner behaviors during training. Several studies continue increasing population diversity using entropy-based methods
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Lupu et al. 2021
)
, reward shaping
(
Lucas & Allen 2022
)
, groups of population
(
Xue et al. 2024
)
, hidden-utility functions to simulate biased or human-like preferences
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, and training with incompatible agents
(
Charakorn et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023b
)
.
Despite these advances, most coordination strategies still require extensive training interactions. To address this, few-shot coordination (FSC)
(
Nekoei et al. 2023
)
has emerged as a promising direction, aiming to enable agents to adapt to new partners with minimal experience rapidly. Approaches like PECAN
(
Lou et al. 2023
)
allow agents to analyze and respond to observed partner behavior for better coordination. Building on this idea, our proposed method,
CooT
, leverages the in-context learning capabilities of transformers to achieve effective few-shot coordination, offering a scalable solution for generalizing coordination strategies to novel partners.

Transfer Learning in Reinforcement Learning.

Transfer learning in reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Duan et al. 2016
;
Gupta et al. 2017
;
Finn et al. 2017
;
Tobin et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Xing et al. 2021
)
aims to enable agents to leverage knowledge acquired from one set of tasks or environments to perform well on new, unseen tasks with minimal additional training. This paradigm is especially critical in scenarios where acquiring task-specific experience is expensive or impractical. Some researches address transfer RL through task-invariant feature spaces
(
Gupta et al. 2017
;
Xing et al. 2021
)
, meta-learning for rapid adaptation
(
Duan et al. 2016
;
Finn et al. 2017
;
Rothfuss et al. 2019
;
Rakelly et al. 2019
)
, and domain randomization
(
Tobin et al. 2017
)
. Notably, recent works
(
Laskin et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2023
)
have drawn conceptual parallels between transfer learning and In-context learning (ICL): both aim to generalize behavior across different conditions. While transfer RL typically uses explicit parameter updates during adaptation
(
Duan et al. 2016
;
Finn et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, ICL allows a fixed model to adapt by conditioning on interaction histories
(
Lee et al. 2023
;
Laskin et al. 2023
)
. In-context learning enables implicit transfer with several interactions in RL environments, towards building more flexible and sample-efficient RL agents. In this paper,
CooT
learns the offline representation and adapts to novel online partners over several episodes.

3
Preliminary

3.1
Hidden-Utility Markov Game

We formulate the in-context coordination problem as a Hidden-Utility Markov Game (HU-MG), inspired by the framework introduced in Hidden-Utility Self-Play
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
. Under this formulation, agents interact within an environment characterized by shared dynamics and an explicit global reward function. However, agents differ in their internal, unobservable utility functions, which shape their coordination behavior. Although hidden utilities are not directly observable, they implicitly influence the policy decisions made by each agent.

Formally, we define a two-agent decentralized Markov game
(
Bernstein et al. 2002
)
as the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R}_{t})
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the state space,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{i}\times\mathcal{A}^{w}
is the joint action space,
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
represents the transition function, and
\mathcal{R}_{t}
denotes the shared global reward function. Both agents, denoted as
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{w}
, aim to maximize this global reward. However, a two-agent HU-MG is defined as the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R}_{t},\mathcal{R}_{w})
, deviating from conventional Markov games by introducing a hidden reward space
\mathcal{R}_{w}
, which contains a variety of hidden reward functions
r^{w}\sim\mathcal{R}_{w}
. The hidden reward function
r^{w}
is formulated as linear functions over event features and is only observable by the partner agent
\pi_{w}
. Consequently, the hidden reward induces distinctive behavioral preferences or coordination styles, often leading agents toward different local optima or emergent conventions.

3.2
In-Context Learning with Decision-Pretrained Transformers

In-context learning refers to a model’s generalization ability by conditioning its predictions on contextual examples provided at inference time. Decision-Pretrained Transformer (DPT)
(
Lee et al. 2023
)
is a prominent approach in sequential decision-making, showing that transformers trained on offline trajectories can solve novel tasks by leveraging past interactions as context. In the DPT framework, transformers are trained on sequences of
(s,a,r)
triplets, representing states, actions, and returns. At inference, the model autoregressively predicts the optimal action for a given state, conditioned on a set of contextual examples. This reframes reinforcement learning as sequence modeling, where the model dynamically adapts by inferring task identity from the provided context. While effective at task generalization, standard DPT methods assume access to extrinsic reward signals and explicitly defined task objectives. This assumption limits their applicability in coordination settings where agents must adapt to partners with diverse and implicit behavioral preferences.

4
Method

To build agents capable of efficient and adaptive coordination with unseen partners, we propose Coordination Transformer (
CooT

), a framework that adapts to novel partners by conditioning on past interactions. Our approach involves training a transformer on trajectories collected from diverse partners and their corresponding best responses, i.e., the behavior that yields the highest expected return given a specific partner’s behavior, enabling effective generalization to previously unseen collaborators. We begin by formalizing coordination as a Hidden-Utility Markov Game (HU-MG) in Section
4.1
, where agents behave according to hidden, agent-specific reward functions. We then describe the process of generating a diverse dataset of interaction histories in Section
4.2
, explain how
CooT
is trained to predict partner-aligned actions in Section
4.3
, and present our deployment protocol for evaluating generalization in Section
4.4
. An overview of the full framework is illustrated in Figure
1
.

4.1
From Task Generalization to Coordination Generalization

Recent advances in In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL) have focused on enabling sequence models to generalize across tasks by conditioning on previous experiences. These approaches typically assume explicit task definitions and observable reward signals, allowing models to infer goals or environment dynamics from context. However, they do not address the challenge of adapting to diverse partners operating under hidden preferences within the same task.

To address this limitation, we reframe coordination as a two-player Hidden-Utility Markov Game (HU-MG), where each agent’s behavior is driven by a hidden reward function that is unobservable to its partner. We refer to such agents as
behavior-preferring agents
, reflecting that their preferences manifest through behavior rather than explicit task annotations. These hidden utilities result in diverse and sometimes unpredictable coordination patterns, even when the environment remains fixed. In this setting, adapting to a partner requires inferring its underlying preferences through observed behavior.

Our approach treats this partner adaptation process as an in-context learning problem. Rather than relying on explicit task labels or extrinsic rewards, the model learns to infer hidden utilities by conditioning on a window of past interactions. The optimal policy becomes a
best-response policy
, conditioned on the inferred partner behavior. This formulation extends Decision-Pretrained Transformers (DPT)
(
Lee et al. 2023
)
to support generalization not only across tasks but also across partners with distinct, unobservable preferences.

4.2
Dataset Generation

To enable in-context adaptation to novel partners, we construct a dataset
D
containing interaction histories between behavior-preferring agents and their corresponding best responses. Our data generation process is grounded in the HU-MG framework (see Section
3.1
), where coordination challenges arise from agent-specific hidden reward differences.

Following the approach of ZSC-Eval
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, we simulate hidden-utility preferences by first defining a set of discrete environmental events, such as picking up an onion from the table, placing a dish down, or delivering soup. We then construct different hidden reward functions using linear combinations of the environmental events to form a space of reward
\mathcal{R}_{w}
. For each hidden reward function
r^{w}\sim\mathcal{R}_{w}
, we jointly train a behavior-preferring policy
\pi_{w}
and its best-response policy
\pi^{\text{br}}_{i}
using Proximal Policy Optimization

To ensure diversity among training partners, we compute an event-based diversity score
d_{i}
for each best-response policy
\pi^{\text{br}}_{i}
by measuring differences in expected event counts. We then select the top-
N
diverse pairs to form the training pool
\Pi_{\text{train}}
. For each selected pair
(\pi_{w},\pi^{\text{br}}_{i})
, we collect
T
trajectories to form a fixed-length context
C
. From each context, we sample a query state
s_{h}
and construct tuples of the form
(s_{h},C,\hat{a})
, where
\hat{a}=\pi^{\text{br}}_{i}(s_{h})
is the best-response action. These tuples are added to the dataset
D
for training
CooT
.

To construct the evaluation set, we extract behavioral features
\phi_{i}
from trajectories generated by each
\pi^{\text{br}}_{i}
and compute the population diversity as introduced in ZSC-Eval. The population diversity (PD) is defined as
PD(\{\pi_{i}\}^{P}_{i=1}):=det(\mathbf{K})
, where
\mathbf{K}
is the similarity matrix of the behavioral featrure set
\{\phi_{i}\}
. To efficiently select a diverse evaluation set, we then apply Determinantal Point Process sampling
(
Kulesza et al. 2012
)
over
\mathbf{K}
, resulting in a behaviorally diverse evaluation set
\Pi_{\text{eval}}\subset\Pi_{0}
.

4.3
Training

Given the dataset of context–query–action tuples, we train the Coordination Transformer (
CooT
) to perform best-response prediction via in-context learning. At each training step, the model receives a query state
s_{h}
and a context window
C
, consisting of past interactions between a behavior-preferring agent and its best response. Conditioned on this context, the transformer predicts the best-response action distribution
\hat{p}_{h}(\cdot)=M_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{h},C)
.

The model is trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\log\hat{p}_{h}(\hat{a})

(1)

where
\hat{a}=\pi^{\text{br}}_{i}(s_{h})
is the ground-truth best-response action at the query state. This objective encourages the model to map partner behaviors to effective responses, supporting generalization through in-context adaptation to unseen collaborators.

4.4
Online Deployment

At test time,
CooT
is deployed without partner-specific fine-tuning and interacts directly with unseen partner policies
\pi^{\text{unseen}}_{w}\sim\Pi_{\text{eval}}
over multiple episodes. A fixed-length context
C
, composed of
T
previously collected trajectories, is initialized before deployment begins.

At each timestep
t
in an episode of length
Z
, the agent observes the current state
s_{t}
, the context
C
, and optionally a short history of prior states (e.g.,
\{s_{t-n},\dots,s_{t}\}
). Based on this input,
CooT
predicts an action distribution
\hat{p}_{t}(\cdot)=M_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},C)
and samples an action
a_{t}\sim\hat{p}_{t}
.

After executing action
a_{t}
and observing reward
r_{t}
, the transition
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})
is recorded. Once the episode is complete, the resulting trajectory of
Z
steps is appended to the context buffer. Repeating this process across
E
episodes allows
CooT
to refine its coordination strategy through in-context adaptation alone. Without parameter updates, it progressively adapts to partner behaviors using recent interaction histories, demonstrating few-shot generalization in multi-agent coordination.

Environments.

We evaluate our method using the Overcooked environment
(
Lauffer et al. 2023
)
, a multi-agent cooperative benchmark designed to test coordination capabilities. In this setting, two agents collaborate to prepare and deliver soups within a fixed time limit. The kitchen environment includes various ingredients (e.g., onions, tomatoes) and interactive objects (e.g., pots, plates, dispensers). Agents can move, wait, or interact with nearby objects to cook and serve soups, receiving a sparse reward of 20 for each successful delivery.

Our evaluation considers five distinct layouts—
Forced Coord.
,
Asymm. Adv.
,
Bothway Coord.
,
Blocked Coord.
, and
Coord. Ring
—as well as two multi-recipe variants. Each layout introduces unique coordination challenges due to variations in spatial arrangement and resource distribution.
For example, only one agent can access ingredients in the
Forced Coord.
layout. This results in fixed subtask assignments: one agent retrieves ingredients, while the other transports them to the pot. This arrangement necessitates tight coordination, as the task cannot be completed independently.

By contrast, layouts like
Asymm. Adv.
have a more balanced distribution of resources, allowing agents to operate mostly independently in separate zones. Such layouts are categorized as low-interaction scenarios, requiring minimal agent-to-agent coordination. Additional layout descriptions are provided in Appendix
A
.

Baselines.

We compare
CooT
against three representative baseline methods: Hidden-Utility Self-Play (HSP)
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, Maximum Entropy Population-Based Training (MEP)
(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, and Behavior Cloning (BC)
(
Bain & Sammut 1995
;
Torabi et al. 2018
)
. Both HSP and MEP follow a two-stage approach designed for human-AI collaboration. In the first stage, they build a diverse policy pool to maximize behavioral coverage. In the second stage, a final adaptive agent is trained to coordinate effectively with these diverse partners after training each policy for 10M timesteps. BC, by contrast, directly imitates expert trajectories without explicitly modeling partner variability, serving as a non-partner-aware baseline. Specifically, we include:

•

Hidden-Utility Self-Play (HSP)

(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, which generates a diverse policy pool by training agents with varied hidden reward functions. This encourages behavioral diversity, improving the final policy’s generalization ability to unseen human partners.

•

Maximum Entropy Population-Based Training (MEP)

(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, which explicitly maximizes behavioral diversity during training using maximum entropy regularization, thus enhancing robustness against unfamiliar partners.

•

Behavior Cloning (BC)

(
Bain & Sammut 1995
;
Torabi et al. 2018
)
, trained through supervised learning to imitate expert demonstrations. To further assess demonstration quality, we train BC on demonstrations corresponding to different return percentiles (
e
.
g
., top
25\%
,
50\%
).

We also evaluate HSP and MEP variants trained using dense reward feedback in the second stage for a comprehensive analysis. While dense reward signals typically aren’t available in realistic human-AI collaboration scenarios, including these variants provides insight into potential upper bounds under idealized conditions.

Evaluation Pipeline.

Our evaluation pipeline is inspired by ZSC-Eval
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. This framework generates diverse evaluation partners and selects subsets for testing based on Best Response Diversity, defined as the determinant of the similarity matrix of the evaluation partners’ best responses. We refer to this selected subset as the evaluation set, agents specifically reserved for testing coordination performance. Unlike ZSC-Eval’s original approach, which uses multiple training checkpoints for evaluation, we simplify the protocol by selecting only fully converged checkpoints. This eliminates variability from partially trained behaviors and isolates pure coordination performance. We choose 10 partner agents for each layout or 15 for more challenging multi-recipe scenarios.

Evaluation Metrics.

We employ two metrics for evaluation: Best Response Proximity (BR-Prox) and average episode reward. BR-Prox, proposed in
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, measures how closely an ego agent’s performance approximates the best-response agent paired with a given evaluation partner. Formally, BR-Prox is defined as the ratio of the ego agent’s return (with the evaluation partner) to the evaluation partner’s return (with its best response). A higher BR-Prox indicates better coordination and generalization. We exclude evaluation pairs where the evaluation partner’s return with its best response is zero to avoid degenerate cases.

Average episode reward captures overall performance across a diverse set of evaluation partners, providing complementary insight into general effectiveness. We average metrics across 50 rollouts per partner to ensure reliable estimates, more than the 30 used in
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
benchmarks. This choice reflects that
CooT
’s performance improves with additional partner interactions, and a larger number of rollouts helps stabilize measurements across varying adaptation trajectories. Additional metrics (min/max, interquartile range) are reported in Appendix
D
.

, characterized by constrained space and clear subtask divisions, show narrower performance differences between methods. However, when complexity increases—for instance, in the multi-recipe variant of
Forced Coord. Multi-recipe
, baseline methods experience significant performance declines (approximately 16.47 points on average episode reward), whereas
CooT
maintains robust performance.

Among baselines, HSP and MEP perform well in layouts with predictable interactions, like
Forced Coord.
. Nonetheless, their dependence on static policy pools limits adaptability to partner behaviors not encountered during training. While incorporating dense reward signals (HSP-dense and MEP-dense) yields better overall performance, these methods still struggle with novel partner behaviors due to fixed coordination strategies.

BC achieves moderate results in low-interaction scenarios such as
Asymm. Adv.
, but fails to adapt in environments requiring substantial partner interaction. Without explicitly modeling partner behavior, BC cannot effectively infer evolving partner strategies.

One potential concern is the mismatch between training and deployment: while
CooT
is trained on best-response trajectories from a diverse population, it must coordinate with arbitrary partners at test time. This gap, though not directly addressed, aligns with a common assumption in population-based coordination that partner diversity during training promotes generalizable strategies. In cooperative environments such as Overcooked, which exhibit reusable coordination patterns and constrained dynamics, we hypothesize that training on a sufficiently diverse set of behaviors equips the agent with strong generalization abilities to infer and adapt to novel partner strategies at test time. Another practical consideration is the increased inference cost of transformer-based in-context learning compared to simpler baselines. Although this is not the primary focus of our work, we provide a brief comparison in Appendix
B.5
, showing that despite the additional computational overhead, the cost remains practical in our multi-agent setting.

Overall, these results suggest
CooT
provides enhanced robustness and superior coordination capabilities, particularly in scenarios requiring nuanced adaptation to diverse and dynamic partners. Its ability to infer and respond effectively to context-specific partner behaviors likely explains these performance gains.

5.3
Adaptation Through Context Accumulation

To examine how
CooT
’s context conditioning supports gradual coordination improvement, we analyze its performance over multiple episodes with a new partner. If
CooT
effectively leverages context, coordination should progressively improve as interaction data accumulates. This analysis builds on our previous finding that context significantly enhances adaptation to novel partners and further explores whether this adaptation occurs immediately or develops over time.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, performance steadily increases across episodes 1 to 10. This improvement arises from
CooT
’s ability to continually update its contextual understanding by integrating newly observed trajectories, which serve as few-shot examples of the partner’s behavior. Notably, in low-coordination layouts such as
Bothway Coord.
and
Asymm. Adv.
, performance gains remain limited. This is likely because these scenarios require minimal inter-agent coordination, reducing the impact of context-based adaptation. Overall, these results highlight
CooT
’s rapid adaptability, which is a valuable property in human-AI collaboration where agents begin with no prior knowledge of their partners’ behaviors.

6
Conclusion

We presented Coordination Transformer (
CooT

), a novel in-context learning framework that enables artificial agents to adaptively coordinate with unseen partners by conditioning on recent interaction histories. Unlike prior approaches that focus on task generalization or rely on diverse training populations,
CooT
shifts the focus to partner generalization and predicts best-response actions without requiring fine-tuning. Our experiments on the Overcooked benchmark demonstrate that
CooT
outperforms strong baselines in both agent-agent and human-agent coordination. Through ablation studies, we show that
CooT
improves with limited interaction data, maintains robustness to suboptimal partner behavior, and is sensitive to the temporal structure of contextual inputs.

Our main contributions are the introduction of partner-centric in-context coordination, a training approach using best-response trajectories, and substantial empirical results in a challenging coordination domain. However, our work is limited by evaluation in a relatively structured environment, the lack of explicit modeling for mutual adaptation, and reliance on passive partner behavior. Future work can address these limitations by extending
CooT
to more complex, co-adaptive, and real-world multi-agent settings.

Impact Statements

This work advances coordination capabilities in multi-agent reinforcement learning through in-context learning. While it does not directly engage with sensitive data or high-risk applications, it may benefit future human-AI collaboration systems by enabling more adaptive, efficient, and personalized interactions. We do not foresee any immediate ethical concerns arising from this research.

Bothway Coordination.

Coordination Ring.

Counter Circuit.

Similar in shape to Coordination Ring, the Counter Circuit introduces a central, elongated table, creating narrow pathways that often cause congestion. This layout demands careful movement planning, as agents must avoid blocking one another. A common cooperative tactic involves staging onions in the center to streamline ingredient transfer.

Asymmetric Advantages.

This layout divides the kitchen into two largely self-contained workspaces while maintaining interdependence through asymmetrically shared resources. Each player has unique access to ingredients and serving stations, while two centrally located pots are jointly accessible. Notably, one player benefits from closer proximity to the serving station, encouraging the development of collaboration strategies to balance workload and improve efficiency.

Forced Coordination with Multi-Recipe.

Building upon the original Forced Coordination setup, this variation introduces tomatoes as an additional shared ingredient, placed on a central counter. It also incorporates multiple recipes, each with distinct preparation times and reward values. These elements demand tighter coordination, as players must adapt to more complex task distributions under time constraints.

Coordination Ring with Multi-Recipe.

This extended version of Coordination Ring includes tomatoes positioned near the serving area in the bottom-left corner, increasing task complexity. The added ingredient and recipe variety heighten the need for coordinated planning and amplify the importance of cooperation in fulfilling diverse orders.

HSP and MEP Training Datasets.

In our setting, we adopt behavior-preferring rewards, which refer to event-based biased reward functions. These rewards assign credit to specific events—such as picking up an onion or placing an ingredient into the pot. We assume that the behavioral requirements of deployment-time partners can be effectively modeled using this set of reward functions. For details on the reward specifications and policy training under these settings, we refer readers to ZSC-eval

Generation of Training Dataset.

To train our Coordination Transformer (
CooT
), we begin with a set of pretrained policy pairs consisting of biased partner agents and their corresponding best-response policies. For each policy pair, we first select one pair and sample
T
episodes of rollouts, which we use to construct a context
C
. This process is repeated
K
times per policy pair to generate
K
distinct contexts. For each context
C
, we then sample
L
different query states
s_{h}
from the rollout trajectories. Each query state, combined with its associated context
C
and the corresponding optimal action
\hat{a}
, forms a single training data of the form
(s_{h},C,\hat{a})
. Given a total of
M
policy pairs, this procedure results in a dataset containing
M\times K\times L
training examples. More detailed information can be found in Table
4
.

Online Deployment.

During online deployment,
CooT
operates with a dynamically updated context
C
to enable in-context adaptation. At the start of the first episode,
C
is initialized as an empty context. Since no prior trajectory is available, CooT selects actions based solely on the current state and the empty context. After completing the first episode, the entire trajectory is stored and used to construct a new context, which is then appended to
C
. As a result,
C
contains one populated trajectory and
T{-}1
empty slots (assuming a fixed context length of
T
). In subsequent episodes, CooT utilizes the current buffer to condition its action predictions, allowing it to adapt based on accumulated experience. The context is managed using a first-in, first-out (FIFO) policy, ensuring that the most recent
T
trajectories are always retained. This procedure is repeated iteratively throughout the evaluation phase to simulate continual adaptation in a coordination setting.

In addition to reporting the average reward
R
and average best-response proximity (BR_Prox, denoted as
B
) in Table
1
we include additional statistics in Table
10
to offer a more comprehensive evaluation of
CooT
compared to baselines. Specifically, we report the minimum, maximum, and interquartile range (IQR) of both
R
and
B
, along with their respective means within the IQR (denoted
\bar{R}_{\text{IQR}}
and
\bar{B}_{\text{IQR}}
).

These supplementary metrics are motivated by the observation that averages alone can be misleading in the presence of high variance across evaluation partners. The IQR captures the central 50% of the distribution, providing a more stable performance estimate. The mean within the IQR further reduces the influence of extreme values, serving as a robust indicator of central tendency. Additionally, reporting the min and max values reveals the full range of performance, offering insights into worst-case and best-case scenarios, especially in stochastic or multi-agent settings. Together, these statistics complement the main results, essential for evaluating real-world applicability.

Results on BC’s Demonstration Quality.

BC, especially those utilizing the complete set of demonstrations, generally achieve higher performance than agents trained on top-return trajectory subsets. This is somewhat surprising given that higher-return trajectories are typically assumed to be more informative. However, we hypothesize that in our setting, where all partners used for rollout training datasets have already converged to their final skill-levels through first-stage training, the benefit of selecting only the highest-return trajectories becomes limited. In particular, because diverse behaviour evaluation partners determine final performance, the diversity of encountered states during training plays a more critical role than marginal improvements in demonstration quality. Thus, retaining a wider range of trajectories likely enhances state coverage, leading to more robust performance.
