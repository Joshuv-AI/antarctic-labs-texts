Thermal Decomposition Mechanism and Product Distribution Control of Retired Wind Turbine Blades Catalyzed by Metals
Authors: Yingyue Teng, Zhengxin Song, Dingze Liu, Jingsi Sun, Yuting Gao, Jiacheng Zhu, Yinmin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyrolysis experiments on decommissioned wind turbine blades were conducted using a fixed-bed reactor to systematically study the compositional characteristics of pyrolysis products and achieve the regulation of high-value chemicals. The results show that the addition of Fe3+ catalyst did not change the release sequence of pyrolysis gases but promoted the cleavage of aliphatic side chains, leading to an increase in free H and small molecules. When the Fe3+ addition was 5 wt %, the H2 content in the pyrolysis gas increased to 1.86 times that of the sample without catalyst; at the same time, Fe3+ inhibited the decomposition of C–O bonds, reducing the production of CO2 to about one-third of the original amount. During the pyrolysis process, Fe3+ promoted the breakdown of large molecular chains, causing the decomposition of hydroxyl and olefinic compounds, thereby increasing the content of phenol in the liquid phase and demonstrating effective control over the composition and yield of tar. When the Fe3+ addition was 15 wt %, the phenol content reached 40%. The study indicates that the presence of Fe3+ promotes the formation of H2, phenolic compounds, and other aromatic compounds, providing a feasible route for the controlled production of high-value chemicals.

Introduction

The rapid development of the wind power industry has led to a substantial increase in the installed capacity of wind turbine blades (WTB), accompanied by a rapid accumulation of decommissioned WTB inventory. The standard service life of WTB typically ranges from 20 to 25 years. , In recent years, a large number of WTB commissioned in the early stages have gradually reached the upper limit of their design life and are thus facing the need for replacement. It is projected that by 2050, the total global quantity of decommissioned WTB will reach approximately 43 million tons, making the efficient and sustainable disposal and resource utilization of these blades a global challenge.

WTB are primarily composed of glass fiber-reinforced polymer composites, sandwich materials, adhesives, and other functional components. , Among these, fiber-reinforced polymer composites use epoxy resin as the matrix and glass fiber as the reinforcing phase, with the fiber content accounting for up to 75% of the total blade mass. Glass fiber or carbon fiber-reinforced thermosetting resin materials meet the mechanical and aerodynamic design requirements of blades during their service life, thanks to their excellent specific strength and favorable aerodynamic performance. , From the perspective of resource circulation, the compositional characteristics of such materials provide potential feasibility for the recycling, regeneration, and high-value utilization of decommissioned WTB. However, the current accumulation of large quantities of decommissioned WTB has exerted significant environmental pressure, which not only poses potential threats to the ecological environment , but also restricts the green development of the wind power industry throughout its entire life cycle. Notably, the fiber components in decommissioned WTB can be recycled and reused after appropriate treatment, which helps reduce resource consumption and production costs. In addition, the polymer fraction contains thermosetting matrices made of polyester, vinyl ester, and epoxy materials, featuring complex covalently cross-linked intermolecular polymer chains. , These polymers can be converted into high-value chemicals such as hydrogen (H2) and phenol, thereby offering possibilities for high-value resource utilization pathways.

In the field of traditional disposal of decommissioned WTB, landfilling and incineration are widely applied technical methods. For landfilling, wind turbine blades need to be crushed before being directly sent to landfills. Although incineration can achieve partial volume reduction, the mechanical properties of glass fibers are significantly degraded during the incineration process, failing to meet the requirements for secondary utilization, and ultimately still requiring disposal via landfilling. Due to the organic resin components contained in wind turbine blades, degradation and release of harmful gases are likely to occur during landfilling; , meanwhile, glass fibers are hardly biodegradable, and long-term landfilling not only occupies a large amount of land resources but also may pose risks of soil and groundwater pollution. To overcome the limitations of traditional disposal technologies, current recycling technologies mainly include mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, and pyrolysis. − Among these, pyrolysis is regarded as one of the most promising technologies for industrialization in the disposal of such composite solid wastes, owing to its relatively simple process, high treatment efficiency, and great potential for resource recovery. Pyrolysis of WTB facilitates the direct decomposition of the matrix without the need for mechanical layer separation. Importantly, waste plastics in the form of polymer foams and resins can be converted into useful value-added chemicals, which contributes to achieving circularity and making the recycling process more economical.

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on the application of pyrolysis in the high-value conversion of wastes. Park et al. performed pyrolysis of wastes at 700 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere and successfully produced hydrogen-rich gas with high yield. Kim et al. carried out pyrolysis experiments using HZSM-11 molecular sieve as a catalyst under a CO2 atmosphere, and the results showed that the calorific value of the recovered char was comparable to that of coal. In addition, studies on the performance of fibers recovered from WTB wastes have indicated a certain degree of decline in mechanical properties, with tensile strength decreasing by approximately 30% and flexural strength decreasing by about 12%. Zhang et al. reported the effect of oxidation time on the recovery rate of glass fibers from pyrolytic char, and demonstrated that oxidation at 550 °C for 40 min resulted in the formation of clean fibers. On the other hand, the liquid pyrolysis products (pyrolysis oil) of decommissioned WTB contain abundant organic components and possess high-value utilization potential. However, limited by issues such as low content of target components and complex composition, pyrolysis oil is currently used only as fuel for combustion heat generation in most cases, failing to achieve high-value development.

The introduction of catalysts can play a dual role in the pyrolysis process: on the one hand, it reduces the reaction activation energy, thereby lowering the temperature required for pyrolysis; on the other hand, it regulates the pyrolysis reaction pathway and improves the selectivity of high-value components in pyrolysis oil. Wang et al. conducted pyrolysis experiments on bisphenol an epoxy resin (BAEP) using iron-based catalysts with different concentrations. The results showed that iron not only significantly reduced the decomposition temperature of BAEP but also promoted the formation of gaseous products. Combined with Py-GCMS and XPS analysis, it was found that iron-based catalysts could promote the generation of light components (especially phenol), which could be further used as volatile products for the preparation of chemicals and reagents. In addition, Li et al. used NiO (10)/Al2O3 catalyst for the pyrolysis of waste WTB, which effectively improved the selectivity of phenol in the liquid products and enhanced the catalytic generation process of phenol. Among various catalysts, iron (Fe) has become a highly promising catalytic material due to its abundant reserves, low cost, and low toxicity, and it exhibits outstanding performance in promoting the cracking of resin components and increasing the yield of target products.

Based on the above, this study takes the basic compositional characteristics of decommissioned WTB as the foundation, systematically investigates their pyrolysis behavior in a fixed-bed reactor, and conducts qualitative and quantitative analysis of pyrolysis products (gas, liquid, and solid phases). It focuses on exploring the influence mechanism of metallic iron as a catalyst on the pyrolysis process of waste WTB, analyzing the compositional evolution laws of gas and liquid products from catalytic pyrolysis, and studying the changes in the micromorphology and crystal structure of pyrolysis residues (solid products). This work aims to provide new technical ideas and theoretical support for the green and efficient recycling of decommissioned wind turbine blades.

Experiments

Materials

WTB were obtained from wind turbine units that had reached the end of their service life. The blades were crushed using a Sample Preparation Grinder (5E-PCIX100), and the crushed samples were sieved with a standard sample sieve. Finally, blade samples with a particle size ranging from 200 to 400 meshes (74–38 μm) were selected and dried in an electric thermostatic blast drying oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The catalyst used was analytical-grade iron­(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O). Mixed samples of WTB and iron­(III) chloride were prepared by a mechanical method. Pyrolysis Process

The pyrolysis experimental setup is shown in Figure , which mainly includes a fixed-bed reactor, a cooling and drying device, and a gas chromatograph (GC). The GC used was model SP-3420A produced by Beijing Bei fen Ruili Analytical Instrument (Group) Co., Ltd. These components were connected via a condenser, a flowmeter, and a drying tube, enabling online analysis of the release amount, release rate, and composition of gaseous products.

Schematic of the pyrolysis reaction experimental setup for retired wind turbine blades.

A proper amount of sample was weighed and placed in a crucible inside the pyrolysis furnace. Argon (Ar) with a flow rate of 60 mL/min was continuously purged for 20 min to ensure the removal of air from the pipeline. Under a sustained Ar atmosphere, a program temperature controller was used to heat the pyrolysis furnace from room temperature to 800 °C, followed by a 2 h holding period, with a heating rate of 4 °C/min. The volatile components generated during pyrolysis were condensed to collect liquid products, and after removing tar and moisture through a drying tube, they entered the GC for analysis of the generation rates of H2, CO, CH4, and CO2 in the gaseous products.

Product Analysis Methods

Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) was used to study the pyrolysis characteristics of the blades. Gas chromatography (GC) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) were employed to analyze the gas and liquid components generated from pyrolysis. An IRTracer-100 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer produced by Shimadzu Corporation (Japan) was used to characterize the functional group composition and microstructural information of the solid pyrolysis products, with a scanning range of 4000–400 cm–1.

Results and Discussion

Thermogravimetric Analysis and Yield Distribution

Thermogravimetric Analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TG/DTG) was used to compare the thermal decomposition behavior of WTB raw materials and iron-doped samples, and the results are shown in Figure . The TG curves show that the initial pyrolysis weight loss temperature of the iron-doped samples is significantly advanced, and the residual weight at 800 °C decreases from about 71% for the raw material to about 66%, with a significant increase in total weight loss, indicating that iron doping can enhance the decomposition of organic components during pyrolysis. The DTG curves further reveal that iron doping significantly shifts the weight loss peak to a lower temperature region (from approximately 360 °C to approximately 344 °C). These results confirm that iron doping catalyzes the bond-breaking decomposition of epoxy resin, effectively reduces the activation energy of the pyrolysis reaction, accelerates the removal of organic components and the release of volatiles.

Gas Analysis

Figure shows the release rates (I) and cumulative amounts (II) of four main pyrolysis gases (H2, CO, CH4, CO2) during the pyrolysis process. The results indicated that CO2 was the main gaseous product of WTB pyrolysis, primarily derived from the cleavage of olefin and carboxylic acid functional groups. The presence of these functional groups in raw WTB was precharacterized by FTIR (4000–400 cm–1) and XPS C 1s analysis (see Section ), and is also a well-recognized structural feature of epoxy resin-based glass fiber reinforced composites for wind turbine bladesepoxy resin matrices contain ester bonds and unsaturated olefinic linkages in their cross-linked structure. H2 was generated from the cleavage of aliphatic side chains during pyrolysis, with an initial release temperature of approximately 190 °C. CH4 was the first gas produced during pyrolysis, mainly originating from the cracking of methyl and dimethyl functional groups in the blades, which has been confirmed by changes in the intensity of the C–H characteristic peaks in the FTIR spectra of solid pyrolysis products. The calculation of gas accumulation shows that CO2 accounts for the largest proportion in the pyrolysis gas, followed by H2.

The addition of Fe3+ as a catalyst led to a significant change in the release behavior of pyrolysis gases. Figure depicts the gas release rates under different Fe3+ addition dosages. As illustrated in Figure , the incorporation of the Fe3+ catalyst remarkably altered the release rates of the four primary pyrolysis gases, with distinct discrepancies observed among different catalyst loadings. Specifically, when the Fe3+ addition dosage reached 5 wt %, the H2 release rate peaked at 0.0067 mmol/(min·g), which was 1.54 times that of the noncatalyzed sample. This result indicated that the introduction of Fe3+ facilitated the cracking of macromolecular side chains. Meanwhile, Fe3+ exhibited an inhibitory effect on the release of CO, CH4, and CO2 to varying degrees.

During the pyrolysis of decommissioned wind turbine blades, the pyrolysis gas is mainly derived from the cleavage and recombination of functional groups such as epoxy groups, ether bonds, and ester bonds. The generation of H2 is primarily associated with the polymerization of hydrogen free radicals, as well as the condensation dehydrogenation, cleavage, and aromatization of aromatic rings. Fe3+ promotes the cleavage of aliphatic side chains, which is directly confirmed by the significant decrease in the intensity of the C–H characteristic peaks (2800–3000 cm–1) in the FTIR spectra of the solid products (Figure ) and the increase in the relative atomic percentage of C–C/C–H in the XPS C 1s analysis (Table ), consistent with the increased H2 release. At the same time, Fe3+ inhibits the cleavage of C–O and CO bonds, thereby reducing the generation of gases including CO and CO2. This inhibitory effect is reflected in the increased intensity of the C–O characteristic peaks (1200–1000 cm–1) in the FTIR spectra and the changes in the relative atomic percentages of CO/COOR and C–O/C–OH in the XPS C 1s analysis (Table ), directly indicating the stabilizing effect of Fe3+ on C–O and other bonds.

Characterization of Solid Products

FTIR Analysis

The FTIR spectra of the solid pyrolysis products of WTB are presented in Figure , which can be divided into five characteristic regions with specific wave numbers and functional group assignments: 3675–3300 cm–1 (hydroxyl and phenolic – OH), 2800–3000 cm–1 (C–H stretching vibration of aliphatic groups), 1650–1475 cm–1 (CO, CC), 1200–1000 cm–1 (C–O bonds), and 550–300 cm–1 (Si–O–Si stretching of glass fiber, inorganic components). After the addition of Fe3+, the intensity of the C–H characteristic peak decreased significantly, indicating that the Fe3+ component promoted the decomposition of aliphatic functional groups during pyrolysis, which was consistent with the release behavior of H2 in the pyrolysis gas. The intensity of the characteristic peaks corresponding to CO and CC functional groups increased, demonstrating that Fe3+ exerted an inhibitory effect on the cleavage of these functional groups. In addition, the intensity of oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., alcohols, phenols, ethers, or esters) increased, which revealed that Fe3+ inhibited the decomposition of CO and C–O bonds to a certain extent. This observation was consistent with the release characteristics of CO and CO2 in the high-temperature stage. Notably, Fe3+ had no significant impact on the variations of inorganic compounds.

XPS Analysis

Figure shows the XPS C 1s fitting results of decommissioned wind turbine blades and their corresponding solid pyrolysis products. As indicated in Figure , the carbon bonding configurations in decommissioned wind turbine blades and their solid pyrolysis products can be categorized into three types: aromatic units and their substituents (C–C/C–H, 284.8 eV), ether or phenolic hydroxyl groups (C–O/C–OH, 286.1 eV), and carbonyl or ester groups (COOR). Among these configurations, C–C/C–H is the dominant bonding form of carbon.

XPS spectra and C 1s fitting results of pyrolysis samples of decommissioned wind turbine blades.

Table presents the XPS C 1s fitting parameters of WTB solid pyrolysis products. As shown in Table , after the addition of iron components, the contents of C–C/C–H and CO/COOR configurations in the solid pyrolysis products both increased. Fe doping can effectively catalyze the deoxygenation of C–O/C–OH type oxygen-containing functional groups, promoting their transformation into CO/COOR type functional groups, thereby enhancing the stability of the C–C/C–H carbon skeleton, which is highly consistent with the increased disorder of the carbon structure observed in Raman spectra and the intensified decomposition of organic components indicated by thermogravimetric analysis. Meanwhile, Fe inhibits the decomposition of phenolic hydroxyl groups, ether bonds, methoxy groups, and oxygen-containing heterocycles, resulting in reduced CO and CO2 contents. This result aligns with the FTIR analysis and the release characteristics of CH4, CO, and CO2 gases.

Raman Analysis

Raman spectroscopy was used to characterize the carbon structure of pyrolytic solids with different Fe doping amounts, and the results are shown in Figure . All samples exhibited characteristic peaks at ∼1350 cm–1 (D band) and ∼1580 cm–1 (G band). Quantitative analysis showed that WTB-800 °C had an I D/I G value of 1.58; when the Fe doping amount increased to 5 wt %, I D/I G rose to 2.03; further increasing to 10 wt %, I D/I G reached 2.33. With the increase of Fe doping, the relative intensity of the D band was significantly enhanced and the I D/I G ratio continued to rise, indicating that Fe doping can catalyze the cleavage of the carbon framework, introduce more structural defects and edge sites, and this disordered regulation effect is strengthened with higher Fe loading, providing a rich structure of active sites for the catalytic application of pyrolytic solid products.

Analysis of Liquid Products

The liquid pyrolysis products were analyzed via gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), and their total ion chromatograms (TICs) are illustrated in Figure . The liquid products derived from the pyrolysis of decommissioned wind turbine blades were mainly composed of phenolic and aromatic compounds, along with aliphatic and furan-based substances. The results demonstrated that Fe3+ facilitated the cracking of macromolecular organic chains in WTB, leading to an increase in the yield of small-molecule substances.

Distribution of liquid pyrolysis products of decommissioned wind turbine blades.

Table presents the relative contents of liquid product components under different conditions. As indicated in Table , the organic compounds with relatively high contents in the liquid products mainly include phenol, 4-isopropylphenol, 2-methylphenol, bisphenol A, and aromatic hydrocarbons, followed by other aromatic hydrocarbons and aliphatic hydrocarbons. Analysis of the organic compound contents in pyrolysis tar under different conditions shows that phenol has the highest relative content, followed by 4-isopropylphenol. Phenol is primarily generated by the direct cracking of hydroxyl-containing benzene structures. In addition, alkyl groups and ether bonds break at high temperatures to produce active free radicals or allyl intermediates, which can then form phenol through hydrogen migration, rearrangement, or reaction with hydrogen. The Fe3+ catalyst promotes the generation of phenolic compounds; specifically, when 10 wt % Fe is added, the relative content of phenols reaches the maximum, which is 2.03 times that of the raw material pyrolysis without catalyst.

Tar Analysis Results

4-Isopropylphenol is the second most abundant substance in the liquid products after phenol, and it is mainly produced by the cleavage of bisphenol A structures as well as the rearrangement and cracking of substituents on the benzene ring. Its generation pathway is similar to that of 4-isopropenylphenol, but it tends to form saturated side chain structures at higher temperatures. Under the catalytic action of Fe3+, the content of bisphenol A in the liquid products decreases significantly (from 11.37% to 0.95%), while the contents of phenol and 4-isopropylphenol increase accordingly, which indicates that Fe3+ promotes the deep cracking of macromolecular bisphenol A into small-molecule phenolic compounds.

It was observed in the experiment that the composition of pyrolysis tar fluctuates within a certain range, depending on the amount of catalyst used and the cracking conditions. This phenomenon may be attributed to isomerization reactions at high temperatures as well as experimental errors. At the same time, WTB, as a typical fiber-reinforced composite material, has a complex resin organic structure, and there may be irregular changes in the liquid-phase pyrolysis products, making the composition of the liquid-phase tar complex. In this experiment, with the addition of an iron catalyst, the general pattern remained unchanged, and its main chemical, phenol, proved to be controllable. Therefore, in subsequent studies, it is necessary to further optimize the experimental conditions.

Mechanism of Fe-Catalyzed Pyrolysis of Retired Wind Turbine Blades

Based on the previous Sections gas analysis, 3.3 solid product characterization, and 3.4 liquid product analysis results, the catalytic mechanism of Fe3+ in the pyrolysis process of retired wind turbine blades can be clarified: Fe does not change the order of pyrolysis gas release, but it participates in the reaction pathway through the effect of “product-directed regulation”. Specifically, as a Lewis acid site, it promotes the cleavage of aliphatic C–H bonds, − releasing hydrogen radicals (•H). With the addition of 5 wt % Fe3+, the cumulative yield of H2 reaches 3.013 mmol/g, which is 1.86 times that of the sample without catalyst. Meanwhile, it forms coordination bonds with oxygen atoms in the resin, stabilizing C–O and CO bonds and suppressing their excessive cracking, As shown in Figure (I). Consequently, the CO2 yield is reduced to one-third of the original, accompanied by corresponding decreases in CO and CH4 yields. Figure (II) illustrates that hydrogen radicals further reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, promoting side-chain cleavage of macromolecules such as bisphenol A. Combined with the hydrogen migration effect of •H radicals, this facilitates the breaking of ether bonds and alkyl substituents on benzene rings, thus enhancing the formation of small-molecule phenols, in agreement with the XRD results. When the Fe3+ addition reaches 10 wt %, the relative content of phenol reaches 40.24%, 2.03 times that of the catalyst-free sample. This ultimately achieves a synergistic enhancement in high-value products such as H2 and phenols.

Conclusions

FeCl3·6H2O effectively catalyzed the pyrolysis of decommissioned wind turbine blades. The addition of 5 wt % Fe increased the H2 yield by 1.86 times and reduced CO2 generation to one-third of that of the uncatalyzed sample. Meanwhile, adding 10 wt % Fe maximized the phenol content in the liquid products (40.24%) by promoting the cleavage of macromolecular chains. Solid-phase analysis results were consistent with the trends observed in the gas and liquid phases, providing evidence for the catalytic effect of iron, while kinetic analysis showed that the activation energy of the reaction decreased after the addition of iron, confirming the catalytic role of iron on WTB. This study quantitatively revealed the regulatory effect of Fe on the three-phase yields of WTB pyrolysis and identified the optimal catalyst loading for high-value products; it achieved highly selective production of phenols using a low-cost iron-based catalyst and optimized the existing catalyst system; it also confirmed that the glass fiber structure remained intact, providing experimental evidence for the high-value utilization of pyrolysis products and the secondary recycling of glass fibers. This offers a feasible approach for the high-value resource utilization of retired wind turbine blades.
