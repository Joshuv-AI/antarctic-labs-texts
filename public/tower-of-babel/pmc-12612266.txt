Early detection of G2SCH through machine learning analysis of physical examination metrics
Authors: Xiying Huang, Ruizi Lin, Yixin Xiao, Zihan Ma, Hongxia Xu, Yulian Wu, Xiawei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Grade 2 subclinical hypothyroidism (G2SCH) is associated with an increased risk of various diseases but is rarely detected before symptom onset. This study aims to develop a machine learning-based prediction model for G2SCH using routine physical examination data. 57,539 samples were collected from one institution for training and 33,880 from another one for testing. Three feature-selecting methods were employed, while five machine learning algorithms were utilized. SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) index of selected features were calculated. Area under curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity were used to evaluate models. Our results demonstrate that the logistic regression model, using features selected by LASSO under a 65:35 SMOTENC ratio, achieved robust predictive performance with an AUC of 0.870 (95% CI: 0.834, 0.906), sensitivity of 86.8%, and specificity of 70.8%. We developed a high-performance machine learning model for G2SCH prediction based on physical examination data, enabling timely referral and appropriate treatment of G2SCH patients.

Introduction

Grade 2 Subclinical hypothyroidism (G2SCH) is characterized by normal concentrations of free triiodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4), alongside elevated levels of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) exceeding 10 mU/L. The prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism varies between 3% and 20%, with a higher incidence observed in female and elderly populations1,2. In China, the prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism is about 16.7%, of which G2SCH constitutes about 10%3,4. Clinical studies have demonstrated that G2SCH is associated with an increased risk of progression to overt hypothyroidism, as well as a higher incidence of cardiovascular diseases and metabolic alterations, such as impaired liver and kidney function5–9. Recent evidence has underscored the clinical relevance of G2SCH, prompting updated guidelines to include specific treatment recommendations10. However, current guidelines do not endorse routine thyroid screening in asymptomatic individuals without risk factors2. This poses a significant detection gap, as large observational studies reveal 4-7% of Western populations have undiagnosed hypothyroidism, with 80% of these cases being subclinical11. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more accessible screening methods to address this clinical challenge.

Over the past decade, the exponential growth of digital information in electronic health records (EHRs) including physical examination results has enabled researchers to leverage these data for diverse clinical informatics applications12. For instance, Zhong Liu et al. utilized three years of physical examination data from 136,713 individuals to investigate the specific health impacts of COVID-19 infection13. Similarly, Ioannis Kavakiotis et al. applied machine learning to diabetes diagnosis14, while Kun Lv et al. employed machine learning to assess the utility of routine physical examination indicators for stratifying diabetes risk15. Drawing on these examples, we hypothesize that routinely collected physical examination parameters could similarly enable early identification of G2SCH. This approach has the potential to address current detection gaps and improve the identification of subclinical thyroid dysfunction in asymptomatic populations.

The lack of early diagnostic tools for asymptomatic individuals with G2SCH presents a significant clinical challenge, as timely detection could enable preventive interventions and reduce long-term complications. Existing machine learning studies on thyroid disorders are often limited by the use of single algorithms, fixed sampling strategies, and narrow feature selection approaches, which can reduce model generalizability and clinical utility16–18. To address these scientific and clinical gaps, this study was designed to develop a predictive model using routine physical examination indicators that are widely available, even in resource-limited settings. This approach is particularly valuable in underserved areas, such as rural regions where access to thyroid function tests may be limited. By focusing on these accessible indicators and integrating multiple sampling ratios, various machine learning algorithms, and rigorous feature selection methods, we aimed to construct a robust and clinically interpretable model for early G2SCH identification, facilitating early detection and intervention in populations where comprehensive thyroid testing may not be readily available.

The features selected through various methods were as follows: Univariate analysis identified AGE, Hb, RBC, SBP, SEX, Ultrasound, AST, Cr, and Albumin. LASSO selected Ultrasound, RBC, HDL, GLU, WBC, AGE, Albumin, BMI, and TCH. mRMR identified GLU, Albumin, DBP, Cr, TCH, WBC, ALT, AGE, and Ultrasound. Different SMOTENC ratios, including 90:10, 65:35, and 50:50, were utilized in the analysis.

The performance of the five machine learning methods (LR, RF, SVM, XGBoost, and NN) was compared by AUC, sensitivity and sensitivity. The best model of each method was selected and the ROC curve of the model was shown in Fig. 2a–c. The logistic regression (LR) model using univariate-selected features at 90:10 ratio demonstrated strong performance (AUC = 0.866, 95% CI:0.825–0.907; sensitivity = 83.0%; specificity = 76.4%), outperforming neural network (NN, AUC = 0.819) and XGBoost (AUC = 0.745) models at this ratio. Subgroup analysis

Given the strong association between ultrasound findings (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis or others) and G2SCH, we performed a subgroup analysis was conducted focusing on patients with ultrasonographic evidence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (n = 4350). Among these individuals, the clinical features most strongly correlated with the presence of G2SCH were identified as age, BMI, ALT, AST, Cr, and TG. The distributions of these key variables between groups were visualized using violin plots (Supplementary Fig. 1), which revealed distinct patterns and central tendencies associated with G2SCH status in this specific population.

Error analysis

Meanwhile, we extracted the samples that were wrongly classified in the best model, and analyzed the distribution of different features in the wrongly classified samples and the correctly classified samples (Supplementary Fig. 2). We utilized the Kruskal-Wallis test to determine if the differences among the four groups (True positive, False negative, True negative, False positive) were significant. The analysis revealed that RBC, AGE, GLU, and Albumin showed significant differences among the groups (p < 2.2e-16).

Discussion

G2SCH has been well-documented to adversely affect health2,19–24. Despite its clinical significance, research on the early diagnosis of G2SCH remains limited. Addressing this gap, we developed machine learning models to predict G2SCH utilizing a dataset comprising 57,539 physical examination records, and the results were validated on a real and independent dataset comprising 33, 880 physical examination records. Drawing on insights from Hasanin et al. regarding class imbalance in medical data25, we employed the SMOTENC method to balance the data, experimenting with three balancing ratios: 90:10, 65:35, and 50:50. We further used five machine learning algorithms in combination with three feature selection methods. Our results demonstrated that the logistic regression model, using features selected by LASSO under a 65:35 SMOTENC ratio, achieved the best predictive performance. We identified age, gender, Hb, RBC, ultrasound findings, AST, Cr, and albumin as key predictors, with ultrasound findings, age, and RBC showing the strongest effects. Our study demonstrates the potential of using routine physical examination data combined with optimized machine learning approaches for detecting asymptomatic G2SCH, which is particularly beneficial in underserved regions where access to thyroid function tests may be limited.

Among all combinations of machine learning algorithms, balancing ratios, and feature selection methods, the LR model using LASSO-selected features at a 65:35 balancing ratio demonstrated the best performance for predicting G2SCH. After simplifying the model by excluding BMI and TCH, its performance remained comparable to the original model, with only a slight decrease in specificity from 77.4% to 76.6%. This simplified model is particularly suitable for use in underserved areas where measuring blood lipid levels like TCH is challenging. Given the strong performance of models using LASSO-selected features, we reintegrated samples with non-core missing data and remodeled. The results showed that at a 65:35 ratio, the optimal model achieved the AUC of 0.870 (95% CI: 0.834-0.906), with sensitivity and specificity of 86.8% and 70.8%, respectively. The 90:10 ratio model also performed well, with an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.838-0.908), sensitivity of 84.9%, and specificity of 73.7%. Although its AUC was slightly higher, it showed lower sensitivity, which is valuable in clinical settings where physicians aim to identify high-risk populations for G2SCH. They can then order thyroid tests or refer these patients to better-equipped facilities. It’s worth noting that the choice between models depends on the specific clinical context. In situations where sensitivity is prioritized to ensure that as many high-risk individuals as possible are identified, the 65:35 ratio model might be preferable despite its slightly lower AUC. Our study provides flexibility in model selection based on the specific needs and resource availability of different healthcare settings.

According to the results obtained by SHAP, Ultrasound findings, age and RBC had the greatest influence on the prediction model, which was consistent with the results of univariate analysis. Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was specifically classified as a distinct category in ultrasound results due to its well-documented pathophysiological relationship with G2SCH, where the autoimmune inflammatory process characteristic of Hashimoto’s disease frequently leads to progressive thyroid dysfunction that manifests as subclinical hypothyroidism26,27. The chronic lymphocytic infiltration and gradual destruction of thyroid tissue in Hashimoto’s patients often results in compromised thyroid reserve, making them particularly susceptible to developing G2SCH28. The G2SCH group exhibited significantly older age (9.62 years higher than non-G2SCH), reflecting the well-documented progression of thyroid dysfunction with aging. While physiological hypothyroidism may resolve spontaneously in some elderly individuals, G2SCH rarely normalizes without intervention, underscoring the clinical value of our model for guiding elderly patient management19,26. Notably, females showed 12.53% higher G2SCH prevalence at a younger average age (52.14 years) than males, aligning with established gender disparities in subclinical hypothyroidism24,29,30. The observed reduction in RBC count (mean decrease of 0.45×10^12/L) and hemoglobin levels (mean decrease of 12.3 g/L) in G2SCH patients reflects a direct hematopoietic impact of thyroid hormone deficiency. This occurs through multiple mechanisms: impaired erythropoietin production by renal cells, reduced bone marrow responsiveness to erythropoietin, and diminished oxygen consumption leading to secondary erythroid suppression31. Other metabolic alterations included elevated AST from disrupted hepatic metabolism, lower albumin from impaired protein synthesis, and higher creatinine reflecting reduced renal perfusion—all consistent with thyroid hormone’s multisystem regulatory role22,23,25,29,32. These findings collectively reinforce G2SCH’s association with metabolic syndrome33, while cardiovascular risk correlations remain controversial2,21,34,35. The model’s ability to capture these pathophysiological relationships supports its clinical utility for early detection.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to develop and validate a predictive model for G2SCH using routine physical examination data. G2SCH is clinically significant as it is more likely to progress and carries an elevated risk of cardiovascular and neurological diseases. Early diagnosis and intervention can slow its progression to overt hypothyroidism and prevent complications, which is particularly important for the elderly26. In remote or underdeveloped areas, as well as community health centers with limited medical resources, thyroid function tests are often unavailable. Our model addresses this gap by relying solely on routine physical examination data, which is widely accessible even in settings where tests like TSH are not readily available. This aligns with guidelines that recommend screening high-risk populations rather than universal screening for the general population. Moreover, the results of this study show great potential for clinical applications, particularly in the form of websites or mobile applications. These tools may facilitate easy access for healthcare providers to predict G2SCH using routine physical examination data, making early detection and intervention more feasible, especially in resource-constrained environments.

Several limitations of our work need to be acknowledged. First, this study’s data represents a cross-sectional snapshot and cannot exclude other causes. The diagnosis of G2SCH requires excluding other causes of physiological, artificial, or transient elevations in serum TSH, and retesting after 3-6 months. Second, thyroid function testing is widely available in secondary and tertiary hospitals in China, and some community health centers also have the capability. Considering the cost of referrals for remote hospitals and health centers, equipping them with thyroid function testing instruments may be more practical. Third, the intervention for subclinical hypothyroidism, including G2SCH, remains controversial36. Existing studies support thyroid hormone intervention for elderly G2SCH patients24 but do not recommend intervention for young individuals with G2SCH or women who are pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or postpartum10,37. Therefore, clinical decisions should consider the patient’s age, gender, and pregnancy status. Finally, this study’s sample may not represent the epidemiology of G2SCH in other regions.

In conclusion, we developed a machine learning model based on physical examination data, which demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity. This model provides a valuable reference for doctors and facilities lacking thyroid function testing capabilities, facilitating timely referrals and appropriate treatment for patients with asymptomatic G2SCH.

Methods

Data collection and processing

The data used in this study were sourced from two independent campuses of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University: Jiefang Road Campus and Binjiang Campus, both of which provided physical examination records of annual examinations from 2019 to 2020. Data cleaning in Fig. 4 included screening of data according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, removal of data with excessive missing values and imputation of missing values. We excluded individuals with overt hypothyroidism (FT4 < 9.01 pg/ml and TSH > 4.94mIU/L), those receiving thyroid disease related treatment (including total or partial thyroidectomy, radioiodine therapy, and thyroid hormone therapy), those lacking thyroid function tests, or those with >20% missing data of all available features. For samples with no more than 20% missing data, we employed Multiple Imputation (MI) via the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) algorithm, implemented through the mice package in R (version 4.4.3) (number of imputations = 5; method = “pmm”). MI is a robust approach for handling missing data that overcomes limitations of single imputation by accounting for imputation uncertainty38. Subsequently, the data were classified based on predefined criteria. The reference ranges for FT3 and FT4 were 2.43–6.01 pg/ml and 9.01–19.05 pg/ml, respectively. Generalized G2SCH was defined as FT3 and FT4 within the normal range and TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L (Fig. 4a). Based on these criteria, G2SCH samples were identified and grouped. Ultrasound results (Ultrasound) were categorized based on standardized reports, with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis identified as a distinct category given its established association with G2SCH2,24. Thyroid enlargement, nodules and no abnormalities were included in the “Others” category of Ultrasound. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). The dataset from the Jiefang Road Campus was designated as the training set, while that from the Binjiang Campus served as the test set. The dataset included 20 features: gender (SEX), age (AGE), BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), albumin (Albumin), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glucose (GLU), creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), total cholesterol (TCH), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), platelets (Plt) and Ultrasound results. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by Zhejiang University School of medicine. The patients/participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

Model construction

To develop an effective predictive model, we employed five machine learning algorithms: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), neural network (NN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Logistic regression is a variant of linear regression used for classification tasks, converting output into probabilities via a sigmoid function39. Random forest consists of multiple decision trees, each trained on a random subset of samples and features, reducing the risk of overfitting and enhancing model accuracy and stability through voting40. Support vector machine classifies samples by maximizing the margin between different classes in the feature space41. For nonlinear data, SVM can utilize linear, polynomial, or radial basis function kernels. NN mimic the decision-making process of the human brain, characterized by interconnected networks of simple processing units (neurons), parallel processing, and adaptive learning42. XGBoost, a scalable tree boosting system, is widely used by data scientists and has achieved state-of-the-art results in numerous applications43.

Data analysis was conducted using R (version 4.4.3). The LR model was built using the “glm” function from the stats package, the RF model using the ranger function from the “ranger” package, the NN model using the nnet function from the “nnet” package (The neural network architecture follows a 9-6-1 structure, comprising 9 input neurons, one hidden layer with 6 neurons, and 1 output neuron for the binary outcome G2SCH), the SVM model using the train function from the “caret” package (kernel = “linear”), and the XGBoost model using the “xgboost” package. Given the significant class imbalance in the dataset, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique for Nominal and Continuous (SMOTENC) algorithm to balance the training set (neighbors = 5; skip = TRUE; trained = FALSE; over_ratio values correspond to different class balancing targets: 0.111 (10:90), 0.538 (35:65), and 1 (50:50)). SMOTENC, a variant of SMOTE, effectively balances both continuous and categorical variables and has demonstrated strong performance in previous studies. Balancing ratios or class-weight were proved that have an influence on model results44. Thus, we tested three balancing ratios: 90:10, 65:35, and 50:50, implemented using the recipes and “themis” packages in R (version 4.4.3). Finally, Z-score normalization was applied to standardize all data based on the mean and standard deviation of the training set.

Feature selection and model evaluation

To enhance the model’s performance, Univariate analysis, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) was employed for variable screening. In LASSO procedure, covariates were constructed as a matrix x from the first 20 columns of the training set, with the outcome variable y set as G2SCH. The model was trained using glmnet with family = “binomial” and alpha = 1. The optimal lambda value (s) was set to 8.430e-06, corresponding to the minimum of the computed lambda sequence. Coefficients were extracted using predict () with type = “coefficients”. mRMR’ s configuration included: target_indices = 21, feature_count = 9, and feature_names = c (“SEX”, “AGE”, “SBP”, “DBP”, “BMI”, “Albumin”, “ALT”, “AST”, “GLU”, “Cr”, “UA”, “TCH”, “TG”, “HDL”, “LDL”, “WBC”, “Hb”, “RBC”, “Plt”, “Ultrasound”, “G2SCH”). The method was set to “classic” with solution_count = 1. In the univariate analysis, variables with two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Continuous variables that conformed to normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were tested by t-test, continuous variables that did not conform to normal distribution or had uneven variance were tested by wilcoxon test, and categorical variables were tested by chi-square test. In order to clarify the practical significance of these differences, we calculated Cohen’ s d of the features. Cohen classified effect sizes as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8)45. Bonferroni correction was applied to all univariable comparisons. LASSO minimizes the absolute sum of the coefficients (L1 regularization), and can significantly reduce the variance and can also perform variable selection. mRMR is a particularly fast feature selection method for finding a set of both relevant and complementary features46. To rank the importance of features, we calculated the SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values for the selected features. SHAP assigns an importance score to each feature based on its contribution to the model’s predictions47. We utilized visualization tools such as bee swarm plots and waterfall plots to illustrate the SHAP values. Importance of features were calculated in models.

We applied 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrapping to assess the predictive performance of the models. Researches showed that using Youden index to calculate the threshold of confusion matrix can improve the correctness of evaluation. Thus, we used the threshold based on the Youden index to compute the predictive performance of the models. We used “epiR” package in R (version 4.4.3) to evaluate models. Youden index, Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, Positive predictive value, Negative predictive value, Positive likelihood ratio, Negative likelihood ratio, the False Positive Rate, the False Negative Rate, the False Discovery Rate, the False Omission Rate and Diagnostic odds ratio were calculated.
