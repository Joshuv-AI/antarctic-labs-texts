Examining the experiences of donor relatives in the organ donation decision process: a qualitative study in Türkiye
Authors: Dilruba Izguden, Abdullah Yasit, Mehmet Zeki Er
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing donor consent rates in countries with low post-mortem donation rates such as Türkiye is paramount. This study explores the experiences of donor relatives in Türkiye who approved post mortem organ donation after brain death, aiming to identify emotional, cognitive, and ethical factors influencing their decisions and post-decision experiences, to inform strategies for increasing consent rates in developing countries.MethodsThis qualitative study involved semi-structured interviews with eight donor relatives who consented to organ donation, and data were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsTwo main fields were identified: (1) Factors influencing the donation decision, with themes: (a) decision-related factors, (b) health system factors, and (c) individual factors; and (2) post-decision experiences, with themes: (a) satisfaction with the decision, (b) curiosity about the recipient, (c) meaning of donation, and (d) creating donation awareness.ConclusionEmpathic motivations, previously stated wishes, the attitude of healthcare professionals, and understanding brain death influence the decisions of donors’ relatives. Trust in healthcare professionals helps reduce ethical uncertainty, contributing to a more confident and informed decision-making process. The study found that concerns during the decision-making process and uncertainties such as ‘What will happen next?’ were replaced by spiritual satisfaction and contentment after the decision. The research results provide evidence-based insights into the organ donation decision-making process and offer recommendations for improving public perception and increasing consent rates.Keywords: Organ donation, Brain death, Transplantation, Qualitative research

Introduction

Organ transplantation is regarded as one of the most significant achievements of modern medicine, offering a life-saving treatment option for patients with end-stage organ failure and substantially improving their quality of life [1]. Although organ donation plays a critical role in improving individuals’ health and quality of life, its rates remain below desired levels globally and vary significantly across countries [2]. Increasing organ donation rates requires identifying the factors that influence donation processes and developing targeted strategies to address them. Key determinants affecting donation decisions include the level of knowledge, societal and religious beliefs, the attitude of healthcare professionals, and legal regulations [3, 4]. Notably, widespread misconceptions and lack of information within society constitute major barriers to organ donation [5, 6]. Although the Presidency of Religious Affairs in Türkiye has explicitly stated that organ donation is permissible in Islam, religious concerns remain prevalent among individuals, including misbeliefs that donation is religiously impermissible, worries that organs could be misused for sinful acts, concerns about bodily integrity, and beliefs regarding the sanctity of the deceased body [7–9]. These misconceptions negatively affect donation rates and hinder efforts to reach the desired levels. Various strategies have been implemented to increase donation rates, including correcting misinformation in society, providing accurate information, and raising public awareness. Visual media, educational programs, the support of trusted community leaders, and efforts to improve health literacy can be effective in promoting positive attitudes toward organ donation [10–14].

In addition to the measures mentioned to increase donation rates, it is particularly important to enhance post mortem organ donation. This form of transplantation offers a significant advantage over living donation, as it allows for the transplantation of a greater number of organs [15]. However, despite high post mortem donation rates in developed countries, these rates remain low in developing countries such as Türkiye, where living donor transplants are more commonly performed [16, 17]. In 2023, the deceased donor rate was only 3.58 per million population in Türkiye, compared to 49.4 in Spain and 48 in the United States [18]. These low rates of post mortem donation in Türkiye pose a serious problem for patients awaiting organ transplantation and necessitate the development of effective strategies to increase donation rates [8, 16, 19].

Globally, organ donation from deceased individuals is generally regulated under two consent models: opt-in (explicit/expressed consent) and opt-out (presumed/assumed consent). In accordance with Law No. 2238 and current practices, Türkiye implements an extended opt-in (“extended consent”) regime, which requires informing relatives and obtaining their consent even if a donor card is present [20–25]. For this reason, in Türkiye, even if individuals provide consent for organ donation while alive, organ transplantation cannot be performed after brain death without the approval of their family members [26]. This results in organ donation consent being granted in only approximately 22.7% of total brain death cases [27]. Increasing the consent rates of donor relatives is of great importance for enhancing post mortem organ transplantation rates. International qualitative studies with families who declined deceased donation have documented core refusal themes. These include uncertainty or disbelief regarding brain death, concerns over bodily integrity and religious or cultural norms, mistrust of institutions, and inadequate timing or communication during the request conversation [28, 29]. Complementary Türkiye-based accounts from organ procurement coordinators likewise highlight refusal-related barriers in the local healthcare context [30]. Building on this literature, we purposively focused on relatives who consented to donation in order to map how consent is actually achieved—identifying facilitators and post-decision trajectories—and thereby provide practice-oriented evidence directly relevant to increasing post mortem donation in Türkiye.

Organ donation is lifesaving for patients awaiting transplantation, yet it represents an extremely challenging experience for donor families. Individuals who have just lost a loved one not only cope with their grief but also face the pressure of making a donation decision within a short time frame. This decision-making process, when combined with the mourning period, can become a traumatic experience in itself [31–33]. At the same time, several studies report positive dimensions for some families, such as meaning-making and a sense of legacy/continuing bonds, relief or pride that others were helped, and even elements of post-traumatic growth, alongside the burdens of acute grief [34–37]. Recognizing both the challenges and potential positive outcomes highlights the complexity of donation experiences. Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to explore in depth the experiences of donor relatives, who hold the final decision-making authority in the organ transplantation process in Türkiye. Specifically, the study aims to explore the factors influencing the decision of donor relatives during the decision-making process and their post-decision experiences. By doing so, the study aims to reduce uncertainties for individuals who may face similar situations. A deeper understanding of the experiences of donor relatives is also crucial for increasing post mortem donor rates, raising public awareness about organ donation, reducing barriers, and informing strategies to improve the overall donation process.

Methods

Participants

Donor relatives who consented to donate were selected as participants in this study. This study adopted a qualitative research approach to explore their experiences in the organ donation decision-making process, as they play a critical role in enabling patients on the waiting list for a transplantation to survive. Participants who consented to donation were first-degree relatives (spouses, siblings, parents, and children). The study investigates their experiences at the intersection of the organ donation consent request made within hours of acute grief and loss, how this context shaped the decision-making process, and their thoughts regarding the post-decision process.

In light of this information, donor relatives were selected through criterion purposive sampling. This method focuses on reaching individuals who can provide rich and relevant information on the topic being studied [38]. The inclusion criteria for this research were as follows: (a) being a donor relative who consented to organ donation, (b) the donation decision having been made between 2020 and 2024, (c) the organ donation having taken place at the university hospital where the study was conducted and (d) understanding the purpose of the research and being willing to participate. Participation of donor relatives in this study was challenging, as it required them to recall traumatic memories associated with the grieving process. However, considering the low number of post mortem organ donations and in order to minimize recall bias and ensure the reliability of participants experiences, it was deemed appropriate to include only those whose donation decision had occurred within the past five years. Participants were selected from among donor relatives whose organ donation procedures had taken place at a university hospital located in southern Türkiye, where the study was conducted. The reason for choosing this hospital was that one of the researchers was serving as an Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator at the institution, which facilitated access to participants. With permission obtained from the hospital where the donation procedures were carried out, the researcher contacted potential participants, informed them about the purpose and scope of the study. In order to ensure confidentiality, participants were identified using numerical codes throughout the study.

Research design and process

Semi-structured interviews were employed to elicit in-depth narratives, and thematic analysis was used to identify and interpret meaningful patterns within the data. The interview questions were developed based on a review of the relevant literature [39, 40].To enhance content validity and contextual relevance, the interview guide underwent expert review by a faculty member in the Department of Health Management, an Organ and Tissue Transplant Coordinator external to the research team, and the study-site Hospital Administrator. The semi-structured interview form included four main questions: (a) Could you share your experiences regarding the organ donation decision-making process?, (b) What were the main factors that influenced your decision?, (c) What did you experience after making the donation decision?, and (d) How do you feel about your decision now?

Participation in the study was voluntary, and the use of the collected data was approved by the participants with full assurance of confidentiality. The interviews were conducted face-to-face at locations chosen by the participants (e.g., home, café, hospital, workplace) and scheduled by appointment. All interviews were conducted by the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator, who is also a member of the author team and had approached the families for the clinical organ-donation request during the index hospital admission (i.e., the hospitalization in which brain death was determined). The research interviews were not contemporaneous with the request; they were scheduled weeks to months after the index admission following a bereavement “cool-off” period to minimize distress and role confusion. Prior to each interview, the interviewer disclosed this dual role and emphasized voluntariness and the right to decline or discontinue without consequence. This professional continuity facilitated rapport and acceptability, as several participants reported feeling more comfortable with a known coordinator. Data collection was completed between December 6, 2024, and February 19, 2025. Interviews were concluded once data saturation was reached, as indicated by repetitive responses from participants. The average duration of the interviews was 45 min. With participants consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis. Following transcription, all participant names were removed to ensure anonymity.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used in the study as a method that enables the discovery of new insights through the interpretation of collected data. The analysis process involved iterative several steps: (1) familiarizing ourselves with the data through repeated reading of transcripts, (2) generating initial codes to capture meaningful aspects of the data, (3) grouping codes into potential category and then themes, (4) reviewing and refining themes to ensure coherence, and (5) defining and naming themes to reflect their significance in the context of the study and (6) producing the report [41]. To ensure a systematic implementation of this process, three researchers collaborated, each with different roles to increase methodological robustness. A reflexive journal was maintained by the interviewer to support bracketing and to document interactional/contextual observations (e.g., participants’ emotional reactions) during data collection [42]. The other two researchers, both academics in the field of health management conducting research on organ donation, assimilated the data through repeated transcript readings and collaboratively conducted coding and theme development. Initial inductive codes were generated collectively, with each researcher offering unique perspectives to enrich interpretation. To deepen contextual understanding, we consulted Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator’s reflexive journal, which illuminated emotional and relational nuances but excluded Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator from the analysis to prevent clinical biases from shaping interpretations [43]. Reflexive team discussions increased analytic clarity by encouraging critical examination of our backgrounds and assumptions. This increased the reliability of the study by ensuring that the emerging themes were firmly embedded in the participants’ narratives [44]. Additionally, an external audit, as proposed by Creswell and Miller [45], was conducted. The identified themes were independently reviewed by an expert in qualitative research, who provided supervisory feedback, and necessary revisions were made to ensure coherence and rigor. During the analysis process, the qualitative data analysis software Maxqda Analytics Pro (Version 24.8.0) was used. The identified themes were presented in the findings section, supported by relevant codes and illustrative quotations from the participants.

Ethical statement

The subject, design, and methodology of the research were ethically evaluated by the Suleyman Demirel University Ethics Committee, and ‘Ethics Committee Approval’ was obtained with the decision dated 14.11.2024 and numbered 156/15. In addition, institutional permission was obtained from Suleyman Demirel Research and Application Hospital, the hospital where the participants decided to donate organs, with the decision numbered E-26515734-605. All participants were informed about the purpose and rationale of the study, their written consent for participation in the study was obtained, and anonymity and confidentiality of all information were guaranteed.

Results

During the study period defined in the research, 35 brain-death cases occurred at the university hospital where the study was conducted. Among these, organ donation was declined in 23 cases, while consent was granted in 12 cases. Of the 12 consenting donor relatives, 10 could be reached, and 8 of them ultimately agreed to participate in the study. Participant–donor characteristics and interview information

In summary, 8 cases were analysed, and interviews with donor relatives lasted on average 45 minutes. Donors’ mean age was 46.6 years. Most participants were first-degree relatives of the donor. All participants stated that their religion was Islam. These characteristics highlight both the diversity and the representativeness of the participants in relation to the donation process.

The findings indicated that donor relatives’ experiences related to the organ donation decision process were categorized under two main fields: “factors influencing the donation decision” and “post-decision experiences.

Factors influencing donation decision

Based on the interviews conducted to explore the experiences of donor relatives who consented to organ donation, the field of“factors influencing the donation decision” was identified. This field was divided into three themes: (a) decision-related factors, (b) health system factors, and (c) individual factors. Figure 1 presents the themes and categories related to the field of ‘Factors Influencing the Donation Decision’.

Factors influencing the donation decision

Decision-related factors

An analysis of the factors influencing the donation decision revealed that participants initially emphasized the distinct nature of organ donation decisions compared to routine decisions made in daily life. The most prominent code under the theme of decision-related factors was the difficulty of the decision. Participants expressed, through their statements, that making an organ donation decision amidst the pain of losing a loved one was an extremely challenging experience.

“We knew it was a good thing, but at that moment, we probably made the most difficult decision of our lives.” (P3)

Another prominent aspect under the theme of decision-related factors, alongside the difficulty of the decision, was its urgency. Participants emphasized the limited time available to make such a critical decision. For instance, one participant described the time pressure involved in the process as follows:

“It was both a short time frame and a difficult process. All we could think about was that we had to act quickly.” (P1)

Health system factors

One of the factors found to influence donor relatives’ organ donation decisions was related to the healthcare system. In this context, the attitude of healthcare professionals, information process, and the trust established through these interactions were found to play a significant role in participants decision-making.

Attitude of healthcare professionals

Participants who consented to organ donation described the “understanding, patient, and kind” attitude of healthcare professionals during the process as a key factor that eased the procedure, reduced the pressure of decision-making, and reinforced a sense of trust. They expressed the positive influence of the informative and supportive approach of healthcare professionals on their decision-making process with statements such as the following:

“While (healthcare professionals) informing me about my mother’s condition in the intensive care unit, they also explained the organ donation process in detail. There was no pressure at all; they gave me time to think… They also offered psychological support. Without their professionalism and empathy, I wouldn’t have been this strong during the process.” (P4)

These statements indicate that healthcare professionals played a role not only in providing medical care but also in offering psychological support throughout the process.

Information process

The importance of being properly informed during the decision-making process was frequently emphasized by participants. They stated that timely and accurate information provided during the organ donation process had a direct impact on their ability to make informed decisions. In particular, participants noted that insufficient understanding of the concept of brain death—often confused with a vegetative state—and the lack of clear explanations on this issue led to confusion, uncertainty, and false hope.

“They told us it was brain death. We had heard of it before, but we didn’t really understand it… In a situation like this, not knowing is a terrible thing.” (P6)

Some participants stated that they did not receive sufficient explanations from healthcare professionals in a timely manner, which made the decision-making process more difficult and prolonged it. The impact of inadequate information in a situation where a rapid decision is required for transplantation to proceed was also reflected in participants’ statements.

“There were gaps in the information provided. The explanations should have been clearer… If there had been a clear explanation, we would have made our decision more quickly.” (P1)

Alongside other healthcare professionals, the role of Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator in informing donor relatives during the decision-making process was also emphasized. Participants expressed this as follows:

“… The Organ and Tissue Transplant Coordinators explained the entire process to us. We learned the difference between brain death and a vegetative state. Understanding that difference was important for us, because people want to hold on to hope… You keep thinking, ‘Is there still a chance?’” (P2)

“While informing us about my mother’s condition, they also explained the organ donation process in detail. They never pressured us; instead, they gave us time to think. In particular, the organ transplantation coordinator reassured us by saying, ‘This is a very special decision, and we respect your feelings.’ They also provided psychological support. Without their professionalism and empathy, I would not have been able to stay this strong during the process” (P4)

All of these statements demonstrated that accurate and timely information significantly facilitates donor relatives decision-making, provides psychological relief, and contributes to saving lives by minimizing delays in the organ donation process.

Trust

Participants stated that the sense of trust they felt toward healthcare professionals played a decisive role in their decision to consent to organ donation. This sense of trust was shaped by the perception that the medical process was being conducted accurately and thoroughly, as well as by the communication, sincerity and transparency demonstrated by healthcare professionals throughout the process.

“Simply put it’s a matter of trust. We wanted to believe that everything that needed to be done was actually being done. More than the behaviour itself, it was important that they explained to us what was being done. That kind of clarity has a positive influence on the donation decision. It affects us—even without us realizing it.” (P3)

“The most important factor that influenced our decision was that the doctors were sincere and convincing.” (P5)

As the participants' statements suggest, trust emerged as a fundamental prerequisite for granting consent to organ donation. Donor relatives were more likely to approve donation when they believe that all necessary treatments have been administered thoroughly and with care, as communicated by healthcare professionals. It is evident that trust is built not only on professional competence but also through open communication and emotional support.

When viewed broadly under the theme of healthcare system factors, accurate and timely information, combined with the attitude of healthcare professionals, fostered trust. This trust, in turn, plays a decisive role in navigating the complex and urgent decision to donate organs. In this regard, the findings indicate that when donor relatives encounter sincere and transparent communication from healthcare professionals, the decision-making process tends to progress more smoothly and positively.

Individual factors

The analysis of participant statements revealed that, in addition to healthcare system-related elements, individual factors of donor relatives also played a role in the decision to donate organs. These individual factors were categorized as empathy, altruism, and the influence of the deceased’s values.

Empathy

Participants indicated that, while making the decision to donate, they not only considered their own loss but also empathized with the struggles of individuals waiting for organ transplants and the hardships faced by their families. It was found that this sense of empathy played an influential role in their decision to approve organ donation.

“I thought about people with children, desperately hoping they wouldn’t die. We saw it in the hospital—everyone was making an effort, just like us.” (P7)

Some participants stated that they considered the possibility of facing a similar situation themselves and emphasized that people should “do for others what they would want done for themselves”.

“Maybe one day I might need an organ too. People should think—if I'm not willing to donate my organs, how can I expect others to donate for me?” (P5)

In summary, participants shaped their organ donation decisions not solely based on the individuals they lost, but through an approach that considered the right of others to live. Empathy emerged as a powerful factor that activated both a sense of personal and societal responsibility throughout the process.

Altruism

Participants expressed a strong need and desire to do good when making the decision to donate. This decision was not only seen as part of the process of saying goodbye to a lost loved one, but also as an opportunity to perform a selfless act, to save another person’s life, and to offer someone a second chance.

“The idea of taking part in something as sacred as saving a life played a very important role in my decision.” (P4)

“Organ donation means giving someone a second chance. A selfless act is done for someone you don’t even know and being able to do that brings great happiness.” (P2)

These findings indicate that the desire to help others and the idea of offering hope to another life were among the key motivations that gave meaning to the decision-making process. For participants, the altruistic nature of organ donation also served as a coping

Deceased’s will

Participants acknowledged that making a decision on such an emotionally intense and morally weighty issue as organ donation was difficult. However, the donor’s positive attitude toward organ donation while alive significantly eased the decision-making process. One participant expressed the difficulty of the decision by stating, “… This decision was very difficult for me and my family…” but also noted that their mother’s supportive views on organ donation made it easier to reach a decision, as reflected in the following statement:

“My mother was a very loving person, and while she was alive, she would often say, ‘It’s such a beautiful thing that death can give hope to others,’ using this as an example. Her words always echoed in my mind. Especially my father and brother said that we should see the donation as her wish and fulfil it accordingly.” (P4)

Another participant referred to previous conversations with their spouse, explaining that the decision became a responsibility taken on behalf of their partner, and that their spouse’s attitude was a determining factor in the process, as expressed in the following statement:

“While my wife was alive, we talked a few times about organ donation. She supported things like this. That’s why making the decision felt like a responsibility for me. In that moment, you don’t think about yourself—you think about what the other person would have wanted. If she had said something like this wasn’t a good thing, I wouldn’t have donated her organs.” (P2)

In this context, it becomes clear that the values embraced by the donor during their lifetime, and prior discussions about those values, positively shaped the decision-making process for the surviving family members. Additionally, the significance of documents such as organ donor cards—which serve as formal consent for donation after death—also comes to the forefront.

Post-decision experiences

Regarding the field of post-decision experience, four themes emerged: (a) satisfaction with the decision, (b) curiosity about the recipient, (c) the meaning of organ donation, and (d) creating donation awareness. Figure 2 presents the themes related to the field of ‘Post-Decision Experiences’.

Post-decision experiences

Satisfaction with decision

An analysis of participants’ post-decision experiences revealed that each of them expressed satisfaction with their decision to approve organ donation, and none reported feelings of regret. Looking back, they developed a strong belief that organ donation was the right thing to do. This sense of satisfaction was primarily rooted in the idea of giving hope to others, the supportive responses they received from their social circles, and a sense of moral relief. Some illustrative participant statements are as follows:

“We decided to donate(organs). We don’t regret it. Someone’s life was saved.” (P1)

“We don’t feel any regret. If it happened today, we would make the same decision again. For us, organ donation was, in a broader sense, an expression of our desire to do good” (P3)

One participant, in emphasizing their satisfaction with the decision, also reflected on the potential regret that others might feel if they choose not to donate. This was expressed as follows:“If it happened again, I would definitely do it. I’m really glad we were able to donate. I think there are many people who didn’t donate and later said,‘I wish we had.’” (P8) This perspective highlights that the sense of inner peace experienced after the decision suggests that organ donation can also serve as a long-term source of comfort for donor families.

It was also observed that participants received positive reactions from their social circles, which affirmed their sense of having made the right decision:

“Everyone supported the decision to donate. They gave us positive feedback and helped us through it. After the donation, when we talked to people around us, they kept telling us that what we did was the right thing.” (P6)

These statements reveal that donor relatives received social approval and appreciation following the organ donation, and this support helped them internalize their decision more easily. The sense of peace and satisfaction expressed in the post-decision period indicates that organ donation was perceived as the right choice by the participants. Moreover, the positive feedback from their social environment, along with the imagined regret they might have felt had they not given consent, further reinforced their sense of having made the right decision.

Curiosity about the recipient

In the post-donation period, one of the strongest emotional needs expressed by donor relatives was the desire to know who received the donated organ and if possible, to establish some form of contact with them. Nearly all participants reported a strong sense of curiosity about the identity, health status, and current life of the recipient. While some stated that simply knowing the recipients were doing well would be enough for them, others expressed a deeper wish to meet these individuals or at least have a brief conversation with them.

“Of course I was curious—who received my sister’s organs, how they’re doing now. I didn’t look into it because they said it wasn’t legally allowed. But I definitely would have wanted to meet them.” (P7)

“We really don’t expect anything from anyone. We just wanted to hear their voice, to know that they are doing well. Honestly, has the other person regained their health or not? If they have, well, then it would be possible to contact them, even if only over the phone.” (P1)

These findings indicate that curiosity about recipients may function as an adaptive attempt to achieve cognitive closure and to integrate the meaning of donation into the bereavement process. At the same time, in the absence of identifiable information, this curiosity can foster imagined recipient representations, which in some cases may lead to projection or moral judgment toward recipients. Participants were also aware that direct contact is generally not permitted under valid legal and ethical safeguards, and some participants emphasized that this request stemmed from curiosity about the recipient's well-being.

Meaning of organ donation

Participant statements reveal that organ donation is perceived as more than a physical transfer of organs from one person to another; it carries profound emotional and spiritual significance. Donor relatives described these meanings from three perspectives: what donation meant for the recipient, for the deceased donor, and for themselves as the donor’s family. For the recipient, organ donation is viewed as a source of hope and a second chance at life. For the donor, it symbolizes leaving behind a legacy and keeping their memory alive. For the donor's family, it serves as a form of comfort and emotional relief amid the pain of loss.

Participants described how knowing that their loved one's organs had given someone else a second chance brought them a sense of hope and consolation. Some of their expressions include:

“His name will live on through another life. That became a great source of comfort for us.” (P2)

“After my mother passed away, I felt such a profound void that I wanted her to live on in some way. Knowing that a part of her would continue to live through others thanks to organ donation deeply moved me.” (P4)

All these statements emphasize that organ donation helps alleviate the pain experienced by all parties involved and plays a meaningful role in making sense of the loss and finding peace. Ultimately, it appears that the process aids individuals in coping with grief and offers a sense of solace.

Creating donation awareness

Finally, participants’ statements regarding organ donation reveal that public awareness on the issue is still insufficient and that there is a significant lack of societal understanding. Therefore, it is emphasized that in order to increase donation decisions, priority should be given to efforts aimed at raising awareness and educating the public.

“We should recognize organ donation as a moral and humanitarian responsibility. Therefore, individuals should not hesitate to donate, and alternative mechanisms and supportive policies should be developed and implemented by the government to facilitate this process.” (P5)

“It seems that the process is not being communicated effectively. Authorities and responsible parties are failing to reach the public.” (P6)

Discussion

This study, which examined the experiences of donor relatives in the organ donation decision-making process, revealed two overarching fields based on participants' responses: factors influencing the donation decision and post-decision experiences. Among the factors influencing the donation decision, participants identified the nature of the decision itself as one of the most challenging aspects. The degree of difficulty experienced during the decision-making process appeared to be closely linked to the donor’s attitudes toward organ donation while still alive. When the donor had previously expressed positive views about donation, the decision became significantly easier for the relatives. In such cases, participants perceived the donor’s pre-death statements as a moral obligation and made the donation decision to honour the donor’s wishes. Similarly, Breitkopf [46] emphasized the importance of family communication about organ donation and De Groot et al. [47] found in interviews with relatives of brain-dead donors that knowing the preferences of the deceased and respecting these wishes facilitated the decision-making process for family members. In addition, Hyde and White [48], in their study on factors influencing consent to organ donation, found that individuals were often reluctant to share their decisions with relatives due to concerns about their reactions, thereby underscoring the importance of communication within families. In the findings of this study, the fact that families emphasized the comforting aspect of conversations about organ donation supports that individuals can talk about this issue without hesitation.

Among the factors influencing the donation decision, one prominent category under health system-related factors was the attitude of healthcare professionals, the quality of the information provided, and the resulting sense of trust. In a study by Aldridge and Guy [49], it was emphasized that well-trained healthcare professionals who communicate effectively with donor families can positively influence family decisions—an assertion that aligns with the findings of the present study. Nearly all participants in this study reported satisfaction with the communication established by healthcare staff during their difficult time, noting that this interaction had a significant impact on their willingness to consent to donation Participants highlighted that empathy, dedication, and a non-insistent approach of professionals helped facilitate the decision-making process. At the core of this positive interaction lies a sense of trust in the healthcare providers. Participants expressed strong belief that healthcare professionals had done everything possible to save the patient. This perception of trust contributed to the belief that healthcare providers fulfilled their ethical responsibilities. Ralph et al. [33] also emphasized that trust in healthcare staff is a critical factor influencing donation decisions. According to the Regulation on Organ Transplantation Services [50], among the responsibilities of the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator is the duty to “conduct interviews with the donor candidate’s family, legal representative, or any relative who may act on their behalf, and ensure the donation form is duly completed in case of consent.” In this study, the pivotal role played by the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator was clearly articulated by participants. The quality of communication established by the coordinator, the trust they fostered, and particularly their ability to provide accurate and comprehensible information regarding brain death significantly contributed to the final donation decision. Similarly, in a study by Cohen et al. [51], the importance of the coordinator’s presence and their relationship with the donor’s family was highlighted and their role in clarifying that brain death constitutes a valid medical and legal definition of death further supports the findings of the present research. In the study conducted by Darnell [52] on families who refused organ donation, participants described the persistent attitude of the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator as inappropriate and disturbing, stating that they felt their freedom to make decisions was being threatened. This finding aligns with our study, supporting the notion that the process can yield more positive outcomes when the organ donation coordinator employs appropriate communication strategies. Notably, a lack of understanding regarding brain death-and its frequent confusion with a vegetative state-emerged as a key issue among participants. Some believed that brain death was potentially reversible, leading to hesitation and distress during the decision-making process. As noted above, such uncertainty persisted until healthcare staff, particularly the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator, became involved. This aligns with other studies in the literature, which demonstrate that similar misunderstandings are prevalent and underscore the critical need to provide clear, timely information to donor families [53].

Among individual factors affecting organ donation decision, utilitarianism and the desire to engage in altruistic acts emerged as core motivations. Participants viewed organ donation as an opportunity to save another person’s life and prevent others from experiencing similar suffering. This outward-facing attitude gave meaning to the grief process and alleviated some of the emotional burden associated with the decision. The literature similarly highlights that utilitarian motivations help individuals develop confidence in the correctness of their donation decision and contribute to a lasting sense of internal satisfaction [54]. In the present study, the role of empathy emerged as a decisive factor in the decision-making process This perspective demonstrates that participants engaged in perspective-taking, which allowed them to assign deeper meaning to their decision to donate. Cohen et al. [55] similarly noted that empathy is a powerful motivator that enhances the willingness to donate and leads to more internalized, meaningful decisions. Thus, empathy not only supports personal moral fulfilment but also serves as a key mechanism for strengthening social solidarity.

Within the field of post-decision experiences, the study identified four key themes: satisfaction with the decision, curiosity about the recipient, the meaning of organ donation, and awareness-building. All participants reported strong and lasting satisfaction with their decision to approve organ donation. Furthermore, positive feedback received from their social circles was found to reinforce this satisfaction, facilitating participants’ internalization of the donation decision. This suggests that organ donation is not merely a personal act but also one that receives broader social validation. Donor families emphasized that they did not experience any regret regarding their decision; on the contrary, they expressed a sense of moral relief and inner peace, stemming from the opportunity to contribute to another person’s life. Similarly, Kim et al. [56] observed that participants experienced comparable emotions and expressed contentment with their decision at the end of the process. Notably, some participants emphasized that those who refused organ donation were likely to experience regret afterward. This perspective is corroborated by various studies in the literature, which report that families who declined donation often experienced regret later in the process [57–59].

Curiosity about the recipient emerged as a prominent post-decision experience. While virtually all participants reported a strong interest in who received the organ—and many expressed a wish for brief contact—this desire coexisted with awareness that direct contact is generally not permitted under prevailing anonymity and confidentiality safeguards. Participants emphasized that their curiosity was non-material in nature and grounded in emotional and symbolic bonds, often centring on whether the recipient was doing well. These findings suggest a need for bereavement-informed follow-up and coordinator-mediated, non-identifying updates (where legally and ethically permissible) to address families’ informational needs without compromising confidentiality. Prior studies similarly highlight that both donor families and organ recipients frequently express curiosity about the other party and an interest in some form of communication after transplantation [40, 60–62].

Within the theme of the meaning of organ donation, it was found that donor relatives who participated in the study perceived the act of donation as a form of solace and a way to leave a lasting legacy. The idea of offering hope to others and making a meaningful impact on someone else's life played a significant role in encouraging participants to make a positive decision. This interpretation aligns with the findings of Kentish-Barnes et al. [63], who demonstrated that when donor families believe in the correctness of their decision, they are more likely to experience the post-donation period as a form of consolation. Such meaning-making helps situate the emotional void caused by loss within a purposeful framework, thus facilitating the grieving process.

Finally, the past-donation experience was characterized by the need to raise awareness. Many participants stated that their initial anxiety stemmed from limited knowledge about the process, which hindered timely decision-making. Furthermore, the findings indicate that government-led initiatives at the societal level should be expanded to support organ donation decisions, and that the effectiveness of current efforts should be critically evaluated. In parallel with these findings, Cotrău et al. [64] highlighted the importance of increasing awareness campaigns conducted by healthcare professionals, particularly through conventional and social media channels and addressing misinformation related to the subject. Limitations

This study is limited to donor relatives who consented to organ donation following brain death at a university hospital located in the southern region of Türkiye. Therefore, the findings specifically reflect the experiences and decision-making processes of these individuals. It does not address the perspectives of families who refused donation; however, understanding the reasons underlying refusal and initiating strategies to address these concerns may play a crucial role in increasing donation rates. Future studies focusing on refusal cases are strongly recommended. To broaden the scope of the research, future studies are recommended at both national and international levels—particularly in other regions of Türkiye and in countries with similarly low post mortem organ donation rates. Comparative investigations that explore the impact of cultural and sociodemographic differences on decision-making and post-decision experiences could provide more comprehensive insights. Such research would enable the revaluation of strategies aimed at increasing organ donation rates and support the development of policies and practices tailored to regional and cultural contexts at both national and global levels.

Another limitation of the study is the inclusion of individuals who made an organ donation decision within the past five years and voluntarily agreed to participate in the interviews. Participation of donor relatives in such research is inherently challenging, as it often requires revisiting emotionally traumatic memories during the grieving process. Moreover, while some participants had lost their loved ones only a year prior, others experienced their loss three to four years earlier, potentially introducing recall bias into retrospective accounts. Future research is recommended to collect data from individuals who have more recently undergone this experience, in order to minimize the influence of recall bias and enhance the accuracy of findings.

A further limitation relates to interviewer effects. The interviews were conducted by the Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator, who had previously approached participants’ families during the index hospital admission for the clinical donation request. Although this professional continuity appeared to enhance rapport and acceptability, it may also introduce interviewer-related bias (e.g., social desirability or role-based deference). We sought to mitigate this risk through explicit role disclosure, reiteration of voluntariness and the right to decline, participant-chosen settings and timing after a bereavement cool-off period, reflexive journaling during data collection, exclusion of the interviewer-author from coding/theme adjudication, team-based analysis, and external audit; nonetheless, bias cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study provides an in-depth exploration of the experiences of donor families regarding the organ donation decision-making process in Türkiye. The findings not only reveal the key factors influencing donor families' decisions but also offer evidence-based insights to address uncertainties such as "What happens next?" or "Will I regret it?" This contributes to shaping a more positive public perception, particularly in countries with low rates of post mortem organ donation.

It was found that empathetic and altruistic motivations, as well as the donor's previously expressed wishes, played a decisive role in the decision-making process of families of brain-dead patients. In addition, a clear understanding of the concept of brain death was shown to influence the timing and ease of decision-making. Trust in healthcare professionals was reported to reduce ethical uncertainty, particularly regarding brain death confirmation and the integrity of medical procedures. Some participants initially feared possible conflicts of interest, but these concerns eased with clear and honest communication. The study revealed that initial concerns expressed during the decision-making phase often gave way to a deep sense of spiritual fulfilment after the donation. Participants emphasized their satisfaction with the decision and expressed a strong willingness to support others in society who may face a similar experience in the future. In light of these findings, it is recommended that awareness campaigns emphasizing the scientific foundations of brain death be prioritized to increase organ donation rates. It is also essential that healthcare professionals particularly Organ and Tissue Transplantation Coordinator, assume active roles and responsibilities in this process. In addition, training programs for healthcare professionals who are directly involved in communicating brain death to families should be implemented to further support the process. Moreover, public awareness programs that encourage individuals to officially document their donation preferences (e.g., via donor cards) and to communicate their intentions with family members should be further developed. Lastly, considering participants' curiosity about recipients and their desire to learn about their well-being, updates that do not contain identity information, provided through the coordinator and based on legal and ethical permissions and restrictions, along with informed follow-up regarding the legal process, can help meet families' information needs and strengthen public trust without compromising anonymity and confidentiality. Ensuring transparency throughout this communication process will be crucial to maintaining confidence in the donation system and resolving ethical dilemmas. Collectively, these strategies have the potential to improve the effectiveness of the organ transplantation system while fostering societal awareness and voluntarism.
