Abstract
We propose a scalable, approximate inference hypernetwork framework for a general model of history-dependent processes. The flexible data model is based on a neural ordinary differential equation (NODE) representing the evolution of internal states together with a trainable observation model subcomponent. The posterior distribution corresponding to the data model parameters (weights and biases) follows a stochastic differential equation with a drift term related to the score of the posterior that is learned jointly with the data model parameters. This Langevin sampling approach offers flexibility in balancing the computational budget between the evaluation cost of the data model and the approximation of the posterior density of its parameters. We demonstrate performance of the ensemble sampling hypernetwork on chemical reaction and material physics data and compare it to standard variational inference.

Introduction

Many models of physical and other processes require degrees of freedom to represent unobservable states that influence the observable response.
The Luenberger observer
[
1
]
from control theory is a widely employed example that provides an estimate of the internal state while modeling measurements of the output of a system.
Augmented neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs)
[
2
,
3
]
is another, now ubiquitous, data representation of this type that expands the modeled dynamics to hidden dimensions for greater expressiveness via
latent
variables.
Generally speaking, since the hidden variables in these types of models are inferred or postulated (as opposed to directly measured), these states should be treated as uncertain.

A wide variety of methods have been developed to perform uncertainty quantification (UQ).
Expensive, sequential Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
are still considered the reference standards methods.
Nevertheless, more efficient optimization-based variational inference (VI) methods
[
7
,
8
]
have arisen as practical alternatives.
VI methods employ a parameterized surrogate posterior and posits UQ as an optimization problem for the parameters of the surrogate.
More recently, hybrid methods that use an empirical posterior distribution composed of model realizations as well as optimization methods to drive these realizations to match data have arisen.
These include Stein VI
[
9
]
and the method we employ, Langevin sampling (LS)
[
10
]
.

The proposed hypernetwork framework consists of a hidden state model and an observation model that are parameterized by a sampler that is trained to data.
Unlike the widely employed Hamiltonian Monte Carlo approach
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
, LS does not have a conditional acceptance criterion, which simplifies backpropagation.
The hypernetwork implementation is relatively straightforward and is fully differentiable; furthermore, LS has a firm theoretical basis which we expand upon in App.
A
.
We illustrate the performance of the framework with process modeling applications from chemical kinetics and material physics.
After verifying the convergence properties on simpler problems, we demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of modifying the method to treat some of the NN parameters with full UQ and others with reoptimized point estimates.

In the next section, Sec.​
2
, we put the proposed method in the context of related work including standard and recently developed UQ methods.
Sec.​
3
provides the theoretical development of the algorithm and implementation details.
Then, in Sec.​
4
, we provide demonstrations of the method’s performance in calibrating and providing UQ for selected physical models.
Finally, Sec.​
5
, provides a summary and avenues for future work.

Related work

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) is a long-standing field of research with ongoing challenges.
Two broad classes of UQ methods are: (a) sampling-based methods, such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods
[
4
,
6
]
, which generate realizations of the posterior, and (b) optimization-based methods, such as VI
[
7
,
8
]
, which calibrate a surrogate distribution.
MCMC algorithms are challenged by high-dimensional parameter spaces, as is the case with NN models with a large number of parameters, while tractable VI methods, such as
mean-field
VI
[
7
]
, suffer from limited expressiveness.

Hybrid ensemble methods have emerged that use
particles
representing realizations and calibrated dynamics.
Stein VI
[
9
]
utilizes Stein’s identity and a kernelized gradient flow to minimize the Kullback-Liebler (KL) divergence between the particles and the posterior underlying the data.
Like Hamiltonian Monte Carlo methods
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
Langevin sampling (LS) borrows from computational statistical mechanics
[
14
]
and was proposed by Welling and Teh
[
10
]
as a generally applicable ensemble method bridging sampling and optimization schemes.
Later, LS was employed by Refs.
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
in various applications.
LS relies on a particular form of the KL divergence between two random
processes

[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, as opposed to random variables/distributions.
In its simplest form, it generates samples from a Gaussian process
[
21
]
.
This resembles techniques such as
flow matching

[
22
]
, which seek to minimize the distance between two random processes.
As with some diffusion models
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
and other paradigms
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
, it is based on learning a
score
function.
Furthermore, methods have been designed that have strongly interacting Langevin diffusions
[
29
]
, unlike the approach adopted in this work.

We formulate our LS method as a hypernetwork, where the learned score-based sampler provides parameters to the data model, which produces outputs that are compared to data.
Hypernetworks have found wide-spread use in a variety of tasks
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
Our utilization of a divided parameter space, where one partition is optimized to improve fit while the other pursues the UQ objective,
resembles
optimal brain surgery

[
39
,
40
]
, a deterministic method for model-parameter pruning.

Methodology

The goal of this work is to provide comprehensive uncertainty quantification (UQ) of a class of generally applicable NN models.
We combine stochastic sampling of a data matching model in a form of a hypernetwork where a trainable sampler provides weights to the data representation model whose predictions are compared to data.

\mathsf{m}

.
The data model
\mathsf{m}
takes weights
\mathsf{w}
from the sampler and data inputs
\mathsf{X}
to produce outputs
\mathsf{Y}

\mathsf{Y}_{\mathsf{w}}=\mathsf{m}(\mathsf{X};\mathsf{w}).

Sampler

The hypernetwork is a probabilistic modeling framework to estimate the model parameters,
\mathsf{w}=\{\mathsf{w}_{\mathsf{R}},\mathsf{w}_{\mathsf{Z}}\}
.
In a Bayesian context, the posterior distribution
\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})
is given by:

\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})\propto\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})\pi(\mathsf{w})

(6)

where
\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})
is the likelihood of observing the data
\mathsf{D}
given the model
\mathsf{m}
parameterized by
\mathsf{w}
, and
\pi(\mathsf{w})
is the prior distributions for the model parameters.

The posterior distribution
\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})
is not, in general, available analytically.
In this work, we use LS via the SDE (
3
) to provide samples of the posterior.
Fig.
1
b provides a schematic of the sampler.

We connect the score-based drift
\mathsf{f}=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}U
in (
3
) with a

potential energy

U(\mathsf{w})
associated with the model (negative log) posterior/
score

U(\mathsf{w})=-\log\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})=-\log\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})-\log\pi(\mathsf{w})+\text{constant}

(7)

so that

\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}U(\mathsf{w})=-\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}\left[\log\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})+\log\pi(\mathsf{w})\right].

(8)

With this connection, we have an SDE that samples the posterior of the weights of the data model:

\mathrm{d}\mathsf{w}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}U(\mathsf{w})\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{\tau}

(9)

where
\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\in{R}^{|\mathsf{w}|}
is a standard multidimensional Brownian motion/Weiner process, with
|\mathsf{w}|
denoting the cardinality of
\mathsf{w}
, and
\tau
pseudo-time.

Weight model

Following Refs.
[
10
,
17
,
19
,
20
]
, we approximate the prior
\pi(\mathsf{w})
and the posterior
\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})
distributions with generative SDEs:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathsf{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{0}(\mathsf{w})\mathrm{d}\tau+\gamma_{0}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\hskip 14.45377pt\textrm{(prior)}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathsf{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{*}(\mathsf{w})\mathrm{d}\tau+\gamma_{*}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\hskip 14.45377pt\textrm{(posterior)}

(11)

where
\mathsf{f}_{*}=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})
and
\mathsf{f}_{0}=\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}\pi(\mathsf{w})
are the drift components for the posterior and prior, respectively, and
\gamma_{*}
and
\gamma_{0}
are the corresponding diffusion components magnitudes.

As in Ref.
[
17
]
, we employ a Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process for the prior with
\mathsf{f}_{0}(\mathsf{w})=-(\mathsf{w}-\bar{\mathsf{w}})
and set
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{*}=1
and
\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathrm{d}\tau\mathsf{I})
.
The steady state OU distribution is then a multivariate normal with diagonal unit covariance,
\mathsf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mathsf{w}},\mathsf{I})
, and mean
\bar{\mathsf{w}}
which is set to zeros, the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) or other point estimate.
The score of the posterior
\bm{\nabla}_{\mathsf{w}}\pi(\mathsf{w}|\mathsf{D})
is approximated by a neural network,
{\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}}_{\mathsf{f}}(\mathsf{w};\bm{\theta})
, where
\bm{\theta}
are the parameters defining the posterior model

\mathsf{f}_{*}={\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}}_{\mathsf{f}}(\mathsf{w};\bm{\theta})\ .

(12)

Loss

We employ the evidence lower bound (ELBO)
[
7
]
to construct the loss function that will drive the search for an approximate posterior distribution for the model parameters
\mathsf{w}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{w};\theta)}\left[\log\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})\right]-D_{\text{KL}}(\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{w};\theta)||\pi(\mathsf{w}))\ ,

(13)

where
D_{\text{KL}}(\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{w};\theta)||\pi(\mathsf{w}))
is Kullback-Liebler (KL) divergence between the prior and the approximate posterior of the weights
\mathsf{w}
of the physical process model.
The first term promotes accuracy by informing the surrogate posterior
\mathsf{q}
by the likelihood
\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})
, and the second term acts as a regularizing penalty by incurring a cost for the surrogate posterior being far from the prior
\pi(\mathsf{w})
.
If both SDEs for the prior and posterior distributions, in Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
), share the same diffusion,
\gamma=\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{*}
, the KL divergence between the prior and the posterior is finite
[
20
]
, and the KL divergence between the two processes can be written as:

D_{\text{KL}}(q(\mathsf{w};\theta)||\pi(\mathsf{w}))=\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathsf{w};\theta)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{f}}\left\|\frac{\mathsf{f}_{*}(\mathsf{w}_{\tau})-\mathsf{f}_{0}(\mathsf{w}_{\tau})}{\gamma}\right\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right].

(14)

This expression is an expectation over the Langevin samples generated by the numerical solution of the SDE model.
We select the
\mathsf{w}
samples corresponding to the pseudo-time
\tau_{f}
as an approximation of sampling from
\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{w};\bm{\theta})
for the purpose of estimating the marginal likelihood

\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathsf{w};\theta)}\left[\log\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})\right]\approx\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\log\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w}_{i})

(15)

where
\mathsf{w}_{i}=\mathsf{w}_{i}(\tau_{f};\bm{\theta})
.
Refer to App.
A
for a brief derivation of this form of the KL divergence and how it bounds the KL divergence in Eq. (
13
) for the distribution of weights at
\tau_{f}
.

In this work, we consider time-dependent processes, with model outputs

\mathsf{Y}_{\mathsf{w}}=\{\mathsf{Y}_{\mathsf{w}}(X_{j},t_{k};\mathsf{w}_{i})|i=1,\ldots,N_{s},j=1,\ldots N_{D},k=1,\ldots,N_{t}\}

(16)

collected at a sequence of (physical) time steps,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N_{t}}
and realizations of the weight samples
\mathsf{w}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{s}
.
Given the set of model evaluations, we construct the likelihood
\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w})
as follows.
We consider data realizations that were generated independently, thus

\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w}_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{D}}\pi(\mathsf{D}_{j}|\mathsf{w}_{i}).

(17)

For both numerical and computational experiments, this is a reasonable approximation as data is typically collected independently for different experimental designs.
Further, we approximate independent observations across each time series, leading to

\pi(\mathsf{D}|\mathsf{w}_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{D}}\prod_{k=1}^{N_{t}}\pi(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k}|\mathsf{w}_{i})

(18)

where
\mathsf{D}=\{\mathsf{Y}_{j,k}|j=1,\ldots N_{D},k=1,\ldots,N_{t}\}
.
This approximation can be relaxed if correlation information is readily available or if sufficient data is available to estimate it numerically, e.g. via entropy-based methods.

Furthermore, we employ a Gaussian mixture model for
\pi(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k}|\mathsf{w}_{i})

\pi(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k}|\mathsf{w}_{i})=\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\alpha_{l}\,g(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k};\mathsf{Y}_{w_{i};j,k,l},\sigma_{k,l})

(19)

where

g(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k};\mathsf{Y}_{\mathsf{w}_{i};j,k,l},\sigma_{k,l})\propto\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k,l}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathsf{Y}_{j,k}-\mathsf{Y}_{\mathsf{w}_{i};j,k,l})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k,l}^{2}}\right).

(20)

The results presented in Sec.​
4
employ either one or two modes,
N_{\text{modes}}=1,2
.
For the results corresponding to
N_{\text{modes}}=2
, the modes were sufficiently distinct to estimate the mode weights
\alpha_{l}
by counting the number of data trajectories that fall into either mode.
In a more generic setting, with multiple and/or less distinct modes, one could use techniques such as k-means clustering to estimate this information.

The standard deviations,
\sigma_{k,l}
, can either be added to the collection of parameters inferred along with the model parameters or can be estimated by other means.
Here, we estimate this value as

\sigma_{\cdot}^{2}=\sigma_{\cdot,\mathsf{D}}^{2}+\sigma_{\cdot,\text{MLE}}^{2}

(21)

where
\sigma_{\cdot,\mathsf{D}}
represents the standard deviation computed using the data only, while
\sigma_{\cdot,\text{MLE}}
represents the discrepancy between the mean data and the model fitted via MLE.
This expression assumes that the data generating process and the process of fitting a model contribute to uncertainty in the outputs.
In practice, however, we found that
\sigma_{\cdot,\text{MLE}}
was much smaller than the data counterpart for all models presented in this paper.

4
Demonstrations

To demonstrate the versatility of the proposed method, we use synthetic data with various amounts of stochastic noise and/or internal variance.
First, in Sec.
4.1
, we illustrate the performance of the UQ scheme with data generating models from reaction chemistry.
Systems of SDEs are widely used in modeling chemical (and physical) processes
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, and we use exemplars to explore the performance of the algorithm to combinations of external noise and internal variations that result in epistemic and aleatory uncertainty.
Then, in Sec.
4.2
, we turn to applications from material physics, specifically a viscoelastic composite with heterogenities that are treated as uncertainty around the mean response.
For this application we have no closed form truth model.
We compare the proposed LS method to BBVI for the chemical reaction demonstrations, and with the material physics demonstrations we contrast various Bayesian last layer adaptations of the LS method.

We use the Wasserstein 1-distance (
\mathcal{W}_{1}
) to measure the similarity of two probability densities
\pi_{a}
and
\pi_{b}
:

4.1
Chemical kinetics

We use two physical SDEs to demonstrate the convergence properties of the proposed method.
The first is classical Langevin dynamics.
Langevin dynamics are used in a variety of chemical models from thermostatted molecular dynamics
[

49

50

52

53

Herein we employ a simple Langevin system that has an exact solution so we can investigate a case with no model discrepancy.
Then we turn to a stochastic model of the Schlögl reaction that exhibits bifurcations and therefore multimodal response density functions.

Langevin dynamics

The goal of this demonstration is to: (a) show convergence of the proposed method to exact results with a NN that is consistent with the data-generating model, and (b) to show behavior of the method in the presence of internal (embedded) data-generating model variability

The data-generating model is a 1D Langevin equation

Schlögl model

The goal of this demonstration is to show the performance of the proposed algorithm in the presence of high-frequency noise in the physical response and a multi-modal posterior.
For this, we employ Schlögl’s model, which is a canonical chemical reaction network model that exhibits bistability
[
55
,
56
,
57
]
.

The Schlögl system involves three species
\{X,A,B\}
and two reactions

\displaystyle\text{R1}:

\displaystyle A+2X

\displaystyle\xrightleftharpoons[k_{2}]{k_{1}}3X

(32)

\displaystyle\text{R2}:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\xrightleftharpoons[k_{4}]{k_{3}}X

(33)

The propensities
\{a_{i}\}
of these reactions to occur in an increment of (physical) time
\mathrm{d}t
are

\displaystyle\{a_{\rightarrow},a_{\leftarrow}\}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{k_{1}AX(X-1)/2,k_{2}X(X-1)(X-2)/6\}

(34)

\displaystyle\{a_{\rightarrow},a_{\leftarrow}\}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{k_{3}B,k_{4}X\}

(35)

with
k_{1}=3\times 10^{-7}
,
k_{2}=10^{-4}
,
k_{3}=10^{-3}
,
k_{4}=3.5
.
In the data-generating model, these forward and reverse transitional probabilities drive the Monte Carlo-like stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA)
[
58
,
59
]
.
The output of the SSA for Schlögl system is multimodal and depends on the particular realization of the stochastic diffusion process and the initial conditions (see Fig.
5
a).

We choose to model the uncertainty in the model for Schlögl trajectories with a stochastic framework similar to the one used for the previous exemplar in Eq. (
4.1
). For the current exemplar we only model and observe the (minor) species
\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{h}=X
.
For this exemplar we considered a model for the right-hand side
{\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}}_{\mathsf{R}}
with two internal layers with 16 nodes on each layer and
\tanh
activations, resulting in 321 weights. The LS employs a drift term
{\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}}_{\mathsf{w}}
with 2 internal layers with 101 nodes each and
\tanh
activations.
We used an uninformative prior and one stage of annealing from
\gamma_{0}=2
to
\gamma_{0}=1
for this demonstration to distribute the replica trajectories across the complex posterior.

Fig.
5

compares the data and predicted trajectories via LS as well as Gaussian mixture representations of densities.
After a short-term zone of bifurcation from the initial conditions,
two modes are apparent with roughly equal weight: one with a narrow long time distribution and another at higher values with a broader distribution.
There are small qualitative discrepancies between the predicted trajectories and those of the data generating model most likely due to the (chosen) lack of stochastic component of the data-fit NN model, although the limited representational capacity of the NN and an undersampling of the likely parameters may also play a role.
Nevertheless the LS hypernetwork does capture the the lower, narrow mode and the broader upper mode, while BBVI with a diagonal covariance for efficiency only explores the wider upper mode.

For this exemplar we use the stress response of periodic stochastic volume elements (SVEs) composed of a viscoelastic matrix with spherical elastic inclusions.

\Psi

which serves as the observation component (

Using the physical principle of coordinate frame invariance, we compact the potential representation in Eq. (

of the strain
\mathbf{E}

where

\mathbf{E}

\mathsf{h}

\mathsf{R}
and
\Psi

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathsf{h}

Conclusion

This work presents a hierarchical framework that combines sampling methods with optimization to endow large-scale models with the capability to generate predictions commensurate with the discrepancy between the model and available training data.
The methodology relies on a hypernetwork the utilizes Langevin sampling to generate ensembles of model parameters in a variational inference context.
The framework simultaneously optimizes a score function that characterizes the posterior distribution for a subset of the physical model parameters while directly searching for optimal values for the other model parameters.
As with other UQ methods, samples of the resulting parameter distributions can be pushed forward to provide epistemic (due lack of training data) and aleatory (due to inherent variability) UQ on quantities of interest for engineering design and performance evaluation.
The method is also adaptable to generating likely responses on-the-fly so that these could be embedded in a suitable simulation host code or optimal experimental design method
[
64
]
.

We demonstrated the algorithm on canonical chemical reaction exemplars and also presented its application to complex material physics.
For the chemical reaction cases we compared our predictions with standard mean-field variational inference results.
These comparisons show that the proposed LS hypernetwork is better equipped to capture the uncertainty in the training data compared to standard variational inference models.
Using the material physics exemplars, we showed that layer or component-level stochastic parameters, akin to Bayesian last layer algorithms albeit with reoptimized point estimates, generate predictions similar to the more expensive fully stochastic ensembles that include all model parameters.
The proposed framework is applicable to a wide variety of heterogeneous complex models and provides a competing alternative to ensemble methods such as the Stein variational gradient descent algorithm that was recently coupled with sparsification methods by our group
[
65
,
66
]
.

In following work, we plan to employ the encoder-decoder U-Nets
[
67
]
, widely used in diffusion models
[
68
]
, to model the score function.
This will also provide latent space for the score that may be useful in providing more interpretability for UQ studies.
We will also pursue methods to embed physical constraints on the posterior, such as weight bounds and specialized NNs such as
input convex
NNs
[
69
]
.
We may also explore alternatives to Langevin sampling in the overall hypernetwork and seek methods that coordinate the Langevin samples
[
29
]
.

72

73

Consider an Euler-Maruyama approximation of two SDEs with the same diffusion term

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{k}+\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{x}_{k})\Delta{\tau}+\sigma{\Delta}\mathsf{B}_{k}

(A-1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{k}+\mathsf{g}(\mathsf{x}_{k})\Delta{\tau}+\sigma{\Delta}\mathsf{B}_{k}

(A-2)

where
\mathsf{B}
is Brownian motion (a Wiener process) and
\Delta{\mathsf{B}_{k}}\propto\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sqrt{\Delta{\tau}}\mathsf{I})
.
These SDEs generate a stochastic process,
\{\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{n}\}
, over times
\{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}\}
, which we associate with the distributions
q
and
p
, respectively.
In this appendix we will derive the KL divergence between these two processes, both in terms of sequence-to-sequence and sample-to-sample divergences.

We will use the notation,

\displaystyle q_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle q(\mathsf{x}_{k})

(A-3)

\displaystyle q_{k|j}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle q(\mathsf{x}_{k}|\mathsf{x}_{j})

(A-4)

\displaystyle q_{[i,j]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle q(\mathsf{x}_{i},\mathsf{x}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{j})

(A-5)

\displaystyle q_{[i,j]|[k,l]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle q(\mathsf{x}_{i},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{j}|\mathsf{x}_{k},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{l})

(A-6)

\displaystyle d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n}d\mathsf{x}_{k}

(A-7)

to simply the following developments
We assume the Markov property, i.e.,
q_{i+1|[0,i]}=q_{i+1|i}
; as a result, the distribution of the path is:

q_{[0,n]}=q_{0}q_{1|0}q_{2|1}\ldots q_{n|n-1}=q_{0}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}q_{k+1|k}=q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}

(A-8)

where
q_{0}
is the distribution of the initial state.
The distribution for
p_{[0,n]}
follows identically.
Note the following identities,

\displaystyle\int d\mathsf{x}_{[i,j]}q_{[i,j]|i-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

(A-9)

\displaystyle\ln q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln q_{0}+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\ln q_{l+1|l}

(A-10)

\displaystyle\int d\mathsf{x}_{[k,l]}q_{[i,j]|i-1}f(\mathsf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{[i,j]|i-1}\int d\mathsf{x}_{[k,l]}f(\mathsf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{n})\quad\mathrm{if}\ j<k.

(A-11)

The KL divergence between the sequences that
q
and
p
generate is,

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(q_{[0,n]}||p_{[0,n]})=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}||p_{0}p_{[1,n]|0})

(A-12)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}\left[\ln\left(q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}\right)-\ln\left(p_{0}p_{[1,n]|0}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}\left(\left(\ln q_{0}-\ln p_{0}\right)+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{0}\left(\ln q_{0}-\ln p_{0}\right)q_{[1,n]|0}+\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{0}q_{[1,n]|0}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathsf{x}_{0}q_{0}\left(\ln q_{0}-\ln p_{0}\right)\overbracket{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[1,n]}q_{[1,n]|0}}^{=1}+\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{[0,n]}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}q_{[0,n]}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,l]}\int d\mathsf{x}_{l+1}\int d\mathsf{x}_{[l+2,n]}q_{[0,l]}q_{l+1|l}q_{[l+2,n]|l+1}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,l]}q_{[0,l]}\int d\mathsf{x}_{l+1}q_{l+1|l}\left(q_{l+1|l}-p_{l+1|l}\right)\overbracket{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[l+2,n]}q_{[l+2,n]|l+1}}^{=1}

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{[0,l]}}\left[D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_{l+1|l}||p_{l+1|l}\right)\right].

Using the transition probabilities,

\displaystyle q_{l+1|l}=\mathcal{N}(\mathsf{x}_{l}+f(\mathsf{x}_{l},\tau_{l})\Delta\tau,{\bm{\Sigma}}\Delta\tau)

(A-13)

\displaystyle p_{l+1|l}=\mathcal{N}(\mathsf{x}_{l}+g(\mathsf{x}_{l},\tau_{l})\Delta\tau,{\bm{\Sigma}}\Delta\tau)

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(q_{l+1|l}||p_{l+1|l})=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})-g(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})\right)^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\left(f(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})-g(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})\right)\Delta t

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(q_{[0,n]}||p_{[0,n]})

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{[0,l]}}\left[||f(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})-g(\mathsf{x}_{l},t_{l})||_{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\Delta t.

(A-15)

In the limit,
n\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain a Riemann integral

D_{\text{KL}}(q||p)=D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\int_{0}^{\tau_{f}}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[||f(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\tau)-g(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\tau)||_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}\right]

(A-16)

where
q
is the distribution over the continuous in time stochastic process. The expectation can be estimated with Monte Carlo as,

D_{\text{KL}}(q||p)\approx D_{\text{KL}}(q_{0}||p_{0})+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\left[||f(\mathsf{x}_{m}(t),t)-g(\mathsf{x}_{m}(t),t)||_{\Sigma}^{2}\right]

(A-17)

where
\mathsf{x}_{m}
are sample trajectories.

Theorem 30.12.4 of Ref.
[
74
]
provides the inequality,

\left(\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}\right)\left[\ln\frac{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}}{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}p_{[0,n]}}\right]\leq\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}\left[q_{[0,n]}\ln\frac{q_{[0,n]}}{p_{[0,n]}}\right]

(A-18)

Integrating both sides we find,

\displaystyle\int d\mathsf{x}_{n}\,\left(\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}\right)\left[\ln\frac{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}}{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}p_{[0,n]}}\right]\leq\int d\mathsf{x}_{n}\,\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}\left[q_{[0,n]}\ln\frac{q_{[0,n]}}{p_{[0,n]}}\right]

(A-19)

\displaystyle\int d\mathsf{x}_{n}\,\left(\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}\right)\left[\ln\frac{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}q_{[0,n]}}{\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n-1]}p_{[0,n]}}\right]\leq\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}\,\left[q_{[0,n]}\ln\frac{q_{[0,n]}}{p_{[0,n]}}\right]

\displaystyle\int d\mathsf{x}_{n}q_{n}\ln\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\leq\int d\mathsf{x}_{[0,n]}\,\left[q_{[0,n]}\ln\frac{q_{[0,n]}}{p_{[0,n]}}\right]

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(q_{n}||p_{n})\leq D_{\text{KL}}(q_{[0,n]}||p_{[0,n]})

which provides a bound on the KL divergence of states at corresponding steps.

Appendix B
Langevin dynamics

Generalized Langevin dynamics are described by the SDE

\mathrm{d}\mathsf{y}=\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{y},t)\mathrm{d}t+{\bm{\eta}}

(B-1)

where
{\bm{\eta}}
is a noise process.
For this work we only need to examine classical, linear Langevin dynamics given by

\mathrm{d}\mathsf{y}=-{\bm{\Gamma}}({\mathsf{y}}-\bar{\mathsf{y}})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{t}

(B-2)

where
{\bm{\Gamma}}
is a positive definite matrix,
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is a covariance matrix, and
\mathrm{d}\mathsf{B}_{t}
is a standard multidimensional Brownian motion.
Diagonalizing
{\bm{\Sigma}}
leads to a set of uncorrelated 1D Langevin dynamics processes.

The classical 1D Langevin equation is an SDE

\mathrm{d}v=\underbrace{-\gamma({v}-\bar{v})}_{f(v)}\mathrm{d}t+\underbrace{\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}}_{\eta(t)}

(B-3)

where
\gamma
and
\sigma
are parameters controlling the decay to the mean
\bar{v}
and the magnitude of the random force
\eta
.
It is also known as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process.
Eq. (
B-3
) has the solution

v(t)=\bar{v}+(v_{0}-\bar{v})\exp\left(-\gamma t\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\exp\left(-\gamma(t-s)\right)\eta(s)

(B-4)

where the integral is an Itô integral.
The mean
\langle v\rangle\to\bar{v}
converges to
\bar{v}
since
\langle\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle=0

while the variance approaches

\langle(v-\bar{v})^{2}\rangle\to\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\eta^{2}\rangle

since

\langle(v-\bar{v})^{2}\rangle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[v_{0}^{2}\exp\left(-2\gamma t\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\left(1-\exp\left(-2\gamma t\right)\right)\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\eta^{2}\rangle

(B-5)

at the statistical steady state.
Eq. (
B-5
) is a form of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem stating that the variance of the solution is determined by the balance between the drag/drift and the random force/excitation.

Clearly the energy
U(v)
associated with the drift is

U(v)=\frac{\gamma}{2}(v-\bar{v})^{2}

(B-6)

so that

\nabla_{v}U(v)=-f(v)=\gamma(v-\bar{v})\ .

(B-7)

The Langevin equation can be reformulated as a Fokker–Planck equation that governs the probability distribution of the random variable which results in a normal state state distribution

\pi_{\infty}(v)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{2}}(v-\bar{v})^{2}\right).

(B-8)

Hence, if
\sigma^{2}=2
then

\log\pi_{\infty}(v)=U(v)+\text{constant}

(B-9)

which implies a specific noise magnitude
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
to sample the distribution connected to the drift.

Appendix C
The score function for the Langevin dynamics model

Here we provide the derivation for the score function corresponding to the parameters of the Langevin dynamics model presented in Section
4.1
.
Let
W
and
\bar{y}
be independent Gaussian random variables defined by:

W\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{W},\sigma_{W}^{2}),\quad\bar{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\bar{y}},\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}).

First, we will derive the joint probability density function (PDF) for
(W,\bar{y})
and then for
(W,b)
where
b=-W\bar{y}
.
Given that
W
and
\bar{y}
are independent random variables, their joint PDF is given by

f(W,\mathsf{y})=f_{W}(W)\cdot f_{\mathsf{y}}(\mathsf{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{W}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{(W-\mu_{W})^{2}}{2\sigma_{W}^{2}}}\right)\exp\left({-\frac{(\bar{y}-\mu_{\bar{y}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}}}\right).

To get to the PDF of
(W,b)
, we first set
\bar{y}
to
\bar{y}=-b/W
. The Jacobian of the transformation from
(W,\bar{y})
to
(W,b)
is:

J=\begin{vmatrix}\frac{\partial W}{\partial W}&\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bar{y}}\\
\frac{\partial b}{\partial W}&\frac{\partial b}{\partial\bar{y}}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\
-\bar{y}&-W\end{vmatrix}=-W.

The joint PDF of
(W,b)
is then given by:

f(W,b)=f(W,-\frac{b}{W})\cdot\frac{1}{|J|}=\frac{1}{|W|2\pi\sigma_{W}\sigma_{\bar{y}}}\exp\left({-\frac{(W-\mu_{W})^{2}}{2\sigma_{W}^{2}}}\right)\exp\left({-\frac{\left(b/W+\mu_{\bar{y}}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}}}\right).

The logarithm of the joint PDF:

\log f(W,b)=-\log|W|-\log(2\pi)-\log\sigma_{W}-\log\sigma_{\bar{y}}-\frac{(W-\mu_{W})^{2}}{2\sigma_{W}^{2}}-\frac{\left(b/W+\mu_{\bar{y}}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}}.

(C-1)

Finally, the partial derivatives w.r.t
W
and
b
are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log f(W,b)}{\partial W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{W}-\frac{W-\mu_{W}}{\sigma_{W}^{2}}+\frac{b}{\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}W^{2}}\left(b/W+\mu_{\bar{y}}\right)

(C-2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log f(W,b)}{\partial b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\bar{y}}^{2}W}\left(b/W+\mu_{\bar{y}}\right).

(C-3)
