DH-EAC: Design of a Dynamic, Hierarchical Entanglement Access Control Protocol
Akihisa Takahashi, Yoshito Tobe

Abstract
We propose Dynamic, Hierarchical Entanglement Access Control (DH–EAC), a pure–quantum protocol for fair and anonymous allocation of scarce entanglement across wide–area quantum networks composed of many quantum LANs (QLANs). Prior Dicke–state–based pure–quantum MACs resolve contention by local measurements without classical signaling, but they mainly target a single QLAN under static conditions; extending them to wide–area, dynamic settings while avoiding post–selection reconciliation remains open. DH–EAC adopts a two–layer pure–quantum lottery: the outer layer selects winning QLANs and the inner layer selects winning nodes within each winning QLAN. A key design principle is that both the winning set and the per–QLAN quota are fixed by measurements alone , so the contention loop requires no classical round–trip. The protocol thus aims to jointly satisfy anonymity (no node IDs revealed until decisions are fixed) and fairness (bias suppression under heterogeneous QLAN sizes). We also provide analytical models for success probability and latency under a standard i.i.d. loss model, and we evaluate DH–EAC against two baselines—single–layer Dicke within one QLAN and a classical GO–driven allocator—using a minimal, reproducible set of scenarios. Metrics include success probability, end–to–end latency, throughput, and Jain’s fairness index. The results indicate that DH–EAC offers an implementable design point in the space of entanglement access control, balancing pure–quantum contention resolution, anonymity, and scalability for multi–QLAN networks.



1
Introduction

The quantum internet is a new networking substrate that enables the distribution, storage, and processing of quantum states over distance, opening the door to capabilities that are impossible in classical networks—information-theoretically secure key distribution, distributed quantum computation, and coordinated quantum sensing, among others
[
1
,
2
]
. The central resource is entanglement, and deciding
which
set of nodes receives entanglement,
when
, and under
what
anonymity and fairness constraints—i.e.,
entanglement access control
(EAC)—is a fundamental problem in the quantum networking stack.

Recent work has proposed a Dicke-state-based pure-quantum MAC (Dicke-EAC) that resolves contention by local measurements without classical signaling during the lottery phase
[
3
]
. This approach is an important step toward granting access fairly while preserving the anonymity of winners. However, prior art largely targets a single QLAN and leaves open several design questions for practical wide-area deployment: handling dynamic demand (the requested number of winners varies per job), mitigating bias caused by heterogeneous QLAN sizes, and minimizing post-selection reconciliation while preserving anonymity.

Our goal is to close these gaps by designing
Dynamic, Hierarchical EAC (DH–EAC)
for wide-area networks composed of multiple QLANs that (i) keeps classical round-trips out of the contention decision loop (with physical-layer feed-forward treated outside the protocol layer), (ii) preserves the anonymity of the winning set until decisions are fixed, (iii) sustains fairness under QLAN-size heterogeneity, and (iv) scales to dynamically changing demand. 5
concludes and outlines future work.

2
Related Work

This section reviews prior work from the perspectives of (i) pure–quantum entanglement access control (EAC), (ii) entanglement routing/scheduling and fairness metrics, (iii) Dicke–state synthesis and symmetric–state preparation, (iv) coherent data loading via quantum read–only memory (QROM), and (v) network simulation foundations.

2.1
Pure–quantum EAC (Dicke–based MAC)

Dicke–state–based pure–quantum MACs fix the winning set by local measurements without delegating contention resolution to classical signaling, thereby providing a framework that preserves anonymity and privacy
[
3
]
. Illiano
et al.
analyze fairness, anonymity, and noise tolerance using many–body Dicke states together with ancilla registers. Their setting, however, primarily targets a single QLAN; simultaneous lotteries across multiple QLANs in wide–area networks and mechanisms that minimize classical reconciliation after selection while preserving anonymity are not treated explicitly. Our work retains the pure–quantum MAC philosophy but contributes a hierarchical extension to multiple QLANs and a design that
simultaneously
fixes both the winning set and per–QLAN quotas by measurement alone—this is the core novelty of DH–EAC.

2.2
Entanglement routing/scheduling and fairness

In parallel to EAC, resource allocation has advanced on the routing/scheduling side. Recent surveys summarize design constraints that differ from classical networking, such as probabilistic swap success, fidelity thresholds, and limited memory lifetimes
[
4
]
. Concrete designs include maximizing simultaneous entanglement generation across multiple demands and proposals for asynchronous routing
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, as well as online or learning–based scheduling
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Many of these works employ
Jain’s index
to quantify fairness—either per demand or per path—and consider joint optimization with throughput/latency trade–offs
[
11
,
5
]
. While DH–EAC does not target routing per se, we adopt Jain’s index as the fairness metric so that contention resolution and allocation at the EAC layer align with fairness practices in upper–layer routing/scheduling.

2.3
Dicke–state synthesis and symmetric–state preparation

Feasibility of DH–EAC relies on realizable circuits for Dicke states. Bärtschi and Eidenbenz give deterministic circuits with depth
O(k\log(n/k))
under all–to–all connectivity and
O(\sqrt{nk})
on grids
[
12
]
. Leveraging mid–circuit measurements and feed–forward, recent work further achieves polylogarithmic scaling in depth/ancilla for Dicke preparation
[
13
]
. Beyond single Dicke states, variational and global–operation frameworks have been proposed to synthesize the entire symmetric subspace (including superpositions of Dicke states) efficiently
[
14
]
. These advances collectively push Dicke/symmetric–state preparation to practical depths and support the implementability of DH–EAC, which assumes Dicke synthesis at both outer and inner layers.

2.4
QROM and coherent table lookup (data loading)

To make selection and allocation finalize
simultaneously
, DH–EAC coherently loads candidates of
capacity–constrained integer partitions
into ancilla registers. Quantum read–only memory (QROM) provides a circuit primitive for conditioned table lookup with low
T
complexity
[
15
,
16
]
, followed by optimizations and practical compilation techniques
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Because QROM can restrict lookups to partitions consistent with the measured outer Dicke outcome, it aligns naturally with DH–EAC’s requirements of anonymity and pure–quantum contention resolution.

3
Proposed Method

3.1
System Model

We consider a wide-area quantum network composed of multiple, independently operated quantum LANs (QLANs). Let
m
denote the number of QLANs and
\mathrm{LO}_{i}
the
Local Orchestrator
that controls QLAN
i
. Each QLAN contains a set of end nodes; the per-QLAN upper bound on concurrently assignable entanglement endpoints at lottery time (the number of
available
nodes) is written
n_{i}^{\mathrm{avail}}
. Above the QLANs sits a
Global Orchestrator
(GO) that accepts application requests, prepares the outer-layer lottery, and delivers the necessary quantum states (outer-layer qubits and ancilla registers) to each LO.

Applications submit a classical request
\mathrm{EAC\_Request}(k_{\mathrm{req}},\mathrm{SLA})
to the GO, where
k_{\mathrm{req}}
is the total number of nodes to be selected and
\mathrm{SLA}
encodes latency/fidelity thresholds and retry limits. The GO accesses only the
aggregate statistics

\{n_{i}^{\mathrm{avail}}\}
collected from LOs (it never handles individual node IDs). From this point on, contention resolution is performed in a
pure–quantum
manner: the outer and inner lotteries (and thus both winner determination and quota fixing) are decided solely by local measurements, and no classical control round–trips are introduced in the contention loop
[
3
]
. The 2-bit feed–forward required by quantum teleportation is permitted as a physical-layer control signal and is considered outside the protocol-layer definition of pure–quantum operation
[
20
]
. After the lottery completes, fidelity notifications and a hash of the winning set (
\mathrm{WIN\_SET}
) are sent over a minimal classical management plane without compromising anonymity (no IDs revealed until decisions are fixed).

DH–EAC implements a two-layer structure. The
outer
layer resolves competition across QLANs: the GO prepares a many-body symmetric (Dicke-type) state together with per-LO ancilla registers and teleports them to LOs. A local
Z
-measurement on the outer state determines, at each LO, whether its QLAN belongs to the winning set. Simultaneously, measuring the local ancilla yields the per-QLAN quota
k_{i}
that the LO must allocate in the inner layer—
the core idea is to embed both the winning set and the per-QLAN quota in the same quantum superposition and to finalize them by measurement alone
. The
inner
layer resolves competition within each winning QLAN:
\mathrm{LO}_{i}
prepares a local many-body symmetric state consistent with
k_{i}
, teleports it to its nodes, and each node’s local measurement determines the final winners. Because both layers terminate by local measurements, the contention decision loop requires no classical round–trip.

Anonymity in this model hinges on two points: (i) no node IDs are exposed during the lottery phase; and (ii) the GO handles only aggregate quantities
\{n_{i}^{\mathrm{avail}}\}
and never retains per-node identifiers. Fairness emphasizes bias suppression under heterogeneous QLAN sizes; in the evaluation we verify this with standard metrics such as Jain’s index (see
Section

4
). The model above underpins the protocol flow (outer/inner time sequence) and core logic (quantum encoding that enables simultaneous determination of winners and quotas).

3.2

The
Dynamic, Hierarchical Entanglement Access Control
protocol (DH–EAC) determines the outer-layer winning QLANs and their quotas in one shot, then selects winning nodes inside each winning QLAN. The decision is completed by local measurements; no classical round–trip appears in the contention loop. The outer Dicke state and ancilla registers are prepared so that, via coherent table lookup, only quota vectors consistent with the measured outer outcome can appear. Inner-layer state synthesis uses known short-depth circuits.

4
Evaluation

This section quantitatively compares the proposed DH–EAC against a classical GO–driven allocator (B2) and, when applicable, the single–layer Dicke baseline confined to one QLAN (B1–CoLocate). We report end–to–end latency, success probability, and throughput; fairness is examined in depth for DH–EAC alone. The target setting is multi–QLAN demand, and the central question is whether pure–quantum contention resolution scales effectively.

4.1
Evaluation Goals

We address the following questions:

•

Q1 (scaling).
As the number of QLANs
m
increases, can DH–EAC curb latency growth by “one outer round + parallel inner layer”?

•

Q2 (completed requests per unit time).
Even accounting for differences in success probability, does there exist a region where DH–EAC achieves higher throughput
\mathrm{THR}=P/\mathbb{E}[L]
?

•

Q3 (fairness).
Under capacity skew and varying demand, can DH–EAC maintain a high node–level Jain index?

4.2
Compared Schemes

DH–EAC (proposed).

A two–layer design: the outer layer anonymously selects winning QLANs via a Dicke measurement, and the inner layer reselects nodes within winners. Safe–Select–
K
fixes the minimal number of participating QLANs; inner–layer quotas are approximated by capacity–proportional integer rounding (while strictly respecting capacity limits).

B1–CoLocate (reference).

Executed only when the entire request fits inside a single QLAN; not applicable across multiple QLANs.

B2 (GO classical selection).

The GO determines the winning QLANs/nodes by consulting available capacities and notifies LOs classically. No outer quantum delivery is required, which tends to raise success probability, but the GO
\leftrightarrow
LO classical round–trips increase latency in proportion to
m
.

4.10
Summary of Findings

(i) In latency scaling, B2’s classical synchronization becomes dominant as
m
grows, yielding clear regions where DH–EAC is faster. (ii) Although B2 can be superior in success probability, gaps are modest in low–to–moderate loss; in throughput DH–EAC overtakes in large–scale regimes. (iii) DH–EAC maintains high fairness under capacity skew and scale.

5
Conclusion

This work designed
Dynamic, Hierarchical EAC
(DH–EAC) for cross–QLAN entanglement allocation, in which QLANs are selected at an outer layer and nodes at an inner layer by pure–quantum lotteries, and in which the winning set and the capacity–constrained per–QLAN quotas are finalized
simultaneously
within a pure–quantum design. Our evaluation showed that the structure of “one outer execution + parallel inner layer” curbs latency growth as the number of QLANs increases, yielding relative latency below that of a classical allocator over wide parameter ranges. Although success probability favors the classical scheme (which has no outer quantum delivery), the gap is modest in low–to–moderate loss regimes; when latency is factored in, DH–EAC achieves higher completed requests per unit time in large–scale, moderate–load conditions. Regarding fairness, DH–EAC maintained node–level Jain indices around 0.995 or higher at medium–to–high demand, and remained near 0.94 even under low demand with extreme capacity skew. By keeping classical round–trips out of the contention decision loop, DH–EAC preserves anonymity—no node IDs are revealed until decisions are fixed. Altogether, for wide–area deployments with many QLANs where both anonymity and scalability are required, DH–EAC emerges as a practically viable option.

Future directions include extending the physical model to incorporate link correlations and memory lifetimes/fidelity evolution, compressing and optimizing the allocation table via QROM compilation techniques, and conducting testbed implementations together with joint evaluations that coordinate DH–EAC with upper–layer routing and scheduling.
