From guidelines to practice: general practitioners' consensus on indicators for managing selected multimorbidity in primary care in Western Australia
Authors: Sage M C Ishimwe, Timothy A Carey, Jacquita S Affandi, Ninh Thi Ha, Amna Mushtaq, Sayyida Anees, Christopher M Reid, Dan Xu, Delia Hendrie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity is rising in primary care, yet adherence to guidelines is hindered by fragmented, disease-specific recommendations. This study sought to establish consensus among general practitioners (GPs) on indicators of adherence to Australian guidelines in managing five common chronic conditions [type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD)], aiming to develop a unified tool for holistic care.MethodsA two-round reactive Delphi survey was conducted with 21 GPs in Western Australia, following RAND methodology. Indicators were drawn from the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' guidelines and covered biophysical markers, lifestyle risks, and pharmacological management. Participants rated indicators for relevance and clarity on a 4-point Likert scale without a neutral option. Consensus was defined as ≥70% of GPs rating an indicator ≥3 for mean relevance. The process ended when ≥80% of indicators reached this threshold, and no new items were proposed. Feedback refined wording and, where relevant, was reported verbatim.ResultsIn Round 1, 57 indicators achieved consensus on relevance, with four additional items suggested. By Round 2, consensus was reached on 61 indicators. Most were rated clear, except two addressing pharmacological management of CKD with T2DM, which require clearer guidance. Feedback emphasized the need for refined lipid management guidelines, prioritizing obesity management for prevention, and challenges in lifestyle counselling.ConclusionThis study represents the first integration of disease-specific indicators into a GP-informed tool for multimorbidity management. Strong consensus highlights its practicality, warranting piloting to evaluate effects on guideline adherence and patient outcomes.

Background

Chronic diseases remain a major global public health challenge. Multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions, affects an estimated 37% of the global population, placing growing pressure on primary healthcare systems [1]. In Australia, 9.7 million people (38%) were living with multimorbidity in 2022; among them, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) co-occurred in 25% of people with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and 24% of those with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2, 3].

Managing multimorbidity in primary care is complex. Although disease-specific guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations, they are difficult to apply to patients with multiple conditions, particularly within time-limited consultations [4, 5]. This often leads to fragmented care, implementation gaps, and clinician fatigue. Delivering effective care also requires healthcare providers to adapt guidelines to individual patient needs using clinical judgment [6, 7]. However, adherence is inconsistent due to variations in knowledge, limited resources, and the challenge of balancing multiple, sometimes conflicting, guidelines [4, 8]. As a result, discrepancies between recommended and actual care are frequently observed [9, 10].

General practitioners (GPs) frequently report difficulty reconciling multiple disease-specific guidelines because of conflicting recommendations, inconsistent prioritization, and limited integration across conditions [11–14]. This misalignment can produce suboptimal care plans that overlook patient preferences or the cumulative treatment burden [12, 15]. Despite the prevalence of multimorbidity, clinical decision-making remains driven largely by single-disease guidelines, leaving GPs without adequate tools to support integrated care [16–18].

Guideline adherence is essential for improving patient outcomes and minimizing clinical error or legal risks [19], yet it is difficult to maintain in complex, time-pressured environments. This underscores the need for practical, user-friendly tools to support clinicians in delivering guideline-based care. To our knowledge, no existing guideline addresses multimorbidity. This study aimed to address this need by achieving expert consensus among GPs on key indicators reflecting adherence to Australian guidelines for five common and interrelated chronic conditions: T2DM, obesity, hypertension, CVD, and CKD. The innovation of this study lies in consolidating disease-specific indicators into a unified, peer-informed checklist to support integrated care. By integrating indicators into a simplified tool, this study provides the groundwork for developing multimorbidity management guidelines tailored to the realities of primary care.

Methods

Study design

The study followed the RAND Methodological Guidance for Conducting and Critically Appraising Delphi Panels [20]. The Delphi method, a structured and iterative process for reaching expert consensus, was applied to define indicators of adherence to the selected chronic conditions management guidelines. A reactive Delphi approach, in which experts review pre-prepared items rather than generating them, was chosen to minimize participant burden while maintaining rigour [21].

Participants and setting

The study was conducted online and involved GPs across Western Australia (WA). Participants, also referred to as “panellists”, were recruited through the research team's professional networks. A sampling grid was used to track recruitment targets, consent, and completion rates, with a cap of two GPs per general practice to maximize diversity of perspectives.

Recruitment, consent, and survey platform

Surveys were developed in REDCap, a secure web platform for managing questionnaires [22]. GPs were invited by email, reviewed a study information sheet, and provided e-consent before completing a brief demographic questionnaire and the Round 1 survey. Each participant was assigned an identification number for use in Round 2 to link feedback across rounds. The study aimed to retain at least 10 GPs to the final round, consistent with recommended panel sizes for homogeneous Delphi studies [23, 24]. Accounting for attrition, 21 GPs were recruited. In collaboration with the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP), participants were eligible for continuing professional development points.

Development of initial indicators

The initial set of indicators was developed by GP researchers from the research team based on the RACGP guidelines [25]. These indicators encompassed three interdependent aspects of chronic disease management: biophysical and special investigations, lifestyle risk factors [smoking, nutrition, alcohol, physical activity, and sleep (SNAPS)], and pharmacological management. The initial list of indicators is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Consensus rules

Indicators were assessed for relevance and clarity using 4-point Likert scales, and mean scores were calculated. Items were retained when at least 70% of panellists rated them 3 or 4, yielding a mean score of ≥3.0. Items with a mean relevance score of <3.0 were excluded, while those with a mean clarity score of <3.0 were revised based on participant feedback and recirculated. GPs could also propose new indicators or suggest rewording.

Delphi procedures

A 2-week pilot phase was conducted to set up REDCap and test survey clarity. Panellists completed surveys between April and August 2025. After Round 1 analysis, an ethics amendment covering newly added items was approved before the Round 2 survey was distributed. Participants had 14 days to complete each round, and non-responders received two reminders, each extending the deadline by 14 days.

Completion time ranged from 20 to 90 minutes, depending on the level of feedback provided. After the amendment was approved, a revised Round 2 survey, including new and reworded indicators, was distributed to those who had completed Round 1. The process concluded after Round 2, when ≥80% of indicators reached consensus, and no new items were proposed. After each round, participants received individual feedback against group consensus and a thank-you note.

Stopping criteria

The Delphi was stopped once at least 70% of GPs rated ≥80% of indicators as relevant (score ≥3), and no further indicators were suggested.

Data analysis

Survey responses were analyzed in Microsoft Excel using descriptive statistics. For each indicator, we calculated the number (N) and percentage (%) of GPs rating it as relevant or clear, and mean relevance and clarity scores with standard deviations (SD). Results were presented as mean ± SD and N (%). Relevant verbatim comments were presented narratively and/or used to refine the indicators.

Section A: Biophysical and special investigations

For T2DM, HbA1c checks received the maximum relevance (3.90 ± 0.29; 100%) and clarity (3.81 ± 0.39; 100%) scores. Annual uACR and eGFR checks were also endorsed. Lipid and CVD risk assessments achieved consensus. Retinopathy and neuropathy screening were missed in this round but addressed later. In obesity, BMI and waist circumference monitoring achieved consensus, and BP checks and CVD risk assessment were also endorsed as relevant and clear. Screening for comorbidities (HbA1c, liver function, lipids) was endorsed. For hypertension, urea/creatinine, electrolytes, albuminuria/proteinuria checks, and lipid/CVD assessments achieved near-maximum agreement. Fasting glucose, microscopic haematuria, and 12-lead electrocardiogram were also endorsed. GP researchers further suggested adding two indicators: BP monitoring and retinopathy screening. In CVD, BP monitoring, BMI, waist circumference, lipids, uACR, and HbA1c checks were endorsed. For CKD, annual eGFR, uACR, urea/creatinine, electrolytes, and comorbidity screening (HbA1c, lipids, BP, BMI, waist circumference) achieved agreement.

One panellist stressed the importance of more frequent HbA1c monitoring in poorly controlled T2DM.

“It is important to check HBA1c every 3 months in patients with poorly controlled T2DM to adjust medication and normalise blood sugar levels quickly. That will minimise complications and end organ damage.” (GP-14, Round 1).

The same panellist described lipid testing guidelines as unclear, particularly regarding frequency, target values, and management.

“Lipid profile testing is not very clear. There are guidelines for statin therapy, but as there is conspiracy about testing frequency, targeting values and management. It will be great if we have clear guidelines of cholesterol management.” (GP-14, Round 1).

Lastly, the panellist underscored obesity management as central to chronic disease prevention, urging stronger policy and funding support.

“Obesity management is vital of all chronic disease management. It is the foundation of healthy society. Need more consideration and value for obesity management, specifically government funds.” (GP-14, Round 1).

Section B: Lifestyle risk factor modification

There was strong consensus on smoking cessation (relevance: 3.86 ± 0.35; 100%, clarity: (3.76 ± 0.53; 95%), alcohol reduction (3.81 ± 0.39; 100% for both), physical activity, and weight management. Three panellists found the physical activity options unclear, leading to the indicator being reworded after re-consulting the guidelines. Condition-specific nutritional advice, including obesity-related psychological interventions, was strongly supported. GP researchers suggested adding sleep pattern screening.

One panellist observed that smoking cessation advice is usually well received, but alcohol counselling is harder to implement because of social norms.

“Patients are often receptive to this [smoking cessation advice],” but noted that alcohol reduction is “more difficult to institute due to alcohol's place in society—patients are more often pre-contemplative with this than with smoking cessation.” (GP-05, Round 1).

The same panellist described nutritional counselling as complex but suggested practical strategies like calorie counting and calorie-restricted diet trial.

“Calorie counting and trial of calorie-restricted diets (e.g. Lite n Easy) can be useful as a guide for patients.” (GP-05, Round 1).

Lastly, the panellist highlighted barriers including cultural diversity, inconsistent dietary programs, and limited psychological support.

“Many dietary programs with uncertain efficacy and varying advice from dietitians,” and described it as “somewhat difficult due to different cultures and dietary preferences.” (GP-05, Round 1).

“Patients are often reluctant to engage in psychological counselling programs, and there is a lack of psychologists specialised in this area.” (GP-05, Round 1).

Section C: Pharmacological management

For T2DM, first-line Metformin and second-line add-ons [Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2), Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4), sulphonylureas, Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP1)] all reached consensus. Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) for comorbid hypertension or microalbuminuria were endorsed, as were SGLT2 in T2DM with CKD or CVD (Heart failure). In obesity, adjunct pharmacotherapy was rated relevant (3.24 ± 0.81; 76%), but clarity ratings fell below the 70% threshold (3.05 ± 1.05; 68%). The indicator was therefore reworded after reconsulting the guidelines. Combining metformin with SGLT2 or GLP1 in T2DM and obesity achieved consensus. For hypertension, first-line therapy with ACEi/ARBs, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), or diuretics was endorsed, with early addition of a second drug from these classes before increasing the initial medication to its maximum dose. Comorbidity management was well supported, including ACEi/ARBs for CKD, ACEi + beta-blockers for myocardial infarction (MI), and beta-blockers/CCBs for Angina. For CVD, consensus was strong on BP-lowering, lipid-modifying, and antiplatelet therapy. In comorbid cases, ACEi, beta-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) were endorsed for heart failure with hypertension, while SGLT2 inhibitors were supported for T2DM with CVD. For CKD, ACEi/ARBs achieved consensus as first-line therapy, with SGLT2 inhibitors endorsed for CKD with proteinuria regardless of T2DM status. Statins ± ezetimibe were supported in CKD with high CVD risk, and GLP1-RA achieved consensus in CKD with T2DM. The use of non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (nsMRAs) for CKD with T2DM and albuminuria was considered relevant (3.19 ± 0.73; 81%), but clarity was low (2.81 ± 0.96; 62%). These last two indicators were therefore reworded after reconsulting the guidelines.

Three new indicators were added: one for T2DM and two for hypertension. Section B: Lifestyle risk factor modification

Two indicators, one new and one reworded, were included for all five conditions. Screening for sleep patterns/disorders achieved consensus (relevance 3.57 ± 0.62; 93%, clarity 3.43 ± 0.62; 93%). Regular physical activity was also deemed clear (3.21 ± 0.86; 71%).

Pharmacological management

Three reworded indicators were rated for clarity—one for obesity and two for CKD. For obesity, adjunct pharmacotherapy in patients with BMI >30 kg/m2, or 27–30 kg/m2 with obesity-related complications and inadequate response to lifestyle interventions, was rated 3.36 ± 0.81; 79%. For CKD coexisting with T2DM, the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists was rated unclear (3.00 ± 1.13; 64%), and the use of nsMRAs for CKD with T2DM and albuminuria was also rated unclear (2.93 ± 1.03; 71%). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to establish expert consensus on a unified checklist of key indicators representing adherence to clinical guidelines for T2DM, obesity, hypertension, CVD, and CKD. Organized into three complementary domains, 61 indicators were consolidated into a practical tool for primary care. Consensus was reached across all indicators within two survey rounds, with high agreement and minimal ambiguity, underscoring their clinical relevance and shared acceptance among GPs. Existing guidelines acknowledge multimorbidity but remain anchored to their primary disease focus. The American Diabetes Association's 2023 Standards of Care centre on diabetes while also addressing CVD and CKD [26]. The American College of Cardiology's Expert Consensus Pathway integrates strategies for atherosclerotic CVD and other chronic conditions [27], yet its emphasis remains cardiovascular health, and multimorbidity is not explicitly specified. Both guidelines therefore lean toward their core domains. In contrast, our study offers an integrated framework that brings together five interrelated conditions, which often act as risk factors or complications for one another. This approach reflects the realities of primary care and supports more coherent, guideline-aligned management of multimorbidity. Importantly, this study was not intended to produce a comprehensive multimorbidity guideline or to imply that every indicator applies to every patient. Instead, it aimed to identify a core, GP-endorsed set of guideline-based indicators to serve as a structured prompt for patients with coexisting conditions. Use of the tool still requires clinical judgment and consideration of patient preferences, contraindications, and treatment burden. The indicators were also not designed to be ranked across conditions, as prioritization cannot easily be separated from the strength and currency of the underlying evidence, particularly for evolving pharmacological recommendations. Future studies should therefore examine how these indicators can be prioritized according to evidence strength, treatment burden, and individual patient context.

Variations in clarity ratings, most notably for pharmacological management of CKD with T2DM, highlighted areas needing refinement. Although the research team revised wording based on guidelines and literature, two indicators still scored low on clarity. This suggests either limited applicability in practice or ambiguity within existing guidelines, consistent with literature that cites complexity, workload, and time constraints as barriers in primary care [4, 28–32]. Feedback reinforced these challenges, with panellists pointing to unclear LDL targets for CKD, difficulties implementing lifestyle changes such as alcohol reduction and dietary advice, and structural barriers to accessing specialized services. These reflections informed refinements to the indicators and underscore the need for future guidelines to address clinical ambiguities and real-world implementation barriers.

The Delphi panel consisted mainly of mid-career GPs with extensive experience in chronic disease management and active CPD engagement, ensuring feedback was both informed and current. Although most were metropolitan-based, their input reflected diverse perspectives from everyday general practice. The co-design process was central to producing indicators that were evidence-based yet feasible in real-world settings, increasing their likelihood of adoption. This aligns with literature showing that co-designed interventions for multimorbidity achieve greater acceptability and contextual fit when end-users are involved from the outset [33]. With multimorbidity rising and disease-specific guidelines remaining fragmented, this study represents a necessary step toward integrated, guideline-based tools. These tools can strengthen primary care delivery and improve outcomes for patients with complex needs.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its rigorous Delphi methodology, which provided a structured and transparent approach to synthesizing expert opinion in the absence of comprehensive multimorbidity guidelines [34, 35]. The iterative process allowed systematic refinement of indicators and highlighted both consensus and areas of tension. While there is no single universally recommended Likert scale for Delphi studies [36], the 4-point Likert scale may have offered less discrimination than a broader scale. Involving experienced GPs as participants and co-developers enhances the clinical validity and practical relevance of the final tool. The study also has limitations. Although the panel size was within the recommended range for Delphi studies [23, 24], it was relatively small, and a 33% drop out rate may have reduced the diversity of perspectives in the final round. However, there was minimal to no disagreement among panellists. Most participants were metropolitan-based, which could limit applicability to rural and remote contexts, though national guidelines apply across all practice settings. Finally, the anonymity of the Delphi process minimizes bias and supports independent judgment but may restrict opportunities for deeper exploration of the reasoning behind participants' ratings.

Conclusion

This study presents the first co-designed tool to support integrated, guideline-aligned care for multimorbidity in primary care. By consolidating 61 evidence-based indicators across T2DM, obesity, hypertension, CVD, and CKD, it addresses the limitations of fragmented, disease-specific guidelines. The Delphi process, involving experienced GPs, achieved strong consensus suggesting clinical relevance. Feedback informed the final tool for practicability and highlighted the need for clearer guidance for real-world implementation. The resulting tool offers a feasible framework for translating complex guidelines into coordinated care. Future research should pilot the tool across diverse geographical and practice settings and explore its potential integration into decision support platforms used in primary care.
