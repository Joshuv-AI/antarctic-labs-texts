Visual, Tomographic, and Biomechanical Outcomes of CAIRS with Accelerated Cross-Linking in Moderate to Advanced Keratoconus: A 6-Month Retrospective Case Series
Authors: Nauman Hashmani, Sharif Hashmani, Javaria Saleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the visual, tomographic, and biomechanical effects of corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments (CAIRS) combined with accelerated epithelium-off corneal cross-linking (CXL) in patients with moderate to advanced keratoconus.MethodsThis retrospective case series included 40 eyes of 40 patients with keratoconus. CAIRS segments were prepared from donor corneal tissue stored in Netisol medium and implanted via femtosecond laser-created intrastromal channels. Accelerated epithelium-off CXL (10 mW/cm2 for 9 minutes; total energy 5.4 J/cm2) was performed immediately after CAIRS implantation; an individualized Sub400 protocol was applied in eyes with post-implantation pachymetry below 400 µm. Outcomes at baseline and 6 months included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), spherical equivalent, keratometric indices, pachymetry, higher-order aberrations (HOAs), and Corvis ST biomechanical parameters.ResultsMedian BCVA improved from 0.52 to 0.22 logMAR (p=0.005), and spherical equivalent improved from −9.3 D to −4.1 D (p=0.007). K1, K2, Km, and Kmax showed statistically significant flattening (all p<0.05). HOAs and coma did not significantly change. SP-A1 decreased from 52.7 to 51.2 mmHg/mm (p=0.015), while integrated radius showed no significant difference. One eye (2.5%) developed donor segment opacification within the first postoperative week without functional visual consequence; BCVA improved in this eye. No rejection, extrusion, or other adverse events were recorded.ConclusionCAIRS combined with accelerated CXL demonstrated significant improvements in visual acuity, refractive error, and keratometric parameters at 6 months. These findings suggest that CAIRS may represent a promising biological alternative to synthetic intracorneal ring segments, warranting further investigation through prospective controlled studies with longer follow-up.

Introduction

Keratoconus is a progressive corneal ectasia that leads to irregular astigmatism and visual impairment.1 Treatment options span a wide spectrum: contact lenses remain effective even in advanced stages, while corneal cross-linking (CXL) is most beneficial when initiated in early progressive disease to halt ectatic progression. Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS) are typically reserved for patients with more advanced irregular astigmatism who are intolerant of or inadequately corrected by contact lenses. While synthetic ICRS, such as INTACS and Kerarings, have been effective in improving corneal topography, they carry risks of complications like extrusion and anterior stromal necrosis.2 CAIRS achieves corneal flattening through an arc-shortening effect: the implanted stromal ring mechanically redistributes tension within the corneal stroma, flattening the central ectatic zone while inducing compensatory mid-peripheral steepening.2,3

Corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments (CAIRS) offer a promising alternative, using donor corneal stromal tissue to achieve similar biomechanical effects with potentially fewer complications. Studies have demonstrated significant improvements in corneal regularity and visual acuity with CAIRS.2,4 The concept of combining intrastromal ring implantation with concurrent CXL for simultaneous biomechanical stabilization and optical rehabilitation was first described for synthetic segments by Rattan et al, who reported significant refractive and keratometric improvements with intrapocket collagen CXL combined with complete ring implantation.5 This study evaluates the safety and efficacy of CAIRS, analyzing its impact on corneal shape and vision in 40 eyes of 40 patients. Additionally, to our knowledge this is the first peer-reviewed study to evaluate corneal biomechanical changes after combined CAIRS and CXL.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective case series was conducted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of CAIRS in keratoconus patients at the Hashmanis Group of Hospitals, Karachi, Pakistan. A total of 40 eyes from 40 patients diagnosed with keratoconus were included. All patients were CXL-naive; no candidate had undergone prior corneal cross-linking. Patients with documented progressive keratoconus, defined as an increase in Kmax of more than 1.00 D on two consecutive Pentacam examinations within a 12-month period, together with irregular astigmatism, and sufficient stromal thickness to allow safe ring implantation were selected. Exclusion criteria included severe allergies, autoimmune diseases, prior viral keratitis, central corneal scarring, or any previous ocular surgery except for CXL. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Hashmanis Group of Hospitals (approval number: 2206–11054). No funding was obtained for this manuscript.

Surgical Technique

CAIRS segments were prepared from preserved donor corneal tissue stored in Optisol-GS equivalent medium (Netisol; Sauflon Pharmaceuticals, UK), ensuring complete removal of the epithelium and endothelium prior to use. Circular stromal ring segments were excised using a double-bladed trephine (Madhu Instruments, New Delhi, India) with an outer diameter of 6.5 mm to 8.0 mm; the arc length of each segment was selected according to the degree of corneal steepness on the preoperative sagittal curvature map, with longer arcs used for steeper cones. In eyes classified as Amsler-Krumeich Stage 3 or 4 (advanced keratoconus), two segments were implanted to achieve greater flattening effect; a single segment was used in all other cases. Once excised, segments were marked on Bowman’s membrane using a surgical marker. They were subsequently placed on their side and flattened using forceps to expel residual fluid, then covered for 15 minutes to facilitate dehydration; additional time was allowed as needed until adequate dehydration was achieved.

Intracorneal channels were created using the WaveLight FS200 femtosecond laser (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) at a depth corresponding to 50% of the minimum stromal thickness, with a maximum depth of 280 microns. Channel inner diameters ranged from 4.6 mm to 6.6 mm and were selected based on the cone location and the planned segment arc length. The channel was centered on the coaxially sighted corneal light reflex to ensure precise placement. A central mark was made prior to docking to ensure central placement of the channels. CAIRS segments were carefully inserted using specialized curved instruments (Epsilon Instruments, USA), following the preplanned arc length and tapering zones.

Immediately after CAIRS implantation, corneal pachymetry was measured at the thinnest point prior to initiating cross-linking. In 23 eyes with a post-implantation pachymetry of 400 µm or greater, a standard accelerated epithelium-off CXL protocol was performed: the corneal epithelium was removed using an excimer laser (EX500; Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA), riboflavin 0.1% solution (dextran-free) was applied for 10 minutes to achieve adequate stromal saturation, and ultraviolet-A (UVA) irradiation was delivered at 10 mW/cm2 for 9 minutes, providing a total energy dose of 5.4 J/cm2. In the remaining 17 eyes with post-implantation pachymetry below 400 µm (minimum thinnest point pachymetry: 307 µm), the CXL protocol was individualized in accordance with the Sub400 protocol described by Hafezi et al, in which the UVA irradiation time was reduced proportionally based on the measured stromal thickness to limit the depth of UV penetration and avoid endothelial damage.6 4 Riboflavin application and irradiance were otherwise unchanged. All patients received a standardized postoperative regimen following CXL: prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops four times daily for 15 days followed by a taper, moxifloxacin 0.5% eye drops four times daily for 15 days, and preservative-free sodium hyaluronate lubricating drops every 2 hours for 1 month.

Outcome Measures and Statistical Analysis

Preoperative and postoperative evaluations included best-corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), keratometric values assessed using the Pentacam (Oculus Optikgeraete GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), and corneal biomechanical values evaluated with the Corvis ST (Oculus Optikgeraete GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to comparative analysis, normality of distribution was assessed for all continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the majority of parameters demonstrated non-normal distribution (p<0.05), non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for all pre- and postoperative comparisons. Data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. As this was a retrospective case series, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; the sample size reflects the number of consecutive eligible patients treated at our institution over the study period.

Results

A total of 40 eyes of 40 patients were evaluated (21 males, 19 females; mean age: 26 years). According to the Amsler-Krumeich classification, 3 eyes (7.5%) were Stage 1, 5 eyes (12.5%) were Stage 2, 12 eyes (30%) were Stage 3, and 20 eyes (50%) were Stage 4, with Stages 3 and 4 comprising 80% of the cohort.

Visual and Refractive Outcomes

There was a significant improvement in median BCVA, from 0.52 logMAR preoperatively to 0.22 logMAR at 6 months (p=0.005). The spherical equivalent improved from −9.3 D to −4.1 D (p=0.007), though changes in sphere and cylinder alone were not statistically significant. Safety Outcomes

No intraoperative complications were recorded. No segment extrusion, migration, or wound dehiscence occurred in any eye. Slit-lamp examination at all postoperative visits confirmed stable ring segment position within the intrastromal channel in all 40 eyes. No keratic precipitates, stromal infiltrates, or signs of immune-mediated rejection were observed at any time point. One eye (2.5%) developed donor segment opacification within the first postoperative week, characterized by whitening of the implanted segment on slit-lamp examination, as seen in Figure 2. The affected eye was treated with topical corticosteroids, which were discontinued at one month. The opacification persisted throughout the 6-month follow-up period; however, no photophobia, anterior chamber reaction, or visual deterioration was observed, and BCVA improved in this eye compared to the preoperative value. No other eyes developed segment opacification or haze. Formal haze grading was not performed systematically. Endothelial cell count was not measured in this study and is acknowledged as a limitation. Discussion

In this retrospective case series of keratoconic eyes, the combined use of CAIRS and accelerated epithelium-off CXL resulted in significant improvements in visual acuity and corneal shape by 6 months postoperatively. This visual improvement coincided with a substantial reduction in refractive error. These findings suggest that the combined intervention effectively flattens and regularizes the cornea, leading to better optical focus and improved vision.

Corneal tomography confirmed significant topographic flattening. The principal keratometry (K1 and K2) and mean keratometry values all decreased by approximately 4–5 diopters, and maximal keratometry was reduced by a similar magnitude by 6 months. Such corneal flattening is comparable to that achieved with traditional synthetic intrastromal ring segments in keratoconus.7,8 For context, published Intacs SK outcomes report mean Kmax reductions of 3.0 to 5.5 D at 6–12 months, with mean keratometry decreases of 3–4 D — consistent with our observed ΔKmax of 4.2 D and ΔKm of 6.1 D, and suggesting CAIRS achieves equivalent or greater topographic effect through a biological rather than synthetic mechanism. Notably, our observed Kmax reduction (4.2 D) exceeds the typical 1–2 D flattening expected from CXL alone,9,10 underscoring the contribution of the allogenic ring in reshaping the cornea. The tomographic cylinder also showed a slight decline, aligning with the more regular corneal curvature. These topographic changes are in line with prior CAIRS studies.11–14 The non-significant increase in mean thinnest point pachymetry (407.9 to 432 µm, p=0.174) is likely attributable to a combination of donor stromal tissue volume addition from the implanted ring segment and epithelial remodeling over the CAIRS. A contribution from posterior corneal segmentation artifact inherent to Scheimpflug-based tomography in eyes with irregular stroma cannot be excluded, as this is a recognized limitation of the technology in ectatic corneas.

The observed statistically significant decrease in SP-A1 (52.7 to 51.2 mmHg/mm, p=0.015) warrants careful interpretation rather than being dismissed as a null biomechanical result. SP-A1 is a force-over-displacement parameter that is heavily confounded by intraocular pressure and corneal geometry. Ye et al demonstrated that a 1 mmHg change in biomechanically corrected IOP (bIOP) corresponds to an average change of 5.612 mmHg/mm in SP-A1, making even minor unrecorded IOP fluctuations sufficient to explain a change of this magnitude.15 Our observed delta of 1.5 mmHg/mm falls within the range of measurement noise attributable to IOP variation alone. Furthermore, Yang et al, in the largest cross-sectional cohort to date (n=702), established that while SP-A1 carries a direction-correct negative correlation with steep keratometry (Ks), the effect size is only weak-to-moderate (r=−0.171 to −0.317 across severity strata), whereas corneal thickness (TCT) is the dominant tomographic correlate (r=+0.382 to +0.687).16 Critically, no significant SP-A1-to-keratometry correlation exists in normal eyes, underscoring that the relationship is ectasia-dependent and geometry-driven. Li et al further confirmed that in normal and suspect-keratoconus eyes, stromal thickness alone predicts SP-A1 in stepwise regression, with astigmatism magnitude failing to enter any predictive model.17 In our cohort, mean pachymetry showed a non-significant increase postoperatively (407.9 to 432 µm, p=0.174), arguing against a thickness-mediated reduction in SP-A1. Taken together, the SP-A1 decrease most plausibly reflects IOP confounding and the geometric effect of substantial corneal flattening (ΔKmax=4.2 D) on a curvature-sensitive measurement, rather than true biomechanical deterioration. No previous peer-reviewed study has assessed biomechanical changes after combined CAIRS and CXL; however, one conference presentation using OCT elastography reported similar findings, with the stress-strain index remaining unchanged with CAIRS implantation.12 Other studies have shown biomechanical benefit with synthetic ICRS using parameters such as corneal resistance factor (CRF) and corneal hysteresis (CH). Flockerzi and Seitz demonstrated that in progressive keratoconus undergoing the same accelerated CXL protocol (9 mW/cm2, 5.4 J/cm2), the mean per-eye SP-A1 change at progression was only −2.485 mmHg/mm — a comparatively modest single-parameter effect size — suggesting SP-A1 alone has limited sensitivity for detecting biomechanical shifts in surgically treated corneas.18 To further explore potential confounders, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the change in SP-A1 and the change in corneal pachymetry; no significant correlation was identified (p>0.05), suggesting that the observed SP-A1 change was not driven by pachymetric shifts alone. Intraocular pressure was not measured intraoperatively or at follow-up visits in this study and therefore its contribution to SP-A1 changes could not be assessed — this is acknowledged as a limitation. Further research incorporating IOP-independent metrics such as the Stress-Strain Index (SSI) is needed to more accurately characterize biomechanical outcomes after combined CAIRS and CXL.

The absence of significant change in integrated radius (IR), ARTh, and DA-ratio is consistent with the established synthetic ICRS literature. Vinciguerra et al reported no significant change in 1/R, DA-ratio, SP-A1, SSI, or HRC following synthetic ring implantation at 3-month follow-up (n=51),19 while Hamon et al similarly found DA-ratio and IR unchanged at 6 months in 112 eyes implanted with Intacs SK.20 Across more than 200 ICRS-implanted eyes studied with modern Corvis ST parameters, the dominant pattern is small or absent changes in SP-A1, IR, ARTh, DA-ratio, CBI and TBI, with any stiffening signal most reliably captured by the SSI — a parameter not available for analysis in the current study. Notably, no peer-reviewed Corvis ST study after standalone CAIRS implantation has been published to date, based on a systematic literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Embase conducted at the time of manuscript revision (August 2026), which identified no peer-reviewed biomechanical outcome data after CAIRS in two independent systematic reviews published in 2025.14,21 Our findings therefore represent the first peer-reviewed in-vivo biomechanical data after CAIRS combined with CXL, and the null results for IR, ARTh, and DA-ratio are in keeping with the broader ICRS biomechanical literature rather than indicating a failure of the combined procedure.

Despite the clear improvements in lower-order aberrations (sphere and cylinder), our intervention did not significantly reduce higher-order aberrations. In particular, the mean coma aberration and total RMS HOAs showed no statistically significant change from baseline. Previous studies have shown improvement in higher-order aberrations following ICRS implantation.11,12 Our cohort was small, with a combination of symmetric and asymmetric ring implantation, which is probably why we did not see a substantial improvement in coma. It is reassuring, however, that BCVA still improved significantly despite residual coma, suggesting that the lower-order corrections made a dominant impact on visual function.

One eye in our cohort developed donor segment opacification within the first postoperative week, which persisted to 6 months without functional consequence. This finding is consistent with reports of early stromal opacification following allogenic tissue implantation, thought to reflect transient donor stromal disorganization during the period before host keratocyte repopulation and biological integration of the segment. The absence of photophobia, anterior chamber reaction, keratic precipitates, or visual deterioration argues against immune-mediated rejection as the underlying mechanism. Notably, BCVA improved in this eye despite the persistent opacification, suggesting that the optical effect of corneal flattening outweighed any potential light-scattering effect of the opacified segment. This observation highlights the importance of monitoring donor segment transparency in future CAIRS studies and warrants further investigation into the incidence and natural history of this phenomenon.

Our findings add to the growing body of literature supporting CAIRS as an effective and safe intervention for keratoconus. Our study employed a combination approach — CAIRS plus accelerated CXL — which mirrors the strategy used in early CAIRS studies and many synthetic ICRS treatments. The rationale for combining CXL is to halt ectatic progression and stabilize the biomechanical environment into which the allogenic segment is implanted, potentially enhancing long-term outcomes. The biological nature of CAIRS, using donor stromal tissue rather than synthetic polymers, offers potential advantages in biocompatibility and integration, though longer follow-up studies are needed to confirm these benefits and characterize the behavior of the allogenic tissue over time.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the absence of a control group — such as CXL alone or CAIRS alone — precludes separation of the individual contributions of each procedure to the observed outcomes. Second, the follow-up period of 6 months is relatively short; the long-term stability of corneal flattening and the biomechanical behavior of the allogenic tissue over time remain unknown. Third, intraocular pressure was not recorded at follow-up visits, preventing adjustment of SP-A1 for bIOP changes — a recognized confounder of Corvis ST biomechanical parameters. Fourth, endothelial cell count was not monitored postoperatively. Fifth, the SSI, which is the Corvis ST parameter most likely to detect a true tissue stiffening signal independent of IOP and corneal geometry, was not available for analysis in this study. Sixth, segment dehydration time was not standardized, which may have introduced variability in segment dimensions and could represent a source of inter-case differences. Seven, the two CXL subgroups (standard protocol, n=23; Sub400 protocol, n=17) were not analyzed separately, as each subgroup was insufficiently powered for meaningful statistical comparison. Future studies with larger cohorts should examine whether outcomes differ between these subgroups. Eighth, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability. Future studies with longer follow-up, a control arm, concurrent IOP measurement, and inclusion of SSI and composite biomechanical indices (CBI, TBI) are needed to comprehensively characterize the biomechanical effects of combined CAIRS and CXL.

Conclusions

In this retrospective case series, combined CAIRS and accelerated CXL was associated with significant improvements in visual acuity, refraction, and corneal topography at 6 months in patients with moderate to advanced keratoconus. The SP-A1 decrease observed is most plausibly attributable to geometric and IOP-related measurement confounding rather than true biomechanical deterioration. The procedure was well-tolerated, with one case of donor segment opacification occurring without functional visual consequence. These preliminary findings suggest that CAIRS may represent a viable biological alternative to synthetic ICRS; however, prospective controlled studies with longer follow-up, IOP monitoring, and inclusion of IOP-independent biomechanical metrics are required before definitive conclusions can be drawn.
