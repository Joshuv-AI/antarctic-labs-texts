Over-reliance on short-acting beta-agonists (SABAs) in asthma: a mixed method study, in primary care, in Greece
Authors: Izolde Bouloukaki, Antonios Christodoulakis, Siân Williams, Ioanna Tsiligianni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOver-reliance on short-acting beta-agonists (SABAs) has been identified as a predictor of poor asthma control and increased asthma-related mortality. The aim of the study was to assess SABA over-reliance and its association with asthma control and the utility of the Reliever Reliance Test (RRT) in addressing SABA over-reliance in primary care patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with both quantitative and qualitative data on patients with asthma, in Crete, Greece. Participants completed the Asthma Control Test (ACT) assessing asthma control and were evaluated for their use of SABA. Those who reported SABA use, also completed the RRT which classified patients as low, medium or high-risk of over-reliance. A qualitative survey followed to evaluate the feedback obtained from patients and General Practitioners (GPs) to assess the applicability of RRT.ResultsThe study included 145 patients (40 using SABA). Of 38 completing RRT, 82% showed a medium-to-high risk of over-reliance on SABAs, while 24% reported overuse (at least three times/week). The likelihood of poorly controlled asthma significantly increased with greater reliance and more frequent SABA use (OR: 20.209, p = 0.011), and with higher SABA canister use (for ≥1 SABA canister, OR: 2.645, p = 0.026 to ≥3 SABA canisters, OR: 8.372, p = 0.025). Preventive SABA use was reported by 63%, while 86% were unaware of side effects. RRT demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha 0.835) and validity. Qualitative data suggested that both patients (n = 27) and GPs (n = 11) found RRT acceptable and helpful in addressing SABA over-reliance. GPs also expressed their intention to incorporate the questionnaire into clinical practice, despite time constraints.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that over-reliance on SABAs is significantly associated with poor asthma control. Moreover, RRT was successfully translated in Greek, and proved to be reliable; This suggests its potential to facilitate the development of more targeted strategies for identifying and addressing this issue, while also promoting collaborative patient-GP discussions with the aim of achieving optimal asthma outcomes.

Introduction

Asthma is a common, chronic inflammatory airway disease with different phenotypes, affecting both children and adults, with a prevalence ranging from 4.3–8.6%, globally1,2. Moreover, asthma is potentially life-threatening, and with unpredictable, recurrent episodes of worsening symptoms (i.e. wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath, and chest tightness) accounting for considerable disability, healthcare costs, and loss of work productivity3. However, these adverse effects could be better managed through targeted and personalized asthma action plans describing prescribed inhaled treatments and how and when to use them4.

The main goals of asthma management are to optimize control of asthma symptoms and reduce the risk of asthma exacerbations, while minimizing medication adverse effects. For decades, short acting beta agonists (SABAs) have been suggested and prescribed for giving symptomatic relief to patients with asthma4, although inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) are the cornerstone of treatment for this inflammatory condition. However, this symptomatic treatment can lead to patient overreliance on SABAs, which is further worsened by their low cost, easy access, and status as the primary treatment for acute exacerbations in emergency and/or primary care settings5. Nevertheless, such overreliance on SABAs could have severe consequences to patients with asthma. A study in the UK found that a significant percentage of asthma-related deaths involved patients who were prescribed more than 12 canisters of SABAs in the year before, while the majority were under-prescribed preventer ICS medication (given fewer than 12 ICS inhalers per year)6.

The 2021 update of the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) strategy document and subsequent annual releases no longer recommend the use of SABA inhalers alone as the preferred reliever for asthma symptoms and SABAs are not part of the preferred approach7. Still, despite this recommended change, overuse of SABA alone remains prevalent, leading to ICS being used ineffectively, with only 25–60% of the prescribed doses being utilized8–10. Therefore, health professionals need to review SABA use with their patients, especially focusing on those who rely on three or more SABA inhalers annually. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) could increase the effectiveness of asthma management and improve control by reviewing SABA use with their patients11. Specifically, patient perspectives’ on asthma and its treatment could help HCPs better understand SABA use and encourage greater patient involvement in treatment decisions, thus improving their adherence12. The Asthma Right Care (ARC) program, developed by the International Primary Care Respiratory Group (IPCRG), offers an integrated community-based method of asthma care to gain insights into patient perspectives on asthma and its treatment13,14. This programme, including a range of interventions, such as videos, slide rules, questionnaires, challenge cards and other materials, is attempting to create a desire for change in the management of asthma by initiating conversations between HCPs and patients about the over-reliance on SABAs and the underuse of effective interventions. One of these tools, the Reliever Reliance Test (RRT), a new patient-friendly tool, was developed based on the SRQ15, a 5-item SABA Reliance Questionnaire (SRQ) to assess patients’ perceptions of SABA, potentially indicating excessive and frequent use and a possible strong emotional dependence to SABA. RRT also includes information about the frequency of reliever SABA use during the past 4 weeks16. This tool could assist HCPs in more effectively assessing the potential overuse of SABAs among their patients, globally.

In Greece, currently, there are no data available on SABA overuse and its association with asthma control. This is mainly because SABAs are cheap, less than 2 euros and are ‘over the counter’ at the pharmacy, so patients can buy them usually without a prescription, and without any means to add the sales information to a patient health record, leading to underestimation of their use. Therefore, there is an urgent need to evaluate SABA use in patients with asthma in Greece. However, tools that assess SABA usage, such as the RRT, have not yet been translated and validated in Greek. Consequently, the aims of this study were to (1) assess the use of SABA for asthma and its association with asthma control in selected primary care practices in Greece, (2) to validate a Greek version of the RRT and to explore whether this questionnaire could be used to help identify asthma patients at greater risk of having SABA over-reliance and (3) to examine perceptions in primary care patients with asthma and General Practitioners (GPs) regarding the use of RRT questionnaire and also (4) explore the patients’ perspectives of SABA use.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional mixed-methods multicenter study was conducted in 10 primary care centers of Crete, Greece, from June 2023 to April 2024. A mixed-methods study involves a quantitative and a qualitative part. Specifically, in our study the quantitative part included patient data using a structured self-administered questionnaire, divided into two sections. The first section collected data relating to patient demographics and associated conditions and co-morbidities and the second section included the translated and validated Greek RRT questionnaire, to assess the prevalence of SABA over-reliance. Meanwhile, the qualitative part included semi-structured interviews from health care providers, and patients from the quantitative part to assess the content, efficacy, and applicability of the RRT questionnaire and explore patients’ perspectives related to SABA use. Participant recruitment (for quantitative part)

Consecutive patients visiting the ten participating primary health care centers of Crete, Greece were invited to participate in this study. A convenient sample of patients with asthma was selected based on their availability, willingness and eligibility. We included subjects who: a) were adults ( ≥ 18 years); b) with a physician-diagnosed asthma. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in addition to their asthma diagnosis were excluded from the study. Study patients underwent a detailed evaluation including clinical history focused on asthma symptoms, associated conditions and comorbidities; they also completed Asthma Control Test (ACT) questionnaire and the translated Greek RRT questionnaire. Treatment adherence was evaluated using a single yes-or-no question. Given the exploratory nature of the study, a formal power calculation was not undertaken; nonetheless, the sample of 145 participants was deemed adequate to highlight the potential significant variations in the over-reliance of SABAs of patients with asthma, as other similar studies and a meta-analysis have comparable sample sizes15,18–20.

Asthma control test (ACT)

The ACT questionnaire is a self-administered instrument for identifying patients with poorly managed asthma. The ACT questionnaire comprises five Likert scale items ranging from one to five on a scale of one to five describing: (1) activity limitation, (2) shortness of breath, (3) awaking due to asthma symptoms, (4) reliever medication and (5) global judgment of asthma. The overall score for the ACT is estimated by summing the scores for the five items with a possible score ranging from 5 (poor control of asthma) to 25 (complete control of asthma). Higher scores reflect better asthma control. ACT cut-off point for uncontrolled asthma was ≤1921.

Reliver reliance test (RRT) questionnaire

The RRT, developed in partnership with patients, clinicians, and the International Primary Care Respiratory Group (IPCRG), is currently being utilized in over 20 countries16. The RRT has two parts: The first part includes the SABA Reliance Questionnaire (SRQ) - a set of five simple questions, assessing patients’ beliefs about their personal necessity for SABA use, and whether they see SABA use as the best way of managing their asthma15. Items are scored using a five-point Likert scale from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5). Participants are assessed for their risk of over-reliance by totaling the scores from these five items and classifying them into three categories: low risk (5–10), medium risk (11–17), and high risk (18–25). The second part includes one question regarding the frequency of reliever SABA use during the past 4 weeks (none, once or twice a week, 3 times a week, between 4–5 times a week, more than 5 times a week)16. If the individual scores 3 or more in this part, the patient is found to be at high risk of poor asthma control. The RRT also uses tailored messaging to highlight the dangers of excessive SABA use and encourages patients to evaluate their reliance on it. This information is designed to raise awareness of the problem of SABA over-reliance and to encourage them to reflect on whether their perceptions of SABA may be causing them to over-rely on it, and to encourage them to discuss their asthma control with their HCPs.

RRT questionnaire translation

The RRT, after obtaining consent from the developer, underwent a process of translation, back translation, and final review of obtained versions, according to the standard back translation methodology22. The questionnaire was first translated into Greek by two bilingual translators working independently, thus generating two Greek versions, which were then translated back into English by another two bilingual individuals. The back translations were compared with the original English so that any necessary adjustments could be made, resulting in a single Greek RRT version that would be equivalent in meaning to the original (Appendix 1). Moreover, 2 independent reviewers discussed the final versions with a group of 10 patients with asthma (not participants of the quantitative part of the study), to test alternative wordings and to check the comprehensibility, interpretation, and cultural relevance of the translation. Adaptations were made based on their feedback.

Participant recruitment (for qualitative study)

Experienced researchers who had no prior relationship with the participating patients or their GPs conducted individual, semi-structured interviews at participating primary care centers to gather qualitative data. This approach, ensured deeper insights into patients’ and GPs’ perspectives without the potential bias of pre-existing relationships. Specifically, semi-structured interviews, guided by an interview guide (developed via literature review), were conducted face-to-face between September 2023 and March 2024 in the primary care centers; we stopped inviting participants to be interviewed when thematic saturation was reached. Experienced interviewers used a piloted topic guide to ensure structure and focus. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed by the research team, and conducted privately, lasting 15–30 min.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented in the form of the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables with a normal distribution, and as the median (25th–75th percentile) for variables without a normal distribution. Quantitative variables are represented as absolute numbers or percentages. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was applied to examine the effect of SABA frequency and over-reliance, on asthma control, after adjustment for potential explanatory variables, including age, gender, BMI, smoking status, exercise, marital status, area of residence, medications and co-morbidities. We checked multicollinearity among the predictors using collinearity statistics to ensure that collinearity between predictor variables was in the acceptable range as indicated by the tolerance value variance inflation factor. For the purpose of this analysis, the term cardiovascular disease referred to any of the following conditions: coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, atrial fibrillation and heart failure. Internal consistency for RRT questionnaire PART 1 was calculated with the Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient and item-total correlations. Based on GINA guidelines7, SABA excessive use was defined as ≥ 3 prescribed inhalers (~200 puffs each) in 12 months. Results were considered significant when p values were <0.05. Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). On the other hand, we used thematic analysis to identify, analyze, and report patterns (themes) in the data. Following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach, we familiarized ourselves with the data, generated initial codes, searched for themes, reviewed them, defined and named them, and finally produced corresponding tables. Finally, qualitative and quantitative data were triangulated (analyzed for convergence or divergence), creating a visual representation of their potential relationship23.

Ethics approval statement and patients’ consent statement

The present study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Moreover, the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Crete (67/24.04.2023) approved this study. All participants voluntarily signed informed consent forms, after understanding the study’s aims and purpose.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Out of the 150 patients with asthma who were initially invited, 145 patients (97%) ultimately agreed to participate in the study (mean age 60 ± 13 years, BMI 32 ± 8 kg/m2). Almost 53 patients (37%) lived in rural regions. Among the participants, 46 (32%) were male, 82 (57%) were obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), and 105 (74%) had a partner. A significant portion of the participants had a higher level of education (59 out of 145, 41%), a smaller percentage had a secondary level of education (48 out of 145, 33%), and38 (26%) exhibited only a primary level of education. In terms of smoking habits, 31 (21%) of the participants were actively smoking during the period of the survey, whereas 51 (35%) had quit smoking. At least one chronic disease other than asthma was present in 126 (87%) of the patients. Asthma treatment

The detailed medical asthma treatment of all patients is presented in Fig. 1. Within the population, 13% of patients reported no maintenance treatment for asthma. It is important to note that a substantial portion of patients (96/145, 66%) reported regular/consistent use of prescribed LABA/ICS as maintenance therapy, and only 5/96 (5%) of those patients used them as Maintenance and Reliever Therapy (MART). On the other hand, a lesser proportion (6/145, 4%) opted for LABA/LAMA. The consistent use of triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS) was reported by 20 (14%) of patients.Fig. 1Medications prescription patterns in all 145 patients with asthma.

Medications prescription patterns in all 145 patients with asthma.

Reliever inhaler therapy with SABA was used by up to 40 patients (28%), whether as monotherapy (n = 7), or in conjunction with steroid preventer therapy (n = 33), with a median (25th–75th percentile) use of 2 (1, 2) canisters during the prior 12 months. Out of the 40 patients, 9 (22.5%) utilized three or more SABA canisters within the past year and 17 purchased SABA over-the-counter.

Across all 145 patients, a higher proportion of patients with not-well controlled asthma used ≥3 SABA canisters compared to those with well controlled asthma (12 vs 2%, p = 0.019). After logistic regression analyses, the odds of having not well controlled asthma increased significantly with higher SABA canister use, with adjusted odds ratios (CIs) ranging from 2.645 (1.124, 6.220) for ≥1 SABA canister to 8.372 (1.302, 53.848) for ≥3 SABA canisters (Supplementary Table 1). Female gender was also significantly associated with poor asthma control with odds ratios (CIs) 2.592 (1.072, 6.269).

RRT questionnaire (in patients who used SABA)

Thirty eight out of the 40 patients who used SABA completed the RRT questionnaire. All patients answered all questions on the questionnaire, indicating a good level of understanding and acceptable feasibility.

Association between SABA reliance and asthma control

High SABA reliance based on RRT questionnaire PART 1 (score 18–25) was seen in 7 (18.4%) of participants; 24 (63.2%) were medium reliance (score 11–17), and 7 (18.4%) showed low reliance (score 5–10). When comparing SABA reliance with asthma control in all patients, in the 58 out of the 145 patients (40%) who had not-well-controlled asthma, 17 (29%) of them had medium to high reliance on their SABA inhalers. Moreover, 14 out of the 85 (16%) patients who reported well-controlled asthma showed a medium to high reliance on SABA.

In logistic regression analyses, the odds of having not well controlled asthma increased significantly with higher SABA reliance, with adjusted odds ratios (CIs) of 2.623 (1.052, 6.541) (Table 6). Patients’ perspectives related to SABA use

The interviews also examined patients’ behaviors and perceptions regarding the use of SABAs, revealing four distinct themes namely, reliance on SABAs, knowledge gaps, precautionary use, and perceptions of efficacy compared to ICS (Table 8). The majority of patients (19/27) valued SABAs for their prompt symptom relief, as evidenced by statements such as, “It helps to quickly make me feel better…” (47-year-old female, urban practice). Conversely, 8/27 found SABAs ineffective in symptom (dyspnea) relief. A substantial portion (17/27) utilized SABAs preemptively to prevent asthma attacks, and 15/27 maintained multiple inhalers in various locations for accessibility. Knowledge gaps were prevalent, with 10/27 being unaware of appropriate SABA usage and 24/27 lacking information regarding the potential risks of overuse. Only 3/27 reported adverse effects, such as hand tremors or tachycardia. Furthermore, 16/27 favored SABAs over ICS due to their immediate effects, while 11/27 acknowledged the necessity of ICS for long-term symptom management, as prescribed by their general practitioners.

Triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data

Finally, the results of the interviews converged with the quantitative data, resulting in a multifaceted illustration that depicts how patients’ SABA overreliance could lead to poor asthma control (Fig. 4). Specifically, the combination of both quantitative and qualitative data highlighted the alignment between measured SABA over-reliance and the insights from the interviews. This provided a comprehensive picture/figure of how patients’ misconceptions impact asthma control. The quantitative data showed that 82% of 38 SABA users were at medium-to-high risk of over-reliance (RRT Part 1 scores 11–25), with 24% overusing ( ≥ 3 times/week, RRT Part 2). This overuse was positively associated with poorly controlled asthma (ACT ≤ 19; adjusted OR = 2.623, p = 0.039 for reliance; OR = 20.209, p = 0.011 for frequency; Tables 6 and 7). These findings aligned with the qualitative themes: 19/27 patients reported emotional dependence on SABAs for quick relief (e.g., “It helps to quickly make me feel better…”) and 24/27 were unaware of side effects, while 17/27 used them preventatively (Table 8). This suggested that patients have a lack of knowledge regarding SABA overuse (adverse effects) and a preference for the immediate relief of SABAs over ICS (16/27), which contributed to the quantitative link with poor asthma control. Meanwhile, limited but insightful divergence also emerged, with 2/11 GPs reporting time constraints for RRT implementation (e.g., “The heavy workload can make it challenging…”; Table 9). This contextualized the potential barriers to routine adoption, despite the tool’s acceptability (25/27 patients, 8/11 GPs). Discussion

Our findings suggest that poorly controlled asthma was positively associated with more SABA canister usage, greater reliance on SABAs, and more frequent use of SABAs, as indicated in the RRT questionnaire. In addition, over half of patients reported preventive use of SABA, while most were unaware of potential side effects. Furthermore, the RRT questionnaire was successfully translatedin Greek, proved to be reliable and also was well-received by both patients and GPs. Specifically, participants found RRT helpful in understanding SABA use and the potential for over-reliance on these medications. However, lack of time was identified as the primary barrier to the implementation of RRT in clinical practice.

A major finding of the present study was that a proportion of patients with asthma use SABA either as monotherapy, or in addition to maintenance therapy. Specifically, the percentage of SABAs users in our study (28%) was similar to that observed in the APPARENT 3 study in Seven Countries (Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates)24, lower to that observed in Netherlands (~50%)25, and Poland (~46%)26, and higher to that observed in France (~5%)27, in the United Kingdom (~10%)27, and Canada (~1%)28. However, the overuse of SABAs and underuse of ICSs, as reported by the participants of our study (qualitative data), highlight poor adherence to asthma guidelines, which can be a major obstacle to effective asthma control29. More importantly, studies have suggested that over-prescribing of SABAs is positively associated with increased number of asthma attacks, healthcare use, and higher mortality rates among patients with asthma30.

Another important finding of the present study was that 6.2% of our patients used ≥3 canisters of SABA within the previous year. This percentage was similar to a recent study from Singapore (~6%)31 and lower to recent studies from Poland (~15%)26, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom (9–38%)32, Finland (10%)33 and China (9.1%)34. Moreover, we found that the patients who utilized three or more SABA canisters exhibited an 8.3-fold increased likelihood of experiencing poorly controlled asthma, potentially elevating future exacerbation and mortality risks. This finding aligns with a previous study of 365,324 asthma patients among individuals with increased dispensed SABA canisters35. A potential explanation of this overuse of SABAs in our study could be due to the fact that in Greece SABAs may be obtained without a prescription, potentially leading to an increased reliance on these medications without the doctor’s knowledge/notice. Interestingly, a recent review indicated that the over-the-counter purchase of SABAs is common in many countries, and that a considerable number of patients who purchased SABAs without a prescription overused them36. For example, the SABINA III study found a higher rate (18–24%) of purchased SABA over-the-counter across 24 countries compared to our study (12%)37–39. Furthermore, when compared to specific countries included in the SABINA III study, such as the Philippines37, Russia38, and Australia39, where 31, 30, and 25% respectively purchased SABA over-the-counter without a prescription, our percentage is even lower. This percentage, however, exceeds the 4% observed in a Singaporean study31. Nevertheless, the relatively low percentage noted in our study may be attributed to the large proportion of patients in Greece receiving anti-inflammatory treatment in the form of ICS-LABA (66%). This is important, since it is recommended to change from SABA to Anti-inflammatory reliever therapy for better asthma management7, which also represents a significant change for many patients with asthma. However, this requires HCPs to identify and address potential misunderstandings their patients may have regarding the importance of SABAs and their concerns about ICSs40,41. Consequently, the initial steps in reducing the overreliance on SABAs may involve gaining a deeper understanding of patient beliefs, and subsequently encouraging and educating patients, and communicating better to transition to anti-inflammatory medications instead of relying on SABAs. Furthermore, another significant finding of our study was that most of our participants (86%) used SABAs as a preventative measure against asthma attacks yet lacked awareness of potential side effects associated with excessive use. On the other hand, the participants self-reported preference for SABA use over steroid preventer therapy due to their lack of immediate and recognizable benefits.

The RRT is a new questionnaire designed to understand patients’ beliefs about SABA16. It also includes personalized advice to warn patients about the risks of overusing SABA, encourage them to think about their views on SABA, and inform them that relying solely on SABA for asthma management might not be the most effective treatment. It also serves to encourage individuals to engage in discussions with HCPs regarding their asthma treatment plan42. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess RRT questionnaire utility as a screening tool to identify patients at risk of SABA over-reliance in Greece42. The RRT PART 1 psychometric properties indicated sufficient concurrent validity and internal consistency. In addition, the results from the RRT questionnaire Part 1 indicated a moderate to high reliance on SABA in 82% of participants. This finding from our study is consistent with the only other existing study using the RRT questionnaire, which was conducted in Argentina43. Meanwhile, the analysis of RRT Part 2 suggested that about one fourth of patients (24%) reported using three inhalations per week in the past four weeks prior to the study visit, a percentage which is lower than that reported in the previous study43.

We also found that higher reliance on SABA and its frequent use (≥3 times /week) were strongly associated with a substantial increase in the likelihood of poorly controlled asthma based on ACT, showing a 2.6-fold and 20.2-fold increase in risk, respectively. These findings support previous results43 that showed that RRT scores were significantly associated with asthma control based on the GINA Symptom Control Assessment44. Specifically, their findings indicated that patients with well-controlled asthma were not at a high risk of SABA over-reliance, while a substantial 47% of those with partially and poorly controlled asthma were at a high risk43. Therefore, it is important for patients and HCPs to have a simple and acceptable tool to address potentially harmful misconceptions about SABA overuse, thereby encouraging them to review asthma treatment.

Another important finding of our study was that most of the patients participating in the qualitative study found the RRT questionnaire acceptable and clear (89%), with only a minority (11%) having difficulty understanding the RRT questions, specifically Question 4 in PART 1 (“The benefits of using my Reliever Inhaler easily outweigh any risks.”). This aligns with a community pharmacy trial45 where 88% of patients introduced to the RRT reported that they understood the purpose of RRT and 17% also faced difficulty with this specific question. It is also noteworthy that a considerable percentage of patients (88%) perceived the questionnaire as a valuable tool in promoting awareness regarding the use of SABA and the potential problem of over-reliance, which is also in accordance with the results of previous trials using RRT in a community pharmacy (85%)42,45 and in community through an online survey exploring the acceptability of the RRT (72–75%)43. These results highlight the potential of the RRT in identifying patients whose beliefs about SABA might place them at risk of SABA over-reliance. Prior research conducted in Argentina43 also brought this aspect to light. A significant number of participants in that study, especially those categorized as having a medium or high risk of over-reliance agreed that RRT made them think about their potential over-reliance on their SABA inhaler and potentially not receiving the most appropriate asthma care43. Therefore, RRT seems to promote a change in patients’ perspective on their SABA use and motivates them to seek support following completion of this questionnaire. This could potentially enhance their preparedness for a consultation during which modifications to their asthma management plan might be discussed.

RRT could assist GPs addressing the diverse perspectives and misunderstandings surrounding SABAs46,47, considering that 63% of participants of our qualitative study used SABA proactively to prevent asthma attacks and 86% were unaware of the potential side effects that could result from excessive use of SABA. Although, some GPs encouraged patients to focus on symptom prevention with ICS, 59% of patients still questioned its efficacy. Importantly, all participating GPs found the RRT questionnaire to be comprehensible, straightforward, and beneficial in gaining insight into the issue of over-reliance on SABAs within their patient population. They also expressed their intention to incorporate the questionnaire into their clinical practice, despite time constraints. The implementation of RRT has been successful in other healthcare settings, including pharmacies in the UK, where all participating pharmacists reported they would use the RRT again and that it influenced their consultations42,45. RRT also facilitated referrals to general practitioners, practice nurses, practice pharmacists, or hospitals for further action or review42,45.

Another important finding of the present study was the importance of identifying and understanding SABA over-reliance within the Greek patients with asthma, further emphasizing the urgency of HCPs in addressing this issue. A previous study in Greece further supports this finding, showing that GPs acknowledge their responsibility to educate patients regarding the use of SABAs, despite the challenges they face40. Consequently, there is an urgent need of GPs to make more targeted changes in their clinical practices and improve patient outcomes in the country.

Our study is the first that highlights the positive association between SABA over-reliance with reduced asthma control of patients with asthma in Crete, Greece. Nevertheless, there are also some limitations that should be highlighted. First, the recruitment of patients solely from southern Greece limits the generalizability of our results to the entire Greek population. Second, participants’ self-reported SABA use might be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to inaccurate measurements. Third, while the substantial odds ratio linking high SABA use (≥3 canisters or ≥3 times/week) to poorer asthma control points to a strong association, the wide confidence interval requires a cautious interpretation of the effect’s magnitude. Fourth, despite the valuable insights of our study the sample size was relatively small, which should be taken into consideration when interpreting our findings. Lastly, as with any observational study the possibility of unmeasured factors like socioeconomic status could have influenced our results. Nevertheless, essential confounders associated with asthma control have been adjusted for our analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that SABA overreliance (more SABA canister usage, greater reliance on SABAs, and more frequent use of SABAs) was positively associated with poor asthma control. Moreover, our study successfully translated RRT in the Greek language, which has potential utility in identifying and addressing SABA over-reliance, addressing misplaced beliefs that put patients at risk of SABA over-reliance and prompting dialogue between patients and GPs to achieve optimal asthma outcomes. Further research is necessary to explore the extent to which the RRT will modify help-seeking behavior and medication use.
