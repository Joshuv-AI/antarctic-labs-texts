Impact of immunosuppressive drugs on efficacy of mesenchymal stem cell therapy for suppressing renal fibrosis
Authors: Kisho Miyasako, Ayumu Nakashima, Naoki Ishiuchi, Yoshiki Tanaka, Keisuke Morimoto, Kensuke Sasaki, Shogo Nagamatsu, Go Matsuda, Takao Masaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreemptive regenerative medicine using mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) may provide a novel therapeutic approach to prevent the progression from organ damage to organ failure. Although immunosuppressive drugs are often used in patients with organ disorder, their impact on MSC therapy remains unclear. We investigated the effects of immunosuppressive drugs on the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. We created unilateral ureteral obstruction models, as a well-established model of renal fibrosis, a preliminary stage of organ failure. Three immunosuppressive drugs (methylprednisolone, cyclosporine, and cyclophosphamide) were intraperitoneally administered 3 days after surgery, and MSCs were injected via tail vein the following day. Preadministration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide interfered with MSC activation by reducing expression of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and high-mobility group box-1 protein, thus significantly attenuating the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. Preadministration of cyclophosphamide downregulated the expression of stromal cell-derived factor-1/C-X-C motif ligand 12, which is a potent migration factor for MSCs, resulting in reduced MSC engraftment in the renal cortex. IFN-γ-preconditioned activated MSCs were unaffected by these drugs and maintained their beneficial therapeutic effects. Cyclosporine preadministration had no effect on the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. Our study demonstrated that the administration of certain immunosuppressive drugs interfered with MSC activation and engraftment at the site of injury, resulting in a significant attenuation of their therapeutic efficacy. These findings provide crucial information for selecting patients suitable for MSC therapy. Use of MSCs preactivated with IFN-γ or other means is preferred for patients on methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide.Keywords: mesenchymal stem cells, immunosuppression therapy, fibrosis, cell migration, HMGB1 protein, chemokine CXCL12

Significance statement

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have shown great potential as a cell therapy. However, the influence of immunosuppressive drugs on the efficacy of MSC therapy remains unclear. We investigated the impact of immunosuppressive drugs on the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. Our findings indicated that MSCs may not provide adequate therapeutic benefit in patients receiving methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide due to insufficient MSC activation. MSCs preactivated with IFN-γ or other means should be used for these patients. Meanwhile, the efficacy of MSC therapy was unaffected in patients receiving cyclosporine. These findings provide essential insights for selecting patients suitable for MSC therapy.

Introduction

Lifestyle-related diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity cause chronic tissue damage and lead to infiltration of inflammatory cells into damaged tissues.1-3 Persistent inflammatory cell infiltration activates fibroblasts and induces fibrosis,4,5 and fibrosis in turn results in organ dysfunction,6 such as liver cirrhosis,7 renal failure, and heart failure. Although various treatments have been used to suppress fibrotic progression, the effectiveness of these treatments is limited and many patients eventually go on to develop organ dysfunction. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been reported to have anti-inflammatory8,9 and apoptosis-inhibitory effects.10 MSC therapy targeting these effects has accordingly attracted attention as a novel therapy to prevent the progression of organ injury.11-13 In fact, MSC therapy has already been initiated for patients with cardiac disease,14,15 renovascular disease,16 stroke,17,18 cirrhosis,19 diabetes,20,21 and autoimmune diseases.22-24

MSCs are adult stem cells that can be isolated from various tissues, such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, placenta, and umbilical cord.8,25 MSCs exert their immunomodulatory function mainly by interacting with T and B cells, as well as natural killer cells, macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, through cell-to-cell contact26,27 and paracrine activity.26,28 Notably, regarding paracrine activity, recent studies have shown that several humoral factors, including tumor necrosis factor-inducible-gene 6 (TSG-6), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) play key roles in the therapeutic effects of MSCs.29-31 TSG-6 attenuates inflammatory cell infiltration in the early stages,29,32 PGE2 inhibits the continuation of inflammation by polarizing the pro-inflammatory phenotype of M1 macrophages to an immuno-regulatory M2 macrophage-like phenotype,33 and IDO exerts its anti-inflammatory effect by decreasing the generation of Th1 and Th17 cells and inducing the conversion of CD4-positive T cells into Foxp3-positive regulatory T cells.31,34

Renal fibrosis is a histological manifestation observed during the progression of most types of chronic kidney disease, involving various mechanisms,35 of which transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/Smad signaling is known to be the key promoter of renal fibrosis in chronic kidney disease.36,37 TGF-β1 produced under chronic inflammation induces the activation of myofibroblasts, a subset of activated fibroblasts characterized by the expression of alfa-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) via this signaling pathway.37-39 Myofibroblasts in turn promote renal fibrosis by accelerating the excessive synthesis and deposition of extracellular matrix components, such as type I and type III collagen, and inhibiting their degradation.38 MSCs not only inhibit inflammatory cell infiltration and consequently fibrosis by exerting immunomodulatory effects but also exhibit direct anti-fibrotic effects by inhibiting TGF-β/Smad signaling.40

Recent studies have reported that MSC activation is necessary for MSCs to exert a favorable therapeutic effect.41,42 Growth factors, inflammatory cytokines, and chemokines are involved in MSC activation.8,41 Interleukin-18 (IL-18) induced by high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1), a damage-associated molecular pattern released from injured tissues, promotes interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion from natural killer cells.2,43,44 Intravascularly administered MSCs are converted to the active form by IFN-γ and demonstrate potent anti-inflammatory effects through enhanced secretion of PGE2 and IDO.12,30,31 MSCs cultured in IFN-γ-added medium are already activated and show strong therapeutic effects immediately after administration.30

The increasingly widespread use of MSC therapy for various organ disorders emphasizes the need to identify which patients are appropriate candidates for this therapy. Although immunosuppressive drugs are used in a high percentage of patients with organ disorders, their impact on the therapeutic effect of concomitant MSC therapy is still unclear. We hypothesized that immunosuppressive drugs would suppress the function of immunocompetent cells, thereby inhibiting MSC activation and attenuating their therapeutic efficacy. We investigated this hypothesis by evaluating the influence of various immunosuppressive drugs on the therapeutic effect and migration ability of MSCs in a renal unilateral ureter obstruction (UUO) model, as a well-established model of fibrosis, a preliminary stage of organ failure.

Materials and methods

MSC preparation

Adipose-derived human MSCs (MSCs) were isolated from adipose tissue collected from patients undergoing breast reconstruction. MSCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/low-glucose (DMEM; Cat# D6046, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat# F0926, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixed solution (Cat# 09367-34, Nakalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2. After reaching 80%–90% confluence, MSCs were passaged with accutase (Cat# AT104, Innovative Cell Technologies, San Diego, CA, USA) and used for subsequent experiments. Cell supernatants at passage 2 were collected and confirmed to be free from mycoplasma infection. We used MSCs at passage 4 for all experiments, and cells were counted using a LUNA-FX7 Automated Cell Counter (Logos Biosystems, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea).

Cell treatment with IFN-γ

MSCs (5.0 × 10⁵ cells/10-cm dish) were seeded into 10-cm dishes and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. After reaching 60% confluence, the medium was removed and fresh pre-prepared medium containing 25, 50, 100, 200, or 400 ng/mL human recombinant IFN-γ (Cat #300-02, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) was added. After 48 hours, the cells were harvested to evaluate IFN-γ-induced changes in prostaglandin E synthase (PTGES) and IDO1. Moreover, following the same cell culture, the medium was replaced by fresh medium with or without 200 ng/mL IFN-γ. After 48 hours, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 0.1% FBS. Cells were collected 24, 48, and 72 hours later to investigate the duration of IFN-γ-induced changes in PTGES and IDO1. Finally, we determined if human MSCs responded to rat IFN-γ. Human MSCs (5.0 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. After reaching 60% confluence, the medium was replaced with fresh medium with or without 200 ng/mL rat recombinant IFN-γ (Cat #400-20, PeproTech). After 48 hours, the cells were harvested to evaluate IFN-γ-induced changes in PTGES and IDO1.

MSC transduction with adeno-associated virus

Enhanced green fluorescent protein cDNA (EGFP cDNA; accession number: MN832871) was synthesized by Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan) and subcloned into the multicloning site of pAAV-CMV vector using AAVpro Helper Free System (Cat# 6651, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). The restriction enzymes used were Eco RI and Sal I. AAVpro 293T cells (Cat# 632273, Takara Bio; 2.0 × 10⁶ cells/10-cm dish) were seeded into 10-cm dishes and cultured in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 hours with 5% CO2 to prepare the EGFP AAV. Purified pAAV-CMV, pRC6, and pHelper vectors were cotransfected into 293T cells with TransIT-293 transfection reagent (Cat# MIR2700, Takara Bio). After 20 hours, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% FBS. AAV was collected and purified (vector genome 1.0 × 1011 copies/mL) after a further 24 hours using an AAVpro Purification Kit Maxi (Cat# 6666, Takara Bio). The purified EGFP AAV was stored at −80 °C and used for subsequent experiments. MSCs (5.0 × 10⁵ cells/10-cm dish) were then seeded, incubated to 40%–50% confluence, and EGFP AAV with transgenic EGFP was added to each culture medium. MSCs were cultured for another 48 hours to generate MSCs overexpressing EGFP (EGFP-MSCs; Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). Successful gene transfer was confirmed by a fluorescence microscopy (BZ-9000; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The above recombinant DNA research was conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant DNA Molecules.

Cell preparation for in vivo experiments

MSCs (5.0 × 105 cells/10-cm dish) were seeded and cultured as described above. After reaching 60% confluence, the medium was replaced by fresh medium with or without 200 ng/mL IFN-γ, followed by further culture for 48 hours. The cells were collected and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and used for in vivo experiments.

Animals

C.B-17/Icr-scid/scid Jcl mice exhibiting severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) phenotype (SCID mice; male, 7 weeks old, weight: 24-27 g) and wild-type C.B-17/Icr-+/+ Jcl mice (wild-type (WT) mice; male, 7 weeks old, weight: 25-28 g) were purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan). Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 7 weeks old, weight: 270-330 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Japan (Yokohama, Japan). Mice and rats were housed in groups and acclimated for 1 week under controlled temperature conditions (21 ± 1 °C) with a 12-hour light-dark photoperiod, with lights off at 20:00 hour.

Experimental animal models

SCID and WT mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of 3 mixed anesthetic agents (0.15 mg/kg medetomidine, 2.5 mg/kg butorphanol, and 2.0 mg/kg midazolam). After making an abdominal midline incision, the left ureter was exposed and ligated to induce the UUO model. Mice in the sham-operated group underwent the same surgical procedure except for ureteral ligation. MSCs (1.0 × 105 cells/mouse) in 0.2 mL PBS were administered via the tail vein at UUO day 4. Mice in the sham-operated and PBS groups were injected with 0.2 mL PBS instead of MSCs at UUO day 4. Mice were sacrificed at UUO day 14 to compare the degree of renal fibrosis.

Sprague-Dawley rats underwent the same UUO procedure as explained above. The rats were then divided randomly into 4 groups (n = 6-8 per group): PBS, MSCs, IFN-γ MSCs, and sham groups, respectively. MSCs (1.0 × 106 cells/rat) and IFN-γ MSCs (1.0 × 106 cells/rat) in 1.0 mL PBS were administered via the tail vein at UUO day 4. Rats in the sham-operated and PBS groups were injected with 1.0 mL PBS instead of MSCs or IFN-γ MSCs at UUO day 4. In addition, the 3 immunosuppressive drugs (methylprednisolone 20 mg/kg, cyclosporine 2.0 mg/kg, cyclophosphamide 80 mg/kg; Cat# 15013, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA, Cat# 031-24931, Wako, Osaka, Japan and Cat# 13849, Cayman Chemical, respectively) were injected intraperitoneally at UUO day 3 in all groups, except the sham group, which received PBS instead of an immunosuppressive drug. PBS, MSCs, and IFN-γ MSCs were administered on UUO day 4, as in the absence of immunosuppressive drugs, and the rats were sacrificed on UUO day 7 or day 14 to evaluate inflammation and fibrosis.

We analyzed the migration capacity and engraftment rate of MSCs. EGFP-MSCs (1.0 × 10⁶ cells/rat) were injected into rats via the tail vein at UUO day 4. Rats not treated with immunosuppressive drugs were sacrificed at days 1, 3, and 5 after MSC administration. We also created UUO model rats treated with the respective immunosuppressive drugs intraperitoneally at UUO day 3, and EGFP-MSCs (1.0 × 10⁶ cells/rat) were administered at UUO day 4 in the same manner. Rats were euthanized the day after MSC administration to measure the number of EGFP-MSCs present in the renal cortex.

Western blotting

Sample collection, protein extraction, and Western blotting were performed as described previously.45 Antibodies for α-SMA (Cat# A2547, Sigma-Aldrich), glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Cat# G8795, Sigma-Aldrich), HMGB1 (Cat# ab79823, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), IFN-γ (Cat# ab133566, Abcam), and Stromal cell-derived factor-1/C-X-C motif ligand 12 (SDF-1/CXCL12; Cat# sc-74271, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) were used as primary antibodies, and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (Cat# P0448, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) or goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (Cat# P0447, Dako) were utilized as secondary antibodies. SuperSignal West Pico and Dura system (Cat# 34580 and 34076, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) and ImmunoStar LD (Cat# 290-69904, Wako) were used to detect signals. The bands were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.47v; National Institutes of Health), and the results were normalized to GAPDH levels.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

Kidney samples were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 18 hours followed by 70% ethanol, processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 2 µm, and stained with Masson trichrome to evaluate morphological and fibrotic features, as described previously.46 Areas of interstitial fibrosis were evaluated using Lumina Vision software (Mitani, Osaka, Japan) by examining 5 randomly selected fields (×100) of the cortex. Kidneys were also sectioned at 4 µm for immunohistochemical staining, in accordance with previously described methods.46 Anti-α-SMA antibody (Cat# A2547, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-collagen type I antibody (Cat# 2150-1908, Bio-Rad, Kidlington, UK), anti-CD3 antibody (Cat# IR503, Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and anti-CD68 antibody (Cat# ab125212, Abcam) were utilized as the primary antibodies. Reaction products were detected using the EnVision System (Dako) and avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex system (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). In the latter case, biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (Cat# 65-6140, Invitrogen, Rockford, IL, USA) or goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (Cat# 62-6540, Invitrogen) were used as secondary antibodies. Color reactions were developed in 3,3-diaminobenzidine (Cat# 049-22831, Sigma-Aldrich). The numbers of CD3- and CD68-positive cells, and areas positive for α-SMA and collagen type I staining were quantified using ImageJ software by examining 5 randomly selected fields (×100) of the renal cortex.

Immunofluorescence staining

Kidneys were treated as for immunohistochemical staining and sectioned at 8 µm for immunofluorescence staining. Sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and 10% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 hour, incubated with primary anti-GFP antibody (Cat# ab1218, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor 546 goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (Cat# A-11003, Invitrogen) secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Color reactions were developed as for immunohistochemical staining and the sections were mounted with Prolong Gold antifade reagent (Cat# P36934, Invitrogen). Fluorescent images were acquired using a Keyence BZ-9000 fluorescence microscope (Osaka, Japan). The number of EGFP-positive cells present in the renal cortex was counted by direct visual inspection.

Quantitative real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR were performed as described previously.46 Specific oligonucleotide primers and probes for human PTGES (assay ID: Hs00610420_m1), human IDO1 (assay ID: Hs00984148_m1), human C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4; assay ID: Hs00607978_s1), human GAPDH (assay ID: Hs02758991_g1), rat Hmgb1 (assay ID: Rn02377062_g1), rat Il18 (assay ID: Rn01422083_m1), rat Cxcl12 (assay ID: Rn00573260_m1), rat Acta2 (assay ID: Rn01759928_g1), rat Tgfb1 (assay ID: Rn00572010_m1), rat Col1a1 (assay ID: Rn01463848_m1), rat Col3a1 (assay ID: Rn01437681_m1), and rat Gapdh (assay ID: Rn01775763_g1) were purchased from TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH levels.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Levels of HMGB1 released from the tissue into the blood were measured by ELISA using rat plasma samples and an HMGB1 ELISA kit (Cat# ARG81310, Arigo Biolaboratories, Hsinchu, Taiwan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transfection of CXCR4 siRNA

Human MSCs were transfected with 20 nM siRNA targeting CXCR4 (Cat# s15412, Applied Biosystems) or negative control siRNA (Cat# 4390843, Applied Biosystems) using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Cat# 11668019, Invitrogen). After 24 hours, the transfected cells were washed with PBS and the medium was changed to fresh medium. EGFP AAVs with transgenic EGFP were subsequently added to the culture medium and incubated for another 48 hours to produce EGFP-MSCs. The cells were then collected to confirm successful knockdown of CXCR4. Once successful knockdown was confirmed, UUO model rats were created and EGFP-MSCs with negative control siRNA or CXCR4 siRNA (1.0 × 10⁶ cells/rat), respectively, were administered on UUO day 4. Rats were euthanized the day after MSC administration and the numbers of EGFP-MSCs present in the renal cortex were compared.

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons and 2-sided Student’s t test was used for between-group comparisons. Within-group comparisons for the 3 groups were initially performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test. No between-group comparisons were conducted here. Comparisons between the PBS, methylprednisolone, cyclosporine, and cyclophosphamide groups in the UUO 1w or UUO 2w model were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test (Figures 2I and 3E). Similar procedures were performed in the respective MSC and IFN-γ MSC administration models for UUO 1w and UUO 2w, with between-group comparisons conducted by one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test. We excluded values greater than 4 times or less than one-fourth of the median. All experiments reported here were performed at least 2 to 3 times independently and all analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism9 (ver. 9.3.1) and JMP Pro 15 software. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Allocation was blinded to the investigators who performed statistical analyses and the quantification of Western blotting and immunohistochemical staining. A level of P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and were indicated as *P < 0.05, #P < 0.01.

Results

Effects of MSCs on renal fibrosis produced by UUO in SCID mice

SCID mice lack functional T and B cells and present with symptoms similar to those of severe combined immunodeficiency in humans.47 To investigate the therapeutic effect of MSCs in immunosuppressed conditions, SCID and WT mice were subjected to UUO. Kidney specimens were collected 14 days later and fibrosis was evaluated by Western blotting, immunohistochemical staining, and Masson trichrome staining. α-SMA protein expression levels in WT mice were significantly upregulated by the UUO procedure and were significantly downregulated after MSC administration (Fig. 1A and B). In contrast, MSCs had no effect on α-SMA protein levels in SCID mice (Fig. 1C and D). Immunohistochemical staining showed that the α-SMA-positive area was significantly enlarged by the UUO procedure in WT mice and significantly reduced following MSC administration. Similarly, the collagen type I-positive area was also significantly enlarged following UUO surgery and this enlargement was significantly reduced by MSC injection. Masson trichrome staining showed that UUO-induced renal fibrosis was suppressed by MSC treatment. All 3 staining methods demonstrated a good anti-fibrotic effect of MSCs (Fig. 1E–H). Conversely, MSCs failed to reduce the α-SMA-positive and collagen type I-positive areas in SCID mice, and Masson trichrome staining also demonstrated little improvement in renal fibrosis in SCID mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 1I–L). These results demonstrated that MSCs improved fibrosis in WT mice but not in immunodeficient SCID mice, suggesting that loss of T- and B-cell functions attenuated the therapeutic effect of MSCs.

Effects of MSCs pre-treatment with IFN-γ

Various mechanisms including paracrine signaling are responsible for the favorable therapeutic effects of MSCs.26-28 Notably, IFN-γ stimulation enhances the secretion of PGE2 and IDO from MSCs, which plays an important role in their therapeutic efficacy.12,30,31 We therefore examined the relationships between IFN-γ concentration and human PTGES and IDO1 mRNA levels in MSCs and found that their levels were significantly increased at IFN-γ concentrations > 25 ng/mL (Supplementary Figure S2A). We also examined the duration of elevated PTGES and IDO1 expression in MSCs following pre-treatment with IFN-γ. PTGES and IDO1 mRNA levels remained significantly upregulated for 3 days after replacement with IFN-γ-free medium (Supplementary Figure S2B). We also investigated the response of human MSCs to IFN-γ produced by rats. PTGES expression levels were significantly increased by both rat and human IFN-γ, respectively. IDO1 expression levels were also significantly elevated by rat IFN-γ, but the response was lower than for human IFN-γ (Supplementary Figure S2C). These results indicated that pretreatment with IFN-γ activated MSCs and that this activation lasted for at least 3 days.

Impact of preadministration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide on the anti-fibrotic effect of MSCs

We evaluated the effects of immunosuppressive drugs on the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs by analyzing kidney samples collected from rats 14 days after UUO, using RT-PCR, Western blotting, immunohistochemical staining, and Masson trichrome staining. Based on the results of the attenuated therapeutic effect of MSCs in SCID mice, 3 immunosuppressive drugs were selected for preadministration: methylprednisolone, which suppresses T- and B-cell functions,48,49 cyclosporine, which mainly depresses T-cell function,50 and cyclophosphamide, which mainly inhibits B-cell function.51 mRNA and protein levels of α-SMA induced by UUO were significantly reduced by the administration of MSCs and further reduced by injection of IFN-γ-preconditioned MSCs (IFN-γ MSCs). None of the pre-administered immunosuppressive drugs affected the suppressive effect of IFN-γ MSCs on α-SMA expression levels. However, pre-injection of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide attenuated the effect of MSCs, but pre-administration of cyclosporine had no effect on the inhibition of α-SMA expression levels by MSCs (Fig. 2A–C). Tgfb1, Col1a1, and Col3a1 mRNA levels demonstrated the same tendencies as Acta2 mRNA levels (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the UUO procedure increased the α-SMA-positive area, and this was significantly reduced by the administration of MSCs and further strongly reduced by IFN-γ MSCs. None of the pre-administered immunosuppressive drugs affected the shrinkage of the α-SMA-positive area induced by IFN-γ MSCs. MSCs failed to decrease the α-SMA-positive area in the methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide pre-administration groups, but did decrease the α-SMA-positive area in the cyclosporine pre-injection group, similar to non-immunosuppressive conditions (Fig. 2D and E). Similar results were observed for the collagen type I-positive area (Fig. 2F and G). Masson trichrome staining showed equivalent results to the immunohistochemical staining results. IFN-γ MSCs prevented fibrosis in each immunosuppressive-drug group, with little improvement in fibrosis in the methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide pre-administration groups but greater improvement in the cyclosporine pre-injection group, similar to the control group (Fig. 2H and I). Thus, although immunosuppressive drug pre-administration did not alter the effectiveness of IFN-γ MSCs, pre-injection of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide attenuated the anti-fibrotic effect of MSCs compared with mice treated with no immunosuppressive drug, while cyclosporine pre-administration had no influence on the MSC-induced suppression of fibrosis.

We further investigated the effects of methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide pre-administration in attenuating the effect of MSCs on renal fibrosis by calculating the values of the MSC-treated and IFN-γ MSC-treated groups relative to the average value of each UUO group with pre-administration of each immunosuppressive drug (set as 1.0). The anti-fibrotic effect of MSCs was significantly reduced following pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide (Table 1 and Figure 2J). These findings suggested that pre-injection of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide attenuated the suppressive effect of MSCs on fibrosis, and may thus play a suppressive role in the activation mechanism of MSCs.

Impact of pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide on the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs

Given that methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide pre-administration attenuated the renal anti-fibrotic effect of MSCs, we examined the impact of pre-administration of each immunosuppressive drug on the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs. The extent of inflammation was evaluated by immunohistochemical staining of CD3 and CD68 in kidney specimens obtained from rats 7 days after the UUO procedure. In the absence of immunosuppressive drugs, MSCs reduced the UUO-induced increase in CD3-positive cells, and this effect was stronger for IFN-γ MSCs. Pre-administration of the immunosuppressive drugs had no effect on the reduction in CD3-positive cells produced by IFN-γ MSCs. MSC treatment did not significantly reduce the number of CD3-positive cells in either the methylprednisolone or cyclosporine pre-administration group, but did decrease the number of CD3-positive cells after pre-administration of cyclophosphamide (Fig. 3A and B). In the absence of any immunosuppressive drug, UUO also increased CD68-positive cells, which were decreased by MSC administration and further reduced by IFN-γ MSC injection. IFN-γ MSC infusion had a favorable therapeutic effect despite pre-administration of any of the immunosuppressive drugs. Although MSC treatment significantly reduced the number of CD68-positive cells following cyclosporine pre-administration, similar to the effect with no immunosuppressive drug, pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide abolished this effect (Fig. 3C and D). To further examine the influences of methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide pre-administration on the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs, we calculated the values for the MSC-treated and IFN-γ MSC-treated groups relative to the average value of the UUO groups pre-administrated with each immunosuppressive drug (set as 1.0), against the quantitative results of CD3 and CD68 immunohistochemistry staining. Pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide significantly elevated the numbers of CD3- and CD68-positive cells in the renal cortex in the MSC-treated groups compared with those without immunosuppressive drugs (Table 2 and Figure 3E). These results indicated that pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide attenuated the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs.

Different effects of pre-administration of methylprednisolone, cyclophosphamide, and cyclosporine on MSC activation

We investigated the expression levels of HMGB1, IFN-γ, and IL-18 in rats sacrificed 1 (UUO day 1), 4 (UUO day 4), and 7 days (UUO day 7) after the UUO procedure. Hmgb1, Ifng, and Il18 mRNA levels increased gradually with time from the UUO procedure. Hmgb1 and Ifng mRNA levels were significantly increased after UUO day 4 and Il18 mRNA levels were statistically upregulated by UUO day 7 (Figure 4A). Consistent with the elevated α-SMA protein levels, protein levels of HMGB1 and IFN-γ were significantly upregulated after UUO day 4 (Fig. 4B and C). We also examined the effects of pre-administration of each immunosuppressive drug on expression levels at UUO day 4. Hmgb1 and Ifng mRNA levels were significantly decreased by pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide, but not by pre-injection of cyclosporine. Il18 mRNA levels were unaffected by methylprednisolone pre-administration, significantly increased by cyclosporine pre-injection, and statistically decreased by cyclophosphamide pre-treatment (Figure 4D). Pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide also significantly reduced HMGB1 and IFN-γ protein levels, while cyclosporine pre-administration had no effect (Fig. 4E and F). HMGB1 released from the nucleus into the cytoplasm and extracellular fluid act in a tissue-protective manner under some conditions.52 In addition to total protein expression, we therefore also measured the concentration of HMGB1 secreted in the same models. Plasma HMGB1 concentrations increased significantly after UUO day 4, similar to renal total HMGB1 protein concentrations (Figure 4G). Pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide significantly decreased HMGB1 concentrations, whereas cyclosporine pre-injection had no effect on the HMGB1 concentration (Figure 4H). Total HMGB1 protein levels thus showed similar tendencies in renal tissue and plasma. These results indicated that pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide could reduce IFN-γ and HMGB1 expression levels, thereby interfering with MSC activation and attenuating their therapeutic effect. In contrast, pre-administration of cyclosporine had no effect on these expression levels.

Effect of cyclophosphamide pre-injection on renal engraftment rate of MSCs

SDF-1, which is also known as CXCL12, belongs to the CXC chemokine subfamily and is released from the site of injury.53 SDF-1 is considered the most potent chemoattractant signaling agent and has been identified as a stem-cell-homing factor,54 and its signaling is initiated by ligation of the chemokine with its receptor CXCR4.55 MSCs administered intravascularly have been shown to exert a therapeutic effect by migrating to target SDF-1 released from the damaged region.54,56 We therefore investigated the expression levels of Cxcl12 mRNA and SDF-1 protein, the renal engraftment rate of MSCs, and the effects of the different immunosuppressive drugs on these changes. Cxcl12 mRNA and SDF-1 protein levels increased progressively from UUO day 1 to day 7, and SDF-1 protein levels were significantly upregulated after UUO day 4 (Fig. 5A–C). We then determined the effects of pre-administration of each immunosuppressive drug on Cxcl12 mRNA and SDF-1 protein levels at UUO day 4. Cxcl12 and SDF-1 expression levels were unchanged by methylprednisolone, greatly enhanced by cyclosporine, and markedly attenuated by cyclophosphamide pre-treatment (Fig. 5D–F). In addition, we evaluated the number of MSCs adhering to the kidneys in EGFP-MSCs administered to rats via the tail vein on UUO day 4. The number of viable cells in the kidney was measured by counting EGFP-positive cells in the renal cortex at days 1 (MSC day 1), 3 (MSC day 3), and 5 (MSC day 5) after MSC injection. EGFP-MSCs were abundant on MSC day 1, but gradually diminished and were barely visible on MSC day 5 (Fig. 5G and H). Regarding the effects of pre-administration of each immunosuppressive drug, the number of EGFP-MSCs engrafted into the kidney was unchanged by methylprednisolone and significantly decreased by cyclophosphamide pre-administration. In contrast, the number of viable MSCs in the kidney was statistically increased by cyclosporine pre-treatment. These results were consistent with changes in the expression levels of Cxcl12 and SDF-1 (Fig. 5I and J), suggesting that expression levels of these factors were correlated with the MSC-engraftment rate. Finally, we performed experiments using CXCR4 knockdown EGFP-MSCs to verify the hypothesis that pre-administration of cyclophosphamide downregulated SDF-1 expression leading to reduced MSC engraftment in damaged tissues. We confirmed successful CXCR4 knockdown by determining CXCR4 expression levels by RT-PCR after transfection of MSCs with CXCR4 siRNA (CXCR4 siRNA/EGFP-MSCs) or negative control siRNA (NC siRNA/EGFP-MSCs) (Figure 5K). CXCR4 siRNA/EGFP-MSCs and NC siRNA/EGFP-MSCs were then injected into rats via the tail vein and the engraftment rate of EGFP-MSCs in the renal cortex at MSC day 1 was evaluated. The CXCR4 siRNA/EGFP-MSC-treated group had a significantly lower engraftment rate than the NC siRNA/EGFP-MSC-treated group (Fig. 5L and M). These results indicated that the pre-administration of cyclophosphamide was responsible for the decreased MSC engraftment rate due to a decrease in SDF-1.

Discussion

MSCs have shown great potential as a cell therapy to prevent the progression of organ injury11-13; however, the influence of immunosuppressive drugs on the efficacy of MSC therapy remains unclear.8,57 In this study, we examined the effects of pre-administration of immunosuppressive drugs on the activation and migration capacity of MSCs, which in turn affect their therapeutic efficacy. We confirmed that the efficacy of MSC therapy was attenuated under immunosuppressed conditions in SCID mice. Pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide weakened the therapeutic effect of MSCs on inflammatory cell infiltration and renal interstitial fibrosis induced by UUO in rat models. Conversely, cyclosporine pre-injection had only a slight effect on the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs, and no impact on the anti-fibrotic effect. The 3 immunosuppressive drugs have different effects on immunocompetent cells. Given the pharmacological effects of methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, combined with the characteristics of SCID mice, we presumed that the reduced B-cell function common to both may have played a role in attenuating the therapeutic effects of MSCs. In addition, these results suggested that pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide downregulated the expression of factors associated with MSC activation and their release from damaged tissues, resulting in reducing their therapeutic effects. Notably, pre-administration of cyclophosphamide decreased the expression of factors associated with MSC migration and reduced the number of viable MSCs in the kidney. This result was supported by the fact that administration of MSCs with CXCR4 knockdown decreased the engraftment rate. Our results thus indicated that pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide, unlike cyclosporine, diminished the efficacy of MSC therapy by interfering with the activation of the infused MSCs.

MSCs exist in an inactive form in healthy organisms, but are converted to an active form by HMGB1 and IFN-γ released from damaged tissues and cytokines produced by immune cells.58,59 Furthermore, MSCs migrate and engraft by targeting factors released from the site of injury, such as HMGB1 and SDF-1 [54, 56]. The number of viable MSCs in target organs is thus an important factor affecting their therapeutic efficacy.60 Immunosuppressive drugs have the potential to affect immune cells and reduce cytokine production,61 thereby inhibiting MSC activation and eventually suppressing their anti-inflammatory effects. However, immunosuppressive drugs themselves may also inhibit the progression of fibrosis by suppressing inflammation. We therefore investigated how immunosuppressive drugs ultimately affected the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. Our results showed that pre-administration of methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide weakened the therapeutic effect of MSC therapy on organ failure by downregulating the expression of the MSC activators HMGB1, IFN-γ, and Il18, and also the MSC migration factors Cxcl12 and SDF-1. Notably, MSCs pre-activated with IFN-γ maintained the therapeutic effect even when after pre-administration of these immunosuppressive drugs. In contrast to methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, pre-administration of cyclosporine did not suppress the expression of MSC activators and migration factors, and unaffected their anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects.

This study had 3 limitations. The first limitation concerns the dose of the immunosuppressive drugs. Some immunosuppressive drugs have different functions at low and high doses,62,63 and we selected doses based on previous studies in which MSCs were co-administered with immunosuppressive drugs.64,65 However, the dose and method of administration (oral or intravenous) differ according to the disease being treated in actual clinical practice. Further research regarding the relationships among dose, method of administration, and therapeutic effect is therefore needed. The second limitation concerns the strain of mice used in this study. Although the results suggested that B cells influence the therapeutic effect of MSCs, we used SCID mice because there is currently no strain in which only B cells are knocked down. Besides, it is difficult to fully elucidate the detailed mechanisms given that each immunosuppressive drug affects specific immune cells, which are closely related to each other, and MSCs exert their therapeutic effects through multifaceted mechanisms. The third limitation was related to the responsivity of human MSCs to rodent cytokines.66 Rat and human IFN-γ both significantly increased PTGES expression levels in human MSCs; however, although IDO1 expression levels were also significantly upregulated by rat IFN-γ, the response was less than for human IFN-γ. It thus appears that the reduction of cytokines in rats by immunosuppressive drugs is at least partly responsible for the attenuated therapeutic effect of MSCs, given that rat IFN-γ increased the expression of PTGES and IDO1 in human MSCs; however, the extent of their contribution is unknown and further studies are required, along with the investigation of other factors involved in the therapeutic effect that could not be investigated in detail in this study.

Methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide are used to treat a variety of diseases such as post-organ transplantation and connective tissue, hematologic, neoplastic, autoimmune, allergic, and skin diseases.50,61,63 Our findings suggest that MSC therapy may not provide adequate therapeutic benefit in patients receiving methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide, and MSCs pre-activated with IFN-γ or by other means should therefore be used for these patients. Meanwhile, the efficacy of MSC therapy is unaffected and maintained in patients receiving cyclosporine.

In summary, our study demonstrated that the administration of certain immunosuppressive drugs inhibits MSC activation and engraftment at the site of injury, thus significantly attenuating their therapeutic efficacy. These findings provide essential information to guide patient selection in clinical studies of MSCs, though further detailed studies are warranted.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that the administration of certain immunosuppressive drugs inhibits MSC activation and engraftment at the site of injury, thus significantly attenuating their therapeutic efficacy. These findings provide essential information to guide patient selection in clinical studies of MSCs, though further detailed studies are warranted.
