The Efficacy of Acupuncture and Bee Venom Injections in Improving Hearing in a Patient with Idiopathic Conductive Hearing Loss: a case report
Authors: Eun Kyung Lee, Min Hee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conductive hearing loss (CHL) is commonly caused by tympanic membrane perforation or otitis media. Congenital ossicular anomalies and otosclerosis are the primary considerations in patients with normal tympanic membranes and external auditory canals, whereas stapedius tendon abnormalities are less frequently observed. Acupuncture and bee venom (BV) injections are frequently used for treating otological disorders; however, their effectiveness in treating CHL remains unexplored. Therefore, this case report describes a 22-year-old man with idiopathic left-sided CHL that persisted for 101 days. While computed tomography, otoscopic examination, and tympanometry did not yield significant results, pure-tone audiometry (PTA) showed elevated hearing thresholds consistent with CHL. This led to the suspicion of middle ear pathologies, including otosclerosis and stapedius tendon anomalies. The patient underwent acupuncture and BV injection therapy. The average PTA improved from 40 to 26 dB after 23 days of treatment, marking the first improvement in hearing thresholds since symptoms began. While causality cannot be established from a single case report, this temporal association suggests a potential therapeutic role of these interventions. This case suggests a potential therapeutic role of acupuncture and BV injections in patients with idiopathic CHL who have not undergone surgical diagnosis and treatment. Further investigation is warranted to determine the efficacy and mechanisms of these interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Hearing loss is classified into three categories: conductive, sensorineural, and mixed. Conductive hearing loss (CHL) has various etiologies, including ear canal blockage, tympanic membrane perforation, ossicular defects, and otitis media. Therefore, a thorough history must be obtained, and the tympanic membrane must be examined otoscopically for diagnosis. Patients with CHL but normal tympanic membranes and external auditory canals may have congenital ossicular malformations, otosclerosis, trauma-induced ossicular damage, or obliterative otitis media [1]. Diagnosis of suspected trauma or obliterative otitis media typically involves patient history and imaging techniques such as temporal bone computed tomography (CT). Exploratory tympanotomy may be required even in patients with normal temporal bone CT findings to confirm middle ear and ossicle abnormalities. Stapedius muscle integrity should be assessed in rare cases without complete ossicular fixation or malformations [2]. Ossification or shortening of the stapedius tendon may be suspected in idiopathic cases, potentially restricting stapes mobility and causing CHL, although this has rarely been confirmed. The current treatment options are limited, with surgery being the only established intervention [3]. Alternative, less-invasive therapies must be explored due to the invasiveness and limitations of surgical options.

Traditional Korean treatments such as acupuncture and herbal medicine are commonly used for various ear disorders [4]. However, no study has specifically investigated the effectiveness of acupuncture for CHL, likely because CHL is typically associated with identifiable structural pathologies, with treatment focused on addressing the underlying cause. Furthermore, no reports have been published on the efficacy of bee venom (BV) injections for auditory disorders.

This case report describes a 22-year-old patient with persistent idiopathic CHL. Ossification or shortening of the stapedius tendon was suspected as the etiology. The patient was treated with a course of acupuncture and BV injections, targeting the middle ear function. Substantial auditory improvement was achieved for the first time since symptom onset. Herein, we report a case providing preliminary clinical observations suggesting a potential therapeutic role of acupuncture and BV injections in patients with idiopathic CHL unresponsive to conventional treatment, particularly in cases where stapedius tendon pathology is clinically suspected. Additionally, we discuss the treatment processes and review the relevant literature. This case provides a reference for managing similar cases in future clinical practice.

CASE PRESENTATION

A healthy 22-year-old man presented to our traditional Korean medicine hospital 101 days after experiencing unilateral left-sided hearing loss, tinnitus, and ear fullness. He fully recovered from sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) in his left ear 6 years ago. The patient had no family history of hearing loss. He visited an otolaryngology clinic on the day of hearing loss and was diagnosed with SSNHL according to pure-tone audiometry (PTA) results (Fig. 1A). His hearing did not improve after eight rounds of intratympanic steroids and two cycles of high-dose systemic steroids (starting at 60 mg/day and tapered over 2 weeks). On Day 68 after the onset, the patient reported additional hearing deterioration in the left ear. PTA revealed a decline in hearing thresholds from 26 dB to 38 dB. Notably, the hearing loss pattern changed from SSNHL to CHL (Fig. 1B). Four additional rounds of intratympanic steroids and high-dose steroids (starting at 50 mg/day, tapered over 1 week; second cycle at 45 mg/day, tapered over 1 week) yielded no improvement in hearing thresholds.

Furthermore, auditory brainstem response, distortion product otoacoustic emission, transient evoked otoacoustic emission, and magnetic resonance imaging were conducted. However, no vestibular schwannoma or central brain abnormalities were observed. No specific findings were observed in the left and right internal auditory canals in the CT scan of the skull. The tympanometry results for both ears were normal (type A). He was diagnosed with left-sided CHL and advised of the potential need for exploratory tympanotomy and future stapes surgery to evaluate possible ossicular fixation before visiting our hospital. The patient was admitted to our hospital 101 days after experiencing symptoms. Electroacupuncture was administered twice daily, and BV therapy was administered once daily. Additionally, no Western medications were administered during this period.

Acupuncture needles measuring 0.25 mm in diameter and 30 mm in length (Dong Bang, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) were manually inserted into specific acupoints for 20 min per session: LI4, ST36, SI19, and SI16 on both sides, and TE21, GB2, ST7, and TE16 on the left side only. The needle insertion depth varied by location: 1-1.5 cm around the ear and 0.5-1 cm in other areas. Electroacupuncture was performed using an electroacupuncture stimulator (STN-330; StraTek Co., Gyeonggi-do, Korea), with needles inserted at LI4 and ST36 on both sides, and at SI19, SI16, TE21, and TE16 on the left side. Stimulation occurred at a steady frequency of 30 Hz, with intensity modified according to the patient’s pain tolerance.

Purified BV (Chungjin Biotech, Korea) diluted 1:30,000 in distilled water was injected into specific points near the affected ear (TE21 and GB2) at a dose of 0.02 mL per injection site. PTA was performed before (Fig. 1C) and after treatment (Fig. 1D). The patient reported considerable reductions in ear fullness and tinnitus throughout the treatment period. Hearing thresholds significantly improved for the first time since symptom onset after 23 days of treatment. The average hearing threshold improved from 40 dB to 26 dB (Fig. 1D) and remained considerably better at discharge, averaging 28 dB with slight fluctuations (Fig. 1E). Fig. 2 illustrates the clinical timeline of the treatment course, with no adverse events reported during the treatment period.

The Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong reviewed and approved this case study (Approval No: KHNMC-OH-IRB 2023-11-003). The IRB waived the requirement for informed consent due to the minimal risk to the human subject involved in the study. Nevertheless, written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this report.

DISCUSSION

CHL without external/middle ear abnormalities and deformities in other body parts is rare in clinical practice. The causes of CHL include congenital ossicular anomalies, otosclerosis, and abnormalities or disruptions in the ossicular chain resulting from trauma [1]. However, identifying the specific cause can be challenging and requires exploratory tympanotomy [5].

Our patient presented with idiopathic CHL in the left ear. The transition from an initial SNHL pattern to a predominantly CHL pattern by Day 77 is a diagnostic feature of this case. At symptom onset (D + 1), markedly elevated bone conduction thresholds at 2,000 Hz (30 dB HL) and 4,000 Hz (35 dB HL) obscured the underlying air-bone gap, producing an audiometric profile consistent with sensorineural or mixed hearing loss (Table 1). Two complementary mechanisms may account for this initial presentation.

First, a concurrent acute cochlear insult superimposed upon the pre-existing stapedius tendon anomaly may have elevated the bone conduction thresholds, thereby masking the conductive component. This phenomenon—in which a simultaneous sensorineural component audiometrically masks a coexisting conductive pathology—has been documented in reported cases of mixed hearing loss arising from concurrent inner ear and middle ear dysfunction [6, 7]. In the present case, the multiple courses of corticosteroids administered during the early clinical stages may have contributed to partial cochlear recovery, potentially unmasking the structural conductive pathology that had been present from the outset.

Second, even independent of a true cochlear insult, the pre-existing stapedius tendon pathology may have contributed to an artificially elevated bone conduction threshold through the Carhart effect. It is well established that fixation or hypomobility of the stapes disrupts the inertial component of ossicular bone conduction, whereby the ossicular chain’s relative lag behind skull vibration normally contributes to cochlear stimulation during bone-conducted sound. This results in mechanical elevation of bone conduction thresholds, particularly in the mid-to-high frequency range, that does not reflect true cochlear dysfunction [8-10]. In either case, this audiometric transition should not be interpreted as the emergence of a new pathological process but as the sequential unmasking of a pre-existing structural middle ear anomaly that had been audiometrically obscured since the initial presentation.

The tympanic membrane findings on otoscopic examination were normal, and the patient had no history of trauma or otitis media; therefore, tympanic membrane perforation, otitis media, and trauma-induced ossicular disruption were not considered. Accordingly, a systematic evaluation of other potential causes of CHL presenting with an intact tympanic membrane was conducted. Superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome (SCDS) can produce a conductive-type air-bone gap through a “third-window” mechanism and may closely mimic otosclerosis clinically, including normal tympanometry and preserved acoustic reflexes. According to the Bárány Society diagnostic criteria, a definitive diagnosis of SCDS requires at least one symptom attributable to third-window pathophysiology—most characteristically, vertigo and/or oscillopsia induced by loud sounds (Tullio phenomenon) or by stimuli that change middle ear or intracranial pressure (fistula symptoms)—in conjunction with corroborating physiological and imaging findings [11]. Although SCDS may occasionally present with audiological symptoms alone, these cardinal vestibular features are considered essential diagnostic anchors of the syndrome [11]. Our patient reported no dizziness, vertigo, oscillopsia, autophonia, or any symptom triggered by sound or pressure stimuli throughout the entire 101-day symptomatic period prior to admission. These hallmark vestibular features were completely absent, and the audiometric pattern featured an air-bone gap that was more prominent at higher frequencies (2,000-4,000 Hz), contrary to the characteristic low-frequency predominance of SCDS [11]. This significantly diminishes the clinical probability of this diagnosis, even in the absence of dedicated multiplanar CT reconstructions in the plane of the superior semicircular canal.

Perilymphatic fistula (PLF) was also considered as a potential etiology, as an abnormal communication at the oval or round window can—analogous to a third-window mechanism—dissipate vibratory energy transmitted through the stapes footplate, thereby producing a conductive-type air-bone gap regardless of whether active perilymph leakage is present [12]. However, PLF was effectively excluded on clinical grounds. PLF typically arises following identifiable precipitating events—including head trauma, physical exertion, barotrauma, or Valsalva-type maneuvers—through sudden elevations of intracranial or intralabyrinthine pressure. Its hallmark presentation combines hearing loss with vestibular disturbances such as vertigo or disequilibrium, often accompanied by tinnitus, aural fullness, and characteristically fluctuating hearing loss [13]. In the present patient, no precipitating mechanical event was identified, vestibular symptoms were entirely absent, and the hearing loss remained stable over the preceding months, a clinical course fundamentally inconsistent with PLF.

This left ossicular fixation, such as otosclerosis or an abnormality of the stapedius tendon, as the possible cause of the patient’s idiopathic CHL. However, in this case, high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the temporal bone—an essential tool for diagnosing otosclerosis—revealed no structural abnormalities [5]. Moreover, while otosclerosis typically presents as progressive hearing loss that worsens at lower frequencies [14], this patient’s hearing loss worsened at higher frequencies. In addition, the tympanometry results showed a normal type A curve, rather than the flattened type As curves commonly associated with otosclerosis [14-16]. Taken together, the patient’s audiometric profile, CT findings, and tympanometry results were inconsistent with the characteristic patterns of otosclerosis. Consequently, we concluded that stapedius tendon ossification or shortening was the most plausible underlying etiology. However, definitive diagnosis of stapedius tendon ossification or shortening requires surgical intervention via exploratory tympanotomy [2]. Indeed, all reported cases to date involving the diagnosis and treatment of stapedius tendon abnormalities have been confirmed through this procedure (Table 2).

Among seven cases of CHL caused by stapedius tendon issues discussed in six studies [2, 3, 5, 17-19], the cause of CHL was ossification in all but one study in which shortening was identified [3]. All studies employed exploratory tympanotomy to confirm the diagnosis. Other examinations, including otoscopy and temporal bone CT, did not reveal any findings suggestive of an underlying cause. Stapedius tendon resection was performed in all patients, which resulted in marked improvement in audiometry.

Exploratory tympanotomy is the best method for determining the precise cause of idiopathic CHL. However, the patient in our case declined further invasive surgical intervention, as substantial hearing improvement was achieved following traditional Korean medicine treatment. Consequently, we could not definitively confirm whether the underlying cause of the patient’s CHL was indeed stapedius tendon pathology.

To date, no treatment other than surgical intervention has been shown to be effective for anomalies within the middle ear, including abnormalities involving the stapedius tendon and fixation of the ossicles. However, in this case, we achieved hearing improvement with a non-invasive intervention, suggesting the potential of conservative therapeutic approaches.

The exact mechanism through which acupuncture and BV injection may have contributed to the observed improvement in CHL remains speculative and has not been directly established. Based on previously reported effects of acupuncture in musculoskeletal conditions, we hypothesize that the treatment may have primarily driven a functional modulation. Specifically, it may have enhanced regional microvascular blood circulation and promoted the neuromuscular relaxation of the surrounding musculature, including the potentially contracted stapedius muscle complex [20].

Additionally, BV has shown promise for reducing muscle tension and pain in musculoskeletal conditions, with proposed mechanisms largely attributed to its melittin and phospholipase A2 (PLA2) components [21]. Furthermore, BV may have contributed to suppressing inflammatory processes associated with tendon ossification through NF-κB-mediated pathways [22]. Regarding clinical safety, although no adverse events were observed in this case using a high dilution of purified BV (1:30,000), BV therapy generally carries an inherent risk of hypersensitivity reactions, including rare cases of life-threatening anaphylaxis [23]. Therefore, prior skin testing and close monitoring remain highly recommended in clinical practice, and safety in a single case should not be generalized to all patient populations.

This concurrent approach of combining electroacupuncture and BV injection was designed to address complementary aspects of the suspected pathology. That is, electroacupuncture primarily targeted the functional neuromuscular relaxation of the hypertonic stapedius muscle complex through regional neural and microvascular modulation, while BV was selected for its proposed biochemical capacity to attenuate the underlying chronic inflammatory and fibrotic components potentially associated with the tendon anomaly.

With respect to the anatomical plausibility of this hypothesis, the following must be acknowledged: the acupoints employed in this case—TE21 (Ermen) and GB2 (Tinghui)—are located at the periauricular skin surface, while the stapedius muscle lies deep within the pyramidal eminence of the middle ear, enclosed by the tympanic cavity. No direct physical pathway exists between the needle insertion site and the target anatomical structure. Therefore, any possible therapeutic effect would necessarily be mediated through indirect neurophysiological mechanisms. We propose two speculative pathways through which periauricular electroacupuncture may exert such indirect effects.

First, regarding local circulatory modulation, a systematic review [24] on acupuncture-induced microcirculatory changes proposed that acupuncture-induced neural signaling alters blood perfusion at and around the needled site via the local action of vasodilative factors such as substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP). Notably, acupoint stimulation increases perfusion not only at the needled site itself but also in adjacent areas along the meridian pathway, suggesting that the circulatory influence of acupuncture extends beyond the immediate insertion site [24].

Second, with respect to neurotrophic modulation, electroacupuncture applied to periauricular acupoints including TE17 (Yifeng), located immediately posterior to the ear, has been demonstrated to significantly upregulate the expression of glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) mRNA and protein in central facial motoneurons [25]. At the same time, it has been shown to reduce peripheral inflammatory cell infiltration in facial nerve tissue in an animal model of facial nerve crush injury [25]. These findings support a model in which electroacupuncture stimulation at periauricular acupoints indirectly affects adjacent neural structures through neurotrophic factor-mediated signaling, extending beyond the immediate needle insertion site [25].

Through these two proposed neurophysiological cascades—regional microvascular modulation and periauricular neurotrophic upregulation—it is speculated that treatment may have indirectly modulated the middle ear microenvironment, potentially including local tissue perfusion, inflammatory tone, and neuromuscular regulation of the stapedius complex. However, direct evidence for these mechanisms in the context of the middle ear is lacking. The anatomical distance between the periauricular acupoints and the stapedius tendon, as well as the absence of serial imaging to document structural changes, preclude any definitive mechanistic conclusion. Thus, these proposed pathways should be interpreted strictly as working hypotheses intended to guide future mechanistic investigation and not as established explanations for the observed clinical outcome.

The temporal association between the initiation of treatment and the improvement in hearing thresholds warrants cautious interpretation, as several alternative explanations cannot be entirely excluded. Spontaneous fluctuation of hearing thresholds has been reported in certain types of hearing loss [26], and delayed therapeutic effects from the multiple prior courses of systemic and intratympanic corticosteroids are also possible [27]. PTA is known to have inherent test-retest variability of approximately 5-10 dB across frequencies, which must be considered when evaluating changes in hearing thresholds [28].

However, several lines of evidence collectively contradict these alternative explanations. First, serial audiograms were obtained at multiple institutions across thirteen timepoints prior to and during treatment (Table 1). These recordings demonstrated a stable plateau of hearing loss ranging from 35 to 40 dB over a period of more than 5 weeks (Day 77 to Day 112 from symptom onset), with no consistent trend toward improvement despite ongoing or recently completed corticosteroid therapy. Notably, two audiometric assessments performed at different institutions on Day 101 from onset yielded identical average PTA values of 40 dB. While individual frequency thresholds showed minor variations of up to 10 dB at specific frequencies, these differences fall within the established range of audiometric test-retest variability, confirming high inter-institutional measurement consistency and supporting the overall reliability of the serial audiometric data.

Second, a delayed corticosteroid response is unlikely in this context. Following the final course of corticosteroids, serial audiometry demonstrated that hearing thresholds remained unchanged or slightly deteriorated over the subsequent 4 weeks, with no evidence of a progressive recovery trend (Table 1). This temporal pattern is inconsistent with the established profile of steroid-mediated delayed recovery: one study reported that 78.2% of patients who experienced delayed recovery after corticosteroid therapy did so within 1 month of treatment completion, with only 3.6% recovering after 3 months [27]. In the present case, no audiometric improvement was detected during this high-probability recovery window. Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of corticosteroids in sudden hearing loss is critically time-dependent. That is, according to one study [29], complete recovery was not observed when treatment was initiated beyond 14 days from symptom onset, and no recovery of any type was reported after 31 days. This renders a new delayed response in a patient who showed no audiometric improvement over the course of 85 days of repeated steroid courses physiologically implausible. Moreover, the mechanistic basis for a corticosteroid response was further undermined by the nature of the residual hearing loss itself. By the time Korean medicine treatment was initiated, the audiogram had transitioned from an initial SNHL pattern to a predominantly CHL pattern, characterized by preserved bone conduction thresholds and a widened air-bone gap. The established mechanisms by which corticosteroids exert therapeutic effects on the inner ear—namely, anti-inflammatory and antiapoptotic signaling through glucocorticoid receptors and ion homeostasis regulation through mineralocorticoid receptor binding in the cochlear lateral wall and stria vascularis [30]—are specific to sensorineural pathophysiology and do not address middle ear mechanical dysfunction.

Third, spontaneous recovery is an unlikely explanation for the improvement observed in this case. Although spontaneous hearing improvement has been documented in approximately 32-65% of untreated patients with idiopathic SSNHL [26, 31], all such data derive exclusively from cochlear-origin SNHL and cannot be extrapolated to the predominantly CHL pattern present at the time Korean medicine treatment was initiated. Even within the SSNHL literature, spontaneous recovery usually occurs within the first 2 weeks of onset [32], and recovery beyond 60 days is exceedingly rare [33]; in the present case, 99 days had elapsed since symptom onset by the time treatment commenced. Additionally, the stable audiometric plateau sustained for more than 5 weeks without any upward trend—rather than a gradually improving trajectory—is consistent with a poor spontaneous recovery prognosis, as early audiometric stasis at 1-2 weeks following onset has been associated with poor long-term hearing outcomes [34]. Furthermore, the most plausible mechanical etiology for the observed CHL pattern—including otosclerosis or stapedius tendon abnormality—signifies a structurally progressive condition for which no spontaneous resolution has been documented in the literature. Hearing recovery in reported cases of stapedius tendon ossification or shortening has been achieved exclusively through surgical division or excision of the tendon [2, 3, 18, 35], further reducing the likelihood that the observed improvement represents spontaneous remission.

Fourth, the improvement seen in this case of approximately 14 dB in average PTA clearly exceeds the upper bound of typical audiometric test-retest variability [28], and it was accompanied by consistent subjective reductions in ear fullness and tinnitus throughout the treatment course. Taken together, these observations support the clinical significance of the hearing improvement, while we acknowledge that it cannot be definitively attributed to the administered therapies from this single uncontrolled case report.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first reports to describe hearing improvement through non-surgical intervention in a patient with idiopathic CHL, particularly one with clinically suspected stapedius tendon abnormalities. It suggests a potential role of acupuncture and BV injection as alternative therapeutic options for patients with idiopathic CHL who either are not suitable candidates for surgery or decline surgical treatment. By presenting detailed clinical procedures and outcomes, this case provides a reproducible framework for future clinical and mechanistic investigations.

Nevertheless, this case has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the specific cause of hearing loss in this patient remained unclear, as a definitive diagnosis was not established without exploratory tympanotomy, and the mechanism through which acupuncture and BV injections contributed to the observed improvement in hearing thresholds is not yet understood. Second, and critically, electroacupuncture and BV injection were administered concurrently throughout the treatment course. Consequently, it is not possible to determine the relative contribution of each modality to the observed improvement or whether their effects were additive or synergistic. Third, the interpretation of the observed hearing improvement warrants careful consideration. Although the improvement in average PTA from 40 dB (pre-treatment) to 28 dB (post-treatment) represents a clinically meaningful gain of 12 dB, this final hearing level closely approximates the patient’s audiometric status at symptom onset (26 dB, Day 1). Accordingly, Korean medicine treatment may primarily facilitate recovery to baseline hearing rather than enhance it beyond baseline levels. This interpretation should be considered when evaluating the overall magnitude of the treatment effect observed in this case. Future studies employing a controlled, single-modality design or crossover approach are necessary to isolate the therapeutic contribution of each intervention. Further mechanistic and clinical studies are therefore warranted to validate these findings and explore their broader applicability.
