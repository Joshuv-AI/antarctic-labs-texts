Dynamical Formation of Self-Similar Wormholes
Yasutaka Koga, Ryota Maeda, Daiki Saito, Daisuke Yoshida

Abstract
We study spherically symmetric, self-similar wormhole solutions supported by colliding streams of negative-energy null dust, and their dynamical formation. Under the assumption of self-similarity, the Einstein equations reduce to a system of ordinary differential equations, which we solve numerically under boundary conditions enforcing the existence of a minimal areal radius (the throat) on constant-time hypersurfaces. For a sufficiently large throat radius, the resulting geometries remain regular at both spatial and future null infinity, while a singularity is retained in the past direction. We then construct a dynamical formation scenario by patching together three regions: a Schwarzschild black hole, negative-energy Vaidya spacetimes, and the self-similar wormhole geometry. These regions are joined across null shells using the Barrabès–Israel formalism, which provides explicit relations among the throat radius, the black hole’s mass and the energy injection by the shell, demonstrating that an initial black hole can evolve into a wormhole. Our analysis generalizes the formation model for static wormhole solutions proposed by Hayward and Koyama in 2004 to non-static wormhole solutions, offering a novel perspective on the formation of regular traversable wormholes.

Introduction

Traversable wormholes, hypothetical spacetime configurations that allow causal communication between distant regions, have been investigated extensively since the pioneering works of Morris and Thorne
[
32
,
33
]
.
Today, they serve as theoretical laboratories for exploring the interplay among geometry, exotic matter sources, and quantum effects, beyond their speculative use as transport devices for interstellar travel.
It has been pointed out
[
32
,
11
,
36
,
20
,
35
,
10
]
that, static wormhole solutions in general relativity necessitate the violation of the (averaged) null energy conditions
[

,
14
]
.
This fact has motivated many studies of possible exotic fields that could support such geometries. For example, wormhole solutions with a phantom scalar field have been investigated in Refs.
[
3
,
8
,
1
,
28
]
.
Realization of the violation of the energy condition without assuming an exotic field has been studied, for example, considering the squeezed states of matter
[
19
]
, holographic setup
[
12
,
31
,
4
,
30
,
23
]
the Casimir energy of fields
[
29
,
13
]
, and the higher derivative corrections to the Einstein equations
[
22
]
.

Alongside the question of matter sources, considerable attention has been devoted to purely geometric constructions of wormhole spacetimes with minimal assumptions on the matter content.
For example, in Ref.
[
18
]
, Hayward constructed the analytic solution of a static and spherically symmetric wormhole.
In that work, the author just assumed that the energy-momentum tensor takes the form of the colliding null dust with the negative energy, without any further assumptions on the matter field.
Due to this minimal assumption, the solution is locally applicable to any matter source that shares the same form of the energy-momentum tensor around the throat.
However, it cannot be regarded as a globally viable wormhole, since singularities appear in the spatial and null directions, as pointed out in Ref.
[
24
]
.
In Ref.
[
24
]
, the Hayward solution was extended to include the electric charges, and it is found that the singularity persists even in the presence of charges.
This indicates that singularities are a generic feature of static models with this type of energy-momentum tensor.

A different but related question concerns the dynamical formation of wormholes.
While black hole formation is widely regarded as a natural outcome of stellar collapse, the dynamical formation of wormholes from realistic initial conditions presents distinct theoretical challenges.
Several mechanisms have been proposed, such as irradiation of negative energy into black holes
[
17
]
and geometric patching that yields Roberts-type solutions as the final state
[
27
]
.
Among these studies,Hayward and Koyama
[
16
,
25
]
showed that injecting negative-energy flux into a Schwarzschild black hole can generate a static Hayward wormhole
[
18
]
through thin-shell matching based on the Barrabès–Israel formalism
[
2
]
.
However, this construction inherits the same limitation as the Hayward solution
[
18
]
, namely the presence of spacetime singularities.

In this paper, we consider a non-static extension of Hayward’s model
[
18
]
and its application to a formation scenario
[
16
,
25
]
.
In particular, we impose self-similar symmetry to analyze time-dependent wormhole solutions in a simplified setting and investigate how the geometry—including the occurrence of singularities—evolves.
This work also aims to construct a comprehensive dynamical formation model by employing the Barrabès–Israel formalism
[
2
]
with Schwarzschild black holes being initial conditions of formation of wormhole geometry.

The paper is organized as follows.

develops the self-similar wormhole solution.
Section

Section
4
is dedicated to the summary and discussion.

2
Self-similar wormhole solution

2.1
Spherically symmetric spacetime with colliding null dust

We begin by introducing the spherically symmetric ansatz describing two
counter-propagating streams of null dust,
extending the static setup introduced by Hayward
[
18
]
.
This matter model provides a particularly transparent way to realize violations of the null energy condition under spherical symmetry.
It therefore serves as a convenient starting point for constructing time-dependent wormhole geometries.

Let us adopt the generic spherically symmetric metric ansatz

\displaystyle\bm{g}=-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(u,v)}}{2\sqrt{A(u,v)}}(\bm{d}u\otimes\bm{d}v+\bm{d}v\otimes\bm{d}u)+uvA(u,v)\bm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(2.1)

Here,
u
and
v
denote the null coordinates, and
\bm{d}\Omega^{2}
denotes the metric of the unit two-sphere.
We assume
\bm{\partial}_{u}
and
\bm{\partial}_{v}
point future direction without loss of generality.

The energy–momentum tensor is taken to be that of two null dust streams
propagating along
\partial_{u}
and
\partial_{v}
directions:

\displaystyle\bm{T}=T_{uu}(u,v)\bm{d}u\otimes\bm{d}u+T_{vv}(u,v)\bm{d}v\otimes\bm{d}v.

(2.2)

Solving the conservation equations
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{uu}(u,v)=\frac{1}{8\pi G}\frac{\tau_{u}(u)}{uvA(u,v)},\qquad T_{vv}(u,v)=\frac{1}{8\pi G}\frac{\tau_{v}(v)}{uvA(u,v)}.

(2.3)

Here, we have introduced the arbitrary functions
\tau_{u}(u)
and
\tau_{v}(v)
, which depend only on
u
and
v
, respectively.

The Einstein Equation
G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}
reduces to two coupled wave equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\quantity(uvA(u,v))

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(u,v)}}{2\sqrt{A(u,v)}},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\partial_{v}B(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(u,v)}}{uvA^{\frac{3}{2}}},

(2.5)

together with

\displaystyle\tau_{u}(u)=\quantity(\frac{1}{2u}+ \partial_{u}B)\partial_{u}(uvA)-\partial^{2}_{u}(uvA),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\tau_{v}(v)=\quantity(\frac{1}{2v}+ \partial_{v}B)\partial_{v}(uvA)-\partial^{2}_{v}(uvA).

(2.7)

The consistency conditions
\partial_{v}\tau_{u}=0
and
\partial_{u}\tau_{v}=0
follow automatically from Eqs. (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).
Thus, for any functions
A
and
B
satisfying the wave equations, the metric (
2.1
) solves the Einstein equations with null dust, with
\tau_{u}
and
\tau_{v}
determined by Eqs. (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).

By solving Eq. (
2.4
) for
B
, we obtain

\displaystyle B(u,v)=\log\left(-2\sqrt{A(u,v)}\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\quantity(uvA)\right),

(2.8)

and substituting this into Eq. (
2.5
) yields a closed equation for
A
,

\displaystyle uvA\quantity[\qty(\partial_{u}\partial_{v}(uvA))^3-uvA\partial^2_{u}\partial_{v}(uvA)\partial_{u}\partial^2_{v}(uvA) + uvA\partial_{u}\partial_{v}(uvA)\partial^2_{u}\partial^2_{v}(uvA)]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}(uvA)\partial_{v}(uvA)\quantity(\partial_{u}\partial_{v}(uvA))^{2}=0.

(2.9)

After eliminating
B
, the metric can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{g}=\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\left(uvA(u,v)\right)(\bm{d}u\otimes\bm{d}v+\bm{d}v\otimes\bm{d}u)+uvA(u,v)\bm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(2.10)

Hence, any solution of Eq. (
2.9
) generates an exact spherically symmetric solution of the Einstein equations with colliding null dust.

2.2
Equations of motion with self-similarity

In the previous subsection, we discussed a general class of spherically symmetric solutions sourced by colliding null dust, which is parametrized by a function
A(u,v)
.
However, the partial differential equation (
2.9
) is highly nonlinear, making the analysis difficult.
To proceed, we now impose an additional simplifying symmetry: self-similarity.
Specifically, we assume the existence of a homothetic Killing vector

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=\xi^{u}(u,v)\bm{\partial}_{u}+\xi^{v}(u,v)\bm{\partial}_{v},

(2.11)

which satisfies the homothetic Killing equation

\displaystyle(\mathsterling_{\bm{\xi}}\bm{g})_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}=2g_{\mu\nu}.

(2.12)

We further assume that
\bm{\xi}
is a future directed timelike vector.
From the equation,
\xi^{u}
and
\xi^{v}
must depend only on
u
and
v
, respectively.
Since
\bm{\xi}
is assumed to be timelike, both
\xi^{u}
and
\xi^{v}
are non-vanishing and, therefore, we can choose the coordinates so that

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=u\bm{\partial}_{u}+v\bm{\partial}_{v}.

(2.13)

We note that
\bm{\xi}
is timelike and future directed only when
u>0,v>0
in these coordinates and we will focus on this case in the following.
We note that at this stage, we still have a degrees of freedom for the choice of
u,v
coordinates up to rescaling.
Under this assumption, the remaining components of the homothetic Killing equation are solved, showing that
A
and
B
depend only on the ratio of
u
and
v
.

Let us introduce new coordinates
(t,l)
by

\displaystyle uv=L^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2t}\qquad\frac{v}{u}=\mathrm{e}^{2l},

(2.14)

where
L
is a constant parameter with the dimension of the length.
In these new coordinates, the homothetic Killing vector is expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=\bm{\partial}_{t},

(2.15)

which implies that the geometry is self-similar in the “time” direction.
Indeed, the metric takes the conformal static form

\displaystyle\bm{g}=\mathrm{e}^{2t}L^{2}\quantity[
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{\sqrt{A(l)}} \left( - \boldsymbol{d}t \otimes\boldsymbol{d}t + \boldsymbol{d}l \otimes\boldsymbol{d}l \right)
+ A(l) \boldsymbol{d}\Omega^2],

(2.16)

with the areal radius being written as
r=\mathrm{e}^{t}L\sqrt{A(l)}
.
In these coordinates, the coupled wave equations (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) reduce to

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}(l)-4A(l)-2\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{\sqrt{A(l)}}=0,

(2.17)

\displaystyle B^{\prime\prime}(l)+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{A(l)^{3/2}}=0,

(2.18)

and the energy-momentum tensor can be expressed as

\displaystyle 8\pi G\bm{T}=\frac{\tau_{+}(l)}{A(l)}(\bm{d}t\otimes\bm{d}t+\bm{d}l\otimes\bm{d}l)+\frac{\tau_{-}(l)}{A(l)}(\bm{d}t\otimes\bm{d}l+\bm{d}l\otimes\bm{d}t),

(2.19)

with

\displaystyle\tau_{+}(l)

\displaystyle:=\tau_{v}\mathrm{e}^{2l}+\tau_{u}\mathrm{e}^{-2l}=A(l)+\frac{1}{2}\quantity(A'(l)B'(l) - A''(l)),

(2.20)

\displaystyle\tau_{-}(l)

\displaystyle:=\tau_{v}\mathrm{e}^{2l}-\tau_{u}\mathrm{e}^{-2l}=A(l)B^{\prime}(l)-\frac{1}{2}A^{\prime}(l).

(2.21)

By substituting Eqs. (
2.17
) and (
2.18
) into Eqs. (
2.20
) and (
2.21
), we can see that
\tau_{u}
and
\tau_{v}
are constant.
Moreover, by using the freedom of the constant scaling of the coordinates
u
and
v
,
which preserving the expression (
2.13
), we can normalize the value of
\tau_{u}
and
\tau_{v}
arbitrarily.
In this paper,
we shall fix
\tau_{u}=\tau_{v}=-\frac{1}{4}
, assuming that both
\tau_{u}
and
\tau_{v}
are negative.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{+}(l)=-\frac{1}{2}\cosh(2 l),\quad\tau_{-}(l)=-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2 l).

(2.22)

We note the negative values of
\tau_{u}
and
\tau_{v}
imply a violation of the null energy condition.
We also note that fixing the scalings of the coordinates
u
and
v
removes the residual rescaling freedom, thereby rendering the normalizations (or origins) of
t
and
l
physically meaningful.

To summarize, with the self-similarity, the components of the Einstein equation reduce to

\displaystyle B^{\prime\prime}(l)+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{A(l)^{3/2}}=0,

(2.23)

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}(l)-4A(l)-2\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{\sqrt{A(l)}}=0,

(2.24)

\displaystyle 2\tau_{+}(l)=2A(l)+B^{\prime}(l)A^{\prime}(l)-A^{\prime\prime}(l)=-\cosh(2 l),

(2.25)

\displaystyle 2\tau_{-}(l)=2A(l)B^{\prime}(l)-A^{\prime}(l)=-\sinh(2 l).

(2.26)

In particular, the independent components can be written as follows:

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}(l)-2A(l)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{A^{\prime}(l)^{2}}{A(l)}-\frac{A^{\prime}(l)}{A(l)}\tau_{-}(l)+2\tau_{+}(l)=0,

(2.27)

\displaystyle B(l)=\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{A(l)}}{2}\left(A^{\prime\prime}(l)-4A(l)\right)\right).

(2.28)

Using these equations (
2.27
), (
2.28
), and the derivatives, we can compute the curvature invariants as

\displaystyle R=0,

(2.29)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\mathrm{e}^{-4t}}{L^{4}\quantity[2A(\cosh(2l) - 2A
) + A'(l)(A'(l) - \sinh(2l))]^{2}},

(2.30)

\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=\frac{12\mathrm{e}^{-4t}(2A\cosh(2l)-A^{\prime}(l)\sinh(2l))^{2}}{L^{4}A^{2}\quantity[2A(\cosh(2l) - 2A)
+ A'(l)(A'(l) - \sinh(2l))]^{2}}.

(2.31)

2.3
Property of solutions

In the previous subsection, under the homothetic symmetry, we have obtained the reduced ordinary differential equations.
In the following, we will analyze their solutions to determine whether they describe wormhole geometries.
For this purpose, we first establish throat conditions and then study asymptotics.

2.3.1
Wormhole throat conditions

In order to examine the geometry, we first discuss the structure of a wormhole.
For a spacetime to be qualified as a wormhole, it must possess a throat.
However, in non-static spacetimes, the definition of a wormhole throat is not unique, and several proposals can be found in the literature (see Refs.
[
21
,
17
,
26
,
34
]
and also App.
A
).
In the present self-similar setting, a natural prescription would be to define the throat as the surface of minimal areal radius on a constant-
t
slice.
This choice is consistent with the homothetic symmetry, which selects constant-
t
hypersurfaces as natural foliations.
Accordingly, we impose condition
A^{\prime}(l_{\rm{th}})=0
at the throat
l=l_{\rm{th}}
.
In addition, we require the flare-out condition
A^{\prime\prime}(l_{\rm{th}})>0
, which means that the surface
l=l_{\rm{th}}
is not only extremal, but also minimal.
We note that the throat determined by this condition is compatible with the definition of Ref.
[
26
]
(See App.
A
).

In this paper, we focus on the solutions which are symmetric under the inversion of the radial coordinate
l\rightarrow-l
.
In this case, a throat must be located at
l=0
.
Motivated by the above discussion, we shall impose the one parameter family of the initial conditions for Eq. (
2.27
) which, we expect, corresponds to the symmetric wormhole solution,

\displaystyle A(0)=A_{\rm{th}}>0,\qquad A^{\prime}(0)=0.

(2.32)

By evaluating Eq. (
2.27
) at
l=0
with the conditions (
2.32
), we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}(0)=2A_{\rm{th}}+1>0,

(2.33)

which means that the flare-out condition is automatically satisfied.
These conditions also mean that the function can be approximated as a quadratic function

\displaystyle A(l)\simeq A_{\rm{th}}+\quantity(A_{\rm{th}} + \frac{1}{2})l^{2}

(2.34)

in the neighborhood of
l=0
.
From Eq. (
2.28
), we also find that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(0)}}{\sqrt{A(0)}}=\frac{1}{2}-A_{\rm{th}}.

(2.35)

Thus, by requiring that
g_{tt}<0
, that is, the vector
\bm{\partial}_{t}
is timelike, the value of the parameter
A_{\rm{th}}
is restricted to

\displaystyle 0<A_{\rm{th}}<\frac{1}{2}.

(2.36)

2.3.2
Asymptotics and singularities

In the previous subsection, we derived the form of the metric (
2.16
) and the equations of motion (
2.27
) and (
2.28
).
As discussed there, Eq. (
2.27
) is the only equation that needs to be solved.
However, it is difficult to obtain the general solution of the equation.
Therefore, in the next subsection we will solve Eq. (
2.27
) numerically.
Here, we examine the asymptotic behavior of the solution in the large-
|l|
region.

One can show that

\displaystyle A_{0}:=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\mathrm{e}^{2l}-\frac{1}{4\gamma-8}\mathrm{e}^{-2l}

(2.37)

is an exact solution of Eq. (
2.27
).
Here,
\gamma
is a constant.
Although
A_{0}(l)
is an exact solution, we will mainly use it as an approximate solution in the large-
l
region, where the first term dominates.
Therefore, we focus on
\gamma>0
to ensure
A(l)>0
asymptotically.
As we will see below, the numerical solution suggests that
\gamma>1

as long as
A_{\rm{th}}
satisfies (
2.36
).
The metric derived from the solution (
2.37
) degenerates since

\displaystyle\exp\quantity(B_{0}(l)):=\sqrt{A_{0}}\left(A_{0}^{\prime\prime}-4A_{0}\right)=0,

(2.38)

where Eq. (
2.28
) has been used.
Thus,
A_{0}
is not viable as a physical solution.
Nevertheless, we may obtain physical geometries by considering perturbations around this degenerate solution.
Let us consider perturbations about (
2.37
) as follows:

\displaystyle A(\epsilon;l)=A_{0}(l)+\epsilon\delta A(l)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(2.39)

assuming
\epsilon\delta A/A_{0}\ll 1
.
Hereafter, we focus on terms up to
O(\epsilon)

\epsilon\delta A

Then, the linearized equation of motion for

\displaystyle\delta A^{\prime\prime}+\left(\gamma-2+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-(\gamma-2)\mathrm{e}^{4l}}\right)\delta A^{\prime}+\left(-2\gamma+\frac{4(\gamma-1)\gamma}{\gamma-(\gamma-2)\mathrm{e}^{4l}}\right)\delta A=0.

(2.40)

Let us evaluate solution of this perturbation equation in the large-

In the regime where

the equation as

\displaystyle\delta A^{\prime\prime}+\left(\gamma-2-\frac{2\gamma}{(\gamma-2)}\mathrm{e}^{-4l}\right)\delta A^{\prime}+\left(-2\gamma-\frac{4(\gamma-1)\gamma}{(\gamma-2)}\mathrm{e}^{-4l}\right)\delta A=0,

(2.41)

which has the solution

\displaystyle\delta A

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma l}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma}{2\gamma-4}\mathrm{e}^{-4l}}\quantity(C_{1}U\qty(\frac{2+\gamma}{2}, \frac{6+\gamma}{4}, \frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma-2)}\mathrm{e}^{-4l

(2.42)

Here,

and
L_{a}^{b}(c)
denote the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind and the generalized Laguerre function, respectively.

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants.
We see that, in the limit
l\rightarrow\infty
, the first term behaves as
\mathrm{e}^{2l}
, which is the same

A_{0}
.
Therefore, its contribution can be removed by a redefinition of
\gamma
, and we shall take
C_{1}
=0.
We also find that the second term can be approximated as

L_{-\frac{2+\gamma}{2}}^{\frac{2+\gamma}{4}}\quantity(\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma-2)}\mathrm{e}^{-4l

Then, for
\gamma\neq 2

\displaystyle A(l)=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\mathrm{e}^{2l}-\frac{1}{4\gamma-8}\mathrm{e}^{-2l}+\frac{C}{\gamma-2}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma l}\quantity(1 +O\qty(\mathrm{e}^{-4l})),

(2.43)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}=\frac{\gamma+2}{4\sqrt{\gamma}}C\mathrm{e}^{(1-\gamma)l}\quantity(1 +O\qty(\mathrm{e}^{-4l})),

(2.44)

with

2.3.3
Global structure of the spacetime

In the previous subsubsection, we derived the asymptotic form of the metric.
In order to discuss the structure of this spacetime, we evaluate null geodesics using the asymptotic solution.
Let us consider the future directed outgoing radial null curve of constant
t-l
whose tangent vector is

\displaystyle\bm{k}=\frac{1}{-g_{tt}}\quantity(\boldsymbol{\partial}_{t} + \boldsymbol{\partial}_{l}).

(2.45)

We see that this vector satisfies the affine parametrized geodesic equation
\nabla_{\bm{k}}\bm{k}=0
.
The affine parameter
\lambda
of this geodesic satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{dl}{d\lambda}=k^{l}=\frac{1}{-g_{tt}}.

(2.46)

Therefore, in the limit
l\rightarrow\infty

t-l
being fixed, the affine parameter
\lambda

\displaystyle\lambda=\int^{l}(-g_{tt})|_{t=l^{\prime}+\text{const.}}dl^{\prime}\propto-C\mathrm{e}^{(2-\gamma)l}+\text{const.}

From this expression, for
\gamma<2

Therefore,

On the other hand, for
\gamma>2

To investigate the regularity for

In order to do that, we shall compute the components of the curvature tensor expanded in parallelly propagated null tetrad.
From the above computation, we see that

\displaystyle\bm{e}^{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\boldsymbol{d}t - \boldsymbol{d}l),

(2.49)

\displaystyle\bm{e}^{-}=\mathrm{e}^{2t}L^{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{B(l)}}{\sqrt{2A(l)}}\quantity(\boldsymbol{d}t +
\boldsymbol{d}l),

(2.50)

\displaystyle\bm{e}^{\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{t}L\sqrt{A(l)}\bm{d}\theta,

\displaystyle\bm{g}=-\quantity(\boldsymbol{e}^{+}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}^{-} + \boldsymbol{e}^{-}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}^{+})+\bm{e}^{\theta}\otimes\bm{e}^{\theta}+\bm{e}^{\phi}\otimes\bm{e}^{\phi}

\displaystyle R^{\mu\nu}e_{\mu}^{+}e_{\nu}^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{e}^{2(\gamma-2)l-4(t-l)},

(2.55)

\displaystyle R^{\mu\nu}e_{\mu}^{-}e_{\nu}^{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{e}^{-4l},

(2.56)

\displaystyle C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}e_{\mu}^{+}e_{\nu}^{-}e_{\rho}^{+}e_{\sigma}^{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{e}^{-2(t-l)}\mathrm{e}^{(\gamma-6)l},

\displaystyle C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}e_{\mu}^{\theta}e_{\nu}^{\phi}e_{\rho}^{\theta}e_{\sigma}^{\phi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{e}^{-2(t-l)}\mathrm{e}^{(\gamma-6)l},

6

In terms of the energy-momentum tensor, this singular component is written as

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}e_{\mu}^{+}e_{\nu}^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{e}^{2(\gamma-2)l-4(t-l)}\tau_{v}.

(2.60)

This implies that the existence of the pp-curvature singularity for the Ricci tensor in the future null region is due to the infinite flux of the negative energy in the

Similarly, we can show that the flux of the negative energy in the

Next, we shall evaluate the behavior of the null geodesic and the curvature scalar in the past direction.

From Eqs. (

3
Formation of wormhole

In Sec.
2
, we constructed the self-similar wormhole solution.
We now turn to the question of how such geometries can form dynamically.
To this end, we construct a composite spacetime by cutting and gluing together exact solutions of Einstein’s equations across null shells, generalizing the scenario proposed by Hayward and Koyama
[
16
,
25
]
.

3.1
Overall strategy

Our aim is to construct a dynamical scenario in which an initial
Schwarzschild black hole is converted into a self-similar wormhole
by irradiation of negative-energy flux. To achieve this, we patch
together three distinct exact solutions of Einstein’s equations:

1.

Schwarzschild region: the initial state of the black hole prior to the injection of the negative energy.
This serves as the starting point of the formation process.

2.

Negative-energy Vaidya regions: the intermediate states in which the ingoing and outgoing streams of null radiation carrying negative energy.
These regions mediate the transition between the Schwarzschild black hole and the
wormhole geometry.

3.

Self-similar wormhole region: the final state described by the solutions constructed in the previous section. This region contains the wormhole throat and its asymptotic domains.

The overall structure is illustrated schematically in Fig.
6
.
The spacetime is divided into patches separated by null hypersurfaces,
across which the geometries are matched using the Barrabès–Israel
junction formalism
[
2
]
.
For simplicity, we assume the junctions are left–right symmetric.
At each junction, thin null shells appear, whose
energy–momentum tensors encode the effect of the negative-energy flux of the shells.
In this model, considering continuity of injected energy, we assume that the shell has no pressure but only energy.

Figure 6:
The schematic picture of the wormhole formation model.
We start from a black hole, and go to a wormhole via Vaidya regions.
The regions are separated by the null shells without pressure, which are shown as purple lines and green arrows.

In the next subsection, we describe each building block in turn:
the Schwarzschild region, the Vaidya regions,
and the wormhole region.
Then, we apply the
junction conditions to derive the relations among the throat radius,
the injected flux, and the initial black-hole parameters, ensuring
consistency of the whole construction.

3.2
Geometries

3.2.1
Schwarzschild black hole

The vacuum solution of Einstein equation with static and spherically symmetry is known as Schwarzschild black hole:

\displaystyle\bm{g}

\displaystyle=-f_{B}(r)\bm{d}t\otimes\bm{d}t+\frac{1}{f_{B}(r)}\bm{d}r\otimes\bm{d}r+r^{2}\bm{d}\Omega^{2},

\displaystyle f_{B}(r)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2M}{r}.

(3.2)

For later calculation, we shall express the metric in the Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates.
If we consider the region which will be patched with the right Vaidya region, the spacetime can be written

\displaystyle\bm{g}

\displaystyle=-f_{B}\bm{d}v_{B}\otimes\bm{d}v_{B}+\bm{d}v_{B}\otimes\bm{d}r+\bm{d}r\otimes\bm{d}v_{B}+r^{2}\bm{d}\Omega^{2}.

\displaystyle\bm{T}=\frac{1}{4\pi r^{2}}\derivative{m(v_{V})}{v_{V}}\bm{d}v_{V}\otimes\bm{d}v_{V}.

\displaystyle\bm{g}

\displaystyle=-f_{R}\bm{d}v_{V}\otimes\bm{d}v_{V}+\bm{d}v_{V}\otimes\bm{d}r+\bm{d}r\otimes\bm{d}v_{V}+r^{2}\bm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(3.7)

For the left Vaidya region, glued with the wormhole region in

\displaystyle\bm{g}

\displaystyle=-f_{L}\bm{d}u_{V}\otimes\bm{d}u_{V}+\bm{d}u_{V}\otimes\bm{d}r+\bm{d}r\otimes\bm{d}u_{V}+r^{2}\bm{d}\Omega^{2},

The energy-momentum tensor is given by

\displaystyle\bm{T}=\frac{1}{4\pi r^{2}}\derivative{m(u_{V})}{u_{V}}\bm{d}u_{V}\otimes\bm{d}u_{V}

3.3
Junction conditions

We will describe the construction by matching the three building blocks introduced above across null hypersurfaces.
In this subsection, we review the Barrabès–Israel formalism
[
2

In order to express the junction conditions in terms of intrinsic quantities of the null hypersurfaces, we introduce some notations.
Consider patching two geometries across thin null shell

\Sigma

We shall express the position of the shell using the equation
\Phi(x^{\mu})=0

Let

Using this ambiguity, we shall fix the tetrad such that
\bm{e}_{(1)}=\bm{n}^{\sharp}

\bm{n}
is a null normal to the shell.
Here, the symbol

\bm{n}^{\sharp}=g^{\mu\nu}n_{\mu}\bm{\partial}_{\nu}=n^{\mu}\bm{\partial}_{\mu}

Analogously,

\bm{V}^{\flat}=g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}\bm{d}x^{\nu}=V_{\mu}\bm{d}x^{\mu}
for a given vector field
\bm{V}

We shall denote the rest of the tetrad as
\bm{e}_{(A)}

(A=2,3)

Then we introduce another null vector

\bm{n}^{\sharp}\cdot\bm{N}=-1

\bm{e}_{(A)}\cdot\bm{N}=0

(A=2,3)

Here, the dot denotes the inner product of two vectors with respect to the metric

Using this definition of

\displaystyle\bm{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\bm{d}\Phi,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{N}}\Phi.

(3.12)

Using these tetrads and vectors, the induced metric and the transverse extrinsic curvature of

\displaystyle h_{AB}:=g_{\mu\nu}e^{\mu}_{(A)}e^{\mu}_{(B)},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{ab}:=-N_{\mu}e^{\nu}_{(b)}\nabla_{\nu}e^{\mu}_{(a)}.

(3.14)

As a practical rule throughout this section, we will choose intrinsic coordinates

The spacetime under consideration is spherically symmetric, and we perform the patching along spherically symmetric null hypersurfaces.
The junction conditions for spherical null hypersurfaces are written as follows;
The first junction condition imposes the continuity of the induced metric

[h_{AB}]=0.

(3.15)

Here, we have introduced the expression for the difference of quantity

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}h^{AB}\quantity[\mathcal{R}_{AB}],\quad j^{A}=\frac{1}{8\pi}h^{AB}\quantity[\mathcal{R}_{1 B}],\quad P=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\quantity[\mathcal{R}_{1 1}].

4
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we explored a non-static extension of the Hayward-Koyama wormhole formation scenario
[
16
,
25
]
.
Our construction employed colliding streams of negative-energy null dust as the matter source and imposed self-similar symmetry to obtain a tractable class of solutions.
This approach allowed us to construct wormhole spacetimes with regular throats and embed them consistently into a dynamical model describing the transition from a black hole to a wormhole.
Under this symmetry, we solved the equations of motion numerically. Boundary conditions were imposed to enforce symmetry under inversion of the radial coordinate and to ensure that the areal radius attains a minimum at the symmetric point.

We found that the large-
|l|
asymptotics of the solutions are governed by a single parameter,
\gamma
.
For
\gamma>2
, pp-curvature singularity develops in the future null direction, associated with the divergence of the negative-energy flux (Eq. (
2.60
)).
When
\gamma>4
, additional scalar curvature singularities arise in the future null direction as well as in the spatial and past null regions, as seen from the expressions (
2.47
) and (
2.48
).
By contrast, for
\gamma<2
, the solutions avoid singularities at both spatial and future null infinity, while still retaining an unavoidable singularity in the past direction.
Moreover, larger throat radii naturally correspond to smaller values of
\gamma
(Fig.
5
), ensuring that spacetimes with sufficiently large throats approach future infinity smoothly.
Thus, the throat size serves as the key control parameter that determines whether the wormhole geometry remains regular or develops singularities.

These findings reveal a novel mechanism by which sufficiently large wormhole throats can evade singularities in the spatial and future null directions, in sharp contrast to previous static constructions.
At the same time, the persistence of an initial singularity underscores the limitations of self-similarity: the wormhole effectively emerges from an initial singularity.

Building on this, we presented a global formation model, schematically depicted in Fig.
6
.
By patching together Schwarzschild, Vaidya, and self-similar wormhole geometries across thin null shells, we constructed a consistent scenario in which an initial black hole is converted into a traversable wormhole via the injection of negative-energy flux.
The Barrabès–Israel formalism
[
2
]
provided the junction conditions, and their explicit evaluation showed how the throat radius is determined by the integrated mass loss during the Vaidya phase (Eq. (
3.71
)).
In particular, the vanishing-pressure condition on the shells yields a first-order ordinary differential equation for the Vaidya mass function, fully fixing the flux profile in terms of the wormhole data.
This establishes a concrete mechanism through which negative-energy radiation can dynamically generate a wormhole.
Moreover, when the throat size is sufficiently large, this process can be interpreted as dynamically resolving the initial black hole singularity through a thin shell of negative-energy null dust.

Several open issues remain. First, the physical mechanism underlying singularity avoidance for large throats deserves further investigation.
In particular, clarifying the role of negative-energy fluxes in shaping the asymptotic structure could provide crucial insights.
Second, the perturbative stability of these self-similar wormholes must be addressed to assess their viability as time-dependent solutions.
Finally, extensions beyond spherical symmetry or the inclusion of additional matter fields may uncover richer classes of singularity-avoiding wormholes.

Appendix A
Definitions of non-static wormholes

In non-static settings several inequivalent definitions of wormhole throats have been discussed in the literature.
In this appendix, let us review the definition by Maeda, Harada, and Carr
[
26
]
, which proposed a definition suitable for cosmological wormhole solutions, and apply to our problem.
Their motivation was that in spacetimes with an initial singularity there is, in general, no past null infinity and no trapping horizon.
As a result, earlier definitions based on null expansions, such as those by Hochberg–Visser
[
20
]
and Hayward
[
17
]
, fail to capture physically interesting wormhole geometries in cosmological scenarios.

The key idea of the definition in Ref.
[
26
]
is to identify the throat on a spacelike hypersurface as a two-sphere of nonvanishing minimal area.
Concretely, introducing a radial spacelike vector

\bm{s}
, the conditions are

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle=0,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle>0,

(A.2)

where
R
is the areal radius.
This criterion generalizes the static minimal-surface condition to time-dependent spacetimes, while avoiding the restriction to null hypersurfaces. A throat defined may coincide with a bifurcating trapping horizon (the “momentarily static” case), but more generally it can lie inside a trapped region without being associated with any trapping horizon.
In this sense, this definition encompasses
cosmological wormholes that would otherwise be excluded from other frameworks such as Refs.
[
20
,
17
]

We now apply this definition to our self-similar solutions.
For a spherically symmetric spacetime, it is natural to take
\bm{s}=\bm{\partial}_{l}
.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}LA^{\prime}(l)}{2\sqrt{A}},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}L}{8A^{3/2}}\quantity(4A\cosh(2l) - A'(l)\sinh(2l)).

(A.4)

Under the boundary conditions (
2.32
), these reduce in the
candidate throat
l=0
to

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle=0,

(A.5)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{s}}R

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}L}{2A_{\rm{th}}}>0.

(A.6)

showing that the throat is located at
l=0
irrespective of the
value of
t
.
Thus, the definition in Ref.
[
26
]
consistently identifies the wormhole throat in the solutions considered in this paper, and it is the definition we adopt throughout.
