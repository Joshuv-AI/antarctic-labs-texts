A Security Framework for Chemical Functions
Frederik Walter, Hrishi Narayanan, Jessica Bariffi, Anne Lüscher, Rawad Bitar, Robert Grass, Antonia Wachter-Zeh, Zohar Yakhini

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce chemical functions, a unified framework that models chemical systems as noisy challenge–response primitives, and formalize the associated chemical function infrastructure. Building on the theory of physical functions, we rigorously define robustness, unclonability, and unpredictability for chemical functions in both finite and asymptotic regimes, and specify security games that capture the adversary’s power and the security goals. We instantiate the framework with two existing DNA-based constructions (operable random DNA and Genomic Sequence Encryption) and derive quantitative bounds for robustness, unclonability, and unpredictability. Our analysis develops maximum-likelihood verification rules under sequencing noise and partial-edit models, and provides high-precision estimates based on binomial distributions to guide parameter selection. The framework, definitions, and analyses yield a reproducible methodology for designing chemically unclonable authentication mechanisms. We demonstrate applications to in-product authentication and to shared key generation using standard extraction techniques.

I
Introduction

Across supply chains, healthcare, and critical infrastructure, there is an ever-present need to bind trust to the physical world. While cryptography provides rigorous guarantees for digital artifacts, ensuring authenticity, integrity, and provenance of physical matter remains challenging. Modern taggants, labels, and device-bound fingerprints (e.g.,
puf
) offer valuable anchors but are typically confined to surfaces, specific components, or narrowly scoped measurement setups. This motivates security mechanisms whose guarantees arise from the behavior of chemical systems themselves—mechanisms that can be embedded throughout materials and verified via standard laboratory operations.

We advocate a unifying view based on chemical functions: mappings from chemical states (e.g., compositions, structures, or sequence pools) and admissible operations (e.g., amplification, separation, hybridization, or reaction protocols) to measurable outputs (e.g., sequencing reads, spectra, or amplification curves). In this view, the complexity originates in intrinsic physical, chemical, or biological processes. This generalizes hardware-centric notions such as
puf
to settings where the function is realized by chemistry, and where the verifier interacts by applying laboratory procedures under a specified protocol.
cuf
as first introduced in
[
1
]
arise as an important subclass: they are designed so that reproducing an equivalent chemical object is infeasible even for powerful adversaries with laboratory access.
We see the key difference between chemical functions and physical functions as the ability to be divided into smaller parts and distributed across space, whereas physical functions are usually bound to a specific device or component.

Despite rapid progress, prior works are often presented with domain-specific abstractions and incomparable metrics, making it difficult to assess fundamental trade-offs, reason about adversarial capabilities, or compose mechanisms. In particular, a lack of common terminology for (i) parties and roles, (ii) power of the adversary, (iii) security goals, and (iv) security notions (e.g., unclonability, unpredictability) hinders systematic analysis.

This paper introduces a framework for chemical functions that abstracts chemical mechanisms into analyzable components and aligns them with cryptographic methodology.
We formalize the components of chemical functions and the necessary infrastructure, adversary power, security goals, and specific properties of chemical functions.

Concretely, our contributions are:

•

We define the chemical function abstraction that generalizes device-focused notions (e.g.,
puf
) to chemistry-driven divisible settings and captures
cuf
as a special case.

•

We define key properties of chemical functions relevant to their practical application in security applications.

•

We formulate security goals and adversary capabilities for unclonability and unpredictability.

•

We instantiate the framework with chemical authentication schemes and
dna
(
dna
)-based mechanisms, including operable random sequence pools and genomic sequence encryption, showing how protocol choices and measurement noise map to security and reliability guarantees.

By decoupling chemical specifics from security reasoning, the framework enables principled design, analysis, and comparison of chemical functions. It clarifies how chemical operations act as cryptographic resources, how access policies constrain adversaries, and how verification can be made robust in realistic laboratory conditions.

In

II-A

Authentication Schemes

Authentication schemes are protocols used to verify the identity of a party or the integrity of data. That is, a receiver of a message is able to verify the sender’s identity (
authentication
) as well as the integrity of the message to make sure it has not been altered (
integrity
). We require that such a scheme cannot be broken using a polynomial-time attack with “relatively good probability” of success.
The polynomial running time, as well as the probability of success of the attack, are defined as functions of a previously fixed
security parameter

n
, which is publicly known, e.g., also to any adversary.
In other words, an authentication scheme is considered to be secure if no polynomial-time attack can break the system with non-negligible probability.

In the following, we focus on symmetric authentication schemes. That is, we assume that the communicating parties share common secret information, which is the
secret key
. One of the most prominent symmetric authentication schemes is a
mac
(
mac

To ensure that the received message identifies its sender and has not been tampered with, a tag is added to the message using the shared secret key. In a nutshell, a

is a protocol consisting of the following three main steps: key generation, tagging, and verification.

II-B

Physically Unclonable Functions

With the goal of ensuring hardware security, i.e., to protect a secret key in vulnerable hardware,
puf
provide a viable way to securely regenerate keys (see, for instance,
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
). The concept of a
puf
was first introduced by Pappu in
[
8
]
using the term Physical One-Way Function, and shortly thereafter in
[
9
]
with the term Physical Random Function. Concisely, a
puf
is a probabilistic function derived from the behavior of a complex physical object. Upon excitation by possible stimuli (called
challenges
), the function outputs the respective responses. In the last few years,
puf
were used in more advanced cryptographic protocols (e.g., authentication schemes) emerging as a novel cryptographic primitive
[
10
,
11
]
. In these cryptographic schemes, only the party currently holding the physical object is able to query for responses to chosen challenges. Due to the complexity of the
puf
’s challenge-response behavior, it is not feasible using any purely numerical method to predict a response to a randomly generated challenge without holding the
puf
at this exact moment of time.

Over the past few years, different notions of
puf
have been introduced capturing different security aspects. In this work, we adopt the framework presented in
[
12
]
which we later translate to chemical functions in Section
III
. There,
pf
(
pf
) are introduced, and the unclonability of the
pf
(
pf
) is defined as one possible security property.
We recap briefly the framework of
pf
presented in
[
12
]
.
A new instantiation of a
pf
can be obtained by the generation process
\mathsf{Gen}
that, upon a generation parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}
chosen by the manufacturer, outputs a physical component
\mathrm{p}
. We call the party triggering the generation process the
manufacturer
.

Once the physical component has been generated with respect to some desired properties chosen by the manufacturer, a
pf
can be defined. A
pf
(
pf
) consists of two main components: the physical component
\mathrm{p}
generated by
\mathsf{Gen}
and an evaluation procedure
\mathsf{Eval}
. Upon a challenge signal input
\tilde{c}
, the physical component
\mathrm{p}
responds with a response signal
\tilde{r}
. The algorithm
\mathsf{Eval}
takes as input a digital signal

, converts it to its physical representation
\tilde{c}
, and excites the physical component
\mathrm{p}
with
\tilde{c}
. Upon excitation with
\tilde{c}
,
\mathrm{p}
responds with the physical signal
\tilde{r}
as part of the evaluation stage. Eventually,
\mathsf{Eval}
outputs a digital representation

of
\tilde{r}
. In the following, denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{resp}}
the set of (digital) challenges and responses, respectively.

Definition 5
.

(Physical Function
[
12
, Definition 1]
)
Given a parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{PF}}
fully defining the algorithm
\mathsf{Eval}
. A

(

and the procedure

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cccl}\mathsf{PF}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\alpha_{\mathrm{PF}}}:&\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{resp}}\\
&c&\longmapsto&r:=\mathsf{Eval}_{\mathrm{p}}(\alpha_{\mathrm{PF}},c).\end{array}

A

With this framework of

Genomic Sequence Encryption
[
2
]

gse
(
gse

Thus, the secret key consists of a list of genomic coordinates in which edits can be introduced. Information is encoded using single-nucleotide edits across various positions of the genome. That is, information is encoded in binary where
0
represents an unedited reference base and
1
for a specific base edit. To edit the bases the authors use

From an adversarial point of view (i.e., without the knowledge of the mutated genome coordinates), all bases of the genome need to be scanned and analyzed for mutations, which are prone to sequencing errors and expensive to do in deep coverage.

Although
gse
was introduced as an encryption scheme, it can also be used for authentication. The authentication consists in determining the
0
/
1
sequence in a given ordered subset of any set of locations.
If that sequence matches the expected one, the authentication is successful.

II-C
2
Operable Random DNA
[
1
]

The concept of
ordna
(

Through the random features, it is impossible to reverse-engineer the input from the output, in analogy to a mathematical one-way function such as used in cryptographic hashes. The authors
[
1
]
use up to

The process is designed in such a way that the input cannot be read back from the output, and the random pool can be operated on but can not be copied by
pcr
. We will explain the details of this scheme during the course of this paper in the examples.

As the extraction algorithm is handling the noise in the response, it needs to be adapted to the noise level of the chemical function. For example, if different devices are used for measurement, the noise characteristics may differ significantly and the extraction algorithm needs to be adjusted accordingly. This helps to maintain a certain robustness of the system as defined in
Section

\displaystyle\longmapsto(z,h^{\prime})

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}
is the set of challenges,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{help}}
is the set of possible helper data and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{out}}
is the set of all possible outputs.

Due to the embedded extraction algorithm, the

Note that even though the extraction algorithm helps mitigate noise in the response, the output

Let us consider a chemical function based on a mixture of up to

IV
Security Properties for Chemical Functions

Now that we have defined the chemical functions framework, we can define properties that a chemical function should satisfy to be suitable for security applications. We adapt the properties defined for
puf
in
[
12
,
18
,
19
]
to our setting. The three main properties are:

i)

robustness,

ii)

unclonability, and

iii)

unpredictability.

In contrast to
[
12
,
18
]
, we will define these properties in a finite domain as well as in an asymptotic manner.

IV-A

Robustness

Similar to
puf
, chemical processes are noisy, meaning that the same challenge may not yield the exact same raw response on every evaluation. Therefore, an extraction algorithm
\mathsf{Extract}
, as in
Definition

7
, is employed to distill a stable output from noisy measurements.
Informally, robustness is the probability that repeated evaluations of the same
cf
on the same challenge agree after extraction. Formally, we follow
[
12
]
as the general setting is applicable here.

Definition 9

(Robustness)
.

Let
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
be a
cfs
with profile
\mathrm{p}
and let
x\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}
be a challenge. The
challenge robustness
of
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
with respect to
x
is defined as the probability

\displaystyle\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x)=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,h)=(z,h)|\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\emptyset)=(z,h)\right].

A first (setup) evaluation on
x
yields
(z,h)
; a later (reconstruction) evaluation on the same
x
using
h
should reproduce the same
z
. The quantity
\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x)
is exactly this agreement probability, absorbing stochastic variability in the chemistry and any randomized steps in extraction.

Example 4
.

Continuing the simplified example of fluorescence dyes from
Example

2
, let us assume that the measurement process has a chance of missing a color or falsely detecting an additional color with probability
p
. Thus, when measuring the same
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
multiple times with the same challenge
x
, the chance that any
k
of the
7
bits in the response
y
are flipped follows a binomial distribution with parameters
7
and
p
. Using a
(7,4,3)
Hamming code as extraction algorithm, up to
1
bit error can be corrected. The challenge’s robustness
\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x)
is then given as the probability of having at most
1
bit error in the response which can be calculated as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x)=\sum_{k=0}^{1}\binom{7}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{7-k}=(1-p)^{7}+7p(1-p)^{6}.

If we have a
cf
with multiple challenges, we also want to define robustness over a set of challenges. This can be done both for a single
cfs
instance as well as for a whole
cfi
when considering also all possible instantiations of the
cfs
.

Definition 10

(Robustness of a
cfs
)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with realizations
x\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}
.
The minimum and average robustness of a
cfs

\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
with respect to the set of challenges
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}
are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\min}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}}\left\{\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x)\right\},

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{avg}}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}}\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[X=x\right]\rho(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},x).

In this definition,
\rho_{\min}
provides a worst-case guarantee over the chosen challenge set, while
\rho_{\text{avg}}
summarizes a typical behavior under a specified challenge distribution. Which one is relevant depends on the specific
cfi
and the purpose it is used for.

Definition 11

(Robustness of a
cfi
)
.

Consider a
cfi

\mathsf{CFI}
and let
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
be a
cfs
instance of
\mathsf{CFI}
with profile
\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
using the same creation, evaluation and extraction parameters. With
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
we denote the set of all valid profiles as defined by the manufacturer. Given a random variable
P
with realizations
\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
, we define the minimum and average robustness of a
cfi
as

\displaystyle\rho_{\min}(\mathsf{CFI},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}}\left\{\rho_{\min}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})\right\},

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{avg}}(\mathsf{CFI},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}}\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[P=p\right]\rho_{\text{avg}}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}).

This lifts robustness from a single instance to an implementation family. The minimum over
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
captures the least reliable valid profile, whereas the average reflects expected behavior across the manufacturer’s admissible profiles.

With these notions in place, we next instantiate them on the

construction to quantify empirical reliability under realistic experimental noise and extraction parameters. We continue the example from
[

to illustrate the concept of robustness. For simplicity, we will only analyze the average robustness of the

IV-B

Unclonability

For electronic
puf
, the notion of unclonability is typically phrased as the infeasibility of fabricating a physical duplicate of the device. In the chemical setting, the relevant barrier instead concerns reconstructing by chemical or analytical means some profile that governs the
cf
responses under admissible challenges.
Thus, unclonability requires that no efficient attack, even after interacting with an authentic
cf
, can engineer a counterfeit
cf
whose challenge–response behavior is (beyond negligible probability) indistinguishable from that of the genuine pool.

Adhering to Kerckhoffs’ principle, we assume that manufacturing details as well as the generation, evaluation, and extraction parameters
\alpha_{G},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{X}
are public. An adversary’s objective is therefore to recover (or emulate) the profile
\mathrm{p}
associated with the authentic chemical function
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
, which plays an analogous role to a cryptographic key. It is realistic to assume that an adversary
\mathscr{A}
can obtain one or more instances of the protected product and elicit a polynomial number of function evaluations to inform a cloning attempt.
At a high level, three generic attack methods are available:

1.

Dilution:
In this approach, an adversary
\mathscr{A}
obtains samples of the original product containing the authentic
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
and reuses them.
Due to the nature of a
cfi
and the initial amplifying step,
\mathscr{A}
can reuse the original
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
in a diluted form to produce responses to new challenges. A potential countermeasure is to specify the expected concentration of DNA in the genuine product, making significant dilution detectable.

2.

Chemical Amplification:
In case the
cf
is made of
dna
, through techniques like
pcr
, an attacker could amplify the
cf
directly in a laboratory, exploiting
dna
’s natural ability to replicate without exposing its full content. This enables the creation of counterfeit
cf
via direct cloning of the original sequences.

3.

Measure and Recreate:
An adversary sequences
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
to obtain knowledge about the profile
\mathrm{p}
and then synthesizes
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
that provides the same challenge–response behaviour. Mitigation includes designing
cf
that are exceptionally difficult (or currently impractical) to sequence or that degrade under standard preparation protocols, hindering attempts to replicate or analyze them.

In this work, we focus on the third attack vector. The feasibility of chemical amplification depends on specific laboratory techniques and assumptions on their limitation.
In the case of
dna
-based chemical functions, any means to amplify them must be prevented for the distributed
cf
. This has to be done by chemical means and must rely on assumptions as in
Example

6
. Therefore, we do not include this attack in the mathematical analysis of this work and assume that a suitable process is in place.

Example 6
.

In
[
1
]
, the prevention of amplification works as follows: The constant handles are cleaved up to the point where the selection
pcr
primers bind and the 5’-overhangs are blunted. The proof that this prevents any further amplification is done in
[
1
]
and is beyond the scope of this work.

When there are trusted methods in place to prevent any chemical amplification and when dilution is assumed ineffective, an adversary is left with sequencing and synthesis attacks. The cost required for measurements and recreations can be determined analytically.
Instead of providing a detailed cost analysis, we focus on defining unclonability in a cryptographic sense. Therefore, we bound the effort of the adversary and the effort in evaluating the
cf
via the security parameter
n
as in
Section

II-A
.
In contrast to
[
18
,
12
]
, we analyze the chemical function in a finite domain as well as in an asymptotic regime, as it is relevant to see the unclonability of the scheme in an evolving context.
We define the adversarial model through a
security game
which specifies the adversary’s capabilities and the conditions for a successful
break
in the spirit of
[
3
]
.

Definition 12

(Chosen Challenge Attack Cloning Game)
.

For a
cfs

\mathsf{CFS}
and an adversary
\mathscr{A}
, the
chosen challenge attack cloning security game

\mathsf{Clone}_{\mathscr{A},\mathsf{CFS}}(n)
is defined as follows.

•

An authentic
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
with profile
\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
is generated via
\mathsf{Gen}(n)
.

•

The adversary
\mathscr{A}
is given oracle access to
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
of
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
and may adaptively query challenges
x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}
of their choice, receiving corresponding responses
y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}
and outputs
z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathscr{A}}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}
denote the set of queried challenges.

•

The adversary produces a chemical function
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
with profile
\mathrm{p}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
such that the corresponding
cfs

\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
utilizes the same extraction algorithm as
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
. The adversary succeeds, if for a randomly chosen fresh challenge
x\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\mathscr{A}}
it holds that
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}(x,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,h)
, where the helper data
h
is obtained through
(z,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\emptyset)
. In this case, the outcome of the game is defined as
1
.

Note that the entire extraction algorithm is public. Therefore, an adversary
\mathscr{A}
can also obtain the outputs
z_{i}
and their corresponding helper data
h_{i}
for each query by only having oracle access to
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
.
This game captures the intuitive goal of an adversary: to create a counterfeit item that behaves identically to an authentic one for a new, previously untested challenge. The oracle access models an adversary who can purchase an authentic product and subject it to a polynomial number of tests to learn its behavior. Remember that we restrict the adversary to polynomial effort in
n
. This also applies to the third step of producing the counterfeit
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
.

As the adversary might be able to analyze the
cf
with different methods, it is suitable to define another variant of the cloning game where the adversary is not restricted to querying challenges but can perform any evaluation of equivalent effort. We call this variant the
open cloning game
, defined as follows.

Definition 13

(Open Cloning Game)
.

For a
cfs

\mathsf{CFS}
and an adversary
\mathscr{A}
, the
open cloning security game

\mathsf{Clone}_{\mathscr{A},\mathsf{CFS}}(n)
is defined as follows.

•

An authentic
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
with profile
\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
is generated via
\mathsf{Gen}(n)
.

•

The adversary
\mathscr{A}
can perform
q
operations with the complexity of evaluating the
cf
on
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}
to obtain information.

•

The adversary produces a chemical function
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
with profile
\mathrm{p}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
such that the corresponding
cfs

\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}
utilizes the same extraction algorithm as
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
. The adversary succeeds, if for a randomly chosen fresh challenge
x\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\mathscr{A}}
it holds that
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}(x,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,h)
, where the helper data is obtained through
(x,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\emptyset)
. In this case, the outcome of the game is defined as
1
.

Now we can define unclonability in both an asymptotic and finite regime. To avoid unnecessary repetition, we only present the definitions for the chosen challenge attack cloning game of

. The definitions for the open cloning game of

are analogous.

IV-B
1
Finite Domain Unclonability

We now define unclonability for the
cfs
with profile
\mathrm{p}
in terms of an adversary’s probability in succeeding in the cloning game after querying at most
q
challenges. This captures the practical scenario where an adversary has limited interaction with the authentic system and must produce a convincing clone.

Definition 14

(Finite Domain Unclonability)
.

Let
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
be a
cfs
with profile
\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{pro}}
using the security parameter
n
.
Let an adversary
\mathscr{A}
play the cloning game of
Definition

12
on
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
, issuing at most
q
(adaptive) challenge queries and thus obtaining
q
responses.
We say that
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
is
(\tau,q)
-
unclonable under chosen challenge attack
if for all such adversaries it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Clone}_{\mathscr{A},\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}}(n)=1\right]\leq\tau.

Note that the cloning game gives an adversary
\mathscr{A}
the power of obtaining some challenge response pairs of
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
. The parameters
\tau
and
q
are related. For a fixed
\tau
, the manufacturer seeks the maximum number of queries
q
in order to guarantee that the success probability stays below the threshold
\tau
. Or vice versa, for a fixed number of queries
q
, we can determine the minimum
\tau
that can be achieved by a protocol used by the manufacturer.

Example 7
.

We continue the example from
[
1
]
to illustrate finite-domain unclonability.
A trivial extreme case is that an adversary can obtain responses for all challenges, i.e.,
q=|\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}|
. In this case, the adversary can fabricate a clone that reproduces the authentic
cf
on every challenge. The probability of success for
\mathscr{A}
is then bounded by the robustness of the
cfs
, i.e.,
\tau\leq\rho_{\min}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}})
. This holds for any
cfs
; in particular, any
\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}
is
(\rho_{\min}(\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}),|\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{chal}}|)
-unclonable.
To bound
\tau
, we formalize that adversarial success reduces to a Reed–Solomon decoding event. As the vectors are obtained through a Minhash algorithm, the expected distance is modeled as a binomial random variable with success parameter equal to the (weighted) Jaccard similarity of the underlying
k
-mer multisets. We upper-bound this similarity by exploiting permutation symmetry and collision counting over supports.

As mentioned, the MinHash algorithm used in
[
1
]
approximates the weighted Jaccard similarity
[
23
]
. Consequently, the probability that two vectors
y
and
y^{\prime}
collide under MinHash is an unbiased estimate of the (weighted) Jaccard similarity of the corresponding
k
-mer sets
[
23
]
. Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
denote the indicator vectors of the
k
-mer multisets obtained from
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}}(x)
and
\mathsf{CF}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}(x)
, respectively. Then we have
K,K^{\prime}\in\left[0,m\right]^{4^{k}}
with
L_{1}
-norm
||K^{\prime}||_{1}=||K||_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}K_{i}=m
and
\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}K^{\prime}_{i}=|K^{\prime}|=m
. Let us denote by
\mathrm{J_{w}}(K,K^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\max(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})}
their weighted Jaccard similarity. Define the random variable
W
as the number of coordinates where
y
and
y^{\prime}
agree, i.e.,
W=n-\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(y,y^{\prime})
. Then, we observe that

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Clone}_{\mathscr{A},\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}}(n)=1\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}(x,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,h)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[z^{\prime}=z\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(y^{\prime},y)\leq t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[W\geq n-t\right],

(1)

where
t
is the error-correction capability of the Reed–Solomon code of length
n
used in the extraction algorithm. By definition, the random variable
W
follows a binomial distribution with parameters
n=255
and
p_{e}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\mathrm{J_{w}}(K,K^{\prime})\right]
.
Since the
k
-mers are sampled from a DNA sequence according to an underlying distribution
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{4^{k}})
, we have that
K=(K_{1},\ldots,K_{4^{k}})
is a random variable following a multinomial distribution with parameters
m
and
\mathbf{p}
. The same holds for
K^{\prime}=(K^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,K^{\prime}_{4^{k}})
with distribution
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
. Assume
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is a permutation of
\mathbf{p}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=(p_{\pi(1)},\ldots,p_{\pi(4^{k})})
for some permutation
\pi\in S_{4^{k}}
.

A standard upper bound relates the weighted Jaccard similarity to the Dice–Sørensen coefficient. Thus,

\displaystyle p_{e}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\mathrm{J_{w}}(K,K^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}K_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}K_{i}^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})}{2m}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})\right].

It is shown in Appendix
A
that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})\right]
is Schur-concave in
\mathbf{p}
(see
Theorem

5
). Hence, the maximum is achieved when
\mathbf{p}
is uniformly distributed over its support
[
27
]
. Let
\tilde{K},\tilde{K^{\prime}}
denote the corresponding uniform random variables over a support of size
s
being the maximum number of unique
k
-mers that can appear in a challenge. Then, we have

\displaystyle p_{e}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(\tilde{K}_{i},\tilde{K^{\prime}}_{i})\right].

Under this simplification,
\min(\tilde{K}_{i},\tilde{K^{\prime}}_{i})
equals

IV-C

Unpredictability

Chemical functions can also serve as a source of shared secrets. Owing to the amplification step, multiple replicas of the same underlying randomness can be produced and distributed. Possessing two pools derived from the same
cf
enables two parties to establish a common secret: both issue an identical challenge and, using publicly shared helper data, extract the same secret. For this construction to be secure, the
cf
must be unpredictable.

Unpredictability differs from unclonability: an adversary should be unable to compute the correct response to a fresh challenge even without constructing a physical clone. For instance, a scheme that is straightforward to analyze but prohibitively expensive to replicate chemically might satisfy asymptotic unclonability while failing to provide unpredictability. We therefore introduce a distinct security game that models an adversary’s task of predicting the response to a new challenge after interacting with the authentic
cf
; it coincides with the cloning game, defined in
Definition

12
, except for the final step.

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}^{\prime}}(x,h)=\mathsf{CFS}_{\mathrm{p}}(x,h)\right]\notin\mathsf{negl}(n).

The probability is over a fresh challenge
x
with corresponding helper data obtained through

\mathrm{p}^{\prime}

V-A

Chemical Authentication Schemes

Chemical functions provide a foundation for designing authentication schemes. At a high level, an instance of a

\mathrm{p}

VI-B

Unclonability of GSE

Next, we look at the unclonability of the scheme.
We may consider the challenge to be a subset of some locations in the genome from all possible locations. Then, a new challenge could include locations that are already observed during previous challenges. Like this, the goal for the adversary becomes a coupon collector problem.

Now we consider the scenario in which
\mathscr{A}
performs full genome sequencing and can therefore obtain noisy information about all possible challenges.
Note that each challenge involves more than
1\,000
reads per position which increases the effort of
\mathscr{A}
significantly.
In this attack scenario, the adversary has different capabilities compared to what is defined in the cloning game in
Definition

12
. The open cloning game from
Definition

13
fits this scenario.
The final goal of the adversary is to obtain knowledge about the key-sites of the challenge, i.e., the positions in the genome that are edited.
To achieve this, we consider the case where an adversary
\mathscr{A}
can obtain
q
full genome reads. For simplicity of the analysis we assume that the ratio of
abe
is the same as that of
cbe
being
\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is edited}\mid\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\right]=p_{\text{edit}}=0.001
(i.e.,
0.1\%
) of all cells are edited. Let the error probability of sequencing be
p_{\text{seq-err}}
.

Given that a position
i
is a key-site,
the probability for
\mathscr{A}
to read an edit is given by
p_{\text{detect}}=p_{\text{edit}}(1-p_{\text{seq-err}})+(1-p_{\text{edit}})p_{\text{seq-err}}
. Hence,
\mathscr{A}
may read an edit at position
i
even though it was not edited. The probability to read
\ell
edits is thus computed using a binomial distribution, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\ell\text{ edits read}\mid\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{q}{\ell}p_{\text{detect}}^{\ell}(1-p_{\text{detect}})^{q-\ell}

\displaystyle\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\ell\text{ edits read}\mid\text{Position $i$ is not key-site}\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{q}{\ell}p_{\text{seq-err}}^{\ell}(1-p_{\text{seq-err}})^{q-\ell}

The best choice for
\mathscr{A}
is to employ a maximum likelihood decision for each position of the challenge. Thus, we define the following likelihood ratio

\displaystyle\Theta=\frac{\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\mid\ell\text{ edits read}\right]}{\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is not key-site}\mid\ell\text{ edits read}\right]}.

The maximum likelihood decision is to decide for
i
to be a key-site if
\Theta>1
and
i
not being a key-site otherwise. Using Bayes’ theorem we can rewrite
\Theta
as

\displaystyle\Theta=\frac{\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\ell\text{ edits read}\mid\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\right]\cdot\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\right]}{\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\ell\text{ edits read}\mid\text{Position $i$ is not key-site}\right]\cdot\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is not key-site}\right]}

As the adversary wants to minimize the errors in the challenges, the probability that a position
i
is a key-site relates to the challenge instead of to the whole genome. Therefore, we can assume
\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[\text{Position $i$ is key-site}\right]=0.5
and we get

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\text{detect}}^{\ell}(1-p_{\text{detect}})^{q-\ell}}{p_{\text{seq-err}}^{\ell}(1-p_{\text{seq-err}})^{q-\ell}}

For
\Theta=1
we get the following decision boundary:

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-p_{\text{seq-err}})-\log(1-p_{\text{detect}})}{\log(p_{\text{detect}})-\log(1-p_{\text{detect}})-\log(p_{\text{seq-err}})+\log(1-p_{\text{seq-err}})}.

A plot of the decision boundary is given in
Figure

cf

VII
Conclusions

This work introduced
chemical functions
(
cf
) and the associated
chemical function infrastructure
(
cfi
) alongside properties relevant for security applications as a unified framework for security mechanisms realized by chemistry. Building on the formalization of physically unclonable functions, we adapted concepts from
puf
to the chemical domain and defined the components of a
cfi
: generation and evaluation procedures, an extraction mechanism with helper data, and a verification interface operating on noisy challenge–response behavior. This abstraction connects laboratory procedures with cryptographic reasoning and enables meaningful comparisons across implementations.

Within this framework, we gave formal definitions of the central security properties—robustness, unclonability, and unpredictability—both in finite domains and asymptotically. We specified adversary capabilities and stated security goals via explicit security games that capture the adversary’s power and success criteria. This places chemically realized mechanisms on a footing comparable to established cryptographic primitives while reflecting laboratory constraints. Instead of quantifying the cost of a specific attack on the scheme, we focused on defining the capabilities of the adversary in the security games and relating the effort required to break the scheme to the effort of evaluating the scheme via a security parameter
n
.

We instantiated the framework with
dna
-based mechanisms, including operable random
dna
(
ordna
) and Genomic Sequence Encryption (
gse
). These case studies show how the
cfi
components and security games apply in practice, how chemical design and measurement models translate into quantitative guarantees for robustness and unclonability, and how standard extraction techniques yield reproducible verification. This led to the conclusion that
dna
-based schemes might be suitable for enabling security applications. Beyond authentication of materials, we outlined how
cf
enable key-generation workflows, including distributed settings where multiple near-identical instances reconstruct the same secret from noisy measurements.

By abstracting chemical mechanisms while retaining measurable interfaces, the framework provides a principled basis for designing, analyzing, and comparing chemically grounded authentication and key-generation schemes. We expect it to support a transition from ad hoc designs toward interoperable, quantitatively analyzable, and practically deployable systems.

Appendix A
Proof of Schur-Concavity of Expected Weighted Jaccard Similarity

Theorem 5

(Schur-concavity)
.

Let
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
denote the counts of the
i
-th
k
-mer in two profiles of length
s
drawn i.i.d. from probability vectors
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
, respectively, where
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is a permutation of
\mathbf{p}
.
Define

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min\big(X_{i},Y_{i}\big)\Bigg],

where
S_{4^{k}}
is the symmetric group on
4^{k}
elements and the expectation is over a uniformly random permutation
\pi
. Then
F(\mathbf{p})
is Schur-concave.

Proof.

To establish Schur-concavity of
F(\mathbf{p})
with respect to the probability vector
\mathbf{p}
over
k
-mers, it suffices to prove symmetry and concavity.

Symmetry

Let
\sigma
be any permutation of the index set
\{1,2,\ldots,4^{k}\}
. Consider
\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}=(p_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,p_{\sigma(4^{k})})
and write

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p}_{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min\big(X_{i}^{\sigma},Y_{i}^{\sigma}\big)\right],

where
X^{\sigma}
and
Y^{\sigma}
are the multinomial count vectors obtained from
\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}
and
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}_{\sigma}
, respectively. Since
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is a permutation of
\mathbf{p}
, we have
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}_{\sigma}=(\mathbf{p}_{\sigma})_{\pi}=\mathbf{p}_{\sigma\pi}
.

The probability of observing a specific count vector
\mathbf{x}^{\sigma}=(x_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,x_{4^{k}}^{\sigma})
under
\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{{Pr}}\left[X^{\sigma}=\mathbf{x}^{\sigma}\right]=\frac{s!}{x_{1}^{\sigma}!x_{2}^{\sigma}!\ldots x_{4^{k}}^{\sigma}!}\prod_{i=1}^{4^{k}}p_{\sigma(i)}^{x_{i}^{\sigma}}

With the change of variables
x_{i}=x_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}^{\sigma}
for all
i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\big(p_{\sigma(i)}\big)^{x_{\sigma(i)}}=\prod_{i=1}^{4^{k}}p_{i}^{\,x_{i}^{\sigma}}.

Hence
X^{\sigma}
has the same distribution as
X
; the same reasoning applies to
Y^{\sigma}
. The summand
\sum_{i}\min(X_{i},Y_{i})
is invariant under index relabeling, and since
\{\sigma\pi:\pi\in S_{4^{k}}\}=S_{4^{k}}
, the expectation is unchanged. Therefore,
F(\mathbf{p}_{\sigma})=F(\mathbf{p})
, i.e.,
F
is symmetric.

Concavity

To show concavity, we must prove that for any probability vectors
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
over
k
-mers and any
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle F(\lambda\mathbf{p}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{q})\;\geq\;\lambda F(\mathbf{p})+(1-\lambda)F(\mathbf{q}).

For a fixed permutation
\pi
, define
F_{\pi}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min(X_{i},Y_{i})
. Then

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}\big[F_{\pi}(\mathbf{p})\big]=\frac{1}{(4^{k})!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{4^{k}}}F_{\pi}(\mathbf{p}).

Hence, it is enough to show that
F_{\pi}(\mathbf{p})
is concave for each fixed
\pi
, since a positive linear combination of concave functions is concave.

Let
X^{(\mathbf{p})}
and
X^{(\mathbf{q})}
be the multinomial count vectors corresponding to
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
, respectively, and let
X^{\mathrm{comb}}
correspond to
\lambda\mathbf{p}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{q}
. Define
Y^{(\mathbf{p})}
,
Y^{(\mathbf{q})}
, and
Y^{\mathrm{comb}}
analogously under the permuted probabilities.

Observe that
X^{\mathrm{comb}}
can be realized by a per-trial mixture: for each of the
s
draws, with probability
\lambda
the draw is from
\mathbf{p}
and with probability
1-\lambda
it is from
\mathbf{q}
. A similar construction holds for
Y^{\mathrm{comb}}
. Using the concavity of the function
\min(a,b)
and summing over
i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min\big(X_{i}^{\mathrm{comb}},Y_{i}^{\mathrm{comb}}\big)\;\geq\;\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min\big(X_{i}^{(\mathbf{p})},Y_{i}^{(\mathbf{p})}\big)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{i=1}^{4^{k}}\min\big(X_{i}^{(\mathbf{q})},Y_{i}^{(\mathbf{q})}\big).

Taking expectations preserves the inequality and yields the desired concavity for each fixed
\pi
, and hence for
F
.
