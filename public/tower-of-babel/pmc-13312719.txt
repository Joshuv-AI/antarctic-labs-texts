Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera, Noctuidae) as a reservoir of potential polystyrene-degrading fungi
Authors: Gabrieli Seiscentos Cardenas, Eduardo Pereira de Souza, Yan Eiji Matuhara, Célio Dias Santos-Júnior, Thais Fernanda-Carlos, Milene Ferro, Silvia Helena Prado Bettini, Flávio Henrique-Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA substantial portion of plastic waste is composed of polystyrene (PS). The polymer’s end-of-life disposal is a concern due to its recalcitrant structure, which makes biodegradation difficult. Studies have shown that certain insect larvae are capable of ingesting, degrading, and mineralizing this material. This process is actively mediated by the intestinal larval microbiota, which comprises bacteria and fungi. The role of fungi is scarcely investigated, despite the extensive research conducted on the involvement of bacteria in the process.ResultsThe cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) was fed with expanded polystyrene (EPS), and the changes in the gut mycobiota were investigated. The ITS metabarcoding revealed that the yeast Diutina sp. was the most representative taxon in the samples, surpassing other taxa. Conversely, the feeding with EPS resulted in a decrease in the presence of Diutina sp. Additionally, four cultivable fungi (i.e., Aspergillus sp., Talaromyces sp., and two Penicillium species) isolated from the larvae’s intestine were evaluated for their capacity to degrade PS. The fungi were cultured in low-carbon PDA and then covered with a PS film for 60 days. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the fungi interacted with and altered the polymer’s surface, resulting in the formation of cavities within the PS film.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that Helicoverpa armigera has the potential to serve as a reservoir for polystyrene-degrading fungi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05021-8.

Background

The manufacturing of plastic products was crucial for the establishment of modern life. Plastic production has increased exponentially since the implementation of this technology, reaching 400.3 million tons in 2022 [1, 2]. Polystyrene (PS) accounts for 5.2% of this amount and has been widely used for various purposes, including food containers, packaging containers, and construction materials [2, 3]. Conversely, the end-of-life disposal of this polymer is a concern.

Currently, PS recycling is still scarce on a global scale [4], and the majority of the collected material is disposed of in landfills with solid waste [5]. Additionally, a substantial portion of PS debris is released into the environment [5–7], resulting in adverse impacts on various levels of biological complexity [8]. Consequently, the interest in polystyrene biodegradation has increased over the years.

Many studies have shown that the intestinal environment of insect larvae allows rapid degradation of plastic material. For instance, it was reported that larvae of Zophobas atratus are capable of ingesting plastic [9]. The larvae of the mealworm beetle (Tenebrio molitor) can break down PS, with evidence of its biodegradation and mineralization due to modifications in its physical and chemical properties [10]. Studies conducted with Galleria mellonella (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) demonstrated the larva’s ability to feed on biodegradable polyethylene (PE) and PS [11]. This fact was related to the intestinal microorganisms of these insects, such as the degradation of PE by the bacteria Enterobacter sp. [12–14].

Despite still being scarce, most studies focus on bacteria. However, the intestinal microbiota also includes unicellular and filamentous fungi, which may contribute to the degradation of plastics such as PS [15–17]. As important decomposers in all ecosystems, fungi colonize a wide variety of surfaces and can produce and secrete a broad arsenal of degrading enzymes. Some soil fungi, like Aspergillus sp., Penicillium sp., and Fusarium sp., have been shown to be able to grow on plastics like polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and PS that are found in the environment and eat away at their surfaces, leaving holes [18]. This suggests that these fungi could potentially serve as biodegradation agents. Also, other studies have shown that pure cultures of Cephalosporium sp. and Mucor sp. can break down PS [19].

The cotton bollworm, Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), is an important crop field insect pest. It is widely distributed, with occurrences reported in Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia, and recently in the Americas [20, 21]. This polyphagous insect has a larval stage divided into six instars. The larvae feed on various economically important crops, including cotton, maize, tomatoes, fruit trees, and ornamental plants. They consume leaves, stems, shoots, inflorescences, and fruits, damaging plants during both vegetative and reproductive stages [20, 21].

The gut microbiota is important for insects because it increases the availability of nutrients in food, facilitates the digestion of difficult food components, protects against parasites and pathogens, and supports the development and maintenance of the insect’s immune system [22, 23]. The gut microbiota of H. armigera makes it less likely to get baculovirus infections [24]; it regulates the signaling processes of ecdysone and juvenile hormones to facilitate the normal growth and development of larvae [25]; and it contains microorganisms that produce cellulolytic enzymes that could be employed to decompose biomass in the biofuel industry [26]. In 2024, Yu and collaborators [27] demonstrated that the yeast microbiota in the guts of H. armigera larvae varied under different diets, including apple, pear, tomato, plant-based, and artificial diets. Changes in the yeast population were observed as a result of the larvae transitioning from an artificial diet to a diet consisting of apples, pears, and tomatoes. This presumably facilitated the yeasts’ adaptation to their new host. Therefore, we anticipate that the intestinal microbiota of H. armigera will undergo a change in favor of fungi that have the potential to degrade PS when it is fed PS.

In this work, we identified the gut mycobiota of Helicoverpa armigera larvae, which may play a crucial role in the biodegradation process of polymers, particularly polystyrene. This interest stems from the observation that the larval stage of this species demonstrates the ability to bite and ingest pieces of this polymer. The composition of the fungal intestinal microbiota of larvae under different feeding conditions (EPS, conventional diet, or mixed diet) was elucidated through metabarcoding, using high-throughput sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region. We also isolated four cultivable filamentous fungi from the microbiota by plating them on solid media and identified each one using dideoxy sequencing of the ITS region. Grown in contact with PS films, these fungi were able to interact with and alter the surface of the polymer. By employing these methods, we enhanced our understanding of the H. armigera larvae intestinal mycobiota, which could potentially play a role in this insect’s degradation of polystyrene.

Methods

Helicoverpa armigera larvae maintenance

We purchased larvae of H. armigera (third instar) from Pragas.com (Piracicaba, SP, Brazil). The same company provided the artificial diet. Larvae were subdivided into 3 groups of 25 individuals each according to feeding: (1) artificial diet (100%), (2) artificial diet (50%) + expanded polystyrene (EPS) (50%), and (3) only EPS. We reared each insect individually in a Panasonic MLR-352 H incubator, maintaining a temperature of 25 °C, 60% humidity, and a 12:12 photoperiod for 7 days.

Plastic materials

Expanded polystyrene (13–16 kg/m3) was kindly provided by MundiEPS (Brodowsky, SP, Brazil). According to manufacturers, no additives were present in the material. The PS film was prepared according to Woo et al. [28]. Styrofoam was dissolved in 7 mL of xylene (0.03 g of polymer per mL of solvent) and then placed in 90 × 15 mm glass petri dishes, and dried for 24 h. The dried film was cut into 20 × 30 mm rectangles, which were then immersed in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 10 min, rinsed with sterilized deionized water, and dried at 50 °C for 4 h.

Gut dissection

Prior to dissection, the H. armigera caterpillars were surface-sterilized by submersion in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 3 min and rinsed in 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4). The heads of the caterpillars were guillotined using surgical scalpels, and the guts were extracted using fine-tipped forceps. The guts were removed from groups of five larvae that were reared in each feeding group. The guts from each group were pooled and placed into sterile 1.5 mL tubes containing 200 µL of TE (Tris 10 mM, EDTA 1 mM). The tubes were vortexed for 5 min to permit the separation of the microorganisms from the gut walls. Part of this solution was used for plating and fungal isolation, while the remainder was preserved in DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research) and stored at -20 °C for later DNA extraction. The pooling of insect guts was a recurring strategy in metabarcoding studies within the context of plastic biodegradation [17, 28, 29]. Besides increasing the initial sample input, it is possible to fix any effect related to differences in the amount of plastic ingestion.

DNA extraction and ITS metabarcoding sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from the pooled guts of each feeding group using the ZymoBIOMICS®-96 MagBead DNA Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Additionally, we used the ZymoBIOMICS® Microbial Community Standard (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) as a positive control for the extraction. The ITS sequencing was performed using the Quick-16 S NGS Library Prep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA), along with primers flanking the ITS1-ITS2 region. Zymo Research custom-designed this primer pair to enhance coverage and maintain high sensitivity, but their sequences are currently unavailable. Libraries were prepared using amplification reactions in a real-time PCR thermocycler to control the number of cycles and limit chimera formation. Amplicons were quantified by reading qPCR fluorescence and were grouped by molarity. The libraries were then filtered using the Select-a-Size DNA Clean & Concentrator (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and quantified using TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Additionally, each library preparation included a positive control using the ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard. After that, the libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform with a 10% PhiX spike-in.

Preprocessing and taxonomic classification

The FastQC program, version 0.11.5 (Babraham Bioinformatics; www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc), was used for quality control analysis to search for adapters and primers and to check the quality of each base in the sequence. We trimmed out low-quality raw reads (Phred Q score < 30) using SeqyClean v 1.10.09 [30] and carried out taxonomic identification with Kraken2 [31], available in the OmicsBox platform (BioBam Bioinformatics S.L., Valencia, Spain). Rarefaction curves were generated in the OmicsBox and represented as the number of expected bacterial genera (Y-axis) per number of reads (X-axis).

Filamentous fungi isolation and identification

Half of the pooled gut contents (100 µL samples) from each of the 3 dietary groups were individually plated on Potato Dextrose Agar (Himedia) and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (OXOID) using serial dilutions (10− 1 to 10− 4). The plates were then incubated at 28°C for 7–8 days. The filamentous fungi were isolated after they grew on serial dilution plates. The isolated fungi were grown individually in PDA plates until sporulation, and spores of each fungus were collected in 3 mL of MilliQ water. An amount of 100 µL, containing approximately 109 spores, was placed on sterilized cellophane discs (3 cm in diameter) on PDA medium. After 16 hours, the grown mycelia were collected and submitted to DNA extraction according to Cassago et al. (2002) [32]. Molecular genetic identification was based on DNA sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region with primers ITS1_f (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS4_r (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’) [33]. PCR amplifications were performed in 25 µL reactions containing 0.2 mM dNTPs (Invitrogen), reaction buffer (1X), reverse and forward primers (0.8 µM), 2.5 U of Taq High Fidelity Pol (Cellco Biotec, Brazil), and 25 ng of extracted DNA. Reactions were carried out with the following cycle: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. The amplicons were analyzed in a 1% agarose gel and subsequently purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey-Nagel) (Fig. S1). Following reaction clean-up, the amplicons were sequenced using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems). The reactions were resolved on an ABI 3500 DNA sequencer at the “Laboratório de Melhoramento de Plantas” of the Nuclear Energy Center in Agriculture (CENA) at the University of São Paulo (USP), Piracicaba (SP). We aligned and analyzed the forward and reverse sequences using the BioEdit software (https://bioedit.software.informer.com/) and the Multiple Alignment tool [34]. We submitted the consensus sequences to the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for comparison with the NCBI database. All obtained ITS sequences were deposited in NCBI GenBank.

Polystyrene biodegradation assays

About 5 × 108 spores (in 200 µL) of each isolated fungus were spread out on a solid PDA medium with low carbon levels (see supplementary material for composition). Two 20 × 30 mm rectangles of sterile 80 μm PS film were placed over the medium. The plates corresponding to each isolated fungus were incubated at 28 °C for 2 months or until evidence of fungal growth was observed. The control experiment consisted of the same system without fungal inoculation. The characterization of the PS film was conducted as described by Kim et al. [35]. Post-degradation modifications were examined on one film after washing with 2% SDS for 4 h, while mycelium growth and morphology were verified on the other. A control film without spore inoculation was used as a control. We analyzed the films using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA).

Data availability

The raw sequence data are available in the Short Read Archive (SRA) GenBank database: BioProject (PRJNA1188033), BioSample (SAMN44818488), and SRA (SRR31395623 - SRR31395625).

Results

A total of 2,441,412 sequences were obtained, with an average length of 243.4 base pairs (bps), and the sampling coverage was above 0.98, indicating that the sequencing was capable of detecting most of the reads. Characterization of fungal diversity

The use of markers to identify species through sequencing and their association with the abundance of reads enables the establishment of the environmental microbiota’s composition and diversity. Here, we constructed relative abundance barplots (Fig. 2) of the microbial composition for each of the feeding strategies. The guts of the EPS-fed larvae exhibited ten different fungal genera, while the insects fed conventional and mixed diets exhibited 8 and 6 genera, respectively. In all situations, there was an overrepresented taxon, the genus Diutina, which may overshadow the other organisms. Therefore, to better understand the community’s composition, we extracted Diutina sp. and constructed new barplots (Fig. 3).

Filamentous fungi isolation and identification

We observed fungal growth as early as five days after plating the intestinal content. Notably, fungal growth and diversity appeared to occur faster and at a higher level on SDA media than on PDA media, leading us to select SDA serial dilutions for further isolation. Furthermore, only one unicellular fungus was observed; the remaining isolates displayed filamentous fungal morphology. Using SDA, we isolated four filamentous fungi with distinct morphologies (Fig. 4), designated as follows: FFHA (Filamentous Fungi from Helicoverpa armigera) 01, FFHA02, FFHA03, and FFHA04.

Characterization of polystyrene film post-fungal activity

On the PS films that were not washed, hyphae and spores can be observed in the SEM images of films that were incubated with four different types of isolated fungi in a medium with a low carbon concentration. This shows that the organisms were able to grow and settle on the PS film (see Supplementary Figure S2). In the washed films, it is possible to observe the formation of trails and cavities, probably caused by the action of the fungus (Fig. 5), indicating degradation of the films after the incubation period, especially for the isolate FFHA02 (Talaromyces sp.). The control group does not exhibit the same pattern.

Discussion

Putative PS-degrading mycobiota diversity

This study evaluated the overall fungal diversity in the caterpillar guts of Helicoverpa armigera for the first time. We implemented three different diets to form three experimental groups: an artificial diet (100%), a mixed diet (50% EPS), and EPS blocks, resulting in distinct mycobiota compositions (Table S1). In all of them, the yeast genus Diutina was overrepresented, overshadowing the other taxa. Notably, the percentage of read counts for this genus reduced in strictly EPS-fed insects. Recent research has characterized the diversity of yeasts in the gut of H. armigera under seven distinct dietary conditions. Three groups of caterpillars originated from the wild, specifically from Urtica fissa, Helianthus annuus, or Zinnia elegans, while the others were laboratory-reared (fed artificial diets based on pear, tomato, apple, or cornmeal). The composition of the yeast communities varied significantly among the tested feedings, with different proportions and exclusive taxa [27]. The genera Wickerhamomyces, Pichia, and Malassezia, present in our samples, can be found in insects fed different sources of food. However, Diutina, the most representative genus in our samples, was not present in any of them. On the other hand, this yeast genus was found in the guts of other lepidopterans, such as Spodoptera frugiperda, fed on Brassica campestris [36]. This fact reveals the influence of the host diet on the community structure, which may have a crucial role in the caterpillar adaptation. It explains the presence of Diutina in our samples, as the collection place and feeding conditions were different.

The relative abundance bar plots revealed that the mycobiota in the EPS-fed H. armigera gut was significantly enriched compared to the control and mixed diets. The same effect was observed for the beetle Alphitobius diaperinus [16]. The presence of EPS in the gut could have posed a challenge for the Diutina establishment, which may have adapted to the artificial diet. This led to a reduction in the presence of this yeast and an increase in other fungal taxa, which was reflected in the read counts. To better comprehend fungal diversity among treatments, we constructed relative abundance bar plots excluding Diutina. Caterpillars fed EPS clearly had higher amounts of Aspergillus, Trematosphaeria, Metarhizium, and Talaromyces than those not fed EPS. In the mixed diet, we observed an increasing proportion of the yeasts Cryptococcus and Malassezia. Studies related to changes in the relative abundances of insect gut mycobiomes under PS diets are still scarce. However, they align with our findings. ITS sequencing revealed an overrepresentation of Aspergillus in the guts of lesser mealworms, Alphitobius diaperinus (Coleoptera) [16]. The coleopteran Tenebrio molitor also showed a relationship between Cryptococcus and PS diets [17].

Fungal PS-degrading abilities

In this work, the fungal diversity of Helicoverpa armigera gut fed on polystyrene was described, and four fungal species were isolated to assess their ability to degrade polystyrene. To our knowledge, this was the first time that fungi isolated from the gut microbiota of an insect were studied in the context of polystyrene biodegradation.

Fungi are a vast and varied kingdom of eukaryotic organisms, found in a wide range of habitats. Some of them have demonstrated remarkable adaptability to varying environmental conditions and possess the capacity to withstand various forms of contaminants. They possess an arsenal of enzymes that catalyze numerous different reactions, making them potential degraders of pollutants, such as petroleum-based polymers. Different intra- and extracellular mechanisms combine to make this possible. The intracellular enzymatic complex serves as an internal mechanism for detoxification, playing a crucial role in fungal adaptation [37–39]. The extracellular enzymatic machinery includes a powerful group of hydrolases that break down large polysaccharides in nature, as well as a complicated oxidative system that breaks down complex structures [40]. In this case, an esterase found in the mushroom Lantinus tigrinus is able to break down a polystyrene film and turn macromolecules into non-toxic molecules [41]. Additionally, a laccase present in the white-rot fungus Abortiporus biennis demonstrated a high depolymerization potential by oxidizing polystyrene powder [42]. Fungi also have machinery for interaction with hydrophobic substrates. The Hsb (hydrophobic surface-binding proteins) are secreted proteins that can interact with and adsorb to hydrophobic surfaces (such as plastics). They amplify the recruitment of enzymes that promote polymer depolymerization. When Lichtheimia ramosa was grown with Sulfonate Polystyrene as a sole carbon source, the Hsb proteins were overexpressed. Also, in silico analysis revealed potential interaction with styrene sulfonate [43, 44].

Isolated fungi and their PS-degrading abilities

Aspergillus sp.

The Aspergillus genus was overrepresented in EPS-fed caterpillars compared to the control. And we also isolated this fungus using culturing methods. It was cultivated in contact with polystyrene for 2 months, and some structural alterations were observed by SEM. It indicates that the fungi interact with the polymer and may use it as a carbon source. Some reports show that some Aspergillus strains may be involved in plastic degradation. Recently, Aspergillus versicolor was reported to be capable of degrading polystyrene microplastics [45]. Polyethylene could also be broken down by other species, such as an A. flavus type found in the guts of wax moth larvae (Galleria mellonella, Lepidoptera) [15, 46]. To date, no research has been conducted on Aspergillus enzymes that break down PS; however, they are believed to possess an arsenal that includes Hsbs and laccases [47, 48], for example.

Talaromyces sp.

We also correlated the Talaromyces species with EPS-fed caterpillars. The isolated strain demonstrated a notable action on the PS film. It was evident from SEM analysis that the microorganisms could form holes, where the hyphae appeared to penetrate. There are a few reports of plastic biodegradation by the Talaromyces species. For example, T. louisianensis was able to grow with polypropylene as its only carbon source. This is likely because it has high and long-lasting laccase and manganese peroxidase activities [49]. Mycodegradation was also observed when the Talaromyces liani strain JA2HS was cultured with high-density polyethylene. Physical and chemical alterations on the surface, in addition to mass reduction and CO2 evolution, were observed [50].

Penicillium sp.

Sanger sequencing of the ITS region identified the four fungi isolated by plating intestinal contents on SDA (Table 2). Metabarcoding sequencing, on the other hand, does not list all of these species as being part of H. armigera’s intestinal microbiota (Figs. 2 and 3). This may have occurred due to some bias during DNA extraction, as some fungi have thicker cell walls, making them more difficult to rupture during this process. Another possible explanation is that some genera are very competitive in vitro due to their high growth capacity compared to others [51]. Plating the samples in culture medium can have a selective effect, and it is possible that the species selected are from rare genera in the sample [52]. The NGS Illumina sequencing platform is widely used in metabarcoding studies on intestinal mycobiota; however, there are flaws in each step of the workflow that may also explain these results [53].

We isolated two strains of Penicillium from the gut of H. armigera, although the metabarcoding sequencing did not detect them in the gut of H. armigera. They were tested against PS films, and in both cases, it was evident that the formation of holes and other structural features reflected the action of the fungi hyphae. Strains from the same genus have already been related to PS degradation. A strain found in Antarctic soil samples could reduce the mass of PS films after they were treated with QUV, thereby accelerating the ageing process. However, films that had not undergone ageing did not exhibit this reduction [54]. 14C-labeled polystyrene films were submitted to biodegradation assays with P. variables. Changes in the film structure observed by MEV and the mineralization of 14C to CO2 revealed a potential for biodegradation, but with a low rate. However, PS ozonation pre-treatment enhanced the subsequent biodegradation of the polymer [55].

Study’s limitations

In this study, we focused on demonstrating the biodegradation of PS by four isolated fungi in a qualitative manner. However, we understand that further studies should be conducted to better characterize surface changes, such as Fourier-transform spectroscopy, including the incorporation of oxygen-related functional groups.

Similarly, we limited our analysis to demonstrating the differences in the composition of the fungal microbiota among the three groups of caterpillars across various feeding strategies. From this, we associated these results with the isolated fungi and identified candidates for biodegradation.

We also used caterpillars reared in a laboratory for many generations, fed a standard diet produced by the same company. The composition of this diet may contain inhibitors of certain intestinal microorganisms, which is a limiting factor for this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this work identified the gut mycobiota of Helicoverpa armigera larvae, which may play a role in the degradation of polystyrene. Additionally, we isolated and identified four cultivable filamentous fungi and confirmed, using scanning electron microscopy, their capacity to modify polystyrene. However, further studies, such as Fourier-transform spectroscopy, should be conducted to better characterize surface changes, including the incorporation of oxygen-related functional groups. Our results suggest potential future applications of these fungi and/or their genes or enzymes in the degradation process of polystyrene.
