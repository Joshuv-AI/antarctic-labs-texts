Modulation of redox state, antioxidant systems and photosynthetic capacity induction of osmotic stress tolerance in Crocus sativus L. through exogenous melatonin
Authors: Parvaneh Hemmati Hassan Gavyar, Hamzeh Amiri, Marino B Arnao, Seifollah Bahramikia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe potential of melatonin as a plant growth regulator biomolecule for mitigating the negative effects of environmental abiotic stresses has gained significant attention. This study aimed to investigate the influence of melatonin on various morpho-physiological and biochemical characteristics of saffron plants under osmotic stress conditions. Osmotic stress was simulated using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-6000 at a concentration of 30% (w/v). Melatonin was applied through foliar spray and root treatment at a concentration of 100 µM. The results revealed that osmotic stress caused an increase in oxidative injury indices, such as ROS (55.62%), MDA (50.43%), H2O2 (3.05%) and EL (16.40%), while decreasing growth parameters including FW (25.65%), DW (38.57%), SH (21.77%), RWC (36.3%), and photosynthetic traits such as chl a (55.43%), chl b (42.56%) and car (23.75%) content, Pn (64.92%), Fv/Fm ratio (1.275%), C (35.92%) and Ci (16.56%) concentration. In response to water stress, the concentration of compatible osmolytes and enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants were increased. Both foliar and root applications of melatonin resulted in a reduction in oxidative injury indices by enhancing the levels of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant compounds. Overall, the findings demonstrated that exogenous melatonin application had a positive impact on physiological indicators, including photosynthetic parameters and the antioxidant system, thereby enhancing plant resistance during osmotic stress. Additionally, the results indicated that foliar application of melatonin was more effective than root application in increasing plant resistance to osmotic stress.Keywords: Melatonin, Fv/Fm, Polyethylene glycol, Osmolytes, Antioxidant systemSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

In many parts of the world, various environmental stresses have restricted plant growth and productivity. These limitations, intensified by rapid climate change, are expected to increasingly impact global agriculture in the coming decades1–3.

Among abiotic stresses, drought is one of the most significant and serves as a major constraint on crop yield1,2. The adverse effects of drought stress typically appear as water deficiency, wilting, enhanced membrane permeability, growth inhibition, and reduced photosynthetic activity. These symptoms are often recognized as consequences of oxidative damage induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS)4. Under stress conditions, plants activate their defense mechanisms to cope with and adapt to the stress. However, prolonged stress exposure leads to excessive ROS accumulation and subsequent lipid peroxidation of cell membranes. This disrupts membrane function and diminishes photosynthesis, resulting in severe negative impacts on plant growth4–6. Furthermore, ROS contribute to reduced crop yields by inactivating enzymes, proteins, and nucleic acids, as well as suppressing photosynthetic processes7.

Currently, plant growth regulators particularly compounds like melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) have emerged as a valuable approach for enhancing plant resilience to stress8. Melatonin was first identified in plants by Hattori in 19958 and is involved in various physiological processes, including growth, photosynthesis, antioxidant defense, osmotic regulation, and the metabolism of other hormones5,9,11.

The most important positive aspect of melatonin’s effects in plants under stress is its multifunctional role10,12,13. Exogenous application of melatonin enhances plant tolerance to environmental stresses such as drought, salinity, and heavy metal toxicity by improving photosynthetic efficiency, boosting antioxidant activity, and regulating gene expression4,12,13.

The beneficial effect of melatonin in mitigating environmental stress is attributed to its role in maintaining ROS homeostasis. Melatonin increases the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT). Additionally, melatonin activates certain stress-related signaling pathways, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, thereby modulating the transcription of stress-responsive genes. Melatonin also protects the photosynthetic apparatus by enhancing cell stability and preventing chlorophyll degradation14,15.

Saffron (Crocus sativus L.) belongs to the Iridaceae family and is a geophytic plant that propagates via corms16. Saffron cultivation is widespread across many countries worldwide, with Iran being the largest producer and exporter, accounting for more than 94% of the global supply. Other notable saffron-producing countries include India, Afghanistan, Greece, Morocco, Spain, and Italy17,18. The red stigmas of saffron contain various chemical compounds, such as crocin, safranal, picrocrocin, crocetin, as well as proteins, sugars, vitamins, flavonoids, carbohydrates, minerals, and gums. The presence of these compounds makes saffron the most expensive spice in the world5,17,18. These valuable compounds and their medicinal properties including antitumor, neuroprotective, and antidepressant effects have led to the use of saffron in the food and pharmaceutical industries5.

Given the economic and medicinal importance of Crocus sativus, as well as the significant role of plant growth regulators particularly novel regulators such as melatonin in inducing stress tolerance, this study was conducted for the first time with the aim of investigating the potential ameliorative effects of melatonin on the antioxidant and photosynthetic responses of Crocus sativus under osmotic stress conditions. To achieve this, growth and photosynthetic parameters, compatible osmolytes, ROS and H₂O₂ levels, electrolyte leakage, and changes in the content and activity of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants were measured.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

All chemicals employed in this investigation were of analytical grade and procured from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany.

Plant material and treatments

Saffron corms weighing approximately 10–12 g were purchased from Mahtoo Company, Khorasan, Iran. Three corms were surface-sterilized with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min and then planted in pots containing perlite. The pots were transferred to a growth chamber with a relative humidity of 75% and a light intensity of 5000 lx, where they were exposed to a controlled environment including a temperature of 25 °C and a 10-hour light followed by 14-hour dark photoperiod. Until the leaves emerge from the perlite surface, they received only distilled water (four weeks), then gradually irrigated with 1/16, 1/8, and 1/4 (each for one week) strength Hoagland’s solution15. In the following and until the end of the experiment and for four weeks, they received 1/2 strength Hoagland’s solution with pH 5.8. From the seventh to the ninth weeks, the treatment groups under osmotic stress gradually received osmotic stress induced by polyethylene glycol 6000, such that in the treatment groups under 30% stress, the osmotic stress level increased to 30% over three weeks15. Foliar melatonin (100 µM) was sprayed five times per day at two-hour intervals using 1 ml per plant to fully wet the leaf surfaces. For root treatment, the same concentration (100 µM) was added once to the Hoagland’s solution. The pots were divided into six groups as follows: the first group did not receive any stress or melatonin treatment, the second group was only exposed to 30% osmotic stress, the third group was not stressed and only received foliar melatonin (FM), the fourth group was subjected to osmotic stress along with foliar melatonin treatment, the fifth group received root melatonin treatment only, and the sixth group exposed to osmotic stress and root melatonin treatment. At week twelve, two-thirds of the above-ground plant material was harvested, instantly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and preserved at -80 °C for later analyses. The remaining one-third was used to measure the fresh and dry weights of the plants.

Preparation of melatonin

The melatonin preparation was conducted following the procedure outlined by Ye et al. in 2016, with certain adjustments. Initially, 0.02328 g of melatonin was dissolved in 506.1 µL of ethyl alcohol to create a stock solution. Subsequently, 101.2 µL of this stock solution was diluted with 200 mL of distilled water, supplemented with 0.05% v/v Tween 20 as a surfactant19.

At harvest time, the fresh weight (FW) of the samples was determined. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to an oven set at 72 °C for a period of 72 h to attain a constant dry weight (DW)15. To assess the relative water content (RWC), the first fresh leaves were immersed in distilled water for 24 h to obtain the turgid weight (TW). Afterward, the samples were placed in an oven set at 70 °C for 48 h to determine their dry weight (DW). Biochemical assays

Compatible osmolytes

To determine the proline content in the leaves, the ninhydrin reagent was employed. In this procedure, 0.3 g of fresh leaves were homogenized with 1 ml of 3% sulfosalicylic acid. Following homogenization, the extract was subjected to centrifugation at 12,766 g for a duration of 20 min. The supernatant was separated, then ninhydrin reagent (200 µL) and glacial acetic acid (200 µL) were added. The samples were placed at 100 °C for 1 h, after cooling and adding toluene (2000 µL), the samples were vortexed for 10 to 15 s and finally, their absorbance was read at 520 nm22.

To measure total soluble carbohydrates (TSCs) 0.01 g of fresh leaves were powdered and homogenized in 1 mL of 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 7). After centrifuging the samples at 12,766 g for 20 min, 100 µL of the extract was added to 300 µL of sulfuric acid and vortexed for 30 s. The samples were transferred to an ice bath, and finally, the absorbance of the samples and glucose as a standard was read at 315 nm23. Dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) and glucose as a reagent and standard were used for measurement of total reducing sugar content, respectively. To prepare the reagent 1 g of DNS and 30 g of sodium potassium tartrate was added to 80 mL of 0.5 M KOH and was gently heated to dissolve the reagents. The volume was then made up to 100 mL with distilled water. 0.01 g of fresh leaves were powdered and homogenized in 1 mL of 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 7) after centrifuging the samples at 12,766 g for 20 min, 400 µL of DNS was added to 100 µL of the supernatant extract. Next, the samples were placed at a temperature of 95 °C for 5 min, after cooling and reaching a temperature of 25 °C, their absorbance was read at 540 nm24.

H2O2 and electrolyte leakage

To measure the H2O2 content, 0.3 g of fresh tissue leaves were homogenized with 1 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid. The resulting mixture was then centrifuged at 12,766 g for a duration of 20 min. Next, 200 µL of the obtained extract was combined with 50 µL of 10 mM phosphate buffer. Subsequently, 200 µL of 1 M potassium iodide was added to the mixture, which was then placed in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance of the sample was read at 390 nm, and the amount of H2O2 was determined using the extinction coefficient of 0.28 mM− 1 cm− 125.

To quantify the extent of electrolyte leakage from leaf cells, a procedure was followed. Initially, 0.2 g of leaf tissue was isolated and rinsed with distilled water. Subsequently, the samples were transferred into glass tubes containing 10 mL of distilled water and left undisturbed for a duration of 24 h. The electrical conductivity of the resulting solution was determined using an EC meter device as EC1. Next, the container housing the sample was subjected to an autoclave at a temperature of 120 °C for a duration of 20 min. After the container had cooled down, the electrical conductivity of the solution was measured once again, referred to as EC2. Measurement of ROS

The formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the reaction mixture was measured using 2, 7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Each sample contained 1700 µL of phosphate buffer solution (50 mM, pH = 7.4), 20 µL of extract, and 100 µL of DCFH-DA solution (10 µM). The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The ROS concentration was then determined by detecting the fluorescence of DCF using a Cary-Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer (Agilent/USA) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 521 nm27.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA)

0.3 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized with 1 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min. Then, 500 µL of a 0.5% thiobarbituric acid solution dissolved in 20% trichloroacetic acid was added to 250 µL of the plant extract. After vortexing, the samples were placed in a 95 °C water bath for 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to an ice bath to cool. The absorbance of the samples at 600 nm was subtracted from their absorbance at 532 nm. The malondialdehyde content of the samples was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM–1 cm–128.

Extraction of protein and enzyme

0.3 g of fresh leaves were homogenized in an ice bath with 1500 µl of extraction buffer containing (2-mercaptoethanol, glycerol, phosphate buffer, and EDTA in a ratio of 1:1:3:1). Then it was centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C and 12,766 g.

Protein content

The protein content of the samples was assessed using the well-known Bradford method. In this procedure, 20 µl of the sample was combined with 1 mL of Bradford’s solution. After allowing the mixture to incubate for 15 min, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 595 nm. To establish a standard curve, bovine serum albumin (BSA) was employed as the reference standard29.

Measurement of antioxidant activities

Enzymatic antioxidants

To assess the activity of various enzymes, specific procedures were followed as described below:

Ascorbate Peroxidase (APX) activity 100 µl of the enzyme extract was combined with 900 µl of enzyme buffer. The enzyme buffer contained 0.009 g of vitamin C, 880 ml of 50 mM phosphate buffer, and 20 µl of pure H2O2. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm for 1 min was considered to measure the activity of ascorbate peroxidase enzyme31.

Polyphenol Oxidase (PPO) activity 100 µl of the enzyme extract was mixed with 900 µl of enzyme buffer. The enzyme buffer consisted of 3.8 µl of H2O2 and 96.2 ml of 0.2% pyrogallol dissolved in 50 mM phosphate buffer with a pH of 7. To measure the activity of the enzyme, the increase in absorbance at 430 nm over a period of 3 min was considered32.

Statistical analysis

This study was conducted as factorial form and in a completely randomized design, all data were reported as the mean ± standard error (SE) values based on triplicate experiments. The statistical analysis of the data was performed using Minitab 16.0 software. To assess the significance of differences between samples, Tukey’s test was employed at a statistical significance level of P ≤ 0.05. Graphs were generated using Microsoft Office Excel 2013.

Result

Growth parameters and RWC

Osmotic stress reduced growth parameters. In these conditions, external application of melatonin, especially foliar treatment, improved these parameters. These changes are as follows: Compared to the control group, osmotic stress resulted in a significant decrease of 25.65% in FW, 38.57% in DW, and 21.77% in SH. However, when melatonin was applied via foliar treatment, these parameters increased by 24.93% in FW, 37.82% in DW, and 25.93% in SH. Similarly, when melatonin was applied to the roots, there was an increase of 19.18% in FW, 32.90% in DW, and 23.00% in SH (Table 1). During osmotic stress, the relative water content RWC experienced a significant decrease of 36.03%. However, the application of melatonin through foliar treatment resulted in a 19.14% increase in RWC, while root application of melatonin led to an 11.85% increase in RWC compared to the control group under osmotic stress (Table 1).

The study’s findings indicate that osmotic stress significantly decreased the Fv/Fm ratio compared to the control group. However, both foliar and root applications of melatonin were found to increase this ratio (Fig. 1B).

Furthermore, under osmotic stress conditions, the content of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids in saffron leaves decreased by 55.43%, 42.56%, and 23.75%, respectively, compared to the control group. However, melatonin treatment led to an increase in the content of these pigments compared to the related control group. This indicates that melatonin treatment may positively influence pigment synthesis and retention in saffron leaves, counteracting the adverse effects of osmotic stress. In the FM treatment, the increase in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids content was 58.39%, 49.62%, and 44.99%, respectively, while in the RM treatment, the increase was 30.14%, 24.14%, and 38.34% (Fig. 1C-E).

Osmotic stress had a significant impact on the intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) and stomatal conductance (C), reducing them by 16.56% and 35.92%, respectively, compared to the control group. However, both root and foliar applications of melatonin increased these parameters compared to the control group. The foliar treatment of melatonin resulted in a more pronounced effect, with an increase of 35.87% and 36.50% in Ci and C parameters, respectively, while the root treatment led to an increase of 27.02% and 20.91% (Fig. 1).

The study’s findings suggest that osmotic stress had an adverse effect on the protein content of saffron plants, causing a decrease of 28.07% compared to the control group. Antioxidant enzymes

Osmotic stress can induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can lead to cell membrane damage. To cope with this damage, plants employ various strategies, including increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes. In this study, it was observed that under osmotic stress conditions, the activity of several antioxidant enzymes increased significantly compared to the control group. Specifically, the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) increased by 53.37%, 75.51%, 62.50%, and 38.47%, respectively, in response to osmotic stress. This indicates that the plants responded to oxidative damage caused by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) during osmotic stress by enhancing the activity of these antioxidant enzymes.

Foliar and root application of melatonin, both in the presence and absence of osmotic stress, increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Under osmotic stress conditions, melatonin foliar spray led to an increase in the activities of SOD, CAT, APX, and PPO by 64.73%, 92.68%, 75.68%, and 51.52%, respectively, compared to the well-watered control group. Similarly, root melatonin treatment resulted in increased activities of these enzymes by 63.56%, 87.76%, 75%, and 46.67%, respectively, compared to the well-watered control group. These findings suggest that melatonin application can enhance the antioxidant defense system in saffron plants, protecting them from oxidative damage induced by osmotic stress (Fig. 6A–D).

Discussion

The current research examined the effectiveness of exogenous melatonin to counteract the negative outcome of osmotic stress on plants with a particular emphasis on the comparative analysis of foliar and root application. Evidence consistent with our hypothesis indicates that melatonin is a powerful plant growth regulator and it is more likely to increase drought tolerance through the improvement of major physiological functions. The two major findings include (1) exogenous melatonin application is very effective in reducing the effects of drought by improving the efficiency of photosynthesis as well as strengthening the antioxidant defence mechanism and (2) the means of application is important, with foliar spraying having a better effect than root drenching in increasing plant resistance.

The findings of our research indicate the positive effect of melatonin on photosynthetic parameters during water deficit stress. Water deficit stress induced by polyethylene glycol typically disrupts the photosynthetic machinery by closing stomata, degrading photosynthetic pigments, and impairing photosystem II (PSII)36. The plants treated with melatonin in our experiment exhibited significantly higher photosynthetic rates (Pn), stomatal conductance (gs), and chlorophyll content than the plants under osmotic stress in the control group. This finding is consistent with other studies that have shown melatonin helps maintain stomatal conductance and thus facilitates CO₂ uptake even under water deficiency37. Furthermore, the preservation of the maximum quantum yield of PSII (Fv/Fm) by melatonin demonstrated that melatonin, through its protective action, reduces oxidative damage to thylakoid membranes and helps maintain chloroplast integrity. The results regarding the reduction in MDA content as well as the decrease in electrolyte leakage, as indicators of membrane damage during the exogenous application of melatonin under water deficit stress treatments, clearly indicate the role of melatonin in preserving chloroplast integrity38.

One of the main processes that melatonin provides drought resistance is by potentiating the antioxidant system of the plant. The impact of osmotic stress also causes an excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including the superoxide radical (O2·–), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), oxidative destruction of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids39. Our findings reveal that melatonin use had a significant positive effect on activities of the key antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT). This enzymatic upsurge enables a more efficient scavenging of the ROS due to the reduced levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) which is one of the main indicators of lipid peroxidation in plants treated with melatonin. This observation is in line with the proven position of melatonin as a master regulator of redox homeostasis. Melatonin does not only scavenge oxidizing substances directly but also increases the transcription and activity of antioxidant enzymes, which inhibit oxidative stress in a multi-layered manner40,41.

Our findings demonstrate that osmotic stress induced significant accumulation of proline (21.93%) and soluble sugars (7%) while reducing sugar content decreased (5.34%), reflecting classic osmotic adjustment responses. However, both foliar and root applications of melatonin effectively reduced proline content (by 20.20% and 19.31%, respectively) and sugar levels under osmotic stress, with root application showing superior efficacy for reducing sugars (10.42% reduction) and foliar application for soluble sugars (4.84% reduction). These reductions do not indicate compromised stress tolerance but rather suggest that melatonin acts as a priming agent that alleviates stress severity, reducing the plant’s reliance on osmolyte accumulation42,43. The decreased proline and sugar levels following melatonin treatment indicate improved cellular homeostasis and reduced oxidative damage, which is further supported by our companion findings of enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities. Collectively, these results confirm that exogenous melatonin enhances drought tolerance in plants by mitigating stress intensity, thereby diminishing the need for energy-expensive osmoprotectant biosynthesis while maintaining cellular integrity through complementary antioxidant defense mechanisms.

Despite the limitations associated with foliar application (e.g., dosage loss, phytotoxicity, scalability), our results overall showed that foliar spraying is more effective than root application in increasing plant resistance to osmotic stress. Leaf surface treated with foliar melatonin showed a greater enhancement in photosynthetic capacity and antioxidant ability than plants treated through the roots. It can be explained by a number of physiological and non-physiological factors. Foliar spraying of melatonin can likely increase its immediate absorption through the stomata and cuticle, which enhances its rapid transport to chloroplasts and other cellular sites under oxidative stress levels44. Conversely, melatonin applied to roots has to overcome the challenges of soil environment, such as possible degradation by microbes, adsorption by soil particles and differing uptake efficiencies by roots, which are affected by the health of roots and the transpiration stream under drought45. Secondly, foliar application is more efficient in targeting the aerial foliage, which is the main site of photosynthesis and ROS generation, and the damage caused by drought is eliminated at the primary location.

The results of exogenous application of serotonin, as one of the intermediates of melatonin biosynthesis, in mitigating the detrimental effects of osmotic stress in saffron plants showed similar results, with the difference that in that study, unlike the present study, root application of serotonin was more effective than its foliar application46.

We find that our findings are supported by the previous works which provide clues to the effectiveness of the application techniques. An example is the study conducted on tomatoes and apples that revealed foliar melatonin to be more beneficial than soil drenching in redressing cold and drought stress, respectively47. It seems that the direct route of foliar absorption is more valid and quicker delivery mechanism of melatonin to the leaves as the frontline organs to atmospheric drought.

To sum up, our research provides evidence that exogenous melatonin is an effective approach to improve drought tolerance in plants. Its protective effect is mediated by the maintenance of photosynthetic activity and the stimulation of a strong antioxidant system. Most importantly, we determine that the method of application is a determining factor towards success, and foliar spraying is the strategy that proves notably effective. These findings have direct practical implications for agricultural management under water-limited conditions: foliar melatonin spraying could be integrated into deficit irrigation scheduling or applied during early osmotic stress symptoms to sustain photosynthesis and reduce oxidative damage, particularly in high-value crops. For field-scale implementation, application at key growth stages (e.g., flowering or early fruit set) using low-volume, high-concentration sprays may enhance resource efficiency. Subsequent studies are needed to clarify the molecular signal transduction induced by foliar-applied melatonin and to determine optimal application timing, concentration, and frequency across various crop species in order to achieve maximum positive results under real field conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that exogenous melatonin application mitigates osmotic stress in Crocus sativus under greenhouse conditions. Foliar application of melatonin was more effective than root application in improving drought tolerance. Melatonin treatment increased photosynthetic pigment content, enhanced photosystem II efficiency (Fv/Fm), and raised net photosynthesis rates. It also boosted the activity of enzymatic antioxidants (SOD, CAT, POD) and non-enzymatic antioxidants, while increasing compatible osmolytes such as proline and soluble sugars. These changes reduced oxidative damage and maintained cellular osmotic balance under osmotic stress (Fig. 8). Therefore, foliar melatonin application is recommended as an effective strategy to improve saffron growth and productivity in drought-affected areas (Fig. 8).
