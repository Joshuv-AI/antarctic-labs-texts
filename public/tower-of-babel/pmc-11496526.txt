Two-year cumulative live-birth rates and maximum number of transfer cycles in women aged ≥ 40 years
Authors: Sakiko Nukaga, Mikiko Tokoro, Emiko Asano, Daichi Inoue, Yoshiki Hashiba, Noritaka Fukunaga, Yoshimasa Asada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study aimed to clarify the cumulative live-birth rates (CLBRs) and cost per live-birth (LB) to evaluate the validity of frozen–thawed embryo transfer without preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) in women aged ≥ 40 years. The study included 1,011 patients aged ≥ 40 years who underwent their first oocyte retrieval at our hospital between January 2010 and September 2017. They were followed up for up to two years or until either treatment discontinuation or a pregnancy that resulted in a live birth. The 2-year CLBRs were 55.6%, 39.0%, 31.3%, 19.1%, 10.6%, 4.4%, and 0% for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, and > 46 years, respectively. In approximately 80% of LB cases, patients aged 40–42 years and 43–44 years became pregnant by the fourth and second transfers, respectively. Costs per LB were $30,207, $49,034, $66,345, $102,759, and $195,862 for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, and 44, respectively. Cost per LB for each number of transfers reached $300,000 and $ 450,000 for the third transfer at 42 and 43 years of age, respectively. For cost-effectiveness, up to two ET cycles are recommended for patients aged 42–43, and none for patients aged ≥ 44 years.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74460-y.Subject terms: Medical research, Outcomes research

Introduction

The demand for assisted reproductive technology (ART) has increased among patients of advanced maternal age, with 40% of ART in Japan is conducted in women aged ≥ 40 years. In women of advanced maternal age, the detection of euploid embryos using preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) has been expected to improve the live-birth rate1. However, female-age-related poor ovarian reserves and a reduced blastocyst development rate2often result in insufficient number of blastocysts, thereby, making it difficult to use PGT-A. Due to the rapid decline in fecundity after 37 years of age in women3, the cost per live birth of fertility treatment has been reported to increase threefold in women aged > 40 years compared with younger patients4,5.

Although there have been a few reports on real-time based cumulative live-birth rates (CLBRs)6,7, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have focused on the number of embryo transfer (ET) cycles required for live births in patients aged ≥ 40 years. Since there are no clear guidelines for discontinuing infertility treatment, there is concern that patients of advanced reproductive age may spend excessively on multiple transfers with unreasonable expectations.

The objective of this study is to clarify the CLBRs of two years from frozen–thawed ET (FET) without PGT-A to evaluate the validity of infertility treatment in women of advanced reproductive age. The cost per live birth (LB) for each number of ET cycle was also calculated to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of FET in women with a poor prognosis.

Materials & methods

This is a retrospective single-center cohort study using individual patient data. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Asada Ladies Shinagawa Clinic and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants enrolled.

Patients’ eligibility criteria

Overview of included and excluded patients are shown in Fig. 1. Patients aged 40–48 years who underwent their first oocyte retrieval between January 2010 and September 2017 were included in the study. They were followed up for 2 years, or until either the treatment was discontinued or the pregnancy resulted in a live birth (defined as any birth event wherein at least one baby was born alive after ≥ 22 weeks) within 2 years from the initial retrieval of oocytes. Additionally, we included patients who underwent multiple retrievals or ETs during the follow-up period. All embryos were cryopreserved via vitrification at the pronuclear stage after retrieval; they were then transferred in artificial hormone replacement cycles. Cleavage-stage embryos were subsequently transferred in the first embryo transfer. Patients receiving fresh ET, those receiving FET via the slow freezing method, and those who could not reach retrieval were excluded from this study. Furthermore, we excluded the patients using donor gametes, PGT-A, and cryopreserved oocytes. “Other reasons” in Fig. 1 reflect missing data (BMI and basal FSH levels). The ovarian reserve for in vitro fertilization (IVF) treatment in our center did not have a lower limit. Patients who had live births within 2 years of treatment were grouped into live-birth arm (LB arm) and the others into non live-birth arm (nLB arm), respectively. Patients who discontinued hospital visits, had no oocytes retrieved after oocytes retrieval were included in the nLB arm.

Treatment protocol

Controlled ovarian stimulation protocol

Stimulation methods were selected depending on the ovarian reserve (basal follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH] level, antimullerian hormone [AMH], and antral follicle count [AFC]). Patients who met an AMH of < 1.0–1.2 were treated with mild stimulation protocol. The other patients were treated with GnRH antagonist protocol or short protocol. Some of the patients received GnRH antagonist or short protocol for the first cycle and mild stimulation for the latter cycle, because of reduction in the ovarian reserve. During stimulation, all patients underwent transvaginal ultrasonography and blood sampling every 2–7 days. Additionally, doses of their medications were adjusted as per follicle diameter and blood hormone levels: FSH, luteinizing hormone (LH), and E2. The gonadotropins used for controlled ovarian stimulation were human menopausal gonadotropin (Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Tokyo, Japan or ASKA Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) or recombinant FSH (Gonal-f®, Merck Serono, Tokyo, Japan). In the mild stimulation protocol, clomiphene citrate (CC) < 100 mg/day was administered and gonadotropin was added as needed. In the short protocol, we started with the administration of nasal buserelin at 600 µg/d on day 3 of the stimulation cycle. A GnRH agonist was administered every day until hCG was injected. In the GnRH antagonist protocol, the alternate day (in principle) administration of a GnRH antagonist (ganirelix, Ganirest®, MSD, Tokyo, Japan) or cetrorelix (Cetrotide®, Merck Serono) was commenced when the follicular size reached a diameter of 14–16 mm or when a premature LH surge was suspected based on blood LH levels. This was followed by final oocyte maturation with a GnRH agonist or hCG (3000–5000 IU). After 36 h following the hCG injection, the mature oocytes were retrieved.

Embryo cryopreservation and culture

The embryos were cryopreserved through vitrification at the pronuclear state for which Vitrification Kits VT101 and VT102 (Kitazato BioPharma, Shizuoka, Japan) were used. The partial cell death of cleavage-stage embryos after thawing may adversely affect the outcome of fertility treatment8–13. On the other hand, pronuclear-stage embryos are single-celled; therefore, cell death after thawing can be ruled out. For these reasons, embryos from women with reduced blastocyst development rates are frozen as pronuclear-stage rather than cleavage-stage embryos. During the study period, the post-warming survival rate at our hospital was 98.9% (52,530/53,124) for pronuclear-stage embryos and 99.8% (15,947/15,972) for blastocysts. The embryos were individually cultured in 15 µL drops of One-step Medium® (NAKA ivf medium®, Naka Medical, Tokyo, Japan). The patients were recommended to bank > 3 pronuclear embryos prior to embryo transfer if possible.

Embryo transfer

Frozen–thawed embryo transfers were performed when ≥ 3 pronuclear stage embryos were obtained; 3–5 cryopreserved pronuclear embryos were simultaneously thawed. They were then cultured until day 3 of embryo development. From several thawed pronuclear stage embryos, the ones that showed the best development in terms of division speed and fragment amount were selected on day 3, and one or two of cleavage-stage embryos among these were transferred. The remaining embryos were continued to culture. Embryos that became blastocysts were refrozen and some were transferred in other cycles. All FETs were performed in artificial hormone replacement cycles, according to the endometrial preparation protocol using transdermal estradiol (Estrana®, Hisamitsu Pharmaceutical, Saga, Japan), combined with chlormadinone acetate (Lutoral®, Fuji Pharma, Tokyo, Japan)14. Estradiol treatment was started on the 2nd or 3rd day of the artificial hormone replacement cycle, and administration of chlormadinone acetate 6 mg/d was started on the 15th day of the cycle. The transfer of cleavage-stage embryos was performed on day 3 by considering the day on which the administration of chlormadinone acetate was commenced as day 0. ET was cancelled if the endometrial thickness was < 7 mm on days 9–11 of the cycle or ovulation occurred before the start of chlormadinone acetate. We also cancelled ET if no embryos were available for transfer because of division arrest, degeneration, or multinucleation.

Data collection

Medical records and the database at our hospital were retrospectively extracted using the age of initial oocyte retrieval; AMH, basal FSH levels; body mass index (BMI); stimulation method as patient background; presence/absence of live birth; and total number of oocytes retrieved, metaphase 2 (MII) oocyte, transferred embryos and ET cycles, as treatment records.

Cost per LB

We used the average cost of oocyte retrieval and embryo transfer cycles at our center, which included outpatient care, medications, ultrasound scanning, blood tests, oocyte retrieval procedures, embryo transfer procedures, and fertilization charges. The costs for each patient were estimated by adding the cost of oocyte retrieval and transfer cycles plus the cost of embryo culture and cryopreservation. The following values were used in the calculations: 500,000 yen ($3,448) per retrieval cycle by the antagonist method, 300,000 yen ($2,069) per retrieval cycle by the mild stimulation method, 300,000 yen ($2,069) per transfer cycle, and 20,000 yen ($138) per embryo for embryo culture and cryopreservation. The cost per LB was calculated as the total fertility treatment cost divided by the total number of live births in each age group4,5,15–17. The cost per LB for each number of ET cycles was calculated from the cumulative cost up to that ET cycle for patients who continued fertility treatment through the ET cycle divided by the number of live births in that ET cycle. The costs are shown in dollars, whereas 145 yen was converted as $1.00.

Main outcome measures

The primary outcome was CLBR within two years after the first cycle of ovarian stimulation. Patients who discontinued hospital visits were deemed “not pregnant (conservative CLBR).” The secondary outcome was the number of ET cycles required for live births.

Statistical analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the LB and nLB arms in terms of AMH; basal FSH level; BMI; total number of oocytes retrieved, MII oocytes, transferred embryos, and ET cycles. Meanwhile, the Chi-square/prop test was used to determine the parity ratio. A significant difference was noted when the p value was ≤0.05. Statistical analysis was done using software R2,14.

Cost per LB

Cost per LB

The cost per LB was $52,138 for the entire cohort, and was $30,207, $49,034, $66,345, $102,759 and $195,862 for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, and 44 years, respectively. The costs per LB for each transfer cycle are presented in Supplemental Table 1. It exceeded $150,000 after the fourth and third ET cycles at the age of 40 and 41 years, respectively. For patients aged 42–43 years, the cost per LB for the third transfer reached $300,000 and $450,000, respectively, more than three times higher than that of the second transfer.

Discussion

The cumulative 2-year live birth rates were 55.6%, 39.0%, 31.3%, 19.1%, 10.6%, 4.4%, and 0% for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, and ≥46 years, respectively. There have been reports of patients aged 40 years and older; CLBRs in the first IVF were 1.2–16.4%18, CLBRs of five years were 16%6, and CLBRs from the first IVF to the end of treatment was 23%19. The CLBRs in our study were higher than those previously reported20. The design of the present study is different from those reported previously owing to the fact in that many of the patients underwent multiple oocyte retrievals and only FETs with vitrification were included.

Devesa et al. examined CLBR after the first oocyte retrieval and reported that nondonor IVF without PGT-A was futile, with live birth rates of only 1.2% for fresh ET cycles and 0% for FET cycles in patients aged ≥ 44 years18. On the other hand, our results suggested that IVF is a reasonable treatment for patients up to 44 years old. In our study, a median of two retrievals and 13 oocytes were required for a live birth, suggesting that a single oocyte retrieval reported by Devesa et al. is typically insufficient for a live birth.

Our hospital typically conducts day 3 ETs, since the embryos of patients aged 40 years and older are less likely to develop into blastocysts2. It has been reported that 6–17% of day 3 embryos in patients in their 40s are euploid embryos3; therefore, day 3 ET without PGT-A in women of advanced reproductive age is usually unsuccessful. Freeze-all strategy with vitrification enabled us to select better developed embryos on day 3 from several pronuclear stage embryos with minimal damage to the embryos compared with slow freezing21,22. The ET cancellation rate in this study was only 0.05%, resulting in a shorter time to pregnancy. This time reduction is important in women of advanced maternal age, because they show faster time-dependent decline in fertility than young women6.

The cost per LB was $52,138 for the entire cohort in this study. Our pricing is lower than the cost of fertility treatment in the United States and is comparable to the cost estimates in European countries5,15,16,23. The cost per LB had been previously reported as $51,660 for patients aged 38 years and older15, £40,320 or $97,884 for patients aged 40 years and older4,5, and $182,794 for women aged 42 years and older5. One reason for the lower cost per LB in this study compared with previous reports is the relatively high CLBR4,24.

In approximately 80% cases of LB, the patients aged 40–42 and 43–44 years became pregnant by the fourth and second transfers, respectively. The cost per LB exceeded $150,000 after the fourth and third ET cycles at the age of 40 and 41 years, and $300,000 and $450,000 at the ages of 42 and 43 years, respectively. Our data reveal that as women age, the chance of an LB is reasonable only in the first few cycles. Patients who showed no results after a certain number of transfers should be advised to quit fertility treatment. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, up to two ET cycles are recommended for patients 42–43 years of age, whereas none are recommended for patients aged 44 years and older.

The limitations of this study include the following: this was a retrospective examination; the period was prolonged; CLBR may have been overestimated as only the patients who reached retrieval were included; and CLBR may have been underestimated as not all embryos were transferred. Furthermore, we used the cost of a single private clinic to calculate the average cost per live birth, which may have been an underestimation and cannot be generalized.

While considering the above limitations, several strengths of this study were identified. First, this is the first report to propose an upper limit of ET cycles for aged women. Although it is very important to prevent futile fertility treatments among women of advanced reproductive age, no useful indicator had been available to determine when to discontinue the treatment. Second, our CLBRs include frozen-thawed embryo transfer using the vitrification method and not fresh embryo transfer or slow freezing FET. Third, the study period for CLBRs was only two years. A 5-year design is common in the calculation of CLBRs, but this design is too long for aged women. Some studies have reported that approximately 90% cases wherein live births were reported in the 5-year follow-up period, resulted in a live birth within two years of treatment6,7. Hence, this study implemented a 2-year period from the initial retrieval of oocytes to pregnancy. This is an extremely useful study in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Cumulative 2-year live birth rates on FET without PGT-A were 55.6%, 39.0%, 31.3%, 19.1%, 10.6%, 4.4%, and 0% for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, and ≥ 46 years, respectively. The cost per LB was $30,207, $49,034, $66,345, $102,759, and $195,862 for patients aged 40, 41, 42, 43, and 44, respectively. After the third transfer, the cost per LB reached $300,000 and $450,000 for patients aged 42 and 43 years, respectively. For cost-effectiveness, up to two ET cycles are recommended for patients 42–43 years of age, whereas none are recommended for patients aged 44 years and older.
