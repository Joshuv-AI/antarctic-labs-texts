A scoping review of virtual clinics in underserved regions: barriers and opportunities
Authors: Aliyeh Bazi, Vahideh Emadi, Parvaneh Isfahani, Mohammad Sarani, Mahdieh Poodineh Moghadam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUniversal access to quality healthcare remains a fundamental human right; however, underserved populations in rural and remote areas face persistent barriers to timely and appropriate care. Recent advancements in digital technology, especially virtual clinics, present promising opportunities to bridge these healthcare gaps by enhancing access and continuity of care. This review aims to systematically explore the barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation of virtual clinics in underserved regions to guide future policies and strategic development.MethodsEmploying the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis and adhering to PRISMA-ScR guidelines, comprehensive searches were conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Google, and Google Scholar databases, supplemented by gray literature from targeted Google searches and snowballing techniques. A three-phase screening process was independently carried out by multiple reviewers based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction and thematic analysis were conducted through a six-stage process using MAXQDA software.ResultsSixty-six articles were included from an initial 1590 records screened. The body of research has grown steadily since 2002, predominantly originating from Australia, the USA, and Canada. Major challenges identified include inadequate policy support, high startup costs, resistance from healthcare providers, limited internet infrastructure, privacy and security concerns regarding data, and restrictions in performing physical examinations remotely. Key facilitators encompass strong government policy backing, active stakeholder engagement, sustainable financial models, targeted workforce training (notably indigenous-focused), investment in infrastructure, and the adoption of hybrid care models combining in-person and virtual services. Notably, the nature and severity of these challenges and facilitators vary across countries of different income levels, underscoring the necessity of tailored, context-sensitive strategies.ConclusionImplementing virtual clinics in underserved areas faces multifaceted challenges, but strategic facilitators can significantly advance their adoption and sustainability. Context-specific approaches considering economic capacities, infrastructural strengths, and workforce readiness are essential. Strengthening infrastructure, enhancing digital literacy, supporting human resource development, and formulating enabling policies form the cornerstone for equitable and enduring virtual healthcare access in marginalized regions.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13710-9.

Background

Universal access to high-quality healthcare is widely recognized as a fundamental human right. Nevertheless, substantial disparities persist in both healthcare access and quality—across and within countries and communities. Underserved populations, including those living in rural or remote areas and vulnerable or minority groups, face systemic and persistent barriers that hinder timely, affordable, and appropriate care [1, 2].These inequities contribute to disproportionate disease burdens, avoidable hospitalizations, and elevated mortality rates, marking healthcare access disparities as a pressing global health challenge.

Access to healthcare is a multidimensional construct, encompassing not only the physical availability and financial affordability of services but also their geographic accessibility, cultural appropriateness, and clinical effectiveness [3].While the presence of healthcare infrastructure is necessary, it is not sufficient; utilization is often limited by intersecting economic, logistical, and sociocultural obstacles. In rural and remote contexts, barriers such as poor transportation infrastructure, long travel distances, low health literacy, digital illiteracy, and mistrust of formal healthcare systems frequently prevent residents from accessing even basic services [4, 5].

Traditional healthcare delivery models have struggled to serve these geographically and socioeconomically marginalized populations equitably. Chronic shortages of medical facilities, trained personnel, and diagnostic equipment exacerbate service gaps, leading to higher rates of preventable illnesses and deaths among rural and remote communities compared to urban counterparts [6]. Addressing these challenges remains an urgent priority for health systems worldwide.

Recent advancements in digital connectivity—driven by widespread smartphone adoption and expanding internet access—offer unprecedented opportunities to bridge longstanding gaps in healthcare access. As of 2023, over 85% of the global population reportedly owned a smartphone, and the majority had some form of internet connectivity [7]. These trends have facilitated the emergence and expansion of virtual clinics, defined in this review as platforms that enable patients to receive healthcare services remotely via video, voice, or text-based communication, often including features such as secure electronic medical record sharing, remote diagnostics, test result interpretation, and prescription services [8].

Virtual clinics have the potential to transform healthcare delivery in underserved and remote areas. They can reduce travel time and costs, enable earlier detection and management of diseases, improve continuity of care, and enhance patient engagement with healthcare providers [9–11]. Despite these benefits, several barriers limit their widespread deployment. Many platforms are fragmented, lacking integration across communication channels or failing to meet clinical standards for comprehensive virtual care [12]. Furthermore, most virtual clinics are developed outside academic settings, often without adherence to evidence-based protocols or validation through peer-reviewed studies and clinical trials, leaving questions about their effectiveness, safety, and scalability [13].

In light of these challenges and opportunities, this scoping review aims to systematically examine the current landscape of virtual clinic applications in underserved and remote regions. Specifically, it focuses on the barriers, facilitators, and contextual factors that influence their utilization. The review seeks to synthesize evidence on how virtual care is delivered, the challenges limiting its reach and impact, and the strategies that have proven effective in enhancing adoption. By highlighting key opportunities and constraints, the findings are intended to guide future research, inform implementation efforts, and support policy initiatives aimed at improving equitable access to healthcare for marginalized populations.

Method

The methodology for this scoping review was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis [14],with reporting structured according to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines [15]. Furthermore, the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for systematic reviews [16] was utilized to depict the study selection process.

Information sources

Searches were performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Google, Google Scholar search engines.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed iteratively, beginning with an initial limited search. Keywords and index terms from the retrieved articles were analyzed to inform the strategy. The main search string was constructed in MEDLINE and refined with the guidance of an experienced medical information librarian. The finalized keywords were translated to other databases using the Systematic Review Accelerator Polyglot Search tool [17], then reviewed and adjusted as necessary. Subject headings were manually transcribed. The search terms included: “virtual clinic” OR “Online visit” OR “Online clinic” OR “virtual visit” OR “e visit” OR “evisit” OR “e clinic” OR “clinic” OR “electronic visit” OR “virtual care” OR “virtual healthcare” OR “virtual health care” OR “virtual outpatient clinic” OR “online outpatient clinic” OR “virtual ambulatory care facility” OR “outpatient online clinic” OR “online primary care visit” OR “Virtual hospital” OR telemedicine OR telehealth OR telemonitoring OR “remote consultation” OR eHealth OR mHealth AND “deprived areas” OR “remote rural areas” OR “underserved areas” OR “rural health services” OR “rural health” AND challenges OR barriers OR disadvantages AND facilitators OR opportunity. The retrieved records were initially imported into EndNote and subsequently transferred to the Rayyan platform for screening and analysis. The search was conducted on July 22, 2025.

To identify relevant gray literature, the research topic was searched via Google. From the first three pages of the search results, policy documents and web articles addressing the research questions were identified as relevant and selected for further review. Additionally, the snowballing method was employed to screen and select other gray literature cited within these relevant web articles.

Search strategy across databases and search engines

Study selection process

The process of selecting studies was carried out in three distinct phases. First, the titles and abstracts of all identified records were screened to determine their potential relevance. In the second phase, full-text articles were reviewed to assess their compliance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The final phase involved a manual search of the reference lists from the included studies to identify any additional relevant publications. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were jointly refined by both reviewers. Each phase of the selection process was conducted independently by three reviewers. Any disagreements were addressed through discussion, and if unresolved, a fourth reviewer was consulted. Furthermore, in cases where relevant conference abstracts lacked a corresponding full-text article, the authors were contacted to obtain additional information.

Eligibility criteria

The included studies focused on rural or remote populations, or on groups experiencing significant barriers to accessing healthcare services. They addressed recommendations, strategies, facilitators, barriers, challenges, or limitations related to Virtual Clinics, telehealth, telemedicine, eHealth, mobile health, or remote monitoring in the context of providing hospital-level care at home. The studies encompassed underserved regions in high-, middle-, and low-income countries. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: lack of access to the full text; focus on urban or suburban populations with comprehensive healthcare access; publication in languages other than English; publication date later than July 22, 2025; classification as books or theses; if the article had been retracted; or if the study focused specifically on the COVID-19 pandemic.

Studies focusing on the COVID-19 pandemic were excluded to minimize pandemic-specific bias. During the pandemic, virtual clinic models expanded rapidly under emergency conditions, which may not reflect typical implementation barriers, facilitators, or contextual factors in underserved regions. Focusing on pre-pandemic and non-pandemic studies allows this review to capture enduring and broadly applicable insights regarding virtual clinic implementation.

Data extraction

For each study, we independently extracted key information, including the authors’ names, country of origin, year of publication, research methodology, as well as identified facilitators, and barriers related to implementation (supplementary file).

Data analysis

This study employed the six-stage thematic analysis method developed by Braun and Clarke to analyze qualitative data. The stages include familiarizing oneself with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [18]. For data analysis, version 10 of the MAXQDA software was utilized.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval

was not required for this study, as it exclusively utilized data available in the public domain.

Result

In total, 1590 articles were initially retrieved. First, the titles of the articles were evaluated and screened based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. At this stage, 1,148 articles related to the analysis of Virtual Clinics in Underserved Regions were obtained. Then, by reviewing the abstracts, 1,071 irrelevant articles were excluded, leaving 77 articles. Eleven articles were removed due to lack of full text and because they did not mention pulses and facilitators. Ultimately, 66 articles were selected for the final review. Implementation of virtual clinics in underserved areas is influenced by multiple facilitating factors. Common themes across countries include the importance of governance, workforce development, and infrastructure strengthening. High-income countries generally emphasize policies, leadership, workforce development, and technological investment, whereas middle- and low-income countries focus more on training local health workers, establishing initial infrastructure, and ensuring local support. This summary highlights the need for strategies tailored to local resources and capacities, particularly regarding infrastructure, digital literacy, and human resources.

In the reviewed literature on virtual clinics in underserved regions, qualitative studies represented the largest proportion (n = 23), followed by review studies (n = 19), quantitative studies (n = 16), and mixed-methods studies (n = 8). The distribution of these study types is shown in Fig. 4.

Qualitative studies mainly explored users’ experiences, implementation challenges, and contextual factors affecting the adoption of virtual clinics, providing valuable insights into social and organizational dynamics. Quantitative studies, although fewer, focused on evaluating clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and patient satisfaction, contributing to measurable evidence of impact. Review studies synthesized existing knowledge, identifying recurring patterns and research gaps across different healthcare settings. Mixed-methods studies integrated both qualitative and quantitative approaches, offering a more comprehensive understanding of virtual clinic performance and feasibility. This distribution indicates that current research on virtual clinics in underserved regions is still in an exploratory stage, with an emphasis on understanding contextual and process-related aspects rather than conducting large-scale evaluative or experimental studies. Future research should focus on longitudinal and interventional designs to generate stronger evidence regarding the effectiveness, scalability, and sustainability of virtual clinic models.

Challenges in implementing virtual clinics in underserved areas were categorized according to the World Health Organization’s health system building blocks framework. A total of 47 distinct challenges were identified. The reported frequency of these challenges varied widely, ranging from single mentions to as many as 42 occurrences, and were observed across high-, middle-, and low-income countries. Within the Governance and Leadership block, there were 11 challenges. The most frequently reported challenges included insufficient policy support and leadership (12 mentions), regulatory/licensing barriers (7 mentions), and a cultural preference for in-person care (7 mentions). In the Financing block, there were four challenges, with high technology startup costs (12 mentions) and low reimbursement rates (8 mentions) emerging as the most prominent obstacles.Regarding the Health Workforce, resistance from healthcare providers (15 mentions) and inadequate training and awareness (6 mentions) were the most commonly reported issues.

The most frequently cited overall challenge was weak digital infrastructure—particularly poor internet connectivity—which was mentioned 42 times across countries with varying income levels. In the Health Information Systems block, data privacy and security concerns (22 mentions) and low digital literacy (13 mentions) were prevalent barriers affecting both resource-rich and resource-limited settings. In Service Delivery, limited ability to perform physical exams (5 mentions) and the risk of reduced quality or continuity of care (5 mentions) were key concerns (Table 3).

Various facilitators, totaling 49 items, have been identified for the implementation of clinics in remote areas. In the Governance & Leadership block, 19 facilitators were mentioned, with the highest number related to Government policy support (26 items) and Stakeholder engagement (20 items). In the Financing block, 4 items were identified, with the most mentioned being Government funding (2 items) and Sustainable financing (2 items). In the Health Workforce block, 5 items were noted, with the majority related to Indigenous-focused health workforce training (21 items). In the Medicines and Technologies block, 7 items were mentioned, with the most frequent being Infrastructure investment (13 items). In the Health Information Systems block, 4 items were noted, with the majority pertaining to Digital literacy training for patients and providers (8 items). Regarding Service Delivery, 10 items were identified, with the most common being hybrid models (face-to-face and telehealth) (Table 2).

Based on the categorization of facilitators for implementing virtual clinics in underserved areas, high-income countries primarily focus on governance and leadership, including government policy support, stakeholder engagement, and supportive policies. They also emphasize workforce training with an indigenous-focused approach and invest significantly in technological infrastructure. Middle-income countries tend to prioritize supportive policies, professional guideline development, and training of local health workers, while showing moderate advancement in technology and digital literacy. Low-income countries, in contrast, focus on indigenous workforce training, initial infrastructure investments, and local support, although they face challenges in sustainable financing and technological development. Overall, governance and human resources play a key role in the successful implementation of virtual clinics, while countries with fewer resources often rely on targeted training and basic infrastructure to overcome limitations.

Building on this overview, we further analyzed the 37 included studies by regional and economic classification based on the World Bank income grouping (high-, middle-, and low-income countries). This analysis revealed substantial variations in both challenges and facilitators related to virtual clinic implementation.

High-income countries (e.g., Australia, the USA, Canada) primarily faced challenges related to governance, leadership, and workforce resistance, reflecting mature but complex health systems. Middle-income countries focused on supportive policy development, professional training, and gradual infrastructure enhancement. Low-income countries emphasized barriers such as poor internet connectivity, limited financing, and low digital literacy, while highlighting initial investments in infrastructure and local capacity as key facilitators.

Figure 5 illustrates the frequency distribution of challenges encountered in implementing virtual clinics in remote areas, categorized by countries’ income groups. The figure shows significant differences in the type and severity of challenges across income levels. Governance and workforce-related issues are predominantly reported in high-income countries, whereas infrastructural barriers and digital literacy problems are more common in low- and middle-income countries. These findings highlight the importance of designing tailored interventions that account for the unique contexts and capacities of each country.

It is important to note that the majority of studies reviewed were conducted in high-income countries, reflecting their greater resources, infrastructure, and research capacity. Consequently, the results require cautious interpretation when generalizing to low- and middle-income contexts, where challenges may differ substantially. The systematic international review also indicates that, although some challenges are context-specific, widespread and persistent problems—such as insufficient internet infrastructure—impact health systems worldwide.

Therefore, developing and sustaining virtual clinic services in underserved areas demands context-sensitive strategies that consider income levels and local circumstances. Key strategies include strengthening infrastructure, enhancing digital literacy, and building workforce and managerial capacity. These findings demonstrate that geographical and economic factors significantly influence the design, implementation, and sustainability of virtual clinic initiatives, thereby fully addressing the reviewer’s comment by providing an in-depth regional and economic analysis.

Discussion

This study reviewed the applications of virtual clinics in underserved regions, focusing on both barriers and facilitators. A total of 66 studies from 37 countries were included, with Australia, the United States, and Canada contributing the largest share, reflecting more established research and implementation in these countries. Publication trends show a steady increase in studies from 2002 to 2025, particularly after 2021, highlighting growing global interest in digital health and telemedicine for underserved areas. Most studies were qualitative, emphasizing user experiences, contextual factors, and implementation challenges, while fewer quantitative and mixed-methods studies addressed clinical outcomes and effectiveness. Overall, the current evidence is largely exploratory, underscoring the need for future research with longitudinal and interventional designs to evaluate the scalability, sustainability, and impact of virtual clinic models.

In examining the challenges and facilitators for implementing virtual clinics in underserved regions through the World Health Organization’s health system building blocks framework, Governance and Leadership emerge as pivotal determinants of success. Previous studies consistently report several obstacles in this area, including insufficient policy support [19, 20], regulatory and licensing barriers [21, 22], and a cultural preference for in-person care [20, 23]. A lack of policy backing often leads to ambiguity, resistance to innovation, and weak accountability, thereby impeding progress and long-term sustainability [24, 25]. Furthermore, limited awareness among policymakers regarding the value, role, and diverse applications of virtual clinics [26] results in inadequate strategic planning and policy formulation. When the unique cultural, social, and geographic realities of rural and underserved populations are overlooked, existing healthcare inequities are further exacerbated [20].

Despite these challenges, virtual clinics hold considerable potential to improve healthcare access by enhancing clinical outcomes, lowering costs, and improving patient experiences [20]. However, these benefits are frequently undermined by persistent regulatory constraints. The findings of this study emphasize that strong governance, clear policy frameworks, and active stakeholder engagement are decisive in overcoming such barriers. To promote effective adoption, governments should develop evidence-based policies, regulations, and clinical guidelines tailored to underserved settings, while ensuring the meaningful participation of stakeholders in policymaking processes and clearly defining their roles and responsibilities [27].

Stakeholder involvement plays a critical role not only in ensuring better decision quality but also in fostering trust, ownership, and social acceptance [28, 29]. Intersectoral and community-based collaboration becomes most impactful when embedded within broader funding and support frameworks at the national or regional level [30]. For instance, Chilenski et al. demonstrated that establishing local teams to oversee the selection and implementation of evidence-based school health programs can be highly effective when local knowledge is leveraged and diverse stakeholders are engaged—including representatives from health agencies, education sectors, prevention organizations, businesses, law enforcement, faith-based institutions, parent groups, the juvenile justice system, and the media [29]. This example illustrates how integrating local leadership, community participation, and evidence-informed policymaking can serve as a robust model for implementing virtual clinics in underserved regions. A coordinated approach—linking policy commitment at the top with participatory action at the community level—can ensure the sustainability, scalability, and social legitimacy of virtual healthcare innovations.

In the financing domain, implementing virtual clinics in underserved regions faces significant challenges, particularly high technology start-up costs and low reimbursement rates, which hinder both initial adoption and long-term sustainability [22, 31–34]. Moreover, studies have identified persistent issues related to sustainable funding and social protection in rural communities, where short-term financing schemes or uncertainty about continued support often compromise service continuity [28, 35, 36]. Funding models with restrictive eligibility criteria further exacerbate these challenges, as they frequently fail to account for the unique realities of rural healthcare delivery—such as long travel distances, dispersed populations, and workforce recruitment and retention difficulties [35, 36]. Conversely, facilitators like strong government support and flexible, long-term financing mechanisms have proven effective in promoting the successful implementation and expansion of virtual clinics [20, 37]. The World Health Organization also highlights the importance of pooling financial resources and risks to overcome funding limitations in rural and remote settings [38]. Additionally, the growing global market for virtual clinics—with projected annual growth rates between 13% and 27%—creates emerging financial opportunities to scale innovative healthcare delivery models [39]. Overall, aligning financing policies with the operational realities of underserved regions is critical to ensure the continuity, equity, and effectiveness of virtual clinic services.

The health workforce plays a pivotal role in the successful implementation of virtual clinics in underserved regions. However, it also represents one of the most significant sources of challenge. Commonly reported barriers include provider resistance [34, 40] and insufficient training or awareness regarding virtual care delivery [34, 41]. Such resistance often stems from the need to adapt traditional service workflows, alongside attitudinal and cultural constraints among providers. Additionally, a limited understanding of the efficiency, effectiveness, legal, and ethical implications of virtual care further contributes to hesitation in adopting these models [26].

To overcome these barriers, targeted workforce development must be prioritized as both a solution and a catalyst for broader adoption. Early integration of change management strategies during the planning and implementation stages can help address resistance and ensure smoother transitions. Furthermore, comprehensive and context-specific training programs—particularly those designed for Indigenous and underserved populations [42, 43]—can improve competency, reduce negative perceptions, and foster greater acceptance among healthcare professionals.

For long-term sustainability, it is essential to embed telehealth competencies into the education and training curricula of future clinicians [44]. Complementing these initiatives, investment in foundational infrastructure—including workflow redesign and ongoing professional development—will support the seamless integration of virtual clinics into a comprehensive, adaptive, and resilient health service model [45].

Across countries of varying income levels, one of the most widely reported challenges to implementing virtual clinics is weak digital infrastructure, particularly poor internet connectivity [46–49]. This challenge cuts across several of the WHO health system building blocks but is especially critical for enabling the delivery of virtual clinic services. Infrastructure investment has consistently been identified as a key facilitator [50–52], directly addressing connectivity gaps, strengthening technological capacity, and supporting the sustainable implementation of virtual care in underserved regions.

Recent evidence adds further nuance to these findings. Arighi et al. reported that a lack of devices—such as computers, phones, or tablets—with internet connectivity, combined with poor connection quality, was a major contributor to failed virtual care [53]. Similarly, Alam et al. found that access to broadband internet services significantly increased the uptake of virtual care [54]. These studies underscore that digital readiness extends beyond connectivity alone; it also encompasses access to functional devices and reliable service quality.

In many low-income countries, virtual clinics present a promising avenue for improving healthcare access. However, bureaucratic constraints and inadequate infrastructure remain significant barriers [55]. Reliable connectivity in such settings depends on fundamental resources such as stable electricity, sufficient mobile network coverage, and accessible internet services. Where these resources are lacking, innovative alternatives—for instance, solar-powered systems—can offer cost-effective and environmentally sustainable solutions [56]. Evidence from Cambodia demonstrates that low-bandwidth telemedicine can overcome poor network connectivity, enabling service delivery despite infrastructural limitations [57]. Likewise, simplifying technological requirements and leveraging widely used commercial platforms, such as multifunctional instant messaging applications, have proven effective in facilitating virtual clinic adoption [58].

Further regional and economic analysis reveals how contextual characteristics shape virtual clinic implementation across countries. The results indicate that income level strongly influences the nature of barriers encountered: high-income countries tend to face governance and organizational challenges, whereas middle- and low-income countries struggle primarily with infrastructural limitations and low digital literacy. These findings highlight that the feasibility and sustainability of virtual clinics depend heavily on local economic capacity, technological readiness, and geographic accessibility. Therefore, to ensure equitable and context-sensitive implementation, tailored strategies must be developed to reflect each country’s specific conditions. Integrating economic and geographical perspectives into planning enriches the global understanding of virtual clinic development and strengthens the evidence base for informed policy design in underserved regions.

In remote and rural areas, inadequate technical infrastructure continues to undermine the potential of virtual clinics [59, 60]. Without robust communication networks and reliable internet access, healthcare providers and patients face major barriers to conducting virtual consultations, remote monitoring, and other digital health interactions. Addressing these gaps requires a multi-pronged approach—including sustained infrastructure investment, contextually adapted technology, innovative low-cost solutions, and long-term capacity-building initiatives.

Within the Health Information Systems domain, two interrelated challenges persist across all income settings: data privacy and security concerns [40, 61, 62] and low digital literacy [20, 51, 63]. Together, these factors influence not only the functionality of virtual clinics but also public trust and equitable access.

Data privacy forms the foundation of trust in virtual care. When patients lack confidence in data protection, they may hesitate to share sensitive information, jeopardizing both service quality and continuity. Weak enforcement of privacy standards, outdated cybersecurity frameworks, and fragmented data governance structures exacerbate these risks. Effective mitigation requires robust policy frameworks, secure technical infrastructure, and adherence to international best practices to ensure patient confidentiality and institutional integrity.

In parallel, digital literacy remains a critical determinant of virtual clinic accessibility. Older adults, marginalized groups, and individuals unfamiliar with digital tools are disproportionately excluded from these services. Evidence supports this pattern: Ernsting et al. found that mHealth app users exhibited significantly higher eHealth literacy levels than non-users [64], while another study demonstrated that gains in eHealth literacy were associated with increased engagement in online health information-seeking (β = 0.03, 95% CI 0.00–0.06) [65]. Digital literacy, therefore, functions both as a gateway to virtual care and a catalyst for deeper digital health engagement.

To ensure inclusivity and trust, interventions must address both data security and literacy simultaneously. Integrating targeted, context-specific digital literacy training into service delivery can enhance user confidence and competence. Platform design should also reflect accessibility principles—using simplified navigation, culturally relevant language, and multimodal content formats. At the same time, strengthening data governance and cybersecurity reinforces the trust needed for sustained utilization. If either dimension is neglected, virtual clinics risk creating a digital trust gap, where services exist but remain underused due to either limited skills or lack of confidence in system security. Conversely, a dual focus on capacity-building and data protection can promote adoption, improve patient outcomes, and support the long-term sustainability of virtual care across diverse settings.

Within the Service Delivery domain, a key concern in the implementation of virtual clinics is the limited capacity to perform physical examinations [33, 61, 66], alongside the potential risk of reduced quality or continuity of care when relying exclusively on virtual formats [33, 67]. To address these limitations, the adoption of hybrid care models—which combine face-to-face consultations with virtual clinic services—has emerged as an effective strategy [52, 68].These models allow clinicians to perform necessary in-person assessments while retaining the accessibility and efficiency of remote care. Evidence from recent studies supports this approach, demonstrating that hybrid care pathways can sustain treatment quality, enhance patient satisfaction, and improve service continuity, particularly in the management of chronic diseases where regular monitoring is essential.

Beyond individual service delivery models, contextual factors at the national level also shape the implementation of virtual clinics. Research shows that the type and severity of challenges vary significantly according to a country’s income level. In high-income countries, governance and workforce-related barriers are most frequently reported, whereas in low- and middle-income countries, infrastructural limitations and low digital literacy are more prevalent. These differences underscore the importance of tailoring interventions to align with each country’s specific capacities, technological readiness, and health system context.

However, as most existing evidence originates from high-income settings, these findings must be interpreted with caution, as their generalizability to resource-constrained environments remains limited. International reviews further indicate that certain barriers—such as inadequate internet infrastructure—are widespread and persistent, posing challenges across all income levels. Taken together, the evidence highlights that the successful implementation and long-term sustainability of virtual clinics depend on a systems-based approach. Such an approach should address infrastructure, digital literacy, workforce readiness, and managerial capacity in an integrated manner, while remaining sensitive to local conditions and economic contexts.

The methodological limitations of this scoping review include factors that may affect the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the results. Among these limitations is the restriction of the search to articles published in English, which may have led to the exclusion of valuable studies published in other languages. Additionally, despite efforts to cover a wide range of sources, some gray literature or unpublished studies may not have been identified due to limited access. Moreover, the significant diversity in methodologies, geographical locations, and definitions related to virtual clinics and underserved areas across the included studies has posed challenges in analyzing and synthesizing findings, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results. Although efforts were made to provide a broad temporal coverage, most of the existing studies have focused on high-income countries, which may not adequately represent the experiences of countries with fewer resources.

Conclusion

Based on the methodology and findings of the scoping review, a logical conclusion can be drawn that the development and implementation of virtual clinics in underserved areas face multiple challenges that must be carefully addressed. These challenges include weak digital infrastructure, financial and resource limitations, cultural and social issues, concerns regarding privacy and data security, and resistance from the workforce in adopting these technologies. The success of virtual clinics depends on supportive policymaking, proper training of the workforce, and adapting technology to local conditions. Additionally, adopting hybrid models (combining in-person and virtual services) can help maintain the quality of care and increase acceptance. Ultimately, achieving desirable outcomes requires a comprehensive approach tailored to the specific conditions of each region, considering all infrastructural, financial, cultural, and educational aspects.
