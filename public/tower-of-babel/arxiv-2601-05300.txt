Time : Temporally Intelligent Meta-reasoning Engine for Context Triggered Explicit Reasoning
Susmit Das

Abstract
Reasoning oriented large language models often expose explicit “thinking” as long, turn-global traces at the start of every response, either always on or toggled externally at inference time. While useful for arithmetic, programming, and problem solving, this design is costly, blurs claim level auditability, and cannot re-trigger explicit reasoning once the model begins presenting. Dialogue models are also largely blind to temporal structure, treating replies after seconds and replies after weeks as equivalent unless time is stated in text. We introduce Time , the Temporally Intelligent Meta-reasoning Engine, a behavioral alignment framework that treats explicit reasoning as a context sensitive resource driven by discourse and temporal cues. Time augments dialogue with optional ISO 8601 <time> tags, tick turns that represent silent gaps, and short <think> blocks that can appear anywhere in a reply. A four-phase curriculum including a small, maximally diverse full-batch alignment step trains Qwen3 dense models to invoke brief, in-place reasoning bursts and keep user facing text compact. We evaluate with Time Bench, a temporally grounded dialogue benchmark probing chronology, commonsense under gaps and offsets, anomaly detection, and continuity. Across 4B to 32B scales, Time improves Time Bench scores over base Qwen3 in both thinking and no-thinking modes while reducing reasoning tokens by about an order of magnitude. Our training data and code are available at https://github.com/The-Coherence-Initiative/TIME , and Time Bench is available at https://github.com/The-Coherence-Initiative/TIMEBench .



1
Introduction

Reasoning oriented language models increasingly make reasoning explicit by exposing thinking traces, often as chain-of-thought style text. In many systems, this reasoning appears as a long block at the start of each reply, either always on, or controlled externally through inference flags. The pattern boosts accuracy on arithmetic, programming, problem solving, and multi step planning, and it provides a practical interface for inspecting rationale when mechanistic interpretability is unavailable. Yet it also turns explicit reasoning into a blunt instrument.

Front loaded thinking has three recurring limitations. First, it is expensive and verbose, because the model emits many reasoning tokens even for trivial or low risk requests. Second, it weakens claim level auditability. A single monologue can loosely justify many later statements, so the mapping from an individual claim to its justification is often indirect. Third, it is not reactive. Once the model begins presenting, it cannot cleanly re enter a thinking phase to re check assumptions or re anchor itself if uncertainty arises mid answer.

A better target is a reasoning policy that is closer to human thought: short bursts, inserted where needed, and re-triggered when new cues appear. Achieving this requires signals that indicate latent state change, not only a task difficulty estimate. Temporal transitions are one of the most common such signals in real interactions. Plans expire, priorities change, and users return after long gaps. Yet unless time is stated explicitly, standard dialogue models treat a reply after two seconds and a reply after two weeks as the same token sequence.

We present
Time
(Temporally Intelligent Meta-reasoning Engine), a behavioral alignment framework that equips reasoning models with context sensitive explicit thinking.
Time
introduces three lightweight primitives: optional
<time>
tags on turns, optional short
<think>
blocks that may appear anywhere in a response, and tick turns that contain only time metadata to represent silence and passage of time. These primitives model temporality explicitly during training and evaluation, but timestamps are not required for the learned policy to operate: the model can still trigger brief reasoning from purely textual cues when temporal metadata is absent.

Because this behavior is rare in existing corpora, we align it with a four-phase curriculum on the Qwen3 family from 4B to 32B parameters. Early phases teach the model to parse the primitives and to keep reasoning bursts short and well delimited. A final phase uses a small but maximally diverse full-batch alignment set whose only invariant is contextually triggered reasoning keyed to temporal and discourse cues. This pushes the model toward a policy rather than a template, while reducing the tendency to collapse back into verbose, turn-global traces.

To measure reasoning competence under uncertainty and transitions as well as reasoning deployment, we introduce
Time
Bench, a temporally grounded dialogue evaluation suite that probes chronological reconstruction, temporal commonsense under gaps and timezone shifts, detection of invalid or implausible timestamps, and continuity across long silences. Beyond task success,
Time
Bench logs where and how often
<think>
blocks are used, enabling analysis of whether models reason only when the situation demands it.

Related Work

Temporal Cognition and Time-Aware Modeling

Prior work on time in language models primarily treats time as
content
: timestamped facts, event ordering, and knowledge that changes across time. Timestamp-conditioned modeling such as
Time-Aware LMs

[
5
]
conditions generation on temporal indices to better handle drift and time-sensitive knowledge. Complementary analyses study temporal non-stationarity and
temporal generalization
, showing that performance degrades when models are evaluated on future slices beyond their training distribution
[
10
]
. More recent evaluation frameworks explicitly probe time awareness over large event collections, for example TimeShift-style methods that test recall and calibration under temporal constraints
[
7
]
.

Benchmarks have also expanded from synthetic temporal arithmetic toward broader temporal competence. ChronoSense evaluates temporal relations and temporal arithmetic, while TimE and EvolveBench test temporal reasoning under real-world dynamics, including dialogue settings, invalid timestamp handling, and temporal misalignment between inputs and queries
[
8
,
18
,
15
]
. In parallel, retrieval and structured knowledge approaches incorporate temporal graphs to represent evolving facts, including temporal GraphRAG-style systems for time-sensitive retrieval and updates
[
6
]
. These lines strengthen temporal factuality, ordering, and retrieval, but still focus on time as part of the
world state
. In contrast,
Time
uses elapsed time and temporal discontinuities as
interaction state
: a ubiquitous cue for latent context shifts that should trigger explicit reasoning, re-grounding, or brevity changes.

Explicit Reasoning Traces and Reasoning Control

Chain-of-thought prompting
[
14
]
improves multi-step task performance and provides explicit reasoning traces that are often treated as operational explanations. However, a growing body of work shows that such traces are not reliably faithful to the computations that drive final answers, limiting their use as audit artefacts and motivating methods that measure or improve faithfulness
[
11
,
13
,
2
,
3
]
. Beyond faithfulness, turn-global reasoning also creates practical issues: cost, latency, and weak claim-level attribution when a long rationale must justify many downstream statements.

Most deployed systems still treat explicit reasoning as a mode rather than a policy. Hybrid reasoners allow inference-time toggles, but the model itself does not decide when to reason. Recent work begins to learn this decision, for example by training hybrid-reasoning models to select whether to think, often using reinforcement learning or controller objectives
[
9
]
. Other approaches control
how much
a model reasons via length-conditioned policy optimization
[
1
]
. Orthogonally, methods such as DOTS search over reasoning action trajectories, and RL-based interleaving trains models to alternate thinking and answering to reduce time-to-first-token and improve efficiency
[
17
,
16
]
. These advances show progress toward adaptive reasoning, but are typically framed around task difficulty, length budgets, or action planning, and often remain effectively turn-global at inference time.
Time
targets a different axis: short, in-place reasoning bursts triggered by situational cues, including temporal transitions and discourse discontinuities, enabling mid-response re-anchoring when uncertainty or drift emerges.

Ticks are not required at inference; they serve as a modeling primitive and a stress-test for reasoning policies under temporal discontinuity.

Methods

4.1
Curriculum and Alignment Protocol

We align
Time
via a staged, curriculum-based supervised fine-tuning (SFT) procedure on the
Qwen3
family at four sizes (32B, 14B, 8B, 4B). Training uses
QLoRA

[
4
]
on a single
NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell (96GB)
. The objective is a
reasoning invocation policy
: emit
<think>
traces only when cues warrant them, keep traces brief and well-delimited, and allow re-triggering later in the same response if warranted.

Why Qwen3 and why curriculum.

Qwen3 is a strong hybrid reasoner, but it is mode-bound: thinking mode tends toward long, front-loaded
<think>
blocks, while no-thinking mode emits none. External toggles do not teach the model when to reason or where to place reasoning.
Time
targets a policy that is optional, brief, and reactive to cues such as uncertainty, instruction conflicts, and time gaps. Direct SFT onto the final behavior is unstable and often collapses back to templated or verbose traces, so we introduce prerequisites first and then consolidate with a small, high-entropy alignment set.

Shared configuration for Phases 1 to 3.

Phases 1 to 3 share a fixed recipe: LoRA rank 32 on projection layers (
\alpha=32
, dropout
0.05
); AdamW-8bit with learning rate
2\times 10^{-5}
and linear decay; effective batch size 32; gradient checkpointing; and 3 epochs per phase. Each phase replays 25% of prior data. About 5% of samples include system prompts describing the protocol with varied wording.

Synthetic data for the first three phases was generated using a template-guided process seeded by hand-curated examples on GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Flash, followed by automated sanity checks. The stylistic variety is intentionally narrow; these phases teach parsing of primitives, local placement of reasoning, and length control.

Phase 1: Structural Seeding

(2,188 train / 387 test)

Single-turn prompts teach the markup:
<time>
metadata plus short
<think>
bursts for compact intermediate steps. The goal is to normalize brief, well-delimited traces and weaken the preference for long, monolithic reasoning.

Phase 2: Temporal Exposure

(4,745 train / 838 test
\rightarrow
5,291 / 935 with replay)

Two-turn dialogues introduce time gaps and the first tick events. The model must condition on temporal metadata to revise assumptions and re-establish context after silence. This phase also introduces early pressure against reflexive verbosity, requiring the model to infer when silence is sufficient or when a concise contextual update is more appropriate.

Phase 3: Contextual Modulation

(4,147 train / 732 test
\rightarrow
5,878 / 1,039 with replay)

Multi-turn dialogues teach suppression and re-triggering of
<think>
blocks while adjusting tone and verbosity. Tick frequency increases (about 33%), encouraging the model to rely on temporal and non-temporal cues. This phase creates behavioral headroom for the final alignment.

Phase 4 is the decisive alignment step. We use a small but maximally diverse set whose only shared property is the target policy:
<think>

Why 128 examples.

The goal is to align a policy, not to expand knowledge. Full-batch updates require the effective batch size to equal the dataset size, and this behavior has a narrow stability window: over-optimization leads to looping,
<think>
bleed, and style collapse. A compact, high-entropy batch concentrates gradients on the intended invariant while remaining stable.

Why It Works

Phase 4 is structured so that the only gradient-aligned invariant is the reasoning policy itself, encoded through a maximally diverse batch of 128 samples. The model must detect contextual cues such as elapsed time, tick events, contradictions, or discourse shifts, decide whether to emit a bounded
<think>
burst and where to place it, and keep the user-facing presentation concise. Unlike mini-batch training, where sampling variance can allow incidental correlations (e.g., topic or formatting artifacts) to dominate updates, full-batch alignment over the entire diverse set induces a regularizing effect that suppresses such spurious signals, yielding the final checkpoints used for evaluation, henceforth referred to as
Time

is a 77-scenario benchmark for

We use temporality because it is one of the most frequent drivers of latent context transitions in real interaction: deadlines move, users disappear and return, assumptions go stale, and coordination depends on time and timezone. These transitions are often invisible in token-only chat logs unless time is represented explicitly.
Time

Scenario design.

Each scenario is a short, naturalistic dialogue thread with timestamps per user query; the model generates only the final assistant turn. Every scenario is paired with a category-specific objective that defines the behavior being tested (e.g., detecting implausible timestamps or adjusting assumptions after a long gap). Objectives focus on everyday temporal plausibility rather than rare trivia, making scores meaningful for any LLM, including non-reasoners, fixed reasoners, and hybrid models in either thinking or no-thinking mode.

Protocol.

Each model is evaluated with
10 trials per scenario
using PCG64-derived seeds (770 runs total). Trials are scored with
binary objectives
(0 or 1) by a third-party LLM judge that sees only the model response and the objective, not the original prompt or timestamps. We aggregate trial scores to scenario scores (mean of 10), then category scores (mean over scenarios, scaled to %), and an overall
Time
Bench score (mean over categories). We estimate
95% confidence intervals
via stratified bootstrapping (10,000 resamples) by resampling scenario scores within each category and recomputing all aggregates.

Behavioral instrumentation.

In addition to correctness,
Time
Bench enables a structural audit of generation. We extract: whether
<think>
appears, its position (start, mid, end), mean reasoning and output token counts, light versus heavy markdown usage, format bleed, and degeneracy indicators including infinite repetition. This second view lets us study how context-triggered thinking policies change temporal commonsense performance and which structural shifts co-occur with gains.

Baselines.

The no-thinking baseline handles local cues well, with strong timezone sensitivity and reasonable short-range retrospection, but fails on transition-oriented categories such as flow anomaly detection and gap awareness and misses most invalid timestamps. Enabling full thinking improves scrutiny with significant rise in invalid time detection, yet transition categories remain near floor. Structurally, the thinking baseline behaves like a classic chain-of-thought model: runs contain a single front-loaded trace, averaging about 911 reasoning tokens and 1,573 total output tokens, with a high degeneracy rate of 18.18%.

Phase 1 (structural seeding).

Phase 1 primarily teaches the model to operate within the new syntax. Scores increase modestly, but the reasoning policy remains unchanged. Almost all runs still use a single preamble-style trace, with long explicit reasoning (about 804 tokens) and large outputs (about 1,435 tokens). Degeneracy drops to 13.90%, largely due to reduced formatting errors.

Phase 2 (temporal exposure).

Phase 2 marks the first point where diagnostic and structural metrics shift together. Categories probing reasoning under transitions and anomalous flows, including adaptivity, flow anomaly detection, and gap awareness, rise sharply, indicating that temporal discontinuities are now treated as informative cues. At the same time, the explicit reasoning budget collapses to about 77 tokens per run and output length to about 362 tokens. Mid-turn
<think>
blocks appear in roughly 29% of runs, and degeneracy drops to 4.68%. Explicit reasoning is no longer a fixed preamble but a resource invoked by context.

Phase 3 (contextual modulation).

Phase 3 emphasizes restraint and stability. Chronological retrospection and adaptivity remain strong while the reasoning budget falls further to about 53 tokens and output length to about 295 tokens. Degeneracy reaches its minimum at 0.26%, and mid-turn reasoning becomes more common than front-loaded traces. However, some transition-sensitive categories lose part of their Phase 2 gains, suggesting that suppression pressure can prevent useful re-anchoring when subtle discontinuities warrant it.

Time
(Phase 4 alignment).

Final alignment in
Time
-32B restores robustness on transition and anomaly categories while preserving intermittent reasoning. It achieves the best overall score, uses only about 84 reasoning tokens and 333 output tokens per run, and emits
<think>
blocks in roughly 81% of runs, now predominantly mid-turn (75.6%) rather than front-loaded (24.1%). Degeneracy remains low at 3.64%. This profile matches the intended behavior: explicit reasoning is neither always-on nor absent, but concentrated where temporal or contextual shifts make commonsense reasoning most brittle. 6
Discussion

The results support viewing explicit reasoning as a learned control policy rather than a fixed decoding style. Under
Time
, Qwen3 shifts from long, always on chains of thought to compact reasoning bursts that are mostly triggered by state changes, such as looming deadlines, long silences, or inconsistent timestamps. The strongest
Time
Bench gains appear in categories that require detecting such shifts and adapting the response, which suggests that the model is tracking conversational state instead of only recalling temporal facts.

Structural metrics reinforce this interpretation. Mid turn think blocks, much smaller reasoning token budgets, and lower degeneracy show that the model is more selective about when it spends explicit computation and more careful about how it separates thought from presentation. This selective allocation matters operationally: it reduces latency and token cost when fast responses are needed, while still enabling explicit reasoning to surface when the interaction becomes uncertain, discontinuous, or high stakes. The same behavior is well aligned with real time and agentic settings, where an assistant must interleave quick actions with occasional deliberate reflection rather than committing to a single global reasoning mode.

Importantly, the learned controller is not tied to explicit timestamps at inference time. When temporal metadata is present it is used as an additional cue, but the policy also operates on purely textual context and task description. Together, these observations point toward a design pattern where compact alignment sets and behaviorally targeted curricula shape how large models allocate and expose reasoning in response to changing context.

7
Conclusion

Time
introduces a lightweight framework for contextual reasoning in dialogue models. By combining a simple protocol for timestamps, ticks, and optional think blocks with a staged curriculum and a diversity driven full-batch alignment step, we convert a fixed chain-of-thought backbone into a family of models that deploy reasoning selectively, in short bursts, and in response to state changes rather than by default.

Time
Bench provides the matching evaluation lens. It shows consistent gains in temporal commonsense across seven diagnostic categories and reveals the accompanying structural shift toward contextual reasoning, smaller thinking budgets, and fewer degenerate outputs.
Time
and
Time
Bench together illustrate how carefully designed alignment interventions can change not only how large models answer, but how and when they choose to explicitly reason.

Limitations

While
Time
provides a practical recipe for context triggered reasoning, its scope and claims are deliberately narrow.

All experiments use dense hybrid reasoners from the Qwen3 family, which already support both instruct style and explicit reasoning modes. This makes them a natural first target for learning a finer grained reasoning policy, but it also limits generality. We do not claim that the same curriculum and alignment recipe will transfer without modification to purely instruct models such as Llama 3 Instruct variants that have not been pre trained for explicit thinking traces. In such models the bottleneck may be the reasoning substrate rather than the controller, and additional pre training or larger supervised traces may be required before a policy like
Time
is effective. We also have not tested
Time
on mixture of experts hybrid reasoners such as GLM 4.5 Air, where routing and capacity considerations may require different adapter placement or larger alignment sets.

Time
Bench focuses on temporal commonsense and conversational robustness under state transitions. It is not a task benchmark and does not measure domain specific skills such as mathematical problem solving, coding, or multi step tool use. In such settings, traditional more verbose reasoning frameworks, including long chain-of-thought or tree structured methods, may still provide higher raw task accuracy than the short bursts encouraged by
Time
. We target situations where compact, intermittent traces are preferred over maximal performance, for example in interactive or resource constrained deployments, and therefore do not evaluate
Time
on standard coding, math, or tool benchmarks.

Our alignment protocol is entirely supervised and adapter based. The final behavior is induced through a small, hand curated full-batch dataset that encodes a particular style of intermittent reasoning and temporal sensitivity. We do not explore reinforcement learning or bandit style objectives that explicitly optimise a trade off between accuracy, latency, and reasoning cost, nor do we combine
Time
with reward models that penalise unfaithful or gratuitous traces. The 128 example full-batch alignment stage is also a methodological choice. We argue that its small size and high diversity help isolate a single behavioral invariant, but we only demonstrate this on Qwen3 and under one protocol; other base models or more complex settings may require larger or differently structured alignment sets.

Time
Bench is a new benchmark created alongside the framework. Although we separate training and evaluation data and organise scenarios by diagnostic category, the benchmark is not independent in the sense of being designed by a separate group and covers only a subset of time dependent reasoning phenomena. Future work should compare
Time
tuned models on other temporal benchmarks and on general purpose evaluation suites.

All
Time
Bench scores are obtained with an LLM judge rather than human annotators. The judge is blind to prompts and timestamps and we use binary scoring, multiple trials, and bootstrapped confidence intervals to reduce variance, but this does not remove all potential biases of the judging model. We do not report human studies on perceived faithfulness, usefulness, or trust in the produced traces.

Our experiments are conducted in English and we do not investigate multilingual behavior. There is no guarantee that the same curriculum and alignment signals would transfer to languages with different tense systems, temporal conventions, or discourse norms. We also do not evaluate safety, bias, or fairness effects of the new reasoning policy. While the method reduces some forms of degeneracy and verbosity, it is not a safety mechanism and should not be treated as such.

Finally,
Time
addresses only one dimension of model behavior, namely when and how explicit reasoning is surfaced in response to contextual cues. It does not provide mechanistic interpretability, and it does not constrain or verify the internal computation that leads to the traces. It should be viewed as a step toward more auditable models, not as a complete solution to transparency or accountability in high stakes deployments.

Appendix C
Training Details

This appendix describes the concrete training pipeline used to obtain all
Time
checkpoints. Our goal was to induce a new reasoning policy under realistic hardware constraints rather than to retrain full models. All experiments were therefore designed to be reproducible on a single workstation and to reuse the same optimization scaffold across phases whenever possible, so that differences in behavior can be attributed to data and curriculum rather than to hidden hyperparameter drift.

For each Qwen3 size, we keep the backbone weights frozen and train lightweight adapters with QLoRA. The same adapter configuration is used for all curriculum phases, and adapters are reinitialised when moving from one phase to the next. Phases 1 to 3 share an identical optimization setup and differ only in data distribution and curriculum structure. Phase 4 keeps the same adapter placement but switches to a full-batch regime over a small, high entropy alignment set. This separation lets us isolate which changes are driven by structural seeding, temporal exposure, and contextual modulation, and which are due to the final convergence step.

We also keep decoding and quantisation choices aligned with realistic deployment conditions. Training uses 4 bit quantisation with low rank adapters and gradient checkpointing to fit 32B backbones comfortably on a single high memory GPU. Inference for evaluation uses uniform decoding parameters across all models, and separate ablations compare quantised and full precision runs. Together, these constraints make the reported setting reproducible and close to what a practitioner could implement without large scale infrastructure.
