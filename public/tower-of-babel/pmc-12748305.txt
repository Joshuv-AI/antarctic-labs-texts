Dietary threonine levels: effect on growth performance and gut organ measurements of indigenous Boschveld chickens
Authors: Tebogo Mack Sekgotodi, Jones Wilfred Ng’ambi, Monnye Mabelebele, Tlou Grace Manyelo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The study was done to determine the effect of various threonine inclusion levels in the diets on the growth performance and gut organ measurements of indigenous Boschveld chickens aged 49 days. A total of 200-day-old chicks, weighing 30 ± 5 g per bird, were randomly allocated to five dietary treatment levels in a completely randomized design, replicated four times with 10 chicks per replicate. Growth performance and gut organ measurements data were analysed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4 and subjected to Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Threonine inclusion levels (4, 7.5, 8, 8.5, and 9 g/kg DM) had an effect (p < 0.05) on feed intakes, body weight gains, feed conversion ratios, duodenum pH, GIT, ileum jejunum, duodenum, large intestine lengths, crop, gizzard, ileum jejunum, duodenum, and caeca weights of the birds. Threonine inclusion levels had no effect (p > 0.05) on crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, caeca, large intestine pH values, and caeca lengths of the birds. Duodenum weights of the birds were optimized at a dietary threonine level of 8.00 g/kg. In conclusion, variables measured were optimized at different dietary threonine levels. This has implications for diet formulation for the chickens. Thus, there is a need for further studies on the subject to ascertain the present findings.

Introduction

Smallholder farmers in Southern Africa and many parts of the developing world generally rely on chicken meat to meet their dietary protein requirements (Azimu et al. 2018). Most of these farmers breed and keep indigenous chickens, which are mostly tolerant of local diseases and parasites; moreover, they provide huge economic benefits and income for rural communities. The sudden increase in the demand for natural or organic meat could have influenced smallholder farmers to consider farming with indigenous chickens because these chickens need minimal use of additives and chemicals (Selaledi et al. 2021). The indigenous chickens are known to be economically, socially, and culturally important to the people of Africa and other developing countries, especially those from poor communities (Manyelo et al. 2020). Although they are associated with poor productivity, most consumers prefer their meat flavour (Manyelo et al. 2020). However, conventional protein sources, such as soybean and fishmeal, which are normally used in poultry diets, are expensive, thus, there is a need for the search for alternative sources of protein (Sajid et al. 2023). Due to high feed costs, NRC (1994) suggested lower dietary nutrient levels for the optimal performance of slow-growing chicken breeds compared with those of fast-growing broiler chickens. Slow-growing indigenous female Venda chickens had lower dietary threonine levels for optimal performance than fast-growing broiler chickens (Ng’ambi et al. 2017). The authors also observed that the threonine requirements for optimal performance were dependent on the chicken parameters, i.e., feed intake, feed conversion ratio (FCR), growth rate, and carcass traits, among others. Threonine is an essential amino acid in chickens as their body cannot synthesize threonine de novo (Pokoo-Aikins et al. 2022; Lehninger et al. 2000; NRC 1994). Thus, dietary threonine levels are important for the optimal performance of broiler chickens as deficiency of this amino acid may result in increased FCR, decreased weight gain and nitrogen retention, low carcass characteristics, poor intestinal morphology, weak immunity, and slow functional development (Qaisrani et al. 2018; Manyelo and Ng’ambi 2024). Chickens fed a diet deficient in threonine perform poorly (Kidd et al. 2004; Dozier et al. 2000). Threonine is known to be the most important amino acid for the maintenance of the intestinal mucosa as it is used in the synthesis of mucin that covers the surface area of the mucosa (Ahmed et al. 2020). According to Debnath et al. (2020), threonine is also known to maintain intestinal strength and growth in addition to providing energy for normal intestinal functions. An increase in supplemental threonine levels has been reported to provide sufficient concentrations for the high turnover of mucosal tissues (Rezaeipour et al. 2012). Increasing the dietary L-threonine improved the morphological aspects of the small intestine of chickens, resulting in a greater development rate compared with chickens fed threonine-deficient diets, which exhibited decreased nutrient absorption in the digestive tract (Wasman 2022). However, dietary threonine can negatively impact feed intake and growth performance by disrupting the amino acid balance, leading to reduced growth, feed efficiency. Increases in the number of heterophils are an essential measure of immune system function, and threonine increases the globulin levels in the blood and lymphocytes (Azzam et al. 2011). The growth rate, carcass yield, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) of chickens fed a diet low in threonine are low (Kidd et al. 2004; Dozier et al. 2001). Amino acids are vital for both growth and meat tenderness and provide key nutritional value and significantly contribute to the taste and flavour of meat (Ma et al. 2020). According to Kidd and Kerr (1996), the impact of dietary threonine on chicken productivity has been inconsistent, ranging from 5.8 to 7.9 g/kg DM. Currently, data on the appropriate threonine requirements for genotypes of slow-growing chickens is limited and inconclusive. As a result, it is challenging to formulate diets with threonine levels that meet the needs of these slow-growing chickens for maximum output. The study was done to determine the effect of various threonine inclusion levels in the diets on the growth performance and gut organ measurements of indigenous Boschveld chickens aged 49 days.

Materials and methods

Experimental diets, designs, and procedures for chickens’ housing and management

This study was done at the University of Limpopo (latitude 27.550S and longitude 24.770E). The study was ethically cleared by the University of Limpopo Animal Research Ethics Committee (ULAREC) (Certificate Number: AREC/20/2023: PG), and all methods used are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. The length of the bird-rearing period was 49 days. A total of 200-day-old chicks, indigenous Boschveld chickens (30 ± 5 g per bird) in a completely randomized design replicated four times with 10 chicks per replicate were used in this study. The experimental diets were isocaloric and isonitrogenous, and they were also made to fulfil the necessary nutritional standards (NRC 1994), but with different threonine inclusion levels of 4, 7.5, 8, 8.5, or 9 g/kg DM of diets (Tables 1 and 2), where the threonine level is the control diet. An open-sided structure was used to house the chickens. For proper ventilation long axis was situated along an east-west direction in a one m2 pen of wire mesh. Natural (V-shaped windows) and artificial means (supplying fans) were used as ventilation mechanisms to enhance the chickens’ microclimatic conditions and to maintain the natural convection act. Moreover, the house had temperatures maintained at 32 to 35 °C for the first week and gradually decreased by 3 °C as chicks get older during the starter and 18 to 21 °C at the grower phase. Two weeks before the start of the experiment, paraformaldehyde was used to disinfect the poultry house. The bedding for the chickens was prepared using wood-shaved sawdust. Disinfectants were used to wash and clean feeders and drinkers daily in the morning before being used. The chicks were vaccinated against infectious bronchitis and Newcastle virus disease using live attenuated virus vaccine (Nobilis® IB 4–91, log10 3, 6 EID50/dose) on days 7, 18, and 28, chickens were given live lentogenic virus vaccine (PESTIKAL 1000 dose, Lasota ≥ 106.0 EID50). Feed and water were provided ad libitum, 24 h of lighting for the first 2–3 days to help chicks find food and water, and gradually reduced to 20 h per day, and 4 h of darkness were provided (NACC 2010).

Data collection

The body weight gain of each chicken was determined at the start of the experiment; thereafter, weekly weights were taken. The growth rate of the chickens was determined using their body weight gains. Daily feed intake was measured by calculating the difference between the total weight of feed offered and the weight of feed leftovers, and the difference was divided by the total number of chickens per pen. The feed conversion ratio was calculated as the total feed consumed divided by the body weight gain of the birds (McDonald et al. 2010). No mortality during the experimental period was recorded.

At the age of 49, three chickens from each replicate were slaughtered using the cervical dislocation method. The chicken’s digestive tract was carefully removed from the abdomen without causing tissue damage, and the weight of the organs was measured using an electronic scale. The dimension of the small and large intestines was measured with a measuring tape. The pH of the ileum, crop, gizzard, and caecum digesta was measured with a pH meter (Crison, Basic 20 pH Meter).

Chemical analysis

The dry matter and nitrogen contents of the diets, refusals, faeces, and meat samples were assessed in accordance with the AOAC’s guidelines (AOAC 2012). An adiabatic bomb calorimeter was used to determine the gross energy of the diet and excreta samples. Ion-exchange chromatography was used to analyse the threonine and other amino acid levels of the feed samples (HPLC, University of Limpopo, South Africa). The calcium and sodium contents were analysed by the methods of (AOAC 2012) using the Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (Labtronics). There was a calculation of the diets’ apparent metabolisable energy (ME) content (AOAC 2012).

Data analysis

Data on performance and gut organ measurements of indigenous Boschveld chickens were subjected to the one-way analysis of variance using SAS version 9.4. A - was used to test the significance of the difference between treatment means (P < 0.05), the treatment means were separated using a least significant difference (LSD) test at 5% level of probability (SAS 2020). Results

Results of the effect of threonine inclusion levels on feed intake (g DM/bird/day), body weight gain (g/bird/day), and FCR (g feed intake/g body weight gain) of the birds aged 1–49 days are presented in Table 3. Threonine inclusion levels had an effect (p < 0.05) on feed intake, body weight gain (BWG), and FCR of the birds aged 49 days. The birds on a diet having 8 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) feed intake than those on diets containing 4, 7.5, and 8.5 g of threonine per kg DM. Similarly, unsexed chickens fed a diet having 4 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) feed intake than those on diets having 7.5 and 8.5 g of threonine/kg DM whereas unsexed chickens fed a diet having 4 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p > 0.05) FCR values than those on diets having 7.5, 8, 8.5 and 9 g of threonine/kg DM. The birds on a diet containing 9 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) BWG than those on diets having 4, 7.5, and 8.5 g of threonine per kg DM. Similarly, chickens fed a diet having 8.5 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) BWG than those on diets having 4 and 7.5 g of threonine/kg DM. The birds on a diet having 8.5 g of threonine per kg DM had lower (p < 0.05) FCR values than those on diets having 4, 7.5, 8, and 9 g of threonine/kg DM. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 8 and 9 g of threonine per kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) feed intake, BWG, and FCR values.

Results on the effect of threonine inclusion levels on gut organ pH, length (cm), and weight (g) of the birds aged 49 days are presented in Table 4. Threonine inclusion levels had no effect (p > 0.05) crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, caeca, and large intestine pH of the birds. However, threonine inclusion levels had an effect (p < 0.05) on the duodenum pH of the birds aged 49 days. The birds on diets containing 8 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) duodenum pH values than those on diets 4, 7.5, 8.5, and 9 g of threonine per kg DM. The birds on diets containing 7.5–8 g of threonine per kg DM had higher (p < 0.05) duodenum pH values than those on a diet containing 4 g of threonine per kg DM. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 7.5, 8, 8.5, and 9 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) duodenum pH values. Similarly, unsexed chickens fed diets having 8.5 and 9 g of threonine/kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) duodenum pH values.

Threonine inclusion levels had an effect (p < 0.05) on GIT, ileum, jejunum, duodenum, and large intestine lengths of the birds aged 49 days. However, threonine inclusion levels had no effect (p > 0.05) on caeca lengths of indigenous Boschveld chickens aged 49 days. Unsexed indigenous chickens on diets containing 4 g of threonine per kg DM had longer (p < 0.05) GIT lengths than those on diets containing 7.5 and 8 g of threonine per kg DM. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 4, 8.5, and 9 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) GIT lengths. Similarly, unsexed chickens fed diets having 8.5 and 9 g of threonine/kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) GIT lengths. The birds on diets containing 4 g of threonine per kg DM had longer (p < 0.05) ileum lengths than those on diets containing 7.5 and 8 g of threonine per kg DM. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 4, 8.5, and 9 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) ileum lengths. Similarly, unsexed chickens fed diets having 7.5 and 8 g of threonine/kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) ileum lengths. The birds fed diets containing 4 g and 8.5 g of threonine/kg DM had longer (p < 0.05) jejunum than other dietary treatments. The birds on diets containing 8.5 g of threonine/kg DM had longer (p < 0.05) jejunum lengths than their counterparts on diets containing 9 g threonine per kg DM. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 4 and 9 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) jejunum lengths. The birds on diets containing 4 g, 7.5 g, and 9.5 g threonine per kg DM had similar lengths. The duodenum length for chickens on diets containing 8 and 8.5 g threonine per kg DM was also similar. The birds on diets containing 4 g threonine per DM had longer duodenum than those on 8 and 8.5 g threonine per kg DM. However, duodenum lengths for birds on diets containing 4, 7.5 and 9 g threonine per kg DM were not significant (p > 0.05) from each other. The duodenum lengths for birds on 7.5, 8.5 and 9 g threonine per kg DM were similar. Similarly, duodenum lengths of the birds fed diets containing 7.5, 8.5 and 9 g threonine per kg DM were not significantly (p > 0.05) different from each other. However, the birds fed diets having 7.5 and 8 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) duodenum lengths.

As shown in Table 4, dietary treatment levels had no effect (p > 0.05) on the length of the caeca. Boschveld chickens fed diets containing 8 g threonine per kg DM had statistically (p < 0.05) shorter large intestines compared to those fed diets containing 8.5 and 9 g threonine per kg DM. On the other hand, chickens fed diets containing 4. 7.5, 8.5 and 9 g threonine per kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) large intestine length. Similarly, the length of the large intestine for birds on diets containing 4, 7.5 and 8 g threonine per kg DM was not significantly different (p > 0.05) from each other.

Threonine inclusion levels had no effect (p > 0.05) on large intestine weights of the birds aged 49 days. However, threonine inclusion levels had an effect (p < 0.05) on crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, duodenum, and caeca weights of the birds aged 49 days. Unsexed chickens fed diets having 7.5, 8.5 and 9 g of threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) crop weights. Similarly, the birds fed diets having 4 and 8 g of threonine/kg DM had the same (p > 0.05) crop weights. The birds on a diet containing 4 g of threonine per kg DM had heavier (p < 0.05) gizzard weights than those on diets containing 7.5, 8, 8.5 and 9 g of threonine per kg DM.

The birds on diets containing 4 and 7.5 g of threonine/kg DM had heavier (p < 0.05) ileum weights than other dietary treatments. On the other hand, diets containing 8 and 8.5 g threonine had a shorter ileum than other dietary treatments. However, unsexed chickens fed diets having 8 and 8.5 g threonine per kg of DM had similar (p > 0.05) ileum weights. Also, unsexed chickens fed diets having 4, and 7.5 g threonine/kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) ileum weight. According to Table 4, chickens on diets containing 8 and 8.5 g threonine per kg DM had lower and heavier jejunum weights compared to other dietary treatments. However, jejunum weight for birds fed diets containing 4 and 8.5 g threonine per kg DM was not statistically different (p > 0.05). Ileum weight for birds on 7.5 and 8 g threonine were similar (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the birds on diets containing 8.5 and 9 g of threonine/kg DM had similar (p > 0.05) jejunum weight. No statistical difference (p > 0.05) could be found for birds on diets containing 4, 7.5 and 9 g threonine/ kg DM. Furthermore, the jejunum weight for birds on 7.5, 8 and 9 g threonine/kg DM was similar. The birds on diets containing 8.5 g threonine/kg DM had heavier (p < 0.05) duodenum weights which was not statistically different (p > 0.05) for birds fed diets containing 7.5 g threonine. The weight duodenum of chickens fed diets having 4, 7.5, 8.0 and 9 g of threonine per kg DM was similar (p > 0.05). The birds on diets containing 4 g of threonine/kg DM had heavier (p < 0.05) caeca weights compared to other dietary treatments. The weight of caeca for chickens fed diets containing 7.5 and 8 g threonine was similar (p > 0.05). No significant difference in the weight of caeca was observed in birds fed diets containing 8.5 and 8 g threonine per kg DM.

Discussion

Sustainable poultry production is essential to meet the growing protein demands of an increasing population. The poultry industry has long recognized the critical importance of gut health, as it directly impacts nutrient uptake, feed efficiency, and overall animal performance. Genetic selection has been the primary driver of improved productivity in the poultry industry, accounting for 80–90% of the progress seen in broiler performance (Alnahhas et al. 2023). Rapid growth in poultry increases susceptibility to gastrointestinal issues, as it can disrupt the gut microbiota balance. This imbalance facilitates the colonization of pathogenic bacteria like Salmonella, leading to higher infection rates and associated health risks in chickens (Aljuwayd et al. 2023). To address these challenges, researchers have explored alternative nutritional strategies, which can help to modulate the gut microbiome and improve overall gut health (Tellez et al. 2012). One promising approach to addressing this challenge is the use of probiotics, which can help to modulate the gut microbiome and improve overall gut health performance (Alnahhas et al. 2023). The gut microbiome has emerged as a cornerstone of health and productivity in poultry. This microbial ecosystem not only influences digestion and metabolism but also impacts immunity and disease resistance. In commercial poultry production, where the aim is to maximize growth and efficiency, the strategic manipulation of the gut microbiome has garnered considerable attention.

Results of the current study indicated that dietary threonine levels affected feed intake (g DM/bird/day), BWG (g/bird/day), and FCR (g feed intake/g BWG) of the birds aged 49 days. The birds aged 49 days fed diets having 8 g of threonine/kg DM had higher feed intakes than those on diets having 4, 7.5, 8.5, and 9 g of threonine/kg DM. A negative relationship between threonine and feed intake was observed; when feed intake decreases, the inclusion of threonine increases, which results in a negative relationship. This may, possibly, be due to higher threonine demand for chicken during early growth stages (NRC 1994). An alteration of tissues takes place, particularly muscle and fat deposits, which may differ in nutrient contents might also be the reason for the negative relationship (Ng’ambi et al. 2017). However, the results of the current study are in line with those of (Ng’ambi et al. 2017) who observed decreased significant differences in feed intakes (g DM/bird/day) of Venda chickens aged 1–49 days fed diets having threonine inclusion level at 6.218 g/kg DM. Moreover, Samadi (2006) observed a significant difference in feed intakes (g DM/bird/day) fed dietary threonine level of 7.9 g per kg DM for optimal intake of broiler chickens aged 1 to 21 days. Thomas (2008) recommended a threonine level of 6.9 g/kg DM for optimal intake of broiler chickens aged 14 to 28 days.

The birds aged 1–49 days on diets having 9 g of threonine/kg DM had heavier BWGs than those on diets having 4, 7.5, 8, and 8.5 g of threonine/kg DM. A positive relationship between threonine and BWG was observed. The results of the current study are in line with Estalkhzir et al. (2013), who observed a significant difference in BWG fed dietary threonine level of 0.0011 g/kg DM of broiler Japanese quail from 1 to 35 days old. NRC (1994) recommended a dietary threonine level of 8.0 g/kg DM for optimal BWG for broiler chickens aged 1 to 21 days. The current results are in line with (NRC 1994), with a significant difference in FCR value in broilers aged 1–21 days and fed a dietary threonine level of 7.4 g/kg DM. (Ng’ambi et al. 2017) observed a significant difference in the FCR of unsexed Venda chickens aged 8 to 13 weeks fed a dietary threonine level of 6.442 g/kg DM.

Results of the current study indicated that dietary threonine levels had no effect on crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, caeca, and large intestine pH values of the birds aged 49 days. However, the present results showed that dietary threonine levels influenced the duodenum pH value of the birds aged 49 days. The authors observed no significant difference at 6.2 and 5.9 g/kg DM threonine inclusion levels on the small intestines, large intestines, and caecum pH values of unsexed Venda chickens aged 91 days. According to Dibner (2004), low pH in the proventriculus is required for adequate protein digestion and a general reduction in the bacterial community in the different segments of the gut. (Choct 2009) reported that high pH in the gut results in the proliferation of pathogenic microbiota, which is detrimental to both the growth and health of chickens. (Ao et al. 2008) stated that gut organ pH for chickens varies between 5.5 and 6.5 and changes as digesta transits different segments of the GIT.

The present results indicate that threonine inclusion levels had an effect on the GIT, ileum, jejunum, duodenum, and large intestine lengths of the birds aged 49 days. However, threonine inclusion levels did not affect caeca lengths of indigenous Boschveld chickens aged 49 days. The difference should be due to dietary threonine levels.

Dietary threonine levels did not improve the GIT, ileum, jejunum, or duodenum lengths of the birds. However, threonine improved the large intestine lengths of unsexed indigenous chickens aged 49 days. These results are inconsistent with Moghaddam et al. (2011), who observed significant differences in optimal gut organ lengths of broiler chickens aged 1 to 14 days and fed a dietary threonine level of 8.7 g/kg DM. (Ng’ambi et al., 2019) observed significant differences in proventriculus and large intestine weights fed dietary threonine levels of 7.5 and 9.1 g/kg DM of unsexed Ross 308 broiler chickens aged 42 days. NRC (1994) recommended a dietary threonine level of 8.0 g/kg DM for optimal gut organ lengths for broiler chickens aged 1 to 21 days.

Results of the current study indicate that threonine inclusion levels had an effect on crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, duodenum, and caeca weights of the birds aged 49 days. However, threonine inclusion did not affect the large intestine weights of the birds aged 49 days. Dietary threonine level of 8.00 g/kg DM optimized the duodenum weight of the birds. Dietary threonine levels did not improve crop, gizzard, ileum, jejunum, and caeca weights of the birds. The results of the current study are in line with Ng’ambi et al. (2017), who observed a significant difference in proventriculus weight-fed dietary threonine level of 7.5 g/kg DM of unsexed broiler chickens aged 42 days. Ngomani et al. (2019) observed a significant difference in large intestine weights fed dietary threonine levels of 9.1 g/kg DM of unsexed broiler chickens aged 42 days. Moreover, El-Faham et al. (2017) observed significant differences in optimal gut organ weights fed a threonine level of 8.7 g/kg DM of broiler chickens aged 21 to 42 days.

Conclusion

Dietary threonine levels of 4, 7.5, 8, 8.5, and 9 g/kg DM used in the current study had an effect on feed intake, BWG, FCR, duodenum pH values, weights, and lengths of the birds aged one to 49 days. The inclusion level of 8 g threonine/kg DM optimizes both growth performance and gut function in Boschveld chickens. However, the accepted threonine level in chickens varies by age and purpose but generally ranges between 0.5% and 1.0% of the total diet. These requirements can also be influenced by factors like breeds, environmental conditions, and the protein content of the feed. This means dietary threonine level requirements for indigenous Boschveld chickens will depend on the production variable of interest. Thus, there is a need for further studies on the subject to determine the present findings.
