MEF2C induces intrinsic apoptosis and reverses cisplatin resistance in A2780 ovarian cancer cell line
Authors: Zahra Fadavi, Hadi Alizadeh, Seyed Javad Mowla, Bahram M. Soltani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer remains the most lethal gynecological malignancy, with cisplatin resistance posing a major challenge to effective treatment. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this resistance is essential for developing new therapeutic strategies. In our study, RNA-seq analysis identified MEF2C as a differentially expressed gene (DEG) associated with the development of cisplatin resistance. This finding was validated by RT-qPCR, confirming significant differences in MEF2C expression between cisplatin-sensitive (A2780) and cisplatin-resistant (A2780cp) ovarian cancer cell lines. Overexpression of MEF2C in A2780cp cells led to a reduction in the IC50 of cisplatin, enhanced caspase activity, elevated levels of the pro-apoptotic nuclear receptor NR4A1 (Nur77), and a marked increase in apoptosis, as demonstrated by MTT assay, western blotting, and PI-Annexin V flow cytometry. Our findings suggest that MEF2C is downregulated in cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells, and its overexpression re-sensitizes these cells to cisplatin through the activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Clinically, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of chemoresistance and targeting key regulators such as MEF2C may provide a promising strategy to reprogram resistant tumor cells and enhance the efficacy of standard chemotherapy, offering a potential translational pathway toward improved therapeutic responses in ovarian cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15348-6.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the most lethal gynecological malignancy, with nearly 60% of patients not surviving the disease with overall five-year survival rate ranging between 20 and 40%, depending on disease stage and treatment response [1–3]. Several risk factors have been implicated, including age, infertility, obesity, and genetic predispositions such as BRCA1/2 gene polymorphisms [4, 5]. Among the five histological subtypes of ovarian cancer, high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) is the most prevalent [6]. Standard treatment typically involves cytoreductive surgery followed by chemotherapy with platinum-based drugs such as cisplatin (cis-diamminedichloroplatinum), often in combination with paclitaxel [7]. Although initial responses to chemotherapy are often promising, disease recurrence is common [8, 9] primarily due to the development of chemoresistance [10].

The molecular mechanisms driving this resistance are complex and remain incompletely understood. Major contributing factors include decreased drug influx and increased efflux, detoxification of drug, enhanced DNA repair, ovarian cancer stem cells (OCSCs), modifications in signaling pathways and the inhibition of programmed cell death, or apoptosis, which allows cancer cells to survive despite exposure to cytotoxic agents [11–14]. Among the molecular regulators, transcription factors have emerged as key players. For instance, Wu et al. reported that downregulation of the transcription factor ZNF11 enhances resistance to cisplatin in ovarian cancer cells [15]. Similarly, the FoxO family has been associated with modulating chemosensitivity to cisplatin [16].

The myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2) family of transcription factors—comprising MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D—has been shown to exhibit dual roles in cancer, acting as both oncogenes and tumor suppressors depending on the cellular context [17]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), for instance, MEF2C promotes malignancy through VEGF-induced pathways, while also suppressing tumor growth by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling. MEF2C overexpression reduces the nuclear localization of β-catenin, whereas elevated β-catenin levels diminish MEF2C nuclear presence [18]. Moreover, MEF2C and MEF2D have been shown to enhance apoptosis in T cells downstream of T-cell receptor (TCR) activation, in part by upregulating the NR4A1 (Nur77) family of orphan nuclear receptors—key mediators of thymocyte apoptosis. Emerging evidence also supports a tumor-suppressive role for MEF2C and MEF2D in rhabdomyosarcoma [19].

Despite these findings, the role of MEF2 family members in cancer chemoresistance remains underexplored. A recent study in mice demonstrated that platinum-based chemotherapy agents downregulate MEF2C expression, suggesting a potential link between MEF2C modulation and the cellular response to such therapies [20]. While MEF2D overexpression has been implicated in promoting cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [21], decreased expression of MEF2C and HIP3 was reported in oxaliplatin-resistant gastric cancer cells [22]. Additionally, transcriptome-based analyses have shown that MEF2C expression is downregulated in paclitaxel- and cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells [23]. However, the precise mechanisms by which MEF2C expression or activity influences the response to cisplatin remain largely unclear.

In this study, we provide evidence that reduced expression of MEF2C is associated with cisplatin resistance in the platinum-resistant A2780cp ovarian cancer cell line. Overexpression of MEF2C in these cells re-sensitizes them to cisplatin and significantly increases the rate of apoptosis. This sensitization effect may be attributed, at least in part, to the functional interaction between MEF2C and the pro-apoptotic nuclear receptor Nur77, as previously suggested in related studies [24, 25].

Material and methods

RNA-seq analysis

Three GEO datasets (GSE98230, GSE173201 and GSE176218) were selected for gene expression analysis between A2780 and A2780cp. Raw data underwent quality control using the FastQC tool (Version 0.73 + galaxy0). Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using Trim Galore (Version 0.6.7 + galaxy0) and Trimmomatic (Version 0.38.0). Cleaned reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2 (Version 2.1.0 + galaxy7). Gene-level quantification was performed with HTSeq-count (Version 0.9.1 + galaxy1), and the resulting count data were normalized. Differential gene expression analysis, including fold change calculations, was conducted using the DESeq2 package (2.11.40.7 + galaxy1). All RNA-seq data processing steps were carried out via the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.org/). DEGs were identified for each study, and the intersection analysis was performed in R, revealing MEF2C as a consistently downregulated gene in cisplatin-resistant samples. Based on its known function as a transcription factor, MEF2C was selected for further investigation as a potential modulator of cisplatin sensitivity in A2780 cells. The DEG data supporting the findings of this study are available in (Supplementary Material 4).

Transcription factor binding site (TFBS) analysis

To explore the regulatory function of MEF2C, its DNA binding motif was extracted using the JASPAR database, an open-access resource for transcription factor binding profiles. TFBS analysis was then performed based on this motif. Nur77 was selected from the predicted target genes of MEF2C for further investigation, in order to study the potential regulatory relationship between MEF2C and Nur77 in the A2780 cell line.

Cell culture and cisplatin treatment

The A2780 and A2780cp ovarian cancer cell lines were obtained from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin (Sigma, UK), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. A2780 and A2780cp cells were treated with different concentrations of cisplatin, depending on the design of each experiment.

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA from cultured cells was isolated using TRIzol™ reagent (Zaverzistazma, Iran) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity and purity of the extracted total RNA were evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA (1 µg) using reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA).

Reverse transcription- quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed using the StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) and Real-Time PCR Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, which served as an internal control (Table 1). Gene cloning, plasmid construction and transient transfection

To overexpress MEF2C, the coding sequence (CDS) of MEF2C was amplified by PCR from A2780 cDNA using cloning primers (Table 1). The MEF2C gene possesses multiple transcript variants with relatively small differences. The primers were designed to amplify a region beginning at exon 4 and ending at exon 12, covering the main CDS while excluding UTRs. This region includes the sequence shared by the major transcript variants encoding the functional protein.

The amplified DNA fragment was purified using a GEL/PCR purification kit (Favorgen, Taiwan), and subsequently cloned into a TA vector. After digestion of the recombinant TA vector, the insert was subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The recombinant plasmid was then transformed into competent DH5-α E. coli cells. Colonies carrying the recombinant MEF2C plasmid were selected on LB-agar plates containing ampicillin and verified by colony-PCR. The final construct was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

A2780cp cells were then transfected with either the empty pcDNA3.1 vector (mock as a control) or the recombinant pcDNA3.1-MEF2C vector (over) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested for downstream assays.

MTT assay

A2780 and A2780cp cells were collected and seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After 24 h, a range of cisplatin concentrations (0, 20, 35, 50, 65, and 80 µM; Sobhan Oncology, Iran) was added. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay: 10 µL of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. Following a 4-h incubation at 37 °C, formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma, UK). After an additional 20-min incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate ELISA reader (ThermoFisher, USA). The experiment was performed with three biological replicates per condition.

In a separate experiment, A2780cp cells were cultured and transfected with either the mock vector or the MEF2C-overexpressing construct. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were treated with cisplatin at 0, 35, 50, and 65 µM. Cell viability was measured using the same MTT protocol. The IC₅₀ values were calculated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1).

Immunoblotting assay

Total protein was extracted from A2780cp cells following transient transfection with mock and overexpression constructs. Cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto a PVDF membrane, and probed with the following primary antibodies: MEF-2C (G-5) (sc-518152), cleaved caspase-3 p11 (H176)-R (sc-22171-R), Nur77 (C-5) (sc-365113), caspase-9 (Human Specific), β-Actin (2A3): sc-517582, and GAPDH (6C5) (sc-32233). After washing, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, m-IgGκ BP-HRP (sc-516102), and a mouse anti-rabbit IgG-HRP: sc-2357. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software.

PI-Annexin V apoptosis assay

Transfected A2780cp cells were treated with 10 μg/ml cisplatin and PBS. Measurement of apoptosis was conducted using the instructions provided by the manufacturer of the FITC-Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI (Bio Legend). FITC-Annexin V and PI dyes were used to stain cells, which were then analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer. The data were analyzed by FlowJo software (version 10).

Visualization

Volcano plots displaying differentially expressed genes, bar plots summarizing multiple test results, and dot plots from the MTT assay were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1). Dot plots from the flow cytometry apoptosis assay were visualized using FlowJo software (version 10).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1) and R software (version 4.4.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Depending on the experimental design, nonparametric t-tests (either one-sample or two-sample) were applied to assess statistical significance. A P-value of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Consistently, MEF2C expression was significantly lower in A2780cp cells across all datasets (Fig. 1D). Experimental validation of MEF2C downregulation in cisplatin-resistant A2780cp Cells

To confirm the differential cisplatin sensitivity between A2780 and A2780cp cell lines, first an MTT assay was performed. The results demonstrated a significantly lower survival rate in A2780 cells compared to A2780cp cells under identical cisplatin concentrations (Fig. 2A). The calculated IC₅₀ values were 30.72 µM for A2780 cells and 55.97 µM for A2780cp cells, indicating enhanced resistance in the latter (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, RT-qPCR analysis showed a marked downregulation of MEF2C mRNA in A2780cp cells, which is consistent with our RNA-seq data analysis (Fig. 2C).

MEF2C overexpression enhances cisplatin sensitivity in A2780cp cells

To assess the impact of MEF2C overexpression on the cisplatin response in A2780cp cells, a recombinant expression vector encoding MEF2C or a mock empty vector was transfected into the cells. RT-qPCR confirmed successful overexpression of MEF2C in the experimental group (Fig. 3A). Following transfection, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of cisplatin (0, 35, 50, and 65 μM), and their viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Results showed that MEF2C-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly reduced viability compared to the mock-transfected group across all tested drug concentrations, with the effect becoming more pronounced at higher cisplatin doses (Fig. 3B). Moreover, the calculated IC₅₀ for the mock group was approximately 52.64 μM, while the IC₅₀ decreased to 43.60 μM in cells overexpressing MEF2C (Fig. 3C) which is biologically significant. Moreover, MEF2C overexpression resulted in a marked upregulation of Nur77 protein, further supporting its role in promoting apoptosis under these conditions (Fig. 4B). MEF2C overexpression enhances apoptosis rate in A2780cp cells

To assess the impact of MEF2C overexpression on apoptosis, a flow cytometry assay using Annexin V/PI staining was performed. A2780cp cells were transfected with either a MEF2C overexpression vector or a mock control vector and subsequently treated with 10 μM cisplatin or PBS as a control. In the PBS-treated groups, the cell survival rate modestly decreased from 74.2% in the mock-transfected cells to 67.5% in MEF2C-overexpressing cells, suggesting a mild pro-apoptotic effect of MEF2C alone (Fig. 5A). In contrast, upon cisplatin treatment, the survival rate dropped more markedly from 42.3% in the mock group to 28.8% in the MEF2C-overexpressing group, indicating a synergistic enhancement of apoptosis by MEF2C in the presence of cisplatin (Fig. 5B). Discussion

Ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies, with persistently high mortality despite advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Platinum-based chemotherapy, particularly cisplatin, is a first-line treatment; however, the frequent development of resistance substantially limits its clinical efficacy and contributes to poor outcomes [35, 36]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms driving cisplatin resistance is critical for developing more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we identified MEF2C as a key gene associated with the cisplatin-resistant phenotype in A2780 ovarian cancer cells. Bioinformatic analysis revealed a significant downregulation of MEF2C in resistant A2780cp cells compared to their parental counterparts, which we confirmed by RT-qPCR. This finding suggested a potential role for MEF2C in mediating cisplatin sensitivity.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis between cisplatin-sensitive and -resistant cells indicated a complex regulatory network of transcription factors with distinct activity profiles. Among these, MEF2C emerged as a significant node. Consistent with our observations, Szenajch et al. (2020) reported that MEF2C was downregulated in ovarian cancer cells exhibiting inverse resistance to paclitaxel and cisplatin, supporting its possible role in drug response [23]. The MEF2 family of transcription factors is known to exert context-dependent roles in tumor biology, acting as oncogenes or tumor suppressors depending on their interacting partners. For instance, Li et al. (2019) showed that MEF2D, a paralog of MEF2C, promotes oncogenic behavior and enhances cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer cells [21]. While MEF2D functions as an oncogene in this context, our findings suggest that MEF2C may exert an opposing, tumor-suppressive role by promoting cisplatin sensitivity.

Previous studies have shown that MEF2C can promote apoptosis in immune cells, such as T cells and macrophages, by regulating activation-induced cell death (AICD). This occurs through transcriptional activation of the nuclear receptor Nur77, which mediates apoptosis via mitochondrial pathways [37]. In our bioinformatic analysis, we identified putative MEF2C-binding sites in the Nur77 promoter region, suggesting a regulatory relationship under cisplatin-resistant conditions. Nur77 is known to translocate from the nucleus to mitochondria, where it converts Bcl-2 from an anti- to a pro-apoptotic protein, leading to cytochrome c release and caspase activation [18, 19]. Consistent with this mechanism, western blot analysis revealed that MEF2C overexpression in A2780cp cells increased Nur77 expression and promoted cleavage of caspase-3 and caspase-9, indicating activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [38].

Functional assays further supported this molecular mechanism. MTT assays showed that MEF2C overexpression significantly decreased the cisplatin IC₅₀, indicating enhanced drug sensitivity. In addition, flow cytometry-based apoptosis assays demonstrated a substantial increase in apoptotic cell populations following MEF2C overexpression, particularly in combination with cisplatin treatment. Together, these findings suggest that restoring MEF2C expression enhances cisplatin-induced apoptosis through the MEF2C–Nur77 axis.

Collectively, our results identify MEF2C as a cisplatin sensitizer in resistant A2780 ovarian cancer cells. By promoting Nur77 expression and engaging downstream apoptotic signaling, MEF2C overexpression re-sensitizes tumor cells to cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity. Despite these promising results, our study has some limitations. Further investigation is needed to delineate the downstream pathways modulated by MEF2C and to determine whether these mechanisms operate in in vivo settings. Also, promotor analysis assays such as CHIP-seq can improve the certainty of the MEF2C-Nur77 axis in A2780 cisplatin resistant cells. Future studies using animal models and clinical samples from patients with cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer will be essential to validate the translational relevance of the MEF2C–Nur77 pathway.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that MEF2C transcription factor is downregulated in A2780cp ovarian cancer cells and that its overexpression re-sensitizes these cells to cisplatin by promoting caspase-3 activation and apoptosis. These findings support the role of MEF2C as a pro-apoptotic factor and suggest that it may act through the MEF2C–Nur77 axis to activate intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Further elucidation of this regulatory axis and its associated network may be crucial for developing new strategies to overcome chemoresistance. Nevertheless, additional studies are required to clarify the precise mechanisms of MEF2C-mediated apoptosis and to validate its clinical relevance in patient-derived samples and in vivo models.
