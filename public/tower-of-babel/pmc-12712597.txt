Integrating genetic insights into pain management and opioid use optimization in chronic pain and substance abuse populations
Authors: Ammar Abdulrahman Jairoun, Sabaa Saleh Al-Hemyari, Moyad Shahwan, Abeer M. Al-Ghananeem, Ghala Rashid Alnuaimi, Thantrira Porntaveetus
Published: 2025

Introduction

Chronic pain affects approximately 20 % of adults worldwide, impacting over 1.5 billion individuals and placing a significant burden on both patients and healthcare systemssubstantial. The ongoing opioid epidemic has further complicated treatment, with approximately 80,000 opioid-related overdose deaths in the United States in 2023 alone, alongside worsening substance use disorder (SUD).1 Clinicians face the difficult challenge of balancing effective pain relief with minimizing the risks of misuse and addiction. Traditional pain management often relies on trial-and-error approaches, leading to suboptimal outcomes, adverse drug events (ADEs), and increased healthcare costs.

A paradigm shift is urgently needed to deliver safer, more effective, and individualized pain management, particularly for vulnerable populations, such as those living with both chronic pain and SUD, amid recent FDA initiatives to expand non-opioid options and curb misuse. Pharmacogenomics (PGx), the study of how genetic variation influences drug response, offers a promising solution.2 By identifying genetic factors that affect drug metabolism and efficacy, PGx can guide more precise prescribing decisions, improve treatment safety and enhance outcomes. Ultimately, PGx has the potential to transform pain management while simultaneously addressing the intertwined challenges of chronic pain and SUD.

In the United States, chronic pain prevalence has surged post-pandemic, affecting roughly 50 million adults who report pain on most days or every day. PGx integrates with other omics technologies, such as proteomics and metabolomics, for a more holistic approach to precision medicine in pain management.

This paper explores PGx in pain management, opioid stewardship, and SUD, the pharmacist's role, challenges, and future directions, before concluding with implications for practice.

Pharmacogenomics in pain management

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) is rapidly emerging as a vital tool for tailoring pain therapy to individual patients.Genetic variation, including polymorphisms in genes such as OPRM1 and COMT, as well as beyond CYP enzymes, plays a critical role in how people metabolize and respond to analgesic medications, including opioids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and antidepressants commonly used in pain management.3 Polymorphisms in cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, particularly CYP2D6, significantly influence the metabolism of opioids, including codeine, tramadol, and hydrocodone. For example, ultra-rapid metabolizers of CYP2D6 convert codeine into morphine more quickly, leading to elevated morphine levels and an increased risk of adverse effects such as respiratory depression. In contrast, poor metabolizers may fail to achieve adequate analgesia because the prodrug is not efficiently converted into its active form.4

Beyond opioids, PGx also informs the selection of non-opioid pain treatments. Antidepressants, including tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), are frequently prescribed for neuropathic pain, yet their efficacy and tolerability vary widely among patients. Variants in CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 can alter the metabolism of TCAs, narrowing the therapeutic window and increasing the likelihood of adverse drug effects.5 By identifying these genetic predispositions, clinicians can more accurately select medications and dosages, reducing reliance on trial-and-error approaches, minimizing adverse drug events (ADEs), and improving pain relief, as evidenced by recent studies showing reduced opioid consumption with PGx-guided therapy.

This precision medicine strategy moves beyond the traditional one-size-fits-all model, offering a more personalized, effective, and safer framework for managing chronic pain.6

Opioid stewardship and substance abuse disorder

Opioid stewardship programs are critical in addressing the opioid crisis by promoting the safe, appropriate, and effective use of opioids while reducing the risks of misuse, abuse, and diversion.7 These programs typically adopt a multi-faceted approach that includes prescriber education, monitoring of prescribing practices, and patient counseling. Incorporating pharmacogenomics (PGx) into opioid stewardship adds a powerful dimension, particularly for patients with chronic pain who have a history of substance use disorder (SUD) or are at heightened risk of developing it.

Pain management is especially challenging for individuals with SUD, as clinicians must strike a careful balance between providing adequate analgesia and avoiding the risk of relapse or worsening addiction. PGx testing offers valuable guidance in these complex scenarios, particularly by identifying genetic variations that contribute to opioid morbidity and addiction susceptibility. For example, identifying genetic variants that confer increased opioid sensitivity or slower drug metabolism can help clinicians initiate therapy with lower dosages or alternative analgesics, thereby reducing the risk of respiratory depression or accidental overdose.8 Conversely, patients who are ultra-rapid metabolizers may require higher dosages for sufficient pain relief—though this must be carefully weighed against the heightened risk of dependence. PGx can also identify patients unlikely to respond to certain opioids, allowing clinicians to select non-opioid alternatives or initiate different therapies earlier in the treatment process.

Beyond pain management, PGx has essential implications for medication-assisted treatment (MAT) of opioid use disorder (OUD). First-line therapies such as buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone show variable effectiveness and tolerability across individuals. Genetic variation may influence both efficacy and adverse effect profiles, enabling the development of more personalized and effective MAT regimens.9 By integrating PGx into both pain management and OUD therapy, including in combination with non-pharmacological interventions like cognitive behavioral therapy, healthcare professionals can design comprehensive, individualized care plans that address analgesic needs while minimizing addiction risks.

The pharmacist's pivotal role in PGx integration

Pharmacists, as medication experts, are uniquely positioned to support the integration of pharmacogenomics (PGx) into routine pain management and opioid stewardship. Their expertise in pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and medication therapy management enables them to interpret PGx test results and translate complex genetic data into actionable clinical guidance.10•Pharmacists can play several key roles in this process, including advocacy for reimbursement and policy changes to support broader adoption: Explaining the benefits and limitations of PGx testing, helping patients understand how genetics influence drug response, and supporting prescribers in applying PGx insights to clinical decisions. This is critical for dispelling misconceptions and building trust in PGx-guided care.•Identifying appropriate candidates, coordinating test ordering, and ensuring proper sample collection and handling.•Integrating genetic findings with patient-specific factors—such as comorbidities, concomitant medications, and social history—to recommend dose adjustments or alternative therapies.•Tracking patient outcomes, identifying potential adverse drug events (ADEs), and making iterative recommendations based on clinical response and PGx data.•Collaborating with healthcare teams to establish institutional policies and standardized workflows for PGx-guided pain management and opioid stewardship.

By fulfilling these roles, including integration with digital health tools like EHR-embedded PGx alerts, pharmacists bridge cutting-edge genomic science with real-world clinical practice, advancing safer, more effective, and individualized pain management—particularly for patients at risk of, or living with, substance use disorder (SUD).11

Effective implementation of the adoption of the use of the PGx requires the collaborative efforts in which the pharmacy technicians are incorporated to fulfill logistical, administrative and educational processes.12 It facilitates the flow of work, and pharmacists will be able to focus on processing genetic data and formulating clinical judgments.13

Pharmacogenomic testing process involves a sample collection job which is also of paramount importance in initiating the process of pharmacogenomic testing, and the pharmacy technicians have a major role to plays in the process of sample collection. They assist in obtaining blood or saliva samples and proper management, labelling, and records to prevent all errors and contamination that could affect the outcome of the tests.14 Pharmacy technicians are responsible to guarantee the existence of accuracy and integrity of the testing process taking care of this very important task. This kind of responsibility allows pharmacists to focus on more complicated aspects of patient services, such as interpretation of the results and informed choices about the individual treatment opportunities basing on the data of heredity, but which also must be accompanied by provision of consultation for technicians' educational needs15 and that which has been shown to improve pharmacist satisfaction.16,17

Besides collecting the samples, pharmacy technicians are also involved in ordering pharmacogenomic tests by making the right choice depending on the medical history and medications of the patient. They work with pharmacists to decide when to test, as this way, they will be timely tested and will have access to genetic information.18 This enables the pharmacists to get quick test results to make decisions regarding drug therapies on time. The pharmacy technicians take care of the administrative side of testing and hence enable pharmacists to concentrate on the clinical side of testing.

When the test results are ready, pharmacy technicians enter the result in the records of the patient and make sure that it is correct and easily accessible to reviewing by the pharmacist. They also input data into electronic medical records (EMR) systems, and all care providers engaged in the patient care can access the required genetic data. Such systematic documentation will ensure the future care such as medication adjustments is based on complete and modernized information.19 Pharmacy technicians facilitate efficiency in care coordination by ensuring that every healthcare provider is informed with the required information to make quality decisions concerning the patient.

Pharmacy technicians are also very crucial in the process of communicating with the pharmacist, prescribing physicians, and other health care professionals. They support the transfer of information on the pharmacogenomic, making sure that the genetic information is properly applied in the treatment plan of a patient.20 Such coordination is critical because without aligning the clinical understanding of the pharmacist to the decision of the prescriber, there will be no alignment between the two parties. The pharmacy technicians improve optimal patient care by controlling the flow of information to ensure timely changes in the medications and to prevent possible adverse effects of genetic factors in treatment process.

Another very significant pharmacy technician responsibility is patient education. Pharmacogenomic testing is done before the test practitioners advise the patients on the nature and significance of testing with the aim of ensuring that they can make informed decisions regarding the treatment.21 This preventive education will empower the patient to make sound judgments about his or her care. After the test, the pharmacy technicians make an effort to follow up the patients and discuss the test results, possible concerns and give any prescriptions to change. Pharmacy technicians can promote the involvement of patients in the decision-making process and involve them in the decision-making process by ensuring that patients are informed about their outcomes and the causes of their treatment changes.22

The chances of pharmacogenomics succeeding in the pharmacy practice is immense because it is able to empower pharmacy technicians. Administrative work, patient education, and logistics are some of the duties that can be performed by pharmacy technicians to help pharmacists commit their time and resources to clinical decision making.23 The result of this collaboration is maximization of efficiency and effectiveness of precision medicine services which ensure improved patient outcomes. Staffing model power and autonomy in the paradigm of continually expanding role of pharmacogenomics in clinical practice, personalized medicine in pharmacy practice will continue to depend on empowerment of pharmacy technicians.

Challenges and future directions

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) holds significant promise for improving pain management and guiding opioid stewardship. However, several barriers must be addressed before its widespread clinical adoption:•Many healthcare professionals have limited knowledge of PGx principles and their clinical applications, compounded by inadequate data infrastructure and a lack of guidelines. Comprehensive training programs are necessary to equip clinicians with the skills to accurately interpret and apply pharmacogenomics (PGx) results effectively.•The high cost of PGx testing, combined with inconsistent reimbursement policies and equity issues in underserved areas, limits accessibility. Stronger advocacy and economic evaluations are necessary to demonstrate its long-term cost-effectiveness in reducing adverse drug events (ADEs), hospitalizations, and treatment failures.•Incorporating PGx testing and result interpretation into existing systems remains challenging. Developing user-friendly decision-support tools embedded within electronic health records (EHRs) will be essential for seamless adoption.•Issues such as patient privacy, data security, and the potential for genetic discrimination require careful attention, supported by robust regulatory and ethical frameworks.•Despite encouraging evidence, more large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate the clinical utility of PGx across diverse populations, particularly individuals with chronic pain and coexisting substance use disorder (SUD). Research should prioritize real-world outcomes, including pain relief, functional improvement, and reductions in opioid-related harms.

Conclusion

Advancements in pharmacogenomics (PGx), including its role in reducing opioid prescribing while improving pain outcomes, are poised to shape the future of pain management and opioid stewardship significantly.Continued research strengthened interprofessional collaboration, and supportive policy initiatives will be essential to realizing its full potential. By enabling more precise, individualized, and safer treatment strategies, PGx can not only improve outcomes for patients with chronic pain but also help mitigate the devastating impact of the opioid crisis—potentially reducing overdoses by integrating with emerging paradigms in crisis management, ultimately contributing to a healthier and more resilient society. Key takeaways include the potential of PGx in tailoring therapies, the pivotal role of pharmacists in integration, and the need to address barriers through education and policy.A call-to-action urges adoption of CPIC guidelines and increased funding for PGx research in SUD populations.
