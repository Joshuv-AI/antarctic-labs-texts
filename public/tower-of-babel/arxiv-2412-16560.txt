On the piecewise complexity of words
Ph. Schnoebelen, I. Vialard

Abstract
The piecewise complexity h ⁡ ( u ) h(u) of a word is the minimal length of subwords needed to exactly characterise u u . Its piecewise minimality index ρ ⁡ ( u ) \rho(u) is the smallest length k k such that u u is minimal among its order- k k class [ u ] k [u]_{k} in Simon’s congruence. We initiate a study of these two descriptive complexity measures. Among other results we provide efficient algorithms for computing h ⁡ ( u ) h(u) and ρ ⁡ ( u ) \rho(u) for a given word u u .

Introduction

For two words
u
and
v
, we write

when
u
is a

subword
, i.e., a subsequence, of

. For example

\mathtt{SIMON}\preccurlyeq\mathtt{STIMULATION}
while

\mathtt{IMRE}\not\preccurlyeq\mathtt{NOISEMAKER}
.
Subwords and subsequences play
a prominent role in many areas of combinatorics and computer science. Our personal
motivations come from descriptive complexity and the possibility of
characterising words and languages via some short witnessing subwords.

In the early 1970s, and with similar motivations, Imre Simon
introduced

(PT) languages in his PhD thesis
(see
[

,
Sim75
,
SS83
]
): a language

there is a

set of words

such that the membership of a word

in

depends only on which words from

F
are subwords of
u
. Since then, PT languages have played an
important role in the algebraic and logical theory of first-order
definable languages, see
[
Pin86
,
DGK08
,
Klí11
,
PZ22
]
and the
references therein. They also constitute an important class of simple
regular languages with applications in learning
theory
[
KCM08
]
, databases
[
BSS12
]
,
linguistics
[
RHF+13
]
, etc. The concept of piecewise-testability has
been extended to variant notions of “subwords”
[
Zet18
]
,
to trees
[
BSS12
]
, pictures
[
Mat98
]
, infinite
words
[
PP04
,
CP18
]
, or any
combinatorial well-quasi-order
[
GS16
]
.

When a PT language

can be characterised via a finite

where all
words have length at most

, we say that

is piecewise-testable

of height

, or

is

, denoted

k
. The

of

, denoted

is
k

with the minimum number of variables needed in any

formula that
defines

[

,
DGK08
]
.

The piecewise complexity of languages was studied by
Karandikar and Schnoebelen in
[
KS19
]
where it is a central
tool for establishing elementary upper bounds for the complexity of
the
\mathsf{FO}^{2}
fragment of the logic of
subwords.

In this article, we focus on the piecewise complexity of

individual words
. For

, we write

for

, i.e., the smallest

such that
[u]_{k}=\{u\}
, where
[u]_{k}

is the equivalence class of

\sim_{k}
. We also introduce a
new measure,

, defined as the smallest

.

Let us explain the idea more concretely. A word can be defined via
subword constraints. For example,
\mathtt{ABBA}
is the only word

over the alphabet

that satisfies the following
constraints:

\displaystyle\mathtt{AA}\preccurlyeq u\>,\quad\mathtt{AAA}\not\preccurlyeq u\>,\quad\mathtt{BB}\preccurlyeq u\>,\quad\mathtt{BBB}\not\preccurlyeq u\>,\quad\mathtt{AAB}\not\preccurlyeq u\>,\quad\mathtt{BAA}\not\preccurlyeq u\>.

(1)

Indeed, the first four constraints require that

contains exactly two

\mathtt{A}
’s and two
\mathtt{B}
’s, while the
last two constraints require that any
\mathtt{B}
in

sits between the two

’s. Since
all the constraints use subwords of length at most

is at most 3.

1

We’ll see

We have two main motivations for this study. Firstly,

Secondly,

Our contribution.

Along

, we introduce a new measure,

Related work.

.

Words, subwords and Simon’s congruence

We consider finite words
u,v,\ldots
over a finite non-empty alphabet
A
.
We write
|u|
for the length of a word
u
and

|u|_{a}
for the number of occurrences of a letter
a
in
u
.
The
empty word is denoted with
\epsilon
.
For a word
u=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}
of length
|u|=n

such that

, we write

for the

a_{i+1}a_{i+2}\cdots a_{j}
. Note that
u(0,|u|)=u
, that

u(i_{1},i_{2})\cdot u(i_{2},i_{3})=u(i_{1},i_{3})
, and that
|u(i,j)|=j-i
. We
write

as shorthand for

, the
i
-th

u
. With

we denote the set of letters that
occur in
u
. We often abuse notation and write “
a\in u
” instead
of “
a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(u)
” to say that a letter

occurs in a word

u
.

We say that

is a

of

, if

can be factored under the form
v=v_{0}a_{1}v_{1}a_{2}\cdots v_{n-1}a_{n}v_{n}

for the

\mathop{\downarrow}\nolimits\mathtt{ABAA}=\{\epsilon,\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{AA},\mathtt{AB},\mathtt{BA},\mathtt{AAA},\mathtt{ABA},\mathtt{BAA},\mathtt{ABAA}\}

Factors are a special case of subwords:

is a

is a

of

when

, and is a

of

.

When

, a word

is a

(or a

) if

is subword of exactly one word among

and

[
Sim72
]
. Observe that any two distinct words admit a distinguisher.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we write

for the set of words over

that have

, and for any words

, we let
u\sim_{k}v\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\mathop{\downarrow}\nolimits{u}\cap A^{\leq k}=\mathop{\downarrow}\nolimits{v}\cap A^{\leq k}

Note that

3.1
Defining words via their subwords

The piecewise complexity of piecewise-testable (PT) languages was defined
in
[
KS16
,
KS19
]
. Formally, for a language
L
over

A
,
h(L)
is the smallest index
k
such that
L
is

\sim_{k}
-saturated, i.e., closed under
\sim_{k}
(and we let
h(L)=\infty

when
L
is not PT). When
L
is a
singleton
\{u\}
with
u\in A^{*}
, this becomes
h\bigl(\{u\}\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\min\{n~|~\forall v:u\sim_{n}v\implies u=v\}
: we write this
more simply as
h(u)
and call it the
piecewise complexity
of

u
. For example,
h(a^{n})=n+1
when
a
is any letter.

Our first result expresses

in terms of
\delta
.

Proposition 1
(After
[
KS19
, Prop. 3.1]
)

For any
u\in A^{*}
,

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=u_{1}u_{2}\\
a\in A\end{subarray}}\delta(u,u_{1}au_{2})+1

(7)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=u_{1}u_{2}\\
a\in A\end{subarray}}r(u_{1},a)+\ell(a,u_{2})+1\>.

(H)

Proof

We only prove (
7
) since
(

) is just a
rewording based on

.

(\geq)
: By definition,

, and in

.

(\leq)
: Let

. By definition of

, there exists some

. By

(

, and by

(

3.2
Reduced words and the minimality index

Definition 1
(
[
Sim72
, p. 70]
)

Let

is

if

for all strict subwords

of

In other words,

is

-minimal word in

.
This leads to a new piecewise-based measure for words, that we call

:

\rho(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\min\{m~|~u\text{ is $m$-reduced}\}\>.

(8)

In particular,
\rho(\epsilon)=0
and more generally

\rho(a^{n})=n
for any letter
a\in A
.

Lemma 3
(
[
Sim72
, p. 72]
)

A non-empty word

is

-reduced iff

with

.

Proof

Assume, by way of contradiction, that

.

This has an immediate corollary:

Proposition 2

For any non-empty word

\displaystyle\rho(u)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=v_{1}av_{2}\\
a\in A\end{subarray}}r(v_{1},a)+\ell(a,v_{2})+1\>.

Formally, the definitions of

depend on the
underlying alphabet

. However, the only situation where the values

3.3
Fundamental properties of side distances

The characterisations given in

Propositions

1
and
2
suggest that
computing
h(u)
and
\rho(u)
reduces to computing the
r
and
\ell

side distance functions on prefixes and suffixes of
u
. This will be
confirmed in
Section

4
.

For this reason, we now prove some useful combinatorial results on
r

and
\ell
. They will be essential for proving more general properties of
h

and
\rho
in the rest of this section, and in the analysis of algorithms
in the next section.

The following lemma provides a recursive way of computing

r(u,t)
.

Lemma 4
(
[
Sim72
, p. 71–72]
)

For any
u,t\in A^{*}
and
a\in A
:

\displaystyle r(u,t)

\displaystyle=\min\bigl\{r(u,a)~|~a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t)\bigr\}\>,

(R1)

\displaystyle r(u,a)

\displaystyle=0\text{ if $a$ does not occur in $u$}\>,

(R2)

\displaystyle r(u,a)

\displaystyle=1+r(u^{\prime},au^{\prime\prime})\text{ if $u=u^{\prime}au^{\prime\prime}$ with
$a\not\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(u^{\prime\prime})$}\>.

(R3)

Proof

(
R1
): Since
r(u,\epsilon)=\delta(u,u)=\infty=\min\emptyset
,
the statement holds when
t=\epsilon
. We now assume
t\neq\epsilon
and
r(u,t)\in\mathbb{N}
.

Pick
a
occurring in
t
and let
k=1+r(u,a)
. By

Equation

3
, there exists
s
of length
k
such
that
s\not\preccurlyeq u
and
s\preccurlyeq ua
. Hence
s\preccurlyeq ut
, and
s

is a distinguisher for
u
and
ut
. With Eq. (
3
) we deduce
r(u,t)\leq|s|-1=r(u,a)
. Since this
holds for any
a
in
t
, we conclude

r(u,t)\leq\min_{a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t)}r(u,a)
.

For the “
\geq
” direction, let
k=1+r(u,t)
and pick a
distinguisher
s
of length
k
such that
s\not\preccurlyeq u
and

s\preccurlyeq ut
. Write
s=s_{1}as_{2}
with
s_{1}
the longest prefix of

s
that is a subword of
u
,
a
the first letter after
s_{1}
, and

s_{2}
the rest of
s
. Now
as_{2}\preccurlyeq t
so that

a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t)
. Since
s_{1}a\not\preccurlyeq u
, we deduce
r(u,a)\leq|s_{1}a|-1\leq|s|=1+r(u,t)
. We have then found some
a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t)

with
r(u,t)\geq r(u,a)
.

(
R2
):
If
a
does not occur in
u
, it is a distinguisher with
ua

hence
r(u,a)=0
by
Equation

3
.

(
R3
):
Assume
|u|_{a}>0
and write
u=u^{\prime}au^{\prime\prime}
with
|u^{\prime\prime}|_{a}=0
.

Let
k=1+r(u^{\prime},au^{\prime\prime})
. By
Equation

3
there exists
a distinguisher
s
of length
k
with
s\not\preccurlyeq u^{\prime}
and
s\preccurlyeq u^{\prime}au^{\prime\prime}
, further entailing
sa\not\preccurlyeq u^{\prime}au^{\prime\prime}=u
and
sa\preccurlyeq u^{\prime}au^{\prime\prime}a=ua
, i.e.,
sa
is a distinguisher for
u
and
ua
. We
deduce
r(u,a)\leq|sa|-1=k=1+r(u^{\prime},au^{\prime\prime})
, proving the “
\leq
”
direction of (
R3
).

For the other direction, let
k=r(u,a)
. Then there is a distinguisher

s
of length
k+1
with
s\not\preccurlyeq u
and
s\preccurlyeq ua
. Necessarily
s
is some
ta
, with

. From

, we deduce

and then

distinguishes
between
u^{\prime}
and

. Then
r(u^{\prime},au^{\prime\prime})\leq|t|-1\leq k-1
,
i.e.,
1+r(u^{\prime},au^{\prime\prime})\leq k=r(u,a)
.

Corollary 1

For any
u,v,t,t^{\prime}\in A^{*}
and
a\in A

\displaystyle\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t)\subseteq\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\implies r(u,t)\geq r(u,t^{\prime})\text{ and }\ell(t,u)\geq\ell(t^{\prime},u)\>,

(9)

\displaystyle a\not\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(v)

\displaystyle\implies r(uv,a)\leq r(u,a)\text{ and }\ell(a,vu)\leq\ell(a,u)\>.

(10)

Proof

We only prove the claims on

since the claim on

can be
derived by mirroring.
The implication in (

) is direct
from (

).
For the proof of (

), the assumption is that

does not occur in

and we consider two cases:

(i)
if

does not occur in

.

(ii) if

occurs in

, we write

occurring in

, so that

Compared with

used by Simon, our next Lemma provides an alternation

Lemma 5

For all
u\in\Sigma^{*}
and any letters

\displaystyle r(\epsilon,a)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle r(ub,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r(u,a)+1&\text{if $a=b,$}\\
\min\bigl(r(u,b)+1,\>r(u,a)\bigr)&\text{if $a\neq b.$}\end{cases}

Proof

First note that
r(\epsilon,a)=0
and

are special

R2
) and (

),
respectively. Now there only remains to consider

assuming

.

(
\geq
): Following

and
4
, write

r(ub,a)=\delta(ub,uba)=|s|
, where
s
is a shortest
distinguisher between
ub
and
uba
. Hence
s
is some
ta
with

and
s\not\preccurlyeq ub
. If
t\preccurlyeq u
, then

s\preccurlyeq ua
and
s\not\preccurlyeq u

is a distinguisher
between

and

r(u,a)=\delta(u,ua)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-delta-distinguisher-2}\!}}{{\leq}}|s|

is a distinguisher between

and

ub

. In both cases we deduce
r(ub,a)=|s|\geq\min\bigl(r(u,b)+1,r(u,a)\bigr)
.

(
\leq
): Let
r(u,a)=|s|
, where
s
is a shortest distinguisher
between
u
and
ua
: necessarily
s
is some
ta

.

If now we let

, where

is a shortest distinguisher

Lemma 6
(Monotonicity of
r
and
\ell
)

For all
u,v,t\in A^{*}

\displaystyle r(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq r(uv,t)\>,

\displaystyle\ell(t,u)

\displaystyle\leq\ell(t,uv)\>.

(11)

Proof

We prove the left-side inequality by induction on
|v|
and then on

|t|
, the right-side inequality being derived by mirroring. We
consider several cases:

(i) if
t=\epsilon
then
r(v,t)=\infty=r(uv,t)
.

(ii) if
t
is a letter not occurring in
v
, then

r(v,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-0}\!}}{{=}}0\leq r(uv,t)
.

(iii) if
t=a
is a letter occurring in
v
, we write
v
under the
form
v=v^{\prime}av^{\prime\prime}
with
a
not occurring in
v^{\prime\prime}
and derive

\displaystyle r(v,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-u'}\!}}{{=}}1+r(v^{\prime},av^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\text{i.h.}\!}}{{\leq}}1+r(uv^{\prime},av^{\prime\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-u'}\!}}{{=}}r(uv,t)\>.

(iv) if
|t|>1
then

\displaystyle r(v,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-t}\!}}{{=}}\min_{a\in t}r(v,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\text{i.h.}\!}}{{\leq}}\min_{a\in t}r(uv,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-t}\!}}{{=}}r(uv,t)\>.

∎

Observe that the monotonicity of
r(v,t)
with regards to
v
only
holds when we extend
v
to its left. And indeed
r(u,a)

can be strictly larger than
r(uv,a)
, e.g.,
r(aa,a)=2>r(aab,a)=1
.

There is an upper bound on how much
r(v,t)
and
\ell(t,u)
may increase
when one add letters in
v
or
u
(at any position):

Lemma 7

For any
u,v\in A^{*}
and
a,b\in A

\displaystyle r(uav,b)

\displaystyle\leq 1+r(uv,b)\>,

\displaystyle\ell(b,uav)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\ell(b,uv)\>.

(12)

Proof

We prove the first inequality by induction on the length of
v
, the second
inequality being derived by mirroring. For the base case, where

v=\epsilon
, we have

r(uav,b)=r(ua,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{\leq}}1+r(u,b)=1+r(uv,b)\>.

For the inductive case, assume
v=v^{\prime}a^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}\in A
.
If
b=a^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle r(uav^{\prime}b,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{=}}1+r(uav^{\prime},b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\text{i.h.}\!}}{{\leq}}2+r(uv^{\prime},b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{=}}1+r(uv^{\prime}b,b)=1+r(uv,b)\>.

If, on the other hand,
b\neq a^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle r(uav^{\prime}a^{\prime},b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{=}}\min\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{c}1+r(uav^{\prime},b)\\
r(uav^{\prime},a^{\prime})\end{array}\!\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\text{i.h.}\!}}{{\leq}}1+\min\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{c}1+r(uv^{\prime},b)\\
r(uv^{\prime},a^{\prime})\end{array}\!\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{=}}1+r(uv^{\prime}a^{\prime},b)\>.

In both cases, we conclude
r(uav,b)=r(uav^{\prime}a^{\prime},b)\leq 1+r(uv^{\prime}a^{\prime},b)=1+r(uv,b)
as requested.

3.4
Relating

and
\rho

Using side distances it is possible to compare

and
\rho
.

Theorem 3.1

h(u)\geq 1+\rho(u)
for any word
u
.

Proof

For the empty word, one has
h(\epsilon)=1
and
\rho(\epsilon)=0
.
We now assume that
u
is non-empty:
by
Proposition

2
, there is a factorization

with

, such that

\displaystyle\rho(u)

\displaystyle=1+r(u_{1},a)+\ell(a,u_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-u'}\!}}{{=}}r(u_{1}a,a)+\ell(a,u_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-hu-delta-u1-u1a-u2-au2}\!}}{{\leq}}h(u)-1\>.

and this concludes the proof.

The difference between
h(u)
and
\rho(u)
can be larger than

4
below. However, when

3.5
Subword complexity and concatenation

While the subwords of
uv
are obtained by concatenating the subwords
of
u
and the subwords of

We start with convexity.

Theorem 3.2

For all

\displaystyle\rho(uv)\leq\rho(u)+\rho(v)\>,

(13)

3.6
Subword complexity and shuffle

Beyond concatenations, we can consider shuffles of words. Let us
write
u\hskip 1.29167pt\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}\hskip 1.29167ptv
for the set of all words that can be obtained by
shuffling, or “interleaving”,
u
and
v
, i.e., the set of all
words
w=u_{1}v_{1}u_{2}v_{2}\cdots u_{n}v_{n}
such that
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
and

v=v_{1}\cdots v_{n}

[
SS83
]
. This notion extends to the
shuffling of three and more words. In
[
HS19
, Sect. 4]

Halfon and Schnoebelen proved the following bound:

\displaystyle w\in u_{1}\hskip 1.29167pt\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}\hskip 1.29167pt\cdots\hskip 1.29167pt\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}\hskip 1.29167ptu_{m}\implies h(w)\leq 1+\max_{a\in A}\>\bigl\{|u_{1}|_{a}+\cdots+|u_{m}|_{a}\bigr\}\>,

(20)

with the corollary

\displaystyle h(u)\leq 1+\max_{a\in\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(u)}|u|_{a}\>.

(21)

With our new tools, we can prove a bound mixing piecewise complexity and length.

Proposition 3

For any
u,v\in A^{*}

w\in u\hskip 1.29167pt\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.31114pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqcup\hskip-1.17781pt\sqcup}}\hskip 1.29167ptv\implies h(w)\leq h(u)+|v|\>.

(22)

Proof

5
Piecewise complexity of binary words

The case where
|A|=2
is special and enjoys some special properties.

To begin with, the inequality in

becomes an equality
with binary words.

Theorem 5.1

Assume
|A|=2
. Then
h(u)=\rho(u)+1
for any
u\in A^{*}
.

Proof

In view of
Theorem

3.1
, there only remains to prove
h(u)\leq\rho(u)+1
.
Let us write
A=\{a,b\}
and assume
u\in A^{*}
.
Using (
H
) we know that

h(u)=1+r(u_{1},x)+\ell(x,u_{2})
for some letter
x\in A
and
factorization

u=u_{1}u_{2}
. W.l.o.g. we can assume
x=a
and consider several
possibilities
for
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.

Case 1:

u_{1}
ends with
a
, i.e.,
u_{1}=u^{\prime}_{1}a
. Then

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=1+r(u^{\prime}_{1}a,a)+\ell(a,u_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-u'}\!}}{{=}}2+r(u^{\prime}_{1},a)+\ell(a,u_{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-m-via-r&l}\!}}{{\leq}}1+\rho(u^{\prime}_{1}au_{2})=1+\rho(u)\>.

Case 2:

u_{1}
ends with
b
and
u_{2}
starts with
b
, i.e.,

u_{1}=u^{\prime}_{1}b
,
u_{2}=bu^{\prime}_{2}
, and
u=u^{\prime}_{1}bbu^{\prime}_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=1+r(u_{1},a)+\ell(a,u_{2})=1+r(u^{\prime}_{1}b,a)+\ell(a,bu^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{\leq}}1+(1+r(u^{\prime}_{1},b))+(1+\ell(b,u^{\prime}_{2}))=3+r(u^{\prime}_{1},b)+\ell(b,u^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-u'}\!}}{{=}}2+r(u^{\prime}_{1}b,b)+\ell(b,u^{\prime}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-m-via-r&l}\!}}{{\leq}}1+\rho(u^{\prime}_{1}bbu^{\prime}_{2})=1+\rho(u)\>.

Case 3:

u_{1}
ends with
b
and
u_{2}
is empty, i.e.,

u_{1}=u^{\prime}_{1}b
and
u=u_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=1+r(u_{1},a)+\ell(a,u_{2})=1+r(u^{\prime}_{1}b,a)+\ell(a,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-sld-0}\!}}{{=}}1+r(u^{\prime}_{1}b,a)+\ell(b,\epsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-new-r-rules*}\!}}{{\leq}}1+1+r(u^{\prime}_{1},b)+\ell(b,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\!\eqref{eq-m-via-r&l}\!}}{{\leq}}1+\rho(u^{\prime}_{1}bu^{\prime}_{2})=1+\rho(u)\>.

If
u_{2}
starts with
a
, we use symmetry and
reason as in Case 1. If
u_{2}
starts with
b
and
u_{1}
is empty,

All cases have been dealt with and the proof is complete.

Remark 4

Theorem

cannot be generalised to words using

∎

When dealing with binary words, the alternation of letters is fixed
and it is convenient to describe a word by just its first letter and
the lengths of its blocks.
In this spirit, we would write a binary word that starts with

\mathtt{A}
under the form

u=\mathtt{A}^{n_{1}}\mathtt{B}^{n_{2}}\mathtt{A}^{n_{3}}\mathtt{B}^{n_{4}}\cdots(\mathtt{A}/\mathtt{B})^{n_{k}}
where
the last letter is
\mathtt{A}
or
\mathtt{B}
depending on the parity of
k
and
where
n_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
. It turns out that we can compute

h(u)
and
\rho(u)
efficiently when the number
k
of blocks is
notably smaller than the length of
u
. We now explain the underlying
technique as a succession of cumulative steps for which the rather
technical proofs have been relegated to the Appendix.

In a word of the form

u=\mathtt{A}^{n_{1}}\mathtt{B}^{n_{2}}\mathtt{A}^{n_{3}}\mathtt{B}^{n_{4}}\cdots(\mathtt{A}/\mathtt{B})^{n_{k}}
an

isolated
(occurrence of a) letter is any block
with
n_{i}=1
. Our first result gives a direct formula for
the piecewise complexity of binary words with no isolated letters
except perhaps at the extremities.

Lemma 10
(See Appendix)

Let
u=\mathtt{A}^{n_{1}}\mathtt{B}^{n_{2}}\mathtt{A}^{n_{3}}\cdots(\mathtt{A}/\mathtt{B})^{n_{k}}
with
k\geq 2
. If
n_{i}\geq 2
for all
i=2,3,\ldots,k-1
and
n_{1},n_{k}\geq 1
then

\rho(u)=k+\max\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}n_{1}{+}1,\>n_{k}{+}1,\\
n_{2},n_{3},\ldots,n_{k-1}\end{array}\biggr)-3.

(25)

For example,
Lemma

10
tells us that, for
u=\mathtt{A}^{34}\mathtt{B}^{23}\mathtt{A}^{18}\mathtt{B}
, one has
\rho(u)=4+\max(35,23,18,2)-3=36
, (and
h(u)=37
thanks to
Theorem

5.1
).
It also validates the claim
h(\mathtt{A}\mathtt{B}\mathtt{B}\mathtt{A})
=3 made in the introduction
of this article.

There remains to account for isolated letters, where
n_{i}=1
. When
they appear in pairs, we invoke the following lemma.

Lemma 11
(See Appendix)

Assume
A=\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}
and
u,v\in A^{*}
.
If
u
does not end with
\mathtt{A}
and
v
does not start with
\mathtt{B}

then
\rho(u\mathtt{A}\mathtt{B}v)=1+\rho(uv)
.

Finally, for isolated letters that are surrounded by multiple letters,
we use another lemma.

Lemma 12
(See Appendix)

Assume
A=\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}
and
u,v\in A^{*}
. Then
\rho(u\mathtt{A}\mathtt{A}\mathtt{B}\mathtt{A}\mathtt{A}v)=\rho(u\mathtt{A}\mathtt{A}\mathtt{A}\mathtt{B}\mathtt{B}\mathtt{B}\tilde{v})
where
\tilde{v}
is the word
obtained from
v
by replacing any
\mathtt{A}
by a
\mathtt{B}
and vice versa.

With these three lemmas we have a complete method for binary words.

Theorem 5.2

For binary words
u\in\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}^{*}
,
h(u)
and
\rho(u)
can be
computed in time
O(|u|)
, even if
u
is given in exponential
notation
u=a_{1}^{n_{1}}a_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots a_{k}^{n_{k}}
with input size

|u|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+\log_{2}n_{i})
.

Let us explain the algorithm on an example. Given

u=\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{5}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{1}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{1}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{7}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{4}
, we first
remove the pairs of isolated letters. This
yields
u^{\prime}=\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{5}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{7}\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{4}
with
\rho(u)=2+\rho(u^{\prime})
.
Then we remove the first isolated letter surrounded by larger blocks,
obtain
u^{\prime\prime}=\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{6}\mathtt{A}^{8}\mathtt{B}^{1}\mathtt{A}^{4}
, remove the other and obtain

u^{\prime\prime\prime}=\mathtt{A}^{1}\mathtt{B}^{6}\mathtt{A}^{9}\mathtt{B}^{5}
with
\rho(u^{\prime\prime\prime})=\rho(u^{\prime\prime})=\rho(u^{\prime})
. Here

Lemma

10
applies and yields

\rho(u^{\prime\prime\prime})=4+\max(1+1,6,9,5+1)-3=10
. Thus
\rho(u)=12
and

h(u)=13
.
Finally,
the above algorithm can work directly on a list of pairs

(a_{1},n_{1}),(a_{2},n_{2}),\ldots,(a_{k},n_{k})
.

An interesting application of these results is that we can derive an
exact value for the maximum length of a binary word having a given piecewise complexity.

Theorem 5.3

For any
u
binary word the following holds:

|u|\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{h(u)^{2}}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+h(u)-1\>.

(26)

Furthermore, the bound is tight.

Proof

We first argue that the claimed bound is reached. If
h(u)=2g
is even, the bound is reached
by taking
u
made of
g+1
alternating blocks of length
g+1
each
except for the two extreme blocks of length
g
. E.g.,
h(u)=6
is
obtained with
u=\mathtt{A}^{3}\mathtt{B}^{4}\mathtt{A}^{4}\mathtt{B}^{3}
having maximum length 14.
For odd
h(u)=2g-1
the claimed bound is reached with
g
blocks of length

g+1
(except for extreme blocks). E.g.,
h(u)=7
is obtained
with
u=\mathtt{A}^{4}\mathtt{B}^{5}\mathtt{A}^{5}\mathtt{B}^{4}
having maximum length 18.

We let the reader prove that
Equation

26
holds. This is
direct from
Lemma

10
when
u
has no isolated
letters. And if
u
has isolated letters,
Lemma

11
or

Lemma

12
shows how to build a longer word with same

.

6
Conclusion

In this article, we focused on the piecewise complexity of individual
words, as captured by the piecewise height
h(u)
and the minimality
index
\rho(u)
, a new measure suggested by
[
Sim72
]
and that we
introduce here.

These measures admit various characterisations, including

Propositions

1
and
2
, that can be leveraged
into efficient algorithms running in quadratic time
O(|A|\cdot|u|)

for
h(u)
and linear time
O(|A|+|u|)
for
\rho(u)
. Our analysis
further allows to establish monotonicity and convexity properties for

h
and
\rho

and to relate
h
and
\rho
.

This first foray into the piecewise complexity of individual words
raise a wide range of new questions. Let us mention just two:

1.

The minimality index was found to be very useful in the analysis of
binary words but its usefulness could certainly be improved. For
example, is there a way to define
\rho(L)
for a PT-language
L
?
Can we relate
\rho(u)
to some more usual measures of logical complexity?

2.

For
fixed alphabet size
k=|A|
, what are the longest words having
piecewise complexity at most
n
? A lower bound can be found
in
[
KS19
]
where is given a family of words

(U_{k,\lambda})_{k,\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
having length
(\lambda+1)^{k}-1

and piecewise complexity
k\lambda+1
. An upper bound is given
in
[
KS19
, Prop. 3.8]
:

|u|\leq\biggl(\frac{h(u)-1}{|A|}+2\biggr)^{|A|}-1\>.

(27)

Here the lower and the upper bounds are similar but do not match
exactly. For
|A|=2
we can give the exact maximum length of
u
as a
function of
h(u)
, see
Theorem

5.3
, but for
|A|>2
the problem is open.
