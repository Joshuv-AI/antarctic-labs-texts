BK Polyomavirus DNAemia With a High DNA Load Is Associated With De Novo Donor‐Specific HLA Antibodies in Kidney Transplant Recipients
Authors: Wouter T Moest, Aiko P J de Vries, Dave L Roelen, Jesper Kers, DirkJan A R Moes, Danny van der Helm, Marko J K Mallat, Soufian Meziyerh, Aline L van Rijn, Mariet C W Feltkamp, Joris I Rotmans
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBK polyomavirus‐associated nephropathy (BKPyVAN) is a well‐known complication of kidney transplantation (KTx). The mainstay of prevention is the reduction of immunosuppression upon detection of BK polyomavirus (BKPyV) DNAemia, which precedes BKPyVAN. However, this reduction may inadvertently increase the risk of alloimmunity particularly in patients with a high BKPyV DNA load, where significant immunosuppression reduction is often necessary. This single‐center, retrospective cohort study assesses the risk of de novo donor‐specific antibodies (dnDSA) development and biopsy‐proven acute rejection (BPAR) following high and low BKPyV DNAemia. All patients who underwent KTx at Leiden University Medical Center between 2011 and 2020 were included. Patients were grouped according to high (maximum BKPyV DNA load > 4log10 copies/mL), low (maximum serum BKPyV DNA load ≤ 10E4 copies/mL), and absent BKPyV DNAemia, and analyzed for the development of dnDSA and BPAR, using Cox regression. Of 1076 KTx recipients included, 108 (10%) developed a BKPyV DNAemia with a maximum DNA load below 4log10 copies/mL, whereas 121 (11.2%) developed a BKPyV DNAemia exceeding 4log10 copies/mL. The risk of dnDSA development was higher in patients with a high BKPyV DNAemia, compared to patients without DNAemia (adjusted hazard ratio of 1.9 (95% CI 1.1–3.2, p = 0.017). No significant difference in dnDSA risk was observed between patients with low and absent BKPyV DNAemia. Risk of BPAR did not differ between groups. Our study shows that higher BKPyV DNA loads in KTx patients are associated with a higher risk for dnDSA development, highlighting the importance of exploring additional strategies for the prevention and treatment of BKPyV infections in KTx recipients.Keywords: biopsy‐proven acute rejection (BPAR), BK virus, donor‐specific antibodies (DSA), kidney transplantation

Introduction

BK polyomavirus (BKPyV) is a notable concern in the field of kidney transplantation, as it has the potential to lead to significant morbidity. The seroprevalence of BKPyV in the general population is notably high (82%–100%). Primary infection typically occurs in early childhood and is believed to transpire via oral and respiratory transmission, generally manifesting as an asymptomatic DNAemia [1, 2, 3]. The landscape shifts for kidney transplant recipients, where BKPyV DNAemia, with a prevalence of approximately 12% and an onset that typically occurs during the first year after transplantation, can evolve into BKPyV‐associated nephropathy (BKPyVAN) in 1%–10% of cases. The consequences of BKPyVAN are far‐reaching, potentially resulting in permanent graft damage ultimately leading to graft loss [4, 5, 6, 7].

To mitigate this risk, current guidelines advocate regular BKPyV DNAemia screening posttransplantation and primarily rely on the reduction of immunosuppression for treatment, as effective antiviral therapies for BKPyV remain elusive [8, 9, 10]. Nevertheless, a noticeable heterogeneity exists among transplantation centers and guidelines concerning the protocols for screening and treating BKPyV DNAemia [11, 12, 13].

Several risk factors for the development of BKPyV DNAemia have been identified, extending beyond the immunocompromised status of the kidney transplant patient. There is compelling evidence that donor‐derived BKPyV plays an important role in the development of BKPyV‐related infection in kidney transplant recipients. Indeed, previous studies revealed a strong and positive association between high BKPyV serotiters in the donor and the risk of BKPyV DNAemia posttransplantation. This association could be explained by a higher viral load or a more virulent BKPyV strain within the donor, predisposing the recipient to BKPyV‐related infections [14]. Furthermore, sequencing the VP1 gene of BKPyV in the urine of 20 donor/recipient pairs revealed that the sequences were completely identical in donor and posttransplantation recipient samples, providing further evidence that donor‐derived BKPyV plays an important role in kidney transplant recipients [15].

An inadvertable focus on the reduction of immunosuppression following BKPyV DNAemia, may increase the risk of de novo donor‐specific antibodies (dnDSA) and renal allograft rejection. We hypothesize that this risk will be highest in patients with a BKPyV DNAemia with a high DNA load as immunosuppression will be reduced significantly in these patients.

In this retrospective, single‐center, cohort study, we investigated the risk of the development of dnDSA, and biopsy‐proven acute rejection (BPAR) upon detection of BKPyV DNAemia in a cohort of kidney transplant recipients, transplanted between 2011 and 2020.

Methods

Study Population and Design

In this retrospective cohort study, we included adult kidney transplant recipients who were transplanted between January 2011 and May 2020 at Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC). We excluded patients with combined transplantation, kidney transplant patients with primary non‐function of the allograft, kidney transplant patients without available follow‐up data, and patients who received induction with alemtuzumab as part of a clinical trial, or treated with mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) as part of a clinical trial.

Local transplantation database from Leiden Transplant Center was used to collect the data. This database was directly linked to structured data of electronic health record, pathology and immunology departments. Data were collected real‐time via data registration at the source principles. Data are frequently validated by a datamanager. The database was used to collect patient characteristics, data about the incidence‐, course of BKPyV DNAemia, and development to biopsy‐proven BKPyVAN (defined as a positive Simian Virus 40 [SV40] immunohistochemical staining on allograft biopsy in combination with BKPyV DNAemia). Furthermore, mortality, graft outcomes, risk factors for the development of rejection (panel reactive antibody [PRA], human leukocyte antibodies [HLAs] mismatching, DGF (defined as need for dialysis in first posttransplant week), re‐transplantation and induction treatment), the development of BPAR and the development of dnDSA were collected. In addition, data were obtained regarding the area under the whole blood concentration‐time curve from 0 to 12 h (AUC0–12 h) of tacrolimus, ciclosporine, and everolimus and plasma AUC0–12 h of mycophenolate mofetil, which were determined at approximately 6 weeks after transplantation. Trough whole blood levels of tacrolimus, ciclosporine and everolimus were determined at approximately 7 months after transplantation. The time point of 7 months was selected to get an indication of the effects of a reduction of immunosuppression following a BKPyV DNAemia episode, generally occurring 4–5 months after transplantation. The mean follow‐up time was 5.4 years (range from 0.0 to 11.4 years).

Rejection episodes were classified based on BPAR and clinical rejection. BPAR was defined according to the histopathological evaluation of kidney biopsies, as recorded in our database, and categorized into T cell‐mediated rejection (TCMR), antibody‐mediated rejection (ABMR), or mixed rejection. Clinical rejection was defined as a high suspicion of rejection that warranted rejection treatment but was not confirmed by a kidney biopsy. These episodes were identified through a review of our local database. Biopsy‐proven rejections, clinical rejection, and dnDSA development from 6 weeks after transplantation were included in the analysis (since the possible effects of a BKPyV DNAemia were not expected before this time). Both protocol and clinically indicated biopsies were included in the analysis.

Patients with no BKPyV DNAemia detected during the screening in the 2 years after kidney transplantation and patients with a BKPyV DNAemia or viruria after BPAR (defined as mentioned above) or dnDSA development were compared to kidney transplant patients with BKPyV DNAemia with a maximum serum BKPyV DNA load of above and below 10E4 copies/mL. This threshold is generally used to distinguish between mild and severe BKPyV DNAemia [16, 17, 18], of which the latter is associated with increased risk of BKPyVAN.

BKPyV and dnDSA Screening and Management Protocol at Our Center

The screening protocol for BKPyV DNAemia encompassed viral load measurements at 1.5, 3, 6, and 12 months after kidney transplantation. BKPyV DNAemia was determined in serum samples using quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), as described previously [14]. When BKPyV DNAemia was detected, the frequency of monitoring was increased to once every 2 weeks. In patients with a BKPyV DNA load of ≤ 4log10 copies/mL, target trough levels of the calcineurin inhibitor were evaluated and when on target, prednisone dose was reduced until 5 mg/day and mycophenolate mofetil dose is reduced to half. When BKPyV DNA load was > 4log10 copies/mL, the dose of the calcineurin inhibitor was reduced to half and mycophenolate mofetil was discontinued. After two consecutive non‐detectable BKPyV serum loads, immunosuppression was increased to match standard protocol trough levels.

Since 2017, our center has implemented the use of Luminex technology for the screening of dnDSA. This method utilized color‐coded microbeads coated with either HLA Class I or Class II glycoproteins, which are incubated with patient serum. HLA antibodies in the serum bind to the antigens on the beads, and an anti‐human IgG antibody, labeled with phycoerythrin (PE), was added to detect this binding. The Luminex reader measured the fluorescence emission from PE from each bead. The signal strength of each bead, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), was compared to negative control beads to determine positivity. Positive control sera were included in each test to ensure the accuracy of the results. Screening for dnDSA was conducted at 6 months and 1‐year posttransplantation, followed by annual screenings thereafter.

BKPyV Serology in Donors and Recipients

For this purpose, serum samples were obtained pre‐transplantation of 424 living donor‐recipient pairs in this cohort. These samples were analyzed by an in‐house Luminex immunoassay detecting IgG‐reactivity against the major viral capsid protein (VP1) of the most common BKPyV genotype Ib1, according to a published protocol [14, 19, 20]. This protocol has been used to analyze serotiters against the various human polyomaviruses, including BKPyV. Serotiters were expressed in median fluorescent intensity (MFI) as semiquantitative measurements.

Immunosuppression Following Kidney Transplantation

Since 2011 standard immunosuppression for transplant recipients consisted of basiliximab induction, followed by a triple maintenance regimen involving tacrolimus, prednisone and mycophenolate mofetil. Our calcineurin inhibitor dosing strategy was based on AUC0–12 h measurements, with target trough levels adjusted according to the individual AUC0–12 h results. We measured the estimated AUC0–12 h for calcineurin inhibitors and mTOR inhibitors at least at 1 week, 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1‐year posttransplantation. Targeted ranges for tacrolimus are 160, 120 µg × h/L and from 6 months > 80 µg×h/L and for ciclosporine 5400, 3250 µg × h/L and from 6 months 2750 µg × h/L, respectively. Everolimus was targeted between 100 and 120 µg × h/L posttransplantation. On average this corresponded to target tacrolimus trough levels of 8–12 µg/L during the initial 6 weeks posttransplantation, subsequently adjusting to a range of 5–7 µg/L beyond this period. Trough levels of Calcineurin inhibitors and mTOR inhibitors were regularly assessed during every outpatient clinic visit.

For mycophenolate mofetil the estimated AUC0–12 h was measured 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1‐year posttransplantation and was targeted between 30 and 45 mg × h/L. Prednisone was tapered from 50 to 10 mg/day within the first week and further reduced to 5 mg/day after 6 weeks.

In cases with an increased risk of rejection, such as ABO blood group incompatible‐ (ABOi) or HLA‐incompatible (HLAi) kidney transplantations, alemtuzumab induction was used. Alternatively, in selected cases, induction with alemtuzumab was given, to allow for the reduction in maintenance therapy in the initial phase after transplantation.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS statistics version 25 was used for statistical analysis. To compare patient characteristics, levels of immunosuppressants and rates of mortality‐ and graft loss 2 years after transplantation between the patients groups (patients without BKPyV DNAemia and those with BKPyV DNAemia categorized by serum loads below and above 4log10 copies/mL), the χ 2 test and One‐way ANOVA test were employed. In cases where a significant difference was found among the three groups, further pairwise comparisons were conducted using the χ 2 and post hoc tests with ANOVA.

For the association between the development of BPAR or dnDSA in kidney transplant patients with and without BKPyV DNAemia, uni‐ and multivariate Cox regression analysis were used. Risk factors (age and gender recipient, age and gender donor, type of kidney transplantation (donation after circulatory death [DCD], donation after brain death [DBD], and living donation), PRA, re‐transplantations, HLA mismatching, delayed graft function (DGF), induction therapy, cold ischemic time (CIT) and BPAR within 6 weeks after transplantation) for development of dnDSAs and BPAR were used in the multivariate cox regression analysis. For all performed tests, a p < 0.05 in a two‐sided test was considered statistically significant.

A linear mixed models (LMM) was utilized to assess the differences in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) over time between the three groups, while accounting for within‐subject correlations and varying times of measurement (6 weeks, 6 months, 1, 3, and 5 years after transplantation). Fixed effects were used for age of both recipient and donor, gender of the donor, HLA mismatch, DGF, CITs, time, different BKPyV DNAemia groups and interactions (between time and recipient age, and between time and the different BKPyV DNAemia groups). Individual patients were designated as a random effect.

The study protocol was submitted to the Medical Ethical Committee of the LUMC that decided formal approval was not needed, due to the retrospective study design (study identification number 131830).

Results

In total, 1192 patients received kidney transplantation between January 2011 and May 2020 in our center. Twenty‐five patients with primary non‐function of the transplanted kidney were excluded, nine patients without available follow‐up data and 82 patients who participated in a clinical trial with alemtuzumab induction or treatment with MSC, were excluded for this study. Therefore, a total of 1076 kidney transplant patients were included for this analysis. Of these, 108 (10%) developed a BKPyV DNAemia with a maximum serum load below 4log10 copies/mL, and 121 (11.2%) patients a BKPyV serum load exceeding 4log10 copies/mL. A total of 28 (2.6%) patients were diagnosed with a biopsy‐proven BKPyVAN, all of whom had developed a peak load above 4log10 copies/mL. The majority of recipients (847 patients, 78.7%) remained BKPyV‐negative throughout the study (Table 1).

A comparison of baseline characteristics revealed that the BKPyV group with a high DNA load included more older males, who received allografts from donors of older age. Furthermore, the average onset of BKPyV DNAemia in this group was earlier after transplantation and duration of the DNAemia was longer, compared to kidney transplant patients with a serum load below 10E4 copies/mL (Table 1).

A higher occurrence of dnDSA was observed in the BKPyV group with a maximum DNA load exceeding 4log10 copies/mL compared to patients without a BKPyV DNAemia [20.7% (25 patients) vs. 13.7% (116 patients), p = 0.042]. Multivariate cox regression revealed a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.9 (95% CI 1.1–3.2, p = 0.017) for the development of dnDSA in the BKPyV group with a high DNA load. There was no notable difference in BPAR rate between groups, further analysis showed no significant difference in TCMR, AMBR or mixed rejections. Additionally, there was no difference in rate of clinical rejection (Tables 2 and 3).

To investigate the potential role of donor factors in the development of BKPyV DNAemia with varying serum loads, we examined pretransplantation BKPyV serotiters in 424 living donor‐recipient pairs. Our analysis revealed a trend of higher BKPyV serotiters in the donor and lower serotiters in the recipient among patients with BKPyV DNAemia with a maximum serum load exceeding 4log10 copies/mL. In patients with a BKPyV DNAemia with a maximum DNA load below 4log10 copies/mL, there were significantly higher BKPyV serotiters in the donor, compared to kidney transplant patients without a BKPyV DNAemia (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study shows a higher risk for dnDSA development in patients with high BKPyV DNA load, alongside a comparable rate of BPAR compared to patients without BKPyV DNAemia. Secondly, trough levels of tacrolimus at 7 months after transplantation were inversely associated with a high serum BKPyV DNA load, reflecting the reduction in immunosuppression upon the detection of BKPyV DNAemia.

It is important to acknowledge that other factors than reduced immunosuppression may also influence the risk of dnDSA development following a BKPyV DNAemia. Previous studies by Zeng and coworkers showed that in kidney biopsies of patients with a BKPyVAN or BKPyV DNAemia, abundant alloreactive T‐cell clones are present that exceed the number of virus‐reactive clones. They speculate that the damage and inflammation caused by BKPyV leads to a secondary influx of alloreactive T‐cells. An alternative explanation could be heterologous cell‐mediated immunity, as they also observe the presence of virus‐reactive cells in patients with rejection [21].

As reduction of immunosuppression is the mainstay of the treatment of BKPyV DNAemia, the lower tacrolimus trough levels at 7 months after transplantation in patients with a high peak load BKPyV DNAemia are not surprising. However, the average level was still within the target range for kidney transplant recipients in our center. Interestingly, the amount of reduction of immunosuppression upon BKPyV DNAemia is very heterogenous across centers [11, 12], while the increased risk of dnDSA formation and rejection is strikingly similar across these centers [18, 22, 23, 24, 25]. In other words, the level of immunosuppression is not the only determinant for dnDSA formation.

The same is true for the risk of BKVyV DNAemia, where donor‐derived factors also seems to play a role. Indeed, the identical VP1 gene sequences in donor and recipients and the strong association between high BKPyV serotiters in donors and increased risk of BKPyV DNAemia in recipients, suggest that a BKPyV infection in kidney transplant recipients is donor‐derived. In our cohort, we observed a nonsignificant trend of higher serotiters in the donor and lower serotiters in the recipient among patients with BKPyV DNAemia with a high DNA load. Further research is necessary to explore this relationship more comprehensively and to determine the underlying mechanisms [21].

Since there is evidence that multiple factors, beyond mere immunosuppression, contribute to dnDSA formation, and given the increased risk of dnDSA in patients with high BKPyV DNAemia, this may indicate that we should exercise greater caution in reducing immunosuppression in this group.

Our study highlights the association between BKPyV DNAemia with high viral serum loads and poor graft function in the following years. However, upon adjusting for confounding factors such as donor age, CITs, and DGF in the LMM analysis, the association of BKPyV DNAemia and graft function was no longer significant. This suggests that patients with poorer donor quality may be more susceptible to BKPyV infection. This observation is in line with previous studies which revealed that CITs, DGF, and older donor age are important predictors of graft outcomes and well‐established risk factors for BKPyV DNAemia [26, 27, 28]. A transplanted kidney with chronic damage may be more susceptible to BKPyV virus infection due to the disrupted cellular structure, creating a more favorable environment for viral replication and spread [26].

In our analysis, no effect of BKPyV DNAemia on the incidence of BPAR was observed. However, dnDSA could still contribute to poorer graft function on the long term. Indeed, dnDSA not only associated with TCMR, but also with subclinical and overt ABMR, which are linked to inferior graft outcomes [29, 30]. Graft dysfunction should, however, manifest from around 2.1 years posttransplantation, following the average onset of dnDSA in the patients with high peak BKPyV DNAemia (Table 2). This was, however, not apparent in the trajectory of eGFR, which showed worse graft outcomes from 6 weeks and remains stable up to 5 years posttransplantation (Figure 1). It was, however, possible that the effect of dnDSA on graft outcomes was beyond these 5 years of follow‐up. Our findings align with previous studies, which consistently revealed poor graft outcomes in patients with persistent BKPyV DNAemia and high DNA loads [18, 29].

Some aspects of our study require further discussion. Possible limitations of our study are the single center and retrospective design. However, in this study, a large number of patients, transplanted over a long period of time were included. Although BKPyV and dnDSA screening was performed at standard time points in the first year after transplantation, it is worth noting that such screenings may not be consistently performed beyond this initial timeframe, potentially leading to missed episodes of BKPyV DNAemia and dnDSA formation. Moreover, standard dnDSA screening has only been implemented since 2017, likely resulting in under‐detection of dnDSA before this period. It was also conceivable that there may be underreporting of BPAR in patients with BKPyV DNAemia, particularly those with a high viral load. In such cases, the high clinical suspicion for BKPyVAN may lead clinicians to be less inclined to perform a kidney biopsy, potentially resulting in missed diagnoses of underlying rejection. Furthermore, although we attempted to assess the level of immunosuppression during BKPyV DNAemia by using trough levels measured at 7 months, these may not accurately reflect the degree of immunosuppression during the actual course of BKPyV DNAemia. This limitation makes it difficult to determine the exact influence of immunosuppression on the development of dnDSA. Finally, although treatment for BKPyV DNAemia is protocolized in our center, adherence to the protocol was not measured.

In this study, we demonstrate an elevated risk of developing dnDSA following a BKPyV DNAemia with a high DNA load in kidney transplant patients. Future studies should focus on investigating the underlying mechanisms driving this association, as well as strategies for preventing BKPyV infections in kidney transplant recipients.
