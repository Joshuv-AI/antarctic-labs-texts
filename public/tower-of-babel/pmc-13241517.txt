Analyzing the geometric dependence of thermoelastic Q-factor in micro hemispherical resonators via a data-augmented CNN-transformer model
Authors: Yuyi Yao, Gongliu Yang, Ruizhao Cheng, Wenqiang Li, Xin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Q-factor dominated by thermoelasticity (QTED) is a non-negligible component of the total quality factor in high-performance design of Micro Hemispherical Resonators (MHRs). However, finite-element analysis (FEA) of QTED is prohibitively time-consuming. This paper presents a rapid and accurate prediction framework based on a hybrid CNN-Transformer model, enhanced by data augmentation via polynomial fitting of FEA simulation results. Ablation studies confirm the optimal architecture, where replacing the feed-forward network with a secondary multi-head self-attention mechanism yields the highest performance. Comparative experiments demonstrate that the proposed model surpasses mainstream prediction methods in both accuracy and robustness, with Monte Carlo Dropout verifying well-calibrated uncertainty. We systematically analyze the influence of geometric parameters (thickness T, height H, and anchor radius r) on QTED through the digital model and physical mechanisms. Results show that an optimal design for maximizing QTED is characterized by low H, low r, and high T. Practical trade-offs and manufacturability considerations are discussed, recommending a low r to reduce sensitivity to fabrication variations in T and H. The framework quickly improves computational efficiency compared to FEA, providing an efficient and reliable tool for the optimization and robust design of MHRs. Subject terms: Sensors, Computational nanotechnology

Introduction

Hemispherical resonator gyroscope (HRGs) are famous for their potential to achieve high-precision inertial navigation due to their exceptional mechanical quality factor and operational stability1–4. The advancement of micro-fabrication techniques has enabled the development of the micro Hemispherical resonator gyroscope (μHRG), a structure that miniaturizes HRG for Micro-Electro-Mechanical System (MEMS) applications, offering a compelling solution that combines excellent performance with a simple structure and compact size5–7. The micro hemispherical resonator (MHR) is the core component of the μHRG. Q-factor is a critical performance metric for MHR, directly determining the gyroscope’s sensitivity and bias stability, while it is governed by thermoelastic and surface loss8–12. Surface loss sets the practical limit, while thermoelastic loss defines the theoretical maximum. With continued improvements in manufacturing techniques, surface loss is being progressively overcome. Singh et al.13 reported a MHR with the Q-factor of 9.81 million, demonstrating that thermoelastic loss has become a dominant and non-negligible limitation. The Q-factor dominated by thermoelasticity, QTED, is mainly influenced by the geometric shape and temperature of MHR; thus, predicting QTED precisely is essential for device optimization.

Due to the irregular geometric shape of MHR, it is difficult to calculate QTED theoretically. Traditional design methods for predicting QTED of MHRs rely heavily on finite-element analysis (FEA). This typically involves complex processes and a significant amount of computation time. A complex and time-consuming glassblowing simulation (e.g., using ANSYS Polyflow) is used to model the nonlinear thermo-fluidic deformation of the fused silica, using nonlinear modeling by FEA. Shi14 modeled the geometric structure of the MHR by using ANSYS POLYFLOW and studied the deformation of the MHR in different process conditions (heat flow, negative pressure, etc.), which shows high consistency between physical measurement and simulation results. Then, the model of the fabricated resonator is exported and trimmed before being imported into the thermodynamic simulation. And finally, QTED is extracted through a thermodynamic simulation, a process requiring complex mesh generation and precise boundary condition setting. While FEA provides a physics-based approach, the process is extremely time-consuming, with a single simulation often taking several hours. Furthermore, the geometric model obtained from fluid dynamics simulation frequently exhibits irregular features like burrs and uneven thickness, which require manual cleaning and meshing refinement to ensure accuracy in the subsequent loss analysis. This entire workflow must be repeated from scratch for any adjustment of design parameters (e.g., resonator height, anchor radius, edge thickness), making it highly dependent on manual expertise, repetitive, and inefficient for broad design space exploration. Therefore, existing studies on performance in MHRs, such as frequency and QTED, typically rely on limited FEA data and simplistic analysis rather than large-scale finite-element models6,15,16, which may often lead to inaccurate conclusions due to data noise.

Existing models based on artificial intelligence have shown good performance in predicting MEMS’s performance17–22. For instance, Guo et al.23 employed pixelated grayscale images to design disc-shaped resonators. By applying a ResNet50 deep learning model, they achieved average prediction accuracies of 98.8% for resonant frequency and 96.8% for Q-factor, while also reducing computational time by roughly 96%. Alattar et al.24 integrated deep learning techniques with the motion-induced current method to analyze the nonlinear dynamics of an electrostatically actuated microcantilever beam, demonstrating high accuracy in predicting resonant peaks and bifurcation behavior. Di Barba et al.25 developed a convolutional neural network (CNN) as a surrogate model for the electric field in an electrostatic MEMS micromotor, achieving low prediction error and significantly reducing computational cost, particularly in finite-element-based optimization. However, these methods mainly target devices with standardized geometric shapes that are easy to parameterize, efficiently generating large-scale training datasets through automated FEA. In contrast, the complex MHR glassblowing process makes highly accurate simulation extremely costly. Consequently, the obtainable training data is not only severely limited to a few hundred samples but is also inherently noisy due to numerical instabilities in the finite-element analysis. This combination of scarcity and noise presents a significant challenge for small-sample dataset deep learning models, severely reducing their generalization capability.

To overcome the challenge of small-sample learning for QTED prediction, this study proposes an innovative approach that combines data fitting with a CNN-Transformer hybrid deep learning model. The main idea is to expand a limited FEA dataset (314 samples) into a larger, more comprehensive dataset (4501 samples) through polynomial fitting, which effectively enlarges the dataset and reduces data noise. This augmented dataset then trains a hybrid model that leverages the self-attention mechanism of the Transformer to capture global dependencies among input parameters and the powerful spatial feature extraction capability of the CNN to model complex, high-dimensional patterns. While CNN-Transformer architectures have been successful in fields like image or video analysis26–29, their application to non-time-series regression problems in MEMS design remains rare.

Our contributions are as follows: We demonstrate that the proposed CNN-Transformer model trained on the fitted dataset achieves superior prediction accuracy for QTED. An ablation study confirms that the proposed “CNN-Transformer based on fitting” framework outperforms both standalone CNN and Transformer models trained on the fitted data, as well as a CNN-Transformer model trained only on the original FEA data. Comparative experiments further validate its advantages over other classic regression algorithms. Furthermore, the model’s robust uncertainty quantification is demonstrated through the Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout test, which confirmed its strong and reliable predictive performance. Most importantly, actual experimental measurements of fabricated resonators confirm that the variation in QTED aligns with the trend predicted by our deep learning model, verifying its practical utility.

Fabrication process and working principles of the MHR

High-temperature glassblowing fabrication process

The MHR is fabricated using a high-temperature glassblowing process, which involves complex thermomechanical and fluid interactions. As shown in Fig. 1a, the process contains two main stages30,31:

Fabrication technology of the MHR

a The high-temperature blowtorch glassblowing process. b Comparison between the finite-element simulation result and the physical object

(1) A fused silica preform is placed on a graphite mold and locally heated. This softens the fused silica, while a vacuum applied through a small hole helps shape the material.

(2) Driven by surface tension and the pressure differential, the softened fused silica conforms to a hemispherical shape adjacent to the mold.

As shown in Fig. 1, the geometric parameters include the resonator radius R, resonator height H, anchor radius r, and resonator edge thickness T. R is determined by the radius of the hollow area of the mold. H is determined by the duration of the glassblowing process—the longer the glassblowing time, the higher the resonator height. By precisely controlling the glassblowing time, resonators of the desired height can be achieved. r is equivalent to the radius of the central circular pillar in the mold. T corresponds to the thickness of the fused silica sheet.

Data fitting

A primary challenge in applying deep learning to MHR design is the limited size of the training dataset. To overcome this data scarcity, we adopt a data fitting strategy to enhance our dataset. Glassblowing simulations indicate that a single pair of parameters (r, T) can create multiple resonator heights (H), mostly generating 3–7 height data points. Therefore, fitting the relationship between H and QTED allows for dataset augmentation.

The relationship between H and QTED for different combinations of r and T parameters is shown in the scatter plot in Fig. 2a. Four different fitting methods are employed: quadratic fitting, cubic fitting, exponential fitting, and power function fitting. It can be observed from Fig. 2a that the R-squared value (R2) of cubic fitting is the highest among all four methods, indicating the best fitting performance. Therefore, cubic fitting is selected as the final fitting method. We applied this method to all combinations of r and T that contained four or more height data points, generating 100 synthetic data points to replace the original data. For combinations of r and T that contained fewer than four height data points, the original data were retained. Finally, 4501 data samples are obtained after data fitting.

Fitting and validation

a Comparison of different fitting methods. b Random sampling verification of cubic fitting

To demonstrate the effectiveness of cubic fitting, we first remove from the 4501 fitted data points those that are identical to the original 314 data points, leaving 4408 unique points. From these, 20 points are randomly selected and re-evaluated using FEA to calculate the relative error between the fitted values and the simulation results, as shown in Fig. 2b. The mean absolute error is found to be 5.12%, confirming the effectiveness of cubic fitting.

The proposed transform-CNN model for QTED prediction

CNN-Transformer model

Accurately predicting the QTED from their geometric and material parameters is a complex, nonlinear problem. Traditional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) focus on capturing local spatial hierarchies in data but can struggle with complex global interactions between input features, which are critical in a coupled physics problem like thermoelastic loss. To achieve high-precision prediction of the QTED, this study employs a hybrid deep learning architecture that cooperatively combines a Transformer encoder with a CNN. The Transformer is a deep neural network architecture originally proposed by Vaswani et al.34 for sequence-to-sequence tasks in machine translation. The fundamental innovation is the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanism, which dynamically computes a weighted sum of all elements in an input sequence, making the Transformer especially powerful at modeling complex dependencies within data35,36. The core idea of CNN-Transformer is to first allow the CNN to learn rich, low-level feature representations from the input parameter set, and then employ a self-attention mechanism to weigh the importance of these features relative to one another, thereby capturing the complex relationship that governs QTED in MHR. The overall architecture of the proposed CNN-Transformer model is illustrated in Fig. 3a.

The model framework of CNN-Transformer and the ablation study

a The model framework of CNN-Transformer in this paper. b Five experimental configurations of the Transformer encoder. c Comparative evaluation metrics of different models in an ablation study. d Comparative evaluation metrics of different models in an ablation study

The network architecture is designed to progressively transform input parameters into an accurate prediction of QTED. The first convolutional layer captures fundamental spatial relationships, while the second learns more complex, abstract features. These features are then flattened into a sequence and passed to the self-attention layers. This module is critical for modeling the global, nonlinear interactions between all features, allowing the model to understand how different geometric parameters collectively influence thermoelastic loss. Then, an additional layer creates a residual connection, merging the outputs of the first and second MHSA layers. This stabilizes training and mitigates gradient issues, ensuring robust information flow. The resulting features are processed by the regression head (dropout and fully connected layer) to produce the final QTED prediction.

Ablation study

To verify the effectiveness of the CNN-Transformer model we proposed, an ablation study is conducted. Ablation study is crucial for evaluating a proposed model’s final performance, as they systematically deconstruct the architecture to quantify the individual contribution of each proposed component, thereby providing essential insights into what truly drives its predictive power. Given the maturity of CNN architectures, the ablation study focuses specifically on the design of the Transformer component. This study allocates 70% of the data as the training set, 15% of the data as the validation set, and the remaining 15% as the test set. A genetic algorithm (GA) is employed to optimize the hyperparameters in each mode variant. Specifically, the hyperparameters optimized by GA include: the number of CNN channels (selected from 8 to 64 in steps of 8), the number of attention heads in the Transformer (ranging from 1 to 10), the dimension of the key in the multi-head attention mechanism (set as an integer multiple of the number of heads, ranging from 1 to 8 times), the dropout rate (in the continuous range [0, 0.3]), and the number of units in the feed-forward network (FNN) (chosen from 16 to 160 in steps of 16). The GA is configured with a population size of 60, a maximum of 20 generations, and default settings for crossover, mutation, and selection operations. To mitigate the influence of random data partitioning on the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), each hyperparameter configuration is trained three times. The final performance metric is taken as the average RMSE across both the training and test sets from these repetitions. This method effectively reduces bias introduced by data partitioning, ensuring reliable model evaluation outcomes. Figure 3c shows the loss of GA during hyperparameter optimization. The full proposed model clearly achieves the best performance among all tested variants.

The ablation study results, shown in Fig. 3d, demonstrate the performance impact of each architectural modification under the optimal combination of hyperparameters obtained by GA. Model Variant B (Full Proposed Model) achieves the lowest RMSE on both the training set (108.31 ×104) and the test set (118.05 ×104), confirming its superior predictive accuracy and generalization capability. This represents a significant performance improvement, with test set RMSE reduced by 23.80%, 21.19%, 29.56%, and 20.76% compared to the Baseline Model, Variant A, Variant C, and Variant D, respectively. Furthermore, the R2 underscores this superiority, with Model Variant B attaining the highest score (0.9996) on the test set. The ablation steps reveal two key insights: Firstly, removing the layer normalization and the first add layer (Model Variant A) reduces the test set RMSE by 3.3% compared to the Baseline, indicating a slight beneficial effect of simplifying the normalization and residual connections. Secondly, replacing the FFN with a secondary MHSA mechanism makes the full proposed model (Variant B) significantly reduce the test set RMSE by 21.19% compared to Variant A, confirming the effectiveness of the dual-attention design. Conversely, restoring layer normalization (Variant C) or removing the remaining add layer (Variant D) leads to increased RMSE, highlighting the importance of the specific architectural choices in the proposed model.

Comparative study

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed CNN-Transformer method, a comprehensive comparative study is conducted against three established deep learning models: a standard CNN, a standard Transformer, and a CNN-LSTM (Long-Short Term Memory) hybrid. The CNN serves as a baseline for localized spatial feature extraction, utilizing its well-known ability to capture hierarchical patterns from structured input data. The Transformer model, built solely on self-attention mechanisms, tests the capability of global dependency modeling without convolutional inductive bias. The CNN-LSTM combines convolutional layers for feature extraction with LSTM blocks to capture temporal or sequential dependencies, representing a common hybrid approach for processing structured sequential data37,38.

For a fair and rigorous comparison, all models are trained and evaluated under identical conditions. To eliminate performance bias, each model undergoes a systematic hyperparameter optimization process by using GA, ensuring every model is evaluated in its best configuration, as shown in Fig. 4a.

The CNN-Transformer model delivers a significant reduction in computational cost, dramatically accelerating the design evaluation process. For the original 314 sets of data, traditional high-fidelity simulation of the glassblowing process and the subsequent calculation of the QTED present a significant computational bottleneck, requiring approximately 250 h and 200 h, respectively. In contrast, the proposed data-driven framework completes the hyperparameter optimization in 8 h and model training in merely 2 min. Once trained, the model generates QTED predictions for 4501 data augmentation within approximately 0.2 s. This efficiency greatly and effectively achieves rapid prediction of QTED, and the evaluation speed is much faster than that of finite-element simulation.

Having established the predictive accuracy of the CNN-Transformer model, we next employ it to systematically investigate the influence of key geometric dimensions on the QTED in MHRs. This section demonstrates the relationship between design parameters and resonator performance.

Changes in a single parameter

Figure 5a–c, respectively, investigate the impact of H, T, and r variation on QTED. Within the allowable range of error, QTED decreases with the increase of H and increases with the increase of T, as shown in Fig. 5a and b. This trend can be explained by the interplay between structural stiffness and thermal gradient dissipation. An increase in T enhances the resonator’s bending stiffness, thereby reducing strain energy dissipation, while a greater H may amplify the surface-to-volume ratio or alter the modal strain distribution, intensifying the thermoelastic coupling loss. Figure 5c shows the relationship between QTED and r; however, QTED does not strictly decrease monotonically as r increases. In some intervals of r, QTED will increase instead with the increase of r. This non-monotonic behavior suggests that within certain intervals, an increase in r may alter the strain energy distribution or thermal relaxation path, temporarily reducing dissipation and leading to a local increase in QTED.

In MHR design, where geometric parameters are often constrained, understanding the parameter interactions becomes essential. For instance, while increasing T can effectively change the resonant frequency and enhance QTED, excessive thickness may compromise other performance aspects. In such cases, simultaneous adjustment of H and T offers an alternative pathway for frequency tuning. This approach, however, requires careful consideration of how the combined effect of H and T influences QTED.

To investigate the complex interaction effects between structural parameters in depth, we adopt a dual-parameter coupling sensitivity analysis method. Dual-parameter analysis can reveal the synergistic or antagonistic effects between parameters, which is crucial for understanding the nonlinear behavior of multi-parameter systems.

Furthermore, the average interaction strength, represented by average |Sij|, varies with parameter values. It decreases rapidly with high H in the r-T interaction, increases rapidly with low r in the H-T interaction, and decreases rapidly with high T in the r-H interaction. These trends indicate that parametric interactions are generally weaker in regions of high H, high T, and low r.

Physical interpretation of parameter interactions

The synergistic and antagonistic effects observed in the dual-parameter analysis can be understood by examining the underlying physics of thermoelastic damping. According to Zener’s theory and its extensions for micro-resonators, the thermoelastic quality factor QTED is governed by the coupling between the mechanical strain field and the resulting temperature field. For an isotropic elastic material like fused silica, the thermoelastic dissipation can be expressed through the thermal elastic damping coefficient, which in the frequency domain is related to the imaginary part of the complex frequency.

The interaction between parameters emerges when changing two parameters simultaneously alters these mechanisms in ways that are not simply additive.

Increasing T suppresses bending strains (beneficial for QTED), while increasing H enhances membrane strains and alters thermal relaxation (generally detrimental for QTED). When both are increased together, the beneficial effect of T is partially offset by the detrimental effect of H, leading to a net change that is smaller than the linear sum.

When it comes to r, after the anchor point is moved outward, the length of the meridian from the edge of the anchor point to the free edge of the shell decreases, and the same vibration mode is compressed into a shorter geometric interval. The amplitude of the volume strain εkk is enhanced in the local area, resulting in a decrease in QTED.

When r and H are increased simultaneously, the stretching caused by the larger H counteracts the compression induced by the larger r. The displacement gradients, which would otherwise rise due to the shortened arc, are instead kept in check by the overall elongation of the mode shape. Moreover, the outward movement of the anchor reduces the curvature of the shell near the clamped edge, diminishing those contributions to εkk that involve curvature (e.g., terms related to the tangential displacement and local geometry). This curvature reduction combines with the global stretching to produce a multiplicative suppression of the volumetric strain amplitude. Although the total volume of the shell increases slightly, the significant decrease in εkk per unit volume outweighs the volume enlargement, so the integrated dissipation increases less than the sum of the increases caused by each parameter alone. Consequently, the drop in QTED is attenuated, manifesting as a positive interaction (synergism) between r and H.

Inspiration from design guidance

For MHR design, the goal is to achieve a high Q-factor while minimizing performance sensitivity to manufacturing variations. As surface loss treatment technology advances, Q-factor can reach levels on the order of 107, making the contribution of thermoelastic loss (QTED) increasingly significant. The preceding analysis indicates that an optimal QTED is achieved with a design featuring low H, low r, and high thickness T. However, practical constraints must be reconciled: a low H may degrade gyroscopic precession performance, a small r can affect assembly precision at the anchor, and an excessively high T introduces challenges in fabrication and increases the resonator’s mass. These trade-offs must be carefully balanced in the final design.

The synergism ratio offers clear value for active parameter optimization, as regions of high synergy allow efficient co-adjustment of multiple parameters. In contrast, for managing passive variations due to manufacturing tolerances, the average |Sij| serves as a more critical indicator of design robustness. In practice, the r is often constrained by the fixed geometry of the graphite mold. Meanwhile, manufacturing tolerances in fused silica and process control during glassblowing primarily affect T and H. Therefore, maintaining a low value of r is recommended to reduce performance fluctuations arising from the interaction between T and H, thereby enhancing manufacturing robustness.

Conclusion

In this work, we have developed and validated a deep learning-based framework for the rapid and accurate prediction of QTED in MHR. The core of this framework is a hybrid CNN-Transformer model, uniquely trained on a dataset expanded through data fitting from high-fidelity FEA simulations. The ablation study validates the core design of the proposed architecture: removing layer normalization provided an initial gain, while replacing the FFN with a secondary MHSA mechanism yielded the highest performance. The performance decline when restoring normalization or removing residual connections further confirms the optimality of the final model. In comparative experiments, this optimized CNN-Transformer outperformed other deep learning architectures (CNN, Transformer, CNN-LSTM) in prediction accuracy (achieving the highest R2 and lowest RMSE). This performance is achieved using enhanced training data, which significantly improves prediction accuracy over the original dataset. Furthermore, the model demonstrates well-calibrated reliability, with MC Dropout analysis confirming low predictive uncertainty through narrow confidence intervals.

A comprehensive study is conducted on the influence and interaction of geometric parameters on QTED using the high-fidelity prediction capability of the CNN-Transformer model. The analysis of single-parameter effects reveals distinct trends: QTED decreases with H and increases with T, while r exhibits a complex, non-monotonic relationship. Global sensitivity analysis quantifies this, identifying T as the most influential parameter with a dominant positive effect, followed by H with a strong negative influence, and finally r with a mixed-sign, variable impact. The interaction between dual parameters is systematically explored. The influence of T and H on QTED significantly outweighs that of r. Notably, the contour analysis indicates that the effects of T and H are comparable in magnitude, while the impact of increasing r is limited and becomes slightly more pronounced at larger T or smaller H. Further analysis of interaction types shows that H and T primarily compete, whereas r can exhibit synergy with either T or H under specific conditions. The average |Sij| is weakest in regions of high H, high T, and low r, offering a key metric for robustness. Additionally, the interaction mechanisms are analyzed among parameters based on physical interpretations.

Therefore, the optimal configuration for maximizing QTED is characterized by low H, low r, and high T. In practical design, this must be balanced against constraints: low H may affect gyroscopic performance, small r can challenge assembly precision, and high T increases mass and fabrication difficulty. To enhance manufacturability, maintaining a low r is recommended to mitigate performance sensitivity to the more variable fabrication parameters T and H.

In conclusion, this work establishes a data-enhanced CNN-Transformer framework that provides accurate predictions of QTED in milliseconds with extraordinary computational efficiency, whereas traditional FEA requires hundreds of hours. By elucidating the quantitative effects and complex interactions of geometric parameters, it provides a powerful, efficient, and insightful paradigm for performance optimization and manufacturability-conscious design of the next generation of high-QTED MHRs. Future work will extend this framework to systematically investigate the influence of temperature, a key factor affecting QTED, thereby further broadening its applicability and predictive capability.
