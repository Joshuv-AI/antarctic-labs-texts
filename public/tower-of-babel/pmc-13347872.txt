MDVM-UNet: lumbar MRI segmentation and lordosis angle measurement via a dual-driven mechanism
Authors: Dongsheng Ji, Yan Zong, Furao Qian, Xiangzeng Chen, Hao Liu, Penghao Chao, Ting Han, Wenhao Fan, Yupu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rising incidence of degenerative lumbar spine disorders, accurate segmentation of spinal structures based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is crucial for intelligent clinical diagnosis and surgical planning, while automated measurement of the lumbar lordosis angle based on segmentation can further support quantitative assessment of the condition. To overcome the issues of limited receptive field and loss of detail in existing deep learning methods when processing low-resolution, edge-blurred images, this paper proposes a segmentation architecture called MDVM-UNet. This architecture integrates three complementary mechanisms: the VSS module constructs a computationally efficient, multi-scale collaborative receptive field through parallel multi-scale hole convolution; the dual-path fusion module performs feature alignment using global average pooling and channel-spatial attention; and the edge enhancement module sharpens blurred boundaries through reverse attention and Laplacian pyramid decomposition. Experiments conducted on both private and public lumbar MRI datasets demonstrate that this method achieves excellent segmentation results for vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs, with an average Dice coefficient of 0.943, a 6.4% improvement over the standard U-Net;The mean absolute error between the lumbar lordosis angle measured automatically based on segmentation results and the manually annotated measurements was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.23^{\circ}$$\end{document}, which is below the clinically acceptable threshold. Overall, the method described in this paper demonstrates excellent performance in both segmentation accuracy and clinical quantification, offering a viable approach for the intelligent assessment of degenerative lumbar spine diseases.

Introduction

Degenerative diseases of the lumbar spine are one of the leading causes of pain and disability among middle-aged and older adults [1]. Degenerative changes in the intervertebral discs, accompanied by structural alterations such as osteophytes, may disrupt the alignment of the spinal axis, typically resulting in a reduction in the lumbar lordosis angle [2]. Accurate identification and delineation of the boundaries between vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs are prerequisites for subsequent biomechanical parameter assessment. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for evaluating lumbar spine anatomy, current clinical practice relies heavily on manual segmentation. This process is not only extremely time-consuming but is also significantly influenced by inter-observer variability, which compromises diagnostic consistency [3]. Therefore, developing a segmentation system capable of automatically and accurately delineating key structures of the lumbar spine is a critical step toward improving the objectivity and efficiency of the diagnosis and treatment of lumbar spine disorders, and it also serves as the foundation for subsequent quantitative analysis [4].

Since its introduction, the U-Net framework [5] has become a cornerstone of medical image segmentation. Its intuitive encoder-decoder architecture, combined with skip connections, effectively preserves fine spatial details and consistently produces high-quality segmentation results. However, as segmentation tasks have become increasingly complex, often involving larger images and finer anatomical structures, the standard U-Net has gradually revealed its limitations [6]. To address these challenges, Transformer-based models [7] such as TransUnet [8], TransFuse [9], and Swin-Unet [10] have emerged one after another. These methods combine the global context-aware capabilities of Transformers with the local feature extraction capabilities of CNNs, capturing long-range dependencies while preserving key local details. However, Transformer models introduce a significant practical constraint [11]: their computational cost scales quadratically with image size, and the resulting resource requirements often hinder their deployment in real-world clinical settings where efficiency is paramount. In contrast, state-space models (SSMs) have a complexity that grows linearly with sequence length, making them a more attractive backbone network for clinical deployment.

However, the challenges don’t end there. The more fundamental issue is that most studies remain limited to simply improving segmentation accuracy [12], with few extending to the next critical clinical quantitative assessment, such as the automatic measurement of the lumbar lordosis angle. Even though segmentation algorithms have achieved fairly good results, clinical workflows often still revert to time-consuming and labor-intensive manual measurements performed by physicians. This reintroduces subjective variability and creates process bottlenecks, undermining the efficiency and consistency that the intelligent diagnostic process was intended to provide. This gap between “segmentation” and “quantification” is particularly evident in the measurement of the lumbar lordotic angle.

To address these challenges, we propose MDVM-UNet, a novel framework based on a state-space model architecture that achieves robust and accurate segmentation under complex pathological conditions and establishes a fully automated workflow from segmentation to quantification. Unlike existing Mamba-based segmentation methods that rely solely on SSM for global context modeling, we integrate multi-scale dilated convolutions and a dual-path attention mechanism into the encoder-decoder architecture to accommodate the variable and ambiguous pathological features of the lumbar spine. More importantly, unlike existing work that stops at generating segmentation masks, we connect the segmentation output to a rule-based geometric analysis module capable of automatically and accurately calculating the lordotic angles of individual vertebral segments as well as the overall lumbar curvature. This integrated design provides an end-to-end solution from image data to clinical applications, Fig. 1 provides an overview of the proposed framework. Related work

Models based on convolutional neural network (CNN)

Convolutional neural networks extract image features through local filters and a layered architecture, converting pixels into semantic information [13]. In medical image segmentation, U-Net has driven advancements in the field through its symmetric encoder-decoder architecture and skip connections [14]. Variants such as Attention U-Net and TransUNet [15] have extended these capabilities to multimodal tasks [16], while SegNet [17] has improved decoding efficiency in resource-constrained environments. U-Net combines spatial attention and residual structures [18] to achieve more precise segmentation in areas such as lumbar spine imaging. However, CNNs’ inherent local field of view limits the modeling of long-range dependencies, often resulting in blurred boundaries and loss of detail [19]. In high-risk applications such as medical diagnosis [20], this flaw may compromise the reliability of subsequent analyses, particularly in lumbar spine images where the contrast between the vertebral body and the intervertebral disc is low and the morphology is highly variable.

Transformer-based models

The Transformer’s self-attention mechanism [21] overcomes the local constraints of earlier networks by processing all elements in a sequence in parallel, thereby reshaping the field of deep learning [22]. This not only captures a broader context but also helps mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and enhances the model’s representational capacity [23]. Vision Transformer [24] introduced this architecture to computer vision and achieved significant success in image classification. Subsequently, models such as SwinUnet extended this approach to segmentation tasks, significantly enhancing global modeling capabilities. However, Transformer-based segmentation methods [25] still face significant computational challenges, as the complexity of self-attention scales quadratically with image resolution, leading to high memory consumption and slow inference. This limitation hinders its deployment in real-time medical imaging scenarios, highlighting the need for a lightweight design.

The Mamba model

The Mamba architecture [26] has garnered attention for its linear computational complexity when capturing long-range dependencies, which reduces computational costs, accelerates training and inference, and holds significant value for medical image segmentation. Frameworks such as Vim and VMamba [27] have simplified the design while maintaining performance on par with Vision Transformers, thereby advancing the development of global context modeling [28]. Building on this foundation, VM-UNet [29] and its subsequent versions [30] integrated Mamba into UNet, demonstrating potential in medical image analysis. However, existing Mamba-based segmentation models primarily focus on global context modeling and lack specific handling of the blurred boundaries and multiscale pathological features commonly found in lumbar MRI. To address these shortcomings, the method proposed in this paper employs the Laplace operator [31] and multi-scale hollow convolution to extract edge and contextual features [32], and refines them using channel and spatial attention mechanisms to achieve effective local-global fusion, with the aim of improving efficiency and generalization capabilities on clinical datasets.

Method

Overall architecture

To address issues such as low resolution, blurred structural boundaries, and diverse lesion morphologies in lumbar spine MRI images, as well as the limited field of view and loss of detail in traditional models, this paper proposes the MDVM-UNet segmentation architecture. This architecture is based on an encoder-decoder framework and employs a three-level progressive design that incorporates global modeling of the visual state space (VSS), multi-scale receptive field optimization, and cross-layer edge-guided enhancement. This approach enables layer-by-layer feature enhancement, cross-layer information coupling, and global-local coordination, thereby forming a unified feature learning pathway. The encoder consists of four layers, each built on the VSS module [33]. It performs progressive downsampling through block embedding and block merging, enriching the semantic representation while reducing the dimensionality of the compressed space. The parameters for each stage are shown in Table 1. Unlike existing Mamba methods, this paper embeds the multi-scale void feature aggregation component MDFA within each VSS module to fuse void convolution features from different receptive fields with global contextual cues, thereby enabling the model to maintain both local accuracy and global structural awareness. The decoder employs a symmetric four-stage upsampling design, with skip connections that introduce corresponding encoder features into each decoding layer. An Edge-Guided Attention (EGA) module [34] is applied at each skip connection to sharpen boundary definitions and recover fine details that may be lost during downsampling. This design allows the multiscale context from MDFA to guide EGA in accurately localizing edges. In return, the boundaries refined by EGA help MDFA distinguish adjacent vertebral bodies, thereby forming a bidirectional synergistic feedback loop. This bidirectional closed-loop system, together with the embedded fusion within VSS, forms a methodologically novel collaborative optimization paradigm that achieves tightly coupled joint optimization, including global state-space modeling, multi-scale receptive field adaptation, and edge-a priori guidance, through an embedded feedback mechanism. Compared to existing methods, the core contribution of this paper lies in embedding MDFA within VSS, introducing EGA closed-loop edge enhancement, and implementing a three-stage progressive end-to-end collaborative optimization, rather than simply combining known modules. State space model SSM

VSS serves as the fundamental building block of MDVM-UNet, enabling the joint representation of long-range structural dependencies and local anatomical details while maintaining linear computational complexity. In the context of discrete-state space modeling, state-space models (SSMs) model [35] sequences using a linear time-invariant system driven by hidden states [36]. The system maps the input sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_t$$\end{document} to the hidden state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document}, and then outputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_t$$\end{document}: 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t = A h_{t-1} + B x_t $$\end{document}2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_t = C h_t$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$$\end{document} is the state transition matrix, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document} are the projection parameters. To accommodate discrete data in deep learning, zero-order holding (ZOH) is employed to transform the continuous system parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(A, B)$$\end{document} into their discrete equivalents \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\bar{A}, \bar{B})$$\end{document}, thereby ensuring numerical stability. When combined with parallel scanning algorithms, this architecture enables SSM to efficiently process long sequences with linear computational complexity [37] while capturing global spatial dependencies [38]. It is particularly well-suited for the dual requirements of detail preservation and computational efficiency in high-resolution medical images, as shown in Fig. 3. In terms of dual-branch complementary extraction, VSS employs two parallel branches: the state-scanning branch models long-range structural relationships, such as the order of the pyramids and the overall morphology of the spine—along the sequence dimension. The deep separable convolutional branches preserve local fine-scale features, such as the texture of the periosteum and intervertebral discs, through lightweight convolutions. The two output channels are finally subjected to a nonlinear activation function for adaptive feature fusion, enabling the base features to simultaneously capture global structural semantics and preserve local details, thereby providing a richer representational foundation for subsequent multi-scale aggregation and edge enhancement.Fig. 3(a) The scan expanding operation in SS2D. MDFA module

To address the challenges posed by the wide variation in the structural dimensions of the lumbar spine and the diffuse nature of pathological regions, an MDFA mechanism has been integrated into the VSS to enable dynamic field-of-view expansion and multi-scale information fusion [44]. As shown in Fig. 5, the MDFA module extracts multiscale contextual information through five parallel convolutional paths: one standard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times1$$\end{document} convolution preserves spatial fidelity, while three \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times3$$\end{document} dilated convolutions with progressively increasing dilation ratios (6, 12, and 18) expand the receptive field to capture broader contextual cues [45]. Additionally, a global average pooling branch is included to encode overall scene statistics. After these multi-scale features are concatenated along the channel dimension, they are refined through channel-spatial joint attention. Channel attention reweights the information channels via global pooling and two fully connected layers, while spatial attention highlights salient regions. The features modulated by the two attention channels are combined via element-wise addition, then further fused with the original concatenated features through a residual connection, and finally projected via a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times1$$\end{document} convolution to generate an enhanced output representation, thereby achieving residual fusion output. The aforementioned multi-scale feature fusion can be expressed as: 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{cat}} = \operatorname{Concat}(F_{1\times1}, F_{3\times3}^6, F_{3\times3}^{12}, F_{3\times3}^{18}, F_{\mathrm{global}})$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{enhanced}} = F_{\mathrm{cat}} \odot \max(M_{\mathrm{channel}}, M_{\mathrm{spatial}})$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{output}} = W_{\mathrm{fusion}} * F_{\mathrm{enhanced}} + b_{\mathrm{fusion}}$$\end{document}Fig. 5Multi-scale dilated convolution branches extract features. Loss function

During training, we employ a combined Dice-CE loss function, called DiceCELoss, to address the common issue of class imbalance in medical image segmentation tasks and improve segmentation accuracy. This composite loss combines the strengths of the Dice loss and cross-entropy loss in a weighted manner, while optimizing both region overlap and pixel-level classification. The Dice loss evaluates segmentation overlap by calculating the Dice similarity coefficient between the predicted mask and the ground-truth label [46], defined as follows: 9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum_{i=1}^N p_i g_i + \epsilon}{\sum_{i=1}^N p_i + \sum_{i=1}^N g_i + \epsilon}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} denotes the predicted probability of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document}-th pixel, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_i$$\end{document} is its ground-truth label, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} is a small constant used to avoid division by zero. The cross-entropy loss focuses on pixel-level classification to capture fine-grained local details, and is expressed as: 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ g_i \log p_i + (1 - g_i) \log (1 - p_i) \right]$$\end{document}

The final total loss function balances the contributions of both terms via the hyperparameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}: 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Total}} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} + (1 - \alpha) \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$$\end{document}

In the experiment, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.5$$\end{document} was set to ensure a balanced contribution from the two loss terms. The auxiliary loss applied to each decoder layer in the aforementioned EGA module is optimized in conjunction with the main loss, with the auxiliary loss focusing on enhancing the learning of edge features, while the main loss is responsible for the overall optimization of the final segmentation output. This composite loss function helps the model simultaneously preserve the overall shape of the target anatomical structure and maintain the accuracy of boundary details during backpropagation. It is particularly well-suited for addressing the anatomical complexity and size variability commonly encountered in vertebral and intervertebral disc segmentation, thereby facilitating the robust delineation of fine structural details.

Experiments

Dataset

This study utilized two lumbar spine MRI datasets: one is the publicly available lumbar spine dataset from Mendeley Data, and the other is a proprietary dataset created in-house. The public dataset contains T1- and T2-weighted sagittal images and pixel-level annotations for 514 subjects, covering a variety of pathological types. The images feature clear anatomical details but also exhibit complex background noise and significant inter-individual morphological variations, making them highly valuable for generalization testing and presenting significant segmentation challenges. This private dataset was retrospectively collected from a hospital in Lanzhou, China, and comprises 308 cases of raw clinical lumbar spine imaging data. The data collection process was anonymized, and informed consent was obtained from the patients. As shown in Table 2, this dataset includes cases of normal lumbar spine, disc degeneration, Schmorl’s nodes, osteophytes, mixed lesions, and various other lumbar spine conditions; representative samples are shown in Fig. 7. The patients ranged in age from 18 to 60 years and included both men and women. Following evaluation by a senior radiologist, typical sagittal sections clearly showing the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs were selected from each examination. Experimental details

The experimental setup was implemented in PyTorch. All training and validation runs were executed on a high-performance workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory, under Ubuntu 22.04 LTS with CUDA 12.1. We used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-3 and weight decay of 1e-4. Training proceeded for 200 epochs with a batch size of 2, which offered a reasonable trade-off between memory usage and convergence stability. During training, Adam adjusts per-parameter learning rates by maintaining estimates of the first and second moments of the gradients, allowing adaptive step sizes throughout the optimization process.

All images in the public dataset were uniformly resized to 320\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}320 pixels, and all lumbar spine images in the private dataset were uniformly resized to 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 pixels to ensure spatial consistency. Each pixel value represents a corresponding anatomical structure category, comprising a total of 11 categories: 5 categories for the vertebrae (L1 to L5) and 5 categories for the intervertebral discs (L1/L2 to L5/S1). The dataset is divided using stratified sampling, with the training set containing 1,660 samples, the validation set containing 553 samples, and the independent test set containing 553 samples, to ensure a balanced distribution across all categories. To improve the model’s generalization ability, online data augmentation is applied during the training phase. This includes random rotation by integer multiples of 90 degrees, horizontal flipping, and vertical flipping, each with a probability of 0.5. The brightness scaling factor is uniformly sampled between 0.75 and 1.25, with a probability of 0.5. Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 8 is added, with a probability of 0.5. Random translations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm$$\end{document}50 pixels were applied with a probability of 0.5. Additionally, arbitrary rotations were applied, with rotation angles uniformly sampled between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-45^{\circ}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+45^{\circ}$$\end{document} at a probability of 0.5. All geometric transformations were applied simultaneously to both the image and the mask, with bicubic interpolation used for the image and nearest-neighbor interpolation used for the mask to preserve label integrity. During the validation phase, only channel selection and dimensionality expansion were performed; no data augmentation was applied.

Evaluation metrics

This study evaluates segmentation results using three core metrics: precision, intersection-over-union (IoU), and the Dice coefficient. Each metric is based on traditional classification categories such as true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. Precision reflects the overall accuracy of pixel-level classification across the entire image. IoU measures the spatial consistency of a given anatomical structure by comparing the ratio of the overlapping area between the predicted region and the ground-truth region to the total area. The Dice coefficient is often emphasized in the field of medical imaging because it exhibits greater sensitivity to overlapping regions in cases of severe foreground-background imbalance, making it a reliable choice for evaluating small, critical anatomical targets. Since IoU and Dice are relatively insensitive to uneven class distributions, they are particularly well-suited for evaluating the segmentation accuracy of compact spinal structures such as vertebrae and intervertebral discs. The corresponding formulas are as follows: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Precision} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} $$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP + FN} $$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2 \times TP}{2 \times TP + FP + FN}$$\end{document}

TP, TN, FP, and FN represent true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Both IoU and Dice range from 0 to 1; higher values indicate better spatial correspondence between the segmentation results and the ground truth annotations.

When segmenting medical images of the lumbar spine, it is crucial to accurately delineate the contours of the vertebrae and intervertebral discs, as each structure exhibits unique pathological features in pathological conditions. For vertebrae, maintaining the integrity of their shape and clear margins is particularly important in cases of degeneration, Schmorl’s nodes, and osteophyte formation, as subtle changes in their contours often reflect the severity of the condition. With regard to intervertebral discs, key morphological features—such as posterior bulging during disc herniation, extrusion of the nucleus pulposus, and a reduction in disc height—provide crucial information about the condition of the disc and potential nerve compression. Our model is capable of accurately segmenting vertebrae and intervertebral discs, clearly revealing disease-specific anatomical changes to support clinical diagnosis and treatment planning. When VM–Unet was enhanced with the MDFA module, measurable gains in performance were observed. At the L5/S1 level, Precision increased to 0.884, intersection–over–union reached 0.816, and the Dice coefficient rose to 0.894, due largely to the module’s dynamic adjustment of the receptive field through parallel multi–dilated convolutions and its ability to integrate multi–scale features for improved structural detail, a pattern also reflected at the L1 level, where corresponding values of 0.971, 0.929, and 0.963 were achieved. The version incorporating the EGA module also exhibited progress, especially in handling ambiguous boundaries, attaining an IOU of 0.776 and a Dice of 0.874 at L5/S1. Ultimately, the full MDVM–Unet, which combines both modules, produced the strongest overall outcome at this anatomically challenging level, with Precision of 0.918, IOU of 0.817, and Dice of 0.900. These results point to a balanced improvement in segmentation precision, edge preservation, and computational efficiency, affirming the design’s coherence and practical utility.

Discussion

In terms of verifying angle measurements, this method has preliminarily demonstrated the reliability of automated angle measurement. In the future, we plan to involve multiple spinal surgeons to independently measure lumbar lordosis angles. We will use the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to systematically assess inter-observer variability and the agreement between our model and expert assessments. Additionally, we intend to validate the generalizability of this method using larger-scale, multicenter clinical data to facilitate its implementation in real-world clinical settings.

Although the model performs well overall, errors can still occur in certain challenging cases. The failure is primarily due to detection errors at the endplate margins caused by extensive osteophytes, or the limitations of linear fitting in cases of severe wedge-shaped deformation or irregular endplates. We will address these issues through a series of technical upgrades. Future work will focus on incorporating adversarial training to enhance the model’s robustness to extreme anatomical variations, adopting adaptive curve fitting in place of traditional linear fitting strategies to more accurately describe complex endplate morphologies, and exploring multimodal image fusion methods to improve segmentation and measurement performance under low-quality image conditions. In addition, we will conduct statistical significance tests on the differences in segmentation performance between our model and the various baseline methods to provide stronger statistical evidence of the model’s superiority. Through these systematic improvements, this method is expected to enable stable and accurate automated measurements in more challenging clinical settings.

Conclusion

This research addresses several persistent issues in lumbar MRI segmentation, such as image resolution limitations, indistinct anatomical edges, and the diverse appearance of pathological structures by proposing MDVM-UNet, an integrated deep learning framework designed with multi-scale feature learning and targeted edge-refinement mechanisms. Through this approach, the model demonstrates reliable delineation of both vertebral bodies L1–L5 and intervertebral discs L1/L2–L5/S1, and builds on these segmentation outcomes to automate the calculation of lumbar lordosis angles. As a result, it may offer clinicians a potentially useful instrument to assist in diagnostic evaluation and to enable more quantitative analysis of degenerative spinal conditions.
