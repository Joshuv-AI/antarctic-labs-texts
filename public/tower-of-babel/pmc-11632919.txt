Provider perspectives on maternal care challenges for Black and Latine women in Indiana: a qualitative interview study
Authors: Lucia Guerra-Reyes, Rossmary D Márquez-Lameda, Ruhun Wasata, Oakley Byrne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndiana consistently ranks among the states with the highest maternal and infant mortality in the United States, particularly affecting Black and Latine communities, endangering their right to safe and respectful maternal care. Providers working with these communities are crucial in identifying challenges faced by their clients, and to inform programs and policies. We interviewed 32 clinical and community-based providers from February to April 2021 to understand their perspectives on the challenges faced by their Black and Latine clients. Participants were identified through online sources and referrals. Interviews, conducted over online video, were recorded, transcribed, and analysed following a six-step thematic approach. Six areas of challenges emerged: non-medical infrastructure and policy problems, effects of systemic racism and bias, insecurity of the Latine community, dissatisfaction with maternal care delivery, issues in navigating maternal healthcare, and limitations to holistic models of care. The results demonstrate the interconnected structural, organisational, and interpersonal nature of the challenges. Though challenges were described at structural and organisational levels, the focus of state maternal care programs is largely at personal and interpersonal levels. Obstetric racism, citizenship restrictions, shortage of Black and Latine providers, and transportation issues are complex problems, requiring multilevel interventions and policies to ensure Black and Latine women can exercise their right to safe and respectful maternal care. A rights-based approach centring the needs of Black, Latine and other minoritised communities should be implemented to make effective changes with an equity focus.Keywords: maternal health, Black, Latine, women, Indiana, United States, provider perspectives

Introduction

Ensuring the human right to safe and respectful maternity care is a key government responsibility, and should be central in maternal health programs and policies.1,2 Maternal mortality serves as a key indicator of a country’s capacity to guarantee this right. The United States (US) is one of very few countries with significant increases in maternal mortality in the last 20 years,3 indicating struggles in their ability to safeguard this right, especially for Black, Latine,* and Indigenous women.4 Indiana, in particular, faces deep challenges in ensuring safe maternal care for minoritised women and infants,¶ consistently ranking among the highest for mortality.δ,5

For example, in 2021 Black mothers, though representing only 13% of all live births, experienced a maternal mortality rate of 156.3 per 100,000 live births – more than 50% above the state average (100.1 deaths per 100 live births) and 73% higher than White women (90.7 per 100,000 live births). Latine women had the lowest rate at 79.4 per 100,000 live births, but saw a marked increase from the previous 4-year average of 55.8 deaths per 100,000 live births.6 Similarly, the average infant mortality rate for all races between 2019 and 2021 was 6.7 per 1000 live births, whereas for the same period the Black infant mortality rate was the highest at 11.1 per 1000 live births, the Latine infant death rate was 6.2, while White infant mortality rate was 6 per 1000 live births.7

Since 2017, Indiana has implemented various initiatives to address these disparities, including maternal mortality review committees, enhanced training for obstetrical emergencies, and competitive grants to fund hospitals, health centres and non-profit organisations to reduce infant deaths.8 These efforts have expanded care options outside of the clinical sphere, through home visiting programs and community-based providers such as doulas. Yet, as earlier data indicate, significant disparities remain.

The experiences of providers of maternal care who work with Black and Latine women in Indiana can offer insights into the challenges faced by their clients and guide effective interventions.

This study adopts a reproductive rights and justice framework, recognising that maternal health outcomes are deeply intertwined with race, gender, and class, influencing both the perspectives of providers and of their Black and Latine clients, and their ability to enact decisions about childbirth and parenting in safe and supportive environments.9

Maternal health of minoritised women in the USA

Recent research and policy efforts have focused on the maternal health of minoritised populations – social and ethnic groups that are placed in subordinate positions or marginalised through systemic oppression.10 Researchers have identified societal and health system factors as primary barriers for minoritised women, particularly Black women, in securing safe and supportive maternal care.

Obstetric racism and discrimination in healthcare are frequent issues for Black, and other minoritised, women.11–18 They have been linked with dismissive and disrespectful treatment, lapses in clinical judgement, and medical abuse.18,19 Black women also report that providers withhold detailed information about both their own care and the health of their babies.19 This asymmetry in information exchange weakens patient–provider relationships.19–21 Language barriers, particularly for immigrants and refugees, further complicate interactions.22 Additionally, inadequate culturally tailored health care and limited cross-cultural understanding contribute to miscommunication and misunderstanding.23 Research indicates that minoritised women are also blamed for poor maternal health outcomes due to stereotypes about age, health, and weight.19,24

Income inequality exacerbates these issues by impacting insurance coverage and ability to pay. Cost of access to maternity care is exacerbated by lack of accommodations and paid leave.11,23 Minoritised women living in maternity care deserts, areas with limited or no access to maternal health services, are doubly affected by insurance limitations and transportation problems.21

To address these issues effectively, it is crucial to understand the Indiana-specific challenges faced by Black and Latine women. However, research on this topic is limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on maternal and infant mortality, treating maternal care as a related factor. Renbarger et al25 interviewed 20 predominantly non-Hispanic White women in Indiana to explore their views on maternal mortality and their perceived risk of death. They found that while participants did not generally feel at risk of death until complications arose, they did feel dismissed and unheard by maternal care providers. Another study, involving nurses from an Indiana professional organisation, found that they did not recognise maternal death as a common outcome, unless they had personal experience with patient complications, and they considered any post-discharge issues as the patient’s responsibility.26 A 2022 report from the Indiana University Policy Institute identified three key factors contributing to maternal mortality in the state: limited access to maternal health services, inadequate maternal mental health and substance abuse care, and discriminatory attitudes and lack of diversity among the health care workforce.27 Lastly, a recent study analysing diverse perspectives on maternal mortality in Indiana identified additional barriers: lack of continuous quality healthcare coverage; racism and implicit bias, insufficient trauma and mental health services, lack of support systems, limited knowledge for self-advocacy, and poor data transparency and reliability.28

This article aims to further elucidate the challenges faced by Black and Latine women in Indiana when seeking safe and respectful maternal care, through the perspective of their care providers. Due to COVID-19 restrictions in early 2021, the possibility of our direct engagement with community women was limited, making provider perspectives especially valuable in this discussion. Care providers, broadly understood, have insider knowledge of local health systems, a close view of the challenges faced by their clients, and the ability to speak to broader trends as they deal with many patients. Indeed, their perspectives are of particular importance for effective local interventions.

Methods

The aim of this qualitative interview study was to describe and analyse provider perspectives on the challenges faced by their Black and Latine clients seeking maternal care in Indiana. The study was part of a larger project aiming to understand the ecology of maternal care for Black and Latine women in Indiana.

We approached the study design through a broadly constructivist theoretical perspective and an interpretive description methodological approach.29,30 We focused on a broad range of clinical and community providers. Our research design was informed by a reproductive justice lens. For us this meant making ongoing and explicit efforts to seek out organisations and individuals that mentioned equity, rights, and reproductive justice in their statements, and actively engaging providers that were themselves from Black and Latine communities.

The research team, based in a local university, was an all-female, multinational, and multilingual group. The lead author is a Latine immigrant medical anthropologist PhD and university faculty with more than 20 years of experience in sexual and reproductive health research. The rest of the research team were, at the time, all graduate student trainees, hailing from the USA, South Asia, and Latin America. The study was funded by the Indiana Minority Health Coalition, a state organisation tasked with promoting the health of minoritised communities in Indiana.

Interviewee recruitment

Recruitment occurred between February and April 2021. We employed a mixture of purposive and referral (snowball) methods to identify participants. The inclusion criteria were intentionally broad. Participants were individuals or organisations providing direct clinical or non-clinical maternal care, who were working preferentially with Black and Latine women. We especially wanted to recruit Black and Latine providers in clinical and non-clinical roles. The research team, in collaboration with the funder, identified a first list of potential participants based on prior knowledge and document reviews. Two graduate assistants called or emailed all identified organisations and individuals. We also created a flyer which we shared online, and with interviewees who offered to share with potential study participants. There was no ideal sample size, rather we aimed to interview as many of those identified as possible, to ensure we captured a variability of experiences and achieved thematic saturation. In all we contacted approximately 62 organisations or individuals, received approximately 40 responses, and completed 32 interviews.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted virtually by video conference by the first author, participants were able to choose if video was turned on. Each interviewee received a study information sheet via e-mail, outlining the goals and objectives of the study and other information necessary for consent. Participants provided oral consent to interview and recording. No prior relationship existed with interviewees. The semi-structured interview guide was developed to understand the existing programs working on maternal health for Black and Latine women, and consisted of three sections: a discussion of the aims and scope of the interviewee’s maternal health-related work with minoritised women; a discussion of other organisations or providers who they connected with for maternal care; and a discussion of their perceptions of the main challenges for maternal health care of Black and Latine women in Indiana. In this article, we report mainly on the last section. The earlier sections resulted largely in a list of programs and how they relate to each other. Each interview lasted 45–60 minutes. Basic demographic information was collected after all interviews were completed via an anonymous self-administered Qualtrics survey. Participants were offered a gift card to an online retailer as a recognition for their time.

Analysis

Audio recordings were uploaded to Otter.ai, an online, password-protected, artificial intelligence, transcription program. Each transcription was reviewed and edited for accuracy by a member of the research team. No full names were included in the recording, but we did not completely anonymise in transcription. We anonymised fully where needed in the manuscript quotations. Interviewees received a copy of the transcribed interview in accordance with informed consent procedures. All protocols associated with this study were approved on 11/30/2020 by the Institutional Review Board at Indiana University (IRB: #2010476289).

We followed a six-step thematic analysis approach aided by Atlas-ti 931: (1) Becoming familiar with the data, by engaging in a close reading of a random sample of interviews and created first set of code categories based on this reading and the interview guide. (2) Generating codes, by coding a sub-set of the interviews. Following our focus on reproductive justice our coding structure made sure to highlight issues of power, justice, equity, and social determinants of health in the data. Each interview was coded by two team members. Codes were added as they emerged. New codes and code variances were discussed in two harmonisation meetings by the whole team and decided by consensus. (3) Searching for themes: the 50 codes generated through analysis were organised into overarching themes. We paid close attention to issues of reproductive justice and to the intersections of race, ethnicity, and class in the establishment of themes. (4) Reviewing themes: themes were reviewed through triangulation and debriefing. We iterated on themes as we incorporated nuanced perspectives and sought out disconfirming experiences. (5) Defining and naming themes, through iterative and reflexive team discussions, and (6) Producing the report: a preliminary analysis was shared with all interviewees. We received one comment further highlighting the problem of increased mental health issues and limited number of available mental health professionals.

Non-medical issues in infrastructure and policy

Interviewees recognised that clients face significant barriers to accessing care due to broader structural issues, which they referred to as “multiple life-stressors” such as finances, work, food security and housing instability. They noted these are interconnected and compound each other, creating an overwhelming impact:

“[I]n my experience working with pregnant women and families with children, young children, is that when one thing is affecting them, that will affect other aspects or elements in the relationship. Okay, so let's say that baby's not gaining weight. Well, you know, maybe there might be some financial issues that they don't have money to buy food. Or maybe there's some other issues our mom is having with her relationship with her partner that she doesn't trust you and will not share with you, you know? […] I tell my supervisor, because, um, it's not about hey, we're going to talk about first trimester today, it might not be that it might be that, hey, they don't have gas.” (P10, Home Visiting Programs)

Interviewees highlighted limited access to reliable public transportation as a prominent infrastructure issue. They discussed significant deficiencies in rural transit systems and the unreliability of the Medicaid supplemental transportation. These challenges were compounded by restrictive clinic policies like the 15-minute tardiness limit on appointments, which exacerbated the impact of transportation limitations. P3 described a situation involving a teen mother unable to use Medicaid transportation without an adult and who was in danger of losing care: “So that lack of transportation, and that just understanding from the doctor's office, what it meant to actually get there that day. So, if I'm 15 minutes late, don't tell me I can't have my appointment, things like that.” (P3, Home Visiting Program)

These transportation barriers not only hindered physical access to appointments but also jeopardised client standing, potentially resulting in appointment cancellations and disengagement from healthcare services.

Effects of systemic racism, organisational and interpersonal bias and microaggressions

Systemic racism was discussed by participants as one of the most pressing challenges facing Black and Latine clients. They described how this issue affected policy and structures of care, and manifested in interpersonal and organisational contexts as lack of empathy or unwillingness to take patients seriously:

“It's hard for me to say but […] the healthcare system is not very open to women as a whole and even more to our minorities in our Black and Hispanic […] they're very dismissive. I am a nurse, and that saddens me very much. So, I definitely, as a White, middle-aged woman, even see the disparity and the bias that's out there. And it saddens me […] this mom is telling you something's wrong, and instead of listening, you're just like, ‘Oh, it's part of pregnancy’.” (P18, Home Visiting Program)

Similarly, in other cases like the one described by a clinician, racist stereotyping, and bias lead to negligent care:

“I think like the most telling story for me … It's just like the way that there was a patient who they ignored her pain. And she was a Black woman, and they just ignored her pain. She kept saying ‘I'm in so much pain’, but she was in postpartum. And the nurses were just like, ‘Oh, you know how those people are?’ […] And it turns out that she had a fibroid that had ruptured, and she was internally bleeding, like she had a real problem. And it just makes me frustrated, because I just would see things like that, that would be these near misses, where it's like, okay, if somebody hadn't eventually listened to this person, what would have happened to them. And even just like the attitudes of nurses towards patients just being on some sort of assistance of any shape, or form, they would make comments like, ‘oh, they're just trying to game the government. That's why they have all these babies’.” (P9, Hospital Affiliated Birth Worker)

Several interviewees describe that their Black and Latine women clients are not heard, are frequently given biased information, and are subjected to forms of coercion and violence which contribute to an unsafe environment in clinic care. Doulas and other independent birth workers with their deep understanding of clinic birthing protocols and multiple birthing experiences, offer valuable insights into these dynamics:

“The biggest problem that my clients have are racist providers, who don't listen to them. That's the number one issue that I've seen across the board, I've had multiple clients, twelve clients last year who I witnessed them being lied to, regarding their health. As in, I know for a fact that this is evidence-based proof, standard practice, and they were not getting that care. And on top of that, they were told a lie, to coerce them into doing something that they wouldn't have had [if] they had informed consent. […] And then seeing practices happen that have sent them into the emergency room and had to have emergency C-sections. Clients who have had their uterus ripped because [..]they were placed in positions that were rough on the uterus that put their bodies in physical harm and then oh, no, you have to go to surgery because this happened when the perineum was up to her ears and we're forcing her to bear down when baby wasn't ready. That put stress on her body, her uterus ruptured. and there were multiple times that has happened, so that is number one issue that I've seen. It's not the moms and it's not their bodies. It's not their choices, is the lack of safe care.” (P13, Independent Birth Worker)

“Women are not being heard. They are not heard, and they're actually not treated like a human. You know like they treat them like they're dumb. So, if I have to take a client that was transferred into the hospital, I see. […] I see it. I see how they treat them, and they don't even realize what they're doing [..] but they totally do it. [In the way] they phrase things, and they tell them they want them to do this, or you know ‘well while you're in for the C-section you want to go ahead and get your tubes tied’. You know like they would never on their third kid have offered that to a White woman. Never. But that Black woman doesn't know, she has no idea that that stuff is happening.” (P17, Independent Birth Worker)

As these participants describe, racist clinical encounters can endanger their clients’ lives, further contributing to the distrust in the medical system, and making other forms of medical advice, like from family and friends, more desirable. This lack of trust also affects the ability of existing maternal health-focused programs, such as home visiting programs, to connect with clients. As this home visiting nurse describes:

“Yeah well, so in our African American communities, I feel like there is a, there's a general mistrust in, you know, specifically the medical community. And there's a lot of turning inward to your family to find out information rather than accepting information from outside. And so, for those families, we do see a lot of certain practices that go on, because it's what the family's done. And now when my mom did this, when I was pregnant, it was fine for her, it's gonna be fine for me. You know, I think that community has a long-standing history of mistrust of you know, the health department and the medical system as a whole, because it hasn't treated them very fairly.” (P24, Home Visiting Program)

Participants emphasised the scarcity of health care providers from Black and Latine communities in Indiana, one clinical provider interviewee asserted that she “could count the number of Black certified nurse midwives in Indiana on one hand”. Consequently, participants described the frustration expressed by their clients when unable to find providers who share their racial or ethnic background, particularly following unpleasant experiences with medical providers in the past.

Insecurity and disconnection of Latine families

According to interviewees, their Latine clients had unstable and disconnected experiences of maternal care. These were largely patterned by language barriers and immigration status.

Participants discussed significant barriers in communication with maternal care providers, impacting their ability to ask questions about their care and express their needs effectively. As P11 describes below, these barriers are exacerbated by organisational deficiencies, such as insufficient resources in Spanish, a scarcity of bilingual providers and translators, and inadequate investment by organisations to adequately support a Latine community with many Spanish speakers:

“Here, I find myself being the [single] Spanish speaker trying to provide services to those Spanish-speaking women. But yet, I can't convince, or we cannot find a nurse to work that also speaks Spanish or a registration person that also speaks Spanish. Or to have the website translated to Spanish, you know, so I think the communication, especially in the Latino community, is a huge issue.” (P11, Hospital Affiliated Birth Worker)

Inability to communicate with providers can have detrimental consequences for Latine women. P12, a Spanish-speaking home visiting nurse whose clients were mostly Spanish speakers describes how the lack of bilingual providers or good translation systems affected her care and follow-up with clients:

“So, the phone [translation] is usually very impersonal. And I’m glad it's there. But it also loses some meaning when you can't put all the other things with it that the person can't see cause they're on the phone. […] Like one of [my clients] ended up with a C-section, I asked, ‘Well, why did you have a C-section?’ And she could not explain why. So, I don't know what she was told. I don't know what she was translated. I don't know whether it was an emergency or not.” (P12, Home Visiting Program)

Providers described several examples of how citizenship restrictions in programs designed to help low-income mothers affect pregnant Latines. For example, uninsured and undocumented women can only access maternal care through established Emergency Medicaid, and few providers outside of large cities will accept presumptive eligibility Medicaid for prenatal care. Additionally, undocumented mothers cannot access Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) which provides cash assistance, and they cannot obtain driving licenses, limiting their mobility to get to appointments. Of these barriers to assistance, the change in accessibility to the Women Infant’s and Children’s (WIC) program, which provides nutritional assistance, has been one of the most impactful as it affects maternal and infant health directly. In 2014, Indiana classified WIC as a welfare program and instituted a citizenship or qualified alien requirement:

“[..] when we sign the paperwork, basically, they have to sign that they are either a qualified alien or a citizen. […] So, um, I think the mom was breastfeeding. So we were able to give her breastfeeding support, because baby was technically on. But we weren't providing her with food. Because she, I mean, she wasn't legally qualified.” (P21, State Managed Federal Programs)

The dissociation inherent in the rule is visible in this case, where the mother’s undocumented body needs, but does not receive, nutritional support to produce milk for a WIC-qualified citizen-born child. This WIC requirement was repealed in 2023. However, given that it was in effect for nine years, it is likely that it continues to impact engagement and trust in WIC.

Citizenship requirements also have detrimental effects on home visiting maternal and child health support programs, such as Healthy Families and Nurse Family Partnership – which the state has touted as the centrepiece to combat maternal and infant deaths – as P12 describes:

“Particularly with the Hispanic [clients], most of them are undocumented immigrants. So, they do not qualify for a lot of assistance. And that's been especially blatantly apparent during the pandemic, they're not getting the stimulus checks to help them out and make the rent, they're not getting any of those things that have come to my other clients who are, you know, born Americans. Um, so that's been very hard as finding other resources that don't require them to be a documented immigrant. And those are very few and far between for a lot of the things that they need. So that has been harder.” (P12, Home Visiting Program)

There is also fear of engaging with the health system due to a hostile and discriminatory immigration climate:

“I think it could be then also having that sense of fear of like, I've had to talk to a lot of people like, ‘Oh, well, if I go to the doctor, does that mean that they call ICE?’ Like, no, they can't. Unfortunately, maybe you will come across those people who are, you know, mean, and do that stuff, but no, like, that's not how this works. And so, I think it's also that fear of, like, will I get caught? Like, you know, is my safety at risk? And so yeah, it could be a hinder of, you know, getting the appropriate resources.” (P6, Community Health Worker)

Several interviewees also identified the lack of awareness of resources as a Latine-specific issue. For example, they mentioned a limited understanding of what a doula is and what midwifery care entails, or accessibility to other community-based resources.

Overall, according to participants, Latines face specific challenges, including federal and state policies and organisational restrictions that affect access and use of maternal care. These are exacerbated by individual circumstances like lack of documentation and limited English proficiency.

Issues in navigating the maternal healthcare system

The immense effort and knowledge needed to navigate the healthcare system was a major challenge discussed by interviewees. This affects access to maternal health for all minority and low-income communities, including non-citizens. For example, a specific problem was timely Medicaid enrolment:

“So, when I think of access, I think of convenience, and there are plenty of services that, you know, advertise ‘we're here!’. But then there are a whole set of special steps you have to do to get the service. And I think that is, I think that's the biggest deterrent. […] Many of the women who come into [our Doula] program, at the end of the first trimester, or beginning of the second is because they spent time in the first trimester, trying to get the services […] we've been told that once [clinics] identify [that women are on Medicaid they tell them] you should wait until the 11th week of your pregnancy to come back [because] at 11 weeks, Medicaid will pay. And so, if I'm sent back home, waiting for this 11th week […] then that whole first trimester is gone.” (P30, Advocacy and Convening)

A further issue is that the system itself, Medicaid especially, is complicated to navigate. It takes a lot of time and paperwork:

“They can access Medicaid […] we have so many organisations in our county that provide that referral to Medicaid. But no one walks the person through the process other than [one organisation]. There's only one, that will literally take your hand and make sure that when it's done, two hours later with the feds that you have insurance. No one else does that. They just say here here's the number, call. And you know people, they're not going to follow up. Right? [..]No, they're not gonna have time to do that.” (P22, Advocacy and Convening)

As participants described, the extra effort that minoritised families and pregnant women expend to access the healthcare system places an additional burden on family income and can further alienate the very people it should be helping.

Women’s dissatisfaction with maternal healthcare delivery

Another significant challenge involves negative healthcare encounters, as highlighted by multiple participants. Issues include requirements for individuals to schedule their appointments, the need to call the same day for an appointment, and cancellation of appointments if clients are 15 minutes late. These organisational policies disproportionately affect women from minoritised communities by overlooking existing barriers to care faced by this population. For example, in the case of appointment scheduling:

“We [home visiting nurses] have seen more and more of clinics and doctors’ offices wanting only the person who the appointment is for to be the one who's scheduling. So, we've had less and less opportunity where we could maybe step in and schedule something for a family if we needed to […] Also, this idea that you have to call the ‘day of’ for your appointment. And that's very difficult for families to do. They have to get up early. They have to call into a clinic where the number’s usually busy, so they have to continue to try to call back, they have to have that day off. And what if they don't get an appointment on that day, now they've got a day off from work, and now they're going to have to get another day off. […] it really puts a burden on families who are already challenged with trying to make everything work.” (P24, Home Visiting Program)

Postpartum follow-up also represents a major issue, particularly regarding mental health. Despite increasing demand for maternal mental healthcare services, community mental health resources are limited. Participants noted limited insurance coverage, long waitlists, and shortage of providers:

“[W]e must do better in Indiana about mental health. We have ignored it for far too long, it has become this awful mess. And we just don't have a good system to get people connected to mental health resources. And so, it's still stigmatized also. […] It keeps women from getting the medical care that they need.” (P24 Home Visiting Program)

Latine women are particularly affected by the shortage of bilingual mental health providers:

“So, gosh, another one that I've been struggling with is also having mental health services for Spanish-speaking clients. So, challenging. Because, I mean, if a pediatrician may not speak Spanish, that's okay. She can have a translator and medical assistant and nurse. And that could be well, or a translation on the phone. But when it comes to mental health, a client expressing her feelings, and then having them translated is very challenging. So, mental health for Spanish-speaking clients has been very tough.” (P23, Home Visiting Program)

Some interviewees described that many of these organisational level policies, such as appointments and limits on tardiness, could be easily amended by hospitals and provider organisations, and would likely dramatically improve their clients’ experiences of care.

Limitations to providing community-based and holistic models of care

Interviewees providing holistic, woman-centred, and culturally respectful care, like midwives, doulas, and other community-based providers, emphasised the importance of offering these options to all women. They noted that while clinicians and policy makers in Indiana are aware of holistic and less interventionist models of birth care, these options are unavailable to women from minoritised communities due to the lack of coverage by public insurance. Participants discussed how state regulations restrict the role and practice of community midwives and doulas, and impose complex rules on independent birth centres, and thereby limit access to alternative forms of maternal care:

“And when we consider how that looks in terms of [the hospital’s] relationship to practitioners, it does not work well with midwifery and the other birth options available to begin with. So, like, if Black women had a choice to birth at home [the hospital] isn't exactly on board with supporting that choice, either. So, I think in terms of why a Black woman would go to a hospital or not, I mean, Dr. Moore died. So, like, we're absolutely not interested in going to the hospital, but birthing at home is financially unavailable. And so, one of the things that's going on and just people are laboring at home, I mean, moments like water breaking in the elevator, like they're just trying to extend it go in, give birth, stay the 24 hours, and get out.” (P16, Advocacy and Convening)

The participant refers to delaying strategies used by Black women to reduce their time in the clinic and to Dr. Susan Moore,32 a Black Indiana physician who documented racist treatment that resulted in delays in her care, and ultimate death, due to Covid-19, to highlight that not even being a physician allays the dangerous situation Black women face in the healthcare system.

Restrictions on reimbursements for care also limit the ability of Black and Latine birth workers like midwives, doulas, and lactation consultants, to provide community-based services to their communities:

“I mean, it's, it's honestly, just like, being a lactation consultant, it's, it's hard. […] You know, like, you want to be able to make a living, you want to be able to feed yourself and your own children, you know, if you have children, and you want to be able to take care of yourself, but if you know, you put all this money in this effort in this time and commitment into […] We want to see us [Black women] live and survive. We want to see our babies live past one, and we want our women to live. And that's what I think drives us and keeps us going. But at the other end of that we also have to be able to provide our own families.” (P2, Community Birth Worker)

As participants described, addressing the limitations on community-based and holistic birth care practices in Indiana will require both policy and systems level changes, yet there are also many potential gains in increasing safe birth options for women from minoritised communities.

Discussion

This study explores the provider-identified challenges for safe and respectful maternal health for Black and Latine women in Indiana, contributing to the scarce research on the subject in the state. While some of these challenges – racism, language barriers, insurance limits, and transportation – are consistent with existing US research, we specifically characterise their forms in Indiana. Additionally, we contribute new insights into the effects of state limitations to alternative birthing models, the impacts of citizenship status on Latine mothers, and issues in navigating the US health care system.

As with other US-based research on this topic,19,23,24,33 systemic racism affects the experiences of minoritised women in maternal care reported by providers in Indiana. Our findings show its direct impacts on organisational and interpersonal culture which harm minoritised women in clinics, including practices of dismissing pain, indifferent attitudes of providers, and receiving inadequate information. This study builds on prior research highlighting racism as a main challenge in Indiana,27 offering a nuanced perspective on how racism manifests from the viewpoint of health providers.

Other structural and policy issues significantly impact the challenges reported by our participants. For instance, restrictions on midwives’ scope of practice and the high cost of midwifery training appear to dissuade minoritised women to enter the profession and to discourage more woman-centred forms of birth care. This finding supports concerns raised by Rouse34 in a 2015 analysis of Indiana’s revised midwifery regulations. Moreover, the shortage of clinical providers from minoritised communities directly affects the clinical experiences of Black and Latine women.35

In addition to racism, Latines face state-endorsed discrimination when citizenship restrictions are imposed on programs like WIC or other forms of support for low-income families, severely limiting women’s access to adequate care and support during pregnancy. This underscores previous research that identified the piecemeal approach to sexual and reproductive care for immigrant women.36–38 Tackling these challenges will require broader policy changes with an equity focus, a difficult proposition in a state that has encountered resistance even on the use of the word “equity” in any maternal care related policies and programs.39

The focus of provider-identified challenges, examined through the social-ecological model, encompasses structural policy, organisational and interpersonal levels. In the context of maternal care in Indiana, these findings suggest a disconnection between what providers report as ongoing challenges, and the current maternal health programs which target individual knowledge and interpersonal relationships. For example, 11 of the 17 projects funded in the first 2 Safety PIN Grant cycles proposed either clinical (i.e. OB navigation, enhanced prenatal care) or patient education emphasising safe sleep and tobacco cessation.8 The remaining six projects involved community health workers, such as enhancing home visiting programs like Nurse Family Partnership and training community doulas. However, only two of these were specifically oriented towards Black and Latine populations, who experience the most adverse outcomes. It is possible that addressing some of the structural issues may be outside the scope of action of the state government or that the existing political climate is unfavourable to making needed changes.

The experiences of study participants articulate the need for an intentional reproductive rights and justice approach.28 This approach should centre the necessary changes that affect minoritised women’s ability to attain their human right to health,40 and especially the right to safe and respectful birth care.41

At a structural level, it is imperative to address transportation, maternity care deserts, and the shortage of Black and Latine maternal care workers.35 The Indiana government should remove all citizenship barriers for access to low-income support programs and recognise undocumented immigrants as rights-bearing people.

A rights-based approach at an organisational level could include creating assessment measures that capture women’s experiences of care and empowering women through opportune evidence-based information about their rights. For example, a Birthing Bill of Rights, such as the one developed by the National Association to Advance Black Birth,42 could be actively included into all prenatal care education undertaken by maternal care providers.

Organisations could engage in the review of their policies and procedures centring the needs of Black, Latine and other historically excluded communities. Moreover, organisations and providers should be held accountable for their promises of equity and for instances of mistreatment or inaction. This could be achieved through establishing quality of care measures in evaluation systems in ways that empower women and are respected by birth providers. One example is the PREM-OB Scale™ a research-based patient-reported experience measure of obstetric racism, which organisations and providers can use to identify, interrogate, and modify acts of obstetric racism in clinical care.43 Providers should engage in explicit anti-racist praxis to care; this could be a requirement of ongoing training and certification, and medical education.44

An explicit rights-based approach should move away from risk discourse that blames and shames birthing people45 for their detrimental health outcomes. In this context, engaging with a broader cadre of maternal providers who can provide women-centred care would allow women to choose low-intervention-low-risk approaches for birth care. This will mean making reimbursement of community-based and holistic birth workers part of public insurance, ensuring holistic birth workers are recognised and respected in their services. Evidence suggests that doula perinatal support can reduce racial inequalities in maternal care,46 and Indiana has approved Medicaid reimbursement for doula services but has yet to put it into effect.

Birth workers, nurses, and midwives can also be part of joint clinic/community health worker efforts to extend support and care longer in the postpartum. Community doula projects in Indiana have started this important work but remain small in scope and reach.

Finally, a rights-not-risk approach to birth care should recognise, share, and highlight the ways in which women of colour experience birth as a joyous, life affirming experience. There is a need for positive examples of birth experiences that move the research and policy discourse from reinscribing trauma towards applying a strengths-based approach.47

Our study offers valuable insights. As qualitative research the aim is to gain the understanding of complex phenomena, and the results are useful to better inform interventions serving communities referenced in the data. The diversity in experience, language, age, and nationality of our research team meant that final interpretations benefited from multiple view points on the meaning of the data. Limitations include that our sample lacked labour and delivery nurses and medical doctors, which was largely a result of the recruitment limitations and the effects of Covid on the workload of these populations.

Conclusion

Maternal health providers described challenges faced by minoritised women that largely correspond to broader structural and organisational policies, and interpersonal levels of care. These issues are interconnected and complex, intersecting with other social determinants of maternal health. The results of this study highlight the need for broad changes to work towards a rights-based approach to maternal health in Indiana. This approach should centre the needs of pregnant people, particularly from minoritised communities, and provide them with the structures and tools to experience pregnancy, birth, and postpartum as safe, supportive, and joyous events.

Further research on maternal health in Indiana should centre direct care experiences of minoritised women. Specifically, it should assess how additional restrictions on reproductive rights, following the Dobbs Supreme Court decision, are impacting maternal care for Black, Latine, and other historically excluded women in the state.
