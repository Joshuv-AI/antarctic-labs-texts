Short-Term 24-h Systolic Blood Pressure Variability and Subclinical Target-Organ Damage in White-Coat Hypertension: A Retrospective Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jing Huang, Zien Huang, Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare subclinical target-organ damage (TOD) across normotension (NT), white-coat hypertension (WCH), and mild-to-moderate hypertension (HT), and examine the association of 24-h systolic blood pressure (SBP) variability with concurrent TOD in untreated WCH.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cross-sectional study included 267 hospitalized participants who underwent ambulatory blood pressure monitoring between 2012 and 2021 (91 NT, 93 WCH, 83 HT). TOD markers were compared across groups. Within WCH, multivariable models assessed 24-h SBP variability in relation to any TOD, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), left ventricular mass index (LVMI), and microalbuminuria (MA), adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, and 24-h mean SBP.ResultsAny TOD occurred in 26.4%, 52.7%, and 71.1% of NT, WCH, and HT participants, respectively (P<0.001). Within WCH, any TOD occurred in 35.5%, 64.5%, and 58.1% across increasing variability tertiles; the unadjusted between-tertile comparison was not statistically significant (P=0.056). Continuous 24-h SBP variability was associated with any TOD (odds ratio [OR] 1.25, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.52), LVH (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.08–1.60), LVMI (β=2.23 g/m2, 95% CI 0.33–4.12), and log-transformed MA (β=0.105, 95% CI 0.052–0.157). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.613 for the base model and 0.678 after adding 24-h SBP variability, with better overall fit of the extended model (likelihood-ratio P=0.009).ConclusionWCH showed an intermediate prevalence of any TOD between NT and mild-to-moderate HT. Within WCH, higher 24-h SBP variability was associated with concurrent TOD after accounting for 24-h mean SBP and basic clinical factors, and may provide Supplementary Information when interpreted alongside mean ambulatory blood pressure. These single-center cross-sectional findings do not establish temporal or causal relationships and require confirmation in prospective multicenter studies.

Introduction

White-coat hypertension (WCH) is defined in untreated individuals as office systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg, with 24-h mean ambulatory SBP <130 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg.1 Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) therefore plays an important role in identifying WCH and other out-of-office blood pressure (BP) phenotypes. Current hypertension guidelines also recommend out-of-office BP measurement, particularly ABPM when available, for BP phenotype assessment.1,2

Although WCH was once considered a relatively low-risk condition close to normotension, increasing evidence suggests that it is not completely benign.3–5 A meta-analysis showed that cardiac structural and functional changes in WCH were generally intermediate between those in normotension and sustained hypertension.3 Cohen further reported that untreated WCH was associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.4 More recent population-based evidence showed that WCH without baseline cardiac or renal organ damage was associated with higher long-term risks of mortality, new sustained hypertension, and new organ damage.6 WCH has also been associated with a greater likelihood of progression to sustained hypertension than normotension.7,8 Subclinical target-organ damage (TOD) may reflect cardiac structural or renal microvascular abnormalities before overt cardiovascular or renal events become clinically apparent.9,10 Assessing subclinical TOD may therefore help characterize heterogeneity within WCH.

Blood pressure variability (BPV) reflects fluctuations in BP over time rather than the average BP level itself. Greater short-term BPV may accompany altered cardiovascular regulation and arterial stiffness and may expose the cardiac and renal circulations to varying hemodynamic loads.11 The 2023 European Society of Hypertension position paper11 also highlights the physiological and methodological complexity of BPV assessment. Short-term systolic BPV has been associated with subclinical TOD in hypertensive populations,12,13 and Kang reported greater BPV in WCH than in normotension.14 More recently, a study of 547 untreated patients with WCH found that non-dipping status was associated with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).15 Although that study examined circadian BP patterns rather than 24-h SBP variability, it suggests that dynamic ABPM features may relate to heterogeneity in concurrent TOD within WCH. Systematic evidence has also linked greater BPV with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, although associations vary according to the BPV metric and monitoring method used.16 However, the association between 24-h SBP variability and concurrent subclinical TOD after accounting for 24-h mean SBP has not been sufficiently characterized in untreated, ABPM-confirmed WCH.

Accordingly, this study first compared subclinical TOD across normotension (NT), WCH, and mild-to-moderate hypertension (HT). It then examined the associations of 24-h SBP variability with concurrent any TOD and the cardiac and renal markers evaluated in this study among patients with ABPM-confirmed WCH, accounting for 24-h mean SBP and basic clinical factors. An exploratory nested-model comparison was also performed to assess the change in overall model fit after inclusion of 24-h SBP variability.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study based on electronic medical records of patients hospitalized in Cardiology Ward 3 of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. Common inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, no antihypertensive treatment, and fulfillment of the predefined criteria for normotension (NT), white-coat hypertension (WCH), or mild-to-moderate hypertension (HT). Exclusion criteria included secondary hypertension, grade 3 hypertension, diabetes mellitus, primary kidney disease, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular complications, use of medications for cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, coronary artery disease, congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, pulmonary heart disease, thyrotoxic heart disease, pericardial disease, severe arrhythmia, anemia, or febrile illness.

Because of the retrospective design, no a priori sample-size calculation based on an anticipated effect size was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available under the predefined case-retrieval strategy. As untreated, ABPM-confirmed WCH was relatively uncommon among hospitalized patients, all patients discharged from Cardiology Ward 3 between 2012 and 2021 with WCH recorded in the discharge diagnosis were screened. Blood-pressure phenotypes were subsequently verified using uniform study definitions based on repeated office BP measurements obtained without antihypertensive treatment and available ABPM records, yielding 93 patients with WCH. The NT comparison group was screened sequentially from the electronic medical record search results, with preference given to eligible patients admitted in the same month as patients with WCH and without a hypertension-related discharge diagnosis. No individual matching was performed, and 91 patients with NT were included. The mild-to-moderate HT group was selected from patients hospitalized in the same ward in 2021 with a discharge diagnosis of grade 1 or grade 2 hypertension who met the same eligibility criteria, yielding 83 patients. Discharge diagnoses were used for case identification, whereas BP phenotypes in all three groups were verified according to uniform study definitions applied throughout the study period. Each participant contributed only one set of cross-sectional clinical, BP, echocardiographic, and laboratory data obtained during the same hospitalization; long-term outcome data were not included. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (approval No. 2024-E742-01).

Blood Pressure Measurement and Phenotype Definitions

BP phenotypes were determined using repeated office BP measurements obtained on three separate days without antihypertensive treatment together with 24-h ABPM results. WCH was defined as office SBP ≥140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥90 mmHg with 24-h mean ambulatory SBP <130 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg.2 Mild-to-moderate HT was defined as office SBP of 140–179 mmHg and/or DBP of 90–109 mmHg with 24-h mean ambulatory SBP ≥130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥80 mmHg.2 NT was defined as office BP and 24-h mean ambulatory BP below the corresponding hypertension thresholds.2 All BP phenotypes were classified at the individual level using uniform study definitions.

Office BP was measured by healthcare professionals trained in routine BP measurement using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer. Participants rested quietly for at least 5 min before measurement and were seated with the upper arm supported at heart level using an appropriately sized cuff. BP was measured in both arms at the initial assessment, and the arm with the higher reading was used for subsequent measurements. SBP and DBP were determined from Korotkoff phases I and V, respectively. Two measurements were obtained 1–2 min apart and averaged; if either SBP or DBP differed by ≥5 mmHg between the two measurements, a third measurement was obtained and the mean of the three readings was recorded.2

ABPM was performed for at least 24 h whenever possible, with measurements scheduled every 20 min during daytime (06:00–22:00) and every 30 min during nighttime (22:00–06:00). A recording was considered valid when at least 70% of scheduled measurements were successful, with at least 20 valid daytime readings, 7 valid nighttime readings, and at least one valid reading per hour.2 Participants received routine instructions before monitoring and were asked to keep the monitored arm still during automatic measurements. Because standardized sleep or activity diaries were not systematically available in the historical records, daytime and nighttime were defined using the fixed clock-time intervals above. Sleep quality, physical activity, and anxiety during ABPM were also not systematically recorded and were therefore not included as covariates.

24-h SBP variability was defined as the standard deviation of all valid SBP readings obtained during the 24-h ABPM recording and was expressed in mmHg. Standard deviation was selected because it could be obtained relatively consistently from routine ABPM summary records throughout the study period and has been widely used in studies of short-term BPV.11,17 Because complete time-stamped ABPM measurement sequences were not consistently retained in the historical records, other indices requiring sequential readings or time weighting, such as average real variability and weighted standard deviation, could not be calculated reliably.11,18

Assessment of Subclinical Target-Organ Damage

This study assessed cardiac and renal markers of subclinical target-organ damage (TOD) that were available from the inpatient records and could be evaluated using predefined criteria. Echocardiographic and renal laboratory data used for TOD assessment were obtained during the same hospitalization as ABPM. Vascular or retinal TOD measures, including carotid intima-media thickness, pulse wave velocity, and standardized retinal examination, were not systematically performed or consistently recorded in all participants and were therefore not included.

Cardiac markers included left ventricular mass index (LVMI) and left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). Transthoracic echocardiography was performed by trained echocardiography personnel according to contemporaneous clinical practice. Left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), interventricular septal thickness at end-diastole (IVSd), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVPWd) were extracted from the echocardiographic records. Left ventricular mass (LVM) was calculated using the corrected American Society of Echocardiography linear cube formula: LVM (g) = 0.8 × {1.04 × [(LV1Dd + IVSd + LVPWd)3 – LVIDd3]} + 0.6. Body surface area (BSA) was calculated as BSA (m2) = 0.0061 × height (cm) + 0.0128 × weight (kg) – 0.1529.19 LVMI was calculated as LVM divided by BSA. LVH was defined as LVMI >115 g/m2 in men or >95 g/m2 in women.20

Renal markers included estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), microalbuminuria (MA), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Laboratory results were extracted from the hospital laboratory information system and formal laboratory reports, with testing performed under contemporaneous standard operating procedures and routine quality-control practices. MA was recorded as 24-h urinary albumin excretion and expressed in mg/24 h. UACR was extracted directly from the formal laboratory report and expressed in mg/g; it was not retrospectively recalculated from urinary albumin and creatinine measurements. Assay-specific platform and manufacturer information was not consistently retained in the historical records and therefore could not be reconstructed reliably. Renal abnormalities were defined as eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, MA >30 mg/24 h, or UACR >30 mg/g.21,22 Any TOD was defined as the presence of at least one predefined cardiac or renal abnormality: LVMI above the sex-specific threshold, eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, MA >30 mg/24 h, or UACR >30 mg/g.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), according to their distribution, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Unadjusted comparisons across the three BP phenotypes were performed using one-way analysis of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis test, the χ2-test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Within the WCH group, continuous 24-h SBP variability was used as the primary exposure, whereas tertiles were used for exploratory description of TOD across different levels of variability. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the associations of 24-h SBP variability with any TOD and LVH, and multivariable linear regression was used for LVMI and natural log-transformed MA. The main models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and 24-h mean SBP to account for basic demographic characteristics, body size, and average ambulatory BP while maintaining model parsimony given the limited WCH sample size. Diabetes mellitus, antihypertensive medication use, and primary kidney disease were controlled through the eligibility criteria and were therefore not additionally included as covariates. Because eGFR contributed to the definitions of renal abnormality and any TOD, it was not included as a covariate in the corresponding models. To assess potential residual confounding, sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for smoking status and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). For each outcome, the primary model and the sensitivity model additionally adjusted for smoking status and LDL-C were fitted using the same outcome-specific complete-case sample. Additional sensitivity analyses further adjusted for admission year to assess potential temporal heterogeneity over the study period.

Because age and BMI differed across the NT, WCH, and HT groups, adjusted between-phenotype comparisons were also performed. Logistic regression was used for binary TOD outcomes and linear regression for continuous outcomes, with adjustment for age, sex, and BMI. MA and UACR were natural log-transformed before modeling because of their skewed distributions. 24-h mean SBP was not included in these between-phenotype models because it contributed to the definition of BP phenotype. Tukey adjustment was applied to pairwise comparisons between phenotypes.

For exploratory comparison of model fit after inclusion of 24-h SBP variability, nested logistic regression models were constructed with any TOD as the outcome. The base model included age, sex, BMI, and 24-h mean SBP, and the extended model additionally included 24-h SBP variability. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated for each model, and the likelihood-ratio test was used to compare overall fit between the nested models. This analysis was exploratory and was not intended to develop or validate a prediction model.

Missing values were not imputed. Descriptive analyses and unadjusted group comparisons were based on available observations for each variable, whereas multivariable analyses used outcome-specific complete cases. Variable-specific available sample sizes and actual analytic sample sizes for the regression models are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Multicollinearity and model assumptions were assessed for the main regression models, including variance inflation factors, linearity of continuous predictors in the logit for logistic models, residual distribution and homoscedasticity for linear models, and influential observations; detailed diagnostic results are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

To account for multiple related comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied separately to two families of analyses: the five TOD outcomes compared across BP phenotypes and the five related adjusted association analyses within WCH. Corresponding false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted q values are reported in Supplementary Table S3. All tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 considered nominally statistically significant, and related findings were interpreted together with their FDR-adjusted q values. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) or β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Blood Pressure Phenotypes, Ambulatory Blood Pressure Parameters, and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 267 participants were included in the final cohort: 91 with NT, 93 with WCH, and 83 with mild-to-moderate HT. Office SBP and DBP, as well as 24-h mean SBP, 24-h mean DBP, and 24-h mean arterial pressure, were generally lowest in NT, intermediate in WCH, and highest in HT. The 24-h mean SBP values were 111 ± 9, 117 ± 7, and 129 ± 11 mmHg, respectively (P<0.001). A similar pattern was observed for 24-h SBP variability, which was 11.6 ± 3.0, 12.3 ± 3.0, and 13.2 ± 3.3 mmHg, respectively (P=0.005). Neither 24-h pulse pressure nor nighttime BP dipping differed significantly among the three groups. Participants with NT were older than those with WCH and HT, and BMI and family history of hypertension also differed across the three groups, whereas the distributions of sex and smoking history were similar. Comparison of Subclinical Target-Organ Damage Markers Among the Three Groups

Subclinical TOD markers showed different patterns across the three BP phenotypes. The prevalence of any TOD was 26.4%, 52.7%, and 71.1% in the NT, WCH, and mild-to-moderate HT groups, respectively (P<0.001). In unadjusted comparisons, LVMI and MA increased overall from NT to WCH and HT, whereas eGFR differed among the groups without a consistent increasing or decreasing pattern. UACR did not differ significantly among the three groups (P=0.198) (Table 2 and Figure 1). Discussion

This study had three main findings. First, the prevalence of any TOD was higher in WCH than in NT but lower than in mild-to-moderate HT, although individual cardiac and renal markers did not show a uniform pattern across the three BP phenotypes. Second, among untreated patients with ABPM-confirmed WCH, higher 24-h SBP variability was associated with concurrent any TOD, LVH, LVMI, and MA after accounting for 24-h mean SBP and basic clinical factors. Third, the exploratory nested-model comparison showed better overall model fit after 24-h SBP variability was added to basic clinical factors and 24-h mean SBP. Taken together, these findings indicate heterogeneity in concurrent TOD within WCH and suggest that 24-h SBP variability may provide Supplementary Information when interpreted alongside mean ambulatory BP.

Previous evidence has challenged the traditional view of WCH as a benign measurement phenomenon.3–6,23 Some meta-analyses showed that LVMI was higher in WCH than in normotension but lower than in sustained hypertension,3,24 suggesting that WCH may be accompanied by subclinical cardiac structural changes. Untreated WCH has also been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.4,5,25 Kang reported greater BPV in WCH than in normotension, with some BPV indices even exceeding those in sustained hypertension.14 However, studies relating BPV to TOD have mainly involved broader hypertensive populations and have less often examined this association specifically within WCH. More recently, a study of 547 untreated patients with WCH found that non-dipping status was associated with a higher likelihood of LVH and lower eGFR.15 Although that study examined nocturnal BP patterns rather than 24-h SBP variability, it further suggests that dynamic ABPM features may relate to heterogeneity in concurrent TOD within WCH. This study further focused on 24-h SBP variability and examined its association with concurrent TOD after accounting for 24-h mean SBP.

The 24-h mean SBP and 24-h SBP variability represent different but related features of ambulatory BP.11,26 The former reflects the average BP level during monitoring, whereas the latter reflects the dispersion of SBP readings around that mean. Accordingly, 24-h mean SBP was included in the main WCH models to account for differences in average ambulatory BP. The 24-h mean SBP did not differ significantly across tertiles of 24-h SBP variability (P=0.098). The prevalence of any TOD was 35.5%, 64.5%, and 58.1% across the three tertiles, and the between-group difference was not statistically significant (P=0.056). The tertile pattern was non-monotonic, whereas 24-h SBP variability remained associated with concurrent any TOD when analyzed as a continuous variable and adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and 24-h mean SBP. Taken together, short-term SBP fluctuation and mean ambulatory SBP may reflect different but related BP characteristics. The 24-h SBP variability may therefore provide Supplementary Information when interpreted alongside mean ambulatory BP.

Among the individual TOD measures, higher 24-h SBP variability was associated with LVH, LVMI, and MA. Previous studies have also reported associations of short-term systolic BPV with LVMI and urinary albumin abnormalities.12,27,28 Differences in BPV metrics, including the use of awake systolic BP variability in the study by Tatasciore12 and 24-h SBP standard deviation in the present study, may contribute to variation in effect estimates across studies. In the unadjusted comparison, LVMI increased overall from NT to WCH and mild-to-moderate HT. This pattern was broadly similar to the intermediate cardiac phenotype of WCH reported by Cuspidi.3,29 After adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, LVMI remained higher in WCH than in NT but did not differ significantly between WCH and mild-to-moderate HT. Differences in comparator populations may partly explain this finding, because previous meta-analyses included broader sustained hypertensive populations, whereas the present HT group was restricted to grade 1–2 hypertension. The renal findings were less uniform across analyses. Within WCH, 24-h SBP variability was associated with MA, whereas the between-phenotype comparisons showed no consistent pattern for UACR and eGFR. MA and UACR assess urinary albumin abnormalities using different approaches and are not directly interchangeable. Overall, these findings support associations with selected cardiac and renal TOD measures rather than a uniform relationship across all renal markers.

Several mechanisms may contribute to these associations. Greater short-term BPV may accompany impaired baroreflex or autonomic regulation and increased arterial stiffness. Repeated BP fluctuations may also expose the cardiac and renal circulations to varying hemodynamic loads. Endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress may further contribute.11,30 The 2023 European Society of Hypertension position paper similarly emphasizes that BPV reflects interactions between behavioral and environmental factors and intrinsic cardiovascular regulatory mechanisms, while methodological heterogeneity remains in its measurement and interpretation.11 These mechanisms provide plausible explanations for the associations of BPV with left ventricular remodeling and increased urinary albumin excretion. However, they were not directly assessed in this study. Whether greater BPV precedes progression of cardiac or renal abnormalities requires prospective evaluation.

The exploratory nested-model comparison showed an AUC of 0.613 for the base model and 0.678 after 24-h SBP variability was added. The likelihood-ratio test indicated better overall fit of the extended model (P=0.009). This P value reflects a comparison of overall fit between the nested models and is not a statistical test of the difference between the two AUCs. Although overall model fit improved, the absolute increase in AUC was 0.065 and was modest in magnitude; whether an improvement of this size is clinically meaningful remains uncertain. Because 24-h SBP variability can be derived from the same ABPM recording used to identify WCH, it may provide Supplementary Information when interpreted alongside mean ambulatory BP and other clinical findings. Given the exploratory nature of this analysis and the lack of external validation, the clinical relevance of the observed model improvement requires further evaluation in the future.

This study evaluated NT, WCH, and mild-to-moderate HT together and then examined 24-h SBP variability specifically within untreated, ABPM-confirmed WCH. The main association models accounted for 24-h mean SBP and basic clinical factors, and the consistency of the findings was further assessed in sensitivity analyses and after FDR correction.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the single-center retrospective cross-sectional design does not allow temporal or causal inference. Participants were hospitalized at a tertiary cardiovascular center, the case-retrieval approaches were not identical across BP phenotypes, and the NT group was not selected by random sampling or formal individual matching. Selection bias therefore remains possible, and generalizability to community or general outpatient WCH may be limited. Second, only 93 patients with WCH were included, with 31 in each exploratory tertile. The tertile analysis and nested-model comparison should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Third, short-term BPV was assessed only as the standard deviation of 24-h SBP from a single ABPM recording. Complete time-stamped ABPM sequences were not consistently retained, preventing reliable assessment of other BPV measures such as average real variability and weighted standard deviation. Sleep quality, physical activity, and anxiety during ABPM were also not systematically recorded. Fourth, some ABPM, echocardiographic, and renal measurements were missing. The records also covered an extended study period, during which equipment, clinical procedures, or laboratory platforms may have changed. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for admission year yielded directionally consistent findings, but residual measurement heterogeneity cannot be excluded. Finally, only cardiac and renal TOD measures that were sufficiently available in the medical records were assessed. Vascular and retinal TOD were not systematically evaluated. Therefore, the composite any TOD outcome was limited to the cardiac and renal measures available in the medical records. Residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely recorded behavioral, metabolic, and lifestyle factors also remains possible. Future prospective multicenter studies should externally validate these findings, examine longitudinal cardiovascular outcomes, establish reproducible and clinically relevant BPV thresholds, determine whether interventions that modify BPV improve clinical outcomes, and evaluate the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of incorporating BPV into routine assessment of WCH.

Conclusion

The prevalence of any TOD was higher in WCH than in NT but lower than in mild-to-moderate HT, although individual cardiac and renal markers showed nonuniform patterns across the three BP phenotypes. Among untreated patients with ABPM-confirmed WCH, higher 24-h SBP variability was associated with concurrent any TOD, LVH, LVMI, and MA after accounting for 24-h mean SBP and basic clinical factors. When interpreted alongside mean ambulatory BP, 24-h SBP variability may provide Supplementary Information on concurrent TOD. Given the single-center retrospective cross-sectional design, its clinical relevance and prognostic utility require confirmation in prospective multicenter studies.
