A DILOGARITHM SERIES AND A GENERALIZATION OF A THEOREM OF BRIDGEMAN
Chance Sanford

Abstract




Received: , Revised: , Accepted: , Published:

Abstract

Using Abel’s five-term relation, we derive a new two-parameter series identity for the Rogers dilogarithm. By specializing this identity, we obtain dilogarithm series involving Lucas sequences. These results generalize certain series identities of Bridgeman related to solutions of Pell’s equations, which were obtained via a completely different approach.

1
Introduction

Let
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x)
denote the dilogarithm, which we define for
0\leq x\leq 1
as

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n^{2}}=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t.

The subject of this paper is a variant of
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x)
, called the Rogers dilogarithm. Denoted by
\mathcal{L}(x)
, the Rogers dilogarithm is defined for
0<x<1
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\log(x)\log(1-x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}\left(\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}+\frac{\log(t)}{1-t}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

and then extended to
0\leq x\leq 1
by setting
\mathcal{L}(0):=0
and
\mathcal{L}(1):=\pi^{2}/6
.

Although not as widely known as other transcendental functions, the dilogarithm and its variants appear in a surprising number of contexts, from number theory and hyperbolic geometry to conformal field theory in mathematical physics. A good introduction to the dilogarithm and its many applications can be found in the survey by Zagier
[
7
]
and the first section of the article by Krillov
[
5
]
.

One interesting feature of the dilogarithm is that it satisfies a seemingly endless array of functional equations. Of particular importance to us are the reflection identity,

\mathcal{L}(x)+\mathcal{L}(1-x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

(1)

valid for
0\leq x\leq 1
and Abel’s five-term relation,

\mathcal{L}(x)+\mathcal{L}(y)=\mathcal{L}(xy)+\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}\right)+\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{y(1-x)}{1-xy}\right),

(2)

which holds for all
0<x,y<1
. The following special values may be deduced from these relations and the definition given above:

\mathcal{L}\left(0\right)=0,\quad\mathcal{L}\left(1\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},\quad\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{12},\quad\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{10},\quad\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{15},

where
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
is the golden ratio. Curiously, these five values represent the only known instances where the Rogers dilogarithm defined on the unit interval may be evaluated in closed-form.

The Rogers dilogarithm also possesses a number of beautiful series identities, of which the Richmond-Szekeres identity
[
6
]
is the prototypical example:

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

Hoorfar and Qi
[
3
]
set out to generalize the Richmond-Szekeres identity, proving a number of new series identities by utilizing Abel’s five-term functional equation for the Rogers dilogarithm. A representative example from that work is the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Hoorfar and Qi
[
3
]
)
.

For
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\theta,\beta<1
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{\beta(1-\theta^{p})(1-\theta^{q})\theta^{n}}{(1-\beta\theta^{n+p})(1-\beta\theta^{n+q})}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{\beta(1-\theta^{p})\theta^{n}}{1-\beta\theta^{n+p}}\right)\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1-\theta^{q}}{1-\beta\theta^{n+q}}\right)-p\mathcal{L}(1-\theta^{q}).

Using a different approach, Bridgeman established a beautiful connection between solutions of Pell’s equations, their associated continued fractions, and series involving the Rogers dilogarithm
[
2
]
. These results were obtained using tools from hyperbolic geometry, which were developed in Bridgeman’s previous work
[
1
]
. Very recently, Bridgeman’s ideas were expanded upon by Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
, who discovered many more series identities for the Rogers dilogarithm.

Although this paper does not utilize any results from hyperbolic geometry, in order to give the reader an idea of the approach taken by Bridgeman
[
2
]
and Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
, we state Bridgeman’s
orthospectrum identity

[
1
]
, which was the starting point for both sets of authors. First, we recall that a hyperbolic surface is a complete, two-dimensional Riemannian manifold equipped with a metric of constant negative curvature. Roughly speaking, the orthospectrum identity relates the lengths of certain geodesic arcs on a hyperbolic surface, called orthogeodesics, to particular properties of that surface. More precisely, the orthospectrum identity is given by the following statement.

Theorem 2

(Bridgeman
[
1
]
)
.

Let
S
denote a hyperbolic surface of finite area with totally geodesic boundary
\partial S\neq\emptyset
, and
N(S)
boundary cusps. In addition, let
O(S)
be the set of orthogeodesics in
S
, that is, the set of geodesic arcs with endpoints on
\partial S
and perpendicular to
\partial S
at both ends. Then

\sum_{\alpha\in O(S)}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\ell(\alpha)/2)}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\left(6\chi(S)+N(S)\right)

where
\ell(\alpha)
is the length of
\alpha
, and
\chi(S)
, the Euler characteristic of
S
, satisfies
\chi(S)=-\mathrm{Area}(S)/2\pi
.

By applying the orthospectrum identity to various hyperbolic surfaces, Bridgeman
[
2
]
and Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
were able to derive dilogarithm series involving certain sequences. The results in Bridgeman’s paper
[
2
]
were expressed in terms of the solutions to Pell’s equations
x^{2}-ny^{2}=\pm 1
. Bridgeman identifies a solution
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
to one of Pell’s equations, with the quadratic irrational
u:=a+b\sqrt{n}
. Depending on whether
(a,b)
is a solution to
x^{2}-ny^{2}=1
or
x^{2}-ny^{2}=-1
,
u
is said to be a positive or negative solution, respectively. One of Bridgeman’s main theorems was the following result, which provides a series expression for
\mathcal{L}(1/u^{2})
.

Theorem 3

(Bridgeman
[
2
]
)
.

Let
u=a+b\sqrt{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{n}]
, with
a,b>0
, satisfy Pell’s equation. In addition, let
u^{k}=a_{k}+b_{k}\sqrt{n}
. If
u
is a positive solution, then

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{b^{2}}{b^{2}_{k}}\right).

(3)

Further, if
u\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{n}]
then
b_{k}/b\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
k
. If
u
is a negative solution, then

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{nb^{2}_{2k}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{a^{2}_{2k+1}}\right).

(4)

Further, if
u\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{n}]
then
b_{2k}/a,a_{2k+1}/a\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
k
.

The aim of this paper is to explore an alternate approach to dilogarithm identities involving integer sequences, and in particular generalize Theorem
3
. Instead of using the ideas developed by Bridgeman and Jaipong et al., we take the approach of Hoorfar and Qi
[
3
]
, relying only on elementary manipulations of Abel’s functional equation. Our results begin from a general two-parameter series, which upon specialization allows us to generalize Bridgeman’s theorem.

2
The Principle Identity

The principle result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
a,b\in(0,1)
be real numbers such that
a\neq b
. Then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{ab(a-b)^{2}(1-a)^{n}(1-b)^{n}}{(a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1})^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}(a)+\mathcal{L}(b)-\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{|{a-b}|}{1-\min(a,b)}\right).

Before proving Theorem
4
, we must first establish a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let the sequences
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be defined by

x_{n}:=\frac{a(a-b)(1-b)^{n}}{a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n}:=\frac{b(a-b)(1-a)^{n}}{a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1}}.

The terms
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
satisfy the coupled recurrence relations

x_{n+1}=\frac{x_{n}(1-y_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n+1}=\frac{y_{n}(1-x_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}.

Proof.

For convenience, let us first define the sequence
(D_{n}(a,b))_{n\geq 0}
as

D_{n}(a,b):=\frac{a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1}}{a-b}.

Then the sequences
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
may be expressed in terms of
D_{n}(a,b)
by writing

x_{n}=\frac{a(1-b)^{n}}{D_{n}(a,b)}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n}=\frac{b(1-a)^{n}}{D_{n}(a,b)}.

Using these expressions we find that

\frac{x_{n}(1-y_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}=\frac{a(1-b)^{n}(D_{n}(a,b)-b(1-a)^{n})}{D^{2}_{n}(a,b)-ab(1-a)^{n}(1-b)^{n}},

(5)

and

\frac{y_{n}(1-x_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}=\frac{b(1-a)^{n}(D_{n}(a,b)-a(1-b)^{n})}{D^{2}_{n}(a,b)-ab(1-a)^{n}(1-b)^{n}}.

(6)

It may be shown by direct calculation that
D_{n}(a,b)
possesses the Cassini-like identity

D^{2}_{n}(a,b)-D_{n-1}(a,b)D_{n+1}(a,b)=ab(1-a)^{n}(1-b)^{n}

as well as the identities

D_{n}(a,b)-(1-b)D_{n-1}(a,b)=b(1-a)^{n},

and

D_{n}(a,b)-(1-a)D_{n-1}(a,b)=a(1-b)^{n}.

Therefore, Equations (
5
) and (
6
) may be written as

\frac{x_{n}(1-y_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}=\frac{a(1-b)^{n+1}D_{n-1}(a,b)}{D_{n-1}(a,b)D_{n+1}(a,b)}=\frac{a(1-b)^{n+1}}{D_{n+1}(a,b)}=x_{n+1}

and

\frac{y_{n}(1-x_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}=\frac{b(1-a)^{n+1}D_{n-1}(a,b)}{D_{n-1}(a,b)D_{n+1}(a,b)}=\frac{b(1-a)^{n+1}}{D_{n+1}(a,b)}=y_{n+1},

which is exactly what we hoped to show. This completes the proof of Lemma
1
.
∎

With Lemma
1
established, we may now move on to the proof of Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

As we have in Lemma
1
, define the sequences
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by

x_{n}:=\frac{a(a-b)(1-b)^{n}}{a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n}:=\frac{b(a-b)(1-a)^{n}}{a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1}}.

Lemma
1
showed that

x_{n+1}=\frac{x_{n}(1-y_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n+1}=\frac{y_{n}(1-x_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}.

From these expressions, it is not difficult to verify by induction that
0<x_{n},y_{n}<1
when
a,b\in(0,1)
. Thus, with Lemma
1
in mind, by setting
x=x_{n}
and
y=y_{n}
in Abel’s functional Equation (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x_{n}y_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(x_{n})+\mathcal{L}(y_{n})-\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{x_{n}(1-y_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{y_{n}(1-x_{n})}{1-x_{n}y_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(x_{n})+\mathcal{L}(y_{n})-\mathcal{L}(x_{n+1})-\mathcal{L}(y_{n+1}).

Therefore, in summing each side we find that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(x_{n}y_{n})=\mathcal{L}(x_{0})+\mathcal{L}(y_{0})-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(x_{n})-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(y_{n}).

(7)

In order to evaluate the limit of the sequences
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
, note that we may write

x_{n}=\frac{a(a-b)}{a(1-b)-b(1-a)\left(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\right)^{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{n}=\frac{b(a-b)}{a(1-b)\left(\frac{1-b}{1-a}\right)^{n}-b(1-a)}.

Thus, the limit of
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
is determined by
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\right)^{\pm n}
. Since
\left(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\right)^{n}
vanishes in the limit when
1-a<1-b
, we arrive at the following evaluations:

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{a-b}{1-b}&\text{if }a>b\\
0&\text{if }a<b\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}y_{n}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a>b\\
\frac{b-a}{1-a}&\text{if }a<b.\end{cases}

Consequently, Equation (
7
) may be written as

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{ab(a-b)^{2}(1-a)^{n}(1-b)^{n}}{(a(1-b)^{n+1}-b(1-a)^{n+1})^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}(a)+\mathcal{L}(b)-\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a-b}{1-b}\right)&\text{if }a>b\\
\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{b-a}{1-a}\right)&\text{if }a<b.\end{cases}

Since this is equivalent to the result stated in Theorem
4
, our proof is complete.
∎

Before proceeding, let us outline the structure of the remainder of the paper. In the following section, we establish our generalization of Theorem
3
and provide a number of special cases. In addition, we show that in our case, the two identities which comprise our generalization are more closely related than they might first appear. Finally, in the last section we explain exactly how Bridgeman’s Theorem
3
relates to our generalization of it, which is not immediately clear since one is expressed in terms of solutions to Pell’s equation while the other is expressed in terms of Lucas sequences.

3
Series Involving Lucas Sequences

The Lucas sequences
(U_{n}(P,Q))_{n\geq 0}
and
(V_{n}(P,Q))_{n\geq 0}
are second order linear recurrence sequences with constant coefficients. They generalize the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers and possess many other well-known sequences as special cases.

The Lucas sequence
(U_{n}(P,Q))_{n\geq 0}
is defined by the recurrence

\displaystyle U_{0}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle U_{1}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle U_{n}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=P\cdot U_{n-1}(P,Q)-Q\cdot U_{n-2}(P,Q),

while the sequence
(V_{n}(P,Q))_{n\geq 0}
satisfies the same recurrence albeit with different initial values:

\displaystyle V_{0}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=2,

\displaystyle V_{1}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=P,

\displaystyle V_{n}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=P\cdot V_{n-1}(P,Q)-Q\cdot V_{n-2}(P,Q).

The characteristic equation for the Lucas sequences is given by

x^{2}-Px+Q=0,

with a discriminant of
D:=P^{2}-4Q
and roots

\alpha=\frac{P+\sqrt{D}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\frac{P-\sqrt{D}}{2}.

In addition, if
D\neq 0
so that
\alpha
and
\beta
are distinct, one may show that

\alpha^{n}=\frac{V_{n}+U_{n}\sqrt{D}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{n}=\frac{V_{n}-U_{n}\sqrt{D}}{2}.

Therefore,
U_{n}(P,Q)
and
V_{n}(P,Q)
possess the Binet forms

U_{n}(P,Q)=\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}=\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\sqrt{D}}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{n}(P,Q)=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}.

In the remainder of this paper we will assume that
P
and
Q
are real numbers such that
P>0,Q\neq 0
, and
D>0
.

The starting point for the results in this section is the following corollary of Theorem
4
.

Corollary 1
.

If
a\in(0,1)
, then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2}\right)^{n}}{\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1-a}{1+a}\right).

Proof.

If we let
a=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2}
and
b=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2}
in Theorem
4
we find that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2})^{n+1}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2})^{n+1}}{((\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2})^{n+2}-(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2})^{n+2})^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2}\right)+\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2}\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{2a}{1+a}\right).

By re-indexing and using the two-term functional equation, Equation (
1
), to simplify the right side, we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2})^{n}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2})^{n}}{((\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2})^{n+1}-(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{2})^{n+1})^{2}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{2a}{1+a}\right).

Then, applying Equation (
1
) once more to the right side completes the proof.
∎

Remark 1
.

Although the form given above is better suited for our purposes below, the series in Corollary 1 can be expressed more simply as

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}\left(1-a^{2}\right)^{n}}{4\left(\left(1-a\right)^{n+1}-\left(1+a\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1-a}{1+a}\right).

With Corollary
1
established, we are now able to present our generalization of Bridgeman’s theorem.

Theorem 5
.

If
Q>0
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}Q^{kn}}{U^{2}_{k(n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{Q^{k}}{\alpha^{2k}}\right).

(8)

If
Q<0
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is an odd integer, then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(-\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{k(2n-1)}}{DU^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{2kn}}{V^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(-\frac{Q^{k}}{\alpha^{2k}}\right).

(9)

Proof.

To prove Equation (
8
), notice that since
Q>0
and
D=P^{2}-4Q>0
,
\beta=(P-\sqrt{D})/2
is also positive.
Therefore, the quotient

\frac{\sqrt{D}U_{k}}{V_{k}}=\frac{\alpha^{k}-\beta^{k}}{\alpha^{k}+\beta^{k}}

is less than 1 for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Thus, we are permitted to set
a=U_{k}\sqrt{D}/V_{k}
in Corollary
1
. In doing so, we find that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n}}{V^{2}_{k}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V_{k}-U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right).

(10)

Examining the left side of Equation (
10
) first, we see that the numerator of the dilogarithm’s argument may be expressed as

\displaystyle U^{2}_{k}D\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=U^{2}_{k}DV^{-2n}_{k}\left(\tfrac{V_{k}-U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n}\left(\tfrac{V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=U^{2}_{k}DV^{-2n}_{k}\beta^{kn}\alpha^{kn}

\displaystyle=U^{2}_{k}DV^{-2n}_{k}Q^{kn},

while the denominator may be written as

\displaystyle V^{2}_{k}\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2V_{k}}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle V^{-2n}_{k}\left(\left(\tfrac{V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\tfrac{V_{k}-U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle V^{-2n}_{k}\left(\alpha^{k(n+1)}-\beta^{k(n+1)}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, the sum simplifies to

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}DQ^{kn}}{(\alpha^{k(n+1)}-\beta^{k(n+1)})^{2}}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}Q^{kn}}{U^{2}_{k(n+1)}}\right).

Turning our attention to the right side of Equation (
10
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V_{k}-U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}-U^{2}_{k}D}{(V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{4Q^{k}}{(V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{Q^{k}}{\alpha^{2k}}\right).

Thus, we have shown that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}Q^{kn}}{U^{2}_{k(n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{Q^{k}}{\alpha^{2k}}\right),

proving Equation (
8
).

Moving on to Equation (
9
), since
Q<0
and
k
is an odd integer, we see that
\beta^{k}<0
. Therefore, the quotient

\frac{V_{k}}{\sqrt{D}U_{k}}=\frac{\alpha^{k}+\beta^{k}}{\alpha^{k}-\beta^{k}}

is less than 1. Setting
a=V_{k}/U_{k}\sqrt{D}
in Corollary
1
, we find that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n}}{U^{2}_{k}D\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}-V_{k}}{U_{k}\sqrt{D}+V_{k}}\right).

Once again, simplifying the numerator and denominator separately, the numerator is seen to be

V^{2}_{k}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n}=(-1)^{n}U^{-2n}_{k}D^{-n}V^{2}_{k}Q^{kn},

while the denominator admits the form

U^{2}_{k}D\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n+1}-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{V_{k}}{2U_{k}\sqrt{D}}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad{}\\
\begin{aligned} &=U^{-2n}D^{-n}\left(\left(\tfrac{V_{k}+U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n+1}-(-1)^{n+1}\left(\tfrac{V_{k}-U_{k}\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{n+1}\right)^{2}\\
&=U^{-2n}D^{-n}(\alpha^{k(n+1)}+(-1)^{n}\beta^{k(n+1)})^{2}.\end{aligned}

Putting this all together yields

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}(-1)^{n}Q^{kn}}{\left(\alpha^{k(n+1)}+(-1)^{n}\beta^{k(n+1)}\right)^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}-V_{k}}{U_{k}\sqrt{D}+V_{k}}\right).

Next, by splitting the series into two sums, one over all even indices and the other over all odd indices, we find that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}(-1)^{n}Q^{kn}}{\left(\alpha^{k(n+1)}+(-1)^{n}\beta^{k(n+1)}\right)^{2}}\right)\\
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(-\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{k(2n-1)}}{\left(\alpha^{2kn}-\beta^{2kn}\right)^{2}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{2kn}}{\left(\alpha^{k(2n+1)}+\beta^{k(2n+1)}\right)^{2}}\right).

Using the Binet formulas for
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
, we arrive at

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}(-1)^{n}Q^{kn}}{\left(\alpha^{k(n+1)}+(-1)^{n}\beta^{k(n+1)}\right)^{2}}\right)\\
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(-\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{k(2n-1)}}{DU^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V^{2}_{k}Q^{2kn}}{V^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right).

Finally, the right-hand side may be simplified in the same way we proceeded in the proof of Equation (
8
) to show that

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U_{k}\sqrt{D}-V_{k}}{U_{k}\sqrt{D}+V_{k}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(-\frac{Q^{k}}{\alpha^{2k}}\right).

This completes the proof of Theorem
5
.
∎

Remark 2
.

When the parameters
P
and
Q
are integers, the Lucas sequence
U_{n}(P,Q)
is a
divisibility sequence
, in the sense that if
m
divides
n
then
U_{m}
divides
U_{n}
. Moreover, if
\gcd(P,Q)=1
, then
U_{n}(P,Q)
is a
strong divisibility sequence
, which means that

\gcd(U_{m},U_{n})=U_{\gcd(m,n)}.

Consequently, when
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}
, the Lucas sequence
U^{2}_{k}
fully divides
U^{2}_{k(n+1)}
in the summand of Equation (
8
).

We now present some specific examples of Theorem
5
. Many of our examples have already been exhibited in the previous works of Hoorfar and Qi
[
3
]
, Bridgeman
[
2
]
, and Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
. Nevertheless, we restate them here as well as present some new identities, including the initial equations of Examples
2
and
3
, which generalize existing results.

Example 1
.

If
P=3
and
Q=1
, then
U_{n}(3,1)=F_{2n}
, where
F_{n}
is the
n
-th Fibonacci number. With these values of
P
and
Q
, Equation (
8
) yields

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{F^{2}_{2k}}{F^{2}_{2kn}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{4k}}\right),

which was given as an example in Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
. In particular, if
k=1
, then we have the nice identity

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{F^{2}_{2n}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{2}{7+3\sqrt{5}}\right).

Example 2
.

If
P=2x
and
Q=1
, then
U_{n}(2x,1)=U_{n-1}(x)
, where
U_{n}(x)
is the
n
-th Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. Equation (
8
) in this case returns

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k-1}(x)}{U^{2}_{kn-1}(x)}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1})^{2k}}\right).

Letting
x=\cosh(\theta)
and
k=1
, the identity
U_{n-1}(\cosh(\theta))=\sinh(n\theta)/\sinh(\theta)
allows us to deduce the interesting series

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{\sinh^{2}(\theta)}{\sinh^{2}(n\theta)}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(e^{-2\theta}\right).

This identity was first derived by Hoorfar and Qi
[
3
]
and later rediscovered by Bridgeman
[
2
]
.

Example 3
.

If
P=x+1
and
Q=x
with
x>1
, then

U_{n}(x+1,x)=\frac{x^{n}-1}{x-1}=1+x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{n-1}.

Applying Equation (
8
), we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(x^{kn}\left(\frac{x^{k}-1}{x^{k(n+1)}-1}\right)^{2}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{x^{k}}\right).

Setting
k=1
gives the special case

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\left(\frac{x^{n/2}}{1+x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{n}}\right)^{2}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),

which was given as an example by Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
.

Example 4
.

If
P=1,Q=-1
, and
k
is an odd integer, then
U_{n}(1,-1)=F_{n}
and
V_{n}(1,-1)=L_{n}
, where
L_{n}
is the
n
-th Lucas number. Equation (
9
) shows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{L^{2}_{k}}{5F^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{L^{2}_{k}}{L^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2k}}\right).

Specifically, for
k=1
we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{5F^{2}_{2n}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{L^{2}_{2n+1}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{15}.

This example was first derived by Bridgeman
[
2
]
.

Example 5
.

If
P=2,Q=-1
, and
k
is an odd integer, then
U_{n}(2,-1)=P_{n}
and
V_{n}(2,-1)=Q_{n}
, where
P_{n}
is the
n
-th Pell number and
Q_{n}
is the
n
-th Pell-Lucas number. With these values, Equation (
9
) yields the identity

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{8P^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{Q^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2k}}\right).

For
k=1
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{2P^{2}_{2n}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{4}{Q^{2}_{2n+1}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt{2}}\right).

Example 6
.

Let
P=1
and
Q=-3
and set
A_{n}=U(1,-3)
and
{B_{n}=V_{n}(1,-3)}
. The first handful of terms of
(A_{n})
and
(B_{n})
are given by

(A_{n})_{n\geq 0}=(0,1,1,4,7,19,40,97,217,508,1159,\cdots)

(11)

and

(B_{n})_{n\geq 0}=(2,1,7,10,31,61,154,337,799,1810,4207,\dots).

(12)

For odd
k
, Equation (
9
) gives

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{B^{2}_{k}3^{k(2n-1)}}{13A^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{B^{2}_{k}3^{2kn}}{B^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\left(\frac{6}{7+\sqrt{13}}\right)^{k}\right).

Letting
k=1
, we find that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{3^{2n-1}}{13A^{2}_{2n}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{3^{2n}}{B^{2}_{2n+1}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{6}{7+\sqrt{13}}\right).

Before moving on, we point out that since we have not required
P
and
Q
to take integer values, it can be shown that Equation (
9
) of Theorem
5
is actually a special case of Equation (
8
) of the same theorem. To see this, suppose that
Q<0
and set

P^{\prime}:=\sqrt{P^{2}-4Q},\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{\prime}:=-Q.

If we also let
D^{\prime}:={P^{\prime}}^{2}-4Q^{\prime}
, then

\alpha^{\prime}:=\frac{P^{\prime}+\sqrt{D^{\prime}}}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{D}+P}{2}=\alpha,

and

\beta^{\prime}:=\frac{P^{\prime}-\sqrt{D^{\prime}}}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{D}-P}{2}=-\beta.

Therefore, the Lucas sequence
U_{n}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
is given by

U_{n}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\frac{{\alpha^{\prime}}^{n}-{\beta^{\prime}}^{n}}{\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}}=\frac{\alpha^{n}-(-1)^{n}\beta^{n}}{P}.

This may be expressed in terms of the Lucas sequences as

U_{n}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{D}U_{n}(P,Q)/P&\text{if }n\text{ even}\\
V_{n}(P,Q)/P&\text{if }n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Similarly, the sequence
(V_{n}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}))
is given by

V_{n}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})=\begin{cases}V_{n}(P,Q)/P&\text{if }n\text{ even}\\
\sqrt{D}U_{n}(P,Q)/P&\text{if }n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Employing this transformation in Equation (
8
) when
k
is odd, we obtain Equation (
9
).
To illustrate this, let us look at the following example which was exhibited in Jaipong et al.
[
4
]
.

Example 7
.

If
P=\sqrt{5}
and
Q=1
, then

U_{n}(\sqrt{5},1)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{5}F_{n}&\text{if }n\text{ even}\\
L_{n}&\text{if }n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

With these values of
P
and
Q
, Equation (
8
) becomes

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{U^{2}_{k}}{U^{2}_{kn}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2k}}\right).

If
k
is odd, then we recover Example
4
given above, which was originally obtained by setting
P=1
and
Q=-1
. Namely,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{L_{k}}{5F^{2}_{2kn}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{L_{k}}{L^{2}_{k(2n+1)}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2k}}\right).

On the other hand, if
k
is even then we find that

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{F^{2}_{k}}{F^{2}_{kn}}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2k}}\right),

which is exactly the identity given in Example
1
.

4
Relation to Bridgeman’s Theorem

The sequences
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}
appearing in Theorem
3
are expressed in terms of powers of the quadratic irrational
u:=a+b\sqrt{n}
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
. In particular, they are defined as

a_{k}+b_{k}\sqrt{n}:=(a+b\sqrt{n})^{k}.

We may express the sequences
(a_{k})
and
(b_{k})
in terms of the Lucas sequences
(U_{k})
and
(V_{k})
in the following way. Suppose that
P,Q\in\mathbb{Q}
and that

\frac{P+\sqrt{P^{2}-4Q}}{2}=a+b\sqrt{n}.

Since
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
, it must be the case that

P=2a\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{4a^{2}-4Q}=2b\sqrt{n}.

Thus,
Q
is found to be

Q=a^{2}-nb^{2}.

However, since Bridgeman’s theorem requires that
(a,b)
is a solution to one of Pell’s equations

a^{2}-nb^{2}=\pm 1,

we find that
Q=\pm 1
. Therefore,
P=2a
and
Q=\pm 1
, dependent on whether
(a,b)
is a solution to the positive or negative Pell equation, respectively. Next, recall that

\left(\frac{P+\sqrt{P^{2}-4Q}}{2}\right)^{k}=\frac{V_{k}(P,Q)+U_{k}(P,Q)\sqrt{P^{2}-4Q}}{2}.

Comparing this with the definition of
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
, we see that

V_{k}(2a,\pm 1)/2+bU_{k}(2a,\pm 1)\sqrt{n}=a_{k}+b_{k}\sqrt{n},

and therefore,

a_{k}=\frac{1}{2}V_{k}(2a,\pm 1)\quad\text{and}\quad b_{k}=bU_{k}(2a,\pm 1).

Using these expressions for
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
, Equation (
3
) of Theorem
3
may be rewritten as

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{U^{2}_{k}(2a,1)}\right),

(13)

while Equation (
4
) may be expressed as

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{nb^{2}U^{2}_{2k}(2a,-1)}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{4a^{2}}{V^{2}_{2k+1}(2a,-1)}\right).

Moreover, by using the fact that
V_{1}(2a,-1)=2a
and
D:=P^{2}-4Q=4b^{2}n
, the previous line may be written as

\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V_{1}(2a,-1)}{DU^{2}_{2k}(2a,-1)}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V_{1}(2a,-1)}{V^{2}_{2k+1}(2a,-1)}\right).

(14)

It is then easy to see that Equations (
13
) and (
14
) correspond to Equations (
8
) and (
9
) of Theorem
5
, with
k=1
, and
Q=\pm 1
, respectively.
