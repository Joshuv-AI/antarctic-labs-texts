Preamble

Since the publication of its first Core Cardiovascular Training Statement (COCATS) in 1995,1 the American College of Cardiology (ACC) has defined the knowledge, experiences, skills, and behaviors expected of clinical cardiologists. Subsequent revisions have moved toward competency-based training based on the 6-domain competency structure promulgated by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) and the American Board of Medical Specialties and endorsed by the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM).2,3 The ACC has taken a similar approach to describe the aligned general cardiovascular disease lifelong learning competencies that practicing cardiologists are expected to maintain. Many hospital systems now use the 6-domain structure as part of medical staff privileging, peer-review, and professional competence assessments.

Whereas COCATS and the associated Lifelong Learning Competencies for General Cardiologists4 focus on general clinical cardiology, ACC Advanced Training Statements5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and associated Lifelong Learning Statements10,11 define selected competencies beyond those expected of all cardiologists and that typically require training beyond a standard cardiovascular disease fellowship curriculum. This includes, but is not limited to, those disciplines for which there is an ABIM subspecialty certification. The Advanced Training Statements describe key experiences and outcomes necessary to acquire skills in a defined subspecialty area of cardiovascular disease in a structured training program. These are supplemented by Lifelong Learning Statements that address the commitment to sustaining and enriching competency throughout a career.

The ACC Competency Management Committee oversees the development and periodic revision of the cardiovascular disease training and competency statements. A key feature of competency-based training and performance is an outcome-based evaluation system. Although specific areas of training may require a minimum number of procedures or duration of training to ensure adequate exposure to the range of clinical disorders, the objective assessment of proficiency and outcomes demonstrates the achievement of competency. Evaluation tools include examinations, direct observation, procedure logbooks, simulation, conference presentations, and multisource (360°) evaluations. For practicing physicians, these tools also include professional society registry or hospital quality data, peer-review processes, and patient satisfaction surveys. A second feature of competency-based training is the recognition that learners gain competency at different rates. For multiyear training programs, assessment of representative curricular milestones during training can identify learners or areas that require additional focused attention.

The recommendations in ACC Training and Lifelong Learning Statements are based on available evidence and, where evidence is lacking, reflect consensus expert opinion. The writing committees are broad-based and typically include early-, mid-, and later-career specialists; general cardiology and subspecialty training directors, practicing cardiologists, committee members working in institutions of various sizes and diverse practice settings across the United States; fellows-in-training; and other members of the cardiovascular care team. All documents undergo a rigorous process of peer review and public comment. Recommendations are intended to guide the assessment of the competence of cardiovascular disease care clinicians beginning independent practice as well as those undergoing periodic review to ensure that competence is maintained.

This Advanced Training Statement addresses the core competencies required of a relatively new type of cardiovascular imaging trainee—the advanced cardiovascular imager—someone who has obtained competence to perform and interpret (analogous to Level II) across all 4 imaging modalities: cardiovascular computed tomography, cardiovascular magnetic resonance, echocardiography, and nuclear cardiology. Furthermore, this statement identifies additional competencies for cardiovascular imagers who choose to further specialize (analogous to Level III) in ≥1 imaging modalities. Notably, this statement emphasizes the importance of integrating findings among the different imaging modalities as a foundational skill and provides examples to guide trainees and programs. Appropriate measures for assessing competence in the context of cardiovascular imaging training are also provided.

Background and Scope

The original 1995 ACC recommendations for training in adult cardiology evolved from a Core Cardiology Training Symposium.1 After several iterations, COCATS 4 focuses on trainee outcomes that require delineation of specific components of competency within the subspecialty, definition of the tools necessary to assess training, and establishment of milestones documenting trainee progression toward independent competency.12 Ultimately, the goal is for trainees to develop the professional skill set to evaluate, diagnose, and treat patients with acute and chronic cardiovascular diseases.

Each iteration of COCATS includes individual task force reports that address subspecialty areas in cardiology, each of which is an important component in training a fellow in cardiovascular disease. Within COCATS 4, Task Forces 4 to 8 addressed training in cardiovascular imaging and updated previous standards for general cardiovascular training for fellows enrolled in cardiovascular disease fellowship programs. Task Force 4 provided an overview of the role of multimodality imaging13; and Task Forces 5 to 8 covered training standards for cardiovascular CT,14 cardiovascular MR,15 echocardiography,16 and nuclear cardiology,17 respectively. These individual modality reports addressed the faculty, facilities, equipment, and additional support required for each modality as well as training components including didactic, clinical, and hands-on experience, and the number of procedures and duration of training for each modality. Importantly, the COCATS 4 training documents focus primarily on Levels I and II training and do not provide detailed guidelines for advanced training in cardiovascular imaging.

This Advanced Training Statement focuses on requirements for training in advanced cardiovascular imaging for adult patients, including acquisition of core competencies across all 4 imaging modalities: cardiovascular CT, cardiovascular MR, echocardiography, and nuclear cardiology. These training requirements will allow trainees to become consulting cardiovascular imaging specialists who can guide decision-making regarding the appropriate imaging test or series of tests that may be required to inform patient management and optimize patient outcomes. For training standards related to pediatric cardiovascular imaging, readers should refer to the “2015 SPCTPD/ACC/AAP/AHA Training Guidelines for Pediatric Cardiology Fellowship Programs: Task Force 2: Pediatric Cardiology Fellowship Training in Noninvasive Cardiac Imaging.”18

Evolution of Cardiovascular Imaging

Cardiovascular imaging is a rapidly evolving field with recent advances in imaging technology resulting in an improved understanding of cardiovascular structure, function, and metabolism. From single-modal techniques such as echocardiography and planar/single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)-based nuclear cardiology, the field has expanded to include advanced modalities such as cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), cardiac computed tomography (CCT), and positron emission tomography (PET) with hybrid modalities that further enhance diagnostic accuracy of cardiovascular pathology and disease. Artificial intelligence and machine learning have the potential to transform the efficiency, image quality, and diagnostic accuracy of each modality. These and other technological advances have given rise to new imaging specialties and increased the demand for dedicated cardiovascular imagers with knowledge and expertise across all 4 cardiovascular imaging modalities.

Trainees interested in advanced multimodality cardiovascular imaging must complete 3 years of cardiovascular disease fellowship training in an ACGME-approved program and be eligible to take the certification examination in cardiovascular disease in addition to completing an advanced cardiovascular imaging fellowship. General cardiovascular disease training programs are encouraged to expose fellows to the breadth of cardiovascular imaging early in training to provide a foundation for further knowledge and skill development, allow for individualized training program planning, and to expose trainees to the career opportunities available in cardiovascular imaging.

Given the complex nature of this field, it is expected that additional or post fellowship dedicated cardiovascular imaging training will be needed to gain the experience necessary to become a competent, independent expert in cardiovascular imaging, depending on the trainee’s career focus. The length of additional dedicated cardiovascular imaging training required beyond Cardiovascular Disease Fellowship will be dependent on the trainee’s prior cardiovascular imaging training and the desired level of additional expertise based on an imaging career focus. This document provides the framework for training across the expanse of cardiovascular imaging to define the advanced cardiovascular imaging specialist with core expertise across all 4 imaging modalities.

Levels of Training

COCATS 4 was charged with updating previously published standards for training fellows in cardiovascular medicine and establishing consistent training criteria across all aspects of cardiology, including cardiovascular imaging modalities. For the Cardiovascular Disease Fellowship, the following 3 levels of training have been delineated for training in various imaging modalities.

Level I training, the basic training required for trainees to become competent consultants, is required by all fellows in cardiology and can be accomplished as part of a standard 3-year cardiovascular disease training program. For cardiovascular imaging modalities, Level I training is defined as an introductory or early level of competency in performing and interpreting imaging studies that is achieved during fellowship training but is insufficient to provide independent interpretation of results.

Level II training refers to additional training that enables cardiologists to perform or interpret specific diagnostic tests and procedures or render more specialized care for patients with certain conditions. Level II training in selected areas may be achieved by some trainees during the standard 3-year cardiovascular disease fellowship, depending on career goals and the use of elective rotations. Level II training is required to provide independent interpretation of all cardiovascular imaging modalities.

Level III or advanced training typically requires additional experience beyond Cardiovascular Disease Fellowship to acquire specialized knowledge and skill in performing, interpreting, and training others to perform specific procedures or render advanced, specialized care for procedures at a high level of skill. Advanced training in imaging is recommended for individuals who intend to perform and interpret complex studies in special populations, lead a research program, direct an imaging laboratory, and/or train others in advanced aspects of a specific imaging modality.

Since the publication of COCATS 4, considerable scientific advances and clinical growth in all cardiovascular imaging modalities have occurred, prompting the proliferation of advanced training programs in multimodality imaging. As a result, the development of a framework to train cardiologists as imaging specialists is needed,19 which is the focus of this document. The expectation of such programs is the training of advanced cardiovascular imaging specialists capable of independently performing and interpreting all 4 imaging modalities, as well as providing expert consultative care for selecting the optimal imaging test for a diverse range of clinical scenarios and patient populations (Figure 1). Trainees would be expected to acquire core (Level II analogous) skills to independently perform and interpret all 4 cardiovascular imaging modalities, regardless of whether they are pursuing focused training (Level III analogous) in ≥1 modalities. Although an advanced cardiovascular imager may ultimately elect to practice fewer than all 4 imaging modalities in their career, competency across, and integration of all 4 modalities during training, will define them as an advanced cardiovascular imager as outlined in this document (Figure 2). It is recognized that some trainees will elect to focus on fewer than all 4 imaging modalities during training and would therefore not be considered an advanced cardiovascular imager based on the principles and competencies outlined in this document. This document does not preclude such training, and those interested in developing competency analogous to Level II or III in a specific imaging modality can continue to use existing COCATS guidelines, advanced training statements, or other societal documents to guide training in those areas of interest. However, it is important to recognize that there have been significant advancements in the field of cardiovascular imaging, and persons pursuing training in individual imaging modalities should consider acquiring the knowledge and skills reflected in this document.

General Standards

Faculty

Dedicated faculty who are committed to teaching and mentoring trainees are the most important resource for the success of an advanced cardiovascular imaging training program. Faculty serve as role models for professionalism and clinical excellence and promote a positive learning environment to optimize the education of fellows in clinical and scholarly activities. Faculty must include specialists with a broad range of expertise in cardiovascular diseases and cardiovascular imaging. Faculty should have knowledge of the optimal clinical utilization and performance of multiple cardiovascular imaging modalities and advanced expertise (analogous to Level III) in at least 1 cardiovascular imaging modality. Imaging faculty may have achieved cardiovascular imaging expertise through training pathways as a cardiologist, radiologist, nuclear medicine physician, emergency physician, critical care physician, physicist, or anesthesiologist (in the case of echocardiography). Exposure to and close working relationships with faculty with diverse training experiences will allow for a comprehensive training experience. A collaborative relationship with radiology should be included because the experience of these key faculty will be valuable in the education of the cardiovascular imaging trainee. In addition, the development of the medical physics curriculum should be overseen by a medical physicist.

The individual imaging modality laboratories in which the training of multimodality imaging fellows occurs must be under the direct supervision of a full-time qualified, Level III trained (or analogous), board-certified laboratory director. Direct experience with experts in each cardiovascular imaging modality provides an important opportunity to understand the strengths and limitations of each modality relative to other techniques. In addition to developing, implementing, supervising, teaching, mentoring, and evaluating the fellows’ clinical and research education, faculty should be involved in research, education, or both.

Facilities and Equipment

To provide advanced training in cardiovascular imaging, the institution should provide a broad range of cardiovascular diagnostic and therapeutic services. This should include care delivery to patients with sufficient diversity, complexity, and volume of cardiovascular conditions, including acquired and congenital heart disease. Preferably, sites should perform comprehensive echocardiography, CCT, CMR, and nuclear cardiology. However, if ≥1 modalities are not performed at the site, access to that modality’s images and reports should be available for review and cross correlation (see Section 4.2.2. for more details). In addition, sites ideally should routinely perform interventional coronary and structural cardiac and valve interventions, cardiothoracic surgery, electrophysiology procedures, and provide intensive care and emergency services. Sites should provide the appropriate equipment for medication, contrast, and/or stress agent administration and patient monitoring and a computer network infrastructure for data transmission, storage, processing, study interpretation, and reporting. Imaging laboratories should be accredited through recognized healthcare facility accrediting organizations such as the Intersocietal Accreditation Commission or the American College of Radiology, as appropriate, and maintain all Joint Commission requirements. This includes appropriate radiation safety training and adherence to the “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) radiation safety principle.

The training environment should provide trainees with participation in quality improvement initiatives, structured reporting, process improvement, application of appropriate use criteria (AUC), and laboratory operations. Some institutions may not be able to offer experience in all recommended procedures or ACHD. Rotations at other sites with a high volume of structural procedures, ACHD, or both would be appropriate to accomplish these specific aspects of training. The program director is responsible for verifying the quality of the training at external sites. The training site’s resources should allow routine correlation with other diagnostic data and patient outcomes. Sites should offer adequate resources to allow for routine multimodality and modality-specific case-based and didactic educational conferences, to include conferences involving imaging for the planning of cardiovascular interventions (eg, heart team conferences) and multimodality correlation conferences.

Contemporary imaging in a multimodality laboratory should include the following.

Cardiovascular CT: Trainees should have access to contemporary cardiovascular CT scanner hardware, equipped with modern radiation dose-reduction and reconstruction technology, and specialized equipment for multiphase contrast administration. Although exposure to photon-counting CT is not feasible in all centers, having access to this technology is recommended when possible. Trainees should have access to contemporary advanced workstations for performance and interpretation of coronary artery calcium scoring, coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), and cardiovascular CT for noncoronary indications, including evaluation before or after valvular or nonvalvular structural heart disease interventions. When possible, attendance at CT protocol committee meetings, or similar meetings, should be encouraged for trainees. Trainees should also have access to specialized technologies used for evaluating coronary artery disease such as CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) and quantitative plaque analysis. In centers where such techniques are not performed, interactive online learning modules could be used to supplement this content.

Cardiovascular MR: Trainees should have access to standard clinical MR scanners (either 1.5- or 3-T), equipped with contemporary software and acquisition sequences. Trainees should have access to advanced image analysis tools for interpretation of cardiovascular MR images, including quantification techniques. Trainees should also have access to specialized technologies for evaluating strain techniques and 4-dimensional flow. However, the technical capability to perform and interpret parametric mapping and stress cardiovascular MR is strongly recommended. In centers where some of the advanced techniques are not performed, interactive online learning modules could be used to supplement this content.

Echocardiography: Trainees should have access to both ultrasound equipment and offline workstations for performance and interpretation of 2-dimensional (2D) and 3-dimensional (3D) transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), Doppler echocardiography, contrast echocardiography, stress echocardiography, and strain imaging. Intraprocedural echocardiography, including intraoperative, interventional, and electrophysiology laboratory procedures, should be available to trainees. Advanced intraprocedural imaging including, but not limited to, intracardiac echocardiography and echocardiographic-fluoroscopic fusion imaging, may be components of advanced training.

Nuclear Cardiology: Trainees should have access to SPECT cameras, ideally equipped with attenuation correction. Although access to traditional nuclear medicine SPECT cameras is sufficient, access to solid-state SPECT cameras would be optimal. Access to PET cameras is strongly encouraged and would provide for optimal training in nuclear cardiology. The preferred means of attenuation would be with hybrid SPECT-CT and PET-CT cameras, which would allow for hybrid imaging. Access to software platforms for image reconstruction, interpretation, and standardized reporting is required. In addition, access to a radiopharmaceutical “hot lab” is required for training in radiotracer preparation, dose calibration, and patient administration to meet U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission requirements or the requirements of the appropriate U.S. governmental regulatory agency.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration

Proficient multimodality cardiovascular imaging training requires multidisciplinary collaboration. Trainees should develop working relationships with general cardiologists and those with advanced training in interventional cardiology, electrophysiology, heart failure, ACHD, and preventive cardiology, as well as with radiologists, cardiovascular surgeons, cardiac anesthesiologists, critical care physicians, neurologists, oncologists, infectious disease specialists, and other specialties, as appropriate. Trainees should have the opportunity to interact regularly with other members of the healthcare team to enhance learning and patient care. Trainees should participate in multidisciplinary management conferences and formal and informal consultations with various specialists.

Collaborative interaction with experienced cardiac sonographers, physicists, and technologists who are committed to training fellows in the creation of high-quality images, as well as nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare team members involved in the performance of exercise or vasodilatory stress imaging, is critical to success. Further, physicists with expertise in regulations, quality control, quality improvement, and MR and radiation safety should be an integral part of fellowship education and members of the teaching faculty. Access to a dedicated physicist will further strengthen the trainee’s ability to develop specialized examination protocols and balance image quality with radiation dose. Trainees should also be exposed to laboratory administrators, physicists, and other leadership committed to educating fellows on the processes of laboratory accreditation, state and local regulations, quality control, and other administrative processes required for successful imaging laboratory operations. Advanced cardiovascular imaging trainees should have an opportunity to correlate imaging with cardiac anatomy and pathology by observing cardiac surgery or reviewing specimens with a cardiac pathologist.

Contemporary imaging in a multimodality laboratory should include the following.

Cardiovascular CT: Although cardiovascular CT is broadly used in the management of patients with cardiovascular disease, additional opportunities for multidisciplinary collaborations, beyond those mentioned previously, may focus on services in preventive cardiology and emergency medicine. On-site scanning experience and education from specialized cardiovascular CT technologists are invaluable for mastering the practical performance of CCT. Access to a dedicated physicist will further strengthen the trainee’s ability to develop specialized examination protocols and balance image quality with radiation dose. Exposure to a dedicated 3D postprocessing laboratory is encouraged.

Cardiovascular MR: In addition to multidisciplinary collaborations described previously, cardiovascular MR trainees should interact with the team responsible for MR safety. Interactive collaboration and education from dedicated cardiovascular MR technologists are a cornerstone of training and is necessary to strengthen the trainee’s ability to protocol and troubleshoot exams. Access to a dedicated MR physicist or MR scientist will further strengthen the trainee’s ability to develop specialized examination protocols.

Echocardiography: In addition to the multidisciplinary collaborations noted previously, trainees should interact with any healthcare clinician that uses bedside echocardiography or point-of-care ultrasound to manage patients with known or suspected cardiovascular disease. This includes clinicians with expertise in critical care echocardiography and emergency point-of-care ultrasound. Hands-on scanning, equipment troubleshooting, and 2D/3D image acquisition and analysis with advanced cardiac sonographers and faculty are essential components of training in echocardiography. In some instances, echocardiography simulation training may be useful to increase the breadth and depth of disease-specific echocardiographic image acquisition and performance. Training in basic principles of radiation protection and radiation safety may also be required depending on the area of focus.

Nuclear Cardiology: Trainees should have access to radiopharmacists, physicists, and/or nuclear medicine technologists for basic training in radiopharmaceutical preparation, quality control, and basic operation of related equipment (eg, dose calibrators). Professionals with expertise in medical physics, health physics, or both to teach basic principles of radiation protection, radiation safety related to radiopharmaceutical handling, and containment of radiopharmaceutical contamination must be included in the teaching faculty. This training should be part of the hands-on and didactic curriculum in accordance with the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission requirements.

Training Components

Didactic Program

Didactic instruction for advanced cardiovascular imaging training may take place in various formats, including lectures, online modules, journal clubs, grand rounds, clinical case presentations, multidisciplinary team conferences both within cardiovascular disease and across other disciplines, research conferences, simulator-based training, and patient safety or quality improvement conferences. Didactic training should focus on development of competencies common to all imaging modalities, including the physics of image acquisition and formation, anatomy, pathophysiology, and hemodynamics. Didactics should incorporate multimodality content covering advanced medical knowledge and patient care relevant to these common competencies. In addition, didactics should include training in comparative indications, limitations, and appropriate use of all imaging modalities for a broad range of cardiovascular diseases. Trainees should also be able to understand downstream utilization and correlate imaging findings, including extracardiac findings, across all imaging modalities. This can be done through self-learning materials, case-based lectures, and teaching conferences. The curriculum can be offered using multiple educational methods including didactic lecture series, participation in national meetings, self-directed reading, and online learning, and should be tailored for the level of expertise the trainee wishes to achieve. Some of this information can be achieved by collaborating with other departments’ educational offerings (eg, radiology) and should include training by imaging faculty and lead technologists and sonographers.

The following modality-specific topics should be covered in the training program.

Cardiovascular CT: The cardiovascular imaging trainee needs to acquire understanding of CT scanning protocol in relation to the clinical question, adapting to patient-specific characteristics, and technical challenges with image acquisition and interpretation. To achieve this, in addition to standard lectures and self-reading materials on acquisition protocols, radiation exposure, and methods to reduce radiation exposure, the cardiovascular CT program should provide direct hands-on exposure at the scanner with appropriate supervision. In addition, with direct supervision of teaching faculty, trainees will acquire the knowledge and skill set to perform all aspects of image acquisition and reconstruction, postprocessing, and imaging interpretation. This will be achieved via direct involvement across many diverse cases. Importantly, cardiovascular CT training will include an emphasis on the clinical reporting of examinations, including the integration of prior knowledge and review of other studies to provide the necessary guidance and recommendations to address the clinical question(s).

Cardiovascular MR: The didactic program in cardiovascular MR should cover the broad spectrum of cardiovascular MR imaging. This includes the basics of MR imaging physics and instrumentation, sequences and troubleshooting at the scanner, image reconstruction techniques, and MR imaging safety. In addition to these MR imaging principles, the curriculum should cover clinical indications and the use of MR imaging across the spectrum of cardiovascular disease, emphasizing the unique role that MR imaging has in various conditions. An important component of cardiovascular MR education is at the scanner application of techniques learned. Trainees must demonstrate the ability to translate the learned information into proper sequence selection and sequence manipulation to achieve optimal results.

Echocardiography: A didactic program should provide advanced trainees with an understanding of ultrasound physics, instrumentation, echocardiographic image formation and optimization, and the clinical application of various echocardiographic modalities. Besides 2D and Doppler echocardiography, didactics should review the clinical application of additional echocardiographic tools including ultrasound-enhancing agents, strain imaging, 3D echocardiography, stress imaging, TEE, and intracardiac echocardiography. Didactic modules should also include the clinical application of advanced 3D echocardiographic imaging modes (eg, multiplanar reconstruction, volume rendering), and fusion imaging. Didactics for advanced trainees in echocardiography should include the appropriate use of these modalities across the spectrum of cardiovascular diseases, including structural heart disease and interventions, cardiac surgery, perioperative settings, and critical care settings. The program should include training on the diagnostic strengths and limitations of each echocardiographic modality, and the integration with multimodality imaging. In addition to didactics related to cognitive skills, acquisition of psychomotor skills through the performance of disease-specific prescriptive protocols for TTE and TEE imaging should be part of this training. The didactic program should equip advanced trainees with the knowledge and experience needed to acquire new echocardiography skills and integrate new echocardiography techniques as they emerge.

Nuclear Cardiology: The didactics should cover basic physics of nuclear cardiology and instrumentation, including SPECT and PET systems, as well as the clinical application of advanced nuclear cardiology techniques. In addition, trainees must meet the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission training requirements and qualifications as detailed in Title 10 of the Code of Federal Law, Part 35, Section 290 A to become an authorized user.20 This will be accomplished by integrating general concepts of the modality (eg, AUC, image interpretation, pitfalls) with the understanding of nuclear physics, the use of radiopharmaceuticals and ionizing radiation, radiation safety, and radiopharmacy procedures into patient care. A minimum of 80 hours of didactics is required by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission to become an authorized user under certification from the Certification Board of Nuclear Cardiology, which can be completed by web-based lectures or approved in-person didactics.

Imaging Modality Selection

Training for advanced cardiovascular imagers should emphasize integrating all available clinical and imaging data to select the most appropriate test(s) for patient evaluation. Formulating such a patient-focused approach also requires a fundamental understanding of the pathophysiology of a wide spectrum of cardiovascular diseases and the impact of patient-level variables on test selection. This advanced training will provide trainees with an understanding of the implications of test results on patient management, both with respect to subsequent diagnostic evaluation and development of patient-focused treatment plans. The first step in determining the appropriateness of any of the imaging modalities discussed in this statement for an individual patient entails patient screening for contraindications or patient characteristics that may compromise safety or image quality. These contraindications or relative contraindications may require changes to a study protocol, which the trainees should understand and be able to apply. Validated or societal-endorsed standardized diagnostic criteria, when available, should be preferentially used to identify the optimum imaging modality for a given clinical scenario. It is instrumental that trainees have detailed knowledge of societal-endorsed documents that provide indications for utilization of the various imaging modalities in appropriate clinical settings.21, 22, 23

Additionally, emergency cardiac imaging is often necessary to help with the management and diagnosis of unstable patients and in those with concern of life-threatening cardiovascular conditions. This requires review of clinical information; rapid determination of the appropriate imaging modality to be used; performance, interpretation, and reporting of the cardiac imaging data within the context of the clinical scenario; and communication with the patient’s healthcare team in a timely manner.

The following modality-specific topics should be covered in the training program.

Cardiovascular CT: Advanced cardiovascular imaging trainees should know the clinical indications and safety for CCT in the context of the clinical presentation and patient characteristics. All patients should be screened for contraindications to CCT or characteristics that may compromise image quality. If contraindications are present (eg, administration of iodinated contrast in the setting of significant renal impairment), CCT should not be performed unless the benefits of the examination are deemed to outweigh the potential risks. If image quality may be compromised, a risk vs benefit assessment should follow, along with a shared decision-making discussion with the patient and referring physician. Patient-related factors that may compromise image quality may include the presence of morbid obesity, the inability to obtain adequate intravenous access for high-flow contrast injection, the inability to comply with breath-hold instructions, and contraindications to receiving sublingual or transdermal nitroglycerin or to receiving heart-rate lowering medications. In those with renal impairment or at risk for contrast-induced nephropathy, an estimated glomerular filtration rate should be determined before the examination.

CCT is a valuable tool in the emergency setting and may be the first imaging modality to document acute aortic syndromes, severe coronary disease, or myocardial and pericardial pathology. Trainees should be able to recognize conditions that require urgent or emergency intervention and communicate this information to the healthcare team in a timely manner. Trainees should also be familiar with certain extracardiac findings on the CCT examination that may require urgent management, such as pulmonary pathology, pulmonary embolism, and venous thrombosis.

Cardiovascular MR: Trainees should understand for all CMR studies that they may supervise, review, and/or interpret: the purpose of the CMR study (eg, to confirm or exclude a clinical suspicion and/or risk stratify disease states), potential contraindications to the study, and how it will impact patient management. These contraindications or relative contraindications, which may require scanning adjustments, may include the presence of non–CMR safe devices or metallic foreign bodies in sensitive areas of the body (eg, intraocular or brain).

CMR is not routinely performed in the setting of hemodynamic instability due to prolonged acquisition time and relative inaccessibility to the patient. It can be considered as a CT alternative for acute aortic syndromes but usually in limited scenarios. Patients being evaluated for ventricular tachycardia ablation or myocarditis may be appropriate for CMR if clinically stable and able to comply with image acquisition. Trainees should understand the relative benefits and risks of performing CMR when triaging potential patients and determining the appropriate imaging protocol in a hemodynamically unstable patient. Communication with ordering clinicians is essential to avoid further clinical deterioration during the scan.

Echocardiography: The advanced cardiovascular imaging trainee should have a comprehensive understanding of the important role echocardiography plays in the diagnosis, medical management, and procedural guidance of patients with cardiovascular diseases. Although echocardiography is often the most widely available and first-line technique for the assessment of cardiac structure and function for most cardiac disorders, trainees should utilize a complementary and integrated multimodality imaging approach in the selection of alternative or additional tests, based on their ability to answer specific clinical questions relevant to diagnosis, risk stratification, and management decisions. Although TTE is considered a safe and noninvasive test without radiation, trainees should also be aware of any relative or absolute contraindications to echocardiographic applications in various disease states (eg, exercise or pharmacologic stress echocardiography with hemodynamic disorders) as well as patient-specific factors, which may increase the risk of echocardiographic imaging modalities (eg, esophageal pathology for TEE, preexisting arrhythmias for pharmacologic stress) or the inability to acquire adequate image quality (eg, large body habitus or severe obstructive pulmonary disease for TTE, hiatal hernia or extreme cardiac malpositioning for TEE). The trainee should understand the relative diagnostic value of TTE vs TEE for different cardiovascular pathologies. The trainee should be able to determine which echocardiographic modes are most appropriate, and the alternative or additional imaging modalities which could be appropriate for the specific diagnostic or management question.

Given its portability, rapid image acquisition and interpretation, and relative ease of use, echocardiography is often the first imaging modality utilized in the setting of cardiovascular emergencies including but not limited to acute aortic syndromes, pericardial tamponade, ventricular and valve dysfunction, and mechanical ventricular support placement and function. Trainees should know the indications for echocardiography in the emergency setting, the strengths and limitations of TTE vs TEE, and the importance of integrating findings with the clinical scenario and other imaging data.

Nuclear Cardiology: A majority of nuclear cardiology focuses on the evaluation of ischemic heart disease. Patient selection for nuclear stress testing should be performed in the context of AUC and a broad understanding of multimodality options for the evaluation of chest pain. Furthermore, the imaging trainee should recognize important considerations when selecting between PET and SPECT imaging and modes of stress (eg, exercise vs vasodilators). The trainee should understand the indications for viability assessment as well as the protocols involved in patient preparation and image acquisition. Indications for nuclear “hot spot” imaging include infection, inflammation, and infiltration, and trainees should understand patient preparation for each of these indications as well as the role of the technique for molecular imaging.

Beyond the patient-specific contraindications to stress testing, few contraindications for nuclear cardiology studies exist. The primary concern is radiation exposure, which should be carefully considered, especially in women of child-bearing age. Radionuclide imaging is usually not performed when hemodynamic instability is present or an expeditious evaluation is needed because of the prolonged acquisition time, although the risk vs the benefit of performing radionuclide imaging should always be carefully considered. Resting perfusion imaging may have a limited role in the assessment of myocardial risk in patients with possible acute coronary syndrome in patients presenting with chest pain but nondiagnostic ECG and negative cardiac injury serum markers.

Image Acquisition and Quality Assessment

Gaining expertise in both the acquisition and the quality assessment of cardiovascular imaging tests is essential for advanced cardiovascular imaging training. Image acquisition requires a sufficient volume of mentored hands-on-experience for each test as well as variability in the technical and clinical complexity of the cases that are acquired.

Training in image acquisition includes patient evaluation and preparation, administration of any necessary medications or contrast, troubleshooting intravenous access issues, selecting image acquisition settings, prioritizing the acquisition of data that is most important for a given clinical question or indication, troubleshooting any technical or medical issues that may arise during the study, communicating with the patient and other healthcare team members, image postprocessing, and study transferring and archiving. Trainees should be aware of when to index to height, weight, or body surface area, given the reliance of indexing to body size for accurate assessment of normal chamber size and valvular function. Blood pressure and heart rate should also be measured in the resting state because of the load-dependence of ventricular and valvular size and function as well as coronary blood flow. Trainees should gain advanced expertise in how changes in image acquisition settings may influence study quality and safety as well as the impact of various patient factors on image quality. Imagers should also learn how to identify and mitigate different artifacts that may arise during study acquisition. Learning how to acquire each imaging study is fundamental for understanding the types of patients who are most and least likely to benefit from the test, for optimizing image quality, and to understand the specific data that is provided by each test.

Although advanced imaging trainees should learn about the acquisition and quality assessment of each individual imaging modality, knowledge and skills are important across multimodality imaging. For example, the same medications are often used in stress CMR, stress echocardiography, nuclear myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), and stress CT. Understanding how to safely administer these agents is a skill that is common for all these tests. Additionally, similar imaging planes may be used across all studies (eg, 4 chamber, short axis). The skill of acquiring a coronary artery calcium scan may also be relevant when acquiring CT-attenuation corrected nuclear MPI studies.

The following modality-specific topics should be covered in the training program.

Cardiovascular CT: Trainees should have a comprehensive understanding of different CT acquisition and processing protocols including coronary artery calcium scans, CCTA, as well as dedicated protocols for structural or electrophysiological interventions (eg, transcatheter valves, atrial appendage occlusion, pulmonary vein isolation) and congenital conditions. Cardiovascular imagers should work closely with and be able to guide the CCT technologists in the acquisition of a high-quality CCT, including troubleshooting when needed. Trainees should understand the differences between scanner types, for example, single vs dual source and dual energy systems, or photon counting vs conventional energy-integrating detectors, as well as the types of electrocardiographic-synchronized acquisition modes, such as prospective electrocardiographic triggering vs retrospective electrocardiographic gating, high-pitch helical scanning, and single-beat acquisition when available. Trainees should be familiar with how different protocols and acquisition elements such as the choice of scan mode, type of electrocardiographic gating, tube potential, tube current, scan range, slice thickness, and pitch impact radiation exposure to the patient. Subsequently, trainees should be able to adjust those parameters while understanding how they impact image quality as well as radiation dose in accordance with the ALARA principle. Trainees should know the distinctive technical strengths and weaknesses of the specific CT system that is used, and based on patient-specific characteristics such as heart rate or body habitus, trainees should be able to decide on the best acquisition protocol. Trainees should be able to choose a contrast injection protocol based on the clinical needs for the scan. Trainees should be able to reconstruct images and know how to choose the cardiac field of view, slice thickness, slice overlap, kernel type, and edit the electrocardiographic synchronization to obtain optimal image quality. Trainees should know how to perform transaxial, multiplanar, maximum intensity projections, curved multiplanar, and volume-rendered reconstructions and know their respective strengths and limitations. In addition, trainees should know how to perform hands-on postprocessing on dedicated 3D workstations.

To properly set up the patient for the examination, trainees need to ensure that the heart is centered within the gantry and that there is a good electrocardiographic signal. Trainees also need to provide patients with instructions on the breath-holding protocol and optimize heart rate with medications as needed depending on scanner parameters. The trainee should optimize the timing of CCT acquisition relative to contrast injection as well as the total volume of contrast based on patient factors and scan parameters. Trainees should be well familiarized with the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography standardized CCT acquisition protocols to tailor protocols to answer the clinical question.24,25

Cardiovascular MR: Learning how to acquire CMR images is of utmost importance for trainees because a typical CMR examination involves the use of multiple different imaging techniques, each with many parameters that can be adjusted to optimize image quality. The specific techniques and the specific imaging views that are acquired can vary significantly based on the clinical indication for the CMR examination. Each technique is further adjusted based on multiple patient-specific factors such as body habitus, heart rhythm, and ability to cooperate with breath-holds. Cardiovascular imagers may often be guiding MR imaging technologists in the acquisition of a high-quality CMR examination, especially when troubleshooting is required. Thus, a trainee should understand every aspect of image acquisition including how to acquire a high-quality electrocardiographic signal, how to optimize a pulse sequence to achieve the optimal combination of signal-to-noise ratio, spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and acquisition time, how to acquire the appropriate imaging view, and how to troubleshoot imaging artifacts. To obtain high-quality images, trainees must have in-depth understanding of cardiac and respiratory gating while also using techniques to shorten acquisition time. Acquisition of high-quality CMR images also requires an understanding of hardware details such as the magnetic field strength and type of coils being used. Because of the potential effect of the magnetic field on ferromagnetic objects, trainees must have a thorough knowledge of how to safely perform a CMR examination, including remaining within appropriate specific absorption rate limits. Most CMR examinations require the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA), thus trainees should be able to choose the appropriate contrast protocol based on patient factors and the clinical indication. As part of patient management and protocol tailoring, trainees should learn which gated acquisition modes will be most appropriate if arrhythmias are present. Trainees should be familiar with strategies and best practices in dealing with anxiety in patients undergoing CMR, such as premedication with benzodiazepines, relaxation techniques, and communication during acquisition. Coaching and guiding patients when performing breath-holds are important skills that trainees should be able to perform to obtain high-quality acquisitions and train MR imaging technologists to keep image quality consistent. Trainees should be familiar with the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance standardized CMR acquisition protocols to tailor protocols to answer the clinical question as the minimum acceptable standard.26 The trainee should be familiar with postprocessing techniques for quantitative assessment of function and tissue characterization, including the strengths and limitations of these techniques.

Echocardiography: The echocardiography trainee should be able to perform a comprehensive, disease-specific TTE and TEE examination that may include the use of specialized modalities including strain imaging and 3D echocardiography with reconstruction. Trainees should understand the impact of physiologic factors on image acquisition and interpretation (eg, body habitus, respirophasic variability, heart rate variability) and appropriate manage these factors. Optimal imaging acquisition requires an understanding of when and how to change machine settings (eg, ultrasound frequency or sector depth/width), how to optimize the position of the imaging probe relative to the cardiac structures of interest, how to utilize maneuvers (eg, patient positioning, breath-holds) or adjunctive imaging modalities and agents (eg, agitated saline contrast, ultrasound enhancing agents, 3D echocardiography, intracardiac echocardiography), how to change image capture settings (eg, prospective or retrospective capture, cardiac beats, time capture) and how to use nonstandard or off-axis imaging to optimize image acquisition and make an accurate diagnosis.

Trainees should understand when a standard imaging protocol, advanced imaging protocol, or focused imaging protocol may be required.27,28 Given the direct contact of the trainee with the patient during image acquisition, trainees should be able to continuously monitor and manage patient symptoms related to image acquisition or medication administration. Because sedation is typically required to perform a TEE examination, trainees must determine the safety for oropharyngeal topical anesthesia and be able to determine the appropriate use of various types of sedation (eg, moderate sedation and analgesia, deep sedation and analgesia, general anesthesia). If more than moderate sedation is required, the trainee should consult an anesthesiologist to provide monitored anesthetic care. Trainees should be well familiarized with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines for image acquisition.27,28

Nuclear Cardiology: Trainees should have a comprehensive understanding of the different acquisition protocols for perfusion imaging and measurements of blood flow, functional imaging, metabolic imaging for both viability and inflammation and imaging for infiltrative diseases. Trainees should understand the differences between nuclear medicine camera designs, including SPECT and PET cameras. This understanding should extend to recognizing different methods for attenuation correction and hybrid camera design and function as well as required quality control procedures for the cameras. Trainees should be familiar with how different protocols, acquisition parameters, and counting efficiency of the nuclear medicine camera contributes to determining the administered radiopharmaceutical dose and radiation exposure to the patient. Trainees should be able to adjust protocols (eg, stress-only perfusion imaging, use of solid-state cameras) in accordance with the ALARA principle. Trainees should be able to reconstruct images and know how to choose reconstruction parameters to obtain optimal image quality. Trainees should know how to perform quantitative and semiquantitative measurements of myocardial perfusion and left ventricular function and understand how to assess the quality of those measurements.

Patient Safety and Management

Patient safety and the diagnosis and management of complications from cardiac imaging testing, such as complications from stress testing, contrast reactions, and sedation, are essential components of cardiac imaging care and training. Trainees should be able to quickly evaluate and diagnose these complications, triage appropriately, and administer treatment if needed. Management of adverse events should be part of advanced cardiac imager training and be reviewed with supervising faculty when they occur during imaging studies. Awareness of and adherence to institutional protocols and procedures are important, and where possible, should reflect societal recommendations or accreditation requirements such as the Intersocietal Accreditation Commission Standards & Guidelines29, 30, 31, 32 or The Joint Commission.33

Stress testing with any of the modalities can be associated with adverse events as described below. Trainees should be able to recognize and mitigate life-threatening and non–life-threatening events that may occur during these procedures. Stress testing with exercise or pharmacologic agents can cause acute coronary syndromes ranging from unstable angina to ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Hypotension, caused either by pharmacologic stress agents or severe myocardial ischemia, may also occur in the stress laboratory. Imaging trainees must be able to identify and manage these emergencies using the latest clinical practice guideline–directed therapies. Bradyarrhythmias, particularly high-degree heart block and asystole, may occur with adenosine, dipyridamole, and regadenoson. Tachyarrhythmias caused by stress agents (eg, dobutamine, ischemia, or primary arrhythmic cause) may also occur in the stress laboratory. Trainees should be trained to manage these arrhythmic events. Specific adverse effects, including bronchospasm and seizures, can occur with certain pharmacologic stress agents. The trainee should understand the risk factors for these complications as well as the treatments for them. Furthermore, trainees should know how to use basic and advanced cardiac life support protocols in emergency situations. Trainees should also be prepared to diagnose, manage, and monitor complications related to intravenous infiltration or contrast reaction.

Cardiovascular CT: CCT involves ionizing radiation; thus, trainees should be familiar with stochastic risks of radiation exposure to the patient, as well as techniques to adjust scan parameters to adhere to the ALARA principle. CCT also involves the intravenous administration of iodinated contrast that may be associated with contrast-induced nephropathy and contrast reactions. Trainees should be able to recognize the spectrum of contrast reactions, including anaphylaxis, and be able to administer appropriate treatment and premedication. Trainees should be able to recognize and treat hypotension and bradycardia resulting from administration of agents used for heart rate control or nitroglycerin for CCTA. Finally, intravenous infiltration and contrast extravasation can lead to the serious complication of compartment syndrome, which trainees should know to evaluate.

Cardiovascular MR: Trainees should be able to implement safety protocols in a CMR laboratory to manage patients with intracardiac and noncardiac devices (eg, monitoring, contraindications, protocol modifications, recommend alternative imaging modalities if contraindicated). Trainees should be able to assess safety of pharmacologic stress protocols and follow magnet safe advanced cardiac life support protocols and magnet safety protocols (eg, zoning, screening staff, quenching protocols). Trainees should be able to manage adverse reactions to GBCA and should have access to and be knowledgeable about the latest version of the American College of Radiology Manual on Contrast Media34 as well as the American College of Radiology Manual on MR Safety35 and recommendations by the American Board of Magnetic Resonance Safety. Trainees should also recognize acute hemodynamic instability during routine CMR scans, particularly anaphylactic shock from GBCA allergies, with the appropriate management and advanced cardiac life support protocols. Some trainees may have implanted devices that are not safe in the MR imaging environment. These individuals should follow appropriate MR image zoning safety guidelines.

Echocardiography: TTE presents minimal to no risk to the patient, other than slight discomfort from probe pressure and manipulation. In contrast, TEE carries potential risks as a semi-invasive procedure that requires sedation. The performance of TEE requires knowledge regarding patient selection, preparation, intraprocedural management and postprocedural care. Specifics for the advanced cardiovascular imaging trainee include ensuring that the patient is adequately consented and fasted, that the procedure can be performed safely with no contraindications to esophageal intubation (eg, known esophageal pathology), and that no allergies or contraindications exist to local anesthetic agents or intravenous sedation. Possible modes of injury for TEE include thermal injury, mechanical injury, and ischemic injury. Although significant complications of esophageal intubation or probe manipulation during routine TEE are rare (eg, esophageal perforation and injury, dental injury), recent studies suggest that prolonged esophageal intubation and manipulation (eg, during structural heart interventions) may result in more frequent esophageal injury. Trainees should be aware of patient and procedural characteristics that may increase the risk of this complication and how to mitigate esophageal injury. In all studies using topical or intravenous sedation, airway management and reversal of sedation are integral skills for all advanced cardiovascular imaging trainees.

Use of topical oral anesthetics and intravenous ultrasound-enhancing agents may have risks associated with their administration. The most concerning adverse risk of administering echocardiographic contrast agent is a rare, but potentially lethal anaphylactoid reaction (∼1:10,000). Trainees must be able to state the indications for contrast agents, as well as the incidence, signs, symptoms, and interventions of this life-threatening complication. Trainees should also be aware of minor reactions to echocardiographic contrast and how to manage these reactions to optimize patient comfort and safety. Finally, the risk of methemoglobinemia with topical anesthetic use is also a rare but potential lethal risk. Defined as an increase in oxidized hemoglobin resulting in a reduction of oxygen-carrying capacity, this complication occurs in 0.25% of TEE cases, and trainees should know the risk factors, recognize the signs and symptoms, and be able to initiate appropriate treatment of methemoglobinemia.

Nuclear Cardiology: Nuclear cardiology procedures use radiotracers for assessment of myocardial perfusion, function, blood flow, inflammation, and infiltration. Selection of the appropriate radiotracer and imaging protocol tailored to the patient’s characteristics and needs will minimize radiation exposure while still obtaining high-quality imaging in keeping with ALARA principles. The trainee should understand the roles of effective dose and target organ exposure in determining ALARA radiotracer exposure that provide images of acceptable quality. Advancement in camera technology and software protocols may also allow for reduction in the radioisotope dose while maintaining image quality, and the trainee should use such resources where available.

Trainees should also know the management of radiotracer spills that is a unique emergency to nuclear cardiology. Radiotracer spills are classified as major and minor spills and according to the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission is determined by the type of radiotracer involved as well as the millicurie amounts involved. Suggested management of radiation spills is outlined by the National Council on Radiation Protection and Measurements.36 Trainees should be able to recognize the spectrum of reactions to administered radiopharmaceuticals and pharmacologic agents, including anaphylaxis, and be able to administer appropriate treatment.

Occupational Risks and Management

Exposure to ionizing radiation is an inherent occupational health hazard in some types of cardiovascular imaging, including exposure while performing imaging studies in fluoroscopy suites. Proper education and training in radiation and MR imaging safety, radiation dose management, and protective equipment are essential to minimize risk to the imager as well as patients and healthcare personnel. Trainees should know additional precautions needed for cardiovascular imagers who are pregnant or of child-bearing age to minimize risk to the fetus. Radiation exposure levels should be regularly monitored and reported in all imagers exposed to ionizing radiation so that measures can be implemented to limit future exposure, always adhering to the ALARA principle. Imagers who wear protective gear to be shielded from ionizing radiation should be aware of methods to minimize orthopedic injury. Imaging and catheterization laboratory directors as well as trainees should be knowledgeable of the recent expert consensus statement on radiation safety in clinical cardiology.37,38

Imaging Study Interpretation and Integration

Gaining experience and developing expertise in the interpretation of different cardiovascular imaging tests are essential for advanced training in multimodality imaging. Competency in image interpretation requires not only sufficient volume but also diversity of clinical cases. A critical part of training in advanced cardiovascular imaging is learning how to integrate data from multiple tests when interpreting examination results and making patient management recommendations.

Training in image interpretation requires understanding of image acquisition skills as well as in-depth expertise in identifying and appropriately reporting normal, normal variant, and pathologic findings. Understanding how image acquisition settings may influence interpretation and how image display settings may alter findings are also important. It is essential to know how to combine quantitative and qualitative data (when relevant) when interpreting study results. Because of the growth of many different quantitative techniques (eg, plaque quantification in cardiac CT, parametric mapping in cardiovascular MR, quantitative techniques for valvular assessment on echocardiography, myocardial blood flow measurements in PET), imagers should learn how to perform appropriate quality control to ensure that the performance of quantitative measures are valid and accurate. Trainees should become familiar with postprocessing software for CCT and CMR studies that are integrated into electrophysiology procedures for mapping and ablation guidance. Finally, training in image interpretation also includes the skill to write accurate, concise, and patient-centered reports that can be understood by clinicians, and that exhibit thorough knowledge of the clinical question and how the test results may impact subsequent patient care.

Similarly, image interpretation skills for multimodality imagers have advantageous synergies and learning how to integrate data from different techniques is essential. For example, learning how to interpret nuclear MPIs may aid trainees in reading stress CMRs. In-depth training in both coronary anatomy and physiology will enable trainees to integrate data from CCTA and nuclear MPI or CMR and may lead to an understanding of which coronary lesions are most likely resulting in ischemia. Diagnosing various other noncoronary pathologies—including valvular heart disease, cardiac masses, infiltrative cardiomyopathies—will also be enhanced by learning how to interpret data from all available imaging modalities as well as when and how such data can be complementary. In addition, advanced imagers should know the basic applications, advantages, and limitations of artificial intelligence and machine learning in cardiac imaging. Applications include but are not limited to examination protocoling, image acquisition, postprocessing, and examination interpretation.

Trainees should be familiar with the imaging findings of less common conditions involving the cardiovascular system. These include complex congenital heart and vascular defects (both repaired and unrepaired), acquired and genetic cardiomyopathies, and the various causes of cardiac masses. Although programs should have a repository of teaching cases that includes less common clinical conditions, trainees, with the guidance of their faculty mentors, would be encouraged to seek out, depending on their clinical interest and needs, additional training through conferences and outside rotations at centers with high case volume and level of complexity, to provide the necessary exposure to such conditions and procedures. Competency in interpreting complex and postoperative CHD may likely require additional advanced training at a site with a high volume of complex CHD.

The following modality-specific topics should be covered in the training program.

Cardiovascular CT: Proficiency and confidence in interpreting coronary artery disease on CT images requires extensive mentored reading of cases with a range of image quality and clinical findings, including case-by-case correlation with invasive angiography and stress testing. When interpreting CCTAs, trainees should be familiar with the CAD-RADS (Coronary Artery Disease – Reporting and Data System) 2.0 classification39 or current reporting guidelines that incorporate the degree of coronary artery stenosis and plaque burden as well as when to include modifiers such as assessment of lesion-specific ischemia using CT-FFR, high-risk plaque, stents, or grafts. Trainees should be familiar with the recommendations for patient management based on this classification system. Trainees should also have the knowledge of parameters required for structural CCT interpretation in situations such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement, transcatheter mitral valve replacement, left atrial appendage occlusion device placement, prosthetic valve dysfunction, and evaluation of pulmonary veins as well as for congenital cases.

Cardiovascular MR: In addition to image acquisition, trainees must know how to integrate the findings from all different imaging techniques and views that were obtained to interpret the cardiovascular MR examination. An important aspect of cardiovascular MR image interpretation is the ability to perform multiple forms of quantification ranging from chamber size quantification, flow quantification, myocardial perfusion, strain, and myocardial tissue characterization including, for example, quantification of the burden of late gadolinium enhancement, native myocardial T1 times, T2 times, T2∗ times, and extracellular volume fraction. Additionally, trainees will be expected to understand basic principles of maximum intensity projection and multiplanar reconstruction to aid in image analysis. Trainees should be familiar with extracardiac contrast-enhanced and noncontrasted pulse sequences for vascular anatomy, strengths and weaknesses of various pulse sequences, and associated imaging artifacts.

Echocardiography: Competency in interpreting echocardiograms requires extensive mentored reading of cases with a range of image quality and cardiovascular pathology. The interpretation of these various pathologies may require the integration of different echocardiographic modalities—for example, strain imaging for structural myocardial changes or 3D imaging for chamber volumes and valvular pathology. The trainee should be familiar with postprocessing of images to acquire additional information relevant to the clinical question and interpretation of findings (eg, atrial or ventricular strain or valvular morphology). An important training tool to gain interpretation competency involves the direct acquisition of echocardiographic images by trainees for a broad range of cardiovascular pathologies and in varied clinical settings. In addition, trainees should be able to integrate the clinical presentation and findings from different imaging modalities into the interpretation of the echocardiogram.

Advanced tools such as strain imaging and 3D assessment of left ventricular ejection fraction use artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms that require the echocardiographer to perform manual editing. Advanced artificial intelligence software can also assist with image acquisition, automate measurements, diagnose certain diseases, and generate reports. Whereas great potential exists for improving the quality of image acquisition, reducing the variability of measurements, and improving the efficiency of workflow, artificial intelligence programs should be used to augment trainee attainment of competence and critical thinking. In addition, the limitations of artificial intelligence algorithms and errors in digital standardized reporting should be understood, and trainees should be able to independently measure these parameters and confirm the accuracy of the artificial intelligence algorithm.

Trainees should have access to TTE and TEE images for interpretation from a broad range of common and rare cardiovascular conditions. This may include, but is not limited to, complex CHD, cardiomyopathies such as amyloidosis and sarcoidosis, and cardiac masses. Given the broad range of cardiovascular and other medical specialties using echocardiography, training may occur outside the typical echocardiography laboratory. These additional training opportunities could, for instance, include either on- or off-site use of ACHD clinics, advanced heart failure clinics, cardio-oncology clinics, surgical clinics, the emergency department, or the operating room. Additional training with simulated cases, case presentations, and virtual learning may be used to increase the breadth and depth of exposure to less common clinical scenarios or disease processes.

Nuclear Cardiology: Competency in the interpretation of MPI requires extensive mentored reading of cases with a range of image quality and disease severity. Optimally, this training should include exposure to SPECT MPI with Tc-99m-based perfusion agents and Tl-201 as well as PET MPI with Rb-82, N-13 ammonia, and other perfusion imaging and blood flow radiotracers. Trainees should also be familiar with the applications of quantitative MPI (SPECT and PET) in evaluating obstructive and nonobstructive coronary artery disease as well as microvascular coronary artery disease. Likewise, trainees should be familiar with the growing application of quantitative myocardial blood flow measurements with PET in evaluating cardiac allograft vasculopathy. When available, case-by-case correlation with CCTA and invasive angiography should be performed. In addition, the trainee should integrate findings with the clinical presentation as well as the results of other imaging modalities when generating a final report. With the increasing role of using Tc-99m-labeled PYP and related bone-avid imaging agents, trainees should understand the indications for use of these agents in the detection of cardiac transthyretin amyloid.

Trainees should be familiar with imaging protocols and findings related to conditions beyond coronary artery disease. Trainees should be experienced with both the planar and SPECT methods of gated blood pool analysis used to determine ventricular function, volumes, and morphology. In addition, the applications for the use of nuclear cardiology are rapidly expanding. These applications include the use of F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT in evaluating patients with suspected or known inflammatory cardiomyopathies (eg, sarcoidosis, myocarditis), infective endocarditis (eg, prosthetic valve endocarditis, cardiac device infection), and large vessel vasculitis. For each of these conditions, trainees should be familiar with study interpretation and reporting of imaging findings, and integration of imaging findings into patient management. In infective endocarditis, trainees should be able to differentiate imaging findings associated with inflammation vs infection and be familiar with the complementary role of radionuclide white blood cell SPECT/CT imaging. Trainees should also be familiar with integrating FDG PET/CT with other imaging modalities (echocardiography, CT, and CMR) in evaluating patients with cardiac masses, including the potential role of other radiotracers (eg, 68 Ga-dotatate for cardiac involvement of neuroendocrine tumors).

Research and Scholarly Activity

A strong foundation of scholarly activity for all areas of cardiovascular imaging is imperative during training to prepare cardiovascular imagers for a lifetime of learning and evidence-based practice. The trainee is expected to work with faculty in clinical, imaging science, and/or translational research. Formal training in research methodology, including biostatistics, clinical trial design, research ethics, and grant writing should be available for fellows who plan to be involved in research activities. Research should lead to presentations at local, regional, or national meetings, publication in peer-reviewed scientific journals, and/or grant support. Alternatively, trainees may elect to participate in quality improvement and educational projects with the expectation that these activities will lead to scholarly output. All trainees should develop and maintain habits of self-learning, by both conducting regular case review and journal review sessions and attending regional and national scientific meetings. Advanced training programs are expected to evaluate progress in scholarly development. Regularly scheduled mentoring meetings and review of presentations, manuscripts, and/or other scholarly activities should be conducted to provide feedback and implement action plans to ensure that trainees achieve predefined goals.

Training Requirements

Competency Development and Evaluation

Training requirements in advanced cardiovascular imaging address the 6 general competencies promulgated by the ACGME and endorsed by the ABIM. These competency domains are: Medical Knowledge, Patient Care and Procedural Skills, Practice-Based Learning and Improvement, Systems-Based Practice, Interpersonal and Communication Skills, and Professionalism. The ACC has used this structure to define and depict the components of the clinical competencies for cardiovascular disease training. The curricular milestones for each competency domain also provide a developmental roadmap for fellows as they progress through training and a framework for programs to improve educational and assessment processes. The ACC has adopted this format for its competency and training statements, career milestones, lifelong learning, and educational programs.

Procedural and Technical Experience

Methods to Determine Procedural Numbers

The recommended number of procedures performed and interpreted by trainees under faculty supervision has been developed based on the experience and opinions of the members of the writing committee and previously published training statements and policies of the ACGME, Certification Board of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography, Certification Board of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance, Certification Board of Nuclear Cardiology, and the National Board of Echocardiography.40, 41, 42, 43 In addition, the writing committee surveyed both cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular imaging program directors for additional insight into procedural volumes. Of 262 directors of ABIM-recognized cardiovascular disease training programs surveyed, 35 responded; of 59 directors of cardiovascular imaging training programs surveyed, 18 responded.

Procedural Number Guidance

Training in advanced cardiovascular imaging implies a high level of medical knowledge and requires competency in all 4 imaging modalities. A competency-based approach to training relies on various tools to assess progress toward, and ultimate achievement of, each component of competency.

For a procedural area, such as cardiovascular imaging, achieving this level of competency depends on both the quality and volume of training. Creating a high-quality training experience involves didactic instruction, self-directed learning, mentoring, case-based education, and exposure to a wide range of pathologies. Volume, a less important but more easily quantified metric, can be measured by the number and diversity of studies to which trainees are exposed. As such, a prescribed volume of studies is necessary, but volume alone is an insufficient metric to guarantee satisfactory achievement of full competence in each specific area. Competence in advanced cardiovascular imaging is ultimately based on successful completion of all training requirements and favorable evaluation by the program director and clinical competency committee.

The procedural volumes suggested in this document were determined to be the minimum numbers sufficient to provide trainees with exposure to a variety and spectrum of complex clinical case material and to give supervising faculty sufficient opportunity to evaluate the competency developed by each trainee. The number of procedures that should be performed to achieve competence are intended as general guidance, realizing that true competency is assessed by proficiency and outcomes and may require more than the minimum numbers outlined in Table 3. Flexibility is inherent to this concept, and all programs should establish milestones for the acquisition of various competencies by trainees during the course of fellowship training. In addition, absolute mastery of all aspects of cardiovascular imaging is unlikely to be achieved based on the fellowship experience alone. For common and straightforward procedures, mastery can occur, but for very complex or infrequently performed procedures, lower levels of proficiency are anticipated for new graduates. Importantly, training in a particular modality cannot be completely virtual or outsourced, and training must occur in a facility that meets the program standards outlined in Section 2.2.

Notably, direct mentorship and supervision by faculty is required for training purposes. Direct involvement in cases by trainees is presumed for training unless otherwise specified in the table. Training numbers acquired during dedicated cardiac imaging rotations documented during Cardiovascular Disease Fellowship can count toward the procedural number guidance in this document as well as research studies if they entail full interpretation and mentorship. If board certification in a particular modality occurs before advanced cardiovascular imaging fellowship, it does not negate the need to be trained and to ensure competency in a particular modality during advanced fellowship training (eg, a fellow who is board certified in echocardiography has opportunity to mature these skills and add procedural competencies not previously obtained such as structural echocardiography). Procedural volume requirements in Advanced Cardiovascular Imaging Fellowship must be met in the context of cross correlating and integrating evaluations of patients whenever possible.

Although knowledge and expertise in each imaging modality are expected of all cardiovascular imagers, a key concept during training is the integration and correlation of results with the clinical presentation and other cardiovascular imaging data (Figure 1, Table 4). This approach focuses on the optimal use of multiple cardiovascular imaging modalities to obtain information about a suspected disease or pathologic process to aid with diagnosis, prognosis, and response to treatment. Knowledge of the clinical question, nonimaging data, and review of prior imaging are essential. These factors in combination with a knowledge of patient-specific factors that may affect imaging choice as well as institutional expertise in a particular imaging modality will result in a more efficient and cost-effective use of imaging to assist with decision-making and patient care. The cardiovascular imager should be able to provide comprehensive diagnostic and prognostic information within institutional expertise to guide referring clinicians on further management. Furthermore, by understanding each modality’s strengths and weaknesses, the imager should be able to provide a more efficient and cost-effective evaluation. Table 4 highlights several recommendations demonstrating the importance of cross correlation for disease diagnosis and management. The program should include exposure to such cases during training, while recognizing that correlation of patient data with imaging results should be an important aspect of all image interpretation. Table 4 is not a comprehensive overview of all types of cross-modality correlations. Myocardial Disease

Myocardial diseases can be broadly differentiated into 2 groups: ischemic and nonischemic disease. The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, risk and benefit, and incremental value in the evaluation of each disease state.

The evaluation of ischemic heart disease (see Section 4.2.6.) requires assessment of both myocardial ischemia and extent of viability. Several methods can assess myocardial infarction, identifying wall motion and/or perfusion abnormalities and adverse remodeling that correspond to disease in epicardial coronary artery distributions. Echocardiography is a commonly ordered test to evaluate structure, function, and potential postinfarct complications. CMR and FDG PET will further delineate myocardial infarct severity and viability. CMR can additionally provide localization and characterization of postinfarct intracardiac thrombus or reveal infarct scar in patients with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA).

Nonischemic myocardial diseases are generally evaluated after ischemic disease has been ruled out or in tandem. Echocardiography is commonly the first test ordered in the evaluation of nonischemic myocardial disease. Certain morphological features provide post-test probability for diagnoses to the cardiovascular imager. CMR is routinely obtained for myocardial tissue characterization and differentiating types of nonischemic cardiomyopathies. Additionally, CMR provides further risk-stratification dependent on institutional expertise, including parametric mapping or late gadolinium enhancement quantification. PET is performed usually to evaluate for active cardiac sarcoidosis.44 Tc-99m pyrophosphate (and the other bone imaging agents, Tc-99m HMDP and Tc-99m DPD) nuclear scintigraphy can be considered in the evaluation of transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis.

Valvular Heart Disease

The cardiovascular imager should understand the indications, methods, strengths, and limitations of all imaging modalities in the anatomic and functional evaluation of acute and chronic valvular heart disease. This includes an understanding of key normal and pathologic findings and the ability to integrate information from multiple modalities to make the most accurate diagnosis.45

TTE is generally considered the first-line imaging modality for the diagnosis and follow-up of patients with valvular heart disease because it is readily available, noninvasive, and free from ionizing radiation while providing detailed anatomic and functional information. In the setting of discordant TTE findings and clinical symptoms (eg, moderate disease severity with symptoms, severe disease without symptoms), stress echocardiography may be useful in confirming either disease severity or the presence of symptoms. In the setting of discordant TTE measures of disease severity (eg, low-flow, low-gradient, severe aortic stenosis) dobutamine stress echocardiography may be used in certain clinical scenarios. If TTE findings are nondiagnostic, TEE may provide diagnostic quantification of disease severity as well as morphologic assessment of valve anatomy.46 In both modalities, the cardiovascular imager should understand how to apply 2D, 3D, and Doppler based techniques to perform a comprehensive evaluation of valvular anatomy and function. Cardiovascular imagers with special competence in interventional echocardiography should also understand anatomic criteria involved in patient selection for surgical and transcatheter interventions, device selection and sizing, and imaging techniques to perform guidance for surgical or transcatheter repair and replacement procedures.47

CCT is also an important imaging modality for the evaluation of valvular heart disease because it can provide higher spatial resolution for anatomic evaluation of the valve complex and surrounding cardiac structures. This is particularly relevant for measurement of valve complex dimensions, evaluating the risk of intervention-related anatomic complications, and evaluating the degree and pattern of calcification as well as the diagnosis of prosthetic valve structural changes. CMR provides excellent functional cardiac data, including additional techniques to quantify valvular lesions, which are particularly helpful in the setting of multivalvular heart disease or when disease severity by echocardiography is unclear. Accurate evaluation of cardiac chamber size and function to better understand the consequence of valvular disease and potential risk of interventions is also possible with CMR.

The most common, albeit infrequent, use of nuclear cardiac imaging in patients with valvular heart disease is the assessment of coronary artery disease in patients being considered for valve replacement. In addition, in cases where there is a question of whether native or prosthetic valves are affected by infective endocarditis, FDG PET may provide important information concerning valvular pathology.

Coronary Artery Disease

CCT, CMR, nuclear MPI, and echocardiography all have a role in the assessment of coronary artery disease. The cardiovascular imager should know the indications and AUC for the use of imaging for assessing patients with known or suspected coronary artery disease.23 They should also recognize clinical presentations where a patient is more likely to benefit from direct referral for invasive angiography rather than an imaging test.

Imagers should know the strengths and limitations of each imaging test for coronary artery disease. The cardiovascular imager should understand which test may be preferable for different scenarios depending on the clinical question at hand, patient characteristics, as well as results of prior testing.

The ACC/AHA 2021 chest pain guideline provides recommendations on preferred tests for different patients.48 For patients with no known CAD, an anatomical or functional testing approach may be adopted. CCTA may be favored in patients who are <65 years of age and not on optimal preventive therapies, because it may identify nonobstructive plaque burden and thus influence the use of preventive therapies. Conversely, in patients with higher likelihood of ischemia (eg, high burden of plaque on prior chest CT, older age), stress testing may be favored. If a patient has had a previously inconclusive or nondiagnostic stress test, a CCTA should be considered. However, if a patient had a previous inconclusive CCTA, a stress test should be considered. In patients with intermediate stenosis on CCTA and persistent symptoms, CT-FFR or stress testing can be considered. Various strengths and limitations correspond to the different types of stress tests, and they should not always be considered as substitutes. In general, PET MPI is preferred over SPECT MPI because it is more accurate and can also be used to evaluate for conditions like ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries and coronary microvascular dysfunction. CMR may be especially useful in patients who have cardiomyopathy or when there is a need to identify scar burden. Stress echocardiography may be useful when there are also questions regarding valvular assessment during exercise (eg, exercise induced valvular dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension).

Additionally, the cardiovascular imager should be able to integrate information from different imaging modalities for making patient management recommendations. When needed, imagers should also have expertise in helping decide on the need and type of further testing.

Stress Testing

The cardiovascular imager who performs or oversees stress testing in conjunction with imaging should have comprehensive knowledge of the indications, contraindications, risks, and limitations of exercise and pharmacologic stress testing. In addition, the imager should know modality-specific protocols and nuanced technical specifications for performing, interpreting, and reporting the stress test in conjunction with individual imaging modalities.

Each type of stress imaging test has its unique advantages and limitations. The choice between the imaging modalities, such as stress echocardiography, stress SPECT, stress PET, or stress CMR, depends on several factors, including test performance for the specific indication, specific patient characteristics, indications, local expertise, local access and availability, cost and insurance coverage, and patient preference.48 Furthermore, the choice between exercise vs pharmacologic stress testing is also influenced by the patient’s symptoms and clinical history, specific imaging modality, and local availability.

In appropriate patients with suspected or known coronary artery disease with the ability to exercise but who require imaging,21 the exercise treadmill test is combined with either echocardiography or SPECT MPI and is widely available. When patients are unable to exercise or in other appropriate clinical scenarios, pharmacologic stress is performed in conjunction with nuclear imaging or echocardiography. For pharmacologic stress SPECT and stress PET, the vasodilator stress agents, adenosine, dipyridamole, and regadenoson, are first-line agents, and may be combined with low-level exercise with the appropriate radiotracer. Dobutamine stress may also be used for pharmacologic stress nuclear imaging if the aforementioned vasodilator stress agents cannot be used. For pharmacologic stress echocardiography, dobutamine is most often used in the United States. For pharmacologic stress CMR, either vasodilators or the inodilator dobutamine may be used, depending on the indication for the test.

For specific noncoronary artery indications, such as assessment of the severity of valvular lesions and exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension, upright or supine bicycle exercise may be performed to facilitate acquisition of echocardiographic images. Dobutamine echocardiography may also be used to assess low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis or viability.

Overall, the cardiovascular imager involved in performing, supervising, or interpreting these stress imaging tests should have extensive knowledge of the nuances of exercise and pharmacologic stress tests, imaging-specific stress testing protocols, and individual patient characteristics to ensure the optimal performance and interpretation of the stress imaging test.

Pericardial Disease

The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, risks, and technical limitations of each imaging modality for the diagnosis and hemodynamic assessment of all forms of acute and chronic pericardial disease.49 Echocardiography is the most common first-line test for assessment of pericardial disease and comprehensive hemodynamic assessment. CMR can be obtained when the pericardium is not well visualized by echocardiography and/or if tissue characterization is required to assess inflammation and/or fibrosis, and it can also assess for constrictive physiology with dedicated maneuvers. CCT can also be obtained when the pericardium is not well visualized by echocardiography and is particularly useful for assessment of calcification of the pericardium. Both CMR and CCT can be considered for further visualization and tissue characterization of the pericardial space and pericardial masses, and nuclear imaging with FDG PET may add additional value in the assessment of pericardial masses with concern for malignancy.

In addition, the cardiovascular imager should demonstrate the ability to integrate the information from different imaging modalities into the clinical assessment of pericardial disease. This is especially important when information obtained from 1 imaging method is limited or the findings conflict with the clinical presentation.

Cardiac Masses

The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, risks, and technical limitations of each imaging modality for the diagnosis of cardiac masses. Multimodality imaging can often help when assessing if a mass is benign or malignant as well as evaluate for potential involvement of different cardiac structures. TTE is the most used testing for the assessment of cardiac masses. A comprehensive echocardiographic examination should be able to define the location, size, attachment, and motion of a cardiac mass. CMR is the preferred imaging modality used for tissue characterization of the cardiac mass. CMR can also delineate the relationship with cardiac and extracardiac structures, the vascularity within the mass and is the ideal method to differentiate suspected thrombus from cardiac tumor.15 CCT is an alternative method for further evaluation of cardiac masses, due to its excellent spatial resolution, ample field of view, and characterization of calcium and perfusion capabilities. CCT can be the key imaging modality for cardiac masses that might involve directly or abut coronary arteries, especially to define better surgical approaches.21 FDG PET may add additional value in the evaluation of suspected malignant cardiac masses by assessment of cardiac tumor metabolic activity.21 The cardiovascular imager should demonstrate the ability to cross correlate the information from various imaging modalities to evaluate the presence and nature of a suspected cardiac mass and particularly be able to identify features that suggest a malignant etiology.

Diseases of the Great Vessels

The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, risks, strength, and technical limitations of each imaging modality in the diagnosis and hemodynamic assessment of great vessel diseases. The cardiovascular imagers should also be able to tailor imaging protocols to answer specific questions related to great thoracic vessels pathology, both acute and chronic.50,51

CT angiography is the most common first-line test for assessment of suspected acute great vessels pathologies, such as pulmonary embolism or acute aortic syndrome. This modality could also be used for a follow-up of Type B aortic dissection or intramural hematoma. Chronic pathologies, such as aneurysm, large vessel vasculitis, and its complications, could also be assessed with CT angiography. CT venography might be a useful modality for assessment of large vein thrombosis. Electrocardiographic-gated CT angiography of the chest (or alternatively high-pitch nongated CT) is recommended whenever feasible for assessment of thoracic aorta and might be useful for assessment of pulmonary arteries.

MR angiography could be used for follow-up of known large thoracic vessels pathology, especially in young patients. It could also assist in tissue characterization of the vessel wall, especially in suspected inflammatory processes, to assess degree of inflammation, fibrosis, aneurysmatic dilatation, or stricture. The emerging role of MR in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension expects familiarity of cardiovascular imagers with dedicated MR protocols.

Both TTE and TEE also allow assessment of aortic pathology and its hemodynamic consequences such as in aortic valve regurgitation or aortic dilatation. In addition to thoracic aorta evaluation, echocardiography can also add additional information on aortic valve and pericardial involvement in acute settings, such as in suspected acute aortic syndrome in unstable patients. Although TTE image quality is highly dependent on operator and acoustic windows, TEE is typically diagnostic of aortic pathology within the imaging planes of this modality. TTE and TEE may also be used to assess the right ventricular remodeling associated with acute or chronic pulmonary embolism. Nuclear imaging with FDG PET may contribute to the assessment of suspected inflammatory processes, malignancy, and occasionally assessing the severity of inflammation associated with atherosclerotic disease. Ventilation/perfusion scans may be used to detect the location and extent of pulmonary embolus when CT angiography is not feasible.

In addition, the cardiovascular imager should demonstrate the ability to integrate the information from different imaging modalities into the clinical assessment of great thoracic vessel pathology and suggest what the best next imaging modality might be, if one is indicated. This is especially important when information obtained from 1 imaging method is limited or the findings conflict with the clinical presentation.

Atria, Systemic, and Pulmonary Venous Anatomy and Physiology

The cardiovascular imager should know the normal and variant anatomy, physiology, and the indications for, risks, and technical limitations of each imaging modality in the morphologic and hemodynamic assessment of the right and left atrium, the systemic venous return, and the pulmonary veins.

CCT or CMR are commonly used for visualizing the atria and pulmonary veins before and after transcatheter procedures, including, but not limited to, pulmonary vein isolation procedures, left atrial appendage exclusion procedures, or atrial septal defect occlusion procedures. Echocardiography is a common test and can assess hemodynamics, morphologic features and function of the atria and pulmonary veins, and systemic venous return.

In addition, the cardiovascular imager should demonstrate the ability to integrate the information from different imaging modalities into the clinical assessment of the pulmonary and systemic venous return and the assessment of the atria. This is especially important when information obtained from 1 imaging method is inconclusive or limited or the findings conflict with the clinical presentation.

Adult Congenital Heart Disease

The advanced cardiovascular imager should know the strengths and weaknesses of each imaging modality as they pertain to the diagnostic evaluation of adult patients with CHD. These imagers should know the indications, contraindications, and technical limitations of each modality in the diagnosis, anatomic evaluation, and physiologic correlation for simple, moderate, and complex ACHD.52 They must consider implications of lifelong follow-up imaging for these young patients and limit ionizing radiation exposure when possible. They should know appropriate modality selection during pregnancy and the associated risks to both the mother and fetus. Trainees should know how to acquire, process, interpret, and report imaging findings in simple and complex ACHD. They should consult with dedicated ACHD cardiovascular imagers for challenging cases, complex ACHD, or when a second opinion is sought.

Echocardiography serves as the initial screening modality for the evaluation of ACHD. Trainees should be familiar with pediatric CHD echocardiographic views, including sweeps and unique subcostal imaging planes. 3D echocardiography and Doppler can quantify chamber volumes and flow and thus determine intracardiac shunt size, ventricular or valvular dysfunction. CMR should be the preferred cross-sectional imaging modality for adult patients with CHD, who require serial, lifelong evaluations to limit ionizing radiation exposure. Trainees should know and follow clinical practice guideline–endorsed CMR imaging protocols for adult patients with CHD, when available.22,53 CMR is the reference standard for quantification of pulmonary regurgitation, biventricular volumetric and functional quantification, and has unique capability to provide tissue characterization to aid in prognostication of long-term cardiovascular risk. CMR additionally serves as the primary modality for the quantification of intracardiac shunting and its hemodynamic consequences. CCT can be obtained for evaluation of complications related to intracardiac devices, including device leads, valves, and stents. CCT offers superior spatial resolution and speed of acquisition that is well-suited for inpatient evaluations, identification of coronary anomalies, and anomalous pulmonary venous connections. Retrospectively gated CCT may also be useful in the quantification of ventricular volumes and function when CMR is contraindicated. Trainees should be familiar with radiation saving techniques in this population. FDG PET may be useful in the diagnosis of infective endocarditis, particularly with indeterminate TEE images of interest (eg, valved conduits with heavy calcification).

Additionally, trainees should demonstrate the ability to integrate findings across various imaging modalities into the clinical assessment of adult patients with CHD. Correlation with prior imaging studies is often necessary to accurately derive conclusions to guide patient care.

Critical Care Management

The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, applications, and limitations of all 4 imaging modalities in the management of patients who are critically ill. TTE and TEE are the most frequently used imaging modalities to diagnose and guide treatment of pathologic conditions in these patients given its portability, broad range of structural and hemodynamic information, and relative safety. The cardiovascular imager should be able to acquire, interpret, and apply the echocardiographic findings to the patient’s care thereby understanding the implications of those findings. The cardiovascular imager should also be familiar with point-of-care ultrasound and its most common applications: echocardiography, lung, abdominal, and vascular imaging. Requirements for proficiency in point-of-care ultrasound have been outlined by numerous societies and can be incorporated by training programs as needed, building on standard programmatic requirements to meet proficiency goals.54,55

Additionally, the cardiovascular imager should be able to use all forms of ultrasonography for procedural guidance and troubleshooting, including the insertion and management of ventricular assist devices, and the recognition of common procedural complications. Moreover, trainees should demonstrate the ability to integrate this information with other imaging results and the clinical assessment of patients who are acutely ill. This is especially important when information obtained from imaging is limited or conflicts with the clinical data.

Patient Populations With Special Imaging Considerations

Cardio-Oncology

The cardiovascular imager should have familiarity with the common cardiovascular complications seen in patients with cancer and survivors, including the high incident rate of cardiovascular complications related to their cancer (eg, primary and secondary cardiac tumors, pericardial effusion) and toxicities related to antineoplastic treatments (eg, left ventricular systolic dysfunction, myocarditis, valve disease).56 Having this fundamental knowledge is essential for guiding clinical decision-making in selecting the most appropriate imaging modality for assessing common clinical presentations in these patients. Furthermore, trainees should demonstrate the ability to integrate imaging information from different modalities to aid in the clinical assessment of patients with cancer.

TTE with global longitudinal strain is the first-line imaging modality to assess cardiac function before, during, and after cancer therapy, including surveillance for cardiotoxicity in patients undergoing treatment with anthracyclines and HER-2 inhibitors (eg, trastuzumab) as well as additional potentially cardiotoxic cancer therapies.57 Global longitudinal strain has been shown to hold value for detection of subclinical left ventricular dysfunction. Therefore, it is recommended to be used in conjunction with left ventricular ejection fraction for cardiotoxicity monitoring, and limited data exist that indicate it may be helpful in identifying patients who would benefit from cardioprotective treatment.

Radionuclide gated blood pool imaging using planar equilibrium radionuclide angiography (ERNA) was the first method to be used systematically to monitor for chemotherapy-associated cardiomyopathy and has a low variability for measurements of left ventricular function. Radionuclide assessment of cardiotoxicity using SPECT ERNA allows for evaluation of both left and right ventricular function. Because echocardiograms are commonly used to assess left ventricular function, ERNA and SPECT ERNA are typically reserved for special circumstances where other imaging methods are not possible or appropriate.57 In addition, SPECT MPI can be used for the detection of myocardial ischemia in cancer patients receiving chemotherapeutic agents that might induce myocardial ischemia.

Although not typically used as a first-line imaging method, CMR imaging is an essential tool in certain situations in cardio-oncology. For example, CMR is instrumental in assessing cases of immune checkpoint inhibitor-related myocarditis57 and, although data remain limited about the sensitivity of the test in this specific disease state, a positive study is very helpful in facilitating the diagnosis. CMR is an important modality in the evaluation of cardiac amyloidosis for both light chain and transthyretin subtypes while a Tc-99m pyrophosphate (or Tc-99m HMDP or Tc-99m DPD) SPECT scan is more specific for detecting transthyretin amyloidosis.58

Because cancer patients and survivors are more susceptible to vascular complications, coagulation disorders, and thrombocytopenia, less-invasive diagnostic approaches can be particularly beneficial for them if coronary artery assessment is needed. CCTA may be considered for evaluating patients with acute or stable chest pain and low to intermediate risk. When presenting with cardiomyopathy of unclear cause, CCTA is helpful in evaluating obstructive CAD as a potential cause.59 In addition, chest pain may also occur in patients with nonobstructive CAD who develop spasm due to various therapies. Cardio-oncology patients often have noncardiac chest CT scans as part of their cancer staging and reviewing these scans for the presence of coronary calcium can assist clinicians in cardiovascular disease risk assessment and selecting an appropriate test for clinical symptoms.59,60 Integrating image findings is important from CCT, CMR, echocardiography, and nuclear imaging with clinical, electrocardiographic, and anticancer therapy to determine the diagnosis and nature of anticancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity.

Cardio-Obstetrics

Cardiovascular imaging is fundamental to the management of pregnant women with known or suspected cardiovascular disease. The multimodality cardiovascular imager should know the indications, contraindications, and maternal-fetal risks for each cardiovascular imaging modality during pregnancy and lactation.

TTE is the first-line imaging modality to diagnose cardiovascular disease in the pregnant patient. TEE can be performed during pregnancy, but endotracheal intubation is frequently recommended in later stages of pregnancy due to the increased risk of aspiration. The cardiovascular imager should understand the potential risks to the fetus when using agitated saline and echocardiographic contrast agents in the pregnant patient and be able to weigh the clinical benefit for maternal care vs potential fetal harm when considering these agents as well as intravenous medications for moderate sedation.

CMR is considered safe during pregnancy. However, imaging with GBCAs should be avoided unless the benefits significantly outweigh the possible fetal risks. In general, cardiac imaging modalities using ionizing radiation, such as CT or nuclear cardiology, should be avoided in pregnancy to minimize the risk of childhood cancers due to fetal exposure. However, if required for maternal health, protocols requiring ionizing radiation should be adapted to minimize radiation exposure consistent with the ALARA principle while maintaining diagnostic image quality. For any cardiac imaging modality, both maternal and fetal risks must be carefully considered and shared decision-making with the patient, cardiovascular imager, and cardio-obstetrics team is essential for optimal outcomes.61

The cardiovascular imager should know which contrast agents are secreted into breast milk to advise appropriate testing in lactating patients. The water solubility of GBCAs and iodinated contrast limits the excretion into breast milk, and therefore, breastfeeding can be continued without interruption after administration of these agents. Radionuclide compounds are excreted into breast milk in varying degrees and for differing periods of time depending on the physical and biological half-lives of the radiotracer being used. The cardiovascular imager should provide individualized consultation when these compounds are used in lactating women. Additionally, it is recommended that any patient undergoing nuclear cardiology imaging with certain radiotracers avoid proximity to pregnant women, infants, and young children for at least 12 to 24 hours after the nuclear medicine scan.62

Cardio-Rheumatology

Cardio-rheumatology is an emerging field that focuses on the intersection between manifestations of cardiovascular and rheumatological diseases. These processes are typically systemic and often hallmarked by chronic inflammation including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, systemic sclerosis, psoriatic arthritis, spondyloarthropathies, and medium/large vessel vasculitis (eg, Takayasu and giant cell arteritis). Multimodality imaging in cardio-rheumatologic diseases is essential, and the use of each modality is dependent on the specific manifestation. Trainees should demonstrate the ability to integrate imaging information from different modalities in the clinical assessment of the cardio-rheumatology patient. As chronic inflammation increases the risk of atherosclerosis in many of these conditions, aggressive management of cardiovascular risk factors is recommended to reduce the higher rates of myocardial infarction, stroke and heart failure within these groups. Further risk stratification of asymptomatic patients with CT calcium score can be considered, and symptomatic patients should be evaluated with stress (echo, nuclear, or CMR) or anatomic (CCTA) imaging approaches.

Myocarditis, valvulitis, pericarditis, and/or aortitis are other cardiac manifestation of these systemic rheumatologic diseases, and symptoms may be difficult to separate from other coexisting conditions such as costochondritis. For myocardial and pericardial involvement, CMR is critical for accurate evaluation. Abnormal myocardial T1- and T2-weighted values, increased extracellular volume, and delayed myocardial enhancement can be seen in myocarditis. Similarly, thickened pericardium with evidence of increased pericardial signal on T2-weighted imaging and/or delayed enhancement is a feature of pericarditis. Tomographic imaging of the aorta and large branches with CT angiography or MR angiography provides comprehensive evaluation of wall thickening, stenosis, or aneurysms in suspected vasculitis. Tissue characteristics of the vessel wall by density (CT angiography) or signal intensity (MR angiography) provides incremental assessment of active inflammation. Nuclear imaging with FDG PET may be helpful to identify active inflammation in myocarditis and aortitis. Cardio-rheumatologic disease may manifest as pulmonary hypertension, and echocardiography is the preferred noninvasive tool for both initial diagnosis and long-term monitoring. Finally, typical valvulopathies may be seen with rheumatologic diseases such as rheumatic fever, rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus; and echocardiography is the test of choice for evaluation of the morphologic and functional valvular changes.

Sports and Exercise Cardiology

Cardiac imaging serves an important role in diagnosing potentially serious cardiac abnormalities in athletes, and differentiating physiologic changes related to intensive training from cardiac diseases with similar morphologic features. The cardiovascular imager should know the indications, applications, and limitations of all 4 imaging modalities in assessing athletes with potential heart disease. Echocardiography is often the first imaging modality to assess cardiac structure and function in athletes. The cardiovascular imager should be aware of the changes in left ventricular size and wall thickness that occur with intensive training and be able to differentiate them from those associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and its variants. When the echocardiographic results are inconclusive or when there is an increased clinical suspicion for an underlying cardiomyopathy, CMR can provide a more accurate morphologic and functional assessment of the myocardium as well as tissue characterization for demonstration of patterns typical of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and other pathologic myocardial conditions. In athletes with suspected coronary disease or coronary anomalies, the cardiovascular imager should be able to select the stress imaging modality that is best able to assess for ischemia when exercise testing alone is inconclusive or additional information related to cardiac structure and function is needed. The cardiovascular imager should also know the wide array of coronary anomalies and the importance of CCT to identifying these anomalies and their myocardial course.

Leadership and Administrative Skills

In addition to clinical competency, advanced cardiovascular imaging specialists are expected to function effectively as leaders in allied efforts to ensure high-quality care and advance the field of cardiovascular imaging. Some of these activities and attributes fall outside the realm of clinical knowledge and skill and instead involve administrative roles in clinical practice, hospitals, health systems, professional societies, or other organizations. Developing familiarity with accrediting organization standards and guidelines for each cardiovascular imaging modality and participation in the accreditation process are examples of how an advanced cardiovascular imaging trainee can gain experience in laboratory administration. The intention of training is to provide a foundation of leadership and administrative skills that would be enhanced and refined throughout one’s career after fellowship. Specific competencies expected of all general cardiologists and cardiovascular specialists, including those with careers involving greater administrative, managerial, or advocacy positions, are delineated in Table 24 of the 2016 ACC Lifelong Learning Competencies for General Cardiologists.4

Evaluation of Proficiency

Evaluation of advanced cardiovascular imaging trainee proficiency involves multiple assessments of trainee ability to clinically diagnose and manage patients across the broad spectrum of diseases, including those with acute and chronic cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, and valvular and structural heart disease.

Trainees must be evaluated regularly with respect to medical knowledge, clinical judgment, case management, and procedural skills including the ability to integrate clinical findings and cross correlate noninvasive and invasive cardiac imaging to make an accurate diagnosis and formulate a comprehensive management plan. They should be directly assessed on their procedural technique, including competency in selecting the appropriate imaging strategy, image acquisition, and image interpretation. Trainees should also be assessed on their ability to recognize and manage complications. Trainees should be evaluated on their interactive behavior with patients and family members as well as their commitment to working in a multidisciplinary and diverse environment with other members of the healthcare team.

Assessment of trainees should include multisource evaluations, direct observation by instructors, case logs, chart reviews (including adherence to guideline recommendations, AUC, and patient outcomes), simulation training, the trainee’s portfolio (including scholarly productivity and quality improvement projects), and assessment of leadership skills. Assessment tools and methods are available through the ACGME with guidance on a practical approach to the assessment of trainees in all 6 core competency domains.63, 64, 65 The trainee’s organization of, and participation in, didactic conferences and case presentations also provides opportunities to evaluate trainee proficiency. Self-assessment programs are available for each imaging modality. Program directors and trainees are encouraged to incorporate these resources into the course of training.

Trainees must maintain records of image acquisition and interpretation using an electronic database or procedure logbook that is Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant and contains pertinent clinical information, including number of cases, clinical diagnoses, disease severity, procedures performed, outcomes, and disposition.

The advanced cardiac imaging training program director is responsible for providing faculty development on use of assessment methods, effective feedback, and coaching and confirming trainee experience and competence. Online resources for assessing clinical competence and providing feedback are available and can be adapted to the needs of a particular program.64,66 The program director, imaging faculty, and in some cases a clinical competence committee, will verify and document trainee performance. Reviewing the overall progress of trainees is important to ensure achievement of training milestones and identification of areas in which additional focused training may be required. On a periodic basis, the program director should review each trainee’s case logbook to ensure adequate exposure to a broad spectrum of pathologies and trainee experiences in acquiring and interpreting diagnostic imaging procedures. In addition, the program director should provide feedback to the faculty on their teaching skills, assessment methods, and evaluations.

After the completion of advanced training in cardiovascular imaging, trainees are eligible to take the certification examination in each of the 4 imaging modalities offered by the respective nationally recognized certification imaging bodies. Information concerning eligibility and prerequisites for these certifications can be obtained online.40, 41, 42, 43 An important component of the certification process requires the cardiovascular imaging program director to certify trainee competency in knowledge and procedural skills as a prerequisite for sitting for each board certification examination. After initial certification, practicing cardiologists are responsible to ensure continued maintenance of certification throughout their careers.
