Factors associated with unintended pregnancies among unmarried adolescents in Eastern Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Makiko Komasawa, Miho Sato, Kiyoko Saito, Sumihisa Honda, Robert Ssekitoleko, Peter Waiswa, Kharim Mwebaza Muluya, Sheba Gitta, Myo Nyein Aung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent sexual and reproductive health and rights remain critical public health issues in low- and middle-income countries, with adverse health, educational, and economic consequences. Uganda faces significant challenges with high rates of unintended pregnancies among adolescents. This study examined factors associated with unintended pregnancies among unmarried adolescents in Eastern Uganda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among unmarried adolescent boys and girls aged 15–19 years in Iganga and Bugiri municipalities between February and March 2024. Data were collected at mobile money shops to target customers through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. The variables included sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behaviors, contraceptive knowledge, usage of mobile money shops, and environmental factors. Multivariate logistic regression was applied to identify factors associated with unintended pregnancies.ResultsAmong the 1267 eligible adolescents, 876 had ever engaged in sexual intercourse. Of these, 22.5% (n = 197) reported experiencing an unintended pregnancy. The factors associated with a lower likelihood of pregnancy included school enrollment (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.36; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24–0.54), co-residences with parents (AOR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.33–0.69), and teachers’ awareness of students’ lives/worries (AOR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.37–0.84). Factors associated with a higher likelihood of pregnancy were knowledge of how to use contraceptives (AOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.16–4.49), communication with parents/guardians (AOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.32–2.75), parental support for contraception (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.03–2.59), and mobile phone possession (AOR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.09–4.90). Adolescents who had experienced pregnancy were significantly more likely to consider community-based distributors as comfortable sources for sexual and reproductive health information (AOR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.11–2.66) and contraceptives (AOR = 4.76; 95% CI: 2.39–9.47).ConclusionsNearly one-quarter of unmarried adolescents have experienced—or caused—a pregnancy in Eastern Uganda. School enrollment and parental engagement were inversely associated with adolescent pregnancy. Many adolescents may gain contraceptive knowledge and support after an unintended pregnancy. Community-based service delivery was preferred over facility-based options. Further researches are needed to develop a community-based platform for reaching underserved unmarried adolescents and promoting appropriate contraceptive use.Trial registrationThis study was registered at Japan’s University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000053332) on 12 January 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02235-3.Keywords: Sexual and reproductive health and rights, Unintended pregnancy, Unmarried adolescent, Teenagers, Mobile money shop, Uganda

Background

Adolescent sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) are pressing public health issues globally. In low- and middle-income countries, approximately 21 million girls aged 15–19 years become pregnant, and almost half of these pregnancies are unintended [1]. These early unintended pregnancies tend to lead to unsafe abortion and, consequently, maternal mortality [1, 2]. The impact of early pregnancy extends beyond health, affecting a girl’s education and income-earning potential and perpetuating a cycle of poverty that can last for generations [3, 4]. This situation is a result of unmet needs for SRHR services among young populations [5, 6].

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has high rates of adolescent pregnancies, and this trend is expected to increase [7]. Uganda ranks fifth with a high prevalence of unintended pregnancies among the 29 SSA countries [8]. In Uganda, many girls engage in sexual activity before marriage, with the median age at first sexual intercourse among women aged 25–49 years reported as 16.9 years in 2021 [9]. The maternal mortality rate remains high at 189 per 100,000 live births, with 17% of these involving girls aged 15–19 years [2, 9]. The rate of any contraceptive use among all women aged 15–49 years remains low at 44.0%, and for modern methods, it is 40.1% [9]. Ninety-seven percent of pregnant female students drop out of school [10]. Additionally, unintended pregnancies attributed to “transactional sex” have increased, particularly among economically disadvantaged girls who engage in sexual activity in exchange for small amounts of financial support or transportation costs [11]. There remains a need to improve the quality and quantity of public SRHR services, enhancing youth-friendly family planning services [12, 13]. The Ugandan government has implemented various policies and programs over the past 15 years, such as expanding the compulsory education period, enacting defilement laws, prohibiting marriage for girls under 18 years of age, and prohibiting sexual intercourse with girls under 18 years of age [11, 14]. However, adolescent pregnancy rates have remained stagnant over the past decade [14].

Since the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, numerous interventions have been undertaken to improve adolescent SRHR [15]. Most of these interventions have been conducted in schools, primary healthcare facilities, and peer-educators [16–18]. However, some studies have highlighted that many programs have not adequately reached unmarried adolescents who need SRHR services [16, 19]. Additionally, the number of SRHR services has been limited, and their adoption has been insufficient, making many of these programs unsustainable and ineffective despite substantial investments [19]. Consequently, youth SRHR programs have stagnated for almost two decades [6, 20]. In light of these challenges, several studies have stressed the need for innovative approaches to reach underserved adolescents [17, 18, 21, 22].

In our exploration of effective approaches in Uganda, we paid attention to mobile money shops (agents) as a channel to connect with underserved adolescents and provide them with SRHR services [23]. These shops were available and used throughout the country. As of 2023, 63% of Uganda’s population was using mobile phones [24]. Our preliminary study revealed that 85% of adolescents access shops more than once a week to use various services such as money transfers, purchasing airtime/data, and making cash deposits [23].

Given the situation in Uganda, we conducted a hybrid effectiveness-implementation study to reach adolescents using agents and to promote condom use [25]. Utilizing the data of the hybrid study, this study aimed to examine factors associated with unintended pregnancy among adolescents aged 15–19 years.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study utilized baseline data from a hybrid effectiveness–implementation study, the overall design of which has been reported elsewhere [23]. For clarity, we briefly describe the components relevant to the present cross-sectional analysis. The study was conducted in the Busoga region, which is located approximately 80 km from Kampala, the capital of Uganda. Two municipalities with comparable demographic and socioeconomic characteristics were selected: Iganga and Bugiri. In 2022, the Iganga municipality had a population of 55,263, 20.9% of which were aged 10–17 years, whereas Bugiri had a population of 28,747, 19.3% of which were in the same age group.

Participants

We selected 30 shops from each municipality, taking into account geographical distribution, population density, and the availability of health facilities and schools. The shop owners’ eligibility criteria included being 18–30 years of age, having at least one year of experience in business operations, and willingness to participate in the study. Stand-alone shops were prioritized to ensure the privacy of customers and commodities. Adolescent customers were then recruited through the selected shops. Eligible adolescent participants were unmarried boys and girls aged 15–19 years who visited the selected shops and had no plans to leave the municipalities during the study period. We adopted the 15–19-year age bracket for this study because the Uganda Demographic and Health Survey employs this age bracket when reporting on teenage pregnancy. Recruitment was discontinued once the target sample size of approximately 20 adolescents per shop was achieved. A systematic sampling at mobile money shops was applied, as customers could not be assigned to specific shops and typically engaged with multiple shops at their own convenience. We decided on a minimum sample size of 600 in each municipality via a formula based on the available data and previous studies, using a calculation method explained in the protocol paper [23].

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was developed using research instruments based mainly on the 2016 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey [26] and other studies conducted in SSA [27–33]. We added questions regarding social and physical opportunities and those related to mobile money shops’ usage [23]. We developed a questionnaire in English and translated it into the local language (Lusoga and Luganda). Before finalizing the questionnaire, we conducted a series of pretests with 26 adolescents (10 boys and 16 girls) and modified them to enhance clarity and ease of response. Although most adolescents use English daily, some participants are comfortable discussing sensitive SRHR topics in their local language. We employed data collectors who were proficient in the local language and had academic backgrounds in social welfare or health. The questionnaire included five categories: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) sexual behavior, (3) knowledge and capability on contraception, (4) environmental factors related to preventing pregnancy, and (5) preference for contraceptive practices. The environmental factors included parental relationship, healthcare workers’ support, community support, and teachers’ awareness of students’ lives/worries.

The questionnaire was administered face-to-face by trained data collectors via preprogrammed tablet computers [23]. The interviews were conducted in a private location near shops or adolescents’ homes between February and March 2024. Data collection lasted 30–40 min per interview.

Data analysis

The primary outcome was the experience of pregnancy, which was defined as either girls becoming pregnant or boys causing a pregnancy; for simplicity, the term “pregnancy” is hereafter used for both sexes. First, we described the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and their sexual behaviors and compared the two groups (pregnancy versus never-pregnancy) using the chi-square test. The variables that showed significant differences in the chi-square test were subjected to logistic regression analyses after confirming no multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was conducted via STATA v.18.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of Uganda Christian University (UCUREC-2023-710), the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (SS2284ES), and the Ethics Review Committee of the Ogata Sadako Research Institute for Peace and Development of Japan International Cooperation Agency in Japan (JICA [DI] 202312220002). Written informed consent was obtained from all adolescent participants and the parents/guardians of adolescents aged 15–17 years. To obtain consent from parents/guardians, skilled researchers visited the homes of the expected participants and explained the study to the parents/guardians.

Results

We included 1267 eligible participants in the study. Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants in the two groups: pregnancy and never-pregnancy. Overall, 52.5% were boys, whereas 47.5% were girls. Approximately 70% were aged 18–19 years. Islam was the most commonly reported religion (54.3%). 51.2% of participants were currently enrolled in school, and 39.8% were engaged in paid work. Among those who ever had boys/girlfriends, 81.6% (876/1073) had had sexual intercourse, with an average age at first intercourse of 15.4 years (standard deviation: 2.0). Among the respondents who had engaged in sexual intercourse, 22.5% (197/876) had experienced a pregnancy, of whom 51.1% (97/190) reported a live birth (data not shown in Table 1). All variables, except sex and religion, were significantly different between the two groups.

Table 2 shows the adolescents’ basic knowledge of contraception and fertility. Nearly all the respondents (96.1%) had heard of contraceptives; however, fewer reported knowledge of how to use contraceptives (79.9%). Knowledge of the fertile period within the menstrual cycle was particularly low, with only 9.1% demonstrating awareness. Table 3 demonstrates the factors associated with experiencing pregnancy. The likelihood of pregnancy among girls was significantly greater than that among boys (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.29, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.52–3.44). The factors associated with a lower likelihood of pregnancy experiences included current school enrollment (AOR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.24–0.54), living with parents (AOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.33–0.69), and teachers’ awareness of students’ lives/worries (AOR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.37–0.84). In contrast, the factors associated with a greater likelihood of pregnancy were knowledge of how to use contraceptives (AOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.16–4.49), communication with parents/guardians (AOR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.32–2.75), parental support for contraceptive use (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.03–2.59), and possession of a mobile phone (AOR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.09–4.90). Furthermore, adolescents who had experienced pregnancy were more likely to seek services from public health facilities (AOR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.10–2.49) and community-based distributors (AOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.11–2.66). When asked about preferred sources of contraceptives, the pregnancy group was 4.76 times more likely to favor community-based distributors than the never-pregnancy group. We additionally conducted sex-stratified analyses, which revealed modest differences between boys and girls. Only girls’ pregnancy group were more likely to report receiving contraceptive support from parents/guardians (AOR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.14–4.29) and to own a mobile phone (AOR = 3.26, 95% CI: 1.23–8.62). In contrast, boys with pregnancy experience were more likely to prefer community-based distributors as their source of contraceptives (AOR = 5.25, 95% CI: 2.32–11.85) (Supplementary file).

Discussion

This study examined factors associated with unintended pregnancy among unmarried adolescents. Over 80% of the adolescents who had ever had a boyfriend or girlfriend reported initiating sexual intercourse, with an average age at first intercourse of 15.4 years. Nearly one-quarter reported a history of pregnancy. School enrollment and parental engagement were associated with lower pregnancy prevalence. Conversely, experiencing a pregnancy was associated with a high prevalence rate of knowledge of contraceptive use and their ability to mobilize information and support. Community-based service delivery was preferred over conventional facility-based delivery channels, particularly among boys.

Knowledge and accessibility to contraception

Insisting on abstinence in school education was ineffective in preventing teenage pregnancies [34]. In our study, the pregnancy group was more knowledgeable about how to use contraceptives and more accessible to public facilities and community-based distributors than the non-pregnancy group was. These findings indicate that adolescents often gain practical knowledge and access to a range of contraceptive services only subsequent to an unintended pregnancy. Although the Ministry of Education and Sports promotes strengthening school-based SRHR education primarily through an abstinence-focused approach [10], the provision of practical information on sex, pregnancy, contraception, and sexually transmitted infections requires substantial enhancement within schools, families, and the broader community [34–36]. In addition, some studies emphasized that younger children were exposed to more unsafe sexual intercourse, suggesting that sex education from an earlier age, before sexual initiation, was necessary [37–40]. A study on children aged 10–14 years in Uganda also supported this suggestion [41]. Greater emphasis is needed on providing comprehensive and practical information on sexuality, pregnancy, and contraception across schools, families, and communities before sexual initiation [3, 11, 32, 34, 35, 37, 41].

Parental involvement

Parental involvement has been identified as a critical factor influencing unintended pregnancy in adolescents across several SSA countries [31, 32, 35]. In our study, co-residence with parents implied that it deterred sexual activity, a finding consistent with evidence from Ghana showing that parental involvement reduced pregnancy occurrence [31, 35]. Conversely, parental interaction and contraceptive support were positively associated with pregnancy experience, suggesting that parents may initiate care and support only after an unintended pregnancy has already taken place. This tendency was particularly pronounced among girls. In Uganda, prior research has noted that parental neglect and hesitancy to provide sex education—often due to uncertainty about how to communicate with adolescents—further exacerbate the risks of unintended pregnancy [11]. Therefore, strengthening parents’ motivation to deliver sex education, improving their own knowledge of SRHR, and enhancing their communication skills are needed [27, 32, 35]. The urgent development of family-based educational programs that equip parents/guardians with both accurate SRHR knowledge and practical skills to engage with their children is necessary in Uganda [11].

Community-based distributors

Global health leaders have reported that facility-based interventions—such as those implemented in schools, health centers, and youth centers—are often limited in reach, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability [19]. Our findings indicate that community-based distributors of contraceptive information and methods were highly preferred among adolescents who had experienced pregnancy. This trend was higher among boys with pregnancy experience. In line with the global call for innovative channels to reach underserved adolescents made nearly a decade ago [4, 16, 21], our results highlight the need to develop community-based distribution approaches beyond conventional approaches further. Such strategies may enhance adolescents’ ability to access and effectively use contraceptives.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we recruited adolescent customers from mobile money shops using systematic procedures; therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to the broader population. Second, since the survey was dependent on self-reports and by face-to-face interviews, the study is susceptible to recall, interview, and social desirability biases. To reduce these biases, we employed skilled data collectors who had rich experience in the SRHR field. Third, the nature of the quantitative study and cross-sectional design could not describe the causality of pregnancy, and limited further interpretation of the underlying social, cultural, and historical factors. Fourth, our study did not explore the economic status of the adolescents because of difficulties in accessing accurate data and comparing adolescents living with their parents and others via a structured questionnaire. Fifth, we focused on whether or not individuals have experienced pregnancy and have not deeply explored gender differences. The dependent variable, “experience of pregnancy,” combines boys who caused a pregnancy with girls who became pregnant, potentially introducing measurement asymmetry that should be taken into account. Sixth, we did not explore the status of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) in this study. Given that there is still high stigma attached to HIV/AIDS status in Uganda, we determined that obtaining such information through self-reports would not provide reliable information. To overcome these limitations, a qualitative study with a diverse population of children, parents, and social decision-makers is needed. Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first community-based quantitative study to identify factors associated with unintended pregnancy among unmarried adolescent boys and girls in Uganda.

Conclusions

This study revealed that a substantial proportion of adolescents aged 15–19 years in Eastern Uganda were sexually active, with nearly one-quarter reporting a history of pregnancy. School enrollment and parental engagement were inversely associated with adolescent pregnancy. The findings also suggested that many adolescents begin to acquire contraceptive knowledge, access diverse contraceptive channels, and receive parental support only after an unintended pregnancy has occurred. Moreover, community-based service delivery channels were preferred over conventional facility-based options. Further studies are needed to develop an innovative community-based platform for effectively reaching underserved unmarried adolescents and promoting appropriate contraceptive use.
