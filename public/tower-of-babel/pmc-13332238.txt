C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index as a predictor of heart failure risk in adults with MASLD: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Haixiang Zheng, Hong Zheng, Guanlin Chen, Matilde Motzo, Weiqiang Wu, Shuohua Chen, Gavino Casu, Leonardo Antonio Sechi, Shouling Wu, Gianpaolo Vidili, Youren Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure (HF) is a major complication of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). The C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (CTI), which reflects systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, is linked to cardiovascular outcomes, but its predictive value for HF in MASLD patients remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate the baseline and cumulative CTI (CumCTI) in relation to incident HF and explore blood pressure as a potential mediator. We included 26,499 participants with MASLD from the Kailuan Study. The baseline and CumCTI values were calculated and examined for associations with HF via Cox regression. Restricted cubic splines were used to assess dose‒response patterns. Predictive performance was evaluated using C-index, reclassification metrics, time-dependent ROC curves, and calibration analyses. During a median follow-up of 16.0 years, 1,158 HF events occurred. A higher CTI was independently associated with increased HF risk (HR for Q4 vs. Q1: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.48–2.11; P < 0.001), with each 1-SD increase corresponding to a 27% greater risk (HR 1.27; 95% CI: 1.19–1.35). CumCTI showed stronger associations with HF and modestly improved model discrimination and reclassification compared with conventional risk factors. Time-dependent ROC analyses showed slight increases in AUC at 5 and 10 years after adding CumCTI (0.782 to 0.783 and 0.763 to 0.767, respectively), with good calibration between predicted and observed risks. Mediation analysis indicated that systolic and diastolic blood pressure explained only a small proportion of the CTI–HF association. Associations were stronger for CumCTI in participants aged < 60 years. These findings suggest that a higher CTI, especially cumulative exposure, is independently associated with HF risk in MASLD and may provide complementary information for cardiovascular risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51503-0.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is one of the leading global health issues affecting more than 64 million individuals worldwide, with an increasing prevalence and persistently high five-year mortality despite therapeutic advances1. In China, the HF patient population is estimated to exceed 13 million, underscoring its significant public health impact2. Systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, key features of metabolic dysfunction, are recognized as pivotal drivers of HF pathogenesis3,4. Chronic low-grade inflammation drives myocardial fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, whereas insulin resistance promotes lipotoxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction, together accelerating the progression of HF5,6.

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is considered the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally, affecting approximately one-third of all adults7,8. The concept has expanded hepatic complications and is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD), which is the major cause of death among these patients9. Recently, MASLD has been proposed to represent an independent risk factor for HF and is influenced by systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction10–12. Similarly, surrogate markers reflecting these processes exist and have prognostic value. The triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) has gained widespread use as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has been consistently associated with increased HF risk13. In addition, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), which is a marker of systemic inflammation, independently predicts cardiovascular events and poor outcomes in high-risk populations, including patients with hypertension14. Recently, the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI), which combines TyG and hs-CRP, has been proposed as a superior indicator of the combined impact of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation. Elevated CTI has been linked with coronary heart disease (CHD), cardiovascular events, and mortality across both general and high-risk populations15–17. However, despite the well-established cardiovascular implications of MASLD and the recognized prognostic value of the CTI, little is known about whether the CTI can be used to predict HF specifically among patients with MASLD. Considering that MASLD uniquely involves both metabolic dysfunction and systemic inflammation, this population may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse cardiovascular consequences reflected by CTI.

We performed a large-scale, prospective cohort analysis within the Kailuan Study to investigate the association between CTI and incident HF in individuals with MASLD, addressing a knowledge gap. We further evaluated the incremental predictive value of the CumCTI over conventional risk models and explored potential mediating pathways, with the aim of clarifying whether the CTI can serve as a dynamic and clinically relevant biomarker for HF risk stratification in MASLD populations.

Methods

Study population and design

The Kailuan Study is a community-based prospective cohort in Tangshan, Hebei Province, China, aimed at the early detection of individuals prone to major noncommunicable diseases, especially cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions. More comprehensive descriptions regarding the research framework and study protocols are available in prior publications18,19. Participants who completed health assessments and structured questionnaires at Kailuan General Hospital and ten affiliated hospitals from 2006 to 2007, 2008–2009, and 2010–2011 were included in this analysis. Among them, 31,806 individuals were initially identified as having MASLD. Following the exclusion of individuals with hepatitis B, liver cirrhosis or cancer (n = 834), excessive alcohol consumption (n = 3,591), HF or death in 2006 (n = 27), and those lacking baseline data for fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), or hs-CRP (n = 855), 26,499 participants remained for baseline CTI analysis. To investigate the correlation between CumCTI and the risk of HF, participants were further excluded if they missed follow-up (n = 10,559), developed cancer (n = 108), experienced HF or death (n = 55), or had missing data for FBG, TG, or hs-CRP in 2008 or 2010 (n = 749), resulting in a final sample of 15,028 (Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kailuan Medical Hospital (ChiCTR-TNC-11001489), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data acquisition and variable definitions

Sociodemographic information, including age, sex, education (high school diploma or above vs. below), monthly income per household member(< 1000CNY or ≥ 1000CNY), smoking status (current vs. noncurrent), physical activity (yes or no), and marital status (with or without a spouse), was collected via standardized questionnaires. We collected information on the use of medications for hypertension, diabetes and lipid reduction. Blood pressure was measured at least twice by trained nurses under standardized conditions with either an automated device or a manual sphygmomanometer, and we analyzed the mean value. Fasting blood samples were centrally analyzed at Kailuan General Hospital. We measured lipid profiles [total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and TG], uric acid, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and FBG levels with an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). We measured the hs-CRP concentration with a particle-enhanced immunonephelometric assay (Cias Latex CRP-H; Kanto Chemical Co., Tokyo, Japan). We measured serum creatinine enzymatically on a Beckman Coulter AU5400 analyzer and estimated the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) via the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes, FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or current use of hypoglycemic medications20.

Assessment of MASLD and CTI

Hepatic steatosis was evaluated via abdominal ultrasonography via a PHILIPS HD15 color Doppler system with a 1–5 MHz convex array probe. Experienced sonographers with more than five years of clinical practice, who had received standardized training, performed all examinations. Steatosis was identified when at least two of the following ultrasound characteristics were observed: (a) increased hepatic echogenicity relative to the renal cortex, (b) ultrasound beam attenuation, and (c) reduced visibility of intrahepatic vascular structures21. To ensure diagnostic consistency, two trained sonographers independently reviewed and jointly confirmed all images. Additionally, other sonography experts re-evaluated a random subset of images to confirm diagnostic reliability. All sonographers were blinded to the participants’ clinical and laboratory data during both image acquisition and interpretation.

The concept of MASLD was first proposed in the 2023 multisociety Delphi consensus and is also aligned with the 2024 EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines22,23. MASLD is characterized by hepatic steatosis, detected by imaging, histology, or blood biomarkers, alongside at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (CMRF), excluding other causes such as significant alcohol intake, drug-induced liver injury, or rare metabolic disorders. CMRFs encompassed (1) overweight/obesity, defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m² or waist circumference > 94 cm for males and > 80 cm for females, with ethnicity-specific criteria; (2) elevated glucose, indicated by FBG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, 2-hour post-load glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, type 2 diabetes diagnosis, or antidiabetic medication use; (3) hypertension, characterized by blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive therapy; (4) hypertriglyceridemia, identified by TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L or lipid-lowering treatment; and (5) low HDL-c, defined as ≤ 1.0 mmol/L for males,≤1.3 mmol/L for females, or lipid-lowering therapy. In the Kailuan study, MASLD was defined according to these criteria on the basis of available clinical and laboratory data (Table S1).

To ensure unit consistency with the published TyG and CTI formulas, TG and FBG (originally measured in mmol/L) were converted to mg/dL prior to index calculation using standard conversion factors (1mmol/L TG = 88.57 mg/dl; 1mmol/L FBG = 18.02 mg/dl). Hs-CRP was measured and used in mg/L as obtained. The CTI was derived via a formula that combines indicators of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance24, as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CTI=0.412\mathrm{*}\mathrm{L}\mathrm{n}(\mathrm{h}\mathrm{s}-\mathrm{C}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{L}\left]\right)+\mathrm{L}\mathrm{n}\left(\frac{\mathrm{T}\mathrm{G}[\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{l}]\mathrm{*}\mathrm{F}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{G}[\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{l}]}{2}\right)$$\end{document}

Cumulative CTI (CumCTI) was calculated as a time-weighted average of CTI measurements across the 2006, 2008, and 2010 examination waves using the trapezoidal rule, in which the area between consecutive CTI measurements was computed and normalized to the total follow-up duration between the first and third examination waves. Specifically,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CumCTI=\frac{\frac{(\mathrm{C}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{I}2006+\mathrm{C}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{I}2008)}{2}*\left(Time2-Time1\right)+\frac{(\mathrm{C}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{I}2008+\mathrm{C}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{I}2010)}{2}*\left(Time3-Time2\right)}{Time3-Time1}$$\end{document}

where Time1, Time2, and Time3 denote the examination times in 2006, 2008, and 2010, respectively. Given the fixed survey-wave design, fixed intervals were applied between consecutive examinations. Under equal intervals, this time-weighted formulation is mathematically equivalent to the arithmetic mean of the three CTI measurements; however, we retained the trapezoidal time-weighted expression for methodological clarity and potential extensibility to unequal intervals. CumCTI was computed only among participants with CTI available at all three waves; therefore, no missing values were present in the calculation of CumCTI.

Ascertainment of heart failure

The main outcome was the first incidence of HF, which was evaluated from baseline until the earliest HF diagnosis, death, or the end of follow-up on December 31, 2023. HF events were identified through annual assessments and multiple supplementary sources, including the municipal social security system, discharge records from 11 regional hospitals, national death registries, and biennial questionnaires since 200625. Diagnoses were conducted according to the guidelines from both the European Society of Cardiology and the Chinese Heart Failure Standards26,27. A confirmed HF case required: (1) typical clinical symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, or edema, documented as NYHA class II–IV or Killip class II–IV, and at least one of the following objective criteria: (2) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 50% measured by two-dimensional Doppler echocardiography via the modified Simpson method; or (3) LVEF > 50% together with elevated plasma N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentration. A panel of specialists annually reviewed discharge diagnoses coded as ICD-10 I50.x to validate the HF classification.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, normally distributed data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD), whereas skewed data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (P25, P75). Categorical variables are shown as frequencies with percentages. One-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables was used depending on the variable’s distribution to test the differences between groups. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

Person-years were calculated from the baseline survey date to the date of first HF or the end of follow-up on December 31, 2023. Owing to the near-normal distribution of the CTI (Fig. S1), we analyzed the CTI as a continuous variable (per 1 SD increase) and in quadrants. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the association between the CTI and HF risk. The proportional hazards assumption was tested via the likelihood ratio method. For each model, we calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Three Cox proportional hazards models with multiple variables were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for age sex, and monthly income per household member; Model 2 included these variables and added education level, physical activity, current smoking status, marital status, uric acid, LDL-c and ALT. Model 3 further controlled for the use of antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications. Multicollinearity was examined via variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a VIF > 10 indicating potential collinearity. In this study, no covariates exceeded this threshold, suggesting model stability (Table S2). Linear trends across CTI quartiles were assessed by modeling the CTI as a continuous variable. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate the cumulative incidence during follow-up, and group differences were assessed via the log-rank test. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles according to optimal Akaike information criterion values was applied to explore possible nonlinear relationships between the CTI and HF risk among MASLD patients. Tests for linearity were performed via the Wald test.

We evaluated the extra predictive value of the CTI and related biomarkers for HF prediction via Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). The conventional risk model was used as a reference. The base model included demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and treatment-related covariates. Each biomarker (hs-CRP, TyG, hs-CRP + TyG, CTI, and CumCTI) was individually added to this model. The extra model performance was measured by the changes in the C-index, NRI, and IDI. To further evaluate the predictive performance of the cumulative index, time-dependent ROC curves were generated to estimatethe area under the curve (AUC) at 5 and 10 years for the conventional model and the model additionally including CumCTI. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots comparing predicted and observed HF risk. We conducted a mediation analysis to evaluate whether systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), hs-CRP, or TyG mediated the association between CTI and incident HF among MASLD patients. We used a linear regression-based causal mediation framework and estimated total, direct, and indirect effects. We evaluated whether SBP and DBP mediated the CTI–HF association in our primary analysis. In a secondary analysis, we evaluated whether TyG and hs-CRP reciprocally mediated the CTI–HF association, with TyG and hs-CRP representing the metabolic and inflammatory components of CTI, respectively. We estimated indirect effects by multiplying path coefficients. The proportion mediated was defined as the indirect effect divided by the total effect. Analyses were conducted via the regmedint R package (survCox method) while adjusting for pertinent covariates. The mediation analysis relies on the sequential ignorability assumption, which assumes no unmeasured confounding of the exposure–outcome and mediator–outcome relationships conditional on measured covariates. To assess the robustness of the mediation estimates to potential violations of this assumption, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using mediational E-values, which quantify the minimum strength of unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding required to explain away the estimated natural indirect effect on the hazard ratio scale.

Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed across categories defined by sex, age, physical activity, education level, and diabetes status. Cox models incorporated interaction terms to assess effect modification. The robustness of the findings was evaluated through sensitivity analyses. First, participants who experienced incident HF events within the first two years of follow-up were excluded to minimize reverse causation. Second, analyses were restricted to participants not using antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or hypoglycemic medications. We additionally conducted sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding participants with glycemic abnormalities, elevated blood pressure, or dyslipidemia as defined by the MASLD diagnostic criteria. Third, to further address potential confounding factors arising from pre-existing CVD that may lead to subsequent HF, we excluded participants with baseline CVD (myocardial infarction, stroke, or atrial fibrillation) and reassessed the associations between CTI, CumCTI and incident HF. Fourth, to account for potential bias from within-follow-up variation in CTI, additional models adjusted for CTI measured in 2006 were used. In addition, based on Model 3, we further adjusted for BMI to examine the robustness of the associations between CTI and incident HF. Fifth, competing risk models were applied via Fine and Gray’s subdistribution hazard approach to account for non-HF mortality. Finally, we employed the VanderWeele method to compute E values for the fully adjusted Cox models, evaluating their robustness against unmeasured confounding variables. E-values were reported for both the point estimates and the 95% CI limits closest to the null.

All analyses were conducted via R (version 4.3.3) and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 26,499 participants from the Kailuan Study (2006–2023) were ultimately included in the present analysis. Over a median follow-up of 16.0 years, HF developed in 1,158 individuals, representing 4.4% of all individuals in the study (Table S3). The mean age of these individuals was 52.7 years (SD 11.6), and 79.2% were men. Among participants with MASLD, metabolic dysfunction components used for diagnosis were highly prevalent: 24,825 (93.7%) met the BMI criterion, 10,822 (40.8%) had glycemic abnormalities, 18,753 (70.8%) had elevated blood pressure, and 15,104 (57.0%) had lipid abnormalities (Table S4). Compared with those without HF development, participants with HF onset at baseline presented significantly elevated baseline levels of hs-CRP, TyG, and CTI (all P < 0.01).

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the participants according to quartiles of the CTI. Individuals in higher quartiles of CTI were older; had higher levels of SBP, DBP, TC, TG, FBG, hs-CRP, and TyG; and had a lower eGFR (all P < 0.01). In addition, with increasing CTI, there was a progressively greater prevalence of DM and the use of antihypertensive, glucose-lowering, and lipid-lowering medications (all P < 0.01). Overall, these findings indicate that a higher CTI is correlated with a more adverse cardiometabolic profile in participants at baseline.

Associations between the CTI and incident heart failure

The association between the CTI and the risk of HF was assessed via Cox proportional hazards regression models. Table 2 shows a steady increase in HF incidence across CTI quartiles, increasing from 1.83 per 1000 person-years in Q1 to 4.39 in Q4. Compared with those of the lowest quartile, the fully adjusted HRs for HF were 1.24 (95% CI: 1.02–1.50; P = 0.029) for Q2, 1.44 (95% CI: 1.21–1.75; P < 0.001) for Q3, and 1.78 (95% CI: 1.48–2.11; P < 0.001) for Q4 (P for trend < 0.001). In Model 1, adjusted for age, sex and monthly income per household member, each increase in the CTI was associated with a 30% greater risk of HF (HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.23–1.38; P < 0.001). This association remained robust in the fully adjusted model, with each SD increase in the CTI corresponding to an HR of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.19–1.35; P < 0.001).

Associations between the CumCTI and incident heart failure

To examine the effects of lasting systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, we further explored the associations between the CumCTI and HF. The CumCTI analysis included 15,028 participants. To assess potential selection bias arising from cohort restriction, we compared baseline characteristics between participants included in the CumCTI analysis (n = 15,028) and those excluded (n = 10,559). The results are presented in Table S5. Although several variables showed statistically significant differences between the two groups, likely reflecting the large sample size, the absolute differences in most demographic and clinical characteristics were relatively small, suggesting that the overall baseline profiles of the included and excluded participants were broadly comparable. As shown in Table S6, the included participants had a mean age of 54.8 years, among whom 525 cases of HF (3.5%) occurred during a median follow-up of 12.0 years. A higher CumCTI was significantly associated with increased HF risk, especially in individuals in the highest quartile. In the age-, sex- and monthly income per household member-adjusted model (Model 1), individuals in Q4 had an HR of 2.15 (95% CI: 1.66–2.78; P < 0.001) compared with those in Q1. Furthermore, the association between the CumCTI and HF remained significant in the fully adjusted model (HR: 1.79; 95% CI: 1.36–2.34; P < 0.001). A significant dose–response relationship across quartiles was presented in both models (P for trend < 0.001). In addition, when modeled as a continuous variable, each SD increase in CumCTI was associated with a 34% increased risk of HF (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.22–1.47; P < 0.001) (Table S7). Overall, our findings indicate that long-term increases in the CTI may represent chronic inflammatory and metabolic imbalances, which are independently correlated with increased HF risk in MASLD patients.

The additional predictive value of CTI and related indices

To evaluate the added prognostic utility of CTI-related markers in predicting HF, model performance was assessed via the C-index, IDI, and NRI. The conventional model yielded a C-index of 0.7336 (Table S8). The addition of hs-CRP, TyG, hs-CRP + TyG, CTI, and CumCTI led to modest increases in discrimination, with C indices ranging from 0.7361 to 0.7419. Among these, CumCTI provided the largest improvement in discrimination (ΔC index = 0.0083). CumCTI also improved reclassification and discrimination metrics, with an IDI of 0.29% ((95% CI)%: 0.15–0.42; P < 0.001) and a continuous NRI of 22.57% ((95% CI)%: 13.92–31.23; P < 0.001). ROC analyses based on individual biomarkers showed that CumCTI had the highest discriminative ability for HF among the evaluated markers (AUC = 0.613), outperforming CTI (AUC = 0.579), hs-CRP (AUC = 0.574), TyG (AUC = 0.538)(Fig. S2). In time-dependent ROC analyses, the conventional model achieved AUCs of 0.782 and 0.763 for predicting 5- and 10-year HF risk, respectively. After incorporating CumCTI, the AUCs increased slightly to 0.783 and 0.767 (Fig. 4A–B). Calibration plots demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed HF risks for both models at 5 and 10 years (Fig. 4C–D). These findings suggest that CumCTI provides a modest incremental improvement in HF risk prediction beyond conventional risk factors, although the magnitude of improvements is relatively limited.

Mediation analyses

To investigate potential mechanisms linking CTI and HF, SBP and DBP were evaluated as mediators in separate models. As shown in Fig. 5, CTI was significantly associated with increased HF risk (total effect: β = 0.399, P < 0.001). SBP partially mediated this association, with an indirect effect of β = 0.012 and a mediation proportion of 3.7% (P < 0.001). Similarly, DBP exhibited a minor indirect effect (β = 0.007, P < 0.001), representing 2.7% of the total effect. Consistent with these findings, the natural indirect effects were small but statistically significant on the hazard ratio scale (SBP: HR = 1.017, 95% CI: 1.010–1.024; DBP: HR = 1.010, 95% CI: 1.004–1.015). Sensitivity analyses using mediational E-values indicated that relatively modest unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding (E-values ranging from 1.11 to 1.15) could explain away the observed indirect effects, suggesting that these mediation estimates should be interpreted with caution (Table S9). Additional analyses decomposing CTI into its components showed no evidence of mediation between TyG and hs-CRP. When TyG was treated as the exposure and hs-CRP as the mediator, the indirect effect was not significant (β = − 0.001, P = 0.172; proportion mediated: − 0.2%, P = 0.185). Similarly, when hs-CRP was considered the exposure and TyG the mediator, the indirect effect remained non-significant (β = − 0.001, P = 0.107; proportion mediated: − 4.4%, P = 0.167). Overall, these findings suggest that SBP and DBP account for only a small proportion of the association between CTI and HF, while no evidence was observed for reciprocal mediation between TyG and hs-CRP; these component analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses (Fig. S3) indicated a consistent association between the CTI and HF across all subgroups, with no effect modifications observed (all P values for interactions > 0.05). In contrast, a significant interaction with age was detected for CumCTI (P for interaction = 0.03), with a stronger association in participants aged < 60 years (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.23–1.62) than in those aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.11–1.43). No statistically significant interactions were observed in the other subgroups. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistent associations between CTI, CumCTI, and HF across multiple models (Table S10 and Table S11). After excluding events within the first two years of follow-up and participants on antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or hypoglycemic medications, the associations remained statistically significant. Consistent results were also observed after excluding participants with glycemic abnormalities, excluding participants with baseline CVD (myocardial infarction, stroke or atrial fibrillation), both CTI and CumCTI remained positively associated with incident HF. Further adjustment for BMI yielded materially similar results. The HR per 1-SD increase in the CTI remained stable across the models, ranging from 1.19 to 1.39, whereas the corresponding estimates for the CumCTI ranged from 1.30 to 1.37. The associations also persisted in competing risk models (Fig. S4) and after adjusting the CumCTI for the baseline CTI. In the E value analyses of the fully adjusted models, the E values were 1.86 for the CTI per SD (CI limit of 1.69) and 2.96 for CTI Q4 vs. Q1 (2.32); for the CumCTI, the corresponding values were 2.01 per SD (1.74) and 2.92 for Q4 vs. Q1 (2.04) (Table S12). These values suggest that an unmeasured confounder would require a risk ratio association of approximately 1.7-3.0 with both CTI and HF to nullify the observed associations, indicating robustness against unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective study of adults with MASLD, higher CTI levels were consistently associated with elevated HF risk. Long-term exposure was captured by the CumCTI. This analysis further confirmed a consistent, dose–response relationship between CTI and HF. As a time-updated measure, the CumCTI provided better predictive performance than CTI and its individual components (hs-CRP and TyG), enhancing both discrimination and risk reclassification for HF. Moreover, mediation analyses revealed that both SBP and DBP significantly mediated a minor portion of the CTI-HF association. These findings remained robust across extensive sensitivity and competing-risk analyses. Results were largely consistent across subgroups, with a steeper risk gradient for CumCTI observed among participants under 60 years of age.

Prior studies highlight the prognostic value of the CTI, a marker that combines systemic inflammation and insulin resistance across various cardiovascular settings. Elevated CTI is associated with a higher incidence of CHD in metabolically heterogeneous individuals16. It has also been linked to higher risks of CVD incidence, HF, and both CVD-specific and all-cause mortality in general populations17. In high-risk adults with coexisting CHD and type 2 DM, the CTI independently forecasted long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, demonstrating linear dose–response relationships across quartiles28. However, evidence focusing on MASLD populations is lacking. Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating a graded, linear association between CTI and incident HF in a MASLD-specific cohort over long-term follow-up. Considering the dynamic nature of inflammatory and metabolic disturbances, we incorporated a time-updated CTI (CumCTI) to capture longitudinal changes. The association between CumCTI and HF risk remained consistent with that observed for baseline CTI, further reinforcing a robust link between the CTI and HF in the MASLD population.

MASLD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with major cardiovascular consequences. Its defining features including chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance. These factors drive adverse cardiac remodeling, diastolic dysfunction, and increased HF risk12. Proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α promote myocardial fibrosis, impair diastolic relaxation, and increase ventricular stiffness4. In parallel, insulin resistance induces lipotoxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction, alters substrate utilization, and weakens cardiac energetics and contractile reserve29. These effects are compounded by endothelial dysfunction and microvascular rarefaction, both of which are reported in MASLD and are linked to impaired diastolic function30. Our study revealed that the composite CTI, integrating TyG as an insulin resistance surrogate and hs-CRP as a systemic inflammation marker, was linearly associated with HF risk in MASLD patients, independent of the CMRF. Although a previous study suggested that MASLD affects blood pressure regulation and increases vascular resistance31, our mediation analyses revealed that SBP and DBP account for only a small proportion of the CTI–HF association (3.7% and 2.7%, respectively). These findings suggest that hemodynamic factors may explain only a limited part of the relationship between cumulative metabolic–inflammatory burden and HF risk. Notably, sensitivity analyses using mediational E-values indicated that relatively modest unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding could potentially explain the observed indirect effects, suggesting that these mediation estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The small mediation proportion suggests that other non-hemodynamic mechanisms may play a more substantial role in the CTI–HF association, potentially involving chronic low-grade inflammation, hepatic metabolic dysfunction, and insulin resistance. Accordingly, strategies for cardiovascular risk reduction in MASLD may need to extend beyond blood pressure control toward multidomain interventions addressing metabolic dysfunction and systemic inflammation. We further explored whether the two components of CTI, TyG and hs-CRP, exhibited reciprocal mediation. In contrast to previous study suggesting mutual mediation between these markers in broader CVD populations32, we did not observe evidence of such mediation in the MASLD cohort. Because CTI is mathematically derived from TyG and hs-CRP, analyses examining mediation between these components may involve a conceptual part–whole relationship. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and primarily intended to provide preliminary insights rather than definitive causal inference.Our findings suggest that metabolic-inflammatory and insulin-resistance pathways may operate largely in parallel rather than sequentially in MASLD-related HF risk. These observations support a multidimensional model of HF risk in MASLD, in which cumulative metabolic and inflammatory burden contributes through multiple interconnected pathways. Future studies incorporating advanced imaging, biomarker profiling, and longitudinal mechanistic assessments may help clarify the biological pathways linking metabolic inflammation to myocardial dysfunction.Inflammatory biomarkers are increasingly acknowledged as independent predictors of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD patients. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio, and systemic immune-inflammation index reflect systemic immune activation and vascular inflammation, which are associated with myocardial injury and cardiovascular events33. In parallel, TyG is an established, simple marker for insulin resistance and has been associated with increased CVD incidence and mortality among individuals with MASLD34. Recently, TyG-related indices, including TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, were reported to improve the prediction accuracy by integrating adiposity indices and metabolic status35,36. Building on this evidence, our study introduced the CTI, which integrates TyG and hs-CRP to capture both metabolic dysfunction and inflammation. In our cohort, CumCTI showed a clear graded association with HF risk and provided statistically significant improvements in model discrimination and reclassification when added to conventional cardiovascular risk factors. However, the magnitude of improvement was modest, as indicated by the small increase in the C-index and the limited gain in time-dependent AUC. Although the continuous NRI suggested improved reclassification, this metric has been noted to be relatively liberal and may overestimate reclassification performance. Moreover, calibration analyses indicated that the prediction models demonstrated reasonable agreement between predicted and observed HF risk. Therefore, these findings suggest that CumCTI may provide complementary prognostic information reflecting cumulative metabolic–inflammatory burden, but its incremental predictive value beyond established risk factors appears limited. Direct comparison with established HF risk prediction tools, such as the PCP-HF or ARIC HF risk scores, would further clarify the clinical positioning of CumCTI within existing risk prediction frameworks. However, several variables required to calculate these scores were not available in our dataset, precluding such comparison. Future studies should evaluate whether incorporating CumCTI into established HF risk prediction models meaningfully improve risk stratification in MASLD populations. Nevertheless, CTI may serve as a complementary marker for early risk assessment by jointly reflecting metabolic disturbance and inflammatory burden.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that MASLD confers elevated cardiovascular risk, with particularly strong effects observed in younger populations37. A nationwide cohort from South Korea revealed that MASLD notably increased the incidence of myocardial infarction, stroke, and HF among adults aged 20–39 years, irrespective of traditional risk factors38. In a separate study, Wang et al. showed that MASLD associated with overweight and obesity in young adults predicted increased coronary artery calcification later in life, suggesting that early metabolic–inflammatory insults accelerate subclinical atherosclerosis39. Likewise, Wu et al. reported pronounced age- and sex-specific effects, showing that MASLD was associated with greater HF risk in younger individuals, underscoring early-life vulnerability40. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that CumCTI poses a greater risk to participants under 60 years of age, with a more pronounced risk gradient than in older adults. These results suggest that prolonged metabolic–inflammatory exposure may exert disproportionate harm earlier in the life course, when competing risks are fewer and cardiometabolic plasticity is greater. This underscores the need for early identification and intervention. Future studies should evaluate whether modifying CTI trajectories through lifestyle or pharmacological strategies can attenuate long-term cardiovascular risk and inform the incorporation of CTI into risk prediction algorithms for MASLD populations.

Several strengths enhance the credibility of our results. First, it is based on a large-scale, prospective Kailuan cohort with a long median follow-up of 16.0 years, providing robust evidence to examine the association between CTI and incident HF. Second, we applied multiple rigorous statistical approaches, including Cox regression, RCS, Kaplan–Meier survival curves, incremental predictive performance assessments, and mediation analyses. The consistent results across sensitivity analyses enhances the credibility of our findings. Third, this study offers the first systematic investigation of the CumCTI in predicting HF. Our findings indicate that the long-term burden of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance has greater prognostic value, expanding current knowledge and highlighting novel implications for risk stratification in MASLD populations. Nevertheless, some limitations should be noted. First, CTI and related indices were measured at baseline and selected follow-up points, which may not fully capture their dynamic changes. Although cumulative exposure was evaluated, potential measurement error cannot be completely excluded. Second, the study population consisted predominantly of male employees and retirees (79.2%) from an industrial community in northern China and was ethnically homogeneous, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations. Third, the CumCTI analysis was restricted to participants who attended all three examination waves and had complete data for CTI-related variables, while individuals who developed HF during the exposure-accumulation window were excluded to preserve temporality. Although baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants were broadly comparable, potential selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out, particularly if non-participation or missingness was related to changes in health status over time or other unmeasured factors. Fourth, even after adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. E value analyses suggest that only relatively strong unmeasured confounders would be sufficient to fully explain the observed associations, supporting the robustness of the main findings. In addition, the mediation analyses rely on the sequential ignorability assumption, which cannot be fully verified in observational studies. Therefore, the estimated mediation effects should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, due to database limitations, we were unable to completely exclude underlying structural heart disease or cardiomyopathy. However, sensitivity analyses excluding participants with prior myocardial infarction, stroke, or atrial fibrillation yielded materially similar results, which partly alleviates concerns regarding potential residual confounding from underlying cardiovascular conditions. Finally, outcome ascertainment relied on hospitalization records and ICD codes, which may have caused misclassification or underreporting of HF, particularly subclinical cases. Furthermore, the lack of information on HF subtypes (HFrEF and HFpEF) limited the interpretation of the results and should be addressed in future studies. Taken together, our findings underscore the potential value of the CTI for HF risk prediction in individuals with MASLD, but further validation across diverse populations and mechanistic studies are warranted to facilitate clinical translation.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrated that higher CTI, particularly cumulative burden, was independently associated with incident HF among individuals with MASLD. CTI, which integrates systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, showed superior predictive value over its individual components and enhanced risk discrimination when added to conventional models. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of chronic cardiometabolic stress and suggest the potential utility of CTI for early cardiovascular risk stratification in individuals with MASLD. Additional validation and interventional studies are warranted to assess whether targeting the metabolic–inflammatory pathways captured by CTI can mitigate long-term HF risk.
