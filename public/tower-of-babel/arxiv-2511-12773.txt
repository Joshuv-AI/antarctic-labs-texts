On planar sections of the dodecahedron
Andreas Thom

Abstract
. In the analysis of three-dimensional biological microstructures such as organoids, microscopy frequently yields two-dimensional optical sections without access to their orientation. Motivated by the question of whether such random planar sections determine the underlying three-dimensional structure, we investigate a discrete analogue in which the ambient structure is the vertex set of a Platonic solid and the observed data are congruence classes of planar intersections. For the regular dodecahedron with vertex set V V , we define the planar statistic of a subset X ⊆ V X\subseteq V of vertices as the distribution of isometry types of inclusions Π ∩ X ⊆ V \Pi\cap X\subseteq V , and ask whether this statistic determines X X up to isometry. We show that this is not the case: there exist two non-isometric 7 7 -element subsets with identical planar statistics. As a consequence, there exists two polytopes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , whose distribution of isometry classes of two-dimensional intersections is identical, while the polytopes are not themselves isometric. This result is an analogue of classical non-uniqueness phenomena in geometric tomography.



1.
Introduction

High-resolution imaging techniques in biological systems frequently produce planar sections of inherently three-dimensional structures. A notable example appears in organoid research, where multicellular aggregates are cultivated in three-dimensional media, yet standard microscopy visualizes them in two-dimensional optical slices,
[
2
,
3
,
6
]
. When we think of one two-dimensional slice through a three-dimensional space filled with more or less identical objects each in a random position and orientation, then we effectively obtain a distribution of random slices of one fixed object without information on what slice exactly we are looking at.
More precisely, the resulting observed data consists of a distribution of planar point configurations or shapes up to planar congruence. A fundamental question, both theoretically and for interpretation of imaging data, is whether this distribution encodes the underlying three-dimensional structure uniquely. The problem is rather different from problems in
X
-ray image analysis or geometric tomography, where the position of the intersecting plane is part of the data that can be analysed.

The aim of this work is to study this question in a highly controlled discrete setting. We fix a finite vertex set
V
of a Platonic solid in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and consider subsets
X\subset V
as discrete models for three-dimensional structures. Planes intersecting
V
produce subset inclusions
\Pi\cap X\subseteq V
of planar sets. Only the Euclidean congruence types of these planar configurations are assumed observable, and each plane is unlabelled. Thus the observable information associated to
X
is merely the distribution of planar congruence classes of all such intersections.

We refer to this distribution as the planar statistic of
X
, see Definition
1
. Our primary question is whether two subsets with identical planar statistics must be isometric in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. By analogy to results of Mallows and Clark
[
4
]
on intercept distributions in the plane, non-uniqueness is expected. We establish such non-uniqueness in the case of the regular dodecahedron, see Theorem
3
.

This also implies that there exist two three-dimensional convex bodies whose distribution of isometry classes of two-dimensional intersections is identical, while the two convex bodies are not themselves isometric, see Theorem
4
.

Let us explain our approach and the relation to previous work in more detail.
Our strategy is closely analogous in spirit to the classical example of Mallows and
Clark
[
4
,
5
]
, who exhibited two convex dodecagons in the
plane with identical chord–length distributions but which are not congruent. Their
construction naturally splits into a combinatorial step and a geometric encoding step.

In the combinatorial step, one starts from the regular octagon and considers its eight
sides or, equivalently, the eight isosceles sectors with apex at the centre. Mallows and
Clark choose two subsets
X
and
Y
of four sides each with the property that the
multiset of unordered pairs of sides in
X
is, up to Euclidean isometry of the octagon,
the same as the multiset of unordered pairs of sides in
Y
. In modern language,
X

and
Y
form a non-congruent homometric pair in the cyclic graph
C_{8}
that records
side adjacency. At this level the construction is purely discrete: one arranges that the
“incidence statistics” of
X
and
Y
inside the octagon agree, even though the subsets
themselves are not related by a symmetry.

The geometric step then promotes this combinatorial configuration to a pair of convex
subsets of the plane. To each side in
X
, respectively in
Y
, one attaches a small congruent
isosceles triangular hat pointing outward, thereby turning the octagon into two convex
dodecagons
K_{X}
and
K_{Y}
. The caps are chosen so small that every chord of

K_{X}
or
K_{Y}
either coincides with a chord of the original octagon, or differs from
it only in a tiny segment inside one of the caps. The contribution of a cap to the
chord–length distribution depends only on which side it sits on, so the matching of
unordered pairs of sides in
X
and
Y
forces the chord–length distributions of

K_{X}
and
K_{Y}
to coincide, although the polygons themselves are not congruent.

The present dodecahedral construction follows the same two-step pattern in a
three-dimensional setting. Here the regular octagon is replaced by the regular
dodecahedron
D
with vertex set
V
, and the role of chords is taken over by planar
sections through vertex–planes. The first, combinatorial step is the search for two
subsets
S,T\subset V
with identical planar statistics in the sense of Definition
1
.
Equivalently, for every vertex–plane
\Pi
the inclusion
\Pi\cap S\subset V
has, up to
isometry of
D
, the same type and multiplicity distribution as
\Pi\cap T\subset V
.
Theorem
3
shows that such a non-congruent pair
(S,T)
exists inside the vertex–plane incidence structure of the dodecahedron.

In the second step we again encode this discrete data into convex bodies by a local
modification along selected faces. Starting from
D
, we cut off a small congruent
triangular pyramid at each vertex
v\in X
, obtaining a polytope
K_{X}
for

X\subseteq V
. When an affine plane
H
meets
D
near some vertices, the local
shape of the section
K_{X}\cap H
in a neighbourhood of those vertices is completely
determined, up to isometry, by the configuration
\Pi\cap X
, where
\Pi
is the nearby
vertex–plane through the intersected vertices. The distribution of isometry classes of
planar sections of
K_{X}
can therefore be written as a convex combination of
contributions coming from the finitely many planar intersection types

\Pi\cap X\subset V
, with coefficients prescribed precisely by the planar statistic

\mathrm{PS}(X)
.

From this viewpoint, Theorem
4
is the exact three-dimensional analogue of the
Mallows–Clark theorem: the equality
\mathrm{PS}(S)=\mathrm{PS}(T)
ensures that the
section distributions of
K_{S}
and
K_{T}
coincide, while the underlying subsets

S
and
T
and hence the patterns of truncated vertices of
D
are not related by
any isometry.

Beyond the discrete-geometric questions addressed here, our section statistics interface naturally with classical inverse problems in geometric tomography and integral geometry
[
1
,
7
]
, where distributions of slices, chords, and projections play a central role. Related stereological ideas go back at least to Wicksell’s corpuscle problem
[
8
]
and remain fundamental in modern microscopy when only section data are available.

Definition 1
.

The

of

\Pi

\mathrm{PS}(X)

\Pi

\mathbb{R}^{3}

\Pi\cap X\subseteq V
, and forgets the actual

\Pi

Remark 2
.

There is a slight variation of this definition which would record just the isometry type of the inclusion
\Pi\cap X\subseteq\Pi\cap V
instead of
\Pi\cap X\subseteq V
. This is almost the same amount of information, except that in the case of a square
Q=\Pi\cap V
with
\Pi\cap X
two adjacent vertices. In this case, the two distinct embeddings of
Q
into
V
give rise to two different planar congruence
classes
[\Pi]_{X}
.

It is a basic observation that the planar statistic of an image of
X
under an isometry of
V

X
; i.e. isometric subsets of
V
yield the same planar statistics. In view of the applications in shape recognition described in the introduction, our initial hope was that the planar statistics
\mathrm{PS}(X)
allows to recover
X
, but we show that this is not the case. Our main result is the following theorem:

Theorem 3
.

There exist two 7-element subsets
S,T\subset V
that are not congruent in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, yet
\mathrm{PS}(S)=\mathrm{PS}(T)
. One such pair is
S=\{0,1,2,3,4,11,17\}
and
T=\{0,1,3,4,5,11,17\}
.

3.
Convex bodies

We will now refine the construction and show that
S,T
can be used to construct three-dimensional polytopes
K_{S}
and
K_{T}
that are not isometric, but their distribution of isometry classes of planar intersections is identitical. Let us first explain more precisely, what that means:
We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the space of compact planar convex sets modulo Euclidean isometries. For each closed convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we consider the natural map

\Phi_{K}\colon\mathcal{A}(3,2)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P},\qquad H\longmapsto[K\cap H]_{\mathrm{iso}},

where
\mathcal{A}(3,2)
is the affine Grassmannian of
planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Note that
\mathcal{A}(3,2)
carries a standard infinite rotation- and
translation-invariant measure
\lambda
and we call the Borel measure
(\Phi_{K})_{*}(\lambda)
on
\mathcal{P}
the distribution of isometry classes of planar sections. Our normalization of
\lambda
is in such a way that
(\Phi_{K})_{*}(\lambda)(\mathcal{P})={\rm vol}(K)
for all compact convex bodies.

Theorem 4
.

There exists three-dimensional polytopes
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, such that

(1)

K
and
L
are not congruent, but

(2)

(\Phi_{K})_{*}(\lambda)=(\Phi_{L})_{*}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular dodecahedron with vertex set
V
, and
let
S,T\subset V
be two subsets as in Theorem
3
: they
are not congruent in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but their planar statistics agree, i.e.
\mathrm{PS}(S)=\mathrm{PS}(T)

as multisets of planar congruence classes in the sense of
Definition
1
.

Let us now explain how we construct polytopes out of
S
and
T
. For each
X\subset V
define a convex polytope
K_{X}
by truncating all vertices

v\in X
by small congruent triangular caps. We will show that
K_{S}
and
K_{T}
are not
congruent in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but the distributions of isometry classes of their
planar sections with respect to the invariant measure on affine planes coincide.

Fix once and for all a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
.
For each vertex
v\in V
let
P_{v}
be the unique plane that intersects the three
edges incident to
v
in points of distance
\varepsilon
from
v
, and denote
by
H_{v}^{-}
the closed half-space bounded by
P_{v}
that contains the barycenter
of
D
. For any subset
X\subset V
we set

K_{X}:=D\cap\bigcap_{v\in X}H_{v}^{-}.

By construction
K_{X}
is obtained from
D
by cutting off, at each
v\in X
, a
small congruent triangular pyramid. In particular, if
g\in\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is an
isometry with
g(K_{S})=K_{T}
, then
g
must map the set of truncated vertices of

K_{S}
onto that of
K_{T}
, and restricts to an isometry
g\colon D\to D
with

g(S)=T
. Since
S
and
T
are not congruent as subsets of
V
by
Theorem
3
, it follows that
K_{S}
and
K_{T}
are not
congruent.

We now compare the distributions of planar sections. For

H\in\mathcal{A}(3,2)
set

m(H):=\bigl|\{v\in V:H\cap B(v,\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset\}\bigr|,

the number of vertex neighbourhoods met by
H
. For each integer
m\geq 0
define

A_{m}:=\{H\in\mathcal{A}(3,2):m(H)=m\},

so that
\mathcal{A}(3,2)=\sqcup_{m\geq 0}A_{m}.

Let
p_{m}:=\lambda(A_{m})
and
\lambda_{m}:=p_{m}^{-1}\,\lambda\!\restriction_{A_{m}}

for all
m
with
p_{m}>0
. Then, we have

\lambda=\sum_{m\geq 0}p_{m}\,\lambda_{m},

a canonical convex decomposition of
\lambda
, and for each
X\subset V
we
obtain a corresponding convex decomposition

\mu_{X}=\sum_{m\geq 0}p_{m}\,\mu_{X,m},\qquad\mu_{X,m}:=(\Phi_{K_{X}})_{*}(\lambda_{m}).

To prove
\mu_{S}=\mu_{T}
it therefore suffices to show that
\mu_{S,m}=\mu_{T,m}
for all
m\geq 0
.

The cases
m=0
and
1
.

If
H\in A_{0}
, then
H
misses all balls
B(v,\varepsilon)
and in particular all caps.
The section
K_{X}\cap H
then coincides with
D\cap H
; it does not depend on
X
. In particular, we can conclude
\mu_{S,0}=\mu_{T,0}.

Similarly, if
H\in A_{1}
, then
H
meets exactly one ball
B(v,\varepsilon)
, say at
v
. The local
shape of the section
K_{X}\cap H
in a neighbourhood of
B(v,\varepsilon)
is completely
determined by the local geometry at
v
and thus only depends on whether
v\in X
or not. Thus, since
D

is vertex-transitive and
|S|=|T|
, we obtain
\mu_{S,1}=\mu_{T,1}
.

The case
m\geq 3
.

Suppose
H\in A_{m}
with
m\geq 3
. By choice of
\varepsilon>0
, this implies that

H
lies in a small neighbourhood of a unique vertex plane

\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with

\Pi\cap V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\},\qquad m\geq 3,

and that the vertices
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
are precisely those whose balls

B(v_{i},\varepsilon)
meet
H
. The local configuration of the section
K_{X}\cap H
is then,
up to isometry, completely determined by the isometry type of the inclusion
\Pi\cap X\subset V

and the position of
H
in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
\Pi
.

The isometry group
D
acts on the set of such local configurations. For
a fixed
X\subset V
the orbits of this action are parametrized precisely by
the planar congruence classes
[\Pi]_{X}
appearing in the planar statistic

\mathrm{PS}(X)
: two vertex planes
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}
give rise to the same local
configuration type if and only if there is an isometry
g
such that
g(V)=V
and

g(\Pi\cap X)=\Pi^{\prime}\cap X
.

Fix a
G
-orbit of local configurations
\mathcal{O}
, i.e. an orbit of a planar inclusion
Z\subset V
with
|Z|\geq 3
. Consider the set of planes
H
whose local configuration belongs to
\mathcal{O}
. By
invariance of
\lambda
and congruence of all caps and faces, the restriction of

\lambda
to this set has the same total mass and the same normalized image
measure under
\Phi_{X}
, independent of
X
; it depends only on
\mathcal{O}
.
Thus for each such orbit
\mathcal{O}
there is a probability measure

\nu_{\mathcal{O}}
on
\mathcal{P}
describing the contribution of one
occurrence of
\mathcal{O}
, and the component
\mu_{X,m}
can be written as a
finite convex combination

\mu_{X,m}=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}c_{\mathcal{O}}(X)\,\nu_{\mathcal{O}},

where the sum runs over all orbits
\mathcal{O}
with
m\geq 3
, and

c_{\mathcal{O}}(X)
is a nonnegative coefficient proportional to the number of
vertex planes whose local configuration with respect to
X
lies in
\mathcal{O}
.
By definition, the multiset of classes
[\Pi]_{X}
in
\mathrm{PS}(X)
records
exactly these multiplicities. Thus the coefficients
c_{\mathcal{O}}(X)
are
completely determined by
\mathrm{PS}(X)
.

Since
\mathrm{PS}(S)=\mathrm{PS}(T)
as multisets of planar congruence
classes, it follows that
c_{\mathcal{O}}(S)=c_{\mathcal{O}}(T)
for every
orbit
\mathcal{O}
, and hence
\mu_{S,m}=\mu_{T,m}
for all
m\geq 3
.

The cases
m=2
.
If
H\in A_{2}
, then
H
meets exactly two balls
B(v,\varepsilon)
, say at vertices

v,w\in V
. The local shape of
K_{X}\cap H
is then, up to isometry, determined by
the orbit type of the unordered pair
\{v,w\}
under the isometry group of

D
together with the membership of
v,w
in
X
. Again, there are only finitely many
such orbit types of unordered pairs in
V
, and for each such type all
corresponding pairs have the same local geometry. Each such pair belongs to various vertex planes with three or more vertices, so its contribution is again
encoded in the planar statistic
\mathrm{PS}(X)
; in particular, the counts of
pairs of each orbit type contained in
X
are determined by
\mathrm{PS}(X)
.
Since
\mathrm{PS}(S)=\mathrm{PS}(T)
, the contributions for
m=2
agree:
\mu_{S,2}=\mu_{T,2}
.

Combining all cases, we have shown that
\mu_{S,m}=\mu_{T,m}
for every
m\geq 0
. Since

\mu_{S}=\sum_{m\geq 0}p_{m}\,\mu_{S,m}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{T}=\sum_{m\geq 0}p_{m}\,\mu_{T,m}

with the same weights
p_{m}
, we conclude that
\mu_{S}=\mu_{T}
. This shows that the
distributions of isometry classes of planar sections of
K_{S}
and
K_{T}
with
respect to
\lambda
coincide, as claimed.
∎
