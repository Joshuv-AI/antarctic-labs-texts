Diverticular bleeding in the third part of the duodenum as a cause of overt obscure gastrointestinal bleeding
Authors: Yasuhiko Hamada, Hayato Nakagawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 65‐year‐old man with a history of chronic renal failure and ischemic heart disease who was receiving aspirin therapy was admitted for recurrent melena. Initial evaluation revealed severe anemia. Other than a diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum, the findings of upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies and computed tomography were unremarkable. Capsule endoscopy detected altered blood in the ileum; however, balloon‐assisted enteroscopy found no definitive source of bleeding. Three years later, he presented with recurrent melena and hemorrhagic shock. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy identified Dieulafoy's lesion within the duodenal diverticulum. Hemostasis was achieved using hemoclips. No recurrence of bleeding was observed during 4 years of follow‐up. The findings from this case highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing obscure gastrointestinal bleeding due to Dieulafoy's lesion in a diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum. Duodenal diverticular bleeding should be included in the differential diagnoses for patients with obscure gastrointestinal bleeding.Keywords: balloon‐assisted enteroscopy, duodenal diverticular bleeding, obscure gastrointestinal bleeding

Introduction

Japanese guidelines define obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) as bleeding of unknown etiology determined after thorough evaluation using both upper and lower gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopies. While OGIB is frequently diagnosed using a combination of video capsule endoscopy (VCE) and balloon‐assisted enteroscopy (BAE), the underlying cause occasionally remains undetected despite the use of these advanced diagnostic techniques. 1

Duodenal diverticula are relatively common, with a prevalence of approximately 22% in autopsy cases. However, bleeding from duodenal diverticula is rare, accounting for only 0.14% of all cases of upper GI bleeding cases. 2 Furthermore, bleeding originating from diverticula located in the third part of the duodenum is rarely reported. 3 This part of the duodenum is challenging to examine using standard upper GI endoscopy, and this can result in misdiagnosis of diverticular bleeding as OGIB. Here we report a case involving a 65‐year‐old man with OGIB caused by diverticular bleeding in the third part of the duodenum.

Case report

A 65‐year‐old man was admitted to our hospital with recurrent melena. His medical history included chronic renal failure and ischemic heart disease, and he was receiving aspirin therapy for the latter. On admission, his vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed pale conjunctiva, suggesting anemia. Laboratory tests indicated a hemoglobin level of 6.8 g/dL. However, both upper and lower GI endoscopies did not reveal any abnormalities. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) also showed no clinically significant findings other than a large diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum (Fig. 1a, arrow). These findings led to a suspicion of bleeding from the small intestine. VCE was performed, and it detected altered blood in the ileum (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, BAE was conducted, confirming the presence of a large diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum. However, detailed observation was hindered by food residue within the diverticulum (Fig. 1c, arrow). Eventually, no clear bleeding source was identified throughout the GI tract. The patient received blood transfusions and was discharged after no further episodes of melena occurred over a period of 10 days.

(a) Abdominal computed tomography shows no clinically significant findings, except for a large diverticulum in the third portion of the duodenum (arrow). (b) Video capsule endoscopy shows altered (coffee‐ground‐like) blood in the ileum. (c) Balloon‐assisted enteroscopy shows a large diverticulum with food residue in the third part of the duodenum (arrow). (d) Abdominal computed tomography shows significant hemorrhage originating from the diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum (arrow). (e) Intraoperatively, fresh blood can be seen pulsating from Dieulafoy's lesion inside the diverticulum (arrow). (f) Successful hemostasis is achieved using hemoclips.

Three years later, the patient presented to the emergency department with recurrent melena. At this time, his vital signs were unstable, with a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg, heart rate of 135 beats per minute, and respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute. Laboratory tests revealed a hemoglobin level of 5.6 g/dL. CT revealed significant hemorrhage originating from the diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum (Fig. 1d, arrow). Following blood transfusions, upper GI endoscopy was performed, and no bleeding source was detected in the esophagus, stomach, and second part of the duodenum. However, the third part of the duodenum showed blood residue and the previously noted large diverticulum. Moreover, fresh blood was seen pulsating from Dieulafoy's lesion inside the diverticulum (Fig. 1e, arrow). Hemostasis was successfully achieved using hemoclips (Fig. 1f). We concluded that the diverticular bleeding was the cause of both the current and previous episodes of overt OGIB. The patient was discharged 2 days later without further bleeding, and no recurrence of overt OGIB was observed during the subsequent 4‐year follow‐up period.

Discussion

We reported a case wherein diverticular bleeding in the third part of the duodenum was identified as a cause of overt OGIB after a follow‐up period of 3 years.

Duodenal diverticular bleeding is a potentially life‐threatening condition that can be challenging to diagnose and manage. It typically affects middle‐aged or older adults and can cause massive hemorrhage. 4 Diagnosis often requires multiple endoscopic examinations, and CT can be helpful when endoscopy fails to identify the bleeding source. 5 Treatment options include endoscopic interventions such as application of hemoclips or electrocoagulation and transcatheter arterial embolization. The choice of treatment depends on the patient's condition and the severity of bleeding. 4 In some cases, a combination of these modalities may be necessary to achieve definitive hemostasis. While nonsurgical management is usually effective, surgery may be required in cases where conservative approaches fail.

Although OGIB is commonly associated with bleeding from the small intestine, it originates from areas outside the small intestine in 12.5%–18.4% cases. 6 Duodenal diverticular bleeding is a rare but recognized cause of OGIB, accounting for approximately 1.2%–4.9% cases. 7 Diverticular bleeding in the third part of the duodenum is particularly uncommon, although it can lead to significant bleeding. In patients with overt bleeding from a diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum, identifying the exact source of bleeding can be difficult because bleeding may occasionally cease spontaneously before or during endoscopic or radiological evaluations. Thus, diverticular bleeding in the third part of the duodenum should be considered in the diagnosis of a patient who presents with overt OGIB.

BAE is a valuable diagnostic and therapeutic tool for OGIB, with a reported diagnostic yield of 75.7%. 8 However, a meta‐analysis demonstrated that the rebleeding rate in patients with negative BAE was 29.1%. 9 Currently, no established guidelines outline the optimal management of OGIB cases following a negative result in BAE. Previous studies have identified that factors such as the need for blood transfusions and recurrent bleeding episodes correlate with a higher risk of overt rebleeding after initial negative BAE. 10 Therefore, OGIB cases with such factors should be closely followed up. In the event of rebleeding, prompt endoscopic evaluation can facilitate the identification of the bleeding source and facilitate timely therapeutic intervention in select cases. In addition, repeat CT during active rebleeding has been shown to aid in identification of the bleeding source, although CT shows a generally lower diagnostic yield for OGIB than does VCE or BAE.

In conclusion, the findings from this case highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing OGIB due to Dieulafoy's lesion in a diverticulum in the third part of the duodenum. Duodenal diverticular bleeding should be included in the differential diagnoses for patients with OGIB.
