Clusters, twistors and stability conditions I
Tom Bridgeland, Helge Ruddat

Abstract
. We consider a quiver Q Q of ADE type and use cluster combinatorics to define two complex manifolds 𝒮 \mathcal{S} and ℒ \mathcal{L} . The space 𝒮 \mathcal{S} can be identified with a quotient of the space of stability conditions on the CY 3 category associated to Q Q . The space ℒ \mathcal{L} has a canonical map to the complex cluster Poisson space 𝒳 ℂ \mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}} which we prove to be a local homeomorphism. When Q Q is of type A A , we give a geometric description of the spaces 𝒮 \mathcal{S} and ℒ \mathcal{L} as moduli spaces of meromorphic quadratic differentials and projective structures respectively. In the sequel paper we will introduce a space π : Z → ℂ \pi\colon Z\to\mathbb{C} whose fibre over over a point ϵ ∈ ℂ \epsilon\in\mathbb{C} is isomorphic to 𝒮 \mathcal{S} when ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 and to ℒ \mathcal{L} otherwise. The problem of constructing sections of this map gives a geometric approach to the Donaldson–Thomas Riemann–Hilbert problems of [ 7 ] .



1.
Introduction

Let
Q
be a quiver whose underlying unoriented graph is a simply-laced Dynkin diagram. We denote by
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
the complex analytic space associated to the cluster Poisson variety
[
18
]
of
Q
. We refer to it simply as the
complex cluster space
, and view it as a possibly non-Hausdorff complex manifold. It is obtained by gluing overlapping copies of the algebraic tori
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
using certain birational maps.
The combinatorics of this process is controlled by the cluster exchange graph via the standard mutation formulae
[
18
,
19
]
which will be recalled below.

The aim of this paper is to use the same combinatorics to define two other complex manifolds: the
cluster stability space

\mathcal{S}
and the
log cluster space

\mathcal{L}
.
In the sequel
[
13
]
we will define a third complex manifold
\mathcal{Z}
, which we call the
cluster twistor space
. It comes equipped with a map
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
whose fibre over a point
\epsilon\in\mathbb{C}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
when
\epsilon=0
and to
\mathcal{L}
otherwise.

The cluster stability space
\mathcal{S}
gives a purely combinatorial approach to the space of stability conditions
[
4
,
5
]
of the CY
3
triangulated category associated to
Q
. On the other hand, the log cluster space
\mathcal{L}
comes equipped with a map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
which we prove to be a local homeomorphism. Thus, to first approximation, our work exhibits the space of stability conditions as a kind of specialisation of the complex cluster space.

When
Q
is of type
A
or
D
we can give descriptions of our spaces
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
in the same vein as Fock and Goncharov’s description of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
as a space of framed local systems
[
17
]
. Work of one of us with Smith
[
6
]
shows that the space
\mathcal{S}
can be identified with a space of framed meromorphic quadratic differentials on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. More recent work of Gupta and Mj
[
26
]
exhibits
\mathcal{L}
as a space of framed meromorphic projective structures. The map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
then sends a framed projective structure to the framed local system defined by its monodromy and Stokes data.

One motivation for our work is a project which aims to encode the Donaldson-Thomas (DT) invariants of a CY
3
category in a complex hyperkähler structure on the total space of the tangent bundle of the space of stability conditions
[
8
,
10
,
11
]
. Any complex hyperkähler manifold has a twistor space
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and in the case of the CY
3
category associated to an ADE quiver we expect that our cluster twistor space is the restriction of this twistor space to
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

1.1.
Summary of the spaces

Let us introduce the semi-closed and closed upper half-planes

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leqslant\arg(z)<\pi\},\qquad\widebar{\mathcal{H}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)\geqslant 0\}.

The complex cluster space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
is constructed by gluing overlapping copies of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
using birational maps of the form

(1)

x_{j}\mapsto\begin{cases}x_{j}\,(1+x_{i}^{+1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
x_{j}\,(1+x_{i}^{-1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
x_{i}^{-1}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

The cluster stability space
\mathcal{S}
is built by gluing copies of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
along their boundaries using maps

(2)

w_{j}\mapsto\begin{cases}w_{j}+v_{ij}\cdot w_{i}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0\text{ and }\operatorname{Re}(w_{i})\geqslant 0,\\
w_{j}-v_{ij}\cdot w_{i}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\leqslant 0\text{ and }\operatorname{Re}(w_{i})\leqslant 0,\\
w_{j}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\operatorname{Re}(v_{ij}w_{i})<0,\\
-w_{i}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

The log cluster space
\mathcal{L}
is obtained by gluing copies of
\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
along theirboundaries using maps

(3)

w_{j}\mapsto\begin{cases}w_{j}+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{+w_{i}}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w_{j}+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{-w_{i}}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-w_{i}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

Setting
x_{i}=\exp(w_{i})
defines a continuous map

\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

In the sequel to this paper we will construct the cluster twistor space
\mathcal{Z}
using maps

(4)

w_{j}\mapsto\begin{cases}w_{j}+\epsilon\cdot v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{+w_{i}/\epsilon}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w_{j}+\epsilon\cdot v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{-w_{i}/\epsilon}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-w_{i}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

This reproduces (
3
) when
\epsilon=1
and limits to (
2
) as
\epsilon\to 0
along the positive real axis.

1.2.
Statement of results

Recall that
Q
is a quiver whose underlying unoriented graph is a simply-laced Dynkin diagram. We denote by
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
the positive real cluster Poisson variety, and by
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
the tropical cluster variety. Then
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
is a topological manifold with a collection of homeomorphisms to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
is a piecewise linear (PL) manifold with a collection of PL homeomorphisms to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There is a well-defined subset
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{Z}^{t}}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
of integral tropical points.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the bounded derived category of the CY
3
Ginzburg dg-algebra
\Pi_{3}(Q)
. It is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear CY
3
triangulated category with a distinguished bounded t-structure whose heart
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{D}
is equivalent to the category of representations of
Q
. We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})
the group of
\mathbb{C}
-linear triangulated auto-equivalences of
\mathcal{D}
, and by
\operatorname{Br}(\mathcal{D})
the subgroup generated by spherical twists in the simple objects of
\mathcal{A}
. The space of stability conditions
\operatorname{Stab}(\mathcal{D})
is a complex manifold which carries a natural action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})
.

Each of the cluster spaces defined above carries a natural action of a discrete group
\mathbb{G}
called the cluster modular group. In the approach to cluster theory using CY
3
triangulated categories, this group is the quotient
\mathbb{G}=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})/\operatorname{Br}(\mathcal{D})
. It therefore has a distinguished element
T\in\mathbb{G}
, known as the DT element, corresponding to the shift functor
[1]\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{D})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

The cluster stability space
\mathcal{S}
is a complex manifold with the following properties:

(i)

as a topological space,
\mathcal{S}
can be naturally identified with an open subset of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
,

(ii)

there is an integral affine structure on
\mathcal{S}
and an action of the additive group
\mathbb{C}
,

(iii)

the action of
\pi i\in\mathbb{C}
coincides with the action of the DT element
T\in\mathbb{G}
,

(iv)

as a complex manifold,
\mathcal{S}
is isomorphic to the quotient
\operatorname{Stab}(\mathcal{D})/\operatorname{Br}(\mathcal{D})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The log cluster space
\mathcal{L}
is a complex manifold with the following properties:

(i)

as a topological space,
\mathcal{L}
can be naturally identified with
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
,

(ii)

the map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
is holomorphic and a local homeomorphism,

(iii)

the fibre of
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}
over a point of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
is naturally identified with the set
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{Z}^{t}}
,

(iv)

there is a
C^{1}
-homeomorphism
h\colon\mathcal{L}\to T\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
, compatible with the projections to
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
.

To prove Theorem
1.2
we apply the inverse function theorem to the composition
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\circ h^{-1}
of the maps from (ii) and (iv). To prove that the derivative of this map is an isomorphism we first construct a complete fan in each tangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
. This is obtained by taking the inverse images of the positive orthants under the derivatives of the cluster charts. To show that these cones fit together to give a fan we continuously deform the gluing maps (
1
) in a one-parameter family so that at
t=0
they become monomial. We then show that this deformation induces what we call a combinatorially constant family of fans.

1.3.
Description in type A

Consider the case when
Q
is an orientation of the A
n
Dynkin diagram, and set
m=n+3
. Similar results can be obtained when the quiver
Q
has type
D
. The analogue of the polynomial (
5
) is a rational function with a double pole at
x=0
and no other finite poles.

Work of one of us with Smith
[
6
]
shows that the space
\mathcal{S}
can be identified with a space of framed meromorphic quadratic differentials on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a single pole at infinity. More explicitly, let
\mathcal{P}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
denote the space of polynomials of the form

(5)

q(x)=x^{n+1}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0},\qquad a_{i}\in\mathbb{C},

and let
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
be the open subset of polynomials without repeated zeroes. Associated to such a polynomial is a quadratic differential
q(x)\,dx^{\otimes 2}
. Using the results of
[
6
, Section 12.1]
we have

Theorem 1.3
.

(i)

There is an isomorphism of complex manifolds

\mathcal{S}\cong\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

(ii)

The flat co-ordinates for the integral affine structure on
\mathcal{S}
are functions of the form

z_{\gamma}=\int_{\gamma}\sqrt{q(x)}\,dx,

where
\gamma
is a cycle on the Riemann surface of
\sqrt{q(x)}
.

(iii)

The action of
s\in\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{S}
corresponds to the map
a_{k}\mapsto e^{2s(m-k-2)/m}a_{k}
.

Given a polynomial
q\in\mathcal{P}
, we can consider the differential equation in the complex plane

y^{\prime\prime}(x)=q(x)y(x),

where primes denote differentiation with respect to
x
.
The space of solutions to this equation is a two-dimensional vector space
V\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
. For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
we can consider the open sector

S_{j}=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\arg(z)-2\pi j/m|<\pi/m\big\}.

Sibuya
[
35
]
showed that there is a unique one-dimensional subspace
\ell_{j}\subset V
consisting of solutions which decay exponentially as
x\to\infty
in this sector.

Results of Fock and Goncharov
[
17
]
imply that the cluster space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
can be identified with an open subset of framed local systems on a disc with
m
marked points on the boundary. More explicitly, it is proved in
[
1
, Section 3]
that

(6)

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}\cong\big\{p\colon\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}:\hash(\operatorname{Im}(p))>2\text{ and }p(j)\neq p(j+1)\text{ for all $j\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}$}\big\}\big/\operatorname{PGL}_{2}.

In Section
12
we use work of Gupta and Mj
[
26
]
to prove the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

(i)

There is an isomorphism of complex manifolds

\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{P}
.

(ii)

Under the above identifications, the map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
sends a polynomial
q\in\mathcal{P}
to the point of the quotient space (
6
) defined by
p(j)=\ell_{j}\in\mathbb{P}(V)
.

We note that relations between moduli spaces of projective structures and log cluster transformations can already be seen in the preprint
[
16
]
which appeared ten years before the invention of clusters!

1.4.
Cluster twistor space

The cluster twistor space
\mathcal{Z}
will be constructed in the sequel
[
13
]
to this paper. It comes equipped with a map
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
. As with all the other spaces, it carries an action of the cluster modular group
\mathbb{G}
, and this action preserves the map
\pi
.

Theorem 1.5
.

The cluster twistor space is a complex manifold
\mathcal{Z}
with the following properties:

(i)

the map
\pi\colon Z\to\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic submersion,

(ii)

there is an action of
\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{Z}
such that
\pi(s\cdot x)=e^{s}\cdot\pi(x)
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

(iii)

the action of
\pi i\in\mathbb{C}
coincides with the action of the DT element
T\in\mathbb{G}
,

(iv)

the fibre of
\pi
over
0\in\mathbb{C}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
,

(v)

the fibres of
\pi
over points of
\mathbb{C}^{*}\subset\mathbb{C}
are isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
.

The motivation for our constructions comes from a line of research which aims to encode the DT invariants of a CY
3
triangulated category in a geometric structure on its space of stability conditions. This proceeds by considering a class of non-linear Riemann–Hilbert (RH) problems involving piecewise holomorphic maps
X\colon\mathbb{C}^{*}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
with prescribed jumps along a collection of rays defined by the DT invariants
[
7
,
8
]
. In cases when these problems have well-behaved solutions, the end result is expected to be a complex hyperkähler structure on the tangent bundle of the space of stability conditions
[
10
,
11
]
.

Penrose
[
33
]
showed that a complex hyperkähler structure has an associated twistor space
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and that moreover the hyperkähler structure can be reconstructed as the moduli space of certain sections of
\pi
known as twistor lines. The reader can consult
[
25
, Section 9.2]
for a precise mathematical statement and detailed references.
The claim is then that the cluster twistor space of Theorem
1.5
is the restriction of the twistor space of the expected complex hyperkähler structure to the subset
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Moreover the solutions to the RH problems describe the twistor lines in this twistor space.

This picture is closely related to the work of Gaiotto, Moore and Neitzke
[
22
]
who explained that solutions to a similar class of RH problems can be interpreted geometrically as holomorphic maps
\mathbb{C}^{*}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
expressed in cluster charts which vary according to the argument of
\epsilon\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. We are attempting to go further and argue that the condition imposed on the solution to the RH problem
X(\epsilon)
in the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
can also be repackaged geometrically by requiring that the holomorphic map extends over
\epsilon=0
to produce a section of the map
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
.

We view the CY
3
categories associated to ADE quivers as being the simplest non-trivial examples of CY
3
categories. In these cases all information about the DT invariants is encoded in the combinatorics of the various cluster charts on the space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and it is therefore reasonable to hope for a complete description of the twistor space in terms of cluster combinatorics. It is worth noting however that even in the very simplest case of the A
2
quiver, the twistor lines and the complex hyperkähler structure are highly non-trivial, and are related to isomonodromic deformations and the Painlevé I equation
[
9
]
.

2.
Exchange graphs

In the first four sections we review some standard background material on clusters and mutations originally due to Fock and Goncharov
[
17
,
18
]
, and Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
19
,
20
]
. We shall take as our basic reference the recent monograph by Nakanishi
[
32
]
. Our point-of-view is strongly influenced by the connection with CY

2.1.
Notation and conventions

An
oriented graph

consists of a set of vertices
V
, a set of edges
I
, and source and target maps
s,t\colon I\to V
. We write
i\colon a\to b\in I
to mean that
i\in I
with
s(i)=a
and
t(i)=b
.
We denote by
I(a)=s^{-1}(a)\subset I
the set of edges emanating from a given vertex
a\in V
. We say that a graph is
finite
if the sets
V
and
I
are finite,
locally-finite
if the sets
s^{-1}(a)
and
t^{-1}(a)
are finite for all
a\in V
, and
connected
if any two vertices can be connected by a finite sequence of edges. By viewing an unoriented edge as a pair of oppositely-oriented edges we define an
unoriented graph
to be an oriented graph equipped with the extra data of a fixed-point free involution
\epsilon\colon I\to I
satisfying
t(i)=s(\epsilon(i))
.
By an
orientation
of an unoriented graph we mean a decomposition
I=I_{+}\sqcup I_{-}
such that
\epsilon(I_{+})=I_{-}
.

By a
quiver
we mean a finite oriented graph. By the
opposite
of a quiver
Q
we mean the quiver
\widebar{Q}
with the same underlying unoriented graph but equipped with the opposite orientation. A quiver
Q
is said to be
2-acyclic
if it has no loops or oriented 2-cycles.

Given a finite set
I
, we consider the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}_{I}=\left\{\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}\cdot e_{i}:n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
with its canonical basis
\{e_{i}:i\in I\}
. We identify the dual group
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}_{I},\mathbb{Z})
with the set
\mathbb{Z}^{I}
of functions
w\colon I\to\mathbb{Z}
by sending a homomorphism
\theta\colon\mathbb{Z}_{I}\to\mathbb{Z}
to the function defined by
w(i)=\theta(e_{i})
. We denote by
\{e_{i}^{*}:i\in I\}
the dual basis of the group
\mathbb{Z}^{I}
.
We write
\mathbb{C}(I)
for the field of fractions of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[I]
of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{I}
, and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(I)
for the image of the basis element
e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{I}
under the canonical inclusion
\mathbb{Z}_{I}\subset\mathbb{C}(I)
. Thus
\mathbb{C}(I)
is the field of rational functions
\mathbb{C}(x_{i}:i\in I)
in indeterminates
x_{i}
indexed by the elements
i\in I
.

We define the real vector spaces
\mathbb{R}_{I}
and
\mathbb{R}^{I}
in the same way as the groups
\mathbb{Z}_{I}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{I}
. By an
orthant
in
\mathbb{R}^{I}
we mean a subset of the form
\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{I}:\kappa(i)\cdot w(i)\geqslant 0\ \forall i\in I\}
, where
\kappa\colon I\to\{\pm 1\}
is some collection of signs.
By a
cone
in
\mathbb{R}^{I}
we always mean a polyhedral cone. We denote by
\langle\sigma\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{I}
the linear subspace spanned by a cone
\sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{I}
.

2.2.
Exchange graphs

We base our treatment of cluster combinatorics on the following slightly non-standard definitions. The relation to the usual terminology of cluster theory will be explained in the next subsection.

Definition 2.1
.

An
exchange graph

\mathbb{E}
is a connected, locally-finite, unoriented graph
(V,I,s,t,\epsilon)
as defined above, equipped with extra data
(v,\rho,\phi)
as follows.

(E1)

Exchange matrices.
For each vertex
s\in V
there is a collection of integers
\{v_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}:i,j\in I(s)\}
satisfying
v_{i,j}=-v_{j,i}
. We often write
v_{ij}=v_{i,j}
.

(E2)

Edge bijections.
For each edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
there is a bijection
\rho_{i}\colon I(s_{1})\to I(s_{2})
such that
\rho_{i}(i)=\epsilon(i)
and
\rho_{\epsilon(i)}=\rho_{i}^{-1}
.

(E3)

Field isomorphisms.
For each pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
there is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear field isomorphism
\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}\colon\mathbb{C}(I(s_{2}))\to\mathbb{C}(I(s_{1}))
satisfying
\phi(s_{1},s_{3})^{*}=\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}\circ\phi(s_{2},s_{3})^{*}
.

This data should satisfy the following conditions:

(E4)

Mutation rules.
If
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
is an edge then for all
j,k\in I(s_{1})

v_{jk}-v_{ji}\,v_{ik}&\text{ if }i\notin\{j,k\}\text{ and }v_{ji}\leqslant 0\text{ and }v_{ik}\leqslant 0,\\
v_{jk}&\text{ if }i\notin\{j,k\}\text{ and }v_{ji}\,v_{ik}\leqslant 0,\\

(8)

\hskip-46.00012pt\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}(x_{\rho_{i}(j)})=\begin{cases}x_{j}\,(1+x_{i}^{+1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
x_{j}\,(1+x_{i}^{-1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
x_{i}^{-1}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

(E5)

Minimality
. Suppose we are given two vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
and a bijection
h\colon I(s_{1})\to I(s_{2})
such that
\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}(x_{h(i)})=x_{i}
for all
i\in I(s_{1})
. Then in fact
s_{1}=s_{2}
and
h
is the identity.

Note that, unlike the field isomorphisms in (E3), the edge bijections in (E2) are not required to satisfy a cocycle property. Thus, in general, given a cycle
s=s_{0}\to s_{1}\to\cdots\to s_{n}=s
in the underlying graph of
\mathbb{E}
, the composition of the bijections
I(s_{i})\to I(s_{i+1})
defines a non-trivial permutation of the set
I(s)
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
isomorphism of exchange graphs

g\colon\mathbb{E}_{1}\to\mathbb{E}_{2}
is an isomorphism of the underlying unoriented graphs which preserves the extra data specified in (E1) – (E3).

To spell this out, the isomorphism of unoriented graphs is defined by a pair of bijections
g\colon V_{1}\to V_{2}
and
g\colon I_{1}\to I_{2}
satisfying

g(s(i))=s(g(i)),\qquad g(t(i))=t(g(i)),\qquad\epsilon(g(i))=g(\epsilon(i)),

for all edges
i\in I
. We then further require that
for all vertices
s\in V
and edges
i,j\in I(s)

v_{g(i),g(j)}=v_{i,j},\qquad\rho_{g(i)}(g(j))=g(\rho_{i}(j)).

Connecting an arbitrary pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
by a chain of edges and applying the mutation rule (E4) it then follows that

\phi(g(s_{1}),g(s_{2}))^{*}\circ g_{s_{2}}=g_{s_{1}}\circ\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}

where for all vertices
s\in V
the map
g_{s}\colon\mathbb{C}(I(s))\to\mathbb{C}(I(g(s)))
is defined by
g_{s}(x_{i})=x_{g(i)}
.

2.3.
Relation to
Y
-patterns

Let us briefly explain the relation of the above definition to the usual notions of cluster theory.

Suppose we are given a
Y
-pattern as in
[
32
, Definition I.2.13]
with values in the universal semifield
[
32
, Example I.1.5(a)]
on
n
generators. We can then define an exchange graph as in Definition
2.1
by quotienting the
n
-regular tree
\mathbb{T}_{n}
by the group of automorphisms of the graph
\mathbb{T}_{n}
which preserve the
Y
-pattern up to permutation. More precisely, we quotient by automorphisms
g\colon\mathbb{T}_{n}\to\mathbb{T}_{n}
such that for each vertex
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
the coefficient tuples corresponding to the vertices
t
and
g(t)
differ by a permutation. Note that by
[
32
, Theorem I.4.27]
any such automorphism preserves the entire cluster pattern up to permutation.

The variables
x_{i}
appearing in (E4) are what are called coefficients in
[
32
]
(and denoted
y_{i}
) and we set
v_{ij}=-b_{ij}
. The edge bijections
\rho_{i}
of (E2) are induced by the canonical identifications between the edges incident with any vertex of
\mathbb{T}_{n}
. The minimality property (E5) holds because an easy argument shows that if the coefficient tuples corresponding to two vertices
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
differ by a permutation then there is an automorphism
g\colon\mathbb{T}_{n}\to\mathbb{T}_{n}
satisfying
g(t)=t^{\prime}
which preserves the
Y
-pattern up to permutation.

In the opposite direction, suppose given an exchange graph as in Definition
2.1
. For each vertex
s\in V
we can consider the semifield
\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{sf}}(I(s))\subset\mathbb{C}(I(s))
consisting of rational functions with subtraction-free expressions
[
32
, Example I.1.5(a)]
. Note that this is the universal semifield on generators
x_{i}
indexed by
i\in I(s)
. The form of the transformations (
8
) ensures that the field isomorphisms
\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}
induce isomorphisms of semifields
\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}\colon\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{sf}}(I(s_{2}))\to\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{sf}}(I(s_{1}))
. Choosing a base vertex and using the bijections
\rho_{i}
to identify the edges incident at each vertex, we can view the underlying oriented graph of
\mathbb{E}
as a quotient of the
n
-regular tree
\mathbb{T}_{n}
. Lifting the data to this tree we obtain a
Y
-pattern with coefficients in the universal semifield on
n
generators.

2.4.
Quivers and mutations

Given an exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
and a vertex
s\in V
, there is an associated 2-acyclic quiver
Q(s)
whose set of vertices is
I(s)
, and which has
\max(0,-v_{ij})
edges with source
i
and target
j
.
We say that an exchange graph
includes
a given 2-acyclic quiver
Q
if there is a vertex
s\in V
such that
Q(s)
is isomorphic to
Q
. Two 2-acyclic quivers are said to be
mutation-equivalent
if they are included in the same exchange graph.
Standard arguments show that every 2-acyclic quiver is included in an exchange graph, and that this exchange graph is unique up to isomorphism.
Thus isomorphism classes of exchange graphs are in bijection with mutation-equivalence classes of 2-acyclic quivers.

An exchange graph is said to be of
finite type
if its underlying unoriented graph is finite. We recall the following result of Fomin and Zelevinsky (
[
20
]
, see also
[
32
, Theorem I.3.15]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

An exchange graph is of finite type precisely if it includes an orientation of a simply-laced (but not necessarily connected) Dynkin diagram. ∎

Given an exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
, the
opposite exchange graph

\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
is defined as follows
[
32
, II.2.2]
. We take the same underlying graph with the same edge bijections:
\widebar{V}=V
,
\widebar{I}=I
and
\widebar{\rho}_{i}=\rho_{i}
. We then set

(9)

\widebar{v}_{ij}=-v_{ij},\qquad\bar{\phi}(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}=\iota^{*}(s_{1})\circ\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}\circ\iota^{*}(s_{2}),

where for any vertex
s\in V
the involution
\iota^{*}(s)
of the field
\mathbb{C}(I(s))
is defined by
\iota^{*}(s)(x_{i})=x_{i}^{-1}
. The mutation rules of (E4) are easily checked. Note that the quivers included in
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
are precisely the opposites of those included in
\mathbb{E}
. Thus a quiver
Q
is mutation-equivalent to its opposite quiver
\widebar{Q}
precisely if the corresponding exchange graphs
\mathbb{E}
and
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
are isomorphic.

3.
Cluster spaces

For the rest of the paper we fix an exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
as in Definition
2.1
, and we use the associated notation
(V,I,s,t,\epsilon)
and
(v_{ij},\rho_{i},\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*})
without further comment. From Section
7
onwards we will assume that
\mathbb{E}
is of finite type.
In this section we review the definitions of the various associated cluster spaces, following Fock and Goncharov
[
18
]
.

3.1.
Complex cluster space

For each vertex
s\in V
we consider the space
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
of
\mathbb{C}
-valued points of the algebraic torus with character lattice
\mathbb{Z}_{I(s)}
and function field
\mathbb{C}(I(s))
. We view elements of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
as maps
w\colon I(s)\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and the element
x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(I(s))
then corresponds to the function
x_{i}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
defined by
x_{i}(w)=w(i)
. The field automorphisms
\phi(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}
induce birational isomorphisms of tori and hence partially-defined maps

\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{1})}\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{2})}.

Given an edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
and an element
w\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{1})}
, the mutation formula (
8
) gives

(10)

\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}w(j)\,(1+w(i)^{+1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w(j)\,(1+w(i)^{-1})^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
w(i)^{-1}&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

We shall view the complex cluster space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
as a possibly non-Hausdorff complex manifold obtained by quotienting the union
\bigcup_{s\in V}(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
by the equivalence relation

w_{1}\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{1})}\sim w_{2}\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{2})}\iff\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})\text{ is regular at $w_{1}$ and $\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w_{1})=w_{2}$}.

There are open embeddings
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s)^{-1}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
and equalities of partially-defined maps
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1})
.

For each vertex
s\in V
we can define a skew-symmetric form

(11)

\langle-,-\rangle_{s}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{I(s)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{I(s)}\to\mathbb{Z},\qquad\langle e_{j},e_{k}\rangle=v_{ij}.

This induces a translation-invariant Poisson structure
\{-,-\}_{s}
on the torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
satisfying

\{x_{j},x_{k}\}_{s}=2\pi i\cdot v_{jk}\cdot x_{j}x_{k}

for all
j,k\in I(s)
. A computation using (
7
) and (
8
) shows that these Poisson structures are intertwined by the maps
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})
, and therefore glue to give a holomorphic Poisson structure
\{-,-\}
on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

3.2.
Positive real cluster space

For each pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
the partially-defined map
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})
restricts to a diffeomorphism

\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s_{2})},

since this is clearly true for each of the maps (
10
).
We define the real positive cluster variety
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
by gluing together the sets
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s)}
using these maps. It is a smooth manifold with a collection of diffeomorphisms
\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s)}
satisfying
\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s_{1})
. Via the embeddings
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s)}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
we can view
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
as a closed submanifold of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

For each vertex
s\in V
there are mutually inverse homeomorphisms

\exp\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s)},\qquad\log\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}

defined by the componentwise exponential and logarithm maps.
We set

\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s)=\log\circ\,\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)},

and define transition functions
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1})^{-1}
.
Given an edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
and an element
w\in\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}
, we have

(12)

\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{+w(i)}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{-w(i)}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-w(i)&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

3.3.
Tropical cluster space

Fix an element
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for each vertex
s\in V
denote by
m_{r}\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
the operation of componentwise multiplication by
r
. We obtain a new system of charts

\phi_{\mathbb{R}}^{r}(s)=m_{r}\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}

with corresponding transition functions

\phi^{r}_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi^{r}_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{2})\circ\phi^{r}_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1})^{-1}=m_{r}\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ m_{r}^{-1}.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
,

(i)

the map
\phi^{r}_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}
has a pointwise limit as
r\to 0
,

(ii)

the limit defines a piecewise linear (PL) isomorphism
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}
,

(iii)

the map
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})
preserves the integral lattices and multiplication by
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(\mathbb{Z}^{I(s_{1})})=\mathbb{Z}^{I(s_{2})},\qquad m_{r}\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ m_{r}.

Proof.

Since any pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
can be connected by a finite sequence of edges it is enough to consider the case of a single edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
. Given an element
w\in\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}
, we have

(13)

\phi^{r}_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot r\cdot\log\big(1+e^{+w(i)/r}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot r\cdot\log\big(1+e^{-w(i)/r}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-w(i)&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

This indeed has a well-defined limit as
r\to 0
and we find that

(14)

\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\max(0,+w(i))&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\max(0,-w(i))&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-w(i)&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

The claims (ii) and (iii) follow immediately by inspecting this formula.
∎

The tropical cluster space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
is obtained by gluing copies of
\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
using the PL isomorphisms
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})
. It is a PL manifold with canonical PL isomorphisms
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
satisfying
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1})
. Lemma
3.1
(iii) shows that there is a well-defined subset
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{Z}^{t}}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
of integral points of the tropical cluster space. For each vertex
s\in V
, the chart
\phi(s)
restricts to a bijection
\phi(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{Z}^{t}}\to\mathbb{Z}^{I(s)}
.

We define the notion of an automorphism of an exchange graph

4.
Sign coherence and the tropical fan

In this section we again fix an exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
as in Definition
2.1
and review some further standard material from cluster theory, namely sign coherence and the tropical fan.

4.1.
Sign coherence

The following fundamental result is known in the cluster literature as sign coherence.

Theorem 4.1
.

Take two vertices
a,s\in V
. Then there is a single orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
which contains
the element
g_{j}=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s)(e_{j}^{*})
for all
j\in I(a)
.∎

We note in passing that although a purely combinatorial proof of this result is difficult, in the approach via CY
3
categorification reviewed in Section
9
it comes essentially for free.

Remark 4.2
.

Let us relate Theorem
4.1
to the usual sign coherence statement in cluster theory. Note first that the formula (
14
) describes the mutation of coefficients with values in the tropical semifield
\mathbb{Z}_{t}
. Consider as in
[
32
, Section I.4.1]
the
Y
-system with principal coefficients at the vertex
a\in V
. For each
j\in I(a)
, the power of the element
y_{j}
in the coefficient at a given vertex
s
transforms under mutation as would a coefficient with values in
\mathbb{Z}_{t}
. Using the definition of the
C
-matrix
[
32
, Definition I.4.3]
it follows that each vector
g_{j}
appears as a row in the matrix
\widebar{C}_{s}
associated to the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
. The opposite comes from the mismatch between the maximum appearing in (
14
) and the minimum convention for the tropical semifield used in
[
32
, Example I.1.4 (b)]
. This statement also follows directly from
[
32
, Proposition I.4.4]
. The claim that all the vectors
g_{j}
lie in a single orthant then follows immediately from the sign coherence property for
c
-vectors
[
32
, Theorem I.4.21]
. To explain our notation, let us also note that by
[
32
, Proposition II.2.7]
the vectors
g_{j}
are the columns of the matrix
G_{s}
associated to the original exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
.

4.2.
Tropical cones

For each vertex
a\in V
, we define the subset

(17)

\sigma^{a}=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}_{\geqslant 0})\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}.

A subset
\sigma\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
will be called a
maximal tropical cone
if it is of the form
\sigma=\sigma^{a}
for some vertex
a\in V
. The terminology is justified by Theorem
4.3
below. Given a maximal tropical cone
\sigma\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
and a vertex
s\in V
, we define

(18)

\sigma(s)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\sigma)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix a maximal tropical cone
\sigma\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. Then

(i)

for each vertex
s\in V
the subset
\sigma(s)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
is a rational simplicial cone of maximal dimension,

(ii)

for any pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
the bijection

\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\sigma(s_{1})\to\sigma(s_{2})

is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-linear and therefore takes faces to faces,

(iii)

for each vertex
s\in V
the subset
\sigma(s)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
is contained in a single orthant.

Proof.

We can write
\sigma=\sigma^{a}
for some vertex
a\in V
and then
\sigma(s)
is the image of the positive orthant
\sigma^{a}(a)=\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}_{\geqslant 0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}
under the map
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s)
.
We can connect the vertices
a,s\in V
by a finite chain of edges
a=s_{1}\to\cdots\to s_{n}=s
. The PL isomorphism (
14
) corresponding to a simple mutation is clearly linear away from the coordinate hyperplanes. Using induction and Theorem
4.1
it follows that for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
the restriction of
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s_{i})
to the positive orthant
\sigma^{a}(a)
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-linear and has image contained in a single orthant. This implies (iii) and shows that
\sigma^{a}(s)
is the cone spanned by the vectors
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s)(e_{j}^{*})
. Part (i) follows since
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s_{i})
is a bijection and preserves the integral lattices, and part (ii) is immediate on writing
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s_{1})^{-1}
.
∎

Consider a maximal tropical cone
\sigma\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
and an edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
. Theorem
4.3
(iii) implies that there is a unique sign
\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\in\{\pm 1\}
such that

w\in\sigma(s_{1})\implies\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot w(i)\geqslant 0.

This sign is called the
tropical sign
of the edge
i\in I
with respect to
\sigma
.
(Strictly speaking, following Remark
4.2
, this is the tropical sign in the sense of
[
32
, Definition II.2.1]
for the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
).

Remark 4.4
.

It is easy to deduce from (
14
) and the relation
\rho_{i}(i)=\epsilon(i)
that
\kappa_{\sigma}(\epsilon(i))=-\kappa_{\sigma}(i)
. Thus, having chosen
\sigma
, we obtain an orientation
I=I_{+}\sqcup I_{-}
of the unoriented graph underlying
\mathbb{E}
by declaring
I_{\pm}\subset I
to be the subset of edges
i\in I
with tropical sign
\kappa_{\sigma}(i)=\pm 1
.

4.3.
Tropical fan

Let
\sigma\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
be a maximal tropical cone. We say that a subset
\tau\subseteq\sigma
is a
face
of
\sigma
if for some vertex
s\in V
the subset
\tau(s)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\tau)
is a face of the cone
\sigma(s)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\sigma)
. Theorem
4.3
(ii) shows that this condition is independent of the choice of vertex
s\in V
. We say that a subset
\tau\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
is a
tropical cone
if it is a face of a maximal tropical cone.

Theorem 4.5
.

(i)

If
a,b\in V
then
a\neq b
implies that
\sigma^{a}\neq\sigma^{b}
.

(ii)

The intersection of any two tropical cones in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
is a face of each.

(iii)

If
\mathbb{E}
is of finite type then
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}=\bigcup_{a\in V}\sigma^{a}
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from
[
32
, Theorem I.4.27]
and our definition of the exchange graph.

Assume for a contradiction that (ii) is false. Then we can find tropical cones
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
, such that the intersection
\tau_{1}\cap\tau_{2}
is not a face of
\tau_{1}
. Let us take a vertex
s\in V
such that
\tau_{2}\subseteq\sigma^{s}
and work in the chart
\phi(s)
. Recall that a subset
F\subseteq\tau_{1}(s)
is a face precisely if
x,y\in\tau_{1}(s)
and
x+y\in F
implies
x,y\in F
.
Thus we can find elements
x,y\in\tau_{1}(s)
with
x+y\in\tau_{2}(s)
but
x\not\in\tau_{2}(s)
. Since
\tau_{2}(s)
is a face of the cone
\sigma^{s}(s)=\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}
this implies that there is an
i\in I(s)
such that either
x(i)+y(i)\geqslant 0
and
x(i)<0
, or
x(i)+y(i)=0
and
x(i)\neq 0
. Either way, we conclude that
x(i)
and
y(i)
have opposite signs. But then
\tau_{1}(s)
is not contained in a single orthant, which contradicts Theorem
4.3
(iii).

Part (iii) follows from a topological argument. Fix a vertex
s\in V
and consider the images
\tau(s)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\tau)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
of all tropical cones
\tau
. The union of these cones defines a closed subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
, and the union of all cones of codimension
\geqslant 2
defines a closed subset
C^{\prime}\subset C
. Consider the complementary open subsets
U=\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\,\setminus\,C
and
U^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\,\setminus\,C^{\prime}
. Then
U^{\prime}
is path-connected, and coincides with the union of
U
with the relative interiors of all cones of codimension
\leqslant 1
. In particular, the intersection
C\cap U^{\prime}
is non-empty since it contains the interior of any maximal cone, for example the positive orthant.

Suppose for a contradiction that
U
is non-empty. Then we can take a point
u\in U
and join it by a continuous path in
U^{\prime}
to a point
w\in C\cap U^{\prime}
. Any such path must necessarily meet the boundary of
C
. But in fact
\partial C\cap U^{\prime}
is empty because by (
14
), the relative interior of every codimension 1 cone is contained in an open set given by itself together with the interiors of the adjacent maximal cones.
∎

We call the set
\Sigma
of tropical cones in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
the
tropical fan
. The map
a\mapsto\sigma^{a}
defines a bijection
V\to\Sigma
. Each PL chart
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s)\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
maps the cones of
\Sigma
bijectively onto the cones of a simplicial fan
\Sigma(s)
in the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
. In the case that
\mathbb{E}
is of finite type this fan is moreover complete.

Remark 4.6
.

The transition function
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})
maps the fan
\Sigma(s_{1})
to the fan
\Sigma(s_{2})
by a PL map taking cones to cones. This does not imply that the fans
\Sigma(s_{i})
are isomorphic in the usual sense since a morphism of fans in toric geometry is required to be induced by a linear map. Nonetheless, all combinatorial properties of cones and their faces are preserved by the maps
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})
, and so for these purposes we can treat
\Sigma
exactly as if it were a fan in a vector space.

5.
Specialisation of the cluster spaces

We again fix an exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
as in Definition
2.1
. In this section we recall how the sign coherence property of Theorem
4.1
leads to a deformation of the cluster space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
whose central fibre is an algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
. Throughout this section we fix a vertex
a\in V
and write
\sigma=\sigma^{a}
for the corresponding maximal tropical cone in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. Everything we do will depend on this choice, but we often suppress this dependence from the notation. The deformation of the space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
depends also on a choice of element
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{I(a)}
.

5.1.
Linear maps

Given a pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
, there are corresponding cones
\sigma(s_{i})\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{i})}

(19)

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}

to be the unique linear map extending
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\sigma(s_{1})\to\sigma(s_{2})
. This is well-defined by Theorem
4.3
. Given three vertices
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in V
, we have

(20)

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{3})=\mu^{\sigma}(s_{2},s_{3})\circ\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2}).

Lemma
3.1
(iii) shows that (
19

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{Z}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{Z}^{I(s_{2})}.

We shall also frequently use the induced maps

(22)

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{2})},\qquad\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{1})}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{2})}.

obtained by tensoring (
21
) with
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{C}^{*}
respectively.

The next result computes the map (

Lemma 5.1
.

For any edge

and any

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}w(j)+\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}\cdot w(i)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
w(j)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}<0,\\
-w(i)&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

Proof.

The formula (
23
) certainly defines a linear map
\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}
so we must just show that it coincides with (
14
) when restricted to the cone
\sigma(s_{1})\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}
. This follows because by definition of the tropical sign, when
w\in\sigma(s_{1})
we have
\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot w(i)\geqslant 0
for all
i\in I(s_{1})
.
∎

Recall from (
11
) the definition of the skew-symmetric forms
\langle-,-\rangle_{s}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
the the dual map to (

(24)

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{I(s_{2})}\to\mathbb{Z}_{I(s_{1})},

intertwines the forms
\langle-,-\rangle_{s_{2}}
and
\langle-,-\rangle_{s_{1}}
.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the case when
s_{1},s_{2}
are connected by a single edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
. The map (
24
) is then given by the formula

(25)

\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}(e_{\rho_{i}(j)})=\begin{cases}e_{j}+\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}\cdot e_{i}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
e_{j}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\kappa_{\sigma}(i)\cdot v_{ij}<0,\\
-e_{i}&\text{ if }i=j,\end{cases}

and the result follows by a direct computation using (
7
).∎

Suppose we are given an edge

6.
Duality and the DT element

Let
\mathbb{E}
be an exchange graph, and recall from Section
2.4
the definition of the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
. Note in particular that the underlying unoriented graphs
(V,I,s,t,\epsilon)
of
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
is the same as that of
\mathbb{E}
. We collect here several results which relate these two exchange graphs. This leads naturally to the definition of a DT element of the cluster modular group
\mathbb{G}
.

6.1.
Identification of tropical spaces

Let
\widebar{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
denote the tropical cluster space associated to the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
. There are PL homeomorphisms
\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)\colon\widebar{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
and cones
\bar{\sigma}=\bar{\sigma}^{a}
indexed by the vertices
a\in V
. Given a cone
\bar{\sigma}
and a pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
, we denote by

\bar{\mu}^{\bar{\sigma}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}
the associated linear map (
19
).

The definition (
9
) of the field isomorphisms of the opposite exchange graph implies that there is a
canonical homeomorphism
\iota\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\widebar{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
satisfying

(34)

\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\iota(x))=-\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(x),

for any vertex
s\in V
and any point
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. This should not be confused with the map constructed in the next result.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\mathbb{E}
has finite type there is a unique homeomorphism
\partial\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\widebar{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
such that for any vertex
s\in V

\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)\circ\partial\,|_{\sigma^{s}}={\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)\,|_{\sigma^{s}}.

Proof.

Uniqueness is clear since the finite type assumption ensures that the tropical fan is complete. The given map is clearly continuous on each cone, so we must just check that these maps glue. That is, supposing
x\in\sigma^{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}
, and setting
w=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1})(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s_{1})}
, we must show that
\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)={\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(w)
. We can connect
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
by a finite sequence of edges
s_{1}=a_{1}\to\cdots\to a_{n}=s_{2}
such that
x\in\sigma^{a_{i}}
for each
i
. Writing
\tau=\sigma^{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}
this follows from the completeness of the quotient fan
\Sigma/\tau
as in the proof of Lemma
7.1
below. Thus it is enough to check the claim for a simple mutation
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}
. Then it follows from the formula (
14
), since the condition
x\in\sigma^{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}
implies that
w(i)=0
.
∎

6.2.
Tropical duality

We shall need the following non-obvious relation between the tropical fans associated to the exchange graphs
\mathbb{E}
and
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

(i)

For any two vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
there is an equality

(35)

\bar{\mu}^{\bar{\sigma}^{s_{1}}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\mu^{\sigma^{s_{2}}}(s_{1},s_{2}).

(ii)

For any vertex
s\in V
, the maximal cones of the fan
\bar{\Sigma}(a)
associated to the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
are given by the subsets

\bar{\sigma}^{s}(a)=\mu^{\sigma^{a}}(s,a)\left(\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}_{\geqslant 0}\right)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}.

Proof.

For (i) recall from Remark
4.2
that in the notation of
[
32
, Section II.2]
the vectors
g_{j}=\mu^{\sigma^{s_{2}}}(s_{2},s_{1})(e_{j}^{*})
are the columns of the matrix
{G}_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}
associated to the exchange graph
\mathbb{E}
. Thus the vectors
g^{\prime}_{j}={\mu}^{{\sigma}^{s_{2}}}(s_{1},s_{2})(e_{j}^{*})
are the columns of the inverse matrix
({G}_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}})^{-1}
.
Similarly, the vectors
\widebar{g}_{j}=\bar{\mu}^{\bar{\sigma}^{s_{1}}}(s_{1},s_{2})(e_{j}^{*})
are the columns of the matrix
\widebar{G}_{s_{2}}^{s_{1}}
associated to the opposite exchange graph
\widebar{\mathbb{E}}
. Combining
[
32
, Propositions II.2.3, II.2.7]
gives an equality of matrices
\widebar{G}_{s_{2}}^{s_{1}}=({G}_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}})^{-1}
which implies
g^{\prime}_{j}=\widebar{g}_{j}
and therefore proves the relation (
35
).

Part (ii) is now immediate since by definition
\bar{\sigma}^{s}(a)=\bar{\mu}^{\bar{\sigma}^{s}}(s,a)(\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}_{\geqslant 0})
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

As explained in Remark
9.3
, from the perspective of CY
3
categorification the relation (
35
) results from the operation of dualising quiver representations, which defines a contravariant equivalence between the categories of representations of a pair of opposite quivers.

6.3.
DT element

Recall the definition of the cluster modular group
\mathbb{G}
. By definition it acts faithfully on the underlying oriented graph of
\mathbb{E}
. As explained in Section
3.4
it also acts by homeomorphisms of the space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. These two actions are related by the diagram (
16
) which implies that for any element
g\in\mathbb{G}
and any vertex
s\in V
we have

g(\sigma^{s})=\sigma^{g(s)}
.

Definition 6.4
.

We call an element
T\in\mathbb{G}
a
DT element
if for all vertices
s\in V
we have

(36)

\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(T(s),s)(w)(i)=-w(T(i)),

where
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(T(s))}
and
i\in I(s)
.

Note that this implies that
\sigma^{T(s)}(s)=\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}^{I(s)}
for all
s\in V
.
The name DT element comes from
[
24
, Definition 3.5]
; see also
[
28
, Theorem 6.5]
.

Proposition 6.5
.

(i)

There is at most one DT element
T\in\mathbb{G}
.

(ii)

If
T\in\mathbb{G}
is a DT element then
T
is central.

(iii)

If
\mathbb{E}
is of finite type then there is a DT element
T\in\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

For (i), suppose we are given two DT elements
T_{i}\in\mathbb{G}
. Then the composite
g=T_{1}^{-1}\circ T_{2}
satisfies
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s,g(s))(w)=w
for all vertices
a\in V
and all
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}
. Thus
\sigma^{g(s)}=g(\sigma^{s})=\sigma^{s}
and hence
g(s)=s
for all vertices
s\in V
. Moreover
g
also fixes all edges
i\in I(s)
since these correspond to the facets of
\sigma^{s}
. Since
\mathbb{G}
acts faithfully on
\mathbb{E}
we conclude that
g=1
and
T_{1}=T_{2}
.

For (ii) let
T\in\mathbb{G}
be a DT element and take
g\in\mathbb{G}
. Then for any vertex
s\in V

g_{s}\left(\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\left(s,(g^{-1}Tg)(s)\right)(w)\right)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\left(g(s),Tg(s))(g_{s}(w)\right)=-g_{s}(w),

for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
, where the linear map
g_{s}\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(g(s))}
is induced by
g\colon I(s)\to I(g(s))
. This implies that
g^{-1}Tg
is also a DT element so by (i) we have
Tg=gT\in\mathbb{G}
.

Part (iii) holds because quivers that are orientations of simply-laced Dynkin diagrams admit maximal green sequences, and such a sequence ensures the existence of a DT element. See
[
30
, Section 2]
and
[
27
, Prop. 5.17]
.
∎

7.2.
Quotient fans

Let
\tau\in\Sigma
be a cone and take a vertex
s\in V
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{s}
. As in the proof of Lemma
7.1
, the images under the quotient map

q_{\tau}(s)\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}/\langle\tau(s)\rangle
of the cones
\sigma(s)\in\Sigma(s)
that contain
\tau(s)

form a quotient fan
\Sigma(s)/\tau(s)
. Suppose we are given two vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}
. Theorem
4.3
(ii) shows that the map
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})
in the middle row of the diagram (
38
) maps cones to cones and induces a bijection
\Sigma(s_{1})\to\Sigma(s_{2})
. The corresponding map in the bottom row therefore also maps cones to cones and induces a bijection
\Sigma(s_{1})/\tau(s_{1})\to\Sigma(s_{2})/\tau(s_{2})
. Thus there is a well-defined set of cones
\Sigma/\tau
in the space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
which are are in bijection with the cones
\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
.

We can view
\Sigma/\tau
as a fan in the PL space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
exactly as in Remark
4.6
. The following result shows that the map
q_{\tau}\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
is a map of fans.

Lemma 7.2
.

Fix a cone
\tau\in\Sigma
. Then for any cone
\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
there is a cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma
such that
q_{\tau}(\tau^{\prime})\subseteq q_{\tau}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
\tau^{\prime}
is a maximal cone.
Recall that the fan
\Sigma/\tau
is complete and has maximal cones of the form
\sigma/\tau
for cones
\sigma\in\Sigma
containing
\tau
. Thus we can find a cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma
such that the interior of
q_{\tau}(\tau^{\prime})
meets the cone
q_{\tau}(\sigma)
. Let us write
\sigma=\sigma^{s}
for some vertex
s\in V
and view everything in the chart
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)
. Then
\sigma(s)=\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}
is the positive orthant, and since
\tau\subseteq\sigma
the map
q_{\tau}(s)\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}/\langle\tau(s)\rangle
is the projection onto some subset of the co-ordinates. Thus
q_{\tau}(s)^{-1}(q_{\tau}(s)(\sigma(s)))
is a union of orthants in
\mathbb{R}^{I(s)}
. By assumption, the interior of the maximal cone
\tau^{\prime}(s)
meets this subset. But by Theorem
4.3
(iii), every maximal cone whose interior meets an orthant is already contained in that orthant. Thus
q_{\tau}(s)(\tau^{\prime}(s))\subseteq q_{\tau}(s)(\sigma(s))
as claimed.
∎

We conclude with one further consequence of Lemma
7.1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Take vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
and a cone
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}
. Suppose we are given maximal cones
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that the subsets
q_{\tau}(\sigma),q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
are contained in the same maximal cone of the quotient fan
\Sigma/\tau
. Then
\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})=\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(s_{1},s_{2})
.

Proof.

By the definition of the quotient fan there is a maximal cone
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Sigma
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{\prime\prime}
such that
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime\prime})
contains both

q_{\tau}(\sigma)
and
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})
. It then suffices to prove the result in the case
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=\sigma
. Thus we can assume that
\tau\subseteq\sigma
and
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})\subseteq q_{\tau}(\sigma)
. Take
x^{\prime}\in\sigma^{\prime}(s_{1})
. Then we can find
x\in\sigma(s_{1})
such that
x^{\prime}-x\in\langle\tau(s_{1})\rangle
. We claim that

\begin{split}\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(s_{1},s_{2})(x^{\prime})&=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(x^{\prime})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(x)+\mu^{\tau}(s_{1},s_{2})(x^{\prime}-x)\\
&=\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})(x)+\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})(x^{\prime}-x)=\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})(x^{\prime}).\end{split}

Indeed, the first equality is the definition, the second is Lemma
7.1
and the third is the fact, obvious from the definition of the maps
\mu
, that since
\tau\subseteq\sigma
, the linear map
\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})
agrees with
\mu^{\tau}(s_{1},s_{2})
on the subspace
\langle\tau(s_{1})\rangle\subseteq\langle\sigma(s_{1})\rangle
.
∎

8.
The cluster stability space

Let
\mathbb{E}
be an exchange graph of finite type. In this section we introduce the associated cluster stability space
\mathcal{S}
. We show that
\mathcal{S}
is a complex manifold with an integral affine structure, a Poisson structure, and an action of the additive group
\mathbb{C}
. In Section
9
we will identify
\mathcal{S}
with a natural quotient of the space of stability conditions on a CY
3
triangulated category.

8.1.
Cluster stability space

We begin by defining a PL manifold
\widebar{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. For each vertex
a\in V
there is a PL homeomorphism

(40)

\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)\colon\widebar{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{C}^{I(a)},\qquad\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(x)+i\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(y).

The corresponding transition functions

\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a,b)=\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(b)\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)^{-1}

satisfy

\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a,b)(u+iv)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,b)(u)+i\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,b)(v).

Recall the semi-closed and closed upper half-planes

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leqslant\arg(z)<\pi\},\qquad\widebar{\mathcal{H}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)\geqslant 0\}.

For each vertex
a\in V
we define the subset
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\times\sigma^{a}\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
. Note that a point
(x,y)\in\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
lies in this subset precisely if
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)(i)\in\widebar{\mathcal{H}}
for all
i\in I(a)
.

Definition 8.1
.

The
cluster stability space
is the subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
consisting of those points
(x,y)\in\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
such that there exists a vertex
a\in V
with
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)(i)\in\;\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
for all
i\in I(a)
.

Given a cone
\tau\in\Sigma
, we define the
relative interior
and the
open star

\operatorname{int}(\tau)=\tau\,\setminus\,\bigcup_{\tau^{\prime}\subsetneq\,\tau}\tau^{\prime},\qquad\operatorname{star^{0}}(\tau)=\bigcup_{\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}}\,\operatorname{int}(\tau^{\prime}).

Note that, since the fan
\Sigma
is complete, the open star is indeed an open subset of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. Note also that for every point
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
there is a unique cone
\tau\in\Sigma
such that
x\in\operatorname{int}(\tau)
.

For each pair of cones
\tau\subseteq\sigma
in
\Sigma
with
\sigma
maximal we introduce the subsets of
\widebar{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle F(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\widebar{\mathcal{S}}:q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau}(\sigma))\text{ and }y\in\operatorname{int}(\tau)\big\},

\displaystyle U(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\widebar{\mathcal{S}}:q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau}(\sigma))\text{ and }y\in\operatorname{star^{0}}(\tau)\big\}.

It is clear that
F(\sigma,\tau)\subset U(\sigma,\tau)
and that
U(\sigma,\tau)\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
is open.

Lemma 8.2
.

(i)

The subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
is the disjoint union of the subsets
F(\sigma,\tau)
.

(ii)

The subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
is the union of the subsets
U(\sigma,\tau)
, and is therefore open.

Proof.

Note first that the subsets
F(\sigma,\tau)\subset\widebar{\mathcal{S}}
are disjoint. Indeed if
(x,y)\in F(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap F(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})
then
y\in\operatorname{int}(\tau_{i})
for
i=1,2
implies that
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
. Setting
\tau=\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
we have
\tau\subseteq\sigma_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Then
q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}q_{\tau}(\sigma_{i})
for
i=1,2
implies that
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
.

To prove (i) we must show that
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{(\sigma,\tau)}F(\sigma,\tau)
. Suppose first that
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. Then there is a vertex
a\in V
such that
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)(j)\in\;\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
for all
j\in I(a)
. Setting
\sigma=\sigma^{a}
we have
y\in\sigma
and we define
\tau\subseteq\sigma
to be the unique face such that
y\in\operatorname{int}(\tau)
. Write
u+iv=\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{I(a)}
. Then by definition of
\;\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, for every
j\in I(a)
we either have
v(j)>0
, or
v(j)=0
and
u(j)>0
. Moreover
q_{\tau}(a)
is the projection onto the components of
\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}
for which
v(j)=0
. It follows that
q_{\tau(a)}(u)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau(a)}(\sigma(a)))
and hence

q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau}(\sigma))
. This proves that
(x,y)\in F(\sigma,\tau)
. Conversely, if
(x,y)\in F(\sigma,\tau)
we can write
\sigma=\sigma^{a}
for some vertex
a\in V
, and the same argument in reverse shows that
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)(x,y)(j)\in\;\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
for all
j\in I(a)
, and hence that
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
.

To prove (ii) suppose that
(x,y)\in U(\sigma,\tau)
. Then
y\in\operatorname{int}(\tau^{\prime})
for a unique cone
\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
, and
y\in\operatorname{star^{0}}(\tau)
then implies that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
. By Lemma
7.2
there is a maximal cone
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that
\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}
and
q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma)\subseteq q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Now
q_{\tau}(x)\in q_{\tau}(\sigma)
implies that
q_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)\in q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma)\subseteq q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Since
q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau}(\sigma))
, which is an open subset of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
, the same argument applies to small perturbations of
x
, and so
q_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime}))
. This proves that
(x,y)\in F(\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{S}
.
∎

8.2.
Collection of charts

We will now introduce a collection of charts

\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
on the space
\mathcal{S}
which will be used to give it the structure of a complex manifold.

Lemma 8.3
.

Fix a pair of cones
\tau\subseteq\sigma
in
\Sigma
with
\sigma=\sigma^{s}
maximal.
Then
there is a unique continuous map
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)\colon U(\sigma,\tau)\to\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
such that any vertex
a\in V
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{a}

(41)

\varpi(\sigma,\tau)|_{U(\sigma,\tau)\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}}=\mu^{\sigma}(a,s)\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)|_{U(\sigma,\tau)\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}}.

Proof.

Uniqueness is clear since by definition
U(\sigma,\tau)
is contained in the union of the subsets
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}
for vertices
a\in V
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{a}
. For each such vertex
a\in V
the map defined by the right-hand side of (
41
)
is clearly continuous on
U(\sigma,\tau)\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}
. So what remains to check is that the maps corresponding to different vertices coincide on the intersections of their domains.

Let us then take another vertex
b\in V
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{b}
and suppose that
(x,y)\in U(\sigma,\tau)
lies in the intersection
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{b}
. What we must show is that, when evaluated at
(x,y)
, the map

\mu^{\sigma}(b,s)\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(b)=\mu^{\sigma}(a,s)\circ\mu^{\sigma}(b,a)\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a,b)\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)

coincides with the right-hand side of (
41
). Taking real and imaginary parts, this is the statement that the maps
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,b)
and
\mu^{\sigma}(a,b)
coincide when evaluated at each of the points
u=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}
and
v=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}
.

Beginning with
u
, take a maximal cone
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that
x\in\sigma^{\prime}
. Since
(x,y)\in U(\sigma,\tau)
, we have
q_{\tau}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(q_{\tau}(\sigma))
, so the subset
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})
meets the interior of the maximal cone
q_{\tau}(\sigma)
. But by Lemma
7.2
, this subset
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})
is contained in some maximal cone of the quotient fan
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}/\langle\tau\rangle
. It follows that
q_{\tau}(\sigma^{\prime})\subseteq q_{\tau}(\sigma)
. Since
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{a}\cap\sigma^{b}
, Lemma
7.3
then implies that
\mu^{\sigma}(a,b)=\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(a,b)
.
The claim follows since by definition
\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(a,b)
coincides with
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,b)
on the cone
\sigma^{\prime}(a)
containing
u
.

Moving on to
v
, note that the assumption
(x,y)\in\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{b}
implies that
y\in\tau^{\prime}:=\sigma^{a}\cap\sigma^{b}
.
By Lemma
7.2
, there is a maximal cone
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that
\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}
and

q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma)\subseteq q_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Lemma
7.3
then implies that

\mu^{\sigma}(a,b)=\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(a,b)
. Since
y\in\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}
the claim again follows since
\mu^{\sigma^{\prime}}(a,b)
coincides with
\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,b)
on the cone
\sigma^{\prime}(a)
containing
v
.
∎

Lemma 8.4
.

Fix a pair of cones
\tau\subseteq\sigma
in
\Sigma
with
\sigma=\sigma^{s}
maximal. Then

(i)

when restricted to the subset
F(\sigma,\tau)\subset U(\sigma,\tau)
the map
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
coincides with
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)
,

(ii)

the map
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Proof.

Part (i) is immediate by taking
a=s
in the relation (
41
). For part (ii) it will be enough to prove that
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
is injective, since the claim then follows by invariance of domain.

Consider a point
(x,y)\in U(\sigma,\tau)
. We can find a vertex
a\in V
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{a}
and
y\in\sigma^{a}
. We set
v=\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}_{\geqslant 0}
. The imaginary part of
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)(x,y)
is then
w=\mu^{\sigma}(a,s)(v)
. Using Lemma
6.2
(i) we can rewrite this as
w=\bar{\mu}^{\bar{\sigma}^{a}}(a,s)(v)
. By definition of
\mu
this is also
w=\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a,s)(v)
. But Lemma
6.1
implies that
v=\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(a)(\partial(y))
, so we conclude that
w=\bar{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(\partial(y))
.

Consider two points
(x_{i},y_{i})\in U(\sigma,\tau)
with the same image under the map
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
. Then, since
\partial
is a homeomorphism, we have
y_{1}=y_{2}
, and so we can take
a\in V
such that both
y_{1},y_{2}\in\sigma^{a}
. But since the right-hand side of (
41
) is clearly injective on
U(\sigma,\tau)\cap\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{a}
this implies that
x_{1}=x_{2}
also.
∎

8.3.
Complex structure

We will now compute the transition functions for the charts
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)
introduced in the last section. Let us consider two vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
and write
\sigma_{i}=\sigma^{s_{i}}
. Take also subcones
\tau_{i}\subseteq\sigma_{i}
.

Lemma 8.5
.

Suppose
F(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})\neq\emptyset
. Then on
U(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})

\varpi(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=\mu^{\sigma_{2}}(s_{1},s_{2})\circ\varpi(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}).

Proof.

Take
(u,v)\in F(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})
. Then
v\in\operatorname{int}(\tau_{1})
and
v\in\operatorname{star^{0}}(\tau_{2})
which implies
\tau_{2}\subseteq\tau_{1}
. By Lemma
7.2
we can find a vertex
a\in V
such that
\tau_{1}\subseteq\sigma^{a}
and
q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{2})\subseteq q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma^{a})
. Then
q_{\tau_{2}}(u)\in q_{\tau_{2}}(\sigma_{2})
implies
q_{\tau_{1}}(u)\in q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{2})\subseteq q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma^{a})
. Since also
q_{\tau_{1}}(u)\in\operatorname{int}q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{1})
we conclude that
\sigma^{a}=\sigma_{1}
and hence
q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{2})\subseteq q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{1})
.

Now take a point
(x,y)\in U(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})
. Let
\tau\in\Sigma
be the unique cone such that
y\in\operatorname{int}(\tau)
, and take a vertex
s\in V
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma^{s}
. Then
y\in\operatorname{star^{0}}(\tau_{1})
implies that
\tau_{1}\subseteq\sigma^{s}
, and similarly
\tau_{2}\subseteq\sigma^{s}
. When evaluated at
(x,y)
we then have

\varpi(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})=\mu^{\sigma_{1}}(s,s_{1})\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s),\qquad\varpi(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=\mu^{\sigma_{2}}(s,s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s).

Now
\tau_{1}\subseteq\sigma^{s}\cap\sigma_{1}
and
q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{2})\subseteq q_{\tau_{1}}(\sigma_{1})
so Lemma
7.3
implies that
\mu^{\sigma_{1}}(s,s_{1})=\mu^{\sigma_{2}}(s,s_{1})
, and the result follows from the relation (
20
).
∎

Suppose
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
lies in the intersection
U(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})
of the domains of two charts
\varpi(\sigma_{i},\tau_{i})
. Then
(x,y)\in F(\sigma,\tau)
for a unique pair
(\sigma,\tau)
, and Lemma
8.5
shows that

\varpi(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=\mu^{\sigma_{2}}(s,s_{2})\circ\mu^{\sigma_{1}}(s_{1},s)\circ\varpi(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})

on the neighbourhood
U(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})\cap U(\sigma,\tau)
of
(x,y)
. Thus the charts
\varpi(\sigma_{i},\tau_{i})
differ by a linear map in an open neighbourhood of any point of
U(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cap U(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})
. It follows that this linear map is the same for all points in a connected component of this intersection. In particular the transition functions are holomorphic, and
\mathcal{S}
becomes a complex manifold.

Since the transition functions of the atlas are linear and preserve the integral lattices
\mathbb{Z}^{I(s)}\times\mathbb{Z}^{I(s)}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
there is a well-defined integral affine structure on
\mathcal{S}
. The fact that the transition functions are linear rather than affine linear gives the extra structure of a canonical holomorphic vector field
E
. Finally, Lemma
5.2
implies the existence of a holomorphic Poisson structure
\{-,-\}
. Consider a chart
\varpi(\sigma,\tau)\colon U(\sigma,\tau)\to\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
as above. Then by definition, the components
w(i)
are a local system of integral affine co-ordinates on
U(\sigma,\tau)
. In these co-ordinates

E=\sum_{i\in I(s)}w(i)\frac{\partial}{\partial w(i)},\qquad\{w_{i}(s),w_{j}(s)\}=2\pi i\,v_{ij}(s).

The integral affine structure, vector field
E
, and Poisson structure
\{-,-\}

together define a
period structure with skew form
on
\mathcal{S}
in the sense of
[
12
, Section 2.4]
. This is the natural structure that exists on the space of stability conditions of a CY
3
triangulated category.

8.4.
Additive group action

It is natural to try to integrate the canonical vector field
E
introduced above to define an action of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{C}^{*}
on the complex manifold
\mathcal{S}
. In fact, as the following results show, this can only be done after quotienting
\mathcal{S}
by the action of the subgroup of the cluster modular group
\mathbb{G}
generated by the DT transformation. Without taking this quotient we instead obtain an action of the universal cover of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

Lemma 8.6
.

The vector field
E
generates an action of the additive group
\mathbb{C}
on the space
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

To construct the action of
\mathbb{C}
we have to show that the flow of the vector field
E
exists for all time
t\in\mathbb{C}
. In terms of the components
w(j)
of a chart

u+iv\in\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{1})}
, let us write

u^{\prime}+iv^{\prime}=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})(u+iv)\in\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{2})}.

Then differentiating (
12
) gives

(47)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}u(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{+u(i)}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
u(j)+v_{ij}\cdot\log\big(1+e^{-u(i)}\big)&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-u(i)&\text{ if }i=j,\end{cases}

(48)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\rho_{i}(j))=\begin{cases}v(j)+v_{ij}\cdot v(i)\cdot\left(1+e^{-u(i)}\right)^{-1}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
v(j)-v_{ij}\cdot v(i)\cdot\left(1+e^{+u(i)}\right)^{-1}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
-v(i)&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

For each vertex
s\in V
we introduce the subset

(49)

\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)^{-1}\left({\widebar{\mathcal{H}}}^{I(s)}\right)\subset\mathcal{T}.

Given a point
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
, we also consider the intersection
\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}\cap\pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(x)
with the corresponding fibre
\pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(x)=T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
of the projection (
46
).

Proposition 10.1
.

Fix a point
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
. Then the subsets
\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\subset T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
are all distinct and are the maximal cones of a complete fan.

Proof.

Choose a maximal cone
\sigma=\sigma^{a}\in\Sigma
and an element
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{I(a)}
.
We then obtain a deformation of the gluing maps
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})_{t}
as in Section
5
. When
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we can restrict these to maps
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I(s_{2})}
. Intertwining with the componentwise
\log
maps exactly as in Section
3.2
we arrive at deformations

\phi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(s_{1},s_{2})_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{2})}

which coincide with
\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})
when
t=1
and with
\mu^{\sigma}(s_{1},s_{2})
when
t=0
.

Put
u=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(a)(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}
and consider the isomorphism

d_{x}\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(a)\colon T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T_{u}\,\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}=\mathbb{R}^{I(a)}.

Under this isomorphism the subsets
\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\subset T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
are identified with the subsets

(50)

\left(d_{u}\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(a,s)_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}\right),

for
t=1
. Allowing
t\in[0,1]
to vary we find that at
t=0
they become

\left(\mu^{\sigma}(a,s)\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}\right)=\mu^{\sigma}(s,a)\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s)}\right).

By Lemma
6.2
(ii) these are precisely the maximal cones
\bar{\sigma}^{s}(a)
of the tropical fan associated to the opposite exchange graph.

We now apply the c.c. cone deformation argument of Appendix
A
. What we must show is that given an edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}
the cones (
50
) corresponding to the vertices
s_{1},s_{2}
meet in a common facet. This reduces to the same statement for the cones

\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s_{1})},\qquad\left(d_{h}\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{I(s_{2})}\right),

where
h=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(a,s_{1})_{t}(u)
.
This follows from the
t
-deformed version of (
48
) obtained by differentiating (
33
). Indeed, this shows that the intersection of the above two cones is cut out by the equation
v(i)=0
.
∎

10.2.
Almost complex structure

For every vertex
s\in V
the chart
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)
induces a complex structure
I(s)
on the smooth manifold
\mathcal{T}
. The underlying almost complex structure gives an automorphism

I_{p}(s)\colon T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}\to T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
satisfying
I_{p}(s)^{2}=-\operatorname{id}
for every point
p\in\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset\mathcal{T}
. We now show that we can glue these almost complex structures to produce a continuous almost complex structure on
\mathcal{T}
.

Lemma 10.2
.

There is a continuously varying family of automorphisms
I_{p}\in\operatorname{Aut}(T_{p}\,\mathcal{T})
such that
I_{p}=I_{p}(s)
for all
p\in\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}
.

Proof.

We must show that if
p\in\sigma^{s_{1}}_{\mathcal{T}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}_{\mathcal{T}}
then
I_{p}(s_{1})=I_{p}(s_{2})
as elements of
\operatorname{Aut}(T_{p}\,\mathcal{T})
.
Connecting
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
by a chain of edges reduces the assertion to the situation of a single edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}
.
We need to show that the derivative of the transition function
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})\colon\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{1})}\to\mathbb{C}^{I(s_{2})}
at the point
p_{1}=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1})(p)
is complex linear. Let us write
u_{j}=u(j)
and
u^{\prime}_{j}=u(\rho_{i}(j))
for all
j\in I(s_{1})
. Similarly we set
v_{j}=v(j)
and
v^{\prime}_{j}=v(\rho_{i}(j))
. The assumption that
p\in\sigma^{s_{1}}_{\mathcal{T}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}_{\mathcal{T}}
gives
v_{i}=0
. Differentiating the formulae (
47
)-(
48
) we get

d_{p_{1}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\prime}_{j}},\qquad d_{p_{1}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{j}}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{\prime}_{j}},

when
j\neq i
. We also get

d_{p_{1}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\right)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\prime}_{i}}+\sum_{v_{ij}>0}\frac{v_{ij}}{1+e^{-u_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\prime}_{j}}-\sum_{v_{ij}<0}\frac{v_{ij}}{1+e^{u_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\prime}_{j}},

d_{p_{1}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\right)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{\prime}_{i}}+\sum_{v_{ij}>0}\frac{v_{ij}}{1+e^{-u_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{\prime}_{j}}-\sum_{v_{ij}<0}\frac{v_{ij}}{1+e^{u_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{\prime}_{j}}.

There are some extra terms in the first formula which vanish because
v_{i}=0
. It is then clear that
d_{p_{1}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})
is complex linear, since multiplication by
i
maps
\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}
to
\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}
and similarly for the primed variables.
∎

The derivative of the projection (
46
) at a point
p\in\mathcal{T}
defines a map

d_{p}\,\pi_{\mathcal{T}}\colon T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}\to T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}},\qquad x=\pi_{\mathcal{T}}(p).

We define the subspace
F_{p}=\ker(d_{p}\pi_{\mathcal{T}})\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
of vertical tangent vectors. It is canonically identified with the tangent space
T_{p}(T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}})
to the fibre
\pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(x)=T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
.

Lemma 10.3
.

There is a decomposition
T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}=H_{p}\oplus F_{p}
where
H_{p}=I_{p}(F_{p})
.

Proof.

The images of the subspaces
H_{p}
and
F_{p}
under the derivative of the chart
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)
are the subspaces spanned by the vectors
\frac{\partial}{\partial{u_{j}}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial{v_{j}}}
respectively.
∎

10.3.
Tangent cones

Given a point
p\in\mathcal{T}
, there is a corresponding subset of vertices of the exchange graph

V(p)=\{s\in V:p\in\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\}\subset V.

For each element
s\in V(p)

there is a a polyhedral cone
T_{p}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
which is the tangent cone to the subset
\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset\mathcal{T}
at the point
p
. We will show that these subsets are the maximal cones of a complete fan in the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
.

Rather than introducing the formalism of tangent cones we proceed as follows.
We first consider the space
\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
with co-ordinates
w(j)=u_{j}+iv_{j}
for
j\in I(s)
. The tangent space to
\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
at any point is a real vector space spanned by the vectors
\frac{\partial}{\partial{u_{j}}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial{v_{j}}}
. We consider a point
w\in\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
so that
v_{j}\geqslant 0
for all
j\in I(s)
. We define the tangent cone to the subset
\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
at the point
w
to be

(51)

T_{w}\,\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}=\left\{\big.\sum_{j}\left(a_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial{u_{j}}}+b_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial{v_{j}}}\right):v_{j}=0\implies b_{j}\geqslant 0\right\}\subset T_{w}\,\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}.

We can then define the tangent cone to the subset
\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset\mathcal{T}
at a point
p\in\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}
to be

(52)

T_{p}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}=\left(d_{p}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)\right)^{-1}\left(T_{\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)(p)}\,\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}\right).

Lemma 10.4
.

Fix a point
p\in\mathcal{T}
. Then the subsets
T_{p}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
for vertices
s\in V(p)
are all distinct, and are the maximal cones of a complete fan. Each of these cones contains the subspace
H_{p}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

The point
p\in\mathcal{T}
is an element of
T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
where
x=\pi_{\mathcal{T}}(p)
. The localisation of the complete fan of Lemma
10.1
at this point gives a complete fan in the vector space
T_{p}(T_{x}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}})
whose maximal cones are indexed by the set
V(p)
. This vector space is canonically identified with the subspace
F_{p}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
of vertical tangent vectors for the map
\pi_{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
. Pulling back the localised fan by the projection
T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}\to F_{p}
with kernel
H_{p}
gives a complete fan in
T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
. We claim that its maximal cones coincide with the subsets
T_{p}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
.

To prove the claim we can take a vertex
s\in V(p)
and work in the chart
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)
. The cone
\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}
is mapped to the standard cone
\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
, and the localisation of this cone at the point
w=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)(p)
is precisely the tangent cone (
51
). On the other hand, the images of the subspaces
H_{p}
and
F_{p}
under the derivative of the chart
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)
are the subspaces spanned by the vectors
\frac{\partial}{\partial{u_{j}}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial{v_{j}}}
respectively. The claim then follows, and this gives the result.
∎

10.4.
Exponential map

The following map will play an essential role in what follows.

Lemma 10.5
.

There is a unique continuous map
\exp_{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
such that for all vertices
s\in V

(53)

\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s)\circ\exp_{\mathcal{T}}|_{\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}}=\exp\circ\,\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)|_{\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}^{s}},

where
\exp
on the right-hand side denotes the componentwise exponential

(54)

\exp\colon\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}.

Proof.

Take
p\in\sigma^{\mathcal{T}}_{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{\mathcal{T}}_{s_{2}}
. We must show that taking
s=s_{1}
or
s=s_{2}
in (
53
) gives the same answer for
\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(p)
. By the usual argument it is enough to consider the case of a single edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}
. Write
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1})(p)=u+iv
with
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{I(s_{1})}
. We must show that

(55)

\exp(\phi_{\mathbb{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})(u+iv))=\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})(\exp(u+iv))\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s_{2})}.

Since
p\in\sigma^{\mathcal{T}}_{s_{1}}\cap\sigma^{\mathcal{T}}_{s_{2}}
we have
v(i)=0
.
Evaluated on an edge
\rho_{i}(j)
both sides of (
55
) are therefore given by the expression

(56)

\begin{cases}e^{u(j)+iv(j)}\left(1+e^{+u(i)}\right)^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}\geqslant 0,\\
e^{u(j)+iv(j)}\left(1+e^{-u(i)}\right)^{v_{ij}}&\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }v_{ij}<0,\\
e^{-u(i)}&\text{ if }i=j,\end{cases}

which proves the claim.
∎

Recall that given a continuous function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
the directional derivative of
f
at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
along a tangent vector
u\in T_{x}\,\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the limit

d_{x}(f)(u):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(x+tu)-f(x)}{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

whenever this exists. One can then define the directional derivative of continuous map between smooth manifolds in the obvious way.

Lemma 10.6
.

(i)

Given a point
p\in\mathcal{T}
, the directional derivative
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(u)
of the map
\exp_{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
along a tangent vector
u\in T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
always exists.

(ii)

These directional derivatives combine to give a continuous map

d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}\colon T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}\to T_{q}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad q=\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(p).

(iii)

For each
s\in V(p)
the map
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
is linear and injective on the cone
T_{p}\,\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}\subset T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
.

(iv)

If
J_{q}
denotes the complex structure on
T_{q}\,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
then

(57)

d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}\circ I_{p}=J_{p}\circ d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}.

Proof.

Take a point
p\in\mathcal{T}
and a tangent vector
u\in T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
. By Lemma
10.4
we can take a vertex
s\in V(p)
with
u\in T_{p}\,\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}
. We can then compute the directional derivative using the charts
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)
and
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s)
. By the defining relation (
53
) this amounts to computing the directional derivative of the componentwise exponential (
54
) at the point
w=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)(p)\in\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
in the direction
d_{p}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s)(u)\in T_{w}\,\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}
. Note that the definition (
51
) shows that small perturbations of
w
in this direction remain in the subset
\widebar{\mathcal{H}}^{I(s)}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I(s)}
. The claims now all follow easily from the fact that the componentwise exponential is holomorphic and has injective derivative.
∎

10.5.
Local homeomorphism

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 10.7
.

The map

\exp_{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}

is
C^{1}
and is a local homeomorphism.

Proof.

To show that
\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
is differentiable we must show that for every
p\in\mathcal{T}
the PL map
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}

is in fact linear.
Consider the decomposition
T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}=F_{p}\oplus H_{p}
of Lemma
10.3
. Given
h\in H_{p}
and
u\in T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
, we claim that

(58)

d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(u+h)=d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(u)+d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}(h).

Indeed, by Lemma
10.4
we can take a vertex
s\in V(p)
with
u\in T_{p}\,\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover
h\in H_{p}\subset T_{p}\,\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}
. The claim then follows from Lemma
10.6
(iii).
Note that (
58
) implies in particular that the map
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
is linear when restricted to the subspace
H_{p}
. It follows from Lemma
10.6
(iv) that it is also linear when restricted to the subspace
F_{p}=I_{p}(H_{p})
.

To prove that
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
is linear consider
u_{1},u_{2}\in T_{p}\,\mathcal{T}
and write
u_{i}=f_{i}+h_{i}
with
f_{i}\in F_{p}
and
h_{i}\in H_{p}
. Set
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
and
h=h_{1}+h_{2}
and
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
. Then, writing
\theta=d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
we have

\begin{split}\theta(u_{1}+u_{2})&=\theta(f_{1}+h_{1}+f_{2}+h_{2})=\theta(f+h)=\theta(f)+\theta(h)\\
&=\theta(f_{1})+\theta(f_{2})+\theta(h_{1})+\theta(h_{2})=\theta(u_{1})+\theta(u_{2}),\end{split}

where in the first line we applied (
58
), and in the second line we used the linearity of
\theta|_{F_{p}}
and
\theta|_{H_{p}}
and then applied (
58
) again.

Lemma
10.6
(iii) shows that the map
d_{p}\exp_{\mathcal{T}}
is injective on each cone
T_{p}\,\sigma^{s}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Since it is also linear it is an isomorphism. To show that this linear map varies continuously with
p\in\mathcal{T}
note that it is enough to check this on a given cone. The result now follows from the inverse function theorem.
∎

11.
The log cluster space

Let
\mathbb{E}
be an exchange graph of finite type. In this section we introduce the log cluster space
\mathcal{L}
which will be the general fibre of our cluster twistor space. We construct a homeomorphism
h\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{T}
which allows us to apply Theorem
10.7
to prove that the natural map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a local homeomorphism. This then gives
\mathcal{L}
the structure of a complex manifold.

11.1.
Definition

We define the log cluster space to be the product

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}
. There is an obvious projection onto the first factor

(59)

\pi_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}.

For each vertex
s\in V
there is a homeomorphism

\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s)\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{C}^{I(s)},\qquad\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s)(x,y)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s)(x)+i\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s)(y).

The corresponding transition functions

\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{1})^{-1}
are given by

(60)

\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{1},s_{2})(u+iv)=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})(u)+i\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})(v).

For each vertex
s\in V
we define the subset
\sigma_{\mathcal{L}}^{s}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\times\sigma^{s}\subset\mathcal{L}
. Since the tropical fan
\Sigma
is complete, these subsets cover
\mathcal{L}
.

We can construct a map
h\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{T}
by gluing the natural systems of cones in the two spaces.

Proposition 11.1
.

There is a unique homeomorphism
h\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{T}
such that for any
a\in V

h|_{\sigma^{a}_{\mathcal{L}}}=\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(a)^{-1}\circ\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(a)|_{\sigma^{a}_{\mathcal{L}}}.

Moreover this homeomorphism intertwines the projections (
46
) and (
59
).

Proof.

For any pair of vertices
s_{1},s_{2}\in V
we need to show that the maps
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{i})^{-1}\circ\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{i})
agree on the intersection
\sigma^{s_{1}}_{\mathcal{L}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}_{\mathcal{L}}
. As the general case follows from concatenating edges, it suffices to check this for a single edge
i:s_{1}\to s_{2}
. The intersection
\sigma^{s_{1}}_{\mathcal{L}}\cap\sigma^{s_{2}}_{\mathcal{L}}
then maps into the real hyperplane
\operatorname{Im}w(i)=0
under the map
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1})
. On this hyperplane, the chart transition map

\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\phi_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1},s_{2})+i\phi_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}(s_{1},s_{2})
agrees with
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(s_{1},s_{2})
as can be seen by comparing (
12
) and (
14
) with (
47
) and (
48
). This establishes the continuity of
h
.

The compatibility
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}=\pi_{\mathcal{T}}\circ h
is clear by definition.
By invariance of domain, to prove that
h
is a homeomorphism it is enough to show that it is bijective, and we can check this in each fibre of
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
. Since the map preserves the fans and is an isomorphism on each maximal cone we are done.
∎

11.2.
Exponential map

The following result is a formal consequence of Lemma

and Proposition

, but since the map

Lemma 11.2

There is a unique continuous map

It follows that the space

along their boundaries using the logarithms of the maps

Local homeomorphism

Note that the definitions of the various maps imply the relation

.
Theorem

then immediately gives

Theorem 11.3
.

The map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a local homeomorphism.

We now equip the space

with the unique complex manifold structure for which the map

is étale. For any vertex

\left\{w(j),w(k)\right\}|_{\sigma_{\mathcal{L}}^{s}}=2\pi i\cdot v_{jk}.

10.7
gives

.

The map

Recall from Section

the subset

Lemma 11.5
.

The fibre of
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}
over a point of

Take a point

12.1.
Exchange graph

Set
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/m)
and let
P
be the
m
-gon viewed as a polygon in
\mathbb{C}
with vertices at the points
\omega^{k}
corresponding to elements
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. By a triangulation of
P
we mean a collection of
n=m-3
distinct, non-crossing isotopy classes of arcs connecting the vertices of
P
. To avoid confusion with the edges of the exchange graph we will refer to arcs of a triangulation rather than edges.
Given a triangulation
T_{1}
and an arc
A_{1}\in T_{1}
, there is a unique triangulation
T_{2}\neq T_{1}
such that
T_{1}\,\setminus\,\{A_{1}\}=T_{2}\,\setminus\,\{A_{2}\}
for some arc
A_{2}\in T_{2}
. The triangulation
T_{2}
is called the flip of
T_{1}
in the arc
A_{1}
.

We introduce the unoriented graph
\mathbb{E}
whose vertices
V
are the triangulations of
P
, and whose oriented edges
I
correspond to the operation of flipping an arc. Thus elements of the set
I
consist of a triangulation together with a chosen arc. In the setting of the previous paragraph, the involution
\epsilon\colon I\to I
of Section
2.1
sends the flip of the triangulation
T_{1}
in the arc
A_{1}
to the flip of the triangulation
T_{2}
in the arc
A_{2}
.
Given a vertex
s\in V
, we denote by
T(s)
the corresponding triangulation. Note that the set
I(s)
of oriented edges emanating from a vertex
s\in V
is identified with the set of arcs of the triangulation
T(s)
. We denote by
A(i)
the arc of
T(s)
corresponding to an element
i\in I(s)
.

The graph
\mathbb{E}
is connected because any two triangulations of
P
are related by a finite chain of flips. It is also locally finite, and indeed
n
-regular. We now give
\mathbb{E}
the structure of an exchange graph as in Definition
2.1
. Given two edges
i,j\in I(s)
, we define
v_{i,j}=+1
(respectively
v_{i,j}=-1
) if the arcs
A(i)
and
A(j)
occur in anti-clockwise (respectively clockwise) order in a triangle of
T(s)
. If
A(i)
and
A(j)
do not occur in the same triangle we set
v_{i,j}=0
. The edge bijection
\rho_{i}\colon I(s_{1})\to I(s_{2})
associated to an edge
i\colon s_{1}\to s_{2}\in I
is induced by the unique bijection between the arcs of the triangulations
T(s_{1})
and
T(s_{2})
extending the identification
T(s_{1})\,\setminus\,A(i)=T(s_{2})\,\setminus\,A(\epsilon(i))
.

Given a vertex
p\in P
of the polygon, there is a corresponding vertex
s(p)\in V
given by the unique triangulation of
P
all of whose edges contain
p
. The associated quiver
Q=Q(s(p))
is a coherent orientation of the A
n
Dynkin diagram.
Conversely, given an arbitrary orientation
Q
of the A
n
Dynkin diagram, we can glue a chain of triangles along their edges to obtain a triangulation of
P
whose associated quiver is
Q
.

To define the field isomorphisms we consider the variety
M=(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{m}
and the quotient
\mathcal{M}=M/\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
by the diagonal action. We view a point of
M
as a map of sets
p\colon\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to M
which then enables us to label the vertices of
P
with elements
p(i)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We define an open subset
M_{0}\subset M
by insisting that
p(i)\neq p(i+1)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, and that the image of
p
contains at least three points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The latter condition implies that
\mathcal{M}_{0}=M_{0}/\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
is a manifold.

Suppose we are given a vertex
s\in V
and an edge
i\in I(s)
. Joining the two triangles in
T(s)
containing the arc
A(i)
defines a quadrilateral whose vertices are a cyclically-ordered subset of the vertices of
P
. Following
[
18
, Section 1.3]
we define

x(s)(i)
to be the cross-ratio of the corresponding points
p(j)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Note that for the description of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
below it is important to normalise the cross-ratio so that
\operatorname{CR}(\infty,-1,0,x)=x
.

For each vertex
s\in V
we can combine the cross-ratios
x(s)(i)
associated to edges
i\in I(s)
to define a rational map

(64)

\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s)\colon\mathcal{M}_{0}\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}.

It is not hard to show (see the nexct subsection) that this map is in fact birational.
The associated transition functions
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s_{2})\circ\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s_{1})^{-1}
then induce the required field isomorphisms
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})^{*}
by pull-back.

Theorem 12.1
.

When equipped with the above data, the unoriented graph
\mathbb{E}
is an exchange graph in the sense of Section
2.2
. The associated quiver mutation class contains all orientations of the A
n
quiver. The mapping class group is
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and acts on
\mathbb{E}
by rotations of the polygon
P
.

Proof.

The mutation rules were verified in
[
17
, Section 10]
, see also
[
1
, Prop. 3.4]
. To verify the minimality property (E5) note that a triangulation of
P
is uniquely determined by the sets of 4-tuples of vertices of
P
that are the vertices of the quadrilaterals obtained by gluing triangles along a common edge. For any two different size
4
subsets of the vertices of
P
, it is straightforward to find some point
p\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
such that the corresponding cross ratios are different. The hypothesis in the minimality condition (E5) implies that
a,b\in V
correspond to triangulations which have the same set of cross ratios. This then implies that they have the same set of quadrilaterals, and hence that they are equal.

The rotation action of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
on the polygon
P
induces an action on the graph
\mathbb{E}
which rotates triangulations in the obvious way. This clearly preserves all the associated data and hence defines an injective homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}
. To prove this is surjective take
g\in\mathbb{G}
and consider a vertex
s\in V
corresponding to a triangulation
T(s)
consisting of all arcs incident with a fixed vertex
p\in P
. The associated quiver
Q(s)
is the coherent orientation of the
A_{n}
quiver. By definition of the group
\mathbb{G}
the quiver
Q(g(s))
is isomorphic to
Q(s)
. But the only triangulations of
P
whose associated quivers are isomorphic to
Q(s)
are the rotations of
T(s)
. Thus composing
g
with a rotation, we can assume that
g
fixes the vertex
s
. Since the quiver
Q(s)
has no non-trivial automorphisms
g
must also fix all the edges
i\in I(s)
. The fact that
g
preserves the edge bijections then implies that
g
is the identity. ∎

12.2.
Complex, real and tropical cluster spaces

The following result appears explicitly in
[
1
]
. For the convenience of the reader we include a sketch of the proof.

Theorem 12.2
.

There is an isomorphism of complex manifolds
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathcal{M}_{0}
under which the cluster charts
\phi_{\mathbb{C}}(s)
correspond to the maps
\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
.

Proof.

We must show that for each vertex
s\in V
, the the inverse of the map (
64
) defines an open embeddding

\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s)^{-1}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}.

We must also show that the images of these embeddings cover the space
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

For the first claim take an element
x\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{I(s)}
. Choose an arc
i\in I(s)
and consider the associated quadilateral
Q(i)
in the triangulation
T(s)
. The component
x(i)\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
defines four points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with at most two the same, unique up to
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
, and we use these points to label the vertices of
Q(i)
. We then inductively construct the points
p(j)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
by passing through the tree that is dual to the triangulation

. The resulting point in

is independent of the choices up to the action of
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
. This defines a regular inverse to
\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
which is therefore an open embedding.
For the second claim, note that given a point
p\colon\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of the space
M_{0}
, we can construct a triangulation
T(s)
of
P
so that
p
lies in the image of
\phi_{\mathcal{M}}(s)^{-1}
inductively by starting with a triangle whose vertices correspond to three distinct points

.
∎

Let
\operatorname{Poly}
be the space parameterising ideal hyperbolic polygons with
m
distinct vertices up to isometry, together with a chosen vertex. This space embeds in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
by taking
p(j)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
to be the position of the

th vertex counting round from the chosen one. Since the points

and edge

is real and positive. We then easily obtain

Theorem 12.3
.

The isomorphism of Theorem
12.2
restricts to an isomorphism of smooth manifolds
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\to\operatorname{Poly}
.

Let
\operatorname{Diag}
to be the space of weighted diagonals in the polygon
P
as in
[
26
, Section 5.1]
. The elements are triangulations equipped with non-negative real numbers associated to each arc. It is helpful to think in terms of the dual trees, with the weights giving the lengths of the edges of the tree. Then the flipping operation happens naturally when an edge becomes length zero. In this way

can be viewed as the parameter space for trivalent metric trees with

vertices. Fock and Goncharov

proved the following result

Theorem 12.4
.

There is an isomorphism of PL manifolds

Cluster stability space

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the space of complex polynomials of the form

q(x)=x^{n+1}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}.

There is an action of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
on
\mathcal{P}
generated by the transformation
a_{k}\mapsto\omega^{k+2}\cdot a_{k}
. This preserves the open subset
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
consisting of polynomials without repeated zeroes.

Let
\operatorname{Quad}
denote the space of quadratic differentials on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a single pole of order

and simple zeroes, considered up to the action of
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

q(x)\,dx^{\otimes 2}
for some polynomial

. Two quadratic differentials of this form are in the same

orbit.

We let
\operatorname{Quad}^{\operatorname{fr}}
denote the
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
cover of the space
\operatorname{Quad}
given by a choice of framing, namely a choice of one of the
m
horizontal directions at the pole. The space
\operatorname{Quad}^{\operatorname{fr}}
can then be identified with
\mathcal{P}
by taking the horizontal direction to be
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Under this identification the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
on
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to the obvious action of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
on
\operatorname{Quad}^{\operatorname{fr}}
rotating the framing.

Let us also introduce the open subset
\operatorname{Quad}_{0}^{\operatorname{fr}}\subset\operatorname{Quad}^{\operatorname{fr}}
consisting of framed quadratic differentials with simple zeroes.
The following result can be obtained by combining Theorem
9.4
with the general results of
[
6
]
. For the particular examples considered here see
[

and also
[

]
.

Theorem 12.5
.

There is an isomorphism of complex manifolds
\mathcal{S}\to\operatorname{Quad}^{\operatorname{fr}}_{0}
. The flat co-ordinates for the integral affine structure on
\mathcal{S}
correspond to integrals
z_{i}=\int_{\gamma_{i}}\sqrt{q(x)}\,dx
along paths connecting zeroes of

The isomoorphism of Theorem

intertwines the action of the cluster modular group

12.4.
Log cluster space

In this section we assume that the reader is familiar with the notion of a projective structure on a Riemann surface, and the extension of this to the meromorphic setting
[
2
]
.
Let
\operatorname{Proj}
denote the space of meromorphic projective structures on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a single pole of order
m+2
. We also consider the
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
cover
\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}
consisting of projective structures equiiped with a framing, namely a choice of one of the
m
horizontal direction at the pole. Given a polynomial
q\in\operatorname{Poly}
, we get a meromorphic projective structure on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
by considering a ratio of two linearly independent solutions to the differential equation

(65)

y^{\prime\prime}(x)=q(x)y(x).

The positive real axis gives a canonical choice of horizontal direction for this projective structure. This construction gives an identification between
\mathcal{P}
and the space
\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}
. Once again, the action of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
on
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to the obvious
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
action on
\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}
rotating the framing.

The space of solutions of the equation (
65
) is a two-dimensional vector space. Consider the decomposition of
\mathbb{C}
into closed sectors of angle
2\pi/5
centered on the rays through the vertices of the polygon
P
. Analytic results
[
35
, Chapter 2]
show that for each such sector there is a one-dimensional space of solutions
y(x)
such that
y(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
in this sector. Such solutions are called subdominant. There is then a map

(66)

m\colon\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}\to\mathcal{M}_{0}

which sends the framed projective structure defined by the equation (
65
) to the collection of lines in the space of solutions defined by the subdominant solutions in the various sectors. This is referred to as the crown-tip map in
[
26
]
. It is known to be a surjective local homeomorphism: see
[
3
, Theorem 7.2]
and
[
35
, Theorem 39.1]
.

Gupta and Mj
[
26
]
construct a homeomorphism

(67)

\operatorname{Poly}\times\operatorname{Diag}\to\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}

which they call the grafting map. The following result is an immediate consequence of their construction and the definition of the log cluster space.

Theorem 12.6
.

There is an isomorphism of complex manifolds
\mathcal{L}\to\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}
. Under this identification the map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
corresponds to the crown-tip map (
66
).

Proof.

Putting together Theorems
12.4
and
12.3
, the grafting map becomes a homeomorphism

(68)

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}^{t}}\to\operatorname{Proj}^{\operatorname{fr}}.

What remains is to show is that, under this identification, the exponential map
\exp_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}
corresponds to the crown-tip map (
66
). Note that this ensures that (
68
) is a biholomorphism.

A point of
\operatorname{Diag}
is a triangulation
T(s)
with a collection of weights
\theta(j)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
associated to the edges
j\in I(s)
. A point of
\operatorname{Poly}
has cross-ratio co-ordinates
r(j)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for edges

. Now perform the grafting operation (
67
), apply the crown-tip map (

) and compute the resulting cross-ratios

for edges

. The claim is that

. Indeed, this is verified in the section ‘Grafting an ideal quadrilateral’ on page 33-34 of
[

.
∎

Under the identification of Theorem
12.6
the action of the cluster modular group again corresponds to the action of

Appendix A
Deformations of fans

In this Appendix we prove a result Proposition
A.3
about deformations of complete fans in a fixed real vector space. Roughly speaking, it states that if we start with a complete fan and then move the constituent cones in such a way that nothing goes wrong in codimension one, then the fan remains complete. More precisely, we require that if a pair of maximal cones meet in a common facet in the original fan, then this remains the case throughout the deformation. This result was applied in Section
10
to prove Proposition
10.1
.

A.1.
Families of cones and fans

We fix a real vector space
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
throughout. By a cone
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
we mean a strongly convex polyhedral cone.
We begin by considering families of cones
\sigma(t)
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
depending on a parameter
t\in[0,1]
. Even if the cone
\sigma(t)
is generated by vectors
n(t)
which vary continuously with
t
, the number of faces of
\sigma(t)
and even its dimension could fail to be constant. We therefore introduce the following more constrained notion.

Definition A.1
.

A combinatorially constant (c.c.) family of cones
\sigma(t)
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
depending on a parameter
t\in[0,1]
is a family of the form
\sigma(t)=\Phi(t)(\sigma)
, where
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
is a fixed cone and
\Phi(t)\in\operatorname{Aut}(N_{\mathbb{R}})
is a continuously varying family of linear automorphisms.

The following result ensures that there is a well-defined notion of a face of a c.c. family of cones
\sigma(t)
, and that such faces are in bijection with the faces of the cone
\sigma(t_{0})
for any fixed
t_{0}\in[0,1]
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\sigma(t)
be a c.c. family of cones and let
\tau\subset\sigma(t_{0})
be a face. Then there is a unique c.c. family of cones
\tau(t)
such that
\tau(t_{0})=\tau
and
\tau(t)
is a face of
\sigma(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

A
fan
is a set of cones that is closed under taking faces and intersections. We say that a collection of cones
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}

generates a fan
if they are all distinct and of dimension
d
, and the union of the sets of faces of
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
is a fan.
We define a
c.c. family of fans
to be a set of c.c. families of cones which forms a fan
\Sigma(t)
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
We say that a set
\sigma_{1}(t),\dots,\sigma_{k}(t)
of c.c. families of cones
generates
a c.c. family of fans if for every
t_{0}\in[0,1]
the set of cones
\sigma_{1}(t_{0}),\dots,\sigma_{k}(t_{0})
generates a fan.

We defer the proof of the following result to the next subsection.

Proposition A.3
.

Let
\sigma_{1}(t),\dots,\sigma_{k}(t)
be a set of c.c. familes of cones such that for some
t_{0}\in[0,1]
the following hold:

(i)

the cones
\sigma_{i}(t_{0})
generate a complete fan,

(ii)

if for some pair of indices

and
\sigma^{j}(t_{0})

.

Then the c.c. families of cones

are complete for every
t\in[0,1]
.

The following example demonstrates the necessity of assumptions (i) and (ii).

Example A.4
.

For
t\in[0,1]
and
0\leqslant k\leqslant 3
we set
n_{k}(t)=e^{2\pi ikt}
and let
\rho_{k}(t)
be the ray in
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
generated by
n_{k}(t)

Let
\sigma_{k}(t)
be the cone
generated by
\rho_{k}(t),\rho_{k-1}(t)
.
We consider the set of cones

\Sigma(t)=\left\{\{0\},\rho_{0}(t),\rho_{1}(t),\rho_{2}(t),\rho_{3}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),\sigma_{2}(t),\sigma_{3}(t)\right\},

and extend the notions of families of cones and fans to include families parameterised by an interval
I\subset[0,1]
in the obvious way.

Restricting to the interval
(0,1/3]

but not for

. Note that, since

Finally and worse, upon including

in the complete fan

Lemma A.5
.

Every cone
\tau({t_{0}})\in\Sigma({t_{0}})
extends uniquely to a c.c. family of cones

in such a way that if
\tau({t_{0}})\subseteq\tau^{\prime}({t_{0}})

\tau(t)\subseteq\tau^{\prime}(t)

Proof.

We use descending induction on dimension with the base case being given by assumption (ii).

k
and higher. Let
\tau(t_{0})\in\Sigma(t_{0})
be a
(k-1)
-dimensional cone. Let
\Sigma^{\geqslant k}_{\tau(t_{0})}
denote the set of cones of dimension
\geqslant k
in

which contain
\tau({t_{0}})
. The c.c. family of cones
\tau^{\prime}(t)
associated to every
\tau^{\prime}({t_{0}})\in\Sigma^{\geqslant k}_{\tau({t_{0}})}
contains a c.c. family of cones deforming
\tau({t_{0}})
and we need to show that the a priori different deformations of
\tau({t_{0}})
actually agree.
Let
\tau^{\prime}(t),\tau^{\prime\prime}(t)
be the c.c. family of cones deforming
\tau^{\prime}({t_{0}}),\tau^{\prime\prime}({t_{0}})\in\Sigma^{\geqslant k}_{\tau({t_{0}})}
, and let
\tau^{\prime}_{\tau}(t),\tau^{\prime\prime}_{\tau}(t)

\tau
inside them. We can find a sequence of
d
-dimensional cones
\sigma_{1}(t_{0}),...,\sigma^{r}(t_{0})
in
\Sigma^{\geqslant k}_{\tau(t_{0})}

\sigma_{1}(t_{0})
containing
\tau^{\prime}(t_{0})
and
\sigma^{r}(t_{0})
containing
\tau^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})
.
Since every
\sigma^{j}(t_{0})

Lemma

gives a set
\Sigma
of c.c. families of cones whose specialization to

yields the fan
\Sigma(t_{0})
. To prove Proposition
A.3
it remains to show that

\Sigma
is a c.c. family of fans. If we glue all the cones in
\Sigma(t)
along their faces as prescribed by
\Sigma(t)
, we get the topological realization
|\Sigma(t)|
of the polyhedral complex
\Sigma(t)
. We do not know at this point whether the natural map
\pi(t)\colon|\Sigma(t)|\to N_{\mathbb{R}}
is bijective. We do know that its restriction to each cone is injective. We also know that
\pi(t)
is a homeomorphism for
t=t_{0}
by assumption (i).
We next make a topological consideration.

Removing the origin in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
and dividing by the positive scaling action gives a projection
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}\supset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\setminus\,\{0\}\to S^{d-1}
. We similarly obtain a quotient

Z(t)=\left(|\Sigma(t)|\,\setminus\,\{0\}\right)/\mathbb{R}_{+}
as well as a map
Z(t)\to S^{d-1}
.
After choosing an orientation of
N_{\mathbb{R}}
, the map
Z(t)\to S^{d-1}
is an oriented map of topological manifolds which is a homeomorphism for
t=t_{0}
. Since the degree is a homotopy invariant,
Z(t)
is a fundamental cycle of
S^{d-1}
for every
t
.

If
\omega
is an orientation form on
S^{d-1}
then
\int_{S^{d-1}}\omega
agrees with the integral of
\omega
over any fundamental cycle.
The integral of
\omega
over the projection of every maximal cone in
\Sigma(t)
to

is positive for all
t
. This observation implies that the interiors of two distinct cones

must have empty intersection in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
for every
t
.

It remains to show that
\pi(t)\colon|\Sigma(t)|\to N_{\mathbb{R}}
is injective for all
t
.
We do so by descending induction on the cone dimension.
We just proved that the restriction of
\pi(t)
to the union of the interiors of all
d
-dimensional cones is injective.
Assume that we proved already that the restriction of
\pi(t)
to the union

Let
\tau\in\Sigma(t)

\tau

\pi(t)
