Malocclusion in 12–13-years old Polish adolescents – an analysis across a spectrum of parental, birth, and developmental factors
Authors: Marta Milona, Joanna Janiszewska-Olszowska, Karolina Węsierska, Alicja Zawiślak, Magdalena Gońda-Domin, Anna Jarząbek, Marina Milona, Andrzej Ciechanowicz, Katarzyna Grocholewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnowledge on the frequency of malocclusion and associated factors allows planning adequate financing in public health care systems for both preventive and treatment measures. The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of malocclusion in Polish adolescents (from West Pomeranian region) and perform an analysis across a spectrum of factors, including BMI, parental age and education, maternal smoking during pregnancy, gestational age and Apgar at birth, breastfeeding, bottle feeding, eruption age of deciduous teeth, eruption age of permanent teeth, lingual frenulum and frequency of dental appointments.MethodsThe study included 738 children aged 12–13 years subjected to a written interview with the parents and a clinical examination by a specialist in orthodontics or pedodontics.ResultsDistal occlusion was found in 22.09%, deep bite was found in 19.92%, dental crowding was observed in 19.51%, crossbite—in 6.23%, anterior open bite—in 1.63%, mesial occlusion in 2.30%, dental spacing—in 2.71%, and scissor bite in 0.68%.The prevalence of malocclusion did not statistically significantly differ between the sexes. A higher proportion of adolescents without malocclusion were children of parents with a higher education level and they visited the dentist more frequently than adolescents with malocclusion. No statistically significant difference was found referring to gestational age at birth, delivery (cesarean or natural), birth weight, Apgar score, maternal smoking during pregnancy, breastfeeding or bottle feeding. Adolescents with malocclusion had fewer permanent teeth erupted. A short lingual frenulum was more prevalent in teenagers with malocclusion. Body height was identified as significant in logistic regression—each additional cm of height resulted in a 3% decrease in malocclusion odds.Conclusion1. Malocclusion is highly prevalent among Polish adolescents.2. Factors associated to malocclusion in adolescents are: parents’ level of education, frequency of dental appointments, short tongue frenulum, number of permanent teeth erupted and body height.3. Gender, body mass index, feeding practices in infancy, pregnancy and birth condition (Apgar, method of delivery and mother smoking while being pregnant) have no association to malocclusion in 12–13 years old adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06887-2.

Introduction

The definition and diagnostic criteria of malocclusion are not precisely unified. According to Edward Angle, “Malocclusion is any deviation from the normal occlusion; it is the result of a lack of harmony in the size and shape of the dental arches, or a lack of balance between the teeth and the supporting bones, or both" [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO), had included malocclusion under the heading of Handicapping Dento Facial Anomaly, defined as “an anomaly which causes disfigurement, or which impedes function, and requiring treatment if the disfigurement or functional defect was likely to be an obstacle to the patient’s physical or emotional well-being” [2].

Nevertheless, malocclusion is an important problem of public health, as together with dental caries and periodontal disease it belongs the three dominant oral diseases in children and adolescents [3]. Malocclusion may be associated to altered facial growth, through impairing the oral functions of chewing, swallowing or speech [4, 5], whereas concurrent incisor protrusion significantly increases the risk of dental injuries. Moreover, malaligned teeth may cause psychosocial burden through their impact on smile, facial attractiveness and self-assessment [6, 7].

Malocclusions are observed all over the world and their type and prevalence depend on internal and environmental factors as well as on diagnostic criteria. Studies can be found referring to various countries, including: China, Estonia, Austria, Saudi Arabia and other countries, performed on children from 3 to 18 years and study groups from 157 to 2810 children. The overall prevalence of malocclusion is variable, ranging from 47.6% to 95.7% [8–11]. Several meta-analyses are available, providing summarized data: 10.6% [12] for pooled prevalence of malocclusion among children in Gulf Cooperation Council countries and 85% for Iranian children [13]. The prevalence of particular types of malocclusion is difficult to indicate precisely due to significant differences in diagnostic criteria used by various authors.

Literature evidence indicates a multifactorial and interdependent character of malocclusion. The presence of malocclusion is attributed to an interaction of genetic, demographic and social factors, including ethnicity [14–16]. The demographic factors considered are age, socioeconomic status and parental level of education [17–19].

As far as environmental factors are concerned, many studies confirm a relationship between malocclusion and nocturnal mouth breathing [5, 20–22] or harmful habits of early childhood (pacifier, thumb sucking) [23, 24]. Moreover, malocclusion is more prevalent in non-breastfed children, and some studies have shown that long-term breastfeeding has a positive effect in preventing malocclusion [25, 26]. Other authors found a relationship between maternal factors during pregnancy or the method of delivery and the occurrence of malocclusion in the child. The frequency of the child's visits to the dentist was also an important modifying factor [27, 28].

Moreover, the occurrence of malocclusion is influenced by oral pathology. Molar hypomineralisation or dental caries, similarly as dental trauma affecting permanent incisors may lead to early permanent tooth loss and dental migration [29–31]. Some authors indicate that ankyloglossia contributes to the development of malocclusion, as well [32, 33].

Knowledge on the frequency of malocclusion and associated factors or conditions allows planning preventive and treatment measures, together with adequate financing in public health care system [34].

The aim of the present study was to assess the prevalence of malocclusion in Polish adolescents (from West Pomeranian region) and analyze potential differences between pediatric groups with and without malocclusion across a spectrum of factors, including: BMI, parental age and education, maternal smoking during pregnancy, gestational age and Apgar at birth, breastfeeding, bottle feeding, eruption age of deciduous teeth, eruption age of permanent teeth, lingual frenulum and frequency of dental appointments.

Material and methods

The present study is a part of a project (Project no. 002/RID/2018/19) within the Program “Regional Initiative of Excellence”, funded by the Polish Ministry of Science and Higher Education. This study was performed according to the latest Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Pomeranian Medical University in Szczecin (decision reference No KB 0012/16/19). An informed written consent has been obtained from all the parents and participants. The study was conducted in the years 2020–2022.

Information for the parents of 12—13 years old children was sent to all 55 public schools in Szczecin and 3 private schools (randomly selected from all the 15 private schools). Primary schools in Szczecin were selected basing on the List of public and non-public primary schools and educational institutions posted on the website of the Szczecin City Office. The inclusion criteria were being a school student, age 12–13 and consent of the parent/guardian for the child's participation in the study. Consent was obtained from parents of 819 adolescents from 28 public schools and 3 private schools – where finally, the study was performed.

The subjects were excluded from the study on malocclusion if the interview data were lacking or incomplete. The flow diagram has been presented in Fig. 1Fig. 1Selection of study participants

The study consisted of a written interview with the parents (developed for the purpose of the study) and a clinical examination by an experienced dentist, specialized in paediatric dentistry or orthodontics. All the investigators had been trained and calibrated before the initiation of the study.

The clinical interview was referred to the parents in the form of a detailed questionnaire on general health information about the child including age, gender, delivery method, week of pregnancy at birth, Apgar, weight at birth, breastfeeding, bottle feeding, eruption age of deciduous teeth, eruption age of permanent teeth, dental appointments as well as chronic diseases. Moreover, the questionnaire comprised data referring to potential risk factors from parents, including parents’ age at child’s birth, parents’ education, cigarettes smoking during pregnancy. The translation of the questionnaire into English has been provided within the study as supplementary material.

The subjects were measured and weighted to the nearest 1 cm, and BMI was calculated using an online calculator [35].

The clinical dental examination was performed in a dental setting, in artificial light, in a sitting position using a dental mirror and a probe. A standard dental diagram was used to mark all the teeth present and calculate the number of deciduous and permanent teeth. All data obtained has been computed for further analysis.

Attachments of upper and lower Lip frenula were described according to the following criteria: 1. Mucosal attachment, 2. Gingival attachment, 3. Papillary attachment, 4. Papilla penetrating attachment. Lingual frenulum was categorized as normal, if tongue mobility was not limited, otherwise it was described as short. Occlusion was classified as normal, anterior open bite, deep bite, distal occlusion, mesial occlusion or crossbite. Moreover, crowding or spacing was noted in the examination chart, if present.

The statistical analysis adopted a significance level (α) of 0.05 for all the tests. The Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized to assess the normality of variable distributions. Variables deviating from a normal distribution were reported using median and interquartile range (Mdn [Q1, Q3]), while categorical variables were described using frequency (n) and percentage (%).

Non-parametric methods were employed for data not conforming to a normal distribution. Differences between two independent groups within numerical variables were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Independence of two categorical variables was tested using Pearson chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and proportion test.

The relationship between the outcome variable, malocclusion occurrence, and selected predictors was explored using a binomial logistic regression analysis with a cauchit link function. Model evaluation was conducted using Nagelkerke's R-squared, a pseudo R-squared measure for logistic regression. The effects of the regression coefficients were quantified using odds ratios (OR).

The discriminative ability of the model was visually represented by a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, with the Area Under the Curve (AUC) computed to indicate the model's overall discriminative capacity. The model's goodness-of-fit, a measure of how well the model's predicted probabilities align with observed outcomes, was tested using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF below 3.0 indicating low correlation.

Residual distribution normality was evaluated using Q-Q plots, and the final determination was based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) normality test. This test also enabled the examination of dispersion and outliers.

Correlation analyses were performed employing the Spearman method to assess non-parametric associations between variables. P-values were estimated using an asymptotic approximation of the t statistic. Given the constrained sample size, the extensive array of parameters in the correlation analysis, and the exploratory objective of generating hypotheses rather than establishing conclusive effects, no adjustments for multiple comparisons were implemented.

Analyses were conducted using the R Statistical language (version 4.1.1; R Core Team, 2021) on Windows 10 pro 64 bit (build 19,045). Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate the relationships between various types of malocclusions – namely, anterior open bite, deep bite, distal occlusion, mesial occlusion, cross bite, crowding, spacing, scissor bite, and treatment status—and a comprehensive set of clinical, demographic, and perinatal parameters.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

Sociodemographic data

The study sample consisted of N = 738 participants, with a slight predominance of male subjects, representing 52.71% (n = 389) of the total sample. Female participants accounted for the remaining 47.29% (n = 349). The median age was 12.74 years, indicating that most of the participants were in their early adolescence, with the interquartile range (IQR) between 12.25 to 13.25 years, encapsulating the age range for the middle 50% of the sample. A deviation from the intended examination timing (age 12), was noted, caused by the duration of the study.

Anthropometric measurements, which included weight, height, derived Body Mass Index (BMI), and BMI percentile, were collected across the sample. The weight of the participants exhibited a median of 50.0 kg, with an IQR of 43.0 to 59.0 kg, demonstrating a moderate spread around the median. Similarly, the median height was recorded as 160.00 cm, with a relatively narrow IQR from 155.0 cm to 166.0 cm.

The calculated BMI, a key indicator of healthy weight considering height, had a median value of 19.23 kg/m2 (IQR: 17.47 kg/m2 to 21.91 kg/m2). The BMI percentile, providing a comparison of a participant's BMI relative to others of the same age and sex, had a median value of 61.00 (IQR: 33.0 to 84.0).

Characteristics of malocclusion

The majority of the adolescents 53.79% (n = 397) had normal occlusion, e.g. Angle Class I, a normal overjet and overbite. However, it's noteworthy that only 39.9% (n = 286) of the participants were entirely free from any form of malocclusion, including crowding or spacing.

A significant proportion of the participants presented with various forms of malocclusions. The most prevalent was distal occlusion, observed in 22.09% (n = 163), followed by deep bite in 19.92% (n = 147) of the participants and by dental crowding in 19.51% (n = 144) of the sample. Less prevalent malocclusions included cross bite (6.23%, n = 46), anterior open bite (1.63%, n = 12), mesial occlusion (2.30%, n = 17), and dental spacing (2.71%, n = 20). The least prevalent malocclusion was scissor bite, which was found in only 0.68% (n = 5) of the participants.

It is noteworthy that, 4.88% (n = 36) of the participants were already undergoing treatment for their malocclusions. The median number of different malocclusions per participant was 1.0, with an IQR of 0 to 1.0, e.g. most participants with malocclusions had a single malocclusion.

The prevalence of malocclusion did not statistically significantly differ between female and male adolescents in the provided sample (p = 0.384). The adolescents’ age did not significantly differ between the two groups (p = 0.249), either.

Anthropometric measures including weight, height, BMI, and BMI percentile did not show significant differences between the two groups.

While no significant differences were detected in these demographic and anthropometric characteristics among adolescents with and without malocclusion, these factors might still play a role in the occurrence and progression of specific types of malocclusions. This underlined the necessity for a multifaceted, integrative approach in malocclusion research. The data presented in Table 3. revealed statistically significant differences in the educational level of parents, particularly mothers, between children with malocclusion and those with normal occlusion. Significant disparities were found in the number of permanent teeth, erupting teeth, and total teeth count between adolescents with and without malocclusion; those with malocclusion exhibited a diminished count of permanent and total teeth, yet a heightened incidence of erupting teeth.

In contrary, the frenulum morphology of the upper and lower lips did not exhibit a significant difference with malocclusion occurrence. However, a notable deviation was observed in the lingual frenulum, with short frenulum manifesting at a higher prevalence in adolescents with malocclusion.

The eruption timelines of both deciduous and permanent teeth did not demonstrate significant variations between adolescents with and without malocclusion within this cohort.

However, a significant divergence was identified in the frequency of dental consultations between adolescents with and without malocclusion, as more frequent dental consultations were associated to a lower prevalence of malocclusion.

Among demographic factors, mesial occlusion exhibited a modest positive correlation with age (ρ = 0.084, p = 0.022). Sex-specific differences were evident, with deep bite demonstrating a negative correlation with female sex (ρ = −0.105, p = 0.004), implying a lower prevalence in females, whereas crowding showed a positive association (ρ = 0.082, p = 0.027), indicating a higher occurrence in females. Body mass index (BMI) was positively correlated with crossbite (ρ = 0.131, p = 0.001). Referring to perinatal variables deep bite was positively associated with gestational age at birth (ρ = 0.101, p = 0.006), while crowding displayed a negative correlation (ρ = −0.080, p = 0.030). Apgar score was negatively correlated with crowding (ρ = −0.089, p = 0.018). Feeding practices during infancy also emerged as influential. Crossbite was negatively correlated with breastfeeding duration (ρ = −0.073, p = 0.048), underscoring the potential benefits of prolonged breastfeeding. Conversely, crowding showed a positive association with bottle feeding duration (ρ = 0.087, p = 0.045), which may relate to altered sucking mechanics or nutritional patterns affecting arch development.

Deep bite was negatively correlated with the number of permanent teeth (ρ = −0.142, p < 0.001). Crossbite demonstrated a negative association with permanent teeth eruption age (ρ = −0.113, p = 0.006).

Referring to frenulum characteristics, crowding correlated positively with upper lip gingival frenulum (ρ = 0.114, p = 0.002) and lower lip mucosal frenulum (ρ = 0.076, p = 0.038), while spacing showed negative (ρ = −0.072, p = 0.050) and positive (ρ = 0.160, p < 0.001, ρ = 0.221, p < 0.001) correlations with upper lip gingival and both upper and lower lip penetrating papillary frenula, respectively. Treatment status was positively linked to upper lip mucosal frenulum (ρ = 0.111, p = 0.002) but negatively to upper lip papillary frenulum (ρ = −0.102, p = 0.005). Scissor bite was positively associated with upper lip papillary frenulum (ρ = 0.078, p = 0.034).

Discussion

The present investigation aimed to assess potential associations between various environmental factors and malocclusion in adolescents, thereby improving the knowledge on the interplay of contributing factors. Each table presented a unique dimension of the child's life and development, facilitating a holistic perspective of potential determinants of malocclusion in the paediatric population. By discerning these distinguishing attributes, the study aspires to contribute to a more intricate understanding of malocclusion's etiopathogenesis, ultimately refining preventive strategies and therapeutic approaches, and enhancing oral health outcomes in paediatric population.

The finding that 53.79% (n = 397) of the children in the present study had normal occlusion (Class I occlusion, a normal overjet and overbite), whereas only 39.9% (n = 286) of participants were entirely free both from of malocclusion and crowding or spacing is consistent with the findings by some studies [27, 36]. The prevalence of different types of malocclusion in the literature has been presented in Table 8.

In the present study distal occlusion was the most prevalent malocclusion, similarly as in most other studies found [8–10, 36–39]. The prevalence of 22.09% in the present study is lower than in most studies; the highest percentage of children with distal occlusion (50.9%) was found in the study on 2810 Chinese children aged 7–9 years [8], whereas much a lower prevalence (7.6%) was noted in Swedish children (mean age 11.5y). The lowest prevalence of distal occlusion noted in Swedish adolescents was found at the final follow-up examination (the previous examinations were performed at ages 3 and 7) [37]. Thus, it is possible that a high percentage of the examined children were subjected to orthodontic treatment prior to the examination. In contrary, the Chinese study [8] with the highest percentage of children with distal occlusion has the youngest study group among the papers found. In the latter studies found, the prevalence of distal occlusion ranged from 25.5% in Estonia [11] to 44.7% in Turkey [38]. The global prevalence of distal occlusion between the ages 7–20 years was 20.2% (4.4–40.7%) in a metaanalysis [39], whereas in the mixed dentition in a metaanalysis by Alhmmadi et al. it was 23.11%, which is very similar to the present study [39, 40]. Referring to the prevalence in Europe, the metaanalysis by Alhmmadi et al. reports 31.95% in the mixed dentition. De Ridder et al. in their study of 2022 report 25.1% of Angle Class II occlusion [40]. The differences between the studies may be due to the age of the examined subjects, previous orthodontic treatment, and ethnic differences. This could be the reason for a huge difference between a Chinese study by Yu et al. with 50.9% and a Chinese metaanalysis by Lin et al. with 9.91% [8, 41]. The lowest prevalence of Class II malocclusion in a metaanalysis (2.09%) was reported for subjects aged 11–40 years from Gulf Cooperation Council Countries [12].

Referring to crossbite, which was present in 6.23% adolescents in the present study (n = 46), with no differentiation between anterior and posterior crossbite, a crossbite was diagnosed when the buccal cusps or incisal edges of upper teeth were in occlusion palatally to buccal cusps or incisal edges of the lower teeth. However, in anterior crossbite Angle Class I (or II) had to be present, as a reverse overjet with Angle Class III was diagnosed as mesial occlusion.

In the study on Romanian children. posterior crossbite was present in 6.7%, which is very similar to present study, although the subjects were slightly younger [42]. An analysis of occlusal traits in Estonian children aged 7,1–10,4y found a higher prevalence of posterior crossbite (10.2%) [11]; the lower prevalence in the present study may reflect effects of early orthodontic treatment in the present study group. In 7–9 years old Chinese anterior crossbite was found with a higher prevalence (10.5), probably due to the definition of anterior crossbite (in the study cited) as an occlusal characteristic—a reverse overjet of one or more incisors or canines, which may be present in sagittal maxillary deficiency (prevalent in the Chinese population). Posterior crossbite of one or more molars was found in 2.6%; the low prevalence comparing to present study may reflect a younger age of the population compared to the present study (premolars and second permanent molars may erupt in crossbite, influencing the prevalence of this malocclusion) [8].

Crossbite was diagnosed, with a higher prevalence (in 14.2%) than in the present study in Italian children aged 12.2 y ± 0.6, probably due to exclusion criteria comprising previous or ongoing orthodontic treatment [36]. Similarly, in the study by Steinmassl et al. anterior crossbite was found in 14.6%, whereas posterior in 15.3% of children at mean age of 8.7 years, which is more than in the present study, probably due to early orthodontic treatment in Poland [9].

In the study on Saudi Arabian 15 years old boys Alyami et al. found 21.7% crossbite [10]; the higher prevalence comparing to present study is probably due to inclusion criteria of the study cited that comprised no history of previous orthodontic treatment. Thus, it seems that early orthodontic treatment of crossbite may significantly reduce the incidence of this malocclusion.

Borzabadi-Farahani et al. in their study on Iranian adolescents 11-14y old found anterior crossbite in 8.4%, and posterior crossbite in 12.4% subjects [43]—the high prevalence is probably due to the diagnostic criteria including edge-to-edge position to crossbite.

The higher prevalence of crossbite in the mixed dentition (12% in children of mean age 8.3 years) noted by Grabowski et al., is probably due to excluding subjects under or after orthodontic treatment from the study cited [42].

A low percentage of crossbite (anterior: 0.4%, posterior: 5.1%) was reported by Dimberg et al. in 11 years-old Swedish children, but a crossbite was diagnosed with at least two teeth involved [38].

In summary, it is difficult to compare results of different studies referring to crossbite, as the diagnostic criteria surprisingly vary.

Anterior open bite in adolescents in the Literature varies in frequency from 0.4% in 11 years-old orthodontically untreated Swedish children to 14.8% in orthodontically untreated boys aged 13–18 years [10, 37]. The high prevalence in the latter study seems to be associated to pubertal mandibular growth and development with concurrent posterior rotation [44]. The prevalence of 1.63% in the present study is the same as by Borzabadi-Fahrahani (1.6%) on Iranian adolescents 11-14y old [45]. The results are consistent, probably due to a similar age (and a low age range) of the subjects included and not using previous treatment as an exclusion criterium.

Referring to deep bite, in the present study it was diagnosed when lower incisors had a contact with the palatal mucosa, whereas in the study by Alyami et al. deep bite was diagnosed when overbite exceeded 3.5 mm [10], The diagnostic criteria for the term “deep bite” vary among the studies making comparisons difficult. Thus, the prevalence of deep bite in the present study is 19.92% and a slightly higher prevalence 21.2% was found in the study by Alyami et al. [10]. A much higher prevalence of deep bite was reported (43.8%) in the study by Yu et al. in children aged 7–9 years, however a “severe deep bite” with overbite exceeding 2/3 was stated in 6.2% children, nevertheless it should be underlined that in the studies discussed the subjects examined were in different stages of dental development (the eruption of second permanent molars in adolescents influences the overbite) [8].

In the study by Todor et al. the prevalence of deep bite was much higher than in the present study, as deep bite was diagnosed with overbite exceeding 2/3 [36]. In the study by Sepp et al. deep bite was diagnosed in 51.8% of the subjects and the criterium was overbite exceeding 3.5 mm [11]. In the Austrian study overbite of 4 mm or more was found in 51% of children (mean age of 8.7 years), whereas overbite equal or more than 5 mm was found in 28.7% [9]. It can be summarized that in most studies deep bite is not understood as a vertical malocclusion, but as an occlusal trait.

Mesial occlusion or Class III malocclusion in the present study of 2.3% was not very prevalent comparing to Literature, where it ranged from 0.4% in 11 years old Swedish children (previously subjected to early treatment) to 11.4% in Saudi Arabian boys (orthodontically untreated) aged 13–18 [10, 37]. The differences in the results reported seem to be due to age difference and previous orthodontic treatment. Moreover, Class III may be more prevalent in boys [46].

Scissor bite is a rare malocclusion; in the present study was found in 5 subjects, (0.68%), which is within the range of incidence in the Literature ranging from 0.3 to 3.5% [36, 38]. The differences may be due to various age of the subjects included. As permanent premolars and second molars erupt, some of them may tip lingually or palatally, causing a scissor bite. Thus, in children the incidence of this malocclusion increases with age [8].

Dental crowding results from a discrepancy between teeth widths and dental arch width (usually caused by a transverse deficiency of the dental arch and manifested as anterior crowding) or from a reduced dental arch length due to caries (or loss) of deciduous molars and a subsequent mesial movement of the first permanent molars (manifesting as crowding of the canines or premolars). Thus, it seems that in adolescents, the incidence of crowding should increase with the eruption of permanent premolars and canines. In the present study, the overall incidence of crowding was 19.51%. Similar results (14.4% in the upper dental arch and 11% in the lower) were recently reported by Alyami et al. for Saudi Arabian children aged 15.24 ± 1.70 [10]. A higher incidence of crowding (29.6%) was reported in China by Yu et al., although in children aged 7–9 years, which might indicate differences between various populations [8]. However, a much higher prevalence of crowding was reported in other countries, including Romania (47.5%) [42], Estonia (49.7%) [11], Turkey (66.2%) [38] and Italy (45.9%) [36]. Nevertheless, it is impossible, that the lower incidence of crowding in the present Polish population of adolescents results from orthodontic treatment, as only 4.88% children were under orthodontic treatment.

Dental spacing results from a disproportion between the width or length of the dental arch (both determining the amount of space) and the number or the widths of the teeth in the dental arch. In permanent dentition dental spacing should not occur. When present, it may be due to protrusion of the incisors in one or both dental arches concurrent with a malocclusion (distal occlusion, deep bite, open bite, mesial occlusion). Alternatively, dental spacing may result from reduced widths of the teeth or from missing or unerupted teeth.

Spacing is a normal phenomenon in the early mixed dentition, as upper incisors often erupt with diastema, which is closed during the eruption of canines. In the present study the mean age of the subjects was 12.74 years, (with the interquartile range (IQR) between 12.25 to 13.25 years, encapsulating the age range for the middle 50% of the sample), thus many of the children were in permanent dentition – the total number of children with 28 permanent teeth was 350 (47.4%). The prevalence of spacing in the present study is 2.71%, whereas in the studies on subjects younger than 10 years it was higher, e. g. 11% and 56%, respectively, as subjects in mixed dentition were examined [8, 9]. A study by Dimberg et al. [38] on Swedish children with mean age of 11.5 years revealed spacing in 31%, which seems normal for late mixed dentition at this age. A study on children in a similar age to the present study was performed in Italy by Perillo et al. [35] on 703 children aged 12.2 y ± 0.6, however the percentage of children with spacing was 22.9, which is much higher than in the present investigation. The Italian children examined were only slightly younger than those in the present study, thus the difference in the prevalence of dental spacing might not result from the age or dentition, but from ethnic differences. A higher prevalence of dental spacing in children older than in the present study was found by Bilgic et al. in their study on Turkish subjects aged 12.5–16.2, WHO found spacing in 14.8% subjects aged from 12.5 to 16.2 years [47]. Similarly, a recent study by Alyami et al. revealed 18.8% children with upper and 11% children with lower spacing in the group of 1094 teenagers aged 15.24 ± 1.70 [10].

The present finding on no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of malocclusion between female and male adolescent is in accordance with most other epidemiologic studies [18, 38, 41, 48] and is partially inconsistent with a study on Chinese adolescents in Shanghai, where it was found that although there were no significant differences in the frequency of malocclusion between boys and girls, the frequency of particular traits of malocclusion was significantly different between the sexes; increased overjet, deep bite and anterior spacing were observed significantly more often in boys than in girls [46]. This confirmed earlier findings by Yu et al., that boys exhibited a higher rate of overbite and a higher frequency of dental spacing than girls, whereas girls showed a higher prevalence of anterior crowding [8]. However, Lin et al., based on their meta-analysis, determined that Chinese males had a slightly higher risk of malocclusion than girls RR 1.04 (95%CI: 1.01–1.06) [41]. Contrary results were published in the study on adolescents in Romania that female gender is an independent risk factor for malocclusion [42] especially for Angle Class II. Also, the study on Iranian children, observed a higher prevalence of malocclusion in girls [13]

In some studies, the authors indicate that adolescent girls are more likely to have orthodontic treatment demands [49, 50].

Previous studies on the prevalence of malocclusion in subjects between ages 7–20 have been summarized in Table 9.

The analysis performed by the authors of the present study embraced a comprehensive range of parameters including demographic and anthropometric data, parental characteristics, perinatal records, infant feeding habits, dental attributes, and the overall health status of the child. The intent was to illuminate the multifactorial nature of malocclusion and pinpoint key variables that may contribute to its onset. By doing so, this research provided a robust and inclusive understanding of the multifaceted factors influencing malocclusion in paediatric cohorts.

The median BMI of the study group indicates that most participants fall within a normal weight range, as per standard BMI categorization for adolescents. As the BMI percentile, providing a comparison of a participant's BMI relative to others of the same age and sex, had a median value of 61.00 (IQR: 33.00 to 84.00), more than half of the participants had a BMI above the median for their respective age and sex groups. Similarly, to the study on adolescents in Karachi [28], we did not find any relationship between BMI and the malocclusion. Nevertheless, some authors draw attention to the relationship between BMI and orthodontic treatment of patients. Hancock et al. in their study point out that growth begins earlier in overweight and obese adolescents and continues at a rate similar to normal-weight children during orthodontic treatment, resulting in larger final skeletal dimensions [50]. Orthodontic treatment could thus begin earlier in overweight patients to time care with growth, and clinicians can anticipate that overweight/obese patients finish treatment with proportionally larger, bimaxillary-prognathic craniofacial dimensions. At the same time, a prospective study reported by Saloom et al. showed that there were no significant differences in treatment duration between normal-weight and obese patients undergoing orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances [51]. The authors of the study cited concluded that obesity was not a risk factor for poorer orthodontic treatment outcome with fixed appliances. Nevertheless, a correlation between BMI and DMFT has been noticed by Militi et al., confirming the influence of poor nutrition habits on both indices [46].

With regard to parental characteristics of our study, the level of mothers’ education (especially higher education) was significantly associated with the prevalence of malocclusion contrary to studies by other authors, who found no relationship between the education level of parents [52, 53], mother [28] or father [54] and orthodontic treatment need of children. The present results indicating that maternal education level may play a role in the risk of malocclusion in adolescents may be due to the fact that children included in the study could be undergoing a treatment with removable appliances. It is known that orthodontic treatment rates are higher in families with a good socioeconomical status [18] and a higher parental level of education. It is important to note that 36 children (4.88%) were currently in orthodontic treatment at the moment of the examination). Thus, normal occlusion in a certain percentage of children could result from finished orthodontic treatment, although no detailed data was collected referring to previous orthodontic treatment.

A preterm or post-term birth was not a determining factor for the development of malocclusion in this particular sample contrary to the deciduous dentition, where premature birth is associated with a higher percentage of malocclusion [55, 56] and an increased the risk of transversal maxillary insufficiency and posterior crossbite [56, 57]. Moreover, prematurely born children have distal occlusion and deep bite more often [58]. An increased prevalence of altered dental eruption and impacted teeth [59] as well as hypodontia and microdontia [60, 61] were found in preterm children. A higher value of IOTN (Index of Orthodontic Treatment Needs) in premature born children reflects a higher complexity of the malocclusions [56]. The severity of malocclusion has been proved to depend on gestation age at premature birth and even 4/5 children born before the 29th week of gestation may have a severe malocclusion [62]. However, from the results of the present study, it can be assumed that the influence of preterm birth on the occlusion is diminishing with further growth and development.

Apgar score, a measure of the physical condition of a newborn, was not significantly associated with malocclusion in this study, similarly as reported for Polish children by Olczak-Kowalczyk et al. [62]. In contrary, Amaral et al. [63] showed that children with an Apgar score less than 7 had a higher risk (PR = 1.32; 95% CI, 1.06–1.64) of malocclusion [64].

Method of delivery in the present study was not associated to malocclusion contrary to some authors; Zhou et al. have been observed that the method of delivery played an important role in the morbidity of malocclusion but it must be noted that the study was conducted among children 6 years old and younger [65]. Cattaneo et al. in a study on adults indicates that among subjects with normal delivery 100% presented class I occlusion, however none of the subjects with a malocclusion had a normal labour/delivery [64]. Similarly, Pirttiniemi et al. and Schoenwetter et al. reported a relationship between the incidence of some types of malocclusions in adolescents and forceps delivery [66, 67]. On the other hand, Janerich and Carlos [68] found no association between complications of pregnancy or delivery and occlusal characteristics. These results were also confirmed in the study by Goymen who’s results suggested that the birth method did not have a considerable effect on the development of the craniofacial skeletal system [69, 70].

The significantly lower odds of developing malocclusion among children who received intensive and frequent treatment for various medical conditions in their first three years of life may indicate a protective effect from early and intensive medical interventions and prompt identification and management of potential dental issues, thus preventing the progression to malocclusion. This observation underscores the potential influence of these parameters on malocclusion development, aligning with the concept that dentition patterns and total teeth count can modulate dental and jaw alignment.

Maternal smoking during pregnancy, a known risk factor for many adverse health outcomes, did not demonstrate a significant association with malocclusion in this sample, either. No previous studies were found referring to maternal smoking and malocclusions, that could be used for comparison. However, it is worth emphasizing that smoking during pregnancy is associated with a significant risk of missing teeth in the offspring [71].

The results obtained in this study suggest that breastfeeding duration, may not be a significant factor in the development of malocclusion in adolescents although many studies indicate that breastfeeding is seen as a determining factor for craniofacial development because it promotes intense activity of the orofacial muscle [72]. According to the WHO, breastfeeding should be exclusive until sixth month age and supplemented until two years old or later. Moreover, some studies indicate that breastfeeding can prevent the occurrence of sucking habits which is considered as a factor predisposing to malocclusion. Children with finger or pacifier sucking habits are more susceptible to excessive overjet and open bite and an increased overbite [23, 24]. Peres et al. in their systematic review and meta-analysis showed that breastfeeding decreased the risk of malocclusion, and in children breastfed for a longer period, malocclusion occurred less frequently in those breastfed for a shorter time [25]. These findings were also confirmed in 2017 by a systematic review and meta-analysis by Doğramacı et al. where young children with a history of suboptimal breastfeeding had a higher prevalence and risk ratio for malocclusions. Children who had shorter duration of breastfeeding (less than 12 months) had an increased risk of developing class II canine relationship (RR 1.65; 95% CI: 1.38 to 1.97) and anterior open bite (RR 3.58; 95% CI: 2.55 −5.03) [73]. In contrary, the findings of a systematic review by Brazilian authors do not support any association between breastfeeding or bottle feeding in term of its duration and the occurrence of malocclusion in mixed and permanent dentition in children [74].

The results of the present study indicate that bottle feeding practices, as represented in this study, may not be major contributors to malocclusion risk, either. Similarly, Teixeira et al. did not find any significant relationship between bottle feeding and its duration with the prevalence of malocclusion in children with permanent dentition [24]. It seems that bottle feeding can have a higher impact on malocclusion in children with primary dentition. A relationship has been observed in deciduous dentition between posterior crossbites and bottle-feeding by Gonzales et al., with the number of crossbites increasing with the duration of bottle-feeding [75]. Chen et al. in a study on 3–6-year-old children in Beijing observed that children WHO were bottle-fed for over 18 months had a 1.45-fold higher risk of Class II malocclusion and a 1.43-fold higher risk of class II canine relationship compared with those WHO were bottle-fed for 6–18 months [76].

The present study found a significant correlation between short lingual frenulum and malocclusion, contrary to Yoon et al. [76] who found no relationship between skeletal malocclusions according to Angle or dental arch widths and ankyloglossia. On the other side, a relationship between ankyloglossia and craniofacial morphology was reported by Jang et al. [77], Meenakshi et al. [32] Pompeia et al. [31], Guilleminault et al. [78] as well as by Srinivasan et al. [79], similarly to the present study. The frenulum morphology of the upper and lower lips did not exhibit a significant difference with malocclusion occurrence.

Referring to the dental appointments, their frequency reflects the level of dental care and patient or parent compliance. Thus, children who had more frequent dental appointments had a better occlusion, similarly as reported by Zhou et al. [65]. This discrepancy highlights the crucial role of regular oral healthcare in mitigating malocclusion risk. Regular dental check-ups encompass preventive care, crucial for early identification and mitigation of potential oral health issues, including those escalating to malocclusion. The irregularity in dental appointments could delay diagnosis and treatment, whereas an irregularity of dental appointments may signify underlying socioeconomic constraints, that impede access to dental care.

In the present study the number of permanent teeth erupted was higher in children without malocclusion than in those with malocclusion, despite that erupting permanent teeth might potentially misalign and new occlusal abnormalities may arise. The finding may be due to previous or ongoing treatment with removable appliances, creating adequate space for normal dental eruption.

A possible Limitation of the present study comprises including solely children Living in a town. Moreover, some data from a questionnaire might be subjective or might not be objectively verified. Future studies on malocclusion in adolescents could focus on other etiologic factors, including genetic background. Another limitation refers to precise timing of the study referring to metrical age of the subjects examined. It was intended to examine 12 years old adolescents. However, the duration of the study caused an imprecision in the exact timing of the examination moment. On the other side, in our opinion this should not bias the results as a high individual variation of development in this developmental period exists.

The significant inverse relationship between height and malocclusion from the logistic regression allow to suspect that taller adolescents may be characterized by superior nutritional status and more balanced overall body development, thus a reduced prevalence of malocclusion. Lastly, genetic factors could play a role, genes contributing to body height could also have effect on the development of occlusion.

Conclusion

Malocclusion is highly prevalent among Polish adolescents. Factors associated to malocclusions are parents’ level of education, frequency of dental appointments, short tongue frenulum, number of permanent teeth erupted, total number of teeth and body height. Gender, BMI, feeding practices in infancy, pregnancy and birth condition (Apgar, method of delivery and mother smoking while being pregnant) have no association to malocclusion in 12–13 years old adolescents.
