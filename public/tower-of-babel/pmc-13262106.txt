Peritraumatic reactions during sexual assault: associations with assault characteristics and mental health
Authors: Isabel Kreis, Akiah Astral Ottesen, Andrea Rustand, Tore Wentzel-Larsen, Siri Thoresen, Ines Blix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Peritraumatic reactions during sexual assault are central to societal rape stereotypes and may play a role in post-trauma mental health. Nevertheless, existing research is limited. In this cross-sectional study, we explored a broad spectrum of emotional, cognitive, and behavioural peritraumatic reactions and investigated their associations with assault characteristics, sexual assault history, and symptoms of post-traumatic stress and depression/anxiety.Methods:Recent sexual assault victims (N = 425, 96% female, M = 26.20 years old) completed a 23-item peritraumatic reactions questionnaire developed in collaboration with lived-experience experts. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was employed to identify latent peritraumatic reaction factors. Associations between factor scores, various assault characteristics, history of sexual assault, and current symptoms of post-traumatic stress and depression/anxiety were assessed with t-tests, ANOVA, correlations and linear regressions.Results:EFA revealed six factors: Dissociation & freeze, Cognitive strategies & thought processes, Active defense behaviour, Negative emotions, Perceived life threat, and Reactive surrender. Feelings of helplessness and non-active behavioural responses, captured by the Dissociation & freeze and the Reactive surrender factors, were much more pronounced than active resistance. Perpetrator strategies involving threats and physical violence were related to higher levels of most peritraumatic reactions, apart from Dissociation & freeze. A closer victim-perpetrator relationship was associated with lower perceived life threat, whereas a history of sexual assault was associated with higher levels of Reactive surrender. All factors except Cognitive strategies & thought processes and active defense behaviour were positively correlated with post-traumatic stress and depression/anxiety symptoms, even when controlling for assault characteristics and sexual assault history.Conclusions:These findings highlight the heterogeneity of peritraumatic responses and challenge rape stereotypes of the actively resisting victim by identifying reactive surrender as a common survival strategy. The robust associations between diverse peritraumatic reactions and post-trauma mental health propose these acute responses as vital clinical indicators for post-trauma adjustment.KEYWORDS: Peritraumatic reactions, trauma, rape, sexual assault characteristics, post-traumatic stress, anxiety, depressionPALABRAS CLAVE: Reacciones peritraumáticas, trauma, violación, características de la agresión sexual, estrés postraumático, ansiedad, depresión

Introduction

In the face of traumatic events, such as sexual assault (SA), victims may exhibit a wide range of peritraumatic reactions; that is, transient patterns of emotions and physiological responses, cognitive and perceptual processes, and concrete behaviours. The nature and intensity of peritraumatic reactions have been linked to subsequent mental health problems among diverse groups of trauma survivors (e.g. Dyb et al., 2008; Lawyer et al., 2006; Rizvi et al., 2008). However, research on peritraumatic reactions specifically in SA victims is limited, despite their pivotal role in post-trauma mental health and legal processes (Burgin, 2019; Rubin & Bell, 2023).

Comprehensive research from natural disaster survivors (Massazza et al., 2021), mixed-trauma samples (victims of sexual or physical assault; Kecala et al., 2023), and adult survivors of childhood SA (Katz et al., 2021) has uncovered various patterns of peritraumatic reactions within and across emotional, cognitive, and behavioural domains. These include distress (e.g. fear, sadness, physical arousal), mental defeat (e.g. ‘giving up’), and cognitive overload (Massazza et al., 2021), or active (e.g. screaming) and passive (e.g. keeping quiet and motionless) behavioural reactions (Kecala et al., 2023). In addition, tonic immobility and dissociation are commonly observed (Kecala et al., 2023; Massazza et al., 2021), sometimes grouped under the umbrella of an overall automatic reaction pattern (Katz et al., 2021). Tonic immobility describes an involuntary state of muscular paralysis, resulting in an inability to move or talk, often accompanied by physiological changes such as lower body temperature (Fusé et al., 2007). It is sometimes referred to as a ‘freeze’ reaction, even though tonic immobility and freeze reactions were originally conceptualized as two distinct responses to threat.1 Peritraumatic dissociation is a closely related response (Abrams et al., 2009; Covers et al., 2022), characterized by feelings of detachment from oneself and/or one’s environment, out-of-body experiences, altered time perception and/or emotional numbness.

Within the field of SA research, tonic immobility (Barbano et al., 2025; Bovin et al., 2008; Covers et al., 2022; de la Torre Laso, 2024; deMello et al., 2023; Fusé et al., 2007; Morabito et al., 2025; Möller et al., 2017; Rubin & Bell, 2023) and dissociation (deMello et al., 2023; Hetzel-Riggin, 2010; Jones et al., 2018; Zylberstajn et al., 2023) have arguably received the most empirical attention. Both peritraumatic immobility (Bovin et al., 2008; Morabito et al., 2025; Möller et al., 2017; Rubin & Bell, 2023) and dissociation (deMello et al., 2023; Hetzel-Riggin, 2010; Jones et al., 2018) have been linked to adverse mental health outcomes in SA victims, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS). However, findings remain mixed, with three recent studies failing to replicate associations between post-trauma mental health and peritraumatic tonic immobility (Barbano et al., 2025; deMello et al., 2023), or dissociation (Zylberstajn et al., 2023).

Notably, research into other peritraumatic reactions during SA remains scarce. Only a few studies have moved beyond tonic immobility and dissociation to examine factors such as a perceived sense of control (Salcioglu et al., 2024) or active resistance behaviour (Feinstein et al., 2011). Consequently, there remains a need for comprehensive assessments that systematically map the full spectrum of victim reactions during SA and evaluate how they relate to later mental health. Distinguishing SA from other forms of trauma in this context is crucial for several reasons:

Firstly, peritraumatic reactions during SA appear to follow different patterns from reactions to other forms of trauma. For example, peritraumatic emotions, such as fear, detachment, shame and guilt, as well as nonactive (passive) behavioural responses, such as doing as told and keeping still, and verbal negotiation attempts seem to be more pronounced among rape victims than victims of other interpersonal crimes (Amstadter & Vernon, 2008; Kaysen et al., 2005). Similarly, peritraumatic tonic immobility appears to be more prominent during SA than other traumatic events (Kalaf et al., 2017; Rubin & Bell, 2023). Peritraumatic reactions during SA may additionally be shaped by assault characteristics and previous SA history. The victims’ relationship to the perpetrator, assault severity and the degree of violence likely affect both emotional and behavioural responses, especially fear (Feinstein et al., 2011). A history of prior trauma, including sexual abuse, has previously been associated with dissociation (deMello et al., 2023) as well as the tonic immobility/freeze response (Möller et al., 2017), and may play a role in other reactions as well.

Secondly, compared to other types of traumas, SA appears to be associated with poorer post-traumatic mental health. For instance, among female undergraduates, PTSS severity was higher following SA than after motor vehicle accidents or the sudden loss of a loved one (Kelley et al., 2009). Similarly, in a large clinical sample of children and adolescents, those identifying SA as the worst traumatic event they had experienced had higher PTSS levels than those citing domestic violence, severe bullying/threats, community violence, non-interpersonal trauma or sudden loss/illness of a loved one (Birkeland et al., 2022). This indicates an enhanced need for post-trauma monitoring and clinical interventions in SA. Peritraumatic reactions could be a valuable indicator of which individuals are at particularly high risk of severe health consequences. However, research in this area has been limited to very few selected reaction patterns, such as tonic immobility and dissociation (e.g. deMello et al., 2023; Möller et al., 2017).

Lastly, comprehensive studies on peritraumatic reactions specifically in SA are needed because their evaluation plays a central role in public and legal perceptions of SA. Unlike many other forms of traumatic experiences, SA remains highly stigmatized and is commonly associated with self-blame, shame, and, sometimes, negative social reactions (Dworkin et al., 2017). Stigmatization is fuelled by societal ‘rape myths’ and victim stereotypes, which hold that a ‘real rape’ involves physical force and harm, an unknown perpetrator, and a physically and verbally resisting victim (Maiorano et al., 2023). As such, behavioural peritraumatic reactions are a core element of rape myths, with non-active responses such as tonic immobility, by definition, not fulfilling the requirement for victim resistance (Rubin & Bell, 2023). Hence, a better understanding and awareness of the diverse peritraumatic reactions that can occur during SA are crucial steps towards normalization and combating stigmatization. Recognition of the different peritraumatic reactions during SA also has important legal implications. Despite reforms in various states of the US, countries in Europe, and Australia, where references to resistance in legal definitions of rape were removed or requirements of affirmative consent were added (Burgin, 2019; Decker & Baroni, 2011; Uhnoo et al., 2024), rape myths continue to shape conceptions of rape and SA by the public and legal professionals. Consequently, scenarios involving alternative reactions, such as tonic immobility, are less likely to be considered a crime (Augustyn et al., 2025), and notions of force and resistance still affect criminal justice processes and actors in the courtroom (Burgin, 2019; Gröndal et al., 2025).

In light of the significant research gaps and the unique clinical, social and legal challenges associated with SA, a more comprehensive investigation into the peritraumatic experience of SA is essential. Systematically mapping the diverse emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses exhibited during SA and uncovering their associations with post-trauma mental health is a critical step towards both normalization and improving clinical risk detection. Against this backdrop, the aim of this study was twofold: (a) to expand our understanding of peritraumatic reactions during SA by mapping a wide range of reactions in the emotional, cognitive and behavioural domains; (b) to elucidate how these reactions relate to assault characteristics, SA history, and post-assault mental health.

Methods

The current study has been preregistered (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/U5XT9) and utilizes data from a larger longitudinal observational study that investigates mental health in the aftermath of SA (TRUST, see osf.io/b4wmr). TRUST encompasses five waves of data collection, some of which are ongoing. The current study only utilizes data from the completed first wave (study baseline; N = 669 participants).

Participants

Inclusion criteria to the TRUST study were mastery of the Norwegian or English language, ≥16 years of age, and experience of SA. In the current study, only participants who had experienced SA within the past year prior to study baseline were included (N = 453). This criterion was applied to ensure sufficient temporal proximity to the assault, thereby reducing recall bias and enhancing the accuracy of self-reported peritraumatic reactions. Of these, n = 25 participants were excluded from analyses because they reported that they did not remember anything about the SA, and n = 3 because they did not respond to any item of the peritraumatic reactions questionnaire (PRQ). The final sample size was N = 425.

Participants were recruited through Norwegian Sexual Assault Centers (SACs) and social media, in order to include both those who sought help and those who did not. Depending on the participants’ preference, data were collected using online surveys or telephone interviews conducted by members of the TRUST study research team (health personnel, M.Sc. psychologist). The encrypted data were directly transferred to a server for sensitive data storage (TSD, University of Oslo). The study followed strict confidentiality protocols and was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics, South-East Norway (REK 398925/2022). All participants gave written informed consent in accordance with the Norwegian Health Research Act, which stipulates that individuals from age 16 are considered consent competent. For more details on sample and procedures see Thoresen et al. (2025).

Measures

Peritraumatic reactions

To capture a wide range of peritraumatic reactions in the emotional, cognitive and behavioural domains, including reactions reflective of tonic immobility and dissociation, a comprehensive item pool was developed. Item development was informed by existing scales (Dyb et al., 2008; Fusé et al., 2007; Holowka et al., 2003; Lawyer et al., 2006) and consensus meetings with employees at the Norwegian Centre for Violence and Traumatic Stress Studies (NKVTS). To validate and refine the items, a preliminary set was presented to three individuals with lived experience of SA, recruited through a support centre for SA victims in Oslo, Norway (DIXI). Their feedback was gathered through cognitive interviews using the ‘think-aloud’ method, which resulted in a final 23-item pool for the PRQ (see Figure 1). In the current study, participants were asked to rate the extent to which they exhibited each of the reactions specified in the PRQ during the assault, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘not at all’ (1) to ‘to a very large extent’ (5).

Assault characteristics

Relation to the perpetrator was recorded as a categorical variable, with levels ‘close’ ([ex-]partner or family), ‘known’ (friend/acquaintance/neighbor/colleague/fellow student or authority figure [e.g. teacher, coach, boss, religious leader, healthcare professional, etc.]), and ‘unknown’ (unknown, known for <24 h or other) to the victim. All other assault characteristics were measured as binary variables (yes vs. no): Threat of harm and physical violence were assessed by asking whether participants were ‘subjected to physical force or threats to harm [them] or someone close to [them]’ and whether they were ‘subjected to violence (e.g. use of weapons, hitting, kicking)’, respectively. Severe chokehold was defined as chokehold (pressure with hand[s], arm[s] or objects applied against or around the throat) that resulted in a difficulty of breathing or loss of consciousness. Perceived intoxication was assessed by asking whether participants perceived themselves as ‘clearly intoxicated/drunk or high’.

History of sexual assault (SA)

History of SA was recorded as a binary variable and coded as ‘yes’ if participants reported SA experiences in either childhood or adulthood (else ‘no’). Childhood experience of SA was measured with an adapted version of item 8 from the KATES, the Norwegian version of the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen for 7–17 Years (CATS; Sachser et al., 2017; Norwegian version by NKVTS, 2019). The item was modified from the original child-friendly, descriptive language to a retrospective format more suitable for adult respondents (e.g. shifting from behavioural descriptions of anatomy to direct inquiries regarding sexual acts). SA in adulthood was measured by asking: ‘As an adult: Have you previously experienced one or more instances of sexual assault?’.

Mental health

To measure post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS), the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018; Norwegian version by Sele et al., 2020) was employed, corresponding to the ICD-11 diagnosis of PTSD. Six items reflecting symptoms of re-experiencing, avoidance, and hypervigilance since the assault were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (‘not at all’ [0] to ‘extremely’ [4]). The 10-item version of the Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-10; Schmalbach et al., 2021; Norwegian version by Strand et al., 2003) was used to measure the extent to which participants were affected by symptoms of depression/anxiety within the past week, rated on a 4-point Likert scale (‘not bothered’ [1] to ‘bothered a great deal’ [4]).

For both scales, average scores were calculated using the half-rule, with scores treated as missing if >50% of items on the respective scale were unanswered.

Involvement of people with lived experience

People with lived experience of SA were involved in the generation of the interview guide of the original TRUST study and during generation and refinement of the PRQ item pool. Factor analysis results were discussed with five lived experience experts who are members of the current user panel of the TRUST study. They assisted in interpreting the factor structure and identifying suitable factor labels that adequately capture the underlying construct while being easily understandable and avoiding stigmatizing language.

Statistical analyses

Factorability of the PRQ was assessed using the Bartlett’s test and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (MSA). Items with MSA <0.6, reflecting less than mediocre sampling adequacy, thus indicating low shared variance with other items, were removed prior to factor analysis. To determine the most appropriate number of factors to extract, parallel analysis was employed and fit indices of the most fitting factor solutions inspected. A combination of Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.06, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08, and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) as well as Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) close to ≥ 0.95 was interpreted as indicating good model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Factor analysis was conducted with an oblique factor rotation method (Geomin) to allow for factor correlations, using the Weighted Least Squares with Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimator to account for the categorical (ordinal) nature of the PRQ items. By default, missing data are handled via pairwise deletion when using the WLSMV. To ensure robustness of the results across different handling approaches for missing data, factor analysis was repeated using Maximum Likelihood with Robust standard errors (MLR), treating PRQ items as continuous variables and using full information maximum likelihood estimation to deal with missing data, assuming missingness at random. To refine the factor structure, ill-fitting items were removed in a stepwise procedure, with parallel analysis and factor analysis repeated after each removal. Exclusion criteria were as follows, based on absolute factor loadings and applied in the indicated order: (1) Items that constitute a single factor (without contribution of other items), (2) Items without substantial factor loading (≥0.4) on any factor, (3) Items with considerable cross-loading (a secondary substantial loading at ≥75% of the main loading; e.g. 0.4 on factor 1 and 0.50 on factor 2). Based on the final factor solution, factor scores were calculated by averaging the ratings of all items with considerable loadings on the respective factor (Fayers & Machin, 2007), using reverse coded ratings for items with negative loadings. For participants who responded to less than 50% of the factor’s items, the respective factor score was treated as missing (half-rule).

Associations between factor scores and assault characteristics, as well as SA history, were investigated using independent t-tests with Welch correction (binary variables) and a one-way ANOVA (categorical variable: relation to the perpetrator), followed by post-hoc tests (Tukey's Honest Significant Difference [HSD] test) if significant. Parametric as opposed to nonparametric group comparison methods were deemed eligible, given their robustness to normality violations in large samples and the risk of Type 1 errors associated with nonparametric alternatives (e.g. Fagerland, 2012). Associations between factor scores and mental health measures (average PTSS and depression/anxiety scores) were assessed with Pearson correlations in combination with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.

All testing was conducted two-sided, at a significance level of α = 0.05. Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this study, multiple testing corrections were not applied, except within ANOVA analyses. Data processing and analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3; R Core Team, 2023; see Supplementary Information for R packages) with the exception of parallel and factor analysis, conducted in Mplus (version 8.11; Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017).

Results

Sample characteristics

Sample and assault characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Most participants were young adults (M = 26.20 years old; n = 358 [84%] < 35), despite a large age range (16-72 years). The majority were women (96%), and most participants had experienced SA previously in their lives (82%). The majority of assaults had occurred rather recently (≤ 6 months prior to the baseline interview: 57%), involved completed penetration (82%), and were perpetrated by someone close or known to the victim (64%). The use of threats was more common (43%) than the use of violence or severe chokehold (26% and 25%, respectively). While missing data counts were generally low (Table 1), non-response rates were higher for severe chokehold (22%) and SA history (11%), resulting either from non-disclosure or respondent uncertainty. We treat these instances as missing at random, positing that non-response is associated with general recall difficulty and interpretative variance rather than the magnitude of the variables themselves.

Peritraumatic reactions

All items of the PRQ are presented in Figure 1, ranked according to the mean value calculated based on all participants’ ratings. Emotional reactions, such as feeling helpless, humiliated, or disgusted, were the most endorsed responses, followed by slowed time perception, freeze reactions and surrender (‘giving up’). Least pronounced were reactions describing behavioural or verbal defense reactions (‘pleading’, ‘screaming’, ‘physically resisting’), feelings of anger, fear of being killed, and accelerated time perception. A similar pattern was observed when items were ranked by the percentage of ‘5’ ratings (the highest possible score) instead of the mean value (Supplementary Figure S1). Missing data counts per item were low (Supplementary Figure S2), with an average 1% per item, and the highest proportion observed for ‘I did exactly what I was told’ (4%).

A 6-factor model fit the data best, after stepwise removal of three items due to low MSA (Q23) or non-substantial factor loading (Q9, Q18; Table 2; see Supplementary Table S1 for detailed information about fit indices of different factor models).

Factor loadings are presented in Table 2. The first factor (F1) captured reactions reflective of detachment (dissociation) and freezing (immobility) responses and was labelled Dissociation & freeze. The second factor (F2) encompassed strategic and automatic thought processes, labelled Cognitive strategies & thought processes. The third factor (F3) included different behavioural and verbal defense strategies, such as trying to get away or to physically resist, screaming or pleading, and was labelled Active defense behaviour. The fourth factor (F4) consisted of various emotional reactions of negative valence, including feelings of disgust or humiliation, and was labelled Negative emotions. The fifth factor (F5) comprised two items describing fear of and thoughts about severe outcomes, labelled Perceived life threat. The sixth factor (F6) contained descriptions of giving in and mental distancing from the situation, labelled Reactive surrender. Replication of the 6-factor analysis using the MLR estimator produced equivalent loading patterns (see Supplementary Table S2).

Internal consistency was good for Active defense behaviour (α = 0.80), acceptable for Perceived life threat (α = 0.73), borderline-questionable for Dissociation & freeze (α = 0.67), Cognitive strategies & thought processes (α = 0.66), and Reactive surrender (α = 0.64), and poor for Negative emotions (α = 0.52).

The three items that were excluded during iterative factor analysis pertained to the altered perception of time (slower and faster) and a feeling of helplessness (Table 2). The low MSA and the absence of absolute factor loadings ≥0.4 of these items in the initial steps of factor analysis indicated no substantial contribution to any specific latent factor. Notably, the item ‘It was like time passed faster than usual’ was the least endorsed by participants, while ‘I felt helpless’ and ‘It was like time passed more slowly than usual’ were among the highest rated responses (Figure 1), with little variation in individual ratings (see Supplementary Figure S2). Exploratory associations with factor scores and post-trauma mental health are reported in the Supplementary Information (Table S3) but should be interpreted with caution.

Associations with assault characteristics and sexual assault history

The effects of assault characteristics and SA history on peritraumatic reactions are illustrated in Figure 2 and summarized in Table 3. Dissociation & freeze responses were significantly more strongly endorsed by study participants who perceived themselves as ‘clearly intoxicated/drunk or high’ during the assault but were not significantly related to any other assault characteristics. Cognitive strategies & thought processes were less evident among intoxicated participants but more pronounced during assaults characterized by threat of harm or physical violence. Threat of harm, physical violence, and severe chokehold were significantly associated with more Active defense behaviour. It should be noted, however, that this behaviour was generally less prominent than any of the other peritraumatic reactions (Figure 2). Affective responses captured by the Negative emotions factor were higher when threat of harm was present but not significantly related to any other assault characteristics. Perceived life threat was the only peritraumatic reaction significantly associated with relation to the perpetrator, with post-hoc tests revealing significantly lower scores when the perpetrator was known (p = .014, d = −0.33) or someone close to the victim (p < .001, d = −0.47) as opposed to unknown. Perceived life threat was significantly higher for assaults involving threat of harm, physical violence or severe chokehold. Threat of harm and physical violence also predicted greater levels of Reactive surrender, as did a pre-existing SA history.

Discussion

The present study aimed to expand the current understanding of peritraumatic reactions during sexual assault (SA) by assessing a wide range of emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses and investigating their associations with assault characteristics, SA history, and symptoms of post-traumatic stress (PTSS) and depression/anxiety.

Six correlated factors of peritraumatic reactions were identified: Dissociation & freeze, Cognitive strategies & thought processes, Active defense behaviour, Negative emotions, Perceived life threat, and Reactive surrender. Although sometimes treated as distinct constructs (deMello et al., 2023; Kalaf et al., 2017), dissociative and freezing responses emerged as aspects of the same underlying factor in the current study. This finding aligns with alternative operationalizations of tonic immobility, where symptoms of dissociation are treated as inherent parts of its facets (Fusé et al., 2007; Massazza et al., 2021; Möller et al., 2017). Indeed, dissociation and tonic immobility are highly interconnected and may represent expressions of the same defense mechanism (Abrams et al., 2009; Covers et al., 2022; Fusé et al., 2007; Katz et al., 2021). Of note, another common facet of tonic immobility assessments is intense fear/perceived life threat (Fusé et al., 2007; Massazza et al., 2021; Möller et al., 2017). In the current study, however, perceived life threat emerged as an independent factor. This factor bears conceptual resemblance to the peritraumatic ‘distress’ factor identified by Massazza et al. (2021) among natural disaster survivors. However, their construct also encompassed other negative emotions, such as sadness and anger. In contrast, these emerged as a separate Negative emotions factor in the current study. The Active defense behaviour and Reactive surrender factors captured elements previously classified as ‘active’ or ‘passive’ coping in mixed-assault samples (Kecala et al., 2023). While some items within Reactive surrender resemble the ‘mental defeat’ construct extracted by Massazza et al. (2021), it contains more direct references to action and behaviour (e.g. doing as told) and includes simultaneous cognitive coping strategies (i.e. distracting oneself).

In the present sample, reactions reflective of Active defense behaviour were least prominent, while various Negative emotions, and reactions reflective of Dissociation & freeze and Reactive surrender were most pronounced. These findings challenge prevailing stereotypes of the actively resisting SA victim and suggest that non-active (passive) behavioural responses may be much more prominent. Notably, these non-active behavioural responses seem to include not only tonic immobility, which is considered a common albeit involuntary response to SA (Möller et al., 2017), but also reactive surrender, which may be less automatic and more strategic.

The associations between peritraumatic reactions and assault characteristics indicate a certain degree of context-dependence. Threat and violence were linked to increased intensity of almost all types of peritraumatic reactions, with the strongest associations observed for perceived life threat. The relations between threat, violence and reactive surrender support the notion that non-active behavioural responses, such as freezing, surrender, or compliance, serve as self-protective strategies against harm (de Heer & Jones, 2024; Wong & Balemba, 2016).

Reactive surrender scores were also higher among those with a prior history of SA. In a similar vein, previous studies have observed that a history of SA is associated with diminished active resistance and a higher degree of peritraumatic freezing (Gidycz et al., 2008; Möller et al., 2017). Among victims with prior SA exposure, a higher prevalence of nonactive responses may reflect a learned self-protective strategy or an internalized sense of powerlessness rooted in previous experiences (Gidycz et al., 2008; Möller et al., 2017). As reactive surrender represents a related form of nonactive behavioural responses, such explanations likely extend to the findings of the current study. Notably, just like reactive surrender, active defense behaviour was more pronounced in the presence of violence and threat, including severe chokehold. While this may reflect an amplified urgency to escape prompted by acute perceived danger, it may also represent an escalation of force by the perpetrator in response to victim resistance (Balemba et al., 2012).

The only peritraumatic reaction factor not significantly associated with threat or violence was Dissociation and freeze. This finding was unexpected, as these reactions are traditionally conceptualized and empirically observed as direct responses to threat and violence (Fusé et al., 2007; Kalaf et al., 2017; Kaysen et al., 2005; Möller et al., 2017). However, in the current study, ‘threat’ was operationalized specifically as explicit physical force or overt threats of harm by the perpetrator. It is plausible that the assault context itself constitutes a sufficient threat to induce dissociation and freezing, even when the perpetrator does not turn to explicit force or threats. Effects of perpetrator violence may, on the other hand, be severity dependent (Möller et al., 2017), a nuance that the binary variable used in this study may have failed to capture. Inconsistencies with previous research may further arise from differences in operationalization of dissociation and freezing responses as separate or overlapping constructs (see above).

Relationship to the perpetrator was significantly associated only with perceived life threat, with closer relations linked to lower perceived threat, possibly reflecting lower levels of violence in assaults by known perpetrators (Ullman et al., 2006). In contrast, other emotional, cognitive and behavioural responses appeared to be independent of the victim-perpetrator relationship, consistent with previous null findings for tonic immobility (Möller et al., 2017).

Peritraumatic reactions were broadly associated with PTSS and depression/anxiety symptoms, with the exception of Cognitive strategies & thought processes and Active defense behaviour. The association observed between active defense behaviour and PTSS seemed to be at least partly driven by assault characteristics such as violence and threat, with attenuated effects after controlling for these. In contrast, all other associations remained robust when accounting for the effects of assault characteristics and SA history. These results highlight peritraumatic reactions as critical indicators for victims at risk of developing more severe post-traumatic psychopathology. Beyond the established roles of dissociation and tonic immobility, our findings suggest that a broader spectrum of emotional and behavioural responses may serve as valuable predictors of post-trauma mental health.

Limitations

The current study was cross-sectional, and causal inferences cannot be drawn. While peritraumatic reactions by default refer to responses preceding mental health as measured at study baseline, it cannot be ruled out that symptoms at the time of assessment tainted the retrospective evaluation of these reactions. Furthermore, mental health symptoms were only measured with self-reports and clinician administered interviews to assess symptoms and potential diagnoses might have strengthened the study. Missing data counts were generally low, and factor analysis results remained robust across different data handling methods (pairwise deletion and full information maximum likelihood estimation). Nevertheless, missingness was comparatively high for severe chokehold (22%) and SA history (11%). Hence, analyses involving these variables may suffer from attenuated power, and the potential for systematic bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Other limitations pertain to the comparatively low internal consistency observed for some of the peritraumatic reactions subscales. The Negative emotions subscale particularly stands out, capturing a wide range of different emotions of negative valence that may be better assessed as separate constructs. The internal consistency for Cognitive strategies & thought processes, Dissociation and freeze, and Reactive surrender fell slightly below the conventional 0.7 threshold. This is likely attributable to the brevity of these subscales (3 items each) and the heterogeneous nature of the reactions they capture. While lower internal consistency can attenuate observed effect sizes, the presence of significant associations with other study variables supports the scales’ utility. Notably, it has been acknowledged that α values below 0.7 can be considered acceptable in early stages of research and that they alone should not determine a scale's usefulness (Schmitt, 1996; Streiner, 2003). Nevertheless, future studies may want to consider expanding the subscales by inclusion of complementary items in order to strengthen the subscales’ internal reliability. Lastly, the sample consisted almost exclusively of female participants, limiting the generalizability of the findings to male victims of SA.

Implications and future directions

The present findings challenge rape stereotypes of the ‘actively resisting’ victim and highlight that non-active responses, such as reactive surrender, are common survival strategies. These insights hold important implications for normalizing the diverse range of peritraumatic reactions during SA and countering stigmatization of non-stereotypical responses. Ultimately, this may also be relevant for legal contexts in which rape myths and victim stereotypes are still prevailing. Despite recent efforts to integrate tonic immobility as a common reaction during SA into existing law and legal practices (de Heer & Jones, 2024), other nonactive reactions have received little attention. The robust associations between the majority of peritraumatic reactions and subsequent mental health may inspire the development of screening tools to be used in clinical contexts. Such tools could improve post-trauma risk assessments and facilitate individualized follow-up and treatment in the aftermath of SA.

Longitudinal studies should investigate the long-term predictive value of peritraumatic reactions for post-trauma mental health. Furthermore, the mechanistic pathways underlying the observed effects of peritraumatic reactions on subsequent mental health remain to be explored. Candidate mediators include memory processes (Halligan et al., 2003; Rubin & Bell, 2023), post-traumatic cognitions, self-blame, shame and related negative beliefs about the self (Salcioglu et al., 2024; Van Buren & Weierich, 2015).

Conclusion

Taken together, this study demonstrates the multifaceted nature of peritraumatic reactions during SA and points towards a role of these reactions for post-trauma recovery. Recognizing the diversity of these reactions is essential for improving the accuracy of post-trauma risk assessments and for fostering a societal environment that acknowledges the diverse realities of those afflicted by SA.

Note

Early definitions of the tonic immobility and the freeze reaction were informed by animal research. Here, a clear distinction was made between freeze reactions that occurred upon detection of a potential threat, as a purposeful act to avoid the threat encounter, and tonic immobility which occurred after an attack, as an involuntary and passive response (Kalaf et al., 2017; Van Buren & Weierich, 2015). Within SA research, however, the term ‘freezing’ is often used to label a facet of tonic immobility (Fusé et al., 2007), referring to an involuntary response to an attack that is already underway, and not a strategy to avoid threat encounter. The term ‘freeze’ is also more accessible to victims of SA and aligns more closely with the language they choose to describe their own experiences. With this in mind, and because both terms are used to describe the same phenomenon in existing SA literature, we use these terms interchangeably.
