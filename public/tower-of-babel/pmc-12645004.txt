RISM clustering algorithm based on relative density and inter-cluster connectivity degree
Authors: Ming Gong, Yuqing Zhou, Yan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClustering complex data structures remains a pivotal challenge in unsupervised learning, particularly when determining the optimal number of clusters in highly non-linear datasets. In this paper, we introduce RISM (Relative Density and Inter-Cluster Connectivity Degree-based Split-and-Merge), a novel clustering algorithm that integrates both density-based and connectivity-driven principles to automatically infer the optimal cluster configuration. RISM operates in two distinct phases: splitting and merging. During the splitting phase, we define a new relative density metric, which evaluates the local density of each data point in relation to its k-nearest neighbors, allowing for the identification of potential cluster centers. Subclusters are then formed based on both relative density and relative distance, effectively capturing dense regions in the data. In the merging phase, we propose a connectivity-aware inter-cluster distance measure that incorporates both inter-cluster distance factors and inter-cluster connectivity degrees, enabling a principled and iterative merging of clusters based on their proximity and structural relationships. The final number of clusters is determined by maximizing the difference in inter-cluster distances observed throughout the merging process. Extensive empirical evaluations on both synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate the superiority of RISM over nine state-of-the-art clustering algorithms, particularly in terms of clustering accuracy, robustness to noise, and scalability.Keywords: K-nearest neighbors, Clustering algorithm, Split-and-merge, Inter-cluster distance, Relative densitySubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Cluster analysis is a crucial tool in the field of data mining and machine learning. It is an unsupervised learning method that partitions data into clusters of high internal similarity and low external similarity. It has found applications in areas such as image segmentation, customer segmentation, and social network analysis, among others1,2. Many datasets exhibit complex cluster structures, including varying densities of distribution, diverse shapes, overlapping clusters, and noise within clusters, which may hinder the performance of clustering algorithms. Traditional clustering algorithms such as k-means3, hierarchical clustering4, DBSCAN5, Gaussian mixture models6, and fuzzy clustering7 are suitable for specific cluster structures but may perform poorly on datasets with varying densities or irregularly shaped clusters.

To better handle complex data structures, split-and-merge clustering algorithms have been proposed. Rather than directly dividing the dataset into K clusters, these methods first generate multiple small clusters to capture intrinsic structure and mitigate the influence of outliers. The small clusters are then merged based on their proximity to form the final K clusters. Algorithms such as CSM8, SMK-means9, and PSM10 employ k-means or 2-means for the splitting process. Some algorithms, such as SAM11 and CciMST12, leverage the topological structure preserved by the minimum spanning tree (MST) to identify initial cluster centers, and then apply k-means or DPC to generate small clusters. In recent years, local neighborhood structures—such as the k-nearest neighbor (kNN) graph or the 1-nearest neighbor (1-NN) graph—have been increasingly exploited to construct small clusters by capturing stable data relationships13–16. This is typically achieved by removing structurally important points16 or partitioning data based on stable neighborhood structures14, resulting in subclusters that better reflect the intrinsic data organization.

After obtaining the small clusters, the next step is to merge them into K final clusters, which relies on accurate inter-cluster distance measurement. Directly using all data points within each cluster may lead to biased estimates, especially for clusters with irregular shapes. To mitigate this, algorithms such as CciMST12 and ANCV17 calculate inter-cluster distances based on representative point pairs between clusters. However, their strong reliance on the minimum spanning tree (MST) can be problematic—if no edge exists between two clusters, their separability may be underestimated. The CSM algorithm8 further considers the spatial influence of data points by introducing a probability-based model to define cluster joinability and cohesion.

More recent methods, including ANCV17, NTHC14, and KD-DBSCAN5, exploit neighborhood-based relationships—such as non-shared neighbors, mutual neighbors, and reverse nearest neighbors—to measure inter-cluster similarity. While these approaches enhance flexibility, they are still sensitive to noise and data density. For example, distant clusters may appear close due to an anomalous connecting point, or sparse internal distributions may inflate the estimated distance despite strong mutual connections. In summary, existing distance metrics remain affected by factors such as cluster shape, density variations, and outliers.

Another fundamental challenge in clustering is determining the optimal number of clusters, especially in unsupervised settings where data distributions are unknown or highly non-uniform. Existing approaches for estimating the cluster number can be broadly categorized into two types: index-based methods and adaptive methods. Index-based methods, such as the Silhouette coefficient, Davies-Bouldin index, Dunn index, and Xie–Beni index18, evaluate clustering performance under different cluster numbers and select the one achieving the best index value. However, these methods often require multiple runs of the clustering algorithm and are computationally expensive. Moreover, different indices may yield inconsistent results, leading to ambiguity in determining the optimal cluster number17.

As an alternative, adaptive methods attempt to eliminate the need for explicit cluster number specification by dynamically adjusting the number of clusters during the clustering process. Notable examples include algorithms like DPC19, HDBSCAN20 and SR-PCM-HDP21, which integrate density information or membership constraints to iteratively remove redundant clusters. Although these approaches improve flexibility and efficiency, they may still suffer from sensitivity to initial parameters or fail to generalize well across different data distributions. These limitations highlight the need for robust clustering algorithms that can automatically determine the optimal number of clusters while maintaining high clustering accuracy and stability.

To address the aforementioned issues, this paper proposes the RISM (relative density and inter-cluster connectivity degree-based split-and-merge) algorithm, which can automatically identify the optimal number of clusters. In the splitting phase, we introduce the concept of relative density for data points, which is determined by the ratio of the absolute density between a data point and its k-nearest neighbors (kNNs). Subsequently, we obtain initial cluster centers based on the relative density and relative distance of data points. The remaining data points are then assigned to the clusters of the nearest data point with higher density, resulting in clusters. In the clustering merging phase, this paper proposes a novel method for measuring inter-cluster distance by utilizing the inter-cluster distance factor and inter-cluster connectivity degree. Specifically, the inter-cluster distance factor takes into account the distance between data point pairs that are mutually neighboring between two clusters, while the inter-cluster connectivity degree is derived from the internal and external connectivity degree of data points. During the merging phase, clusters with the smallest inter-cluster distance are continuously merged until a single cluster is obtained. Finally, the optimal number of clusters is determined by selecting the cluster number that corresponds to the largest difference in inter-cluster distance between consecutive merging steps.

Related work

The split-and-merge clustering algorithms commonly utilize cluster centers to identify small clusters and facilitate data splitting. Generally, data points with higher densities have a higher probability of being selected as cluster centers. Therefore, the density of data points is an important factor in determining cluster centers. The methods for defining data point density can be divided into two categories: global density and local density. The calculation of global density involves employing a kernel function to assess the similarity between each data point and other data points, and then aggregating these similarities to determine the density of each data point19,22,23. Global density is susceptible to the shape of data distribution and parameters in the kernel function. Many algorithms address this issue by introducing geodesic distance and adopting measures such as adaptively adjusting the bandwidth parameter of the kernel function12,24.

In split-and-merge clustering algorithms, the cluster number obtained through splitting is typically greater than the actual cluster number K. As local density represents the density of data points within a specific range, most split-and-merge clustering algorithms often rely on the local density of data points. Typically, the kNNs of data points are employed to define their local density, as this approach effectively captures local characteristics of the data14,16. For instance, algorithms such as STClu25 and ICKDC13 multiply the reciprocal of the average distance between a data point and its kNNs by the value of k. Consequently, we can conclude that denser data point distributions correspond to higher local densities, and vice versa. This density definition, which takes into account the local range of the k-neighborhood, meets the criteria for selecting cluster centers. In other words, the closer a data point is to the cluster center, the closer its kNNs are to that data point. As a result, these data points possess higher density.

However, when considering the dataset as a whole, the density definition mentioned above may cause clustering centers to concentrate in densely distributed regions, leaving sparsely distributed regions without clustering centers, thus potentially leading to splitting errors. To mitigate the influence of sparse data point distributions on clustering centers, the AKDPC algorithm26 considers the number of mutual kNNs as the density of data points. According to this definition, the density of data points can only take k + 1 possible values ranging from 0 to k, which limits the discriminability of data point densities. To address this limitation, the ERDPC algorithm1 proposes a better approach for fine-grained differentiation of data point densities. It defines the relative local density of a data point as the number of data points with higher tightness than its k-nearest neighbors, and adds this tightness to the local density to obtain the final data point density. To mitigate noise, the DDC algorithm27 and LGD algorithm28 construct kNNG based on mutual k-nearest neighbors relationships. They define density by calculating the average length of neighboring edges for each data point. This definition enhances the discriminability of data point densities. However, due to the concentration of high-density points in densely populated regions, clustering centers may not be evenly distributed throughout the entire dataset.

The utilization of k-nearest neighbors relationships extends beyond determining data point density and encompasses the evaluation of inter-cluster distance as well. Recently proposed clustering algorithms, including NTHC14, KR-DBSCAN5, ANCV17, and SMKNN16, leverage inter-cluster relationships such as mutual k-nearest neighbors, reverse k-nearest neighbors, and shared neighbors to quantify inter-cluster distances. However, the intricate nature of inter-cluster relationships poses challenges, and the reliance on neighbor relationships may not always provide accurate measurements of inter-cluster distance.

In Fig. 1a, the blue and yellow clusters share only one closely located pair of mutual nearest neighbor points (a, b), leading to a greater distance between the blue and red clusters compared to the distance between the blue and yellow clusters. However, in reality, the blue cluster should be closer to the red cluster. In Fig. 1b, the red cluster is closer to the blue cluster than the yellow cluster. However, the neighborhood distance between the red and blue clusters is unexpectedly greater than the distance between the yellow and blue clusters. This is primarily due to the smaller size of the red cluster, which causes the majority of the mutual nearest neighbor point pairs between the red and blue clusters to be concentrated within the blue cluster. However, the denser data distribution within the red and yellow clusters, in contrast to the sparser data distribution within the blue and green clusters, leads to an unexpected result that the neighborhood distance between the blue and green clusters is greater than the neighborhood distance between the red and yellow clusters.

Methodology

Figure 2 presents an overview of the algorithm. The proposed algorithm consists of two main phases: a splitting phase and a merging phase. In the splitting phase, cluster centers are selected based on the relative density and relative distance of data points. Figure 2 presents a dataset with 64 data points. The 8 () data points with the highest product of relative density and relative distance are selected as cluster centers (represented by red pentagrams). Subsequently, the remaining data points are assigned to their respective clusters, resulting in the formation of 8 smaller clusters.

The overview of the algorithm.

During the merge phase, cluster pairs (represented by blue dashed lines) with the smallest inter-cluster distance are identified by considering both the inter-cluster distance factor and inter-cluster connectivity degree, and these pairs are merged accordingly. This process of merging cluster pairs is repeated iteratively until only 2 clusters remain. Finally, the optimal number of clusters Kopt is determined by assessing the largest difference in inter-cluster distance observed throughout the merging process.

The proposed algorithm depends on one input parameter—the number of nearest neighbors k. This parameter is used throughout the algorithm to compute local density, relative distance, and inter-cluster connectivity. As shown in "Parameter sensitivity" section, the algorithm performs robustly when k is set to 10 across a wide range of datasets.

The proposed algorithm measures inter-cluster distance based on three factors: (1) the average distance between pairs of neighboring points from different clusters, known as the inter-cluster distance factor ; (2) the number of connected points between clusters; and (3) the number of kNNs of connected points distributed between the two clusters. When two clusters simultaneously meet the following conditions: a small inter-cluster distance factor, a large number of connected points between clusters, and a relatively balanced distribution of kNNs of connected points between the two clusters, it is considered that the inter-cluster distance is small. The numerator of the inter-cluster connectivity degree is obtained by summing up the unified connectivity degree of all external connected points. As the number of connected points increases, the numerator becomes larger, indicating a higher inter-cluster connectivity degree. Therefore, this term reflects the number of inter-cluster connected points. is directly proportional to the product of the internal connectivity degree and the external connectivity degree of the data point x. When and are close in value, it indicates that the kNNs of x are uniformly distributed between the two clusters, thereby causing an increase in the value of .

This algorithm effectively mitigates the influence of inter-cluster noise points on inter-cluster distances by considering the number of inter-cluster connected points. As shown in Fig. 1a, the yellow cluster has only one external connected point (Point b) relative to the blue cluster, resulting in a smaller numerator, , of the inter-cluster connectivity degree. Consequently, this leads to an increased inter-cluster distance. In addition, the inter-cluster connectivity degree also depends on the number of inter-cluster connected points and the distribution of k-nearest neighbors among these connected points. Therefore, the inter-cluster distance defined in this study is less susceptible to the sparsity or density of the data point distribution.

Experiments and results

To evaluate the performance of our algorithm under known cluster numbers, we compared it with 9 benchmark algorithms on 15 synthetic datasets and 10 real datasets. Since our algorithm can automatically determine the number of clusters, we also conducted comparisons with three other algorithms that possess the same capability. Moreover, we carried out ablation experiments and parameter sensitivity analysis. All experiments were conducted on a system running Windows 11 with an i7-13700H processor and 32GB of memory. All algorithms were implemented in MATLAB. The source code of our algorithm has been uploaded to GitHub.

Parameter sensitivity

In this study, our algorithm solely relies on a single parameter, referred to as the nearest neighbor value k. This subsection will analyze the impact of k on the performance of RISM. As k varies from 3 to 50, Figs. 9 and 10 show the F1 scores of the RISM algorithm on 15 synthetic datasets and 10 real datasets, respectively.

k vs F1 on synthetic datasets.

k vs F1 on real datasets.

There is significant variability in the F1 scores for different synthetic datasets when k is less than 9. The F1 scores remain consistently high for all datasets when k is between 9 and 12. However, when k exceeds 12, the F1 scores of DS2 and DS3 show a noticeable decline. As k increases further, the F1 scores of most datasets exhibit a stepwise decrease.

When k is set to 9 or 10, the highest F1 scores are maintained for all real datasets, except for the Dnatest and Vowel datasets. When k surpasses 9, a stepwise decrease in F1 scores can be observed for the Leuk72_3k and Dermatology datasets. Additionally, when k is set to 10, the Dermatology and Libras datasets yield relatively higher F1 scores. Considering the results from both synthetic and real datasets, we choose k = 10 as the optimal nearest neighbor value.

The sensitivity of the RISM algorithm to the parameter k can be attributed to its influence on both the local density estimation and the inter-cluster distance computation. Specifically, k affects the internal and external connectivity degree of data points, which are critical components in calculating the inter-cluster connectivity degree (Definition 10) and the overall inter-cluster distance (Definition 11). When k is too small, the connectivity information becomes sparse, potentially underestimating the similarity between clusters and leading to insufficient merging. Conversely, when k is too large, the neighborhood structure may cross cluster boundaries, resulting in overestimated connectivity and possibly erroneous merging. These changes directly affect the sequence of cluster merging and the computation of (Definition 12), thereby impacting the determination of the optimal number of clusters. This explains the observed sensitivity in F1 scores across different values of k in Fig. 9.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a split-and-merge clustering algorithm based on relative density and inter-cluster connectivity degree. In the splitting phase, the concept of relative density is introduced, which considers the ratio of absolute density between a data point and its kNNs. The proposed relative density definition mitigates the impact of sparse or dense data point distributions on density estimation. Cluster centers are then determined considering the relative density and relative distance of data points, leading to a more uniform distribution of these centers on the dataset and reducing the impact of cluster shape on the resulting smaller clusters after splitting. In the merging phase, the algorithm iteratively selects the two clusters with the minimum inter-cluster distance for merging and updates the inter-cluster distances. Additionally, the inter-cluster distance considers both inter-cluster distance factor and inter-cluster connectivity degree, mitigating the impact of noise points on the merging results. This process continues until only two clusters are left. And the optimal number of clusters can be determined based on the minimum inter-cluster distance at each merge step. The proposed algorithm is compared with 9 other algorithms on 15 synthetic datasets and 10 real datasets for clustering and identifying the optimal number of clusters. Furthermore, the ablation and parameter sensitivity experiments are conducted. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm outperforms the comparative algorithms.

In addition to the empirical comparisons with state-of-the-art clustering algorithms, it is also important to clarify the conceptual differences between the proposed RISM algorithm and t-SNE-based clustering frameworks, which have been widely used in recent studies for pattern discovery and visual analytics. For example, Dhalmahapatra et al.36 employed a combination of MCA, t-SNE, and k-means clustering to develop a decision support system for safety analysis. Their method aims to reduce high-dimensional categorical data into a two-dimensional perceptual space using t-SNE, followed by clustering and rule extraction. While this pipeline is useful for visual interpretation and exploratory analysis, it relies heavily on dimensionality reduction and requires additional steps for clustering.

In contrast, RISM is a fully self-contained clustering algorithm that directly operates in the original feature space without resorting to projection-based transformations. It integrates relative density estimation and inter-cluster connectivity into a unified split-and-merge framework, enabling it to capture both local and global structural characteristics of the data. By avoiding dimensionality reduction, RISM mitigates the risk of structure distortion and preserves the intrinsic cluster relationships. Moreover, RISM can automatically determine the optimal number of clusters through an iterative merging mechanism based on connectivity-aware distance dynamics. These design features allow RISM to achieve superior robustness and adaptability in handling complex cluster shapes, density variations, and noise, in comparison to methods that rely on t-SNE and subsequent clustering algorithms. This distinction highlights the methodological contribution of RISM beyond existing visualization-driven clustering approaches.

Our future work will concentrate on enhancing the automatic detection of the optimal number of clusters and improving the identification performance of the algorithm. It will specifically address the limitations observed on real datasets.
