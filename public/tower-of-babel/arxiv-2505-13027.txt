Unpacking Positional Encoding in Transformers: A Spectral Analysis of Content-Position Coupling
Zihan Gu, Han Zhang, Ruoyu Chen, Hua Zhang

Abstract
Positional encoding (PE) is essential for enabling Transformers to model sequential structure. However, the mechanisms by which different PE schemes couple token content and positional information—and how these mechanisms influence model dynamics—remain theoretically underexplored. In this work, we present a unified framework that analyzes PE through the spectral properties of Toeplitz and related matrices derived from attention logits. We show that multiplicative content-position coupling—exemplified by Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) via a Hadamard product with a Toeplitz matrix—induces spectral contraction, which theoretically improves optimization stability and efficiency. Guided by this theory, we construct synthetic tasks that contrast content-position dependent and content-position independent settings, and evaluate a range of PE methods. Our experiments reveal strong alignment with theory: RoPE consistently outperforms other methods on position-sensitive tasks and induces “single-head deposit” patterns in early layers, indicating localized positional processing. Further analyses show that modifying the method and timing of PE coupling, such as MLA in Deepseek-V3, can effectively mitigate this concentration. These results establish explicit content-relative mixing with relative-position Toeplitz signals as a key principle for effective PE design and provide new insight into how positional structure is integrated in Transformer architectures.



1
Introduction

Transformer architectures have become the prevailing paradigm for sequence modeling across diverse domains owing to their powerful capacity to capture long-range dependencies. A cornerstone of their success is the attention mechanism’s ability to capture dependencies regardless of token distance. However, lacking inherent order sensitivity, Transformers critically rely on positional encoding (PE) to inject information about token positions and enable the modeling of sequential structure
vaswani2017attention
. The field has seen a proliferation of diverse PE variants, ranging from simple additive and learnable absolute embeddings to relative biases
raffel2020exploring
and sophisticated multiplicative schemes like Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE)
SU2024127063
. Yet, despite extensive empirical evaluation comparing their performance on downstream tasks, our fundamental understanding of the internal mechanisms by which these different PE variants specifically couple content and positional information, and how this coupling influences the model’s dynamics and internal learned structure, remains limited.

Existing evaluations of PE methods often rely on aggregate metrics such as perplexity
HuaFoPE2025
or on length-extrapolation benchmarks
kazemnejad2023impact
. While providing valuable performance comparisons, these benchmarks shed little light on
how
positional signals are actually processed or on the mechanisms by which different encodings influence model behavior. Moreover, a focus on length-generalization often implies that, within a fixed sequence length, all PE schemes perform similarly. However, to our knowledge, no prior work has systematically confirmed or challenged this perception regarding within-length performance differences. This lack of understanding and empirical investigation highlights a critical gap in developing a unified theoretical framework that rigorously connects the specific design principles of PE (i.e., how they formulate positional coupling) to their impact on the attention dynamics and learned internal structure.

In this paper, we bridge this gap by introducing a spectral-theoretic framework complemented by targeted empirical studies. As illustrated in Figure
1
, our method begins with the decomposition of each token embedding into content and position components (
c_{i}
and
p_{i}
). We then assume that the position component gives rise to a Toeplitz-structured term in the attention logits, and we characterize how various PE schemes—whether additive biases or multiplicative rotations—map to distinct Toeplitz (or related) matrix operations within the attention computation.

\mathbf{B}

G_{\mathbf{e}}
) structure attention logits.
(Right)

Our framework shows that content–position coupling can take two principal forms in the attention logits: (i) additive mechanisms, where position information (e.g., relative-position encodings) contributes by adding an explicit Toeplitz matrix
\mathbf{B}
to the logits; and (ii) multiplicative schemes, most notably RoPE’s mechanism involving a Hadamard product with the relative-position Toeplitz matrix
G_{\mathbf{e}}
. Applying spectral analysis techniques, including Szegő’s theorem and Schur-product inequalities, we demonstrate theoretically that multiplicative coupling induces spectral contraction—yielding tighter eigenvalue bounds for the relevant matrix structures. This spectral contraction is known to improve optimization stability and efficiency. Consequently, schemes with this property are expected to learn the positional coupling function more rapidly and robustly in shallow layers, leading to its localization within a small set of attention heads. By contrast, additive mechanisms, lacking a direct content–relative position interaction in the logit, are predicted to underperform on tasks that critically depend on combining content with relative positional information.

To empirically validate our theoretical predictions, we design two synthetic tasks: Task 1, which requires content–relative positional reasoning, and Task 2, a position-agnostic control. We evaluate several PE methods (RoPE, Absolute, Relative (T5-style), ALiBi, NoPE) and conduct systematic layer- and head-wise ablations—zeroing out each head’s outputs—to probe the resulting internal structures. We also examine alternative coupling strategies, including MLA variants inspired by Deepseek-V3, to assess how different mechanisms and mixing timings shape positional processing.

Our results align closely with theory. On Task 1, requiring content-relative coupling, RoPE’s multiplicative mechanism consistently outperforms other methods, yielding superior generalization. Head-wise ablations confirm the predicted localization: RoPE produces an early-layer “single-head deposit,” wherein one shallow head carries the bulk of positional computations. By contrast, additive-bias schemes (Absolute, Relative, ALiBi) and NoPE exhibit diffuse or negligible head specialization in both tasks, and RoPE itself shows no deposit pattern on Task 2. Furthermore, MLA variants—specifically those employing a concatenation-based coupling mechanism (e.g., before the inner product)—produce a more distributed “average deposit” pattern. These findings provide strong empirical evidence that the form of content–position coupling fundamentally determines the model’s internal structure and learning dynamics.

•

We introduce a unified spectral-theoretic framework analyzing positional *interaction* in attention logits as structured (Toeplitz) matrices, distinguishing fundamental additive (bias/summation) and multiplicative (Hadamard) coupling mechanisms.

•

We show theoretically that multiplicative coupling (e.g., RoPE) induces spectral contraction—tighter eigenvalue bounds—which theory links to improved optimization stability. We empirically validate that PE methods employing these fundamental mechanisms exhibit predicted performance differences and deposit patterns (e.g., RoPE’s shallow single-head localization) on tasks requiring content-position coupling.

•

Via systematic layer- and head-wise ablations, we further investigate alternative
strategies
beyond fundamental mechanisms, such as MLA’s concatenation-based design. We demonstrate that these approaches yield contrasting, more distributed deposit patterns (e.g., average deposit vs. single-head localization), providing insights into how processing and combination choices influence learned internal structures.

3
Theoretical Structure

3.1
Preliminaries and Assumptions

In Transformer architectures, the attention mechanism relies on the inner product between the query vector
q_{i}
and the key vector
k_{j}
at positions
i
and
j
, respectively. This inner product,
q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}
, serves as the foundation for computing attention scores. Different positional encoding (PE) strategies influence this computation by injecting positional information in distinct ways.

Absolute Positional Encoding
vaswani2017attention
introduces position information at the input layer by directly adding a position vector
p_{i}
to each token embedding
e_{i}
:

\displaystyle h_{i}=e_{i}+p_{i}.

(1)

Consequently, the attention scores are computed based on position-infused vectors:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,j}\propto\exp\bigl((e_{i}+p_{i})^{\top}(e_{j}+p_{j})\bigr).

(2)

The residual connections throughout the Transformer layers ensure that this form of position-content coupling persists at deeper levels.

Relative Positional Encoding
raffel2020exploring
modifies the attention logits post inner-product, by adding a relative position-dependent bias term
b_{j-i}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,j}\propto\exp\bigl(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}+b_{j-i}\bigr).

(3)

Here,
b_{j-i}
is often a learnable parameter, either directly or as a function of relative distance
j-i
parameterized through shared weights. This method allows position-dependent modifications without altering the token representations themselves.

Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE)
SU2024127063
instead applies a position-specific rotation matrix
R_{i}
(or
R_{j}
) to each query and key vector before the inner product is computed. Due to the properties of these rotational transformations, the resulting dot product admits a key equivalence:

\displaystyle q_{i}\leftarrow R_{i}q_{i},\quad k_{j}\leftarrow R_{j}k_{j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(R_{i}q_{i})^{\top}(R_{j}k_{j})=q_{i}^{\top}R_{j-i}k_{j}.

(4)

Here,
R_{j-i}
is a rotation matrix determined solely by the relative position difference. RoPE thus achieves a tightly coupled interaction between content and relative position within the attention score computation.

Notably, even in the absence of explicit positional encoding—commonly referred to as NoPE
haviv-etal-2022-transformer
—large Transformer models have demonstrated an ability to implicitly encode position through learned patterns in attention heads or depth-wise representation dynamics. This motivates our first key assumption:

Assumption 3.1

(Representation Decomposition)
.

Each token representation
x_{i}
at position
i
can be decomposed into a position-independent content component
c_{i}
and a position-dependent component
p_{i}
, such that

\displaystyle x_{i}=c_{i}+p_{i}.

(5)

This decomposition provides a conceptual foundation to analyze how different PE mechanisms construct or interact with the positional component
p_{i}
, and how this interaction shapes attention.

To formalize the structural implications of positional interaction in attention, we invoke a classic object in matrix analysis:

Definition 3.1

(Toeplitz Matrix)
.

A matrix
A
is a Toeplitz matrix if its entries are constant along each diagonal for some sequence
{a_{k}}
. An
N\times N
Toeplitz matrix
T
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle T=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\cdots&a_{-(N-1)}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{-1}&\cdots&a_{-(N-2)}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}&\cdots&a_{-(N-3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{N-1}&a_{N-2}&a_{N-3}&\cdots&a_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(6)

We denote this compactly as
T=[a_{i-j}]
.

Toeplitz matrices naturally emerge in systems with translation-invariant or shift-dependent structure, making them particularly suited to model attention patterns influenced by relative position.

Our second foundational assumption connects this matrix structure to positional information within the attention mechanism:

Assumption 3.2

(Toeplitz Structure from Positional Interaction)
.

The attention score matrix induced solely by the position-dependent components
p_{i}
exhibits a Toeplitz structure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Attention}(p_{i},p_{j})=[a_{i-j}].

(7)

Remark 3.1
.

The equal sign is used here because the normalization operation does not change the Toeplitz property of the matrix.

Remark 3.2
.

It can be verified that all forms of sine-cosine position embedding of absolute position encoding satisfy this assumption and the actual effect of relative position encoding is also presented in the form of a Toeplitz matrix.

Together, these two assumptions enable us to explore how positional information modulates the spectral behavior of attention matrices, and to quantify the effects of different PE strategies using tools from the spectral theory of Toeplitz operators.

3.2
Toeplitz Representation of Positional Encoding

In the Transformer’s attention mechanism, the interaction between representations at positions
i
and
j
is captured by the attention logit matrix
\mathbf{L}
, where
L_{i,j}=\text{AttentionLogit}_{i,j}
. Different positional encoding (PE) mechanisms influence this logit matrix in distinct ways to incorporate positional information.

Following our decomposition
x_{i}=c_{i}+p_{i}
, where
c_{i}
is the content component and
p_{i}
is the position component, and defining
q_{i}^{c}=W_{Q}c_{i}
,
q_{i}^{p}=W_{Q}p_{i}
,
k_{j}^{c}=W_{K}c_{j}
, and
k_{j}^{p}=W_{K}p_{j}
, we analyze the structure of
\mathbf{L}
.

We denote the Gram matrix of a set of column vectors
\{v_{i}\}
and
\{w_{j}\}
as
G_{v,w}
, where
(G_{v,w})_{i,j}=v_{i}^{\top}w_{j}
. Under this notation, for PE methods that operate via additive modification of token representations or attention biases (excluding RoPE), the attention logit matrix can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\text{Non-RoPE}}=G_{q^{c},k^{c}}+G_{q^{c},k^{p}}+G_{q^{p},k^{c}}+G_{q^{p},k^{p}}+\mathbf{B}

(8)

Here,
\mathbf{B}
denotes an explicit bias matrix (e.g., from T5 or ALiBi). Under our theoretical assumptions, the term
G_{q^{p},k^{p}}
forms a Toeplitz matrix, since it depends only on relative positions. Similarly,
\mathbf{B}
is Toeplitz, as it typically encodes relative-position-dependent biases (
B_{i,j}=b_{i-j}
). The remaining terms involving content (
G_{q^{c},k^{c}}
,
G_{q^{c},k^{p}}
,
G_{q^{p},k^{c}}
) are not Toeplitz due to their dependence on specific token content.

In contrast, RoPE applies complex-valued rotations to token representations. By pairing adjacent dimensions into complex numbers, we construct complex-valued vectors
\mathbf{q}_{i}
,
\mathbf{k}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{p}_{i}
, and define a modulation vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
such that
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{(j)}=e^{i\theta_{j}}
. The logit matrix then takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\text{RoPE\_complex}}=\left(G_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}}+G_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}}+G_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k}}+G_{\mathbf{p}}\right)\circ G_{\mathbf{e}}

(9)

with
\circ
denoting the Hadamard (element-wise) product, and the final logit is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\text{RoPE}}=\operatorname{Re}\left\{\mathbf{L}_{\text{RoPE\_complex}}\right\}

(10)

Since
G_{\mathbf{e}}
is Toeplitz by construction, the Hadamard product introduces relative-position dependence multiplicatively across all terms.

This formal structure allows us to compare how various PE mechanisms couple content and positional information. In the Non-RoPE case:

•

G_{q^{c},k^{c}}
encodes content-content similarity.

•

G_{q^{p},k^{p}}
captures position-position interactions and is Toeplitz.

•

G_{q^{c},k^{p}}
and
G_{q^{p},k^{c}}
are cross terms encoding interactions between content and absolute position.

•

\mathbf{B}
encodes a content-independent, relative-position bias and is Toeplitz.

While
G_{q^{p},k^{p}}
and
\mathbf{B}
directly provide relative-position structure, they do not incorporate content-specific modulation. The cross-terms
G_{q^{c},k^{p}}
and
G_{q^{p},k^{c}}
may encode interactions between content and absolute position, but for these to collectively capture content-relative position coupling, the model would need to learn mappings where the cross-term interactions depend solely on
i-j
, yet are modulated by content. Such constrained mappings are difficult to realize in practice.

By contrast, RoPE’s multiplicative structure enables a more direct content-relative interaction: each component in the Gram matrix sum is modulated entrywise by the Toeplitz matrix
G_{\mathbf{e}}
, allowing relative-position structure to directly shape the contribution of content vectors. This results in a more expressive and learnable coupling mechanism between content and relative position.

Based on this analysis
, we hypothesize that PE mechanisms enabling direct content-relative interaction within the attention logits will perform better on tasks requiring such coupling. RoPE, via its Hadamard product with
G_{\mathbf{e}}
, provides this mechanism, whereas Non-RoPE methods rely on structurally constrained additive approximations.

To test this hypothesis
, we will design a synthetic evaluation task specifically tailored to probe the model’s ability to utilize content-relative positional information. This task will serve as our first benchmark for evaluating the expressive power of different positional encoding strategies.

3.3
Spectral Properties and Theoretical Prediction of Deposit Patterns

The structure of the attention logit matrix
\mathbf{L}
, particularly the properties of its Toeplitz or Toeplitz-like components, provides crucial insights into the stability and dynamics of positional information processing. The spectral properties of these matrices, especially the distribution and range of their eigenvalues, are known to influence the convergence of optimization algorithms.

For Toeplitz matrices, classical results such as Szegő’s theorem connect matrix structure to asymptotic eigenvalue distributions. We recall a relevant form below:

Theorem 3.1

(Szegő’s Theorem on Eigenvalue Distribution (From
grenander2002toeplitz
))
.

Let
T_{N}(a)
be an
N\times N
Toeplitz matrix with
(T_{N}(a))_{i,j}=a_{i-j}
, and let
a(e^{i\theta})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{k}e^{ik\theta}
be its symbol. If
a(e^{i\theta})
is real-valued and continuous, then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{(N)}
of
T_{N}(a)
asymptotically fill the interval
[\min a(e^{i\theta}),\max a(e^{i\theta})]
as
N\to\infty
.

Applying this theorem and related spectral analysis techniques to the Toeplitz structures induced by various PE mechanisms:

•

In Non-RoPE methods, the Toeplitz components
G_{q^{p},k^{p}}
and
\mathbf{B}
contribute additively to the logit, and their spectral ranges are determined by their respective coefficient sequences.

•

In RoPE, the complex logit involves a Hadamard product with the Toeplitz matrix
G_{\mathbf{e}}
. This multiplicative interaction theoretically contracts the eigenvalue range relative to additive compositions, resulting in tighter spectral bounds.

Spectral contraction—manifested as a reduced eigenvalue range or lower condition number—is well known to accelerate and stabilize gradient-based learning
boyd2004convex
;
gray2006toeplitz
. The spectral properties associated with RoPE’s coupling mechanism, through this contraction, facilitate faster and more stable learning of the content-relative positional interaction. We give a more detailed analysis in Appendix
B
.

This enhanced convergence property leads to a concrete structural prediction: in early layers, the model tends towards specializing attention heads for content-relative positional coupling, while other heads in the same layer may exhibit less specific positional roles. This specialization results in a localized
deposit pattern
, where certain heads disproportionately contribute to processing positional information. Such behavior is expected to emerge more prominently under PE mechanisms with favorable spectral properties—most notably, RoPE—and to be absent or weaker in less structured encodings.

To validate this prediction
, we design ablation experiments to confirm the emergence and characteristics of this deposit pattern.

4
Task Design and Experiment

4.1
Experiment Setup and Task Design

To evaluate our theoretical predictions on positional encoding and learning dynamics, we use a 6-layer Transformer decoder and compare six PE configurations: Absolute Positional Encoding, T5-style Relative Positional Encoding, ALiBi, NoPE (no positional encoding), Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE), and random, fixed embeddings. Full training details—including hyperparameters, optimizer settings, and vocabulary construction—are provided in Appendix
C
.

We introduce two synthetic classification tasks that isolate positional requirements:

Task 1: Trigger Word Relative Distance Prediction.

Each sequence contains two designated trigger words drawn from a small vocabulary (500–1000 tokens). The target label is the absolute index distance between these words. This task demands tight coupling of token identity and relative position. We generate 100,000 examples with a 7:3 train/test split.

Task 2: Trigger Word Counting.

Each sequence is labeled by the total count of occurrences of a fixed set of 10–30 trigger words, irrespective of their positions. This position-agnostic task tests pure content recognition. We generate 50,000 examples with a 7:3 train/test split.

These tasks enable a controlled comparison of PE methods under position-sensitive (Task 1) and position-agnostic (Task 2) settings, directly testing our theoretical framework.

Relative encoding attenuates its learned bias toward zero and thus matches NoPE’s performance. ALiBi suffers most, as its rigid, fixed Toeplitz bias obstructs even simple counting.

Ablation Studies and Mechanistic Insights

5.1
Attention Head Specialization for Positional Processing

To complete our theoretical argument, we predict that PE mechanisms with spectrally stable position coupling—such as RoPE—will localize essential positional computations into a few specialized attention heads, especially in early layers, yielding a deposit pattern. We test this via a causal, head-wise ablation study: for each layer
l
and head
h
, we zero out the head’s output
H_{l,h}
and measure the resulting drop in Task 1 test accuracy relative to the intact model. This accuracy reduction quantifies each head’s criticality for content–position coupling.

5.2
Modulating Deposit Patterns via MLA

Motivated by the observed deposit pattern and the “partial RoPE” results, we explore two strategies to diffuse positional responsibility across heads. First, we augment RoPE by adding an absolute positional embedding before the first layer, which provides an initial position cue that can be adjusted by deeper layers. Second, we adopt the MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention) mechanism from Deepseek-V3, which mixes NoPE and RoPE signals before the attention inner product.

We present the result of the early method in Figure

However, starting from the second layer, the deposit pattern reappears, which also indirectly reflects that the deposit pattern is an inherent phenomenon of the coupling between rope capture content and relative position. It will not be completely eliminated by adding absolute position information to the input.

6
Conclusion and Limitations

In conclusion, we have introduced a spectral-theoretic framework that connects positional encoding designs to the eigenstructure of attention logits and shown—through synthetic tasks and head-wise ablations—that these spectral signatures reliably predict learning dynamics and internal processing patterns (e.g., RoPE’s “single-head deposit”). Our results illuminate how different coupling mechanisms shape the interplay between content and position in attention and suggest practical strategies—such as controlled mixing—to achieve both spectral stability and distributed encoding. These insights lay the groundwork for principled design of more robust and interpretable PE schemes in Transformer models.

Limitations:
This study is primarily theoretical and relies on controlled synthetic tasks, so its findings may not directly translate to performance on complex, real-world datasets or broad benchmarks. We also explore only a limited range of task complexities and positional relationships; more intricate challenges—such as sequence reversal or Dyck-language recognition—likely exceed a Transformer’s positional reasoning capabilities. Finally, our proposed connection between single-head deposit patterns and length extrapolation remains theoretical and requires direct empirical validation.

Appendix B
Detailed Theoretical Analysis

In this section of the appendix, we provide detailed theoretical analysis and derivations supporting the non-trivial conclusions presented in the main text. Our analysis relies on idealized proofs that simplify the complex optimization dynamics of neural networks, aiming to reveal a theoretical explanation for the observed experimental phenomena. While the actual simulation and analysis of non-convex optimization in full Transformer models is extremely difficult, this theoretical framework offers valuable insights into the underlying principles.

Our analysis is predicated on a core theoretical link regarding the role of spectral properties in learning. We rely on the established principle that favorable spectral properties of matrices involved in optimization—such as a more contracted eigenvalue spectrum (tighter bounds) or a smaller spectral norm—correlate with faster and more stable convergence of learning algorithms. Building upon this, our theoretical framework posits that the rapid and stable convergence of the positional coupling function, driven by these favorable spectral properties, will lead to a specific phenomenon during training: the localization of this function within a limited set of attention heads in shallow layers, resulting in the empirically observed deposit pattern
[
30
]
.

Proposition B.3 (Spectral Contraction):
This proposition formally proves the spectral property claimed in the main text. By applying theorems like Szegő’s theorem and inequalities concerning the Hadamard product, it demonstrates that the Toeplitz matrix structure associated with multiplicative coupling (as in RoPE) has a more desirable eigenvalue spectrum (e.g., more compact or tighter bounds) compared to Toeplitz structures associated with additive coupling mechanisms.

We simplify the proposition into the following: Let

W

Here, each element of E is a complex number with a membrane length of 1. We can directly use Schur’s inequality to prove that the membrane length of the eigenvalue of

\mathbf{Symble}(W\circ E)(\theta)=\sum_{i=-N+1}^{N-1}w_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}(i\theta_{i}+i\theta)}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}2\mathbf{Re}\{w_{i}\}\cos(i\theta_{i}+i\theta)

(Amplitude bound and phase–alignment criterion)
.

Fix an integer
N\geq 1
and non-negative weights
w_{i}\geq 0
. Define

Then

\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\!\bigl|f(\theta)\bigr|\;\leq\;\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\!\bigl|g(\theta)\bigr|\;=\;2\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}w_{i}.

Equality holds
iff
all phase offsets coincide modulo
2\pi
:

\theta_{0}\equiv\theta_{1}\equiv\cdots\equiv\theta_{N-1}\pmod{2\pi}.

Consequently the peak-to-peak range of
g
(width
4\sum_{i}w_{i}
) never falls
below that of
f
, and both ranges coincide only under complete phase
alignment.

Proof.

Denote

P(z):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}c_{i}z^{\,i},\quad c_{i}:=w_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}},\qquad Q(z):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}w_{i}z^{\,i},\quad|z|=1.

Upper bound.

For any
|z|=1
the triangle inequality gives

|P(z)|\;\leq\;\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|c_{i}|\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}w_{i}.

Hence

|f(\theta)|\;=\;2\bigl|\Re P(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\bigr|\;\leq\;2\!\left|P(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right|\;\leq\;2\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}w_{i}\;=\;\bigl|g(0)\bigr|.

Conversely,
\bigl|\cos(i\theta)\bigr|\leq 1
implies

|g(\theta)|\leq 2\sum_{i}w_{i}
for every
\theta
, while

g(0)=2\sum_{i}w_{i}
shows that this bound is attained, so

\max_{\theta}|g(\theta)|=2\sum_{i}w_{i}
. Combining the two yields the desired

Equality case.

Suppose
\max_{\theta}|f(\theta)|=2\sum_{i}w_{i}
. Then at some

\theta^{\star}
both previous inequalities are tight:

\bigl|P(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\star}})\bigr|=\sum_{i}w_{i},\qquad\bigl|\Re P(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\star}})\bigr|=\bigl|P(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\star}})\bigr|.

The first equality forces **all terms in the sum for
P
to share a common
phase**, i.e.

is the same for every

i

0
or
\pi
, which does not
affect alignment. Thus

\theta_{i}+i\theta^{\star}\equiv\theta_{0}\pmod{2\pi}\quad\forall i\;\Longrightarrow\;\theta_{i}\equiv\theta_{0}\pmod{2\pi}\quad\forall i.

Conversely, if all
\theta_{i}
are equal, choosing

The lemma follows.

C.4
Why Position Embedding Complex?

We conclude this section by explaining why understanding positional encodings is difficult and almost impossible to decouple.

We first give our conclusion that the position component in the token comes from manifold embedding rather than subspace decomposition, which means that extracting the position component requires us to have an algorithm to solve the changes in the position manifold. In an extremely ideal case, we can use the Levinson–Durbin algorithm in signal processing to recursively solve it through the value of the Toeplitz matrix. However, extracting a Toeplitz matrix from a general matrix is not unique, and we cannot determine whether the extracted Toeplitz matrix truly represents the position information.

This conclusion comes from a very simple observation: the position embedding of absolute position encoding is not orthogonal to word embedding. Therefore, there is no subspace decomposition that can completely separate the two components.

If we denote f as a continuous embedding from
S_{1}\otimes S_{1}...\otimes S_{1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and g as a continuous embedding from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
S_{1}\otimes S_{1}...\otimes S_{1}
, then the essence of absolute position encoding is actually
f(g(i))
. In other words, the explicit position encoding that we can understand is essentially a manifold embedding.

The general method of dealing with manifold embeddings requires a specific embedding mapping. For general positional encodings, this mapping is not solvable, so it is almost impossible to decouple the positional encoding.

Appendix D
Relationship to Length Generation

Here, we give a more detailed qualitative statement to describe our thinking on the relationship between deposit patterns and length generation.

More than one study has shown that transformer’s understanding of position information starts from the first layer
[
19
,
9
,
5
]
. The key to successful length generalization is that responses to relative position information within the training length can be naturally transferred to a longer length (test length). Therefore, the lower the coupling between location and content, the better the scalability. If the coupling degree is high, it is necessary to learn the coupling mode.

Our experimental results show that the deposit pattern is not difficult to improve through dropout, which means that once a head of trasnformer of RoPE captures the pattern of training position information, the other heads no longer capture the position information. Therefore, the number of parameters responding to position content coupling is much less than that of relative position encoding and no position encoding, resulting in higher compression and lower regularization.

At the same time, Deposit Patterns shows that only some frequency bands are fully trained during training like
[
4
]
. These frequency bands are passed through the fully connected layer, spreading the influence to most of the frequency bands in the deep layer, which means that in fact only specific relative position differences are considered by the training process, so the length generalization ability is drastically affected.
