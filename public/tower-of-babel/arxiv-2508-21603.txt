On the boundary Carrollian conformal algebra
Lucas Buzaglo, Xiao He, Tuan Anh Pham, Haijun Tan, Girish S Vishwa, Kaiming Zhao

Abstract
. We initiate the mathematical study of the boundary Carrollian conformal algebra (BCCA), an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra recently discovered in the context of Carrollian physics. The BCCA is an intriguing object from both physical and mathematical perspectives, since it is a filtered but not graded Lie algebra. In this paper, we first construct some modules for the BCCA and one of its subalgebras, which we call 𝒪 \mathcal{O} , by restriction of well-known modules of the BMS 3 and Witt algebras respectively. After showing that such modules are free or “almost free”, we go through some structure theory on the BCCA to define a new basis and a decreasing filtration on the algebra which lets us construct BCCA-modules intrinsically. In particular, we construct Whittaker modules over the BCCA and the subalgebra 𝒪 \mathcal{O} and prove criteria for their irreducibility.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, research on infinite-dimensional Lie algebras has enjoyed continuous motivation and inspiration from theoretical physics. Indeed, with the discovery of more examples of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras in physical contexts comes the need to better understand these algebras and their representations. On many occasions, this leads to a productive cycle of progress in both fields, due to the resulting mathematics research informing physics in some manner, which once again motivates another mathematical study. The origin of this paper is no different – we present a first study on another infinite-dimensional Lie algebra that was recently discovered in physics, known as the boundary Carrollian conformal algebra (BCCA), which we denote by

Certainly, there exists strong physical and mathematical motivation for this paper. The former arises from the fact that an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra which is not integer-graded has made an appearance in a modern theoretical physics setting, meaning that we need to develop the tools needed to study such objects thoroughly, starting with the BCCA as the inaugural case study. The latter comes from the desire to understand infinite-dimensional subalgebras of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras more thoroughly.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2

Section
3

and Virasoro algebras. This would be the naive approach to the studying the representations of the BCCA based on its physical origins in
[

]
. The emergence of free and “almost free” modules motivates the need to study modules of the BCCA intrinsically. This requires the change of basis presented in Section

5
.
All this effort pays off in Section
6
, in which we construct the universal Whittaker modules over the BCCA and its subalgebra
\mathcal{O}
, and determine criteria for their irreducibility.
Finally, in Section
7

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{n}\cdot\widetilde{P}_{m}^{(b)}=-(bn+m)\widetilde{P}_{n+m}^{(b)}-(bn-m)\widetilde{P}_{n-m}^{(b)}\quad(n,m\geq 1).

For
b=-1
, we write
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{-1}
. Note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{-1}
is the adjoint module of
\mathcal{O}
.

It is easy to see that
I(0,b)
decomposes as a direct sum of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module.

Lemma 2.7
.

As
\mathcal{O}
-modules,
I(0,b)\cong\mathcal{P}_{b}\oplus\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
.

Proof.

There exists an
\mathcal{O}
-module homomorphism
\rho\colon I(0,b)\to\mathcal{P}_{b}
given by
\rho(I_{n})=P_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We then have a short exact sequence of
\mathcal{O}
-modules

(2.6)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I(0,b)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}_{b}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

The inclusion
\mathcal{P}_{b}\hookrightarrow I(0,b)
is a section of the short exact sequence, and thus the sequence is split. It follows that
I(0,b)\cong\mathcal{P}_{b}\oplus\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
for all
b\in\Bbbk
.
∎

We therefore see that Definition
2.4
can be succinctly summarised as
\mathfrak{b}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P}_{-1}=\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P}
, analogous to how the centreless BMS
3
algebra is defined as
\mathcal{W}(0,-1)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes I(0,-1)
.

2.2.
Whittaker modules

Whittaker modules were first discovered for
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
by Arnal and Pinczon in
[
AP74
]
, following which Kostant defined them for finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebras in
[
Kos78
]
. This was then generalised to arbitrary Lie algebras with triangular decomposition.
Today, Whittaker modules are defined more generally via the notion of “Whittaker pairs”
[
BM11
,
MZ14
]
and play an important role in the representation theory of various infinite-dimensional Lie algebras
[
Chr08
,
OW09
,
ZTL10
,
GL11
,
Wan11
,
LWZ10
,
ALZ16
,
LPX19
,
CJ20
,
Che21
,
DGL24
,
CGLW24
]
.

In this paper, we construct Whittaker modules of
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
based on the definition given in
[
BM11
, Section 3.2]
and
[
MZ14
, Section 5.1]
, thereby presenting explicit examples of their constructions for infinite-dimensional Lie algebras that are not integer-graded.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra.

(1)

Define
\mathfrak{g}^{0}\coloneq\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{k+1}\coloneq[\mathfrak{g}^{k},\mathfrak{g}]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
quasi-nilpotent
if
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{g}^{k}=0
.

(2)

A
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
is said to be
locally nilpotent
if for every
v\in V
, there exists
s(v)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{s(v)}\cdot v=0
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{s(v)}\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Equipped with the above definition, we can now define Whittaker modules.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with a quasi-nilpotent subalgebra
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}
is a locally nilpotent
\mathfrak{n}
-module under the adjoint action, and let
V
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module.

(1)

The
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
is a
Whittaker module
if
U(\mathfrak{n})\cdot v
is finite-dimensional for all
v\in V
(in other words,
V
is a
locally finite

\mathfrak{n}
-module).

(2)

A Lie algebra homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{n}\to\Bbbk
is called a
Whittaker function
(so
\varphi([\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{n}])=0
). A vector
v\in V
is called a
Whittaker vector of type
\varphi
if
x\cdot v=\varphi(x)v
for all
x\in\mathfrak{n}
. The
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
is called a
Whittaker module of type
\varphi
if it is generated by a Whittaker vector of type
\varphi
.

(3)

Let
\Bbbk_{\varphi}\coloneq\Bbbk 1_{\varphi}
be the one-dimensional
\mathfrak{n}
-module defined by
\varphi
, in other words,
x\cdot 1_{\varphi}=\varphi(x)1_{\varphi}
for all
x\in\mathfrak{n}
. Then the induced module

M_{\varphi}\coloneq\Ind_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathfrak{g}}\Bbbk_{\varphi}

is the
universal Whittaker module of type
\varphi
.

We proceed to briefly show how Definition
2.9
can be readily applied to filtered Lie algebras.

Definition 2.10
.

A
filtered Lie algebra
is a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with a decreasing sequence of subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}

\dots\supseteq\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\supseteq\mathfrak{g}_{0}\supseteq\mathfrak{g}_{1}\supseteq\mathfrak{g}_{2}\supseteq\cdots

such that
[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{i+j}
. We further demand that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

The filtration is
weakly convergent
. That is,
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{g}_{k}=0
.

(2)

The filtration is
bounded from below
. That is, there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{k}=\mathfrak{g}
for all
k<N
.

Using this definition, it is easy to show that filtered Lie algebras always have Whittaker modules.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a filtered Lie algebra as per Definition
2.10
. If
n\geq 1
, then
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
is a quasi-nilpotent Lie algebra, and
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n}
is a locally nilpotent
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
-module.

In particular, one can always construct Whittaker modules for
\mathfrak{g}
by choosing
\mathfrak{n}\coloneqq\mathfrak{g}_{n}
in Definition
2.9
for any
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Use conditions (
1
) and (
2
) from Definition
2.10
.
∎

We introduce a related but slightly different class of modules known as
quasi-Whittaker modules
that were recently studied in
[
CGL+25
]
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a non-perfect ideal of a non-semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, let
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\Bbbk
be a Lie algebra homomorphism, and let
V
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module.

(1)

A vector
v\in V
is called a
quasi-Whittaker vector of type
\varphi
if
h\cdot v=\varphi(h)v
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}
. The
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
is called a
quasi-Whittaker module of type
\varphi
if it is generated by a Whittaker vector of type
\varphi
.

(2)

Let
\Bbbk_{\varphi}\coloneq\Bbbk 1_{\varphi}
be the one-dimensional
\mathfrak{h}
-module defined by
\varphi
. Then the induced module

W_{\varphi}\coloneq\Ind_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}\Bbbk_{\varphi}

is the
universal quasi-Whittaker module of type
\varphi
.

Remark 2.13
.

Definition
2.12
applies to
\mathfrak{h}
, which need not be quasi-nilpotent, as opposed to
\mathfrak{n}
in Definition
2.9
. However, it requires that
\mathfrak{h}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
, while
\mathfrak{n}
need not be.

Next, we define the notion of the
Whittaker annihilator
corresponding to a Whittaker function, a key concept introduced in
[
CGL+25
]
.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a non-perfect ideal of a non-semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\Bbbk
be a Lie algebra homomorphism. The
Whittaker annihilator of
\varphi
over
\mathfrak{g}
is defined to be

\mathfrak{g}^{\varphi}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\varphi([x,y])=0\text{ for all }y\in\mathfrak{h}\}.

We conclude this section by presenting two key results from
[
CGL+25
]
which emphasise the importance of the Whittaker annihilator.

Theorem 2.15

(
[
CGL+25
, Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.12]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be non-semisimple Lie algebra,
\mathfrak{h}
a non-perfect ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\Bbbk
a Lie algebra homomorphism. Then the following hold.

(1)

The universal quasi-Whittaker module
W_{\varphi}=\Ind_{\mathfrak{h}}^{\mathfrak{g}}\Bbbk_{\varphi}
is irreducible if and only if
\mathfrak{g}^{\varphi}=\mathfrak{h}
.

(2)

If
\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\varphi}/\mathfrak{h})=1
, choose
y\in\mathfrak{g}^{\varphi}\setminus\mathfrak{h}
, so that
\mathfrak{g}^{\varphi}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\Bbbk y
. Then all maximal submodules of
W_{\varphi}
are of the form

J_{\xi}\coloneqq U(\mathfrak{g})(y-\xi)\cdot 1_{\varphi}

for
\xi\in\Bbbk
.

3.
Representations of
\mathcal{O}
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
by restriction

Recall that in its original form presented in
[
BCC+25
]
, the BCCA emerged as the subalgebra

\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}\coloneqq\spn\{\mathcal{O}_{n}\coloneqq L_{n}-L_{-n},P_{m}\coloneqq M_{m}+M_{-m},C_{M}\mid n\geq 1,\ m\geq 0\}

of the BMS
3
algebra. Hence, the most natural course of action is to study the restrictions of representations of
\Vir
and
\mathfrak{bms}
to
\mathcal{O}
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
respectively. In doing so, we will also motivate the need for the alternative, intrinsic approaches of constructing representations of
\mathfrak{b}
presented in this paper. Note that the representation theory of
\Vir
and
\mathfrak{bms}
is fairly extensive. Hence, we restrict our attention to the most popular choices of modules over these algebras, namely the Verma modules (over both) and the so-called massive modules (over
\mathfrak{bms}
).

3.1.
\mathcal{O}
-modules via restriction of Verma modules

We first recall the definition of a Verma module of the Virasoro algebra. Recalling Definition
2.1
, the Virasoro algebra admits a triangular decomposition

\Vir=\Vir_{+}\oplus\Vir_{0}\oplus\Vir_{-},\text{ where }\Vir_{\pm}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\pm}}\Bbbk L_{n}\text{ and }\Vir_{0}=\Bbbk L_{0}\oplus\Bbbk C.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
h,c\in\Bbbk
. Define the one-dimensional
\Vir_{0}\oplus\Vir_{+}
-module
\Bbbk_{h,c}\coloneqq\Bbbk\ket{h,c}
, where

L_{0}\ket{h,c}=h\ket{h,c},\quad C\ket{h,c}=c\ket{h,c},\quad\text{and }\Vir_{+}\ket{h,c}=0.

The
Verma module of the Virasoro algebra

V(h,c)
is the
\Vir
-module induced from
\Bbbk_{h,c}
. That is,

V(h,c)\coloneqq\Ind_{\Vir_{0}\oplus\Vir_{+}}^{\Vir}\Bbbk_{h,c}.

Remark 3.2
.

For all
h,c\in\Bbbk
, the module
V(h,c)
admits a Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt (PBW) basis of monomials

(3.1)

L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}\ket{h,c},

where
n_{k}\geq n_{k-1}\geq\dots\geq n_{1}\geq 1
. Therefore,
V(h,c)
is cyclically generated by the action of
\Vir_{-}
on
\ket{h,c}
. As left
U(\Vir)
-modules,

V(h,c)\cong U(\Vir)/\big(U(\Vir)\cdot(L_{n>0},L_{0}-h,C-c)\big).

Furthermore,
\Vir
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Lie algebra, with

\deg(L_{n})\coloneqq n,\quad\deg(C)\coloneqq 0

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
U(\Vir)\cdot(L_{n>0},L_{0}-h,C-c)
is a graded left ideal of
U(\Vir)
, the Verma module
V(h,c)
inherits this grading. In other words, for
n<0
, we define

V(h,c)_{n}\coloneqq\spn\{L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}\ket{h,c}\mid n_{i}\geq 1\text{ and }n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=-n\}.

Currently, this a general statement about graded algebras, so we do not make use of the PBW theorem and therefore do not require the monomials
L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}\ket{h,c}
to be ordered.
By the discussion above,
V(h,c)
is a graded
U(\Vir)
-module:

V(h,c)=\bigoplus_{n\leq 0}V(h,c)_{n},\quad L_{m}\cdot V(h,c)_{n}\subseteq V(h,c)_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, where

V(h,c)_{0}=\Bbbk_{h,c}\text{ and }V(h,c)_{n}=0\text{ for all }n\geq 1.

We further define

V(h,c)_{\geq n}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{m\geq n}V(h,c)_{m}

for all
n<0
.

The following proposition describes
V(h,c)
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module.

Proposition 3.3
.

The Verma module
V(h,c)
is a free
U(\mathcal{O})
-module of rank 1 for all
h,c\in\Bbbk
.

Proof.

First, we claim that
V(h,c)
is generated by
\ket{h,c}
as a
U(\mathcal{O})
-module. This is most easily seen by considering the basis
\{\mathcal{O}_{n},L_{m},C\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 0\}
of
\Vir
. This then induces a different PBW basis on
U(\Vir)
:

\mathcal{O}_{n_{k}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{n_{1}}L_{m_{\ell}}\dots L_{m_{1}}C^{r},

where
1\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{k}
,
0\leq m_{1}\leq\dots\leq m_{\ell}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. In this PBW basis, the description of
V(h,c)
given in Remark
3.2
as a quotient of
U(\Vir)
by a left ideal easily translates to the fact that

\{\mathcal{O}_{n_{k}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{n_{1}}\ket{h,c}\mid 1\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{k}\}

is a basis for
V(h,c)
. This proves the claim.

Next, we show that
V(h,c)
is a free
U(\mathcal{O})
-module. To the contrary, we assume that
X\ket{h,c}=0
for some non-zero
X\in U(\mathcal{O})
. By the PBW theorem, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
X
can be written as

X=\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}a_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\mathcal{O}_{n_{k}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{n_{1}}+\sum_{m_{1}+\dots+m_{\ell}<n}b_{m_{1},\dots,m_{\ell}}\mathcal{O}_{m_{\ell}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{m_{1}}

where
n_{k}\geq\dots\geq n_{1}\geq 1
,
m_{\ell}\geq\dots\geq m_{1}\geq 1
,
a_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}},b_{m_{1},\dots,m_{\ell}}\in\Bbbk
, and
a_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\neq 0
for some
n_{i}
. Then

0=X\ket{h,c}\equiv\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}a_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}\ket{h,c}\pmod{V(h,c)_{\geq-(n-1)}}.

It follows that

\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}a_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}\ket{h,c}=0

in
V(h,c)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Free modules are not the most interesting from a representation theoretic point of view as they are not irreducible. This motivates our approach in the later sections, where we change basis and construct
\mathcal{O}
-modules as modules of a subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
that is isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}
. This will give us explicit examples of classes of irreducible
\mathcal{O}
-modules.

3.2.
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-modules via restriction of Verma modules

Just like the Virasoro algebra, the BMS
3
algebra is also a graded Lie algebra: we have
\mathfrak{bms}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{bms}_{n}
, where
\mathfrak{bms}_{n}\coloneqq\Bbbk L_{n}\oplus\Bbbk M_{n}
for
n\neq 0
, and
\mathfrak{bms}_{0}\coloneqq\Bbbk L_{0}\oplus\Bbbk M_{0}\oplus\Bbbk C_{L}\oplus\Bbbk C_{M}
.
Its triangular decomposition is given by

\mathfrak{bms}=\mathfrak{bms}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{bms}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{bms}_{-}
, where

\mathfrak{bms}_{\pm}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\pm}}\Bbbk L_{n}\oplus\Bbbk M_{n}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}\in\Bbbk
. Define the one-dimensional
\mathfrak{bms}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{bms}_{+}
-module

\Bbbk_{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}\coloneqq\Bbbk\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},

where

\displaystyle L_{0}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}=h_{L}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},\quad

\displaystyle M_{0}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}=h_{M}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},

\displaystyle C_{L}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}=c_{L}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},\quad

\displaystyle C_{M}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}=c_{M}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},

and
L_{n>0}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}=M_{n>0}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}
.
The
Verma module of the BMS
3
algebra

V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})
is the
\mathfrak{bms}
-module induced from
\Bbbk_{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}
. That is,

V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})\coloneqq\Ind_{\mathfrak{bms}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{bms}_{+}}^{\mathfrak{bms}}\Bbbk_{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}.

The Verma module
V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})
admits a Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt basis of monomials

L_{-n_{k}}\dots L_{-n_{1}}M_{-m_{\ell}}\dots M_{-m_{1}}\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}},

where
n_{k}\geq n_{k-1}\geq\dots\geq n_{1}\geq 1
,
m_{\ell}\geq m_{\ell-1}\geq\dots m_{1}\geq 1
.

V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})
is therefore cyclically generated by the action of
\mathfrak{bms}_{-}
on
\ket{h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M}}
.
As left
U(\mathfrak{bms})
-modules,

V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})\cong U(\mathfrak{bms})/\big(U(\mathfrak{bms})\cdot(L_{n>0},M_{n>0},L_{0}-h_{L},M_{0}-h_{M},C_{L}-c_{L},C_{M}-c_{M})\big).

The following proposition describes
V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})
as a
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-module.

Proposition 3.5
.

As
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-modules,

V(h_{L},h_{M},c_{L},c_{M})\cong\frac{U(\widehat{\mathfrak{b}})}{U(\widehat{\mathfrak{b}})\cdot(P_{0}-2h_{M},C_{M}-c_{M})}.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Although such “almost free” modules are not the most interesting from a representation theoretic point of view, this restriction seems to hint at some interesting physics. There exists a notion that the distinction between closed and open strings blur in the tensionless limit

\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
is irreducible if and only if
\mathbf{M}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{M}\neq 0
for any positive integer
n
.

Proof.

We would like to apply Theorem
2.15
. Hence, we first aim to relate
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
to a universal quasi-Whittaker module of
\mathfrak{bms}
as per Definition
2.12
. Consider the ideal
\mathfrak{a}\coloneqq\spn\{C_{M},C_{L},M_{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
of
\mathfrak{bms}
and define the Whittaker function
\phi\colon\mathfrak{a}\to\Bbbk
given by

\phi(M_{i})=\delta_{i,0}\mathbf{M},\quad\phi(C_{L})=c_{L},\quad\phi(C_{M})=c_{M}.

Letting
W_{\phi}\coloneqq\Ind_{\mathfrak{a}}^{\mathfrak{bms}}\Bbbk_{\phi}
be the associated universal quasi-Whittaker module, it is easy to see that

\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})\cong W_{\phi}/J_{\mathbf{s}},

where
J_{\mathbf{s}}\coloneqq U(\mathfrak{bms})(L_{0}-\mathbf{s})\cdot 1_{\phi}
.

Suppose first that
\mathbf{M}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{M}\neq 0
for any positive integer
n
. By setting
n=1
, we see that
\mathbf{M}\neq 0
. From Definition
2.14
, we may quickly deduce that the Whittaker annihilator of
\phi
is
\mathfrak{bms}^{\phi}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\Bbbk L_{0}
. Now, Theorem
2.15
implies that
J_{\mathbf{s}}
is a maximal
\mathfrak{bms}
-submodule of
W_{\phi}
. Therefore, the quotient
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})\cong W_{\phi}/J_{\mathbf{s}}
is irreducible.

Conversely, assume that
\mathbf{M}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{M}=0
for a positive integer
n
. It is easy to compute that the Whittaker annihilator of
\phi
is
\mathfrak{a}\oplus\Bbbk L_{0}\oplus\Bbbk L_{n}\oplus\Bbbk L_{-n}
. Therefore, we see that
L_{n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
generates a non-zero proper submodule of
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
. Thus
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
is reducible in this case.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

By comparing
[
JZ15
, Theorem 2.3]
with Theorem
3.8
, we see that the Verma modules of
\mathfrak{bms}
from Definition
3.4
are irreducible under the same conditions as the massive modules
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
. This is an unexpected, intriguing similarity between two very different classes of
\mathfrak{bms}
-modules that could potentially be explained by structural features of these modules that are yet to be discovered.

Using the findings from the proof of Theorem
3.8
and another result of
[
CGL+25
]
, it is not difficult to show that
L_{n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
and
L_{-n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
together generate a maximal submodule of the massive module
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
when
\mathbf{M}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{M}=\mathbf{s}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{L}=0
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M}\in\Bbbk
and suppose
\mathbf{M}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{M}=\mathbf{s}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{L}=0
for some positive integer
n
. Then the submodule of
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
generated by

L_{n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
and
L_{-n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
is a maximal submodule of
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon\mathfrak{a}\to\Bbbk
be as in the proof of Theorem
3.8
. Let

\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}\coloneqq\spn\{L_{0},L_{n},L_{-n},M_{k},C_{M},C_{L}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\Bbbk L_{0}\oplus\Bbbk L_{n}\oplus\Bbbk L_{-n},

and define
\Phi\colon\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}\to\Bbbk
by
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{a}}}=\varphi
, and

\Phi(L_{0})=\mathbf{s},\quad\Phi(L_{n})=\Phi(L_{-n})=0.

As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we have
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}^{\phi}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}
. The condition that
\mathbf{s}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{24}c_{L}=0
guarantees that
\Phi
is a well-defined Lie algebra homomorphism, since

\Phi([L_{n},L_{-n}])=\Phi\left(2nL_{0}+\frac{n^{3}-n}{12}C_{L}\right)=2n\mathbf{s}+\frac{n^{3}-n}{12}c_{L}=0.

Therefore,

extends the quasi-Whittaker function
\phi
to the entirety of its Whittaker annihilator. By
[
CGL+25
, Lemma 3.7]
, it follows that
\Ind_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}}^{\mathfrak{bms}}\Bbbk_{\Phi}
is an irreducible
\mathfrak{bms}
-module. It is easy to see that this module is isomorphic to the quotient of
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s},c_{L},c_{M})
by its submodule generated by
L_{n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
and
L_{-n}\ket{\mathbf{M},\mathbf{s}}
, yielding the result.
∎

Remark 3.11

We were informed by Blagoje Oblak that Theorem

We now consider the reducibility of the

Proposition 3.12
.

Let

3.4.
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Omega(\lambda,a)

We finish this section by restricting one final class of Virasoro modules to
\mathcal{O}
, first defined in
[
LZ14
]
. For any pair
(a,\lambda)\in\Bbbk\times\Bbbk^{*}
, one can define a Virasoro algebra module structure on
\Bbbk[X]
by

C\cdot f(X)=0,\quad L_{n}\cdot f(X)=\lambda^{n}(X+na)f(X+n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The module is irreducible if and only if
a\neq 0
, and
\Omega(\lambda,0)
has a unique irreducible submodule which is isomorphic to
\Omega(\lambda,1)

[
LZ10
, Section 4.3]
. Restricting the above action to the subalgebra
\mathcal{O}
of
\Vir
, the
\mathcal{O}
-module structure on
\Bbbk[X]
is given by

(3.2)

\mathcal{O}_{n}\cdot f(X)=\lambda^{n}(X+na)f(X+n)-\lambda^{-n}(X-na)f(X-n),\quad n\geq 1.

We still denote the corresponding
\mathcal{O}
-module by
\Omega(\lambda,a)
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Suppose
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
and
a\in\Bbbk
. If
\lambda\neq\pm 1
and
a\neq 0
, then the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is irreducible.

To prove Proposition
3.13
, we need the following lemma from
[
TZ13
]
.

Lemma 3.14

(
[
TZ13
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\Bbbk
,
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with

s_{1}+\dots+s_{m}=s
. Define a sequence of functions on
\mathbb{Z}
as follows:

f_{1}(n)=\lambda_{1}^{n},f_{2}(n)=n\lambda_{1}^{n},\dots,f_{s_{1}}(n)=n^{s_{1}-1}\lambda_{1}^{n},f_{s_{1}+1}(n)=\lambda_{2}^{n},\dots,f_{s_{1}+s_{2}}(n)=n^{s_{2}-1}\lambda_{2}^{n},\dots,f_{s}(n)=n^{s_{m}-1}\lambda_{m}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{M}=(y_{pq})
be the
s\times s
matrix with
y_{pq}=f_{q}(p-1)
,
q=1,2,\dots,s
,
p=u+1,u+2,\dots,u+s
where
u\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\det(\mathcal{M})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(s_{j}-1)!!\lambda_{j}^{s_{j}(s_{j}+2u-1)/2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})^{s_{i}s_{j}},

where
s_{j}!!=s_{j}!\times(s_{j}-1)!\times\dots\times 2!\times 1!
with
0!!=1
, for convenience.

In particular, we have the following.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
\mu\in\Bbbk
and
\mu\neq 0,1
.
For
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and a fixed
u\in\mathbb{N}
let
k_{j}=u+j,j=1,2,\dots,2r
. Letting

\tau(\mu,r,u)\coloneqq\begin{vmatrix}1&k_{1}&k_{1}^{2}&\cdots&k_{1}^{r-1}&\mu^{k_{1}}&\mu^{k_{1}}k_{1}&\mu^{k_{1}}k_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\mu^{k_{1}}k_{1}^{r-1}\\
1&k_{2}&k_{2}^{2}&\cdots&k_{2}^{r-1}&\mu^{k_{2}}&\mu^{k_{2}}k_{2}&\mu^{k_{2}}k_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\mu^{k_{2}}k_{2}^{r-1}\\
1&k_{3}&k_{3}^{2}&\cdots&k_{3}^{r-1}&\mu^{k_{3}}&\mu^{k_{3}}k_{3}&\mu^{k_{3}}k_{3}^{2}&\cdots&\mu^{k_{3}}k_{3}^{r-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&k_{2r}&k_{2r}^{2}&\cdots&k_{2r}^{r-1}&\mu^{k_{2r}}&\mu^{k_{2r}}k_{2r}&\mu^{k_{2r}}k_{2r}^{2}&\cdots&\mu^{k_{2r}}k_{2r}^{r-1}\\
\end{vmatrix},

then
\tau(\mu,r,u)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Taking
m=2
,
\lambda_{1}=1,\lambda_{2}=\mu
and
s_{1}=s_{2}=r
in Lemma
3.14
we see

\tau(\mu,r,u)=\det\big((y_{pq})_{2r\times 2r}\big)
with
y_{pq}=f_{q}(p-1)
and
q=1,2,\dots,2r,p=u+1,u+2,\dots,u+2r
. So

\tau(\mu,r,u)=\big((r-1)!!\big)^{2}\mu^{r(r+2u-1)/2}(\mu-1)^{r^{2}}\neq 0,

as desired. This completes the proof.
∎

As the following result shows, the key difference between the cases where
\lambda=\pm 1
or
a=0
and the other cases is whether
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is generated by
1
.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
\lambda\neq\pm 1
and
a\neq 0
, then the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is generated by
1
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that
X^{n}\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}.
We prove this by induction on
n
, the base case
n=0
being trivial. For the induction step, let
n>0
and assume
X^{n^{\prime}}\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1
for any
0\leq n^{\prime}<n
. We aim to show that
X^{n}\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1.
Since
X^{n-1}\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1
, it follows from (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle\lambda^{k}\mathcal{O}_{k}\cdot X^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2k}(X+ka)(X+k)^{n-1}-(X-ka)(X-k)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{2k}-1)X^{n}+g(X)\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1

for some
g(X)\in\Bbbk[X]
. Note that
g(X)\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1
by the induction hypothesis, since
\deg(g(X))\leq n-1
. Therefore,

(\lambda^{2k}-1)X^{n}=\lambda^{k}\mathcal{O}_{k}\cdot X^{n-1}-g(X)\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1.

Since
\lambda\neq\pm 1
, there always exists
k\geq 1
such that
(\lambda^{2k}-1)\neq 0
, which implies that
X^{n}\in U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1
. Thus,
U(\mathcal{O})\cdot 1=\Omega(\lambda,a)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we can prove Proposition
3.13
.

Proof of Proposition
3.13
.

To show that
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is simple is enough to show that any nonzero submodule of
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is the module
\Omega(\lambda,a)
itself. Let
M
be a nonzero submodule of
\Omega(\lambda,a)
. To show
M=\Omega(\lambda,a)
is, by Lemma
3.16
, to show
1\in M
. To the contrary, assume that
1\notin M
. Let
f(X)\in M
be a nonzero element with minimal degree
r>0
. Denote

f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}c_{i}X^{i}
with
c_{i}\in\Bbbk
and
c_{r}\neq 0
. Then
by (
3.2
) we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lambda^{k}\mathcal{O}_{k}\cdot f(X)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2k}(X+ka)f(X+k)-(X-ka)f(X-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r+1}\varphi_{i}(X)\lambda^{2k}k^{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{r+1}\psi_{i}(X)k^{i}\in M

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
\varphi_{i}(X),\psi_{i}(X)\in\Bbbk[X]
and
\varphi_{r+1}(X)=ac_{r}
and
\psi_{r+1}(X)=(-1)^{r}ac_{r}
are non-zero constants. Let
\mu=\lambda^{2}
,
k_{j}=j
, where
1\leq j\leq 2(r+2)
and
\mu\neq 0,1
. By Lemma
3.15
we know that

\tau(\mu,r+2,0)\neq 0
, which implies that

\varphi_{i}(X),\psi_{i}(X)\in M
for all
0\leq i\leq r+1
. In particular,
\varphi_{r+1}(X),\psi_{r+1}(X)\in M
, contrary to the choice of
f(X)
. Therefore,
1\in M
. So
M=\Omega(\lambda,a)
and
\Omega(\lambda,a)
is a simple
\mathcal{O}
-module. This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we consider the case
\lambda=\pm 1.

Proposition 3.17
.

If
\lambda=\pm 1
and
a\neq 0
, then the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Omega(\lambda,a)
carries a filtration of submodules of
\Omega(\lambda,a)
as follows:

\Omega(\lambda,a)_{n}\subseteq\Omega(\lambda,a)_{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where

\Omega(\lambda,a)_{n}\coloneqq\{f(X)\in\Omega(\lambda,a)\mid\deg f(X)\leq n\}=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}X^{i}\mid c_{i}\in\Bbbk\right\}.

Proof.

We only need to show that each
\Omega(\lambda,a)_{n}
is a submodule of
\Omega(\lambda,a)
, which follows from the fact that for any
k\geq 1
and any non-zero
f(X)\in\Bbbk[X]
,

\mathcal{O}_{k}\cdot f(X)=\lambda^{k}\Big(X(f(X+k)-f(X-k))+ka(f(X+k)+f(X-k))\Big)

has degree
\leq\deg(f(X))
for
\deg(f(X+k)-f(X-k))=\deg(f(X))-1
if
\deg(f(X))\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

Since
\Omega(\lambda,0)
as a
\Vir
-module has a submodule
X\Bbbk[X]
, which is isomorphic to
\Omega(\lambda,1)
, we see that

\Omega(\lambda,0)
as a
\mathcal{O}
-module also has a submodule
X\Bbbk[X]\cong\Omega(\lambda,1)
. If
\lambda\neq\pm 1
, then, by Proposition
3.13
,
X\Bbbk[X]
is a simple
\mathcal{O}
-module; and if
\lambda=\pm 1
, then, by Proposition
3.17
,
\Omega(\lambda,0)
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module has a filtration of submodules:

X\Omega(\lambda,0)_{0}\subseteq X\Omega(\lambda,0)_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq X\Omega(\lambda,0)_{n}\subseteq\dots\subseteq X\Omega(\lambda,0)\subseteq\Omega(\lambda,0).

4.
Alternate basis of the BCCA

Our next goal is to construct a decreasing filtration on
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, which will allow us to define and study Whittaker modules for these Lie algebras as per Lemma
2.11
. Unfortunately, the basis of
\mathfrak{b}
from Section
2
makes it difficult to construct the desired filtration. Therefore, in this section we present an alternate basis for
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, from which the decreasing filtration becomes manifest.

4.1.
Subalgebras of the Witt algebra

Since
\mathfrak{b}=\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{O}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
, we now spend some time outlining some general facts about subalgebras of the Witt algebra. Note that
\mathcal{W}
is naturally a
\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
-module, and thus the most natural subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
are those which are also
\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
-submodules.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
f\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, define

f\mathcal{W}\coloneqq\{hf\partial\mid h\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]\},

so that
f\mathcal{W}
consists of all derivations of
\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
which are divisible by
f
. Given
f\in\Bbbk[t]
, we define
f\mathbb{W}_{1}
in a similar way. The Lie subalgebras
f\mathcal{W}
and
f\mathbb{W}_{1}
are known as
submodule-subalgebras
of
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathbb{W}_{1}
.

Note that the bracket in
f\mathcal{W}
is given by

(4.1)

[gf\partial,hf\partial]=(gh^{\prime}-g^{\prime}h)f^{2}\partial

for all
g,h\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
. The same formula holds in
f\mathbb{W}_{1}
, except we require that
f,g,h\in\Bbbk[t]
.

Example 4.2
.

Letting
f\coloneqq t^{2}-4
, we define
\mathfrak{f}\coloneqq f\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Then
\mathfrak{f}
is spanned by

f_{n}\coloneqq-t^{n-1}f\partial=-(t^{n+1}-4t^{n-1})\partial=L_{n}-4L_{n-2},

where
n\geq 1
. The bracket in
\mathfrak{f}
is

\displaystyle[f_{n},f_{m}]

\displaystyle=[L_{n}-4L_{n-2},L_{m}-4L_{m-2}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)(L_{n+m}-8L_{n+m-2}+16L_{n+m-4})

\displaystyle=(n-m)(f_{n+m}-4f_{n+m-2}).

In fact, all subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension are “essentially” submodule-subalgebras, in a sense that is made precise in the next result.

Proposition 4.3

(
[
PS23
, Proposition 3.2.7]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
of finite codimension. Then there exist
f\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f^{n}\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\subseteq f\mathcal{W}.

The analogous result for finite codimension subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
is also true with a nearly identical proof, although this is not explicitly proved in
[
PS23
]
.

However, the Lie algebra
\mathcal{O}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
of infinite codimension, so Proposition
4.3
does not apply. Thankfully, there is an analogous classification of subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of infinite codimension
[
BB25
, Theorem 2.8]
. Although the result classifies subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, not of
\mathcal{W}
, the same ideas and techniques can be applied to study subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
. To state the result, we must first introduce the subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
which play the role of the submodule-subalgebras in the infinite codimension setting. Following
[
Buz23
,
BB25
]
, we introduce the notions of the
set
and
field of ratios
associated to a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
. The
set of ratios
of
\mathfrak{g}
is

R(\mathfrak{g})=\left.\left\{\frac{g}{h}\in\Bbbk(t)\right|g\partial,h\partial\in\mathfrak{g},h\neq 0\right\}.

The
field of ratios

F(\mathfrak{g})
of
\mathfrak{g}
is the subfield of
\Bbbk(t)
generated by
R(\mathfrak{g})
.

The next result gives one of the most important reasons why
F(\mathfrak{g})
is a useful tool to study
\mathfrak{g}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\Der(F(\mathfrak{g}))
.

Proof.

Let
u\partial,v\partial,w\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
with
v\neq 0
, where
u,v,w\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, so that
\frac{u}{v},\frac{w}{v}\in R(\mathfrak{g})
. We claim that
w\partial(\frac{u}{v})\in F(\mathfrak{g})
, which means that elements of
\mathfrak{g}
map
R(\mathfrak{g})
to
F(\mathfrak{g})
. Since
F(\mathfrak{g})
is generated by
R(\mathfrak{g})
as a field, it will then follow that all derivations in
\mathfrak{g}
preserve
F(\mathfrak{g})
.

We have

w\partial\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)=w\cdot\frac{v\partial(u)-u\partial(v)}{v^{2}}=\frac{w}{v}\cdot\frac{v\partial(u)-u\partial(v)}{v}.

Certainly,
\frac{w}{v}\in R(\mathfrak{g})
by definition. Note that
[v\partial,u\partial]=(v\partial(u)-u\partial(v))\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
, so we also have
\frac{v\partial(u)-u\partial(v)}{v}\in R(\mathfrak{g})
. It follows that
w\partial(\frac{u}{v})\in F(\mathfrak{g})
, as claimed.
∎

As the next example illustrates, it is often useful to consider
\mathfrak{g}
as a subalgebra of
\Der(F(\mathfrak{g}))
rather than as a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
, at least when
\mathfrak{g}
has infinite codimension in
\mathcal{W}
.

Example 4.6
.

Consider the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\spn\{L_{2n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{g}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
of infinite codimension and that
F(\mathfrak{g})=R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(t^{2})
, since
\mathfrak{g}=\Bbbk[t^{2},t^{-2}]t\partial
. Thus, we see that
\mathfrak{g}
is the subalgebra
\Der(\Bbbk[t^{2},t^{-2}])
of
\Der(\Bbbk(t^{2}))
. Therefore, when we view
\mathfrak{g}
as a subalgebra of
\Der(F(\mathfrak{g}))
, we forget about the basis elements
L_{2n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which are not relevant when studying Lie algebraic properties of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Since
F(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\Bbbk(t)
, Lüroth’s theorem implies that
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(s)
for some
s\in\Bbbk(t)
, so Lemma
4.5
implies that
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\Der(\Bbbk(s))
. In
[
Buz23
, Theorem 5.3]
, it was shown that
s
can be chosen to be a polynomial when
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathbb{W}_{1}
. In fact, even more is true:
\mathfrak{g}
is contained in
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
with finite codimension.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
BB25
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Then there exists
s\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(s)
, and
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
. In particular, there exists
g(s)\in\Bbbk[s]
such that

g(s)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s},

where
\partial_{s}\in\Der(\Bbbk[s])
is the unique derivation of
\Bbbk[s]
such that
\partial_{s}(s)=1
, so that
\Der(\Bbbk[s])=\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathbb{W}_{1}
,
s\in\Bbbk[t]
, and
\partial_{s}
be as in Theorem
4.7
. Certainly, the Lie algebra
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, so Theorem
4.7
says that
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension. The derivation
\partial_{s}\in\Der(\Bbbk[s])
can be uniquely extended to a derivation of
\Bbbk(t)
: we have
\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\partial
, allowing us to embed
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
into
\Der(\Bbbk(t))
as follows:

\Der(\Bbbk[s])=\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\Bbbk[s]\partial\subseteq\Bbbk(t)\partial=\Der(\Bbbk(t)).

Of course,
\mathbb{W}_{1}=\Bbbk[t]\partial
is also contained in
\Der(\Bbbk(t))
. Therefore, Theorem
4.7
implies that
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathbb{W}_{1}\cap\Der(\Bbbk[s])
, where the intersection is taken in
\Der(\Bbbk(t))
.

Although Theorem
4.7
only considers subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, the same ideas can be applied to subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
. To that end, we introduce the following definition.

Notation 4.8
.

If
s\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, we define
L(s)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\cap\Der(\Bbbk[s])
.

The Lie algebra
L(s)
can be described as follows.

Lemma 4.9

(
[
BB25
, Lemma 2.7]
)
.

Let
s\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, and let
g_{s}\in\Bbbk[t]
be the unique monic polynomial of minimal degree such that
s^{\prime}g_{s}\in\Bbbk[s]
. Letting
f_{s}\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
s^{\prime}g_{s}=f_{s}(s)
, we have
L(s)=\Bbbk[s]g_{s}\partial
.

Remark 4.10
.

Although the condition
s^{\prime}g_{s}\in\Bbbk[s]
might seem unusual, it is precisely the necessary condition for
g_{s}\partial
to be a derivation of
\Bbbk[s]
.

We can also describe
L(s)
as a subalgebra of
\Der(\Bbbk[s])=\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
(in other words, viewing it as a Lie algebra of derivations of
\Bbbk[s]
instead of
\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
). Since
\partial=s^{\prime}\partial_{s}
, it follows that

L(s)=\Bbbk[s]g_{s}\partial=\Bbbk[s]s^{\prime}g_{s}\partial_{s}=\Bbbk[s]f_{s}(s)\partial_{s}.

Therefore,
L(s)
is the subalgebra
\Bbbk[s]f_{s}(s)\partial_{s}
of
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
. By changing variables
s\mapsto t
, we see that
L(s)\cong\Bbbk[t]f_{s}\partial=f_{s}\mathbb{W}_{1}
, so
L(s)
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension. We now give an example to illustrate the construction.

Example 4.11

(
[
Buz23
, Example 4.16]
)
.

If we let
s=t^{3}+3t
then
g_{s}=(t^{2}+1)(t^{2}+4)
and
f_{s}=3(t^{2}+4)
, since
s^{\prime}g_{s}=3(s^{2}+4)\in\Bbbk[s]
. Therefore,
L(s)=\Bbbk[t^{3}+3t](t^{2}+1)(t^{2}+4)\partial
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
. As a subalgebra of
\Der(\Bbbk[s])
, we have
L(s)=\Bbbk[s](s^{2}+4)\partial_{s}
. Changing variables
s\mapsto t
, we see that

L(s)\cong\Bbbk[t](t^{2}+4)\partial=(t^{2}+4)\mathbb{W}_{1},

so
L(s)
is isomorphic to the subalgebra
(t^{2}+4)\mathbb{W}_{1}
of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of codimension 2.

We summarise this discussion below.

Proposition 4.12

(
[
Buz23
, Lemma 4.12, Proposition 4.13]
)
.

Let
s\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, and let
f_{s},g_{s}\in\Bbbk[t]
be as in Lemma
4.9
. Then

L(s)=\Bbbk[s]g_{s}\partial=\Bbbk[s]f_{s}(s)\partial_{s}\cong f_{s}\mathbb{W}_{1},

where the isomorphism comes from the change of variables
s\mapsto t
.

4.2.
Change of basis for
\mathcal{O}

We now apply the ideas of Subsection
4.1
to the Lie subalgebra
\mathcal{O}
of
\mathcal{W}
to construct a new basis of
\mathcal{O}
which will allow us to define a descending filtration for the BCCA. In fact, we work in a slightly more general setting:
\mathcal{O}
is part of a family of Lie subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
. It is not much more difficult to consider the entire family for the proofs in this section, so we work in this level of generality.

Definition 4.13
.

For
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n}\coloneqq L_{n}-\lambda^{n}L_{-n}
. Notice that
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{0}=0
and
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{-n}=-\lambda^{-n}\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
. Define
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\coloneqq\spn\{\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
.

Of course
\mathcal{O}(1)=\mathcal{O}
. On the other hand, it is not immediately clear that
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is closed under the Lie bracket of
\mathcal{W}
for
\lambda\neq 1
. We prove this next.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Then
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is a Lie algebra with the following Lie bracket:

[\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n},\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{m}]=(n-m)\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n+m}-\lambda^{m}(n+m)\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n-m}

for all
n,m\geq 1
. In particular,
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is generated by
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}
.

Proof.

We compute the bracket
[\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n},\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{m}]
as follows:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n},\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{m}]

\displaystyle=[L_{n}-\lambda^{n}L_{-n},L_{m}-\lambda^{m}L_{-m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)L_{n+m}-\lambda^{m}(n+m)L_{n-m}+\lambda^{n}(n+m)L_{m-n}-\lambda^{n+m}(n-m)L_{-(n+m)}

\displaystyle=(n-m)\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n+m}-\lambda^{m}(n+m)\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{n-m}.

The final sentence follows by an easy induction.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

One might ask if we can get different subalgebras of the Witt algebra by combining
L_{n}
and
L_{-n}
differently to the way it is done in
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
. In other words, can we generate different Lie subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
with elements
L_{1}-\lambda L_{-1}
and
L_{2}-\mu L_{-2}
, where
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{*}
? This question is answered in Appendix
A
: if
\mu\neq\lambda^{2}
, then these two elements generate the entire Witt algebra. As a result, the subalgebras
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
are the unique family of proper subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}
generated by elements of the form
L_{1}-\lambda L_{-1}
and
L_{2}-\mu L_{-2}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{*}
.

We now aim to describe an alternative basis
u_{n}^{(\lambda)}
for
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
. To achieve this, we analyse the structure of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
following the methods of Subsection
4.1
. In terms of polynomials, we have
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}=-(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial
, and

\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}=-(t^{3}-\lambda^{2}t^{-1})\partial=-t^{-1}(t^{4}-\lambda^{2})\partial=-t^{-1}(t^{2}-\lambda)(t^{2}+\lambda)\partial.

Dividing
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}
by
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}
, it follows that

\frac{t^{-1}(t^{2}-\lambda)(t^{2}+\lambda)}{t^{2}-\lambda}=t+\lambda t^{-1}\in R(\mathcal{O}(\lambda)).

We therefore define
s_{\lambda}\coloneqq t+\lambda t^{-1}
, so that
\Bbbk(s_{\lambda})\subseteq F(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))
. In fact, as we show next, we have
R(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))=F(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))=\Bbbk(s_{\lambda})
and
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)=L(s_{\lambda})
, where this notation is defined in Definition
4.4
and Notation
4.8
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
, and define
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}\coloneqq f_{s_{\lambda}}\mathbb{W}_{1}
, where
f_{s_{\lambda}}=t^{2}-4\lambda
. Then

\mathcal{O}(\lambda)=L(s_{\lambda})=\Bbbk[t+\lambda t^{-1}](t^{2}-\lambda)\partial=\Bbbk[s_{\lambda}](s_{\lambda}^{2}-4\lambda)\partial_{s_{\lambda}}\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda},

where the isomorphism comes from the change of variables
s_{\lambda}\mapsto t
. Consequently,
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
has another basis
\{u^{(\lambda)}_{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
defined as follows: for
n\geq 1
, let

u^{(\lambda)}_{n}\coloneqq-s_{\lambda}^{n-1}(s_{\lambda}^{2}-4\lambda)\partial_{s_{\lambda}}=-(t+\lambda t^{-1})^{n-1}(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial.

The Lie bracket of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
with this basis is given by

[u^{(\lambda)}_{n},u^{(\lambda)}_{m}]=(n-m)(u^{(\lambda)}_{n+m}-4\lambda u^{(\lambda)}_{n+m-2})

for all
n,m\geq 1
.

Proof.

We adopt the notation
g_{s_{\lambda}}
and
f_{s_{\lambda}}
from Lemma
4.9
. It is not difficult to see that
g_{s_{\lambda}}=t^{2}-\lambda
, since

s_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t^{2}-\lambda)=(1-\lambda t^{-2})(t^{2}-\lambda)=t^{2}-2\lambda+\lambda^{2}t^{-2}=(t+\lambda t^{-1})^{2}-4\lambda=s_{\lambda}^{2}-4\lambda\in\Bbbk[s_{\lambda}].

Consequently,
f_{s_{\lambda}}=t^{2}-4\lambda
. It follows from Proposition
4.12
that

L(s_{\lambda})=\Bbbk[s_{\lambda}](s_{\lambda}^{2}-4\lambda)\partial_{s_{\lambda}}=\Bbbk[s_{\lambda}]g_{s_{\lambda}}\partial=\Bbbk[t+\lambda t^{-1}](t^{2}-\lambda)\partial.

Note that
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}=-(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial\in L(s_{\lambda})
and

\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}=-(t^{3}-\lambda^{2}t^{-1})\partial=-(t+\lambda t^{-1})(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial\in L(s_{\lambda}).

Since
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}
generate
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
by Lemma
4.14
, this implies that
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\subseteq L(s_{\lambda})
.

For the other inclusion
L(s_{\lambda})\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
, we will show that
L(s_{\lambda})
is also generated by
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}
. To that end, noting that
\{u^{(\lambda)}_{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
is a basis for
L(s_{\lambda})
, we compute the bracket
[u^{(\lambda)}_{n},u^{(\lambda)}_{m}]
. Letting
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}\coloneqq f_{s_{\lambda}}\mathbb{W}_{1}=(t^{2}-4\lambda)\mathbb{W}_{1}
, the change of variables
s_{\lambda}\mapsto t
yields an isomorphism
L(s_{\lambda})\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}
, as in Proposition
4.12
. Noting that

u^{(\lambda)}_{n}=-(t+\lambda t^{-1})^{n-1}(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial=-s_{\lambda}^{n-1}(s_{\lambda}^{2}-4\lambda)\partial_{s_{\lambda}},

we see that

u^{(\lambda)}_{n}\mapsto f^{(\lambda)}_{n}\coloneqq-t^{n-1}f_{\lambda}\partial=L_{n}-4\lambda L_{n-2}

under the change of variables
s_{\lambda}\mapsto t
. We have

\displaystyle[f^{(\lambda)}_{n},f^{(\lambda)}_{m}]

\displaystyle=[L_{n}-4\lambda L_{n-2},L_{m}-4\lambda L_{m-2}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)(L_{n+m}-8\lambda L_{n+m-2}+16\lambda^{2}L_{n+m-4})

\displaystyle=(n-m)(f^{(\lambda)}_{n+m}-4\lambda f^{(\lambda)}_{n+m-2}),

and therefore,

[u^{(\lambda)}_{n},u^{(\lambda)}_{m}]=(n-m)(u^{(\lambda)}_{n+m}-4\lambda u^{(\lambda)}_{n+m-2}).

An easy induction shows that
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{1}=u^{(\lambda)}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}^{(\lambda)}_{2}=u^{(\lambda)}_{2}
generate
L(s_{\lambda})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.17
.

Theorem
4.16
implies that
R(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))=F(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))=\Bbbk(s_{\lambda})
, although a priori it is not obvious that
R(\mathcal{O}(\lambda))
is a field.

Example 4.18
.

The Lie algebra
\mathcal{O}(-1)
is spanned by
L_{n}+(-1)^{n+1}L_{-n}
and is isomorphic to
(t^{2}+4)\mathbb{W}_{1}
.

Using Theorem
4.16
, it is now easy to see that
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is never simple.

Corollary 4.19
.

For all
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
, the Lie algebra
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
is not perfect, and therefore is not simple.

Proof.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}
is not perfect: this is because the derived subalgebra of
f^{(\lambda)}
is

[\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda},\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}]=[f_{s_{\lambda}}\mathbb{W}_{1},f_{s_{\lambda}}\mathbb{W}_{1}]=f_{s_{\lambda}}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{1},

by (
4.1
). The result now follows by the isomorphism
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}
from Theorem
4.16
.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

Corollary
4.19
implies that
\mathfrak{b}
is not perfect, meaning it does not have a universal central extension
[
Wei94
, Theorem 7.9.2]
.
Nonetheless, it would be interesting to compute the
centrally closed
version of
\mathfrak{b}
, which would be the central extension of
\mathfrak{b}
with trivial second cohomology group (i.e., does not admit any more non-trivial central extensions).
From a physics perspective, this would tell us the possible “central charges” that a quantum field theory with
\mathfrak{b}
symmetry could admit.
It remains to be seen whether this centrally closed Lie algebra is indeed
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
.

As we aim to show next, the Lie algebras
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
are often isomorphic to each other for different values of
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Recalling that
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}=(t^{2}-4\lambda)\mathbb{W}_{1}
, it suffices to consider when two subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension are isomorphic. In fact, this can only happen if the isomorphism is induced by an automorphism of
\mathbb{W}_{1}

[
Buz24
]
. The automorphisms of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
are well-known
[
Rud86
]
.

Theorem 4.21

(
[
Buz24
, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.12]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
be subalgebras of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension and suppose there is an isomorphism
\varphi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
. Then
\varphi
extends to an automorphism of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. In particular,
g\mathbb{W}_{1}\cong h\mathbb{W}_{1}
if and only if there exist
\alpha,\gamma\in\Bbbk^{*}
and
\beta\in\Bbbk
such that
g(z)=\gamma h(t)
, where
z=\alpha t+\beta
and
g,h\in\Bbbk[t]
.

We are now ready to characterise when
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathcal{O}(\mu)
.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Then
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathcal{O}(\mu)
if and only if
\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\mu}}\in\Bbbk
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.16
, we have
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}=(t^{2}-4\lambda)\mathbb{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}(\mu)\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}=(t^{2}-4\mu)\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Therefore, we will prove the result by showing that
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}
if and only if
\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\lambda}}\in\Bbbk
.

First, assume that
\alpha\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\mu}}\in\Bbbk
. Letting
z\coloneqq\alpha t
, we have

f_{s_{\lambda}}(z)=z^{2}-4\lambda=\alpha^{2}t^{2}-4\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t^{2}-4\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}(t^{2}-4\mu)=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}f_{s_{\mu}}(t).

By Theorem
4.21
, the change of variables
t\mapsto\alpha t
induces an isomorphism
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}
.

Conversely, assume that
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}\cong\mathfrak{f}_{\mu}
. By Theorem
4.21
, there exist
\alpha,\gamma\in\Bbbk^{*}
and
\beta\in\Bbbk
such that
f_{s_{\lambda}}(z)=\gamma f_{s_{\mu}}(t)
, where
z=\alpha t+\beta
. Therefore,

\displaystyle t^{2}-4\mu

\displaystyle=f_{s_{\mu}}(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}f_{s_{\lambda}}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma}(z^{2}-4\lambda)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big((\alpha t+\beta)^{2}-4\lambda\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}(\alpha^{2}t^{2}+2\alpha\beta t+\beta^{2}-4\lambda).

It follows that
\gamma=\alpha^{2}
,
\beta=0
, and
\lambda=\gamma\mu=\alpha^{2}\mu
. Therefore,
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\mu}}
must be an element of
\Bbbk
.
∎

With
\mathcal{O}(1)=\mathcal{O}
, the next result follows immediately from Proposition
4.22
.

Corollary 4.23
.

If
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed (for example, if
\Bbbk=\mathbb{C}
), then
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\mathcal{O}
for all
\lambda\in\Bbbk
. If
\Bbbk=\mathbb{R}
, then

\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{O},&\text{if }\lambda>0,\\
\mathcal{O}(-1),&\text{if }\lambda<0,\end{cases}

but
\mathcal{O}(-1)\not\cong\mathcal{O}
. ∎

Remark 4.24
.

Corollary
4.23
is intriguing in the context in which the BCCA was discovered
[
BCC+25
]
. Specifically, we have shown that the subalgebras
\mathcal{O}=\spn\{L_{n}-L_{-n}\mid n\geq 1\}
and
\mathcal{O}(-1)=\spn\{L_{n}+(-1)^{n+1}L_{-n}\}
are isomorphic as complex Lie algebras but not as real ones. In the realisation of the BMS
3
algebra given by
[
BCC+25
, Equation (4)]
in terms of vector fields which preserve Carrollian structure on cylinder (see
[
CSZ24
, Section 2.1]
for more details), we have

L_{n}=e^{in\sigma}(\partial_{\sigma}+in\tau\partial_{\tau}),

where
\sigma
and
\tau
are the coordinates of a cylinder. The Lie algebra
\mathcal{O}
was the constructed as the subalgebra of the Witt algebra spanned by
\{L_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
which leaves the choice of boundary conditions
\sigma=0,\pi
invariant
[
BCC+25
, Equation 5]

\mathcal{O}_{n}=L_{n}-L_{-n}=2i\sin(n\sigma)\partial_{\sigma}+2in\tau\cos(n\sigma)\partial_{\tau}.

At
\sigma=0,\pi
,
\mathcal{O}_{n}=\pm 2in\tau\partial_{\tau}
do not contain any non-zero
\partial_{\sigma}
terms and thus satisfy the boundary conditions.

In terms of vector fields on the cylinder, the algebra
\mathcal{O}(-1)
preserves a different choice of boundaries, in this case at
\sigma=\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}
. This can be seen as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2n}^{(-1)}

\displaystyle=L_{2n}-L_{-2n}=2i\sin(2n\sigma)\partial_{\sigma}+4in\tau\cos(2n\sigma)\partial_{\tau},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2n+1}^{(-1)}

\displaystyle=L_{2n+1}+L_{-2n-1}=2\cos((2n+1)\sigma)\partial_{\sigma}-(4n+2)\tau\sin((2n+1)\sigma)\partial_{\tau}.

Therefore, at
\sigma=\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}
we have
\mathcal{O}_{2n}^{(-1)}=\pm 4in\tau\partial_{\tau}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2n+1}^{(-1)}=\pm(4n+2)\tau\partial_{\tau}
, which satisfy the boundary conditions.

When working over
\mathbb{C}
, this can be undone by a simple change of coordinates to
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma-\frac{\pi}{2}
, with the resulting boundary conditions in terms of
\sigma^{\prime}
being equivalent to the original one. Consequently, one would expect to have recovered
\mathcal{O}
. Indeed, this change of coordinates is just a rescaling of
\mathcal{O}
by
i
, which reiterates the fact that the authors of the original paper were working over
\mathbb{C}
. However, Corollary
4.23
implies that the boundary conditions at
\sigma=\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}
result in a non-isomorphic Lie algebra when working over
\mathbb{R}
.

We now focus on the case of
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Notation 4.25
.

We make the following definitions:

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\coloneqq s_{1}=t+t^{-1},

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq u_{n}^{(1)}=-(t+t^{-1})^{n-1}(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial=-s^{n-1}(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\coloneqq f_{s_{1}}=t^{2}-4,

\displaystyle f_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f_{n}^{(1)}=-t^{n-1}f\partial=L_{n}-4L_{n-2},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathfrak{f}_{1}=f\mathbb{W}_{1}=(t^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}.

Of course,
\{\mathcal{O}_{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
and
\{u_{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
are two bases for the same Lie algebra
\mathcal{O}
, so it is natural to ask how they are related. The following result answers this.

Lemma 4.26
.

We have

u_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(\binom{n-1}{k}-\binom{n-1}{k-1}\right)\mathcal{O}_{n-2k}

for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

First, note that
u_{1}=-(t^{2}-1)\partial=L_{1}-L_{-1}=\mathcal{O}_{1}
. Furthermore,

(4.2)

\displaystyle-(t^{n}+t^{-n})(t^{2}-1)\partial

\displaystyle=-\Big(t^{n+2}-t^{n}+t^{-n+2}-t^{-n}\Big)\partial

\displaystyle=L_{n+1}-L_{n-1}+L_{-n+1}-L_{-n-1}

\displaystyle=(L_{n+1}-L_{-(n+1)})-(L_{n-1}-L_{-(n-1)})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{n+1}-\mathcal{O}_{n-1}.

We now compute for
n\geq 2
:

u_{n}=-(t+t^{-1})^{n-1}(t^{2}-1)\partial=-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}t^{n-2k-1}\right)(t^{2}-1)\partial.

The result now follows by pairing up
t^{m}
with
t^{-m}
and using (
4.2
).
∎

4.3.
Change of basis for
\mathcal{P}_{b}

In Theorem
4.16
, it was shown that the change of variables
s\mapsto t
yields the isomorphism
\mathcal{O}\cong\mathfrak{f}=\Bbbk[t](t^{2}-4)\partial
. Therefore, it is natural to ask if this change of variables gives a similar description of
\mathcal{P}
. In this section, we show that this is indeed the case: the change of variables will allow us to define a new basis for
\mathcal{P}
. We will work in a slightly more general setting: we consider the change of variables on the entire family of
\mathcal{O}
-modules
\mathcal{P}_{b}
for arbitrary
b\in\Bbbk
(see Definition
2.6
). Just like for
\mathcal{O}
, it will be useful to have a generating set for the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\mathcal{P}_{b}
.

Lemma 4.27
.

For all
b\in\Bbbk^{*}
, the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is generated by
P^{(b)}_{0}
. For
b=0
, the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is generated by
P_{0}^{(0)}
and
P_{1}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

If
b\neq 0
, this follows immediately (
2.4
), since

\mathcal{O}_{n}\cdot P^{(b)}_{0}=-2bnP^{(b)}_{n}

for all
n\geq 1
. If
b=0
, then (
2.4
) gives

\mathcal{O}_{1}\cdot P_{n}^{(0)}=n(P_{n+1}^{(0)}-P_{n-1}^{(0)})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The result now follows by an easy induction.
∎

To describe how the change of variables
s\mapsto t
affects
\mathcal{P}_{b}
, we first introduce some notation. As above, we make the identification
\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\partial
, so that
\partial_{s}(s)=1
. Letting
r\coloneqq t-t^{-1}
, we have

(4.3)

\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\partial=\frac{1}{1-t^{-2}}\partial=\frac{t}{t-t^{-1}}\partial=\frac{t}{r}\partial.

Note that

(4.4)

r^{2}=(t-t^{-1})^{2}=t^{2}-2+t^{-2}=(t+t^{-1})^{2}-4=s^{2}-4.

In other words,
r
is a square root of
s^{2}-4
.

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is the
\mathcal{O}
-submodule of
I(0,b)
spanned by
P^{(b)}_{n}=I_{n}+I_{-n}
. As mentioned in Definition
2.2
, we view
I(0,b)
as
I(0,b)=t^{-b}\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]dt^{b}
with
I_{n}=t^{n-b}\ dt^{b}
. With this perspective, we have

(4.5)

P^{(b)}_{n}=I_{n}+I_{-n}=(t^{n}+t^{-n})t^{-b}\ dt^{b}.

Now, we want to express
\mathcal{P}_{b}
in terms of polynomials in
s
, just like we did with
\mathcal{O}
. By definition of the Kähler differential, we have
ds=s^{\prime}\ dt
. It follows that
ds^{b}=(s^{\prime}\ dt)^{b}=(s^{\prime})^{b}\ dt^{b}
, and therefore

(4.6)

dt^{b}=(s^{\prime})^{-b}\ ds^{b}=(1-t^{-2})^{-b}\ ds^{b}=\frac{t^{b}}{r^{b}}\ ds^{b},

so
t^{-b}\ dt^{b}=r^{-b}\ ds^{b}
. The next result shows what happens to the generators of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
when written in terms of
ds^{b}
.

Lemma 4.28
.

For
b\in\Bbbk
, we have
P^{(b)}_{0}=-2r^{-b}\ ds^{b}
and
P^{(b)}_{1}=-sr^{-b}\ ds^{b}
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) upon recalling that
s=t+t^{-1}
.
∎

Inspired by Lemma
4.28
, we now introduce the following notation.

Notation 4.29
.

For
b\in\Bbbk
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
v_{n}^{(b)}\coloneqq-s^{n}r^{-b}\ ds^{b}=-(t+t^{-1})^{n}t^{-b}\ dt^{b}\in I(0,b)
.

By Lemma
4.28
, we have
v_{0}^{(b)}=\frac{1}{2}P^{(b)}_{0}
and
v_{1}^{(b)}=P^{(b)}_{1}
. In fact, as we show next,
\{v_{n}^{(b)}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is another basis for
\mathcal{P}_{b}
.

Theorem 4.30
.

For all
b\in\Bbbk
, we have

\mathcal{P}_{b}=\spn\{v_{n}^{(b)}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}=\Bbbk[s]r^{-b}\ ds^{b}=\Bbbk[t+t^{-1}]t^{-b}\ dt^{b}.

The
\mathcal{O}
-action on
\mathcal{P}_{b}
with this basis is given by

u_{n}\cdot v_{m}^{(b)}=-(bn+m)v_{n+m}^{(b)}+4(b(n-1)+m)v_{n+m-2}^{(b)}

for all
n\geq 1
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In basis-free notation, the
\mathcal{O}
-action is

(4.7)

(s^{2}-4)g\partial_{s}\cdot(hr^{-b}\ ds^{b})=\Big((g\partial_{s}(h)+b\partial_{s}(g)h)(s^{2}-4)+bghs\Big)r^{-b}\ ds^{b}

for all
g,h\in\Bbbk[s]
.

Proof.

We start by computing
u_{n}\cdot v_{m}^{(b)}
. To that end, note that

\partial_{s}(r)=\frac{r^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}}=\frac{(t-t^{-1})^{\prime}}{(t+t^{-1})^{\prime}}=\frac{1+t^{-2}}{1-t^{-2}}=\frac{t+t^{-1}}{t-t^{-1}}=\frac{s}{r},

and thus

\partial_{s}(r^{-b})=-br^{-b-1}\partial_{s}(r)=\frac{-bs}{r^{b+2}}=\frac{-bsr^{-b}}{s^{2}-4},

where we used that
r^{2}=s^{2}-4
from (
4.4
) in the last equality. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle(s^{2}-4)g\partial_{s}

\displaystyle\cdot(hr^{-b}\ ds^{b})=\Big((s^{2}-4)g\partial_{s}(hr^{-b})+b\partial_{s}((s^{2}-4)g)hr^{-b}\Big)ds^{b}

\displaystyle=\Big((s^{2}-4)g\big(\partial_{s}(h)r^{-b}+h\partial_{s}(r^{-b})\big)+b\big(\partial_{s}(s^{2}-4)g+(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s}(g)\big)hr^{-b}\Big)ds^{b}

\displaystyle=\left((s^{2}-4)g\left(\partial_{s}(h)r^{-b}-\frac{bhsr^{-b}}{s^{2}-4}\right)+b\big(2sg+(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s}(g)\big)hr^{-b}\right)ds^{b}

\displaystyle=\Big(\big(g\partial_{s}(h)+b\partial_{s}(g)h\big)(s^{2}-4)+bghs\Big)r^{-b}\ ds^{b},

as required. It follows that

\displaystyle u_{n}\cdot v_{m}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=[s^{n-1}(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s},s^{m}r^{-b}\ ds^{b}]

\displaystyle=\Big(\big(s^{n-1}\partial_{s}(s^{m})+b\partial_{s}(s^{n-1})s^{m}\big)(s^{2}-4)+bs^{n+m}\Big)r^{-b}\ ds^{b}

\displaystyle=\Big((m+b(n-1))s^{n+m-2}(s^{2}-4)+bs^{n+m}\Big)r^{-b}\ ds^{b}

\displaystyle=\Big((m+bn)s^{n+m}-4(b(n-1)-m)s^{n+m-2}\Big)r^{-b}\ ds^{b}

\displaystyle=(n-m)v_{n+m}^{(b)}+4(b(n-1)-m)v_{n+m-2}^{(b)}.

Thus, letting
V\coloneqq\spn\{v_{n}^{(b)}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}=\Bbbk[s]r^{-b}\ ds^{b}
, we see that
V
is an
\mathcal{O}
-module. Since
P^{(b)}_{0}=2v_{0}^{(b)}
and
P^{(b)}_{1}=v_{1}^{(b)}
are elements of
V
, Lemma
4.27
implies that
\mathcal{P}_{b}\subseteq V
.

For the other inclusion
V\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{b}
, it suffices to show that
v_{0}^{(b)}
and
v_{1}^{(b)}
generate
V
. We have

\displaystyle u_{n}\cdot v_{0}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=-bnv_{n}^{(b)}+4b(n-1)v_{n-2}^{(b)}

\displaystyle u_{1}\cdot v_{n}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=-(m+b)v_{n+1}^{(b)}+4mv_{n-1}^{(b)}

for all
n
. The result now follows by an easy induction.
∎

The next result, which shows that
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is a reducible
\mathcal{O}
-module for all
b\in\Bbbk
follows immediately from Theorem
4.30
.

Corollary 4.31
.

Let
b\in\Bbbk
. Then
(s+2)\mathcal{P}_{b}
and
(s-2)\mathcal{P}_{b}
are proper
\mathcal{O}
-submodules of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is a reducible
\mathcal{O}
-module for all
b\in\Bbbk
.

Proof.

If
h
is a multiple of
s\pm 2
in (
4.7
), then
[(s^{2}-4)g\partial_{s},hr\ ds^{-1}]
is also a multiple of
s\pm 2
for all
g\in\Bbbk[s]
, so we see that
(s\pm 2)\mathcal{P}_{b}
is an
\mathcal{O}
-submodule of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
.
∎

On the other hand,
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is indecomposable as an
\mathcal{O}
-module, provided
b\neq 0
.

Lemma 4.32
.

If
b\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
, then
\mathcal{P}_{b}
is an indecomposable
\mathcal{O}
-module. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}_{0}
decomposes as

\mathcal{P}_{0}=\Bbbk P_{0}^{(0)}\oplus(s+2)\mathcal{P}_{0}=\Bbbk P_{0}^{(0)}\oplus(s-2)\mathcal{P}_{0}.

Proof.

The first part of the result follows from the following easy claim: if
M
is a non-zero submodule of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
with
b\neq 0
, then
M
has finite codimension in
\mathcal{P}_{b}
. The second part is immediate from (
2.4
) and Corollary
4.31
.
∎

We finish by considering some consequences of Theorem
4.30
for
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{-1}
. First, we simplify the notation for the new basis of
\mathcal{P}
, since this is the main
\mathcal{O}
-module of interest in this paper.

Notation 4.33
.

We define
v_{n}\coloneqq v_{n}^{(-1)}=s^{n}r\ ds^{-1}=(t+t^{-1})^{n}t\ dt^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}
.

The next result computes the bracket of the centreless BCCA
\mathfrak{b}
with the new bases for
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{P}
.

Corollary 4.34
.

We have
\mathfrak{b}=\spn\{u_{n},v_{m}\mid n\geq 1,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
. The bracket of
\mathfrak{b}
with this basis is given by

\displaystyle[u_{n},u_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)(u_{n+m}-4u_{n+m-2})

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 1),

\displaystyle[u_{n},v_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)v_{n+m}-4(n-m-1)v_{n+m-2}

\displaystyle(n\geq 1,m\geq 0),

\displaystyle[v_{n},v_{m}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 0).

Proof.

Follows immediately from Theorems
4.16
and
4.30
.
∎

As was done in Lemma
4.26
, we consider the relationship between the two bases
P_{n}
and
v_{n}
of
\mathcal{P}
. Since
P_{0}=2v_{0}
, we need to be a bit careful, but otherwise the proof is nearly identical to that of Lemma
4.26
.

Lemma 4.35
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

v_{n}=Q_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\left(\binom{n-1}{k}+\binom{n-1}{k-1}\right)Q_{n-2k},

where
Q_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}P_{n},&\text{if }n\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2}P_{0},&\text{if }n=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Similar to Lemma
4.26
.
∎

Thanks to Lemmas
4.26
and
4.35
, we can easily deduce what the central extension of
\mathfrak{b}
giving rise to
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
looks like using the new basis
\{u_{n},v_{m}\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 0\}
of
\mathfrak{b}
. As the next result shows, the central extension becomes rather unwieldy with this choice of basis.

Corollary 4.36
.

View
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
as elements of the (centrally extended) BCCA
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
. Then

\displaystyle[u_{2n},v_{2m+1}]

\displaystyle=(2(n-m)-1)v_{2(n+m)+1}-8(n-m-1)v_{2(n+m)-1}

\displaystyle[u_{2n+1},v_{2m}]

\displaystyle=(2(n-m)+1)v_{2(n+m)+1}-8(n-m)v_{2(n+m)-1}

\displaystyle[u_{2n},v_{2m}]

\displaystyle=2(n-m)v_{2(n+m)}-4(2(n-m)-1)v_{2(n+m-1)}

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,m)}\left(\binom{2n-1}{n-k}-\binom{2n-1}{n-k-1}\right)\left(\binom{2m-1}{m-k}+\binom{2m-1}{m-k-1}\right)2k(4k^{2}-1)\right)C_{M}

\displaystyle[u_{2n+1},v_{2m+1}]

\displaystyle=2(n-m)v_{2(n+m+1)}-4(2(n-m)-1)v_{2(n+m)}

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,m)}\left(\binom{2n}{n-k}-\binom{2n}{n-k-1}\right)\left(\binom{2m}{m-k}+\binom{2m}{m-k-1}\right)4k(2k+1)(k+1)\right)C_{M}

for all
n,m
. ∎

Remark 4.37
.

The centreless BCCA is strongly almost-graded according to
[
Sch17
, Definition 4.1]
: setting
\mathfrak{b}_{n}\coloneqq\spn\{u_{n},v_{n}\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
(where
u_{0}=0
), we see that

[\mathfrak{b}_{n},\mathfrak{b}_{m}]\subseteq\bigoplus_{k=n+m-2}^{n+m}\mathfrak{b}_{k}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, by Corollary
4.34
. However, Corollary
4.36
shows that cocycle on
\mathfrak{b}
giving rise to
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
is not compatible with the almost-grading, in the sense that is impossible to extend the almost-grading of
\mathfrak{b}
to an almost-grading of
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
. This motivates our approach in later sections, where we focus on the centreless BCCA, since we would like to use the new basis
\{u_{n},v_{m}\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 0\}
to study the representation theory of the BCCA. This becomes very difficult if we have to deal with the central extension as described in Corollary
4.36
.

Furthermore, representations of the BCCA where
C_{M}
acts trivially are to be expected when one takes the tensionless limit on the spectrum of the tensile open bosonic string. This is because of how the tensionless limit of the open bosonic string corresponds to a
Lie algebra contraction
of its worldsheet symmetry algebra (which is simply the Virasoro algebra). If one were to define this contraction
[
BCC+25
, Equation (8)]
at the level of representations, then one would obtain
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-modules on which
C_{M}
acts trivially. Thus, we note that the study of centreless BCCA modules is also physically motivated.

Finally, we consider what happens to
\mathcal{P}
under the change of variables
s\mapsto t
. Since
r
is a square root of
s^{2}-4
by (
4.4
), we get that
r\mapsto\sqrt{t^{2}-4}=\sqrt{f}
under this change of variables.

Corollary 4.38
.

Under the change of variables
s\mapsto t
, the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to the
\mathfrak{f}
-module
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq\Bbbk[t]\sqrt{f}\ dt^{-1}
. In other words,
\mathfrak{b}\cong\mathfrak{f}\ltimes\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

Follows from Theorems
4.16
and
4.30
, and the observation that
r=\sqrt{s^{2}-4}
from (
4.4
).
∎

We finish by summarising the results of this section for the centreless BCCA.

•

We have defined two different bases for the centreless BCCA, denoted by
\{\mathcal{O}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{m}\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 0\}
and
\{u_{n},v_{m}\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 0\}
. The Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{b}
with these two bases is given by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{O}_{n},\mathcal{O}_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)\mathcal{O}_{n+m}-(n+m)\mathcal{O}_{n-m}

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 1),

\displaystyle[\mathcal{O}_{n},P_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)P_{n+m}+(n+m)P_{n-m}

\displaystyle(n\geq 1,m\geq 0),

\displaystyle[P_{n},P_{m}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 0),

\displaystyle[u_{n},u_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)(u_{n+m}-4u_{n+m-2})

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 1),

\displaystyle[u_{n},v_{m}]

\displaystyle=(n-m)v_{n+m}-4(n-m-1)v_{n+m-2}

\displaystyle(n\geq 1,m\geq 0),

\displaystyle[v_{n},v_{m}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(n,m\geq 0).

•

In basis-free notation, we have
\mathcal{O}=\Bbbk[t+t^{-1}](t^{2}-1)\partial
and
\mathcal{P}=\Bbbk[t+t^{-1}]t\ dt^{-1}
. Letting
s=t+t^{-1}
,
r=t-t^{-1}
, and
\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}}\partial=\frac{t^{2}}{t^{2}-1}\partial
, we can also write
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{P}
as

\mathcal{O}=\Bbbk[s](s^{2}-4)\partial_{s},\quad\mathcal{P}=\Bbbk[s]r\ ds^{-1}.

•

There is an isomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P}\to\mathfrak{f}\ltimes\mathfrak{m}
given by the change of variables
s\mapsto t
, where
\mathfrak{f}=f\mathbb{W}_{1}=(t^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{m}=\Bbbk[t]\sqrt{f}\ dt^{-1}
. Explicitly,
\Phi(u_{n})=f_{n}
and
\Phi(v_{n})=t^{n}\sqrt{f}\ dt^{-1}
for all
n
, where
f_{n}=L_{n}-4L_{n-2}
. In basis-free notation,

\Phi(p(s)(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s})=p(t)(t^{2}-4)\partial,\quad\Phi(p(s)r\ ds^{-1})=p(t)\sqrt{f}\ dt^{-1}

for all
p\in\Bbbk[t]
. In particular,
\mathcal{O}
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
of finite codimension.

5.
Filtering the BCCA

5.1.
Defining the filtation

Using the alternate basis for the BCCA from Corollary
4.34
, we construct a descending filtration for
\mathfrak{b}
, thus allowing us to define and study Whittaker modules for the BCCA. We first filter the subalgebra
\mathcal{O}
of the BCCA. It is convenient to introduce a shift in the basis
u_{n}
of
\mathcal{O}
: define

\mathcal{U}_{n}\coloneqq u_{n+2}=(t+t^{-1})^{n+1}(t^{2}-1)\partial=s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s}

for
n\geq-1
. We can now construct a filtration of
\mathcal{O}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\coloneqq s^{n+1}\mathcal{O}=\spn\{\mathcal{U}_{k}\mid k\geq n\}.

Then
\mathcal{F}
is a filtration of
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

We have

(5.1)

\displaystyle=[u_{n+2},u_{m+2}]=(n-m)(u_{n+m+4}-4u_{n+m+2})

\displaystyle=(n-m)(\mathcal{U}_{n+m+2}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{n+m}),

so we see that
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathcal{O}]\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+m}\mathcal{O}
for all
n,m
.
∎

We now extend the filtration
\mathcal{F}
to the full BCCA. As before, we must shift the basis
v_{n}
: define

\mathcal{V}_{n}\coloneqq v_{n+1}=-(t+t^{-1})^{n+1}t\ dt^{-1}=-s^{n+1}r\ ds^{-1}

for
n\geq-1
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}\coloneqq s^{n+1}\mathcal{P}=\spn\{\mathcal{V}_{k}\mid k\geq n\}.

Then
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathcal{P}]\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+m}\mathcal{P}
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently, we can filter the full centreless BCCA as follows:

\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}=\mathcal{F}_{n}(\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}=\spn\{\mathcal{U}_{k},\mathcal{V}_{k}\mid k\geq n\}.

Proof.

For all
n,m\geq-1
, we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle=[u_{n+2},v_{m+1}]=(n-m+1)v_{n+m+3}-4(n-m)v_{n+m+1}

\displaystyle=(n-m+1)\mathcal{V}_{n+m+2}-4(n-m)\mathcal{V}_{n+m},

and thus
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathcal{P}]\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+m}\mathcal{P}
. It is now clear that
\mathcal{F}
is a filtration of the entirety of
\mathfrak{b}
, by Lemma
5.1
. In other words,
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathfrak{b}]\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+m}\mathfrak{b}
, and

\mathfrak{b}=\mathcal{F}_{-1}\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\dots.\qed

We finish this section by considering the associated graded algebra to the filtration
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The associated graded Lie algebras to the filtration
\mathcal{F}
are

(1)

\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{O}\cong\mathbb{W}_{1}
, and

(2)

\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{b}\cong\mathbb{W}_{1}\ltimes\ad(\mathbb{W}_{1})
, where
\ad(\mathbb{W}_{1})
is another copy of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, viewed as the adjoint module of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
with abelian Lie bracket.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{n}+\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{U}_{m}+\mathcal{F}_{m+1}\mathcal{O}]

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}_{n},\mathcal{U}_{m}]+\mathcal{F}_{m+n+1}\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle=-4(n-m)\mathcal{U}_{n+m}+\mathcal{F}_{n+m+1}\mathcal{O}.

Thus, the Lie algebra homomorphism
\mathcal{U}_{n}+\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\mathcal{O}\mapsto-4L_{n}
establishes the isomorphism
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{O}\cong\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{n}+\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{V}_{m}+\mathcal{F}_{m+1}\mathfrak{b}]

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}_{n},\mathcal{V}_{m}]+\mathcal{F}_{n+m+1}\mathfrak{b}

\displaystyle=-4(n-m)\mathcal{V}_{n+m}+\mathcal{F}_{n+m+1}\mathfrak{b}.

It is now clear that
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{b}\cong\mathbb{W}_{1}\ltimes\ad(\mathbb{W}_{1})
using a similar isomorphism to the above, as required.
∎

5.2.
The derived subalgebras of the filtered subspaces

To study Whittaker functions on
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, we must study their Whittaker functions. By definition, these are Lie algebra homomorphisms
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
or
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\Bbbk
is an abelian Lie algebra, these homomorphisms must necessarily vanish on the derived subalgebras
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
or
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]
. We compute these in this section.

First, note that
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{O}
, by (
5.1
). We now prove that
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
is spanned by
\mathcal{U}_{2n+2+k}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{2n+k}
for
k\geq 1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
n\geq-1
, the derived subalgebra
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
has the following form:

[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]=(s^{2n+4}-4s^{2n+2})\mathcal{O}=(s^{2n+4}-4s^{2n+2})(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}.

Consequently, the set
\{\mathcal{U}_{2n+3+k}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{2n+1+k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a basis for
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
.

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
4.16
that
\mathcal{O}=(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
, and that
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}=s^{n+1}\mathcal{O}
by definition. Therefore,

[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]=[s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s},s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}]=\Big(s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Big)^{2}\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s},

by (
4.1
). Simplifying, we get

[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]=\Big(s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Big)^{2}\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}=s^{2n+2}(s^{2}-4)^{2}\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}=(s^{2n+4}-4s^{2n+2})(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}.

Noting that

\mathcal{U}_{2n+3+k}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{2n+1+k}=(s^{2n+4+k}-4s^{2n+2+k})(s^{2}-4)\partial_{s},

it is now clear that the result follows.
∎

We now proceed as in Lemma
5.4
to compute the derived subalgebra
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]
for the filtration of the full BCCA. Similarly to the above, (
5.2
) implies that
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{P}
. In fact, this is an equality: we have
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{P}
, as we prove next.

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
n\geq-1
, we have
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}]=\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{P}
. Consequently,

[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]=[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]\ltimes\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{P},

and therefore the set
\{\mathcal{U}_{2n+3+k}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{2n+1+k},\mathcal{V}_{2n+1+k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a basis for
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]
.

Proof.

For all
m\geq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{m+1}]\in[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2m+2}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{m}]\in[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}],

by (
5.2
). The result follows.
∎

5.3.
The abelianisations of the filtered subspaces

We finish this section by computing the dimensions of the abelianisations of
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
. This computation will tell us exactly how many degrees of freedom one has when defining a Whittaker function on
\mathcal{O}
or on
\mathfrak{b}
. The following easy observation will be useful for this purpose.

Lemma 5.6
.

For any
g\in\Bbbk[t]
, the codimension of
g\mathbb{W}_{1}
in
\mathbb{W}_{1}
is
\deg(g)
. ∎

Of course, Lemma
5.6
also implies that
g(s)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
has codimension
\deg(g)
in
\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
, via the change of variables
s\mapsto t
.

Proposition 5.7
.

We have

\dim\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}{[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]}\right)=n+3,\quad\dim\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}}{[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]}\right)=2n+4,

for all
n\geq-1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.16
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}=s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}.

This is a subalgebra of
\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
of codimension
n+3
, by Lemma
5.6
. On the other hand, Lemma
5.6
also implies that

[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]=\Big(s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\Big)^{2}\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}

is a subalgebra of
\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
of codimension
2n+6
. Therefore,
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
has codimension
n+3
in
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
, as required.

For the second part of the result, we observe that the codimension of
\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}
is
m-n
, for all
m\geq n\geq-1
. Now, Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
imply that

\dim\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}}{[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]}\right)=\dim\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}{[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]}\right)+\dim(\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{F}_{2n+1}\mathcal{P})=n+3+n+1=2n+4,

which concludes the proof.
∎

More precisely, write
\overline{x}
for the image of
x
in the abelianization
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}/[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]
or
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}/[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
, where
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
or
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
, and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then Proposition
5.7
implies that
\{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{n},\dots,\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{2n+2}\}
forms a basis of
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}/[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
, and
\{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{n},\dots,\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{2n+2}\}\cup\{\overline{\mathcal{V}_{n}},\dots,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2n}\}
forms a basis of
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}/[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}]
.

The simplest application of Proposition
5.7
is that we can completely classify all one-dimensional representations of
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
, and define the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Bbbk_{\lambda,\mu}
as follows: as a vector space,
\Bbbk_{\lambda,\mu}=\Bbbk 1_{\lambda,\mu}
, and the
\mathcal{O}
-action is given by

\mathcal{U}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}=\lambda 1_{\lambda,\mu},\quad\mathcal{U}_{0}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}=\mu 1_{\lambda,\mu}.

The action of the other basis elements is determined by these and the formula

\mathcal{U}_{n}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}=4\,\mathcal{U}_{n-2}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}\quad(n\geq 1).

For example, we have
\mathcal{U}_{1}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}=4\lambda 1_{\lambda,\mu}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}\cdot 1_{\lambda,\mu}=4\mu 1_{\lambda,\mu}
. In fact, all one-dimensional representations of
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are of the above form, which is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
, and Proposition
5.7
.

Corollary 5.9
.

If
M
is a one-dimensional
\mathcal{O}
-representation, then
M\cong\Bbbk_{\lambda,\mu}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
. If
M
is a one-dimensional
\mathfrak{b}
-representation, then
\mathcal{P}\cdot M=0
, so
M
can be viewed as a representation over
\mathfrak{b}/\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{O}
. ∎

6.
Whittaker modules

Having defined and analysed the filtrations for
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, we now study Whittaker modules for these Lie algebras (recall Lemma
2.11
).

6.1.
Whittaker modules for
\mathcal{O}

We start by defining Whittaker functions for
\mathcal{O}
, which in turn define universal Whittaker modules for
\mathcal{O}
. We further characterise precisely when these modules are irreducible.

Definition 6.1
.

A
Whittaker function on
\mathcal{O}
is a Lie algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

As we show next, a Whittaker function
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
is determined by its values on
\mathcal{U}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{2n+2}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

A Whittaker function
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
is determined by its values on
\mathcal{U}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{2n+2}
, and
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m+2})=4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m})
for all
m\geq 2n+1
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from Lemma
5.4
and Proposition
5.7
.
∎

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function and
\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}=\Bbbk 1_{\varphi_{n}}
be the one-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
-module defined by
\varphi_{n}
, in other words,

x\cdot 1_{\varphi_{n}}=\varphi_{n}(x)1_{\varphi_{n}}

for all
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
. Define the
universal Whittaker module for
\mathcal{O}
of type
\varphi_{n}
to be the induced module

M_{\varphi_{n}}\coloneqq\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}.

Remark 6.4
.

Note that
\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}
is a continuous module for
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
in the sense of Rudakov
[
Rud74
]
if and only if
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})=\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})=0
. In fact, if
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})\neq 0
, then
\varphi(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2k+1})\neq 0
for all
k\geq 0
, and if
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})\neq 0
, then
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2m+2k+2})\neq 0
for all
k\geq 0
, by Lemma
6.2
.

Remark 6.5
.

The Lie algebra
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}
is not quasi-nilpotent
[
Buz24
, Remark 3.5]
, so
M_{\varphi_{0}}
is not a Whittaker module according to Definition
2.9
. As we will see, the criteria for irreducibility for
M_{\varphi_{0}}
and their proofs are slightly different to those for
M_{\varphi_{n}}
with
n\geq 1
, highlighting the importance of the quasi-nilpotence condition. However, to avoid piling onto the nomenclature, we abuse terminology and opt to call
M_{\varphi_{0}}
a universal Whittaker module.

We also highlight that
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
is not an ideal of
\mathcal{O}
if
n\geq 0
. Therefore, the universal Whittaker modules
M_{\varphi_{n}}
are never quasi-Whittaker modules according to Definition
2.12
.

The following result is the main theorem of this subsection.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function. If
n=0
, the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{0}}
is irreducible if and only if
\varphi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{2})\neq 4\varphi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{0})
. If
n\geq 1
, the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{n}}
is irreducible if and only if
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})\neq 4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n-1})
or
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})\neq 4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n})
.

We now proceed to prove that
M_{\varphi_{n}}
is reducible if the condition from Theorem
6.6
is not satisfied, proving one direction of Theorem
6.6
. As mentioned in Remark
6.5
, the proofs are slightly different depending on whether
n=0
or
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\varphi_{0}\colon\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function with
\varphi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{2})=4\varphi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{0})
. Then
U(\mathcal{O})\mathcal{U}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}
is a proper submodule of the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{0}}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{i})
for
i\geq 0
, and
w\coloneqq(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}=[\mathcal{U}_{0},\mathcal{U}_{-1}]\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}
. We claim that
U(\mathcal{O})\cdot w
is a proper
\mathcal{O}
-submodule of
M_{\varphi_{0}}
.

Lemma
6.2
together with the assumption that
\lambda_{2}=4\lambda_{0}
imply that
\lambda_{n+2}=4\lambda_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}\cdot w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{0}(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}-4[\mathcal{U}_{0},\mathcal{U}_{-1}]-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1}\mathcal{U}_{0}\Big)\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}-4(\mathcal{U}_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})-4\lambda_{0}\,\mathcal{U}_{-1}\Big)\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big((\lambda_{0}-4)\lambda_{1}-4(\lambda_{0}-4)\mathcal{U}_{-1}\Big)\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{0}-4)w.

Now letting
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{n}\cdot w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{n}(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{n}\lambda_{1}-4[\mathcal{U}_{n},\mathcal{U}_{-1}]-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1}\mathcal{U}_{n}\Big)\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{n}\lambda_{1}-4(n+1)(\mathcal{U}_{n+1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{n-1})-4\lambda_{n}\,\mathcal{U}_{-1}\Big)\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}-4(n+1)(\lambda_{n+1}-4\lambda_{n-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}w,

where we used that
\lambda_{n+1}=4\lambda_{n-1}
in the last equality, since
n\geq 1
. This proves the claim, and the result follows.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

When
\lambda_{2}=4\lambda_{0}
, the submodule
U(\mathcal{O})(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}
in the proof of Lemma
6.7
is isomorphic to
M_{\varphi_{0}^{\prime}}
, where
\varphi^{\prime}_{0}\colon\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
is the Whittaker function with
\varphi^{\prime}_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{0})=\lambda_{0}-4
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{n})=\lambda_{n}
for
n\geq 1
.

In this case, the quotient module
M_{\varphi_{0}}/(U(\mathcal{O})(\lambda_{1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{-1})\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}})
is a
1
-dimensional
\mathcal{O}
-module determined by
\mathcal{U}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4}1_{\varphi_{0}},\mathcal{U}_{0}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{0}}=\lambda_{0}1_{\varphi_{0}}
.

The case
n\geq 1
follows from the existence of an analogous submodule.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function with
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})=4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n-1})
and
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})=4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n})
. Then
U(\mathcal{O})\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{n}}
is a submodule of the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{i})
for
i\geq n
, and
w\coloneqq\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{n}}
. We claim that
U(\mathcal{O})\cdot w
is a proper submodule of
M_{\varphi_{n}}
.

Lemma
6.2
together with the assumptions that
\lambda_{2n+1}=4\lambda_{2n-1}
and
\lambda_{2n+2}=4\lambda_{2n}
imply that
\lambda_{k+2}=4\lambda_{k}
for all
k\geq 2n-1
. For
m\geq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{m}\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{U}_{n-1}]\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}+\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}

\displaystyle=(m-n+1)(\mathcal{U}_{m+n+1}-4\,\mathcal{U}_{m+n-1})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}+\lambda_{m}w

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}w,

since
m+n-1\geq 2n-1
, where we used that
\lambda_{k+2}=4\lambda_{k}
for all
k\geq 2n-1
.

The above shows that
\Bbbk w
is a one-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
-module isomorphic to
\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}
. It is now easy to see that
U(\mathcal{O})\cdot w
is a proper submodule of
M_{\varphi_{n}}
isomorphic to
M_{\varphi_{n}}
itself.
∎

Note that together Lemmas
6.7
and
6.9
prove one direction of Theorem
6.6
. To prove the converse, we use the theory of local functions and the orbit method, which we introduce in the next subsection.

6.2.
Local functions and the orbit method for
\mathcal{O}

Kirillov’s orbit method
[
Kir04
]
is a guiding principle in Lie theory, establishing a correspondence between equivalence classes of unitary irreducible representations and coadjoint orbits. In this subsection, we explain the connection between the Whittaker functions on
\mathcal{O}
and the orbit method for the Lie algebra
\mathbb{W}_{1}
constructed in
[
Pha25
]
. This will allow us to use results in
[
Pha25
]
to prove the other direction of Theorem
6.6
. Note that in this subsection, we view
\mathbb{W}_{1}=\Der(\Bbbk[s])=\Bbbk[s]\partial_{s}
(in other words, we use the variable
s
instead of
t
), so that we can view
\mathcal{O}
as the subalgebra
(s^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}
of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
.

We start by recalling the notion of a local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, originally defined in
[
PS23
]
.

Definition 6.10
.

Letting
x,\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\Bbbk
with
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
, we define a
one-point local function

\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}}\in\mathbb{W}_{1}^{\ast}
by

(6.1)

\chi_{x;\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}}\colon\mathbb{W}_{1}\to\Bbbk,\quad f\partial\mapsto\alpha_{0}f(x)+\alpha_{1}f^{\prime}(x)+\dots+\alpha_{n}f^{(n)}(x),

where
f\in\Bbbk[s]
and
f^{(i)}
denotes taking the
i^{\text{th}}
derivative of
f
with respect to
s
. We call
x
the
base point
of
\chi
and
n
the
order
of
\chi
.

A
local function

\chi
on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a sum of finitely many functions of the form (
6.1
) with (possibly) distinct
x
. In other words,

\displaystyle\chi=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{\ell},

where
\chi_{i}
is a one-point local function based at
x_{i}\in\Bbbk
of order
n_{i}
, and the
x_{i}
are pairwise distinct.

Next, we recall the notion of a polarisation for a local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
in
[
Pha25
]
.

Definition 6.11
.

For every
\chi\in\mathbb{W}_{1}^{*}
, we define a bilinear form

\displaystyle B_{\chi}:\mathbb{W}_{1}\times\mathbb{W}_{1}

\displaystyle\to\Bbbk

\displaystyle(u,v)

\displaystyle\mapsto\chi([u,v]).

A
polarisation

\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}
of
\chi
is a finite-codimensional subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
that is maximally totally isotropic subspace with respect to
B_{\chi}
, in other words,
B_{\chi}([\mathfrak{p}_{\chi},\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}])=0
.

Remark 6.12
.

In general, an arbitrary element
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
for a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
might not have a polarisation. However, if
\chi
is a local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, then
\chi
always has a polarisation
[
Pha25
, Proposition 3.2]
. In this case, the polarisation can moreover be chosen as a submodule-subalgebra of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
.

We consider an example of a local function and its polarisation to illustrate the concept.

Example 6.13

(
[
PS23
]
)
.

Let
\chi\coloneqq\chi_{x;\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}
be a one-point local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
where
x\in\Bbbk
and
(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})\in\Bbbk^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. In other words,

\chi(f\partial)=\nu_{0}f(x)+\nu_{1}f^{\prime}(x).

Consider the submodule-subalgebra
(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}
of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Certainly,
(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}
has codimension 1 in
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Moreover, (
4.1
) implies that

\chi([(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1},(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}])=\chi((s-x)^{2}\mathbb{W}_{1})=0,

and therefore
(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a totally isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
with respect to
B_{\chi}
. Note that
(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}
is maximally totally isotropic by a dimension count
[
Pha25
, Lemma 3.1]
, and therefore is a polarisation of
\chi
.

Remark 6.14
.

The polarisation
(s-x)\mathbb{W}_{1}
is in fact the unique polarisation for the local function
\chi_{x;\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}
(see, for example,
[
PS23
, Section 7.1]
for a proof). However, polarisations for local functions of
\mathbb{W}_{1}
are in general not unique (for an example, see
[
Pha25
, Example 3.3]
).

Remark 6.15
.

If
\mathfrak{q}
is a subalgebra of a polarisation of a local function
\chi
, then
\chi([\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}])=0
, and thus
\chi
defines a one-dimensional representation of
\mathfrak{q}
:

\displaystyle{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{q}}}\colon\mathfrak{q}

\displaystyle\to\Bbbk

\displaystyle f\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto\chi(f\partial).

We now associate a local function to each Whittaker function
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
.

Proposition 6.16
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function. Then there exists a local function
\chi=\chi_{0;\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{2n+1}}+\chi_{2;\beta,\beta_{1}}+\chi_{-2;\gamma,\gamma_{1}}
on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
such that
\varphi_{n}={\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
.

Moreover, we can always choose
\alpha_{0},\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk
arbitrarily, and

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})\neq 4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n})

\displaystyle\iff\alpha_{2n+1}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})\neq 4\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n-1})

\displaystyle\iff\alpha_{2n}\neq 0\quad(\text{provided }n\geq 1).

Proof.

Choose arbitrary
\alpha_{i},\beta,\beta_{1},\gamma,\gamma_{1}\in\Bbbk
, and let

(6.2)

\displaystyle\chi\coloneqq\chi_{0;\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{2n+1}}+\chi_{2;\beta,\beta_{1}}+\chi_{-2;\gamma,\gamma_{1}}.

It is easy to check that
\chi
vanishes on
s^{2n+2}(s^{2}-4)^{2}\mathbb{W}_{1}=[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
, thus
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
is contained in a polarisation of
\chi
. Hence,
\chi
restricts to a one-dimensional representation
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
of
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
. Since both
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
and
\varphi_{n}
vanish on
[\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}]
, we require the following system of equations to have
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}=\varphi_{n}
:

(6.3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{k})

\displaystyle=(k+3)!\alpha_{k+3}-4(k+1)!\alpha_{k+1}+2^{k+3}(\beta_{1}+(-1)^{k}\gamma_{1})\text{
for }n\leq k\leq 2n-2,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n-1})

\displaystyle=-4(2n)!\alpha_{2n}+2^{2n+2}(\beta_{1}+(-1)^{2n-1}\gamma_{1}),

(6.5)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n})

\displaystyle=-4(2n+1)!\alpha_{2n+1}+2^{2n+3}(\beta_{1}+(-1)^{2n}\gamma_{1}),

(6.6)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+1})

\displaystyle=2^{2n+4}(\beta_{1}+(-1)^{2n+1}\gamma_{1}),

(6.7)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})

\displaystyle=2^{2n+5}(\beta_{1}+(-1)^{2n+2}\gamma_{1}).

Note that
(
6.3
)
only exists for
n\geq 2
, and
(
6.4
)
only exists for
n\geq 1
. For all
n
, there are
n+3
equations (from
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{n})
to
\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{2n+2})
) with
n+3
variables (from
\alpha_{n+1}
to
\alpha_{2n+1}
, as well as
\beta_{1}
,
\gamma_{1}
). Furthermore, there are no inconsistencies on the right hand side, thus there always exist
\alpha_{n+1},\dots,\alpha_{2n+1},\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}\in\Bbbk
satisfying the above system. In other words, there always exists
\chi
of the form (
6.2
) such that
\varphi_{n}={\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
. Since
\alpha_{0},\beta,\gamma
are not involved in the system of equations, we can choose them arbitrarily.

The last part of the statement follows from the combination of (
6.4
) and (
6.6
) (if
n\geq 1
), and the combination of (
6.5
) and (
6.7
).
∎

For the rest of this section, we fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a Whittaker function
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
. Given Proposition
6.16
, we introduce notation for the local function associated to
\varphi_{n}
.

Notation 6.17
.

For
k\geq n
, define
\lambda_{k}\coloneqq\varphi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{k})
. Furthermore, using Proposition
6.16
, choose
\alpha_{i},\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}\in\Bbbk
such that
\varphi_{n}={\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
, where

\chi\coloneqq\chi_{0;1,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2n+1}}+\chi_{2;1,\beta_{1}}+\chi_{-2;1,\gamma_{1}}.

We will denote

\eta\coloneqq\chi_{0;1,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2n+1}},\quad\theta_{a,b}\coloneqq\chi_{2;1,a}+\chi_{-2;1,b},\quad\chi_{a,b}\coloneqq\eta+\theta_{a,b}

for
a,b\in\Bbbk
. For simplicity of notation, write
\theta\coloneqq\theta_{\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}}
, so that
\chi=\eta+\theta
.

Although it is not obvious at this point why we have introduced the family of local functions
\chi_{a,b}
, this soon become clear.

Remark 6.18
.

The splitting
\chi=\eta+\theta
was introduced because
\theta
defines a Lie algebra homomorphism
\theta\colon\mathcal{O}\to\Bbbk
, since
\theta
vanishes on
[\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}]=(s^{2}-4)^{2}\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Therefore,
\Bbbk_{\theta}
is a one-dimensional representation of
\mathcal{O}
. This is not true for
\eta
, and we will soon see that
\eta
completely controls the reducibility of
M_{\varphi_{n}}
.

Having introduced the necessary background and notation, we proceed to prove the irreducibility of these representations under the conditions of Theorem
6.6
, using the perspective of local functions. We begin this proof with realising the subalgebra
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
as a polarisation of the local function
\chi
, using results in
[
Pha25
]
.

Lemma 6.19

(
[
Pha25
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

Let
\chi=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{\ell}
be a local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, where
\chi_{i}
is a one-point local function at
x_{i}
with order
n_{i}
. Letting
m_{i}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{n_{i}}{2}\right\rfloor
, the subalgebra

\mathfrak{p}_{\chi_{i}}\coloneqq(s-x_{i})^{m_{i}+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}

is a polarisation of
\chi_{i}
. Furthermore,

\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}\coloneqq\mathfrak{p}_{\chi_{1}}\cap\dots\cap\mathfrak{p}_{\chi_{\ell}}=(s-x_{1})^{m_{1}+1}\dots(s-x_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}

is a polarisation of
\chi
.

Corollary 6.20
.

If
n=0
, then
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}
is a polarisation of
\chi_{a,b}
for all
a,b\in\Bbbk
. If
n\geq 1
, and
\lambda_{2n+1}\neq 4\lambda_{2n-1}
or
\lambda_{2n+2}\neq 4\lambda_{2n}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
is a polarisation of
\chi_{a,b}
for all
a,b\in\Bbbk
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we assume that
\lambda_{2n+1}\neq 4\lambda_{2n-1}
or
\lambda_{2n+2}\neq 4\lambda_{2n}
if
n\geq 1
.

We first claim that
s^{n+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a polarisation of
\eta
. If
n=0
, then by definition we have
\eta=\chi_{0;1,\alpha_{1}}
for some
\alpha_{1}\in\Bbbk
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{p}_{\eta}=s\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a polarisation of
\eta
, by Lemma
6.19
. If
n\geq 1
, then Proposition
6.16
implies that either
\alpha_{2n}\neq 0
or
\alpha_{2n+1}\neq 0
. Thus, by Lemma
6.19
, it follows that
\mathfrak{p}_{\eta}=s^{n+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a polarisation of
\eta
. This proves the claim.

Recalling that
\theta_{a,b}=\chi_{2;1,a}+\chi_{-2;1,b}
by definition, we also see that
\mathfrak{p}_{\theta_{a,b}}=(s^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}
is a polarisation of
\theta_{a,b}
, by Lemma
6.19
. We conclude that

\mathfrak{p}_{\chi_{a,b}}=\mathfrak{p}_{\eta+\theta_{a,b}}=s^{n+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}\cap(s^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}=s^{n+1}(s^{2}-4)\mathbb{W}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}

is a polarisation of
\chi_{a,b}
.
∎

The conditions under which
\Ind^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}}\Bbbk_{\chi}
is a simple
\mathbb{W}_{1}
-module are completely characterised in
[
Pha25
]
. We summarise the relevant results in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.21

(
[
Pha25
, Corollary 4.27, Proposition 4.23]
)
.

Let
\chi=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{\ell}
be a local function on
\mathbb{W}_{1}
, where
\chi_{i}
is a one-point local function at
x_{i}
with order
n_{i}
. Letting
m_{i}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{n_{i}}{2}\right\rfloor
, let

\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}=(s-x_{1})^{m_{1}+1}\dots(s-x_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}+1}\mathbb{W}_{1}

be the polarisation of
\chi
from Lemma
6.19
. Then

\Ind_{\mathfrak{p}_{\chi}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\Bbbk_{\chi}

is an irreducible
\mathbb{W}_{1}
-module if and only if
n_{i}>0
for all
i
.

Corollary
6.20
and Theorem
6.21
imply that
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\Bbbk_{\chi}
can only be an irreducible
\mathbb{W}_{1}
-module if
\beta_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}
are non-zero. However, this is not guaranteed by Proposition
6.16
. Thankfully, we can solve this issue: the reducibility of
M_{\varphi_{n}}=\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi}
does not depend on the values of
\beta_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}
, as we aim to show next. In other words, the reducibility of
M_{\varphi_{n}}
is equivalent to that of
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{a,b}}
for any choice of
a,b\in\Bbbk
.

We now decompose the universal Whittaker module of
\mathcal{O}
into the tensor product of an induced module of a local function and a one-dimensional representation. This helps us view the universal Whittaker module of
\mathcal{O}
as a twisted induced module of the one-point local function
\eta
.

Lemma 6.22
.

Let
a,b\in\Bbbk
, and let
\Bbbk_{\eta}
and
\Bbbk_{\theta_{a,b}}
be the one-dimensional representations of
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{O}
induced by
\eta
and
\theta_{a,b}
, respectively. Then

\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{a,b}}\cong(\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\eta})\otimes\Bbbk_{\theta_{a,b}}.

In particular,

M_{\varphi_{n}}\cong(\Ind^{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\eta})\otimes\Bbbk_{\theta}.

Proof.

Note that
\Bbbk_{\chi_{a,b}}=\Bbbk_{\eta+\theta_{a,b}}\cong\Bbbk_{\eta}\otimes\Bbbk_{\theta_{a,b}}
as a representation over
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
. The proof now follows by
[
Dix96
, Proposition 5.1.15]
or
[
LZ20
, Lemma 8]
. The final sentence of the result follows by the observation that
\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}=\Bbbk_{\chi}
as
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
-modules.
∎

The reducibility of a module is preserved when taking the tensor product with a one-dimensional representation of a Lie algebra. Thus, by Lemma
6.22
, the irreducibility of the universal Whittaker module is completely governed by the local function
\eta
.

Corollary 6.23
.

Let
a,b\in\Bbbk
. Then the
\mathcal{O}
-module
\Ind^{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{a,b}}
is irreducible if and only if
\Ind^{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\eta}
is irreducible. Consequently, the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{n}}=\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi}
is irreducible if and only if
\Ind^{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}
is irreducible. ∎

Remark 6.24
.

Corollary
6.23
implies that reducibility of the universal Whittaker module of type
\varphi_{n}
is equivalent to the reducibility of the universal Whittaker module of type
\varphi^{\prime}_{n}\coloneqq{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi_{1,1}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}}
.

We are now ready to complete the proof of Theorem
6.6
.

Proof of Theorem
6.6
.

First, assume that
\lambda_{2}\neq 4\lambda_{0}
(if
n=0
) or that
\lambda_{2n+1}\neq 4\lambda_{2n-1}
or
\lambda_{2n+2}\neq 4\lambda_{2n}
(if
n\geq 1
). By Corollary
6.23
,
M_{\varphi_{n}}
is irreducible if and only if
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}
is irreducible. Now, Proposition
6.16
implies the following:

•

If
n=0
, then
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
.

•

If
n\geq 1
, then
\alpha_{2n}
and
\alpha_{2n+1}
are not simultaneously 0.

By Corollary
6.20
, we know that
\mathfrak{p}_{\chi_{1,1}}=\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
is a polarisation of
\chi_{1,1}
. Furthermore, the order of each one-point local function which makes up
\chi_{1,1}
is at least 1. Thus, Theorem
6.21
implies that
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}
is an irreducible
\mathbb{W}_{1}
-module. It is easy to see that

\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}=\Ind_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}(\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}),

so the irreducibility of
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbb{W}_{1}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}
as a
\mathbb{W}_{1}
-module implies the irreducibility of
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\chi_{1,1}}
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module. We therefore conclude that that
M_{\varphi_{n}}
is irreducible.

For the converse, the case
n=0
follows from Lemma
6.7
and the case
n\geq 1
follows from Lemma
6.9
.
∎

6.3.
Whittaker modules for the centreless BCCA

We move on to the study of Whittaker modules over the full centreless BCCA. Although these may seem more complicated, since the BCCA is a larger Lie algebra than
\mathcal{O}
, we will see that the conditions governing the reducibility of the universal Whittaker modules over
\mathfrak{b}
are much simpler, and our proof is more elementary and direct. This is thanks to the abelian subalgebra
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathfrak{b}
. As we did in Subsection
6.1
, we start by defining Whittaker functions on
\mathfrak{b}
.

Definition 6.25
.

A
Whittaker function on
\mathfrak{b}
is a Lie algebra homomorphism
\psi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following result is an immediate consequence of Lemma
5.5
and Proposition
5.7
.

Lemma 6.26
.

A Whittaker function
\psi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
is determined by its values on
\mathcal{U}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{2n+2}
and
\mathcal{V}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{2n}
. Once these values are determined, we have

\psi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m+2})=4\psi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m}),\quad\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{m})=0

for all
m\geq 2n+1
. ∎

We now define the universal Whittaker modules for the full centreless BCCA, and then proceed to study their reducibility.

Definition 6.27
.

Let
\psi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function and
\Bbbk_{\psi_{n}}=\Bbbk 1_{\psi_{n}}
be the one-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
-module defined by
\psi_{n}
, in other words,

x\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=\psi_{n}(x)1_{\psi_{n}}

for all
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
. Define the
universal Whittaker module for
\mathfrak{b}
of type
\psi_{n}
to be the induced module

M_{\psi_{n}}\coloneqq\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathfrak{b}}\Bbbk_{\psi_{n}}.

As in Remark
6.5
, the
\mathfrak{b}
-modules
M_{\psi_{n}}
for
n\geq 1
are Whittaker modules, but
M_{\psi_{0}}
are not Whittaker modules.

The following result is the main theorem of this subsection.

Theorem 6.28
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\psi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
be a Whittaker function. If
n=0
, then
M_{\psi_{0}}
is irreducible if and only if
\psi_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{0})\neq 0
. If
n\geq 1
, then
M_{\psi_{n}}
is irreducible if and only if
\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n})\neq 0
or
\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n-1})\neq 0
.

We first prove the easier direction of Theorem
6.28
: we construct a non-zero proper submodule of
M_{\psi_{n}}
when the conditions of Theorem
6.28
are not satisfied. We first consider the case
n=0
.

Lemma 6.29
.

Suppose
n=0
and
\psi_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{0})=0
. Then
U(\mathfrak{b})\mathcal{V}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}
is a proper submodule of
M_{\psi_{0}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.26
, we have
\psi_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{m})=0
for all
m\geq 0
. In other words,
\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}=0
for all
m\geq 0
. Let
w\coloneqq\mathcal{V}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot w=0
for all
m\geq 0
, since the
\mathcal{V}_{k}
commute. Furthermore, for
m\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{m}\mathcal{V}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}=[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{-1}]\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}+\mathcal{V}_{-1}\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big((m+2)\mathcal{V}_{m+1}-4(m+1)\mathcal{V}_{m-1}\Big)\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}+\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{m})w

\displaystyle=-4(m+1)\mathcal{V}_{m-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}+\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{m})w,

since
m+1\geq 0
, where we used that
\mathcal{V}_{k}\cdot w=0
for all
k\geq 0
. If
m\geq 1
, then the above implies that
\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot w=\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{m})w
. On the other hand, if
m=0
, then

\mathcal{U}_{0}\cdot w=(\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{0})-4)w.

It follows that
\Bbbk w
is a one-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}
-module, and thus
U(\mathfrak{b})\cdot w
is a proper submodule of
M_{\psi_{0}}
.
∎

Remark 6.30
.

In the notation from the proof of Lemma
6.29
, it is easy to see that
U(\mathfrak{b})\cdot w
is isomorphic to
M_{\psi_{0}^{\prime}}
, where
\psi_{0}^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
is given by
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U}_{0})=\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{0})-4
,
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U}_{m})=\psi_{0}(\mathcal{U}_{m})
for
m\geq 1
, and
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\mathcal{V}_{m})=0
for
m\geq 0
. Therefore, Lemma
6.29
implies that there is chain of submodules

M_{\psi_{0}}\supseteq U(\mathfrak{b})\mathcal{V}_{-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}\supseteq U(\mathfrak{b})\mathcal{V}_{-1}^{2}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}\supseteq U(\mathfrak{b})\mathcal{V}_{-1}^{3}\cdot 1_{\psi_{0}}\supseteq\dots.

Next, we construct an analogous submodule to the one in Lemma
6.29
in the case
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 6.31
.

Let
n\geq 1
. If
\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n})=\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n-1})=0
, then
U(\mathfrak{b})\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\varphi_{n}}
is a proper submodule of
M_{\psi_{n}}
isomorphic to
M_{\psi_{n}}
itself.

Proof.

Let
w\coloneqq\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
. For
m\geq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{m}\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{n-1}]\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}+\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}

\displaystyle=\Big((m-n+2)\mathcal{V}_{n+m+1}-4(m-n+1)\mathcal{V}_{n+m-1}\Big)\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}+\psi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m})w

\displaystyle=\psi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m})w,

since
n+m-1\geq 2n-1
, where we used that
\mu_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 2n-1
. Similarly,

\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot w=\mathcal{V}_{m}\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=\mathcal{V}_{n-1}\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{m})w,

where we used that
\mathcal{V}_{m}
and
\mathcal{V}_{n-1}
commute.

The above shows that
\Bbbk w
is a one-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
-module isomorphic to
\Bbbk_{\psi_{n}}
. It is now easy to see that
U(\mathfrak{b})\cdot w
is isomorphic to
M_{\psi_{n}}
.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we assume that at least one of
\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n})
and
\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{2n-1})
is non-zero (if
n\geq 1)
or that
\psi_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{0})\neq 0
(if
n=0
). As a result, we introduce the following notation.

Notation 6.32
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a Whittaker function
\psi_{n}\colon\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}\to\Bbbk
. For
m\geq n
, define

\lambda_{m}\coloneqq\psi_{n}(\mathcal{U}_{m}),\quad\mu_{m}\coloneqq\psi_{n}(\mathcal{V}_{m}).

If
n\geq 1
, let
\kappa\in\{2n-1,2n\}
be maximal such that
\mu_{\kappa}\neq 0
. If
n=0
, let
\kappa=0
.

By Lemma
6.26
, we have
\lambda_{m+2}=4\lambda_{m}
and
\mu_{m}=0
for all
m\geq 2n+1
.

Having introduced the necessary notation, we now proceed to prove the other direction of Theorem
6.28
. To achieve this, we need to show that we can use actions of
\mathfrak{b}
to get
1_{\varphi_{n}}
from any non-zero element of
M_{\psi_{n}}
when
\mu_{2n}\neq 0
or
\mu_{2n-1}\neq 0
. We do this by successively removing
\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
\mathcal{V}_{j}
from elements of
U(\mathfrak{b})
. First, we prove that
\mathcal{V}_{m}
annihilates elements in
M_{\psi_{n}}
which do not contain
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
, provided
m>\kappa
. Recall that
\mathcal{P}=\spn\{\mathcal{V}_{k}|k\geq-1\}
is an abelian subalgebra of
\mathfrak{b}
.

Lemma 6.33
.

Let
x=\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v\in M_{\psi_{n}}
, where
v\in U(\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
and
0\leq r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dots\leq r_{\ell}\leq n-1
. Then
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m}\cdot x=0
for all
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

The result is clearly true if
n=0
, so assume that
n\geq 1
. We proceed by induction on
\ell
, the base case
\ell=0
being trivial. For the induction step, assume that the statement holds for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
x=\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v
, where
v\in U(\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
and
0\leq r_{0}\leq r_{1}\leq\dots\leq r_{\ell}\leq n-1
. For all
m\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m}\cdot x

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m}\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m},\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}]\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v+\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v

\displaystyle=\Big((\kappa+m-r_{0}-1)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m+r_{0}+2}-4(\kappa+m-r_{0})\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m+r_{0}}\Big)\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}v

\displaystyle=0,

where we used the induction hypothesis in the last two equalities.
∎

Next, we extend Lemma
6.33
to the situation where the element of
M_{\psi_{n}}
contains
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
.

Lemma 6.34
.

Let
x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
. Then
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+m}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x=0
if
m>N
.

Proof.

Follows via an easy induction on
N
, with Lemma
6.33
as the base case.
∎

In the following result, we show that the action
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x
results in the complete removal of
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
, in other words, that
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x
is a non-zero element of
U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
, where
x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
.

Proposition 6.35
.

Let
x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, for any
N\geq 1
, there exists
c\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
such that

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x=cx+\text{lower degree terms in }U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\neq 0,

where
lower degree terms
means terms with lower degree than
x
under the PBW filtration on
U(\mathfrak{b})
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
N
. First, consider the base case
N=0
:

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot x=\mu_{\kappa}x+\text{lower degree terms in }U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P}),

which is non-zero.

For the induction step, assume the statement is true for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N+1}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N+1}x

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N+1},\mathcal{U}_{-1}]\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x+\mathcal{U}_{-1}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N+1}\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x

\displaystyle=\Big((\kappa+N+1)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N+2}-4(\kappa+N+2)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N}\Big)\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x

\displaystyle=-4(\kappa+N+2)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N}\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N}x,

where we used Lemma
6.34
in the last two equalities. By the induction hypothesis, it follows that
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+N+1}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{-1}^{N+1}x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\setminus\{0\}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Having shown how
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
can be removed from any element of
U(\mathfrak{b})
, we now proceed to also remove
\mathcal{U}_{0}
.

Proposition 6.36
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
. Then the following hold.

(1)

Let
x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot x=\mu_{\kappa}x
.

(2)

Let
x\in U(\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{O}\ltimes\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then there exists
c\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
such that
(\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}-\mu_{\kappa})^{N}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{0}^{N}x=cx
.

Proof.

For (
1
), it suffices to consider
x=\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y
, where
y\in U(\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
and
1\leq r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dots\leq r_{\ell}\leq n-1
. The result is clearly true for
n=1
, so assume that
n\geq 2
. We proceed by induction on
\ell
, the case
\ell=0
being trivial. For the induction step, assume that the statement is true for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y=\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y+[\mathcal{V}_{\kappa},\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}]\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y.

By the induction hypothesis, we have

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y=\mu_{\kappa}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y.

Therefore,

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y=\mu_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y+\Big((\kappa-r_{0}-1)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+r_{0}+2}-4(\kappa-r_{0})\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+r_{0}}\Big)\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y.

By Lemma
6.33
, it follows that
\Big((\kappa-r_{0}-1)\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+r_{0}+2}-4(\kappa-r_{0})\mathcal{V}_{\kappa+r_{0}}\Big)\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y=0
, since
r_{0}\geq 1
. We conclude that
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y=\mu_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{r_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{r_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{r_{\ell}}y
, which concludes the proof of (
1
).

For (
2
), the proof is similar to that of Proposition
6.35
, using (
1
).
∎

The next result shows how to remove all the other
\mathcal{U}_{i}
from an element of
U(\mathfrak{b})
by successively acting by
(\mathcal{V}_{m}-\mu_{m})
for some appropriately chosen
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Of course, if
n=0
or
n=1
, then we have already removed all
\mathcal{U}_{i}
in the previous results, so we do not need to consider these cases.

Proposition 6.37
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
, and let
x=\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y
, where
y\in U(\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
and
1\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<\dots<q_{k}\leq n-1
. Then

(\mathcal{V}_{m}-\mu_{m})\cdot x=\begin{cases}-4p_{1}\mu_{\kappa}(\kappa-2q_{1})\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-1}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y,&\text{if }m=\kappa-q_{1},\\
0,&\text{if }m>\kappa-q_{1}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Rewrite
x
as

x=\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}y,

where
1\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\dots\leq s_{\ell}\leq n-1
. Of course, we have
s_{1}=q_{1}
. For
m\geq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot x

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{m}\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}\mathcal{V}_{m}y+[\mathcal{V}_{m},\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}]y

\displaystyle=\mu_{m}x+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{j-1}}[\mathcal{V}_{m},\mathcal{U}_{s_{j}}]\mathcal{U}_{s_{j+1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}y

\displaystyle=\mu_{m}x+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{j-1}}\Big((m-s_{j}-1)\mathcal{V}_{m+s_{j}+2}-4(m-s_{j})\mathcal{V}_{m+s_{j}}\Big)\mathcal{U}_{s_{j+1}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{s_{\ell}}y.

If
m+s_{j}>\kappa
, then the
j^{\text{th}}
term of the above summation is zero, by Lemma
6.33
. In particular, if
m>\kappa-s_{1}=\kappa-q_{1}
, then the entire summation vanishes, and thus
\mathcal{V}_{m}\cdot x=\mu_{m}x
, as required.

Now suppose
m=\kappa-s_{1}=\kappa-q_{1}
. By the above discussion, we can only get a contribution from the
j^{\text{th}}
term of the above summation if
s_{j}=s_{1}
, since in this case we have
m+s_{j}=\kappa
. Therefore, switching back to the original notation for
x
, we get

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa-q_{1}}\cdot x=\mu_{\kappa-q_{1}}x-4(\kappa-2q_{1})\sum_{i=0}^{p_{1}-1}\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{i}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-i-1}\mathcal{U}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y.

By Proposition
6.36
(
1
), it follows that

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-i-1}\mathcal{U}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y=\mu_{\kappa}\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-i-1}\mathcal{U}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y,

and thus

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa-q_{1}}\cdot x=\mu_{\kappa-q_{1}}x-4p_{1}\mu_{\kappa}(\kappa-2q_{1})\mathcal{U}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-1}\mathcal{U}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{U}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}y,

which concludes the proof.
∎

The next result is a direct consequence of Propositions
6.35
,
6.36
, and
6.37
.

Corollary 6.38
.

Let
M
be a non-zero submodule of
M_{\psi_{n}}
. Then
M
contains a non-zero element in
U(\mathcal{P})\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}
. ∎

Thanks to Corollary
6.38
, we can simply focus on elements of
M_{\psi_{n}}
not containing any
\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Thankfully, the removal of
\mathcal{V}_{j}
is much easier, since the
\mathcal{V}_{j}
commute with each other.

Lemma 6.39
.

Let
y=\mathcal{V}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}\in M_{\psi_{n}}
with
-1\leq q_{1}<\dots<q_{k}\leq n-1
. Then

(\mathcal{U}_{m}-\lambda_{m})\cdot y=\begin{cases}-4p_{1}\mu_{\kappa}(\kappa-2q_{1})\mathcal{V}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-1}\mathcal{V}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}},&\text{if }m=\kappa-q_{1},\\
0,&\text{if }m>\kappa-q_{1}.\end{cases}.

Proof.

Rewrite
y
as

y=\mathcal{V}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{\ell}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}},

where
-1\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\dots\leq s_{\ell}\leq n-1
(and thus
s_{1}=q_{1}
). We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{m}\mathcal{V}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{\ell}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}=\mathcal{V}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{\ell}}\mathcal{U}_{m}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{V}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{j-1}}[\mathcal{U}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{s_{j}}]\mathcal{V}_{s_{j+1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{\ell}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}y+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{V}_{s_{1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{j-1}}\Big((m-s_{j}+1)\mathcal{V}_{m+s_{j}+2}-4(m-s_{j})\mathcal{V}_{m+s_{j}}\Big)\mathcal{V}_{s_{j+1}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{s_{\ell}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}}

Suppose
m=\kappa-q_{1}=\kappa-s_{1}
. Then
m+s_{j}>\kappa
if
s_{j}>s_{1}
. Therefore, we only get a contribution from the
j^{\text{th}}
term in the above summation if
s_{j}=s_{1}
, since in this case we have
m+s_{j}=\kappa
. It follows that

\mathcal{U}_{\kappa-q_{1}}\cdot y=\lambda_{\kappa-q_{1}}y-4p_{1}\mu_{\kappa}(\kappa-2q_{1})\mathcal{V}_{q_{1}}^{p_{1}-1}\mathcal{V}_{q_{2}}^{p_{2}}\dots\mathcal{V}_{q_{k}}^{p_{k}}\cdot 1_{\psi_{n}},

which concludes the proof. The case where
m>\kappa-q_{1}
follows similarly.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
6.28
.

Proof of Theorem
6.28
.

One direction of the statement is simply Lemmas
6.29
and
6.31
. For the converse, assume that
\mu_{2n}\neq 0
or
\mu_{2n-1}\neq 0
, and let
M
be a non-zero submodule of
M_{\psi_{n}}
. Using Corollary
6.38
, choose an element

. Now apply Lemma

to

repeatedly to deduce that
M
contains a non-zero scalar multiple of the generating vector

of

, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.40
.

One may wonder about using the methods of
[
CGL+25
]
to prove Theorems
6.6
and
6.28
. The main issue is that
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
are not ideals of
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathfrak{b}
for
n\geq 0
, making it impossible to apply their results directly, since quasi-Whittaker functions must be defined on ideals of the Lie algebra (recall Definition
2.12
). However,
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
are ideals of
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}
, so one could get some irreducibility results by inducing from
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}
or
\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}
to
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}
or
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}
and analysing the Whittaker annihilator, as is done in
[
CGL+25
]
. However, deducing the irreducibility of the universal Whittaker module
M_{\varphi_{n}}
or
M_{\psi_{n}}
from the irreducibility of
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{O}}\Bbbk_{\varphi_{n}}
or
\Ind_{\mathcal{F}_{n}\mathfrak{b}}^{\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathfrak{b}}\Bbbk_{\psi_{n}}
is a non-trivial task which would require similar analysis to our proof of Theorem
6.28
. For this reason, we opted for a more direct proof in the case of
\mathfrak{b}
, and a proof using the orbit method for
\mathcal{O}
.

7.
Future work

In summary, this paper provides new insights into the algebraic structure of the BCCA and studies some of its representations, namely restrictions of representations of larger Lie algebras and Whittaker modules.
We hope our work serves as a useful starting point for further exploration of the BCCA and the related mathematical and physical concepts that one would encounter. We conclude this paper by presenting some of these future research directions:

(1)

The BCCA can be obtained as the Lie algebra contraction of
\Vir
(see
[
BCC+25
, Equation (8)]
). Hence, one could study
\mathfrak{b}
-modules obtained as contractions of
\Vir
-modules. Contractions at the level of representations are essential in Carrollian physics
[
CGOR16
]
, but there is still much that is unclear in this regard. For instance, the contractions of
\Vir\times\Vir
-modules built from “mode algebras” or free fields (i.e.,
BC
-systems, Heisenberg algebra etc.) have been considered in the physics literature
3
3

3

However, the procedure by which these representations are contracted seems different to the one introduced in
[
CGOR16
]
.

(see for example
[
HSXZ22
, Sections 5.1.2 and 5.2]
,
[
BBC+20
, Section 4]
and
[
BBM+24
, Section 3.2.1]
). However, there are still technical subtleties (e.g., issues with “normal-ordering”) when it comes to contracting these infinite-dimensional representations that need to be addressed. Attempting to construct sensible representations of the BCCA as contractions of representations of the Virasoro algebra may help address some of these subtleties. They should also help guide us towards the quantisation of the tensionless open string since we expect such representations to describe the possible spectra of this string. It would be particularly interesting if representations constructed in this manner belong to one of the classes of
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-modules discussed in this paper.

(2)

The above line of enquiry leads naturally to the consideration of restrictions of
\Vir\times\Vir
-modules that are vertex operator algebra (VOA) modules, such as the modules formed from the Heisenberg VOA and
BC
-systems (i.e., semi-infinite wedge representation). The restrictions will not be VOAs, but may still provide field-theoretic insights for the tensionless open string, possibly informing us about its spectrum, quantisation etc. Some of these
\Vir\times\Vir
-modules are well-known examples of highest weight modules, so one should expect that after carefully dealing with normal-ordering ambiguities, their restriction to
\widehat{\mathfrak{b}}
-modules does indeed give rise to the “almost free” modules discussed in Section

. Overall, together with the work in Section
3
, we expect these two strands of exploration to aid in understanding the commutative diagram given in Figure

at the level of Lie algebra representations.

(3)

A similar programme to the one in this paper can be undertaken for higher spin BCCAs
[
BCC+25
, Equation (26)]
.

(4)

One could compute the low-dimensional cohomology of the two-parameter family of higher spin BCCAs, analogous to what was done for the Lie algebras
\mathcal{W}(a,b)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes I(a,b)
(see Definition
2.2
) in
[
GJP11
]
.

(5)

Finally, since the BCCA was very recently discovered in the context of tensionless open string theory, there naturally exists a “supersymmetric BCCA” that is awaiting discovery, and the study of its modules would serve to provide novel insights into the spectrum of the corresponding tensionless open superstring.

Appendix A
More subalgebras of the Witt algebra

Given the results of Subsection
4.2
, it is natural to ask if we get similar subalgebras of the Witt algebra by choosing generators

\mathcal{X}_{1}\coloneqq L_{1}-\lambda L_{-1},\quad\mathcal{X}_{2}\coloneqq L_{2}-\mu L_{-2}

for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{*}
. As the following lemma shows, these elements generate the entirety of
\mathcal{W}
unless
\mu=\lambda^{2}
.

Lemma A.1
.

If
\mu\neq\lambda^{2}
, then
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
generate
\mathcal{W}
.

Proof.

This proof is most easily done with the help of a computer. As such, we omit the tedious computations and instead focus on the main arguments of the proof.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
generated by
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. For
n\geq 3
, inductively define
\mathcal{X}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n-2}[\mathcal{X}_{n-1},\mathcal{X}_{1}]\in\mathfrak{g}
. In this way, we have
\mathcal{X}_{n}=L_{n}+\text{lower degree terms}
for all
n\geq 1
. There is another way to generate an element of degree 5: we have
\mathcal{Y}_{5}\coloneqq[\mathcal{X}_{3},\mathcal{X}_{2}]\in\mathfrak{g}
. It can be checked that

\mathcal{X}_{5}-\mathcal{Y}_{5}=-\frac{16}{3}\lambda L_{3}+\text{lower degree terms},

so we define
\mathcal{Y}_{3}\coloneqq-\frac{3}{16\lambda}(\mathcal{X}_{5}-\mathcal{Y}_{5})\in\mathfrak{g}
. Similarly, we can check that

\mathcal{X}_{3}-\mathcal{Y}_{3}=-\frac{45}{16\lambda}(\lambda^{2}-\mu)L_{1}+\text{lower degree terms}.

Since we assumed that
\mu\neq\lambda^{2}
, we know that
\mathcal{X}_{3}-\mathcal{Y}_{3}\neq 0
(in fact,
\mathcal{X}_{3}=\mathcal{Y}_{3}
if and only if
\mu=\lambda^{2}
). Thus, define
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\coloneqq\frac{16\lambda}{45(\lambda^{2}-\mu)}(\mathcal{X}_{3}-\mathcal{Y}_{3})\in\mathfrak{g}
. Continuing this process, we have

\mathcal{X}_{1}-\mathcal{Y}_{1}=-\frac{16}{15}\lambda L_{-1}+\text{lower degree terms},

so we define
\mathcal{Y}_{-1}\coloneqq-\frac{15}{16\lambda}(\mathcal{X}_{1}-\mathcal{Y}_{1})\in\mathfrak{g}
. Specifically, we have

\mathcal{Y}_{-1}=L_{-1}+\frac{3\mu}{16\lambda}L_{-3}+\frac{\mu}{16}L_{-5}.

We also have the following element of
\mathfrak{g}
:

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq[\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{-1}]=2\lambda L_{0}+\frac{3}{4}\mu L_{-2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\mu}{4}L_{-6}.

Notice that
\mathcal{W}_{\leq 1}\coloneqq\spn\{L_{n}\mid n\leq 1\}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. By
[
Buz23
, Lemma 4.7]
, it follows that
\mathcal{Y}_{-1}
and
\mathcal{Z}
generate a subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}_{\leq 1}
finite codimension. Therefore,
\mathfrak{g}\cap\mathcal{W}_{\leq 1}
has finite codimension in
\mathcal{W}_{\leq 1}
.

By a completely symmetric argument, we also get that
\mathfrak{g}\cap\mathbb{W}_{1}
has finite codimension in
\mathbb{W}_{1}
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
\mathcal{W}
. But now Proposition
4.3
implies that

(A.1)

g^{n}\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\subseteq g\mathcal{W}

for some
g\in\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]
, and therefore
g
must divide both
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
g\in\Bbbk[t]
,
g
is monic, and
g(0)\neq 0
. Note that
\mathcal{X}_{1}=-(t^{2}-\lambda)\partial
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}=-t^{-1}(t^{4}-\mu)\partial
. But
\gcd(t^{2}-\lambda,t^{4}-\mu)=1
, since
\mu\neq\lambda^{2}
, and therefore
g=1
, since
g
is a common divisor of
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Now (
A.1
) gives that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathcal{W}
.
∎
