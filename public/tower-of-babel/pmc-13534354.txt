Relationship of Adult Cervical Spinous Processes to Vertebral Midline for Cervical Disc Replacement
Authors: Kevin G Liu, Jacob T Wechsler, Daniel Rusu, Aidan Lindgren, Marc A Abdou, Jeffrey C Wang, Raymond J Hah, Ram K Alluri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective Cohort.ObjectivesAccurate, midline artificial disc placement is crucial when performing cervical disc replacement (CDR). Although cervical spinous processes (SPs) are considered midline structures, clinical experience has often shown noticeable deviation at times. This study aims to quantitatively characterize this known observation to better understand the utility of SPs as midline indicators during CDR.MethodsPatients who underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion with preoperative cervical spine computerized tomography (CT) scans were retrospectively identified, excluding those with previously surgically altered spines. Relative lateral deviation at C6 was greater than at C3 (p = 0.027). Introduction

The cervical spine serves multiple crucial purposes, including protecting the spinal cord and supporting the head to maintain forward gaze. Each of the spine’s important functions may be compromised by various pathologies, the most common of which is degenerative disease. With a reported yearly incidence of 27.3% in the United States, degenerative processes often lead to a range of radiculopathic and myelopathic illnesses. 1 While discectomy and fusion is by far the most common surgery used to correct these types of pathologies, cervical disc replacement (CDR) has seen increasing usage over the past few decades. 2 CDR was developed as surgeons began to better understand the role of preserving spinal motion in reducing rates of postoperative morbidities, such as dysphagia and adjacent segment degeneration or disease.3,4

When performing CDR, maximizing the preservation of normal biomechanics requires placement of the artificial disc as centered as possible. This is commonly accomplished using intraoperative fluoroscopy in both the lateral and anteroposterior (AP) planes. 5 On AP view, visual landmarks that may be used to help approximate midline include the edges of the vertebral bodies, uncinate processes, and SPs. However, although the SPs are commonly regarded as midline structures, clinical practice has shown that there can sometimes be considerable lateral deviation of cervical SPs. The fact that most cervical SPs bifurcate at their tips adds an additional layer of uncertainty in how well they may approximate midline. Variations in SP length and bifurcation patterns have been well-described in prior morphometric studies,6-11 but none have quantified the degree of SP deviation from midline as it pertains to being an intraoperative landmark.

As such, we sought to characterize the angular and lateral deviation of cervical SPs to determine whether they may be reliable as intraoperative markers of vertebral midline during procedures like CDR. In this study, we describe the deviation patterns of SPs of C3–C7 and evaluate their utility in the context of being landmarks for guiding artificial disc placement.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective review of electronic medical records was conducted to identify adult patients with CT imaging of unaltered cervical spines. Patients were selected from those who underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion by a single surgeon at a university-affiliated academic medical center between December 2020 and February 2024. Patients were included if they had available preoperative CT scans of the cervical spine prior to surgery. Exclusion criteria included the presence of existing spinal instrumentation, prior surgery that involved clear trauma to or removal of any cervical SP, and CT evidence of ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament. Relevant demographic data and radiographic measurements pertaining to SP deviation were collected. This study was approved by our institutional review board (HS-23-00758).

All measurements were conducted in Synapse (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) using three-dimensional multiplanar reconstruction views of each cervical vertebra. Vertebrae were sectioned such that the uncinate processes and SP were completely visible in the plane. The angle of SP deviation was defined as the angle between an antero-posteriorly drawn midline through the vertebral body and a line drawn from the anterior canal to the tip of the SP. Lateral deviation was defined as the distance between the vertebral midline and a parallel line intersecting the SP tip Figure 1. In cases of bifid SPs, the point between the two cusps was considered the tip. Use of the symmetric uncinate processes helped to account for image rotation at any level. Given known variations in vertebral body size between individuals and by level, percentages of lateral deviation were calculated with respect to the lateral width of the respective vertebral bodies (relative lateral deviation), which was measured as the distance between the medial borders of the transverse foramina. All measurements were collected at each of the C3–C7 vertebral levels in each patient.

Methods for measurement of angular deviation (left) and lateral deviation (right) from midline

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy package (version 1.16.2). The two-sample t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to assess correlations between radiographic values and various groupings. Post hoc testing after ANOVA was conducted using Tukey’s test. All plots are shown as means with error bars representing one standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of the 62 patients included in the study, 64.5% were male, with the average age being 60.7 ± 12.0 years. Averaged radiographic measurements are presented in Table 1. The average angular deviation off midline for each cervical level was 2.94˚ at C3, 3.52˚ at C4, 3.50˚ at C5, 4.31˚ at C6, and 3.53˚ at C7 (Figure 2). The average lateral deviation off midline for each level was 1.38 mm at C3, 1.56 mm at C4, 1.67 mm at C5, 2.37 mm at C6, and 2.51 mm at C7 (Figure 3). The relative lateral deviation was 5.43% at C3, 6.10% at C4, 6.14% at C5, 8.17% at C6, and 7.52% at C7 (Figure 4). Although C7 demonstrated the greatest absolute lateral deviation, when adjusted for vertebral body width, C6 showed the greatest relative lateral deviation. C7 had the largest variation between individuals both for angular deviation (SD = 2.86˚) and for lateral deviation (SD = 2.52 mm and 7.51%), while C3 showed the smallest variation in both angular deviation (SD = 2.06˚) and in lateral deviation (SD = 1.03 mm and 4.04%).

Both angular and lateral deviation showed significant differences when compared by level using ANOVA (p = 0.026 and p < 0.001, respectively). Significance remained when lateral deviations were adjusted to relative values (p = 0.019). On post hoc testing using Tukey’s test, angular deviation at C6 was found to be significantly greater than at C3 (p = 0.009), although no other pairs proved to be significantly different. For lateral deviation, statistically significant pairs included C7 being greater than C3 (p = 0.001), C4 (p = 0.006), and C5 (p = 0.021), and C6 being greater than C3 (p = 0.004) and C4 (p = 0.031). For relative lateral deviation, only C6 being greater than C3 remained significant (p = 0.027). There were no significant differences at any level for angular deviation or lateral deviation (absolute or relative) when results were compared by sex (p > 0.05 for all).

Discussion

Our results show that in adults, there is marked anatomic variability in the orientation and position of cervical SPs between individuals, with C3 having the least variability and C7 having the greatest. These results support the idea that the cervical SPs may not be reliable indicators of vertebral midline due to their commonly variable deviations between individual patients and between levels. Larger magnitudes and variability of SP deviation were noted especially in C6 (4.31 ± 2.15 degrees, 2.37 ± 1.42 mm) and C7 (3.53 ± 2.86 degrees, 2.51 ± 2.52 mm) compared to more cranial levels. This finding remained similar when lateral deviations were adjusted to be relative to their respective vertebral body widths (8.17 ± 4.81% at C6 and 7.52 ± 7.51% at C7). In our sample, it was common to find lateral deviations at C7 between 10–20%, with four patients showing 20% or over and one patient with 40% relative SP deviation at C7. Although many patients do have accurately midline SPs, it is important to note that this is not always the case. As such, in spine procedures such as disc arthroplasty, surgeons should hesitate to use the cervical SPs as sole indicators of midline when gauging proper artificial disc placement and positioning, particularly at more caudal cervical levels.

The variations in SP deviation shown by our results are likely due to asymmetric growth of the primary ossification centers within each vertebra during embryogenesis. Given that the fused neural arch only fully matures after the first few years of life, various biomechanical forces exerted by attached muscles during early life also likely contribute to deviation of the SPs in adulthood.12,13 In addition to deviation of SPs themselves, pairs of bifid cusps also seem to deviate drastically between levels and between patients. The bifid nature of the cervical SP tips stems from secondary ossification centers that mature later in life, by around 25 years. 14 Functionally, the existence of bifid SPs may be to allow for better attachment of the nuchal ligament, which is important in supporting and extending the head.

Our main finding that there is significant variability in SP deviation off midline between patients and between levels shows that the cervical SPs may not always be reliable indicators of midline when precise accuracy is required. Although SPs almost always emerge from midline at their base, it has previously been demonstrated that it is the tips of SPs that produce the shadows seen on AP radiographs. 15 As off-centered placement of an artificial disc leads to asymmetric weight loading and increases the risks of implant subsidence or loosening,16,17 it cannot be recommended to rely only on the positions of the SPs for proper disc placement. Other commonly used radiographic landmarks include the uncinate processes and medial border of the pedicles, which have been found to be accurate in determining midline; 18 however, use of these requires subjective determination of the midpoint between the landmarks (i.e., “eyeballing”) in an intraoperative setting. Nevertheless, given that lateral deviation of the C3–C5 SPs are lower in magnitude and standard deviation compared to at C6 and C7, these higher levels may be used with more confidence (in combination with other landmarks) when attempting to assess accurate midline positioning, with the presumption of obtaining a true AP radiograph.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may limit power to detect further significant differences, if present, between subgroups and between vertebral levels. Despite this, we were still able to detect strongly significant differences between the studied radiographic characteristics, supporting the veracity of these results. Additionally, the fact that our cohort was derived from patients who received operative treatment may have led to biased results, although given that SP deviation is not currently known to play a role in degenerative spine changes, it is unlikely that these parameters would be impacted by this selection criteria. Finally, manual collection of radiographic variables inherently leads to potential measurement error. However, this error was minimized through the use of a strict, standardized radiographic measurement protocol that relied heavily on clear anatomical landmarks.

Conclusion

Although the cervical SPs are considered midline structures, there is significant variation in deviation from midline between patients and between levels at C3–C7. Given the requirement for accurate midline disc placement during CDR, surgeons should hesitate to use the cervical SPs as sole landmarks for guiding proper positioning. However, among the studied levels, the C3–C5 SPs showed the lowest deviation and thus may be more useful as radiographic landmarks when used in conjunction with the uncinate processes or medial border of the pedicles during midline disc placement. Further research utilizing intraoperative fluoroscopic views in addition to morphometrics via CT should be conducted to better elucidate the clinical utility of these findings.
