Machine-learning guided engineering of Mo4+ activated halide near-infrared phosphors for AI-augmented medical imaging
Authors: Tao Huang, Bingzhen Wang, Lina Yang, Quan Niu, Bingsuo Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping highly efficient lead-free near-infrared (NIR) phosphors with strong thermal stability is a key challenge in material design and optoelectronics applications. Here, a machine-learning (ML) guided co-doping strategy to construct a broadband NIR-emitting phosphor, Cs2Zr(Cl0.46Br0.54)6:12%Mo4+/3.6%Sb3+ (SM-CZCB) is reported, achieving record-high internal and external quantum efficiencies of 92.4% and 65.9% at 920 nm, respectively. Guided by ML, Sb3+ and Br- were selected to co-dope and synergistically enhance energy transfer through the spin-orbit coupling, d-d correlation, and lattice distortion to enhance NIR emission of Mo4+. Notably, a [SbCl6]3+-[ZrCl6]2--[MoCl6]2- sequential energy transfer chain form a near-resonant configuration to reach the emission centers. The fabricated NIR light-emitting diode using SM-CZCB exhibits a record-high power conversion efficiency of 27.07% with an operational T50 exceeding 4000 hours at 450 nm excitation. Moreover, the AI-enhanced biomedical imaging was demonstrated using NIR light with high-resolution. This marks the integration of AI-guided material design with practical AI-enhanced medical imaging.Subject terms: Optical materials and structures; Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Broadband near-infrared (NIR) phosphor materials hold significant potential in biomedical imaging, medical diagnostics, night vision, remote sensing, and optical communication1–5. In particular, the spectral range of 800–1400 nm offers distinct advantages such as long lifetime, deep tissue penetration, low autofluorescence, and minimal thermal damage, making it a key window for deep, non-invasive, and high-resolution imaging applications6–8. However, most existing NIR phosphors are based on Cr3+ or rare-earth ions (e.g., Nd3+ and Er3+), each with distinct limitations9,10. Cr3+-activated NIR phosphors are predominantly fabricated via conventional solid-state reactions in tube furnaces, which typically require high temperatures (often >1000 °C) and prolonged thermal treatment, resulting in energy-intensive and relatively complex processing11,12. Although alternative lower-temperature routes have been reported—such as molten-salt/flux-assisted synthesis (generally several hundred degrees Celsius) and solvothermal/soft-chemistry methods (∼200 °C)—these approaches still involve elevated-temperature/pressurized conditions and are less straightforward for rapid, room temperature, large-batch production13,14. In addition, while several studies have reported that Cr3+-activated phosphors can be synthesized in air without detectable Cr6+15,16, Cr3+ may still be oxidized to toxic Cr6+ species under certain synthesis or processing conditions17,18. The emission properties of Cr3+ are highly dependent on rigid crystal fields, making them unsuitable for soft or low-dimensional hosts. Most importantly, Cr³⁺ emitters generally show emission wavelengths below 900 nm, making them incompatible with the second biological window (900–1300 nm), which is critical for deep-tissue imaging19–21. Rare-earth phosphors exhibit stable energy levels, but their 4f-4f transitions are spin- and parity-forbidden, resulting in low quantum efficiency. In addition, they exhibit narrow emission bands with limited tunability, which restricts their use in broadband imaging and communication22–24.

In contrast, transition metal ions such as Mo4+ and Mn2+ offer high radiative transition probabilities, long lifetimes, and broadband emissions due to d-d transitions. Their emissions can strongly couple with phonons, enabling excellent spectral tunability25–27. Among these, Mo4+ ions are particularly promising for generating broadband NIR emission with high intensity and thermal robustness28. The vacancy-ordered double perovskite Cs2ZrCl6 (CZC), a lead-free halide derivative, has emerged as a promising host due to its low toxicity, high structural stability, and tunable electronic structure29,30. However, it exhibits no intrinsic NIR emission, and introducing functional dopants is essential to activate NIR luminescence. To address this challenge, we propose a structure-function regulation strategy based on the synergistic co-doping of Sb3+, Br−, and Mo4+. In this approach, Sb3+ modulates the band structure by aligning the energy levels of [ZrCl6]2− and [MoCl6]2−, enabling a near-resonant configuration that facilitates energy transfer. Br- enhances spin–orbit coupling (SOC), breaks crystal symmetry, and suppresses the formation of Mo5+ impurity states. Together, Sb3+ and Br− co-doping induces strong lattice distortion and boosts electron-phonon coupling, enhancing the probability and thermal stability of Mo4+ d-d transitions. However, introducing multiple dopants narrows the compositional window and significantly increases experimental complexity. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted materials design has become a powerful tool for navigating such high-dimensional parameter spaces31.

Here, a machine learning model trained on experimental and theoretical data is employed to predict the optimal concentrations and combinations of Mo/Sb/Br co-doping, thereby greatly accelerating material optimization. Using this approach, we successfully synthesized a high-performance lead-free NIR phosphor, Cs2Zr(Cl0.46Br0.54)6:12%Mo4+/3.6%Sb3+ (denoted as SM-CZCB), which achieves a record-high internal quantum efficiency (PLQY) of 92.4% and external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 65.9% at 920 nm. SM-CZCB exhibits a ~20% higher PL intensity than the optimal composition obtained through orthogonal design. The material maintains 88% of its emission intensity at 420 K, demonstrating excellent thermal quenching resistance and environmental stability. When integrated into a near-infrared light-emitting diode (NIR-LED) device, SM-CZCB achieves a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 27.07% under 450 nm blue-light excitation, with an operational lifetime exceeding 4000 h. More importantly, to overcome the limited spatial resolution of conventional NIR medical imaging, we combined the NIR-LED with a cutting-edge AI image reconstruction algorithm based on diffusion models. This AI-enhanced NIR imaging system enables clear visualization of blood vessels through more than 11 cm of tissue, representing a major advance in resolution and penetration depth. This work represents the demonstration of AI-guided material design combined with intelligent medical imaging, providing a paradigm for integrating functional materials, AI algorithms, and optoelectronic devices. All relevant code for the machine learning and image reconstruction models is publicly available, promoting data sharing and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Results

CZC was selected as the host matrix and doped with non-toxic, NIR-active Mo4+ ions. To further enhance emission performance, Sb3+ and Br- were co-doped to achieve a synergistic effect. As shown in Fig. 1a, the materials were synthesized via a rapid room temperature co-precipitation method. This method is simple and scalable, enabling the formation of a large quantity of product at room temperature within 10 s (Supplementary Movie 1), showing promise for mass production. CZC adopts a typical vacancy-ordered double perovskite structure, where one Zr and six Cl- form an isolated [ZrCl6]2− octahedron. These units are spatially separated by Cs+ cations, resulting in a zero-dimensional configuration favorable for localized excitation. Mo4+ can substitute Zr4+ in the octahedral sites to form [MoCl6]2− emission centers. Since CZC shares the same structure with Cs2MoCl6, the [MoCl6]2− units exhibit good compatibility with the host lattice28,32. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of x%Mo:CZC samples with varying Mo doping levels are shown in Fig. 1b. All samples maintained the original CZC structure without noticeable impurity phases, indicating good dopant solubility. A slight redshift in the diffraction peaks was observed with increasing Mo⁴⁺ content, due to the smaller ionic radius of Mo4+ (0.65 Å) compared to Zr4+ (0.72 Å), which causes lattice contraction. In contrast, Mo/Sb co-doped (Supplementary Fig. 1) and Mo/Br co-doped (Supplementary Fig. 2) samples exhibited a slight blueshift, attributed to the larger ionic radii of Sb3+ (0.76 Å) and Br− compared to Zr4+ and Cl−, respectively33. Fig. 1c presents the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images of the SM-CZCB. The Mo, Sb, Cl, and Br elements are homogeneously distributed without aggregation, indicating uniform incorporation into the lattice. Figure 1d compares the XRD patterns of SM-CZCB and pristine CZC, confirming that the crystal structure remains intact after co-doping.

The unit cell parameters of Cs2Zr(Cl0.46Br0.54)6:12% Mo4+/3.6%Sb3+ was provided to enable a more complete structural analysis. The phase was refined by Rietveld refinement of powder X-ray diffraction data (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 1), yielding satisfactory agreement factors, confirming the reliability of the refined structural model. The refined lattice parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Figure 1e shows the high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of Mo, Sb, Cl, and Br in SM-CZCB. Peaks at 231.5 and 234.6 eV correspond to Mo4+ (3d5/2 and 3d3/2), confirming the +4 oxidation state of Mo32. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) measurements (Supplementary Table 2) showed that the actual concentrations of Mo4+ and Br- closely match the nominal values, whereas Sb3+ was significantly under-incorporated. This is likely due to its charge mismatch with Zr4+, which limits its solubility.

To investigate the dopant spatial distribution, we constructed multiple site occupation models using density functional theory (DFT) and calculated their formation energies (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). The results reveal that two Mo4+ ions preferentially occupy second-nearest-neighbor Zr4+ sites. In the presence of Sb3+, it tends to locate between the two Mo4+ ions as a nearest neighbor. This bridging-type cooperative occupation lowers the formation energy and facilitates an efficient energy transfer path from [ZrCl6]2− to [MoCl6]2−. DFT-relaxed structures further confirm that Sb³⁺ doping shortens the distance between [ZrCl6]2− and [MoCl6]2− octahedra (Supplementary Fig. 7), thereby enhancing orbital overlap and reducing the energy transfer barrier. The photoluminescence (PL) and excitation spectra of pristine CZC are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. Under 250 nm excitation, it exhibits blue emission with a lifetime of 11.87 μs (Supplementary Fig. 9), attributed to self-trapped exciton (STE) emission from the [MoCl6]2− octahedra. Figure 1f shows the PL spectra of Mo4+-doped CZC under 365 nm excitation. Two asymmetric NIR peaks are observed at 914 and 976 nm, corresponding to the d-d transitions (3T2g → 3T1g) of Mo4+ (Supplementary Fig. 10). These emissions are split due to local crystal field perturbations and structural distortions, especially at low temperatures, as discussed later. As the Mo concentration increases, PL intensity increases and reaches a maximum at 12% Mo (denoted M-CZC), followed by a concentration quenching effect. Figure 1g shows the PL decay curves of Mo-doped samples, with the calculation method detailed in Supplementary Note 1. The lifetime remains around 45 μs across all doping levels (Supplementary Table 3), indicating that Mo doping has a minimal effect on the excited-state lifetime. The data were fitted using a biexponential function, and the residuals are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11. This is consistent with the d2 configuration of Mo4+, which typically leads to microsecond-scale decay, in contrast to the nanosecond-scale decay of Mo5+ with a d1 configuration27. To further clarify why the PL spectral shape and intensity evolve with Mo4+ concentration while the lifetime remains nearly unchanged, time-resolved emission spectroscopy was performed on samples with different Mo4+ contents (Supplementary Fig. 12). The time-resolved spectra collected at different delay times exhibit nearly identical spectral profiles after normalization, and spectrally resolved decay curves extracted at different emission wavelengths overlap well and yield comparable lifetimes within experimental uncertainty (Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, the steady-state PL spectra of samples with different Mo4+ concentrations collapse onto an identical curve after normalization (Supplementary Fig. 14), indicating that the intrinsic emission profile of Mo4+ centers is preserved. The temperature-dependent spectra at different concentrations also show nearly identical shapes (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results collectively demonstrate that the concentration-dependent PL variations mainly originate from changes in the population of emissive Mo4+ centers rather than modifications of the intrinsic radiative or nonradiative decay rates. Further experiments showed that both Sb3+ and Br- co-doping can enhance Mo4+ emission (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). The strongest PL signals were observed in 4.2%Sb/12%Mo:CZC (SM-CZC) and 12%Mo:Cs2Zr(Cl0.52Br0.48)6 (M-CZCB).

A comprehensive dataset was constructed by systematically varying the Sb3+ and Br- co-doping concentrations in M-CZC and recording the corresponding emission intensities. Specifically, the Sb3+ doping ratio ranged from 0 to 0.078 and the Br- doping ratio from 0 to 0.78. For each (Sb3+, Br−) pair, the emission intensity was measured under identical synthesis and characterization conditions to ensure data reliability and comparability. In total, over 80 unique data points were collected, comprehensively covering the relevant compositional space (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the transformer-based regression model exhibits predictive performance for the emission intensity of Mo(Ⅳ)-activated halide phosphors. The AI-predicted intensity contour map accurately locates the optimal Br− and Sb3+ dopant concentrations for maximum NIR emission. Furthermore, the parity plot (Fig. 2b) shows a strong linear correlation between predicted and experimental values, with R² values reaching 0.995 and 0.996 for the training and test sets, respectively. These results validate the high reliability and robustness of the model, enabling precise compositional optimization and efficient design of high-performance NIR phosphors. Furthermore, multiple compositions near the predicted optimal doping ratios were synthesized experimentally, serving as a direct validation of the AI predictions. As shown in Fig. 2c, the PL spectra indicate that the SM-CZCB sample exhibits the highest emission intensity, closely matching the machine learning model’s prediction. Notably, its PL intensity is nearly 20% higher than that of the best-performing sample identified via traditional orthogonal design (12%Mo/4.2%Sb3+:Cs2Zr(Cl0.52Br0.48)6).

The UV-vis absorption spectra of CZC, S-CZC, M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB are presented in Fig. 2d. Pristine CZC displays an absorption edge around 300 nm. In S-CZC, two additional absorption bands appear in the 290–350 nm and 350–420 nm ranges, corresponding to Sb3+ transitions of 1S0 → 1P1 and 1S0 → 3P1, respectively33. For M-CZC and SM-CZC, a distinct absorption peak at 320 nm is assigned to the Zr-Cl-Mo charge-transfer transition27. Additional Mo4+-related absorption peaks are observed at ~400, 524, and 780 nm, attributed to d-d transitions of 3T1(t2g) → 3T1, 3T1(t2g) → 3T2(t2e), and 3T1(t2g) → 1E/1T1, respectively. A peak at 358 nm may also originate from Sb³⁺. For SM-CZCB, the absorption edge undergoes a notable redshift due to Br− incorporation, accompanied by significant broadening in the high-energy region, which is attributed to enhanced spin–orbit SOC induced by Br−. Figure 2e shows the excitation (PLE) and PL spectra of M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB. The PLE curves are generally consistent with the absorption spectra. For SM-CZCB, the maximum excitation peak redshifts to 400 nm, with a pronounced enhancement at 450 nm, facilitating compatibility with commercial blue-light excitation sources. The PL spectra of M-CZC and SM-CZC show dual emission peaks at ~914 and 976 nm, while SM-CZCB displays a simplified single band centered at ~920 nm. This spectral simplification is attributed to the Br−-induced SOC enhancement, which promotes energy level mixing, breaks the symmetry of the MoCl₆ octahedra, and strengthens electron-phonon coupling. These effects collectively reduce d-orbital splitting, resulting in wider emission profiles11,25. The redshift in the absorption and PLE spectra of SM-CZCB is due to the substitution of Cl− with Br−, which narrows the host bandgap due to their M-X charge transfer nature. The PL emission band originates from Mo4+ d-d transition, with unchanged positions, as it is only affected by the crystal fields. Compared to M-CZC, SM-CZCB exhibits significantly stronger NIR emission. To further investigate this enhancement, PL decay lifetime measurements were performed at 920 nm (Fig. 2f). The decay lifetime of SM-CZC reaches 72.44 μs (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 20), ~1.58× that of M-CZC (45.60 μs), indicating more efficient energy transfer from the [ZrCl6]2− host to [MoCl6]2− activators upon Sb3+ doping34. In contrast, the SM-CZCB sample shows a slightly faster decay, suggesting that Br- incorporation facilitates radiative transitions by relaxing spin selection rules in d-d transitions35,36. Additional PL lifetime measurements at 450 nm were conducted for CZC, M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB (Fig. 2g). The lifetimes decrease from 11.87 μs (CZC) to 7.94 μs (M-CZC), and further to 4.27 μs (SM-CZC), indicating progressive quenching of STE emission from [ZrCl6]2- units due to more efficient energy transfer to [MoCl6]2−. The energy transfer efficiency increases from 33.1% in M-CZC to 64% in SM-CZC (Fig. 2h), nearly doubling after Sb3+ co-doping. Supplementary Fig. 21 further confirms this trend: as Mo4+ doping increases, the host emission at 450 nm diminishes more significantly in the presence of Sb3+. The role of Sb3+ as a non-emissive energy-transfer mediator was verified through PL spectra of S-CZC and Mo4+-doped S-CZC (Supplementary Fig. 22). Notably, the Sb³⁺ emission band disappears with just 1% Mo4+ addition, indicating complete energy funneling from Sb3+ to Mo4+ without independent radiative output from Sb3+ itself. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2i) further confirms the successful incorporation of dopants and their impact on lattice distortion. Pristine CZC shows two peaks at 159 and 322 cm−1, corresponding to the T2g and A1g vibrational modes of Zr-Cl. In SM-CZC, additional weak peaks at 231 and 259 cm−1 appear, attributed to the Eg vibrational modes of Mo-Cl and Sb-Cl bonds, respectively32,37. This provides structural evidence that both Mo4+ and Sb3+ substitute into Zr4+ sites. In SM-CZCB, the Mo-Cl Raman mode becomes significantly broader and asymmetric, indicating severe distortion of the [MoCl6]2− octahedra due to Br- doping. This distortion also implies stronger phonon coupling, further supporting the enhanced electron-phonon interaction inferred from PL behavior. The characteristic lattice phonon modes of the CZC host exhibit negligible shifts or linewidth broadening upon Mo/Sb/Br co-doping, indicating that the host lattice framework remains largely preserved. Only localized vibrational modes associated with Mo-Cl/Br and Sb-Cl bonds appear, without the emergence of additional low-energy phonons. This suggests that co-doping does not promote enhanced multiphonon nonradiative relaxation.

Recent reports have pointed out that distinguishing Mo4+ in ([MoCl6]2− from Mo5+-containing molybdenum oxychlorides (e.g., [MoCl5O]2−) can be ambiguous when relying on conventional characterization techniques alone38,39. To address this issue, we compared our samples with a reference Cs2Mo(OxCl1-x)6 compound. Cs2Mo(OxCl1-x)6 exhibits a characteristic near-infrared emission band centered at ~970 nm, together with a clear Mo-O Raman vibration. In contrast, the Mo-doped CZC samples show an emission band centered at ~920 nm with a markedly different band shape, and their Raman spectra display only Mo-Cl related vibrational modes, with no detectable Mo-O vibrations (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. S23). Furthermore, Mo K-edge XANES measurements place the absorption edge positions of all samples close to that of MoO2, indicating a molybdenum oxidation state of ~+4 (Supplementary Fig. 24). Taken together, these results support the assignment of Mo4+ in predominantly chloride-coordinated environments in the present system.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed for four representative samples (CZC, M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB) to elucidate the electronic mechanisms governing energy transfer and bandgap modulation via multi-element co-doping. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, the conduction and valence bands of CZC are primarily composed of [ZrCl6]2− octahedra, forming a wide bandgap semiconductor with a conduction band minimum (CBM) at 3.5476 eV and a valence band maximum (VBM) at −0.16 eV. This is consistent with the experimentally observed STE emission under 250 nm excitation. After Mo4+ doping, the CBM in M-CZC shifts down to 0.783 eV and is dominated by spin-down [MoCl6]2− states (Fig. 3c, d). The VBM lowers to −0.777 eV, resulting in a narrowed bandgap of 1.56 eV. Although [ZrCl6]2− states remain present above ~1.7 eV, the large energy offset (~0.92 eV) between [ZrCl6]2− and [MoCl6]2− may lead to inefficient nonradiative energy transfer. In SM-CZC, the CBM further decreases to 0.691 eV, and the VBM rises to −0.766 eV, yielding a bandgap of ~1.46 eV (Fig. 3e, f). Importantly, the [ZrCl6]2- state now resides at 0.804 eV—just 0.11 eV above the [MoCl6]2− state—forming a near-resonant alignment that significantly lowers the energy barrier for Zr-to-Mo energy transfer. This modulation arises from the incorporation of Sb³⁺, which possesses a closed-shell 4d¹⁰ configuration. The filled 4d orbitals of Sb³⁺ introduce strong electron correlation effects that perturb the local electrostatic environment. This enhanced electron density around Sb³⁺ induces an overall downward shift of the [ZrCl6]2−-derived conduction band states. Sb3+ itself introduces no significant density of states near the band edges, with its orbitals located above 2.6 eV, confirming its non-emissive role. As supported by experimental results (Supplementary Fig. 25), even trace amounts of Mo⁴⁺ (~1%) are sufficient to completely quench the intrinsic Sb3+ emission. Structural modeling indicates that Sb3+ preferentially occupies sites adjacent to two Mo4+ centers. This spatial configuration bridges [ZrCl6]2− and [MoCl6]2− units, modulates the local crystal field, and facilitates energy migration—an effect we define as a structure-energy-space bridging effect. To validate the uniqueness of Sb³⁺, we also simulated Mo-doped samples co-doped with Te4+, Sn4+ and Bi3+ as alternatives (Supplementary Figs. 26–28). However, none of these three dopants can achieve the favorable modulation observed with Sb3+. In both Te:M-CZC and Sn:M-CZC systems, the CBM comprises hybridized states of Te/Sn, Mo, and Zr, with Zr still dominating. Moreover, both Te2+ and Sn4+ introduce STE states that may act as self-emissive centers, leading to parasitic NIR emission and increased nonradiative losses. These factors further disrupt the energy transfer pathway and reduce spectral purity. In contrast, Sb³⁺ serves as a nonradiative structural modulator, enabling efficient Zr-to-Mo energy migration without introducing undesired emission. Upon Br- incorporation in SM-CZCB, the CBM and VBM are governed by spin-up [Mo(Cl/Br)6]2− states, shifting to 0.58 and −0.30 eV, respectively. The bandgap is further reduced to 0.88 eV. Simultaneously, [Zr(Cl/Br)6]2− states also move downward to ~0.68 eV, maintaining a slight energy offset above [Mo(Cl/Br)6]2−. Br- doping increases lattice distortion and enhances SOC, leading to broader DOS features near the band edges. This is experimentally corroborated by redshifted absorption edges and enhanced excitation intensity near 450 nm in SM-CZCB. Moreover, the broadened bands and mixed states explain the simplified NIR emission in SM-CZCB: the original dual emission peaks (914/976 nm) merge into a single peak at 920 nm due to SOC-induced fusion of 3T2g sublevels in Mo4+. Furthermore, no defect-induced mid-gap states are observed, and the bandgap remains clean upon co-doping. The only states appearing within the gap originate from the intentionally introduced Mo–Cl/Br-related levels. These results indicate that co-doping does not create nonradiative recombination centers associated with defect-assisted carrier trapping.

This was followed by charge density mapping of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) to reveal the spatial confinement of excited carriers (Fig. 4a). In CZC, both orbitals are delocalized over [ZrCl6]2- units. However, in M-CZC, they become highly localized on [MoCl6]2-, indicating stronger spatial confinement of excitons, which supports efficient d-d emission. Figure 4b and Supplementary Fig. 29 show 2D total charge density maps and 1D profiles along the (010) plane. As Sb3+ and Br- are introduced, charge density becomes increasingly concentrated at Mo4+ sites, while Zr4+ sites show reduced density. This trend is validated by Bader charge analysis (Fig. 4c), where Mo4+ receives more electron density upon Sb3+/Br− doping, confirming its enhanced role as the luminescent center. Figure 4d compares optimized octahedral geometries, and Fig. 4e quantifies octahedral distortion via bond-length distortion (Δd) and bond-angle variance (σ2, Supplementary Table 4) based on the equations in Supplementary Note 2. CZC exhibits near-ideal octahedra with minimal Δd and σ2. With Mo4+ and Sb3+ doping, both metrics increase significantly, especially σ2, reflecting stronger crystal field asymmetry. Br− co-doping in SM-CZCB amplifies these distortions, with Δd and σ2 increasing by several orders of magnitude, indicating enhanced lattice softness. Such structural distortions also intensify electron-phonon coupling, facilitating phonon-assisted transitions. This coordinated tuning of structure and energetics lays the foundation for synergistic improvements in both emission efficiency and thermal robustness40.

Mo K-edge XANES and EXAFS analyses have been added to experimentally probe the local coordination environment of Mo4+ and to validate the DFT-predicted changes in bond lengths and local distortion induced by Sb3+ co-doping and Br− substitution. An enlarged view of the normalized Mo K-edge XANES spectra (Supplementary Fig. 24b) reveals a clear increase in pre-edge peak intensity in the order SM-CZCB > SM-CZC > M-CZC. This pre-edge feature is known to be enhanced by reduced local symmetry and increased octahedral distortion6. The observed evolution, therefore, indicates a progressive distortion of the [MoCl6]2- octahedra induced by Sb3+ co-doping and further Br- substitution. Supplementary Fig. 30 presents the magnitude of the Fourier-transformed Mo K-edge EXAFS spectra in R space (1.2–2.4 Å), with fitting details provided in Supplementary Note 3. A clear and systematic rightward shift of the first-shell peak is observed, following the order: SM-CZCB > SM-CZC > M-CZC. Since this peak predominantly arises from Mo–Cl/Br scattering, the shift unambiguously indicates an increase in the average Mo–ligand bond length upon Sb3+ and Br⁻ incorporation. To further quantify the local coordination environment, we performed detailed EXAFS fitting in R space using theoretical scattering paths (Fig. 4f–h). The fitting results are summarized in Supplementary Table 5 and provide direct experimental parameters: For M-CZC, the Mo-Cl coordination number is refined to ~5.98 with an average bond length of 2.417 Å, consistent with a nearly regular [MoCl6]2- octahedron. This value agrees well with the DFT-relaxed structure, which predicts six Mo-Cl bonds of 2.448 Å (×2) and 2.403 Å (×4), yielding an average Mo-Cl distance of ~2.418 Å. Upon Sb3+ co-doping (SM-CZC), the Mo-Cl coordination remains essentially unchanged (CN ≈ 5.97), while the average Mo-Cl bond length increases to 2.423 Å accompanied by a larger Debye–Waller factor (σ²), indicating enhanced static distortion39. Correspondingly, DFT predicts six inequivalent Mo-Cl bonds with an averaged bond length of ~2.421 Å, in excellent agreement with the experimental EXAFS result. In contrast, SM-CZCB exhibits a clear splitting of the first-coordination-shell peak into Mo-Cl (CN ≈ 3.65, R = 2.416 Å) and Mo-Br (CN ≈ 2.32, R = 2.586 Å) contributions, providing direct experimental evidence for partial Cl− → Br− substitution and the formation of a mixed-ligand [Mo(Cl1-xBrx)6]2− environment. Notably, DFT predicts three Mo-Cl bonds (average ~2.413 Å) and three longer Mo-Br bonds (average ~2.577 Å), which closely match the EXAFS-derived bond lengths. The significantly larger σ² associated with the Mo-Br path further reflects increased local disorder and distortion.

PL behaviors under 405 nm continuous-wave (CW) laser excitation were systematically analyzed for M-CZC, M-CZCB, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB to identify the emission origin and co-doping effects. (Fig. 5a). All samples exhibited broad NIR emission centered near 900 nm, which is attributed to Mo4+ d-d transitions. However, an additional narrow emission band at 717 nm was observed in M-CZC and SM-CZC, particularly in the latter. This peak completely disappeared in M-CZCB and SM-CZCB, suggesting a different origin, likely related to trace defect states or impurity-induced emissions. To clarify the nature of these emissions, we measured the power-dependent PL spectra of SM-CZC and SM-CZCB (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). The main emission at ~900 nm increased linearly with excitation power in both samples, indicating a non-defect-related, intrinsic radiative transition of Mo4+. In contrast, the 717 nm emission in SM-CZC gradually saturated with increasing power, showing the typical behavior of defect or impurity emissions. To identify possible origins, we constructed a Cl− vacancy model (Supplementary Fig. 33) and calculated the defect density of states (Fig. 5c). The Cl− vacancy introduced shallow states near the VBM, but did not support radiative transitions around 717 nm. Additionally, Cl- vacancy-related emissions in CZC are usually broadband and persist under Xe-lamp excitation41. However, the 717 nm peak only appeared under laser excitation, further excluding Cl− vacancies as the origin. Mo5+ ions are known to exhibit weak red emissions (650–750 nm) through the 3T2g → 1A1g transition in octahedral fields42,43. Although these transitions are typically too weak to observe under standard conditions, the saturation behavior of the 717 nm peak suggests that it likely arises from trace amounts of Mo5+. Mo5+ exists even without Sb3+. Upon Sb3+ doping, the charge mismatch with Zr4+ promotes further oxidation of Mo4+ to Mo5+. This behavior is supported by Mo 3d XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 34), which reveals a reduced Mo5+ contribution in SM-CZCB compared with M-CZC and SM-CZC, indicating that Br incorporation effectively suppresses the formation of Mo5+ species. Upon Br− co-doping, the 717 nm emission was entirely suppressed in SM-CZCB, implying that Br- facilitates lattice charge compensation, stabilizes Mo4+, and effectively eliminates unwanted impurity (Mo5+) states. This leads to higher energy utilization for the desired Mo4+ NIR transition. We further compared the optical efficiencies of M-CZC and SM-CZCB, including PLQY, EQE, and absorption efficiency (ABS) (Supplementary Figs. 35–37), and calculation details are provided in Supplementary Note 4. Under optimal 365 nm excitation, M-CZC showed moderate performance (PLQY: 36.34%, EQE: 16.47%, ABS: 45.32%). In contrast, SM-CZCB achieved significant improvement under 400 nm excitation, with a PLQY of 92.41%, ABS of 71.41%, and EQE of 65.99%. More importantly, under commercial 450 nm blue-light excitation, it maintained a PLQY of 87.98% and EQE of 52.66%. The normalized PLE spectra (Supplementary Fig. 38) of M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB show a pronounced enhancement of the excitation band at 450 nm upon Sb3+ doping, which is further intensified by Br⁻ substitution. EXAFS analyses and DFT calculations reveal that Sb3+ and Br− co-doping progressively distort the [MoCl6]2− octahedra, reducing local symmetry and relaxing the parity-forbidden Mo4+ d-d transitions. This distortion-dominated mechanism enables efficient absorption and NIR emission under 450 nm excitation. It is worth noting that although Cr3+-based NIR phosphors can exhibit very high photoluminescence quantum yields, in some cases approaching ~99%, their emission peaks are typically located below 900 nm44. Consequently, their applicability in longer-wavelength NIR optoelectronics and bio-relevant spectral windows remains limited. By contrast, SM-CZCB combines high efficiency with ultrabroad NIR emission and delivers simultaneously high PLQY and EQE, which are among the highest values reported to date for phosphors operating in the >900 nm NIR-I region, together with strong absorption and excellent excitation compatibility with commercial blue LEDs. Compared with reported NIR-emitting halide and Cr3+-doped and other phosphors (Supplementary Table 6), SM-CZCB exhibits record performance among NIR phosphors with emission wavelengths beyond 900 nm.

To further explore the thermal stability and temperature-dependent PL behavior of the materials, temperature-dependent PL measurements were carried out for M-CZC, SM-CZC, and SM-CZCB over the 80–480 K range (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 15). As shown in Fig. 5f, the integrated PL intensities of all three samples exhibit non-monotonic temperature dependence: PL intensities decrease from 80 to 220 K, show an anomalous enhancement between 220–320 K, and then drop again above 320 K. Temperature-dependent PXRD measurements on M-CZC in the range of 180–350 K to examine the possibility of a structural phase transition (Supplementary Fig. 39). The results reveal that an additional diffraction peak appears at ~22.6° below 220 K and disappears upon heating above 220 K, indicating the occurrence of a temperature-induced phase transition around 220 K. This structural change correlates well with the abnormal temperature-dependent PL behavior observed, where the emission intensity increases with increasing temperature in the 220–320 K range. Therefore, we attribute this anomalous behavior primarily to the phase transition, rather than solely to a thermally activated phonon-assisted exciton process45. The enhancement is particularly pronounced in SM-CZCB, indicating that the Sb3+/Br− co-doping significantly strengthens electron-phonon coupling and facilitates a thermally assisted re-excitation process that improves NIR emission efficiency at elevated temperatures. At 420 K, SM-CZCB maintains 88.2% of its room temperature PL intensity, whereas M-CZC drops to 67.7%, confirming the superior thermal stability of SM-CZCB and its promise for high-temperature applications. When using integrated intensity to assess thermal stability, the emission at 420 K retains over 90% of its room temperature value (Supplementary Fig. 40), which is higher than that derived from peak intensity due to spectral broadening at elevated temperatures. At 80 K, three sharp emission peaks were observed (Supplementary Figs. 41–43), with consistent excitation spectra (Supplementary Fig. 44) and identical PL lifetimes (Supplementary Fig. 45), confirming they originate from vibronic transitions of the same 3T2g excited state of Mo4+. To quantify the electron-phonon coupling strength, the temperature-dependent full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the main emission was fitted using the Huang-Rhys model to extract the Huang-Rhys factor (S) and the associated phonon energy ℏω (calculation method detailed in Supplementary Note 5), as shown in Fig. 5g. Given the transition near 220 K, the fitting was performed separately in the 80–220 K and 220–480 K regions. In the low-temperature region, SM-CZCB exhibited an S value of 4.09, higher than that of M-CZC (3.52), with ℏω ≈ 28 meV, corresponding to the Mo-Cl stretching phonon mode (see Fig. 2i). In the high-temperature region, SM-CZCB also maintained a stronger coupling (S = 2.95) than M-CZC (S = 2.46), with a phonon energy of ≈40 meV, consistent with the A1g mode of Zr-Cl vibrations. These results indicate that co-doping not only enhances electron-phonon interactions in the Mo-Cl coordination environment but also strengthens coupling within the local crystal field of the [MoCl6]2− octahedron. Furthermore, thermal quenching of the main NIR emission band was modeled using the Arrhenius equation to estimate the activation energy (Eₐ) (Fig. 5h). The Eₐ value of SM-CZCB was 367 meV, nearly twice that of M-CZC (183 meV), indicating a much more thermally stable excited state and explaining its excellent emission retention at high temperature. Figure 5i compares the thermal stability of SM-CZCB with several state-of-the-art NIR-emitting materials, using the I420K/I300K ratio as a metric. SM-CZCB not only shows superior thermal stability but also offers broadband emission around 920 nm, a wavelength range advantageous for deep-tissue imaging and device applications12,19,20,46–56. More importantly, SM-CZCB demonstrates excellent scalability and environmental stability. The material can be synthesized rapidly within 10 s using a room temperature co-precipitation method, with a single-batch yield up to 14 g (Supplementary Fig. 46). Thermogravimetric analysis reveals a decomposition temperature of 650 °C (Supplementary Fig. 47), and the material retains its phase purity and PL intensity after six months of storage under ambient humidity (65%) (Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49), confirming its dual resistance to heat and moisture—critical for practical applications.

We then fabricated a near-infrared NIR-LED using SM-CZCB and a commercial 450 nm blue chip to evaluate its device-level applicability (Fig. 6a). The resulting NIR-LED exhibited strong, broadband electroluminescence (EL) centered around 920 nm under various driving currents ranging from 20 to 700 mA (Fig. 6b, c). The emission intensity increased linearly with current, indicating excellent current responsiveness. In the EL spectra of the SM-CZCB-based pc-LED, the sharp feature near ~700 nm originates from an instrumental artifact. A weak and broad emission appearing in the same region under high drive current is similar to the photoluminescence observed under high-power 405 nm excitation and is attributed to the triplet-state emission of Sb3+. As shown in Fig. 6d, the optical output power rose steadily with current, reaching 14.41 mW at 20 mA and 254.66 mW at 500 mA. Although the PCE slightly decreased at higher currents, it remained above 15.2% under 500 mA. Notably, under low-power excitation (20 mA), the device achieved a high PCE of 27.07%, indicating efficient photon conversion and minimal loss within the phosphor layer, which, to the best of our knowledge, represents the highest value reported for 920 nm near-infrared LEDs. The device’s thermal stability was evaluated by monitoring the surface temperature during operation (Fig. 6e). Even under high current density, the temperature rise was moderate, indicating good thermal management. At 500 mA, the surface temperature reached only ~76 °C, and the SM-CZCB phosphor retained over 95% of its room temperature emission, confirming its excellent thermal robustness. We further tested the electrical reliability through alternating current stress cycles between 20 and 500 mA (Fig. 6f). The EL intensity remained stable throughout, demonstrating strong tolerance to electrical and thermal stress. In addition, continuous operation at 20 mA showed a T50 lifetime exceeding 4000 h (Fig. 6g), indicating excellent long-term durability and practical viability. To benchmark its performance, the SM-CZCB-based NIR-LED was compared with state-of-the-art NIR emitters (Fig. 6h)8,29,46,47,57–59. At 20 mA, the PCE reached 27.07%, significantly outperforming most reported halide-based NIR-LEDs (the PCE of commercial 450 nm chips is ~35.8%, Supplementary Table 7). Even at 700 mA, the PCE remained above 11% (Supplementary Table 8), highlighting superior PCE retention. The overall device performance not only exceeds that of typical halide NIR-LEDs but also surpasses several Cr3+-doped oxide phosphors recently reported (Supplementary Table 9), underscoring the engineering potential of SM-CZCB for next-generation high-efficiency NIR optoelectronics.

To further demonstrate the practical potential of SM-CZCB in high-performance near-infrared (NIR) light-emitting devices, we applied the SM-CZCB-based NIR-LED to real-world scenarios such as vascular imaging and night vision systems. As illustrated in Fig. 7a, the imaging workflow involves using the SM-CZCB@LED as the NIR source, which emits broadband NIR light capable of penetrating human tissue. A NIR-sensitive camera captures the transmitted light to generate raw vascular images of the hand. For post-acquisition image enhancement, we employed the pre-trained RETI-Diff diffusion model60, which utilizes a generative denoising process trained on large-scale biomedical datasets. This model enables high-fidelity recovery of fine vascular and anatomical features from raw NIR images. Compared with conventional denoising or CNN-based methods, RETI-Diff offers superior performance in preserving structural integrity, suppressing background noise, and improving overall contrast. Notably, this approach requires minimal domain-specific fine-tuning, further broadening its applicability in real-world scenarios. Figure 7c presents the raw and AI-enhanced NIR images of human tissue with varying thicknesses: the back of the hand (~3 cm), wrist (~6.2 cm), and upper arm (~11 cm). Notably, even at tissue depths exceeding 11 cm, the 920 nm emission from SM-CZCB penetrates efficiently and enables clear visualization of subcutaneous veins. After enhancement via the diffusion AI model, the image sharpness and contrast are significantly improved, with even fine capillaries clearly resolved. To quantitatively evaluate the spatial resolution of the NIR imaging system, a standard line-pair resolution card was employed. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 50–53, the raw NIR images exhibit resolvable contrast only up to 8 LP/mm at the finger region and 5 LP/mm at the arm region, beyond which adjacent line pairs cannot be clearly distinguished. In contrast, after AI-based image restoration, significant improvements are observed, with spatial frequencies resolvable up to 16 LP/mm at the finger and 7 LP/mm at the arm region. This result indicates that the AI processing effectively restores high-spatial-frequency information in the effective spatial resolution of NIR imaging. These results highlight not only the superior tissue penetration and thermal stability of SM-CZCB, but also mark the integration of AI-driven image reconstruction into inorganic NIR phosphor-based biomedical imaging. This strategy addresses the long-standing limitations of traditional NIR imaging caused by photon scattering and noise, enabling a paradigm of high-performance phosphors + intelligent vision algorithms for medical diagnostics and real-time imaging. Beyond biomedical use, the SM-CZCB@LED also demonstrates great promise in night vision applications. As shown in Fig. 7d, the LED clearly illuminates natural targets such as plants under complete darkness, without the aid of visible light. The resulting images are sharp and ghost-free, revealing the system’s potential for night surveillance, outdoor detection, and passive infrared identification.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a comprehensive strategy to achieve high-performance, scalable, and thermally stable lead-free NIR phosphors by designing a co-doped perovskite-derived material, SM-CZCB. Guided by machine learning predictions, we realized broadband emission centered at 920 nm, with a PLQY of 92.4% and an EQE of 65.9%. Compared to the best-performing composition identified via conventional orthogonal design, SM-CZCB exhibits a ∼20% enhancement in PL intensity. DFT calculations reveal that Sb3+ modulates the electronic band structure by reducing the energy gap between [ZrCl6]2- and [MoCl6]2- units and bridges their spatial arrangement, facilitating efficient resonant energy transfer. Br- co-doping enhances spin–orbit coupling and phonon-assisted transitions while suppressing Mo5+ formation, resulting in improved radiative recombination and reduced nonradiative losses. The resulting SM-CZCB@LED exhibits a PCE of 27.07% under 450 nm excitation, with a T50 exceeding 4000 h. Moreover, we demonstrate the integration of an NIR-emitting LED with a diffusion-model-based AI image reconstruction system for biomedical imaging. This approach enables high-resolution visualization of vascular structures through over 11 cm of human tissue, setting a benchmark for AI-enhanced medical imaging. Our work offers a unified framework that connects AI-guided materials design, photonic device engineering, and intelligent imaging, marking a key step forward in smart optoelectronic innovation.

Methods

Materials

All reagents were used as received without further purification. Cesium chloride (CsCl, 99%), zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl4, 99%), antimony trichloride (SbCl3, ≥99%), molybdenum pentachloride (MoCl5, 99%), and hydrobromic acid (HBr, 48%) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 wt%) was obtained from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory.

Synthesis of Cs2ZrCl6

Cs2ZrCl6 was synthesized via a rapid room temperature co-precipitation method. First, 2 mmol of CsCl was completely dissolved in 3 mL of concentrated HCl to form precursor solution A. Separately, 1 mmol of ZrCl4 was dissolved in 2 mL of HCl to form precursor solution B. While stirring precursor A at room temperature, precursor B was rapidly injected into the solution. Within 10 s, a large amount of white precipitate formed. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (926×g, 1 min), washed thoroughly with ethanol, and dried at 80 °C.

Synthesis of Mo/Sb:Cs2ZrCl6

Mo4+-doped samples were prepared using a similar co-precipitation route. MoCl5 was used as the Mo4+ dopant source. In a typical procedure, 2 mmol of CsCl was dissolved in 3 mL of HCl (precursor A), and 1 mmol of ZrCl4 along with an appropriate amount of MoCl5 (x% molar ratio relative to Zr) was dissolved in 2 mL of HCl (precursor B). Precursor B was rapidly injected into A under stirring, resulting in a light-yellow precipitate. The product was isolated and washed as described above. To obtain Sb3+/Mo4+ co-doped samples, a defined amount of SbCl3 was added to precursor B together with ZrCl4 and MoCl5. The rest of the procedure remained unchanged.

Synthesis of Mo/Sb:Cs2Zr(Cl/Br)6

For Br- incorporation, partial substitution of CsCl with CsBr and the use of HBr as the co-solvent were employed.

Devices fabrications

The NIR-LED devices were fabricated by blending the as-prepared NIR phosphor with phenyl silicone resin, followed by coating the mixture onto a commercial 450 nm blue LED chip. The devices were then thermally cured to form a uniform luminescent layer. The electroluminescence performance, including emission spectra, output power, and light-to-electrical conversion efficiency, was measured using an integrated LED photometric and electrical measurement system (HP9000; Haopu Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd.).

Transformer model parameters

The dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) using stratified sampling to ensure unbiased evaluation. The regression model was implemented in PyTorch as a Transformer encoder (two layers, four attention heads per layer, model dimension 64, feedforward hidden size 128, dropout 0.1). The input vector was linearly projected into the model embedding space, followed by positional encoding (implicit, as each sample is treated as a single sequence token). Training was performed for 200 epochs with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1 × 10−3, batch size 32), and the mean squared error (MSE) loss was minimized. Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA GPU, ensuring computational efficiency. After each epoch, model performance was monitored, and after completion, the final model was evaluated on the independent test set using the coefficient of determination (R²).

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer over a 2θ range of 5° to 50°. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) measurements were performed on a Zeiss Supra 55 field-emission electron microscope. Photoluminescence (PL), excitation, temperature-dependent spectra, and PL decay lifetimes were measured using an Edinburgh FLS1000 fluorescence spectrometer. Raman and laser excitation spectra were recorded on a WITec alpha300R Raman spectrometer. Thermogravimetric and differential thermal analyses (TGA/DTA) were carried out in nitrogen using a Shimadzu DTG-60H thermal analyzer. UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra were measured using a Shimadzu UV-3600 spectrophotometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi+ system. XAS measurements were carried out at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The XAS data were collected over a wide energy range covering both the near-edge (XANES) and extended (EXAFS) regions, with good statistical quality up to a maximum photoelectron wave vector of 14 Å−1. A metal foil (Mo) was mounted in the reference channel for absolute energy calibration during data collection. The acquired XAS data were processed using the Athena software package (part of the Demeter suite)61,62, including energy alignment and calibration, removal of monochromator glitches, normalization, background subtraction, and self-absorption correction for fluorescence-mode data. Energy calibration of all XAS spectra was performed by aligning the E0 values of the reference metal foils to their standard edge energies (Mo: 20.0 keV)39. The absorption edge position (E0) for all samples and reference compounds was determined as the maximum of the first derivative of the corresponding XANES spectra.

Calculation details

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)63. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method was employed, and the exchange–correlation interactions were treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional64. A kinetic energy cutoff of 350 eV was used for the plane-wave basis set. Spin polarization was included in all calculations. The atomic structures were modeled using a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell of Cs2ZrCl6 and its doped variants. Structural relaxation was performed until the energy convergence threshold reached 10⁻⁶ eV. The density of states (DOS) and band structure were post-processed using the VASPKIT package to facilitate projection analysis and data visualization65.

Ethical oversight

The AI-augmented imaging presented in Fig. 7c was generated using anonymized images from a single healthy volunteer (male, 28 years old, East Asian, Chinese). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board of Guangxi University. The study protocol was approved by the IRB under protocol number Gxu-2026-047. Demographic factors such as sex, age, and ethnicity were not considered during the experiment. The study did not involve vulnerable populations or procedures beyond minimal risk. Written informed consent was collected from the participant prior to data collection.
