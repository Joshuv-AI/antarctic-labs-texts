AS-IV attenuates nigral NLRP3 inflammasome in a Parkinson’s disease mouse model via gut microbiota
Authors: Jinfeng Cai, Man Wang, Yifei You, Fengjiao Sun, Mengdi Wang, Nan Wang, Rui Wang, Kan Zhang, Ruli Ge, Hongcai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAstragaloside IV (AS-IV) neuroprotection against rotenone (ROT)-induced Parkinson’s pathology was examined via microbiota-gut-brain axis mechanisms. Methods included intraperitoneal AS-IV, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from AS-IV-treated mice, and AS-IV-modulated microbiota transplantation. Substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons/microglia were evaluated by confocal imaging, while NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and α-synuclein (α-syn) levels were quantified via Western blot. Gut microbiota (16S rRNA sequencing) and striatal metabolites (LC-MS/MS) were analyzed. AS-IV attenuated ROT-induced motor deficits, dopaminergic neuron loss, α-syn overexpression, and NLRP3/caspase-1 activation, while elevating fecal SCFAs and increasing Bacteroidetes/Porphyromonadaceae with reduced Firmicutes. FMT from AS-IV-treated to ROT mice improved motor function, suppressed TH⁺ neuron loss, and inhibited microglial/NLRP3 activation. AS-IV-modulated microbiota transplantation upregulated Bacteroidetes, Porphyromonadaceae, Barnesiella, and downregulated Firmicutes, Lactobacillaceae, Lactobacillus, and Desulfovibrio. Crucially, FMT from AS-IV-treated to naïve mice alleviated ROT-induced damage, whereas ROT microbiota transplantation induced rotations and decreased Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Porphyromonadaceae, Sutterellaceae, and Parasutterella. AS-IV reversed these microbial changes. Genus-level microbiota alterations correlated with motor severity. ROT microbiota reduced 5-HIAA, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, thyroxine, and glutathione; AS-IV restored indole-3-carboxaldehyde and thyroxine. AS-IV exerts neuroprotection by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation via gut microbiota remodeling and metabolic regulation through the microbiota-gut-brain axis.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Neurology, Parkinson's disease

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, is pathologically characterized by dopaminergic (DA) neuronal degeneration in the substantia nigra (SN) and pathological α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregation1. Neuroinflammation mediated by microglial activation is now recognized as a crucial driver of PD pathogenesis. Specifically, the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, highly expressed in microglia, plays a pivotal role in neuroinflammation and disease progression2. Its activation by pathological α-syn aggregates and oxidative stress triggers caspase-1 maturation and release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18), exacerbating DA neuron loss2. Robust preclinical evidence supports NLRP3’s therapeutic targeting: Genetic ablation of NLRP3 in PD models attenuates neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration3; pharmacological inhibitors (e.g., MCC950, OLT1177) reduce inflammasome activation, α-syn pathology, and motor deficits in animal models; NLRP3/caspase-1 inhibition protects dopaminergic neurons independently in multiple PD models4. These findings collectively underscore NLRP3 as a validated therapeutic target for PD.

The gut-brain axis has emerged as a pivotal contributor to PD pathogenesis. Alterations in gut microbiota composition—such as reduced Prevotellaceae and Lachnospiraceae abundances and elevated Ruminococcaceae and Akkermansia levels—are consistently observed in PD patients5. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), key microbial metabolites, exhibit dual roles: while some studies report SCFA supplementation alleviates motor deficits by restoring gut microbiota and suppressing neuroinflammation6, others suggest SCFA accumulation may exacerbate PD pathology7, highlighting context-dependent effects.

Astragaloside IV (AS-IV), a bioactive saponin from Astragalus membranaceus, possesses anti-inflammatory and microbiota-modulating properties8. Preclinical studies demonstrate its efficacy in mitigating depressive behaviors and autoimmune disorders via gut microbiota regulation9. In PD models, AS-IV attenuates motor dysfunction and DA neuron loss by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation10. However, whether its neuroprotection involves microbiota-driven NLRP3 suppression remains unexplored. Here, we employed three experimental paradigms—AS-IV administration, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), and microbiota transfer to naïve mice-to dissect the role of AS-IV in modulating the gut-brain axis and NLRP3 inflammasome activity in rotenone (ROT)-induced PD mice.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks, 20–30 g; Ji’nan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd.) were housed under SPF conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% humidity, 12 - hour (h) light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Naïve mice refer to mice that grow naturally in a suitable environment without any intervention. Vehicle mice refer to the “Vehicle control group.” CON mice refer to the “Normal control group.” All procedures complied with the Institutional Animal Care Guidelines of Binzhou Medical University (Ethics approval no: 20221014-102). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Group assignments were randomized, and independent evaluators blinded to treatment conditions conducted behavioral assessments.

Experimental design

AS-IV intervention: mice received unilateral stereotaxic (ST) injections into the SN: vehicle (n = 12): 0.5 μL DMSO; ROT (n = 12): 0.5 μL rotenone (8 μg/μL); AS-IV (n = 12): ROT + daily intraperitoneal AS-IV (20 mg/kg) for 14 days. Following anesthesia, mice were affixed to a stereotaxic frame, and unilateral injections were performed targeting the right substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) using the following stereotaxic coordinates: 3.08 mm posterior to the bregma, 1.0 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.5 mm ventral to the dorsal surface. The selection of the ROT dosage, administration route, and exposure time was based on our previous study11. The choice of AS-IV was based on its documented neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties10, with dosage and administration informed by previous studies12.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) protocol

Microbiota suspensions were derived from normal mice (designated *FMT CON*), ROT-treated mice (designated *FMT ROT*), and AS-Ⅳ-treated mice (designated *FMT ROT AS*). Microbial suspensions were prepared by collecting fresh fecal pellets. The pellets were immediately diluted with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (20 mg fecal pellets/ml). Feces were placed in sterile PBS for approximately 15 min, shaken, and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove large particulate matter. The resulting supernatant was subjected to further centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet bacterial cells. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and sequentially filtered through sterile membrane filters (100 μm pore size) twice to obtain a clarified bacterial suspension. The final suspension was mixed with sterile glycerol to a final concentration of 20 % (v/v) and stored at –80 °C for long-term preservation. On the day of transplantation, aliquots were thawed either at 37 °C in a water bath with gentle agitation or at room temperature (RT) prior to administration13.

Normal mice were subjected to FMT of feces from the AS-Ⅳ and ROT group. The animals were randomized into three groups: the CON group (n = 12) was gavaged with an antibiotic mixture for 10 consecutive days; the CON + FMT ROT group (n = 12) was gavaged with an antibiotic mixture for 10 consecutive days, followed by 1.5 ml of PEG 4000 and 200 µl of ROT bacterial solution for 14 consecutive days; and the CON + FMT ROT AS group (n = 12) was gavaged with an antibiotic mixture for 10 consecutive days, followed by 1.5 ml of PEG 4000 and 200 µl of ROT + AS bacterial solution from the AS-Ⅳ-treated group.

For the fecal transplantation experiments, all the mice were given 200 μl of a freshly prepared antibiotic mixture by oral gavage for 10 consecutive days (orally after a daily 6-h fast). The mixture contained ampicillin (1 g/L, #A8180, Solarbio), neomycin (0.5 g/L, #N090, Solarbio), vancomycin (0.5 g/L, #V8050, Solarbio), and metronidazole (1 g/L, #M060, Solarbio). To prevent fungal overgrowth, amphotericin B (1 mg/kg, #A8250, Solarbio) was added to the antibiotic mixture for the first 3 days. At the end of the antibiotic treatment, the mice were treated with PEG 4000 (#P8240, Solarbio) to remove antibiotic residues in the intestine. A total of 1.5 ml of bowel cleansing solution was administered orally by gavage to each mouse (2 × 500 µl, the day before FMT at 1-h intervals; 1 × 500 µl, 4 h before FMT). The mice were fasted for 4 h prior to PEG 4000 administration and received 200 μl of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/ml)14,15.

Behavioral assessments

Pole test

A wooden rod (0.55 m long and 1 cm in diameter) with a rough surface was fixed to a wooden board as a base. Mice were positioned at the top and allowed to descend the pole. The time from release to the hind limb reaching the bottom was recorded. Prior to the final test, all mice underwent training three times daily for three consecutive days. Three trials were conducted, and the mean time was calculated to analyze motor function16.

Rotarod test

Mice were placed on a rotating rod with five separate lanes. The rod accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. The latency to fall was recorded. All mice received training for three consecutive days before the test. Three trials were performed, and the mean latency was determined to analyze motor function16.

Grip strength test

Grip strength was assessed using a rope grasp test. Mice were positioned on a horizontal rope (5 mm diameter) and permitted to grasp it with their forepaws. Performance was scored on a 0–5 point scale as follows: 0 point if the mouse fell off within 10 s; 1 point for gripping with one forepaw; 2 points for gripping with two forepaws; 3 points for gripping with three paws; 4 points for gripping with all four paws; and 5 points for gripping with all four paws while actively attempting to escape toward the end of the rope17. Each mouse underwent three test repetitions, with 1-h intervals between trials.

Total gastrointestinal transit time

Mice were fasted for 6 h before testing and placed in a quiet environment for acclimatization. The transit time was determined by oral gavage with 200 µL of a 10% phenol red solution. The time to the first expulsion of a red-stained fecal pellet was recorded18. The total gastrointestinal transit time was defined as the interval from the gavage of the phenol red solution until the stained feces exited the anus.

Preparing coronal brain section specimens

The mice were anaesthetised via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 0.8 ml/kg. The brain tissue was then perfused and fixed using a solution of PBS buffer and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Following this, it was dehydrated in a 30% sucrose solution for two days before being stored at 4 °C. The tissue was then embedded and rapidly frozen using a cryostat (Leica CM1950). The final 40 μm sections were stored in cell protection solution at −20 °C18.

Molecular analyses

Western blot

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS18. Following perfusion, mice were euthanized, and the SN tissue was rapidly dissected. SN proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Technology, China), and the lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a BCA protein assay. The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% non-fat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: mouse anti-NLRP3 (1:800, #AG-20B-0014-C100, AdipoGen), rabbit anti-caspase-1/P20 (1:1000, #22915-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:1000, #AF5103, Affinity), mouse anti-α-syn (1:1000, #2644S, Cell Signaling Technology), and mouse anti-β-actin (1:800, #BM5180, BOSTER). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: anti-rabbit IgG (1:2500, #7074P2. Cell Signaling Technology) or anti-mouse IgG (1:2500, #7076P2. Cell Signaling Technology). The relative optical density of the target protein bands was normalized to β-actin and quantified using image analysis software.

Confocal immunofluorescence

Mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)18. Brains were rapidly isolated and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 h. Following fixation, tissues were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h. Coronal sections were cut at 30 µm thickness using a cryostat (Leica, CM1950) and stored in cryoprotectant solution at −20 °C.

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in PBS containing 10% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.3% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies—mouse anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH; 1:50, sc-25269, Santa Cruz) and rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1; 1:100, 17198, Cell Signaling Technology)—were diluted in blocking solution and incubated with sections overnight at 4 °C. After primary incubation, sections were washed and incubated for 4 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (1:400, ab150119, Abcam) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (1:400, A32790, Invitrogen). Finally, sections were coverslipped using antifade mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam).

Fecal collection and 16S rRNA sequencing analysis

Mouse feces were collected in individual sterile test tubes. Immediately after collection, tubes were stored at −20 °C and then transferred to long-term storage at −80 °C13. Gut microbial absolute quantification using 16S rRNA sequencing was performed by Shanghai Genesky Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using the TransStart® TopTaq DNA Polymerase Kit (Transgen, China). Genomic DNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer. Pre-amplification PCR assays were performed on the samples. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was targeted for amplification using the primers: 341F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). Library construction followed amplification. Finally, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform using a 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing strategy. Subsequent bioinformatic analysis was performed on the generated sequencing data.

Fecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)

The contents of SCFAs were detected using MetWare based on the Agilent 7890B-7000D GC-MS/MS platform13. Initially, 20 mg of the sample and 1 ml of 0.5% phosphoric acid solution were added to a 2 ml centrifuge tube. Then, one grinding bead was introduced into the tube, and the mixture was uniformly homogenized using a ball mill. Subsequently, the mixture was vortexed for 10 min to ensure thorough mixing, followed immediately by sonication in an ice bath for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. From the resulting supernatant, 100 μl was combined with 500 μl of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) in a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube. This mixture was vortexed for 3 min and sonicated for 5 min under ice-bath conditions. After another centrifugation step at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, 200 μl of the supernatant was collected and transferred to a glass-lined vial. Finally, the sample was stored at −20 °C for analysis. The Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with a 7000D mass spectrometer, equipped with a DB-FFAP column, was employed for GC-MS/MS analysis of the SCFAs.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Neurotransmitter levels in the striatum were quantified using LC-MS/MS on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 platform via MetWare13. The sample was transferred to a 2 mL tube and vortexed for 3 min. After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (300 μL) was then collected and added to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. This mixture was stored at −20 °C in a refrigerator for 30 min. Subsequently, it was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (200 μL) was transferred to an injection vial and stored at −20 °C. Finally, the metabolites were analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS).

Results

AS-Ⅳ administration ameliorates ROT-induced behavioral impairments by inhibiting DA neuron loss and microglial activation in PD mice

The experimental design is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1A. PD mouse models were established through SN infusion of ROT. Apomorphine (APO)-induced contralateral rotations were subsequently assessed, with successful model validation defined as >210 rotations per 30 min (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Following intraperitoneal (IP) administration of AS-Ⅳ in ROT-treated mice, behavioral assessments revealed no significant difference in rotation frequency between the AS-Ⅳ and ROT groups at 2 weeks (281.0 ± 4.24 vs. 291.9 ± 4.98 rotations/30 min, P > 0.05) (Fig. 1C). However, after 4 weeks of treatment, AS-Ⅳ administration significantly attenuated APO-induced contralateral rotations compared to the ROT control group (293.8 ± 4.26 vs. 357.0 ± 3.62 rotations/30 min, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C).

Motor behavior was evaluated using the pole test, rotarod test, and grip strength test. In the pole test, the descent time in the ROT group was significantly longer than in the vehicle group (18.58 ± 1.49 s vs. 8.47 ± 0.64 s, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). The rotarod test revealed a marked decrease in latency time in the ROT group compared to the vehicle group (18.33 ± 2.56 s vs. 57.72 ± 3.33 s, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E). Similarly, grip strength scores were lower in the ROT group (1.56 ± 0.33 vs. 4.47 ± 0.10, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1F). Compared to ROT treatment, AS-Ⅳ treatment reduced descent time in the pole test (18.58 ± 1.49 s vs. 13.81 ± 0.81 s, P < 0.01), increased latency time in the rotarod test (18.33 ± 2.56 s vs. 28.47 ± 2.46 s, P < 0.05), and improved grip strength scores (1.56 ± 0.33 vs. 2.97 ± 0.33, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D–F).

In the gastrointestinal motility test, the total gastrointestinal transit time was significantly longer in the ROT group than in the vehicle group (177.3 ± 12.66 min vs. 90.58 ± 4.23 min, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1G). AS-Ⅳ treatment reduced the gastrointestinal transit time compared to ROT treatment (177.3 ± 12.66 min vs. 108.4 ± 6.95 min, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1G).

A significant reduction in tyrosine hydroxylase-positive (TH+) cells (dopaminergic neurons) was observed in the SN of the ROT group compared to the vehicle group (P < 0.001). AS-Ⅳ treatment effectively attenuated the loss of TH+ neurons in ROT-treated mice (Fig. 1H, I, P < 0.001). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a marked increase in Iba-1-positive microglial cells in the ROT group relative to the vehicle controls. AS-Ⅳ administration significantly suppressed this neuroinflammatory response by reducing Iba-1-positive microglial activation (Fig. 1H, I, P < 0.001).

AS-Ⅳ administration alleviates the NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β inflammatory pathway and α-syn expression in the SN

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying AS-Ⅳ‘s neuroprotective effects in PD mice, we analyzed NLRP3 inflammasome pathway components and α-syn expression via western blotting (Fig. 1J, K). Quantitative densitometric analysis revealed significant upregulation of inflammasome markers in ROT-treated mice compared to vehicle controls: NLRP3/β-actin (0.857 ± 0.059 vs. 0.514 ± 0.041; P < 0.01), caspase-1/β-actin (1.497 ± 0.069 vs. 1.032 ± 0.028; P < 0.01), and IL-1β/β-actin (0.947 ± 0.083 vs. 0.673 ± 0.016; P < 0.05). AS-Ⅳ treatment substantially downregulated these inflammatory mediators, showing reduced expression ratios of NLRP3/β-actin (0.421 ± 0.046 vs. 0.857 ± 0.059; P < 0.05), caspase-1/β-actin (0.779 ± 0.0786 vs. 1.497 ± 0.069; P < 0.001), and IL-1β/β-actin (0.579 ± 0.041 vs. 0.947 ± 0.083; P < 0.01).

Concurrently, ROT administration induced pronounced α-syn accumulation, as evidenced by elevated α-syn/β-actin ratios compared to vehicle-treated mice (1.624 ± 0.120 vs. 0.736 ± 0.060; P < 0.01). This pathogenic protein expression was significantly ameliorated by AS-Ⅳ intervention (0.874 ± 0.083 vs. 1.624 ± 0.120; P < 0.01) (Fig. 1J, K).

AS-Ⅳ administration ameliorates intestinal dysbiosis and increases fecal SCFA levels in ROT-induced PD mice

Alpha diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon), reflecting microbial diversity and richness, were significantly elevated in vehicle-treated and AS-Ⅳ-treated mice relative to ROT-treated mice based on 16S rRNA sequencing (Fig. 2A, B). Beta diversity analysis using Bray–Curtis distance-based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) demonstrated distinct clustering of gut microbiota communities across the vehicle, ROT, and AS-Ⅳ groups (Fig. 2C). At the phylum level, AS-Ⅳ administration increased both the absolute microbiota abundance and the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, while concurrently reducing Firmicutes proportions (Fig. 2D, E).

LEfSe analysis (LDA score >6, P < 0.05) identified significant compositional shifts in gut microbiota based on absolute abundances (Fig. 2F, G). Compared to vehicle controls, the ROT group exhibited marked reductions in Bacteroidetes, Porphyromonadaceae, Enterococcaceae, and Enterococcus populations. These decreases were effectively counteracted by AS-Ⅳ treatment (Fig. 2H–K). Relative abundance comparisons further revealed ROT-induced reductions in Bacteroidetes and Porphyromonadaceae alongside increases in Firmicutes, Cyanobacteria, Lactobacillaceae, Alteromonadaceae, Rhodobacteraceae, Family_II, Lactobacillus, Alteromonas, and GpIIa (P < 0.05; Fig. 2L–V). All these alterations were significantly attenuated by AS-Ⅳ intervention (P < 0.05; Fig. 2L–V).

Fecal SCFA analysis demonstrated that ROT treatment reduced levels of propionic, acetic, isobutyric, valeric, and butyric acids compared to vehicle controls (fold change [FC] < 0.8). AS-Ⅳ administration restored these SCFA concentrations to levels exceeding baseline (FC > 1.20) (Fig. 3A–E).

Microbiota-behavior correlation analysis identified significant associations (|r| ≥ 0.6, P < 0.05) between specific taxa and clinical parameters. Enterobacter, Aestuariispira, Klebsiella, Alistipes, and Vampirovibrio correlated with motor performance metrics (pole test descent time, rotarod latency, and grip strength). Notably, Enterobacter, Aestuariispira, Klebsiella, Alistipes, and Vampirovibrio demonstrated dual correlations with both motor and non-motor symptoms (Fig. 3F).

FMT inhibits the NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β inflammatory pathway in the SN

Western blot analysis demonstrated significantly elevated protein levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β in the ROT + NS group compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4J, K). FMT from AS-IV-treated mice effectively reduced α-syn levels relative to the ROT + NS group (0.54 ± 0.03 vs. 0.89 ± 0.05, P < 0.01), FMT from normal mice equally reduced α-syn levels relative to the ROT + NS group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4J, K).

Discussion

Emerging evidence implicates gut microbiome dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of PD, with microbial metabolites potentially influencing neuroinflammatory processes19. Our study demonstrates that AS-IV exerts neuroprotective effects in ROT-induced PD models through microbiota modulation and suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Notably, FMT from AS-IV-treated mice ameliorated motor deficits and dopaminergic neuron loss in PD models, establishing a causal relationship between AS-IV-mediated microbiota remodeling and neuroprotection.

Three experimental approaches consistently revealed AS-IV’s therapeutic potential: (1) intraperitoneal administration improved motor function and gut microbiota composition in ROT models; (2) FMT from AS-IV-treated donors rescued motor deficits and nigral TH+ neuron loss in ROT-exposed recipients; (3) microbiota transplantation from AS-IV-treated donors alleviated ROT-induced neuroinflammation via the gut–brain axis. Transplantation of microbiota from ROT-exposed donors into naïve mice recapitulated PD-like pathology. This ROT-shaped microbiota triggered gut dysbiosis, compromised intestinal barrier integrity, and induced systemic inflammation. These peripheral disturbances subsequently propagated neuroinflammation and α-syn pathology along the gut–brain axis. We posit that these cascading events exacerbate pre-existing or stochastic asymmetries within dopaminergic circuits, ultimately manifesting as rotational behavior. These findings position gut microbiota modulation as a key mechanism underlying AS-IV’s neuroprotective properties.

At the molecular level, AS-IV treatment suppressed microglial activation and NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β signaling in the substantia nigra, aligning with previous reports of AS-IV’s anti-inflammatory effects in MPTP models20. This inflammasome inhibition likely contributes to the preservation of dopaminergic neurons observed in both AS-IV-treated and FMT recipient groups.

Sampson et al. demonstrated that in a mouse model of PD, gut microbiota regulate disease progression through their metabolic products, SCFAs, indicating SCFAs are both necessary and sufficient for driving α-syn pathology and microglial activation. This study suggests that altered SCFA profiles may directly or indirectly promote α-syn aggregation and activate neuroimmune responses, ultimately leading to motor dysfunction21. Additional studies indicate that reduced SCFA levels correlate with PD onset and symptom severity6. Butyrate, in particular, plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and regulating gut inflammation22. Its diminished levels may impair the enteric nervous system, contributing to non-motor symptoms, such as gastrointestinal motility disorders5. Our gut microbiome analysis revealed that AS-IV significantly modulated bacterial taxa associated with PD pathogenesis and reversed ROT-induced alterations in gut microbiota composition and SCFA levels. AS-IV treatment effectively suppressed ROT-induced proliferation of cyanobacteria—a known source of PD-associated neurotoxins23, and reversed multiple PD-related microbial alterations: including increased Bacteroidetes24,25 and Pasteurella (anti-inflammatory bacteria)26, reduced Saccaromyces (associated with PD progression)27, and decreased Desulfovibrio (an α-syn aggregation-promoting bacterium)28. These microbial alterations were accompanied by restored short-chain fatty acid production, potentially mediating AS-IV’s anti-inflammatory effects through the gut-brain axis.

Notably, FMT experiments demonstrated that AS-IV-modified microbiota could: (1) transfer neuroprotection to PD models, and (2) prevent PD-like pathology induction in healthy recipients. The ROT microbiota’s capacity to induce striatal acetylcholine elevation and decrease neuroprotective metabolites (5-HIAA, indole derivatives, thyroxine, and glutathione) in recipient mice29–31 was mitigated by AS-IV microbiota transfer. These findings suggest AS-IV’s therapeutic effects are partially mediated through microbial regulation of neurotransmitter systems and neuroprotective metabolite production. These findings may exhibit equivalent beneficial effects as those in the FMT ROT AS group, but for most outcomes, FMT ROT AS demonstrated superior performance compared to FMT CON. Notably, most differences between the two groups were not statistically significant. As this was not the primary focus of our current investigation, we did not conduct an in-depth analysis. Further exploration may require a larger sample size. FMT from healthy donors is relatively straightforward to perform, and control FMT may represent a more practical and scalable therapeutic option for PD, particularly in scenarios where cost-effectiveness and ease of implementation are critical. We acknowledge that although both interventions demonstrate efficacy, their translational potential may differ. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving the efficacy of these two interventions.

The temporal progression of behavioral improvements—with significant APO-induced rotation changes emerging at 4 weeks post-AS-IV treatment—aligns with the proposed mechanism of chronic microbiota remodeling. Importantly, our FMT protocol not only validated AS-IV’s microbiota-dependent effects but also established a novel method for modeling PD through dysbiosis transfer.

Regarding the analysis of microbiota differences, LefSe is considered a univariate model with limitations in covariate corrections, such as for cage effects and handling multiple conditions. Although all experimental conditions in our mouse models were consistent, minimizing the need for extensive covariate corrections, we conducted a re-analysis using MaAsLin, a multivariable association model that enables covariate adjustments to account for potential confounders. This ensures a more robust assessment of microbial community associations by considering multiple variables simultaneously. The results of this analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). Additionally, we implemented ADONIS testing for beta diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices to evaluate community composition differences (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1D–F). ADONIS, a form of permutational multivariate analysis of variance, is a standard method for beta diversity assessment, allowing for the detection of significant variations in microbial community structure based on distance matrices.

This study elucidates a gut-brain axis mechanism for AS-IV’s neuroprotection in PD, involving: (1) remodeling of gut microbiota composition; (2) restoration of SCFA production; (3) suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation; (4) attenuation of neuroinflammation and dopaminergic degeneration. These findings position AS-IV as a promising therapeutic candidate for PD, targeting the gut-microbiome-brain axis, with FMT emerging as both a valuable research tool and potential treatment strategy.

Additionally, previous studies have shown that transplanting gut microbiota from PD patients into mice exacerbates their motor symptoms, accompanied by alterations in specific bacterial taxonomic groups (such as a reduction in protective bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria).

Interestingly, transplanting microbiota from healthy controls produced a protective effect in PD mice. This suggests that identifying altered bacteria or their metabolites in PD, such as fecal short-chain fatty acid ratios, holds promise as disease biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Correcting this dysbiosis through intervention may offer a safe and effective therapeutic strategy to delay the progression of PD motor symptoms.

Limitations

This study was conducted only in male mice.
