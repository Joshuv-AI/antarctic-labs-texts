Clip Combined With Rubber Band vs Clip-Assisted Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography for Difficult Biliary Cannulation in Periampullary Diverticulum: A Propensity Score-Matched Analysis
Authors: Longping Chen, Jin Zheng, Chuanshen Jiang, Junguo Chen, Zhiping Chen, Zanxuan Qiu, Dazhou Li, Wen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Successful biliary cannulation is critical to the success of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). While various devices and auxiliary techniques have been developed to manage difficult cannulation in patients with periampullary diverticulum (PAD), however, comparative studies on different traction methods remain understudied. The aim of this study was to compare the safety and efficacy of clip-assisted ERCP (C-ERCP) vs clip combined with rubber band-assisted ERCP (CRB-ERCP) for difficult biliary cannulation in patients with PAD.METHODS:This study was a single-center retrospective analysis including 193 patients with difficult biliary cannulation and concomitant PAD. Using propensity score matching based on age, sex, and diverticulum type, 120 patients (60 per group) were selected for comparison. The primary outcomes assessed were cannulation success rate, procedure time, and incidence of procedure-related complications.RESULTS:The cannulation success rate was significantly higher in the CRB-ERCP group than in the C-ERCP group (96.7% vs 73.3%, P < 0.001). The cannulation time was also markedly shorter in the CRB-ERCP group (52 ± 47 vs 106 ± 73 seconds, P < 0.001). To achieve successful cannulation, the C-ERCP group required additional techniques-such as the double-guidewire method or transpancreatic biliary sphincterotomy-more frequently than the CRB-ERCP group (38.3% vs 13.3%, P = 0.003). The overall incidence of adverse events was comparable between the 2 groups (P = 0.632), all of which resolved with conservative management. No serious adverse events occurred in either group.DISCUSSION:Both CRB-ERCP and C-ERCP are viable options for difficult biliary cannulation in PAD. However, CRB-ERCP shows superior advantages in shortening cannulation time, improving success rates, and reducing the need for additional rescue techniques, making it a safe, feasible, and highly effective approach.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a pivotal technique for the diagnosis and treatment of biliary and pancreatic duct diseases, representing the most technically demanding and high-risk procedure in digestive endoscopy. The key to successful ERCP lies in achieving selective deep cannulation of the duodenal papilla. Although various auxiliary devices and techniques have been clinically applied to improve the success rate of cannulation by endoscopists, studies indicate that 10%–20% of cases still face biliary cannulation failure (1). This challenge is particularly pronounced in patients with periampullary diverticulum (PAD), where cannulation difficulty frequently arises, leading to prolonged procedure times.

PAD is defined as an extraluminal mucosal outpouching of the duodenal wall within a 2–3 cm radius of the major papilla. Its prevalence and size increase with age, with a reported clinical detection rate ranging from 5.6% to 46.1% (2,3). Based on the anatomical relationship between the diverticulum and the papilla, Boix et al classified it into 3 types (4): Type I (papilla within the diverticulum), Type II (papilla located at the edge of the diverticulum), and Type III (papilla adjacent to the diverticulum). The presence of PAD poses a significant anatomical challenge by distorting the papillary architecture-causing traction, folding, and poor exposure, which substantially increases cannulation difficulty. Consequently, numerous studies have demonstrated that the positional relationship between PAD and the major papilla is a critical factor affecting the success rate and safety of ERCP. A study by Lobo et al found PAD to be the direct cause of ERCP failure in 24.6% of patients aged younger than 75 years, a proportion that rose to 46.1% in those aged 75 years or older (5).

For difficult biliary cannulation due to PAD, clinicians often resort to auxiliary techniques and devices, such as endoscopic clip-assisted techniques, clip-and-snare lifting techniques, or dual-instrument methods through a single channel. The common goal of these approaches is to reposition the papilla, align the bile duct axis, and thereby create a more favorable angle for cannulation (6). Amid these, traction techniques represent a particularly promising direction. Fujiwara et al (7) reported that multiloop traction device-assisted papillary cannulation during ERCP demonstrated excellent clinical outcomes. In addition, Mengpei recently described a case where metal clips combined with rubber bands (CRBs) were used to provide traction, achieving full exposure of the intra-ampullary papilla for treating ERCP difficulty caused by PAD (8). These cases highlight the technical potential of traction for papillary exposure and biliary access. Nevertheless, robust evidence is still scarce; the literature currently consists mainly of case reports, and comparative studies systematically assessing the safety and efficacy of these techniques are absent.

Therefore, to address this research gap, we performed a comparative study to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of CRB-assisted ERCP (CRB-ERCP) vs clip-assisted ERCP (C-ERCP) in patients with PAD and difficult biliary cannulation.

METHODS

Study design and ethics

This study is a single-center, retrospective clinical investigation designed to evaluate the efficacy of CRB-ERCP vs C-ERCP in patients with PAD and difficult biliary cannulation. The protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

End points

The primary end point was the rate of successful bile duct cannulation. Secondary end points included total procedure time, cannulation time, the incidence of ERCP-related adverse events, and the requirement for additional rescue cannulation techniques.

Participants

A total of 4,900 patients underwent ERCP at our center between February 2017 and June 2025. Among them, 193 patients with PAD and difficult biliary cannulation were initially enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: having an indication for ERCP, providing preoperative informed consent, and having intraoperatively confirmed PAD with difficult cannulation. Exclusion criteria included the following: (i) suspicion of ampullary malignancy on preoperative imaging, (ii) successful cannulation by conventional methods, and (iii) a history of pancreatobiliary surgery (e.g., choledochojejunostomy). To control for confounding factors, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2. Matching variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), indication for ERCP, diverticulum type, and history of gastrectomy. Rationale for PSM variables: The following variables were included in the propensity score model: age, sex, BMI, indication for ERCP, diverticulum type (I/II/III according to Boix classification), and history of distal gastrectomy. These variables were selected a priori based on established predictors of difficult biliary cannulation and PAD-related anatomy. Specifically, age is associated with diverticulum prevalence and size; diverticulum type directly affects papillary exposure and cannulation difficulty; and the history of distal gastrectomy alters the endoscopic approach and cannulation angle, representing a well-known technical challenge. Indication for ERCP may influence the urgency and complexity of the procedure. BMI and sex were included as standard demographic confounders. For patients with a history of distal gastrectomy, the specific type of surgical reconstruction (Billroth I, Billroth II, or Roux-en-Y) was retrieved from operative records. In the matched cohort, all such patients had either Billroth I (n = 5) or Billroth II (n = 10) reconstruction; no patient had Roux-en-Y anatomy. After PSM, 120 patients were included in the final analysis, with 60 patients in each group (CRB-ERCP and C-ERCP). The baseline characteristics of the matched cohort are presented in Table 1.

Sample size and power analysis

Post hoc power analysis for the primary outcome (cannulation success) was performed using a 2-sided χ2 test with α = 0.05. Based on the observed success rates of 96.7% and 73.3% in the 2 groups, the achieved power was >99% for the sample size of 60 patients per group. Moreover, to detect a 20% absolute difference in success rate (e.g., from 70% to 90%) with 80% power, a minimum of 24 patients per group would be required, indicating that our study was sufficiently powered.

ERCP procedures

All procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists (>5 years in ERCP, >1,000 independent procedures). With the patient in the left lateral position, the duodenoscope was advanced to the descending duodenum. After identifying the major papilla and assessing its relationship to the diverticulum, conventional cannulation was attempted using a sphincterotome and guidewire. If cannulation failed, the rescue technique used was either C-ERCP or CRB-ERCP, depending on the clinical decision at that time. Patients were retrospectively assigned to groups based on the technique they actually received:C-ERCP group: Based on peri-diverticular mucosal anatomy and papillary orientation, clips were applied to fix the mucosal fold below or lateral to the papilla to achieve full exposure. Cannulation was then reattempted until success.CRB-ERCP group: After determining optimal clipping sites around the diverticulum, a traction system was created by fixing one end of a rubber band to a mucosal fold within the diverticulum and the other end to the contralateral or lateral duodenal wall using clips. This traction folded the mucosa to fully expose the papilla and stabilize the biliary axis, after which cannulation was attempted (Figure 1, Video 1).

The endoscopic clips used in this study were rotatable, reopenable hemoclips (Micro-Tech Rotatable Clips, opening width 8–11 mm; Nanjing Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China). All clips were compatible with a duodenoscope working channel of at least 2.8 mm and were capable of normal opening and closure even when the duodenoscope elevator was fully deflected. Orthodontic rubber bands (inner diameter, 6.5 mm (1/4”), 3.5 OZ, China) were used. The rubber band was prestretched to provide sustained traction. This setup does not occupy the working channel and allows dynamic adjustment of traction direction.

Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS 29.0 statistical software and R software version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for data analysis. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± SD, with intergroup comparisons conducted using independent samples t-tests, and multigroup comparisons performed through one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented as median and interquartile range, with between-group comparisons made using the Mann-Whitney U test (rank sum test). Categorical data were expressed as counts (percentages), with intergroup comparisons analyzed by the χ2 tests. When the theoretical frequency T < 5 with total sample size n < 40, or T < 1, the Fisher exact test was applied instead. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study population

Between February 2017 and June 2025, 1,142 patients with PAD underwent ERCP at our center, representing 23.3% of all ERCP procedures during this period. Of these, 209 patients (18.3%) presented with difficult biliary cannulation. After excluding 6 patients with biliary-duodenal fistula discovered intraoperatively, 3 with incomplete clinical data, and 7 for other reasons, 193 eligible patients were included. These patients were then divided into the CRB-ERCP group and C-ERCP group following PSM (Figure 3).

Patients

After matching, the final cohort comprised 120 patients with PAD and difficult cannulation, including 64 men and 56 women, with a mean age of 68.5 years. No statistically significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in sex, age, clinical indications, or diverticulum types. The baseline characteristics of the matched cohort are summarized in Table 1.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The CRB-ERCP group demonstrated significantly shorter procedure times and higher cannulation success compared with the C-ERCP group. Specifically, the mean total procedure time (165 ± 72 vs 214 ± 121 seconds, P = 0.001) and cannulation time (52 ± 47 vs 106 ± 73 seconds, P < 0.001) were both markedly reduced in the CRB-ERCP group. The cannulation success rate was also significantly higher (96.7% [58/60] vs 73.3% [44/60], P < 0.001; Table 2).

Among the 12 patients with initial cannulation failure in the C-ERCP group, 7 achieved success after crossover to rubber band-assisted traction as a salvage measure. Analysis by diverticulum classification revealed comparable proportions of difficult cannulation (PAD type I) between the 2 groups (48.3% vs 43.3%, P = 0.859). No significant difference was observed in the number of metal clips used (P = 0.599), but a significant disparity existed in the requirement for additional auxiliary cannulation techniques (C-ERCP group 38.3% vs CRB-ERCP group 13.3%, P = 0.003). The C-ERCP group demonstrated a greater tendency to use techniques such as the double-guidewire method, pancreatic duct stent placement, and transpapillary biliary sphincterotomy.

Complications and adverse events

The overall incidence of adverse events was comparable between the 2 groups (P = 0.632). These events primarily included post-ERCP pancreatitis, biliary infection, and bleeding (Table 3). All complications were mild and resolved with conservative management; no serious adverse events occurred. At the 6-month follow-up, all clips had detached spontaneously without causing any clip-related adverse reactions.

DISCUSSION

ERCP is a cornerstone in the management of pancreatobiliary diseases. Despite its widespread adoption and technical refinement, successful selective biliary cannulation remains a challenge even for experienced endoscopists. The difficulty of selective biliary cannulation is multifactorial, influenced by papillary morphology, anatomical location, and the scope of surgical intervention.

The impact of PAD on ERCP cannulation remains debated. Although some studies report no increased risk of cannulation failure, accumulating evidence suggests that PAD elevates procedural difficulty and complication rates (11). In our cohort, the conventional cannulation success rate for PAD patients was 81.7%, slightly lower than previously reported. This observation may be attributed to the high proportion of intradiverticular papillae (Type I) in our study population. The deep location and poor exposure of Type I papillae often necessitate repeated endoscopic repositioning, significantly increasing cannulation difficulty (12). Notably, 18.3% of our PAD patients experienced difficult cannulation wherein standard techniques failed. Thus, while PAD itself may not universally increase difficulty, specific anatomical presentations (e.g., Type I) or the coexistence with other risk factors can pose substantial challenges, leading to markedly higher failure rates (13). In clinical practice, the coexistence of multiple risk factors for difficult biliary cannulation is more common than a single factor. However, there is currently no consensus on which combinations of factors are more likely to result in difficult cannulation.

A meta-analysis of 16 studies (2,794 patients) provided compelling evidence that the presence of PAD reduces the ERCP cannulation success rate by approximately 50% compared with normal anatomy (14). Proposed mechanisms for this increased difficulty include the following: (i) poor exposure and unstable positioning when the papilla lies deep within a diverticulum; (ii) increased mobility and softer consistency of the papilla at the diverticular rim, complicating stabilization; and (iii) potential compression or distortion of the bile duct by the diverticulum itself. To overcome these challenges, traction techniques—using clips, sleeves, or clip-and-snare devices—have been developed to retract mucosal folds and improve papillary exposure. Notably, the CRB traction technique used in this study demonstrated superior efficacy, achieving a 96.7% cannulation success rate while significantly reducing procedure time. Furthermore, it serves as a viable salvage option when standard clip traction fails, thereby preventing procedure termination due to cannulation failure.

The study by Ryuji Inoue et al further detailed the advantages of traction devices (15): (i) The procedure can be performed using only a duodenoscope, without occupying the working channel. (ii) By applying traction in various directions, it stably exposes the papillary orifice—whether concealed within a diverticulum or situated at its margin—and maintains a stable field of view. (iii) The sustained traction provided by the rubber band effectively stabilizes the axis of the bile duct, thereby facilitating successful cannulation.

In recent years, the adoption of advanced techniques such as the double-guidewire method, precut papillotomy, and needle-knife fistulotomy has steadily improved overall ERCP cannulation success rates. Nevertheless, in patients with PAD, the altered anatomy may increase the risks of bleeding and perforation during precut procedures, necessitating greater caution. Our study found that in cases of difficult biliary cannulation with PAD, the C-ERCP group required significantly more frequent use of the double-guidewire technique and transpancreatic biliary sphincterotomy. This not only prolonged and complicated the procedure but also potentially raised the risk of adverse events. By contrast, the CRB-ERCP group showed markedly lower dependence on such auxiliary measures. This likely stems from its ability to more effectively reposition the papillary orifice and align the bile duct axis, creating a more favorable trajectory for direct guidewire passage and thereby reducing the need for complex rescue techniques. Consequently, the CRB-ERCP approach combines high success rates with relative simplicity, acceptable cost, and a favorable safety profile.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, there was a temporal trend: Simple clip traction was used predominantly in earlier cases, whereas the combined technique was adopted more frequently later. Although PSM balanced several baseline variables, potential biases related to unmeasured factors such as endoscopist's individual skill or evolving technique preference cannot be entirely excluded. Future prospective randomized trials should standardize the rescue technique allocation to eliminate such bias. Second, the procedures were performed by multiple endoscopists rather than a single operator, and interoperator heterogeneity in technique could have affected success rates. Third, the time interval between switching from the initial approach to rescue traction was not standardized, and it remains unclear whether prolonged manipulation-induced papillary edema or sphincter of Oddi spasm would affect the success rate of subsequent rescue cannulation. Fourth, although we recorded the type of gastrectomy reconstruction (Billroth I or Billroth II) in our limited number of such patients, no patient with Roux-en-Y anatomy was included, which would have posed even greater technical challenges. Therefore, the performance of CRB-ERCP in post-Roux-en-Y gastrectomy remains unknown and requires separate evaluation. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate the generalizability of this technique and assess its long-term outcomes.

In summary, managing difficult biliary cannulation in patients with PAD demands particular caution. This study demonstrates that the CRB traction technique offers a safe, simple, and highly effective solution. It significantly shortens cannulation time and markedly improves success rates, providing a valuable alternative for these complex ERCP cases.
