TRAFFICGEN: a multi-agent LLM orchestration for smart mobility and emergency corridor pre-emption
Authors: Gaurav Soni, Ganesh Khekare, Yash Kumar, S. Rupaak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research work introduces TrafficGen, a proof-of-concept software prototype that explores the possibility of adaptive traffic signal management using LLM-based agentic orchestration. The framework is evaluated in a single-intersection, deterministic simulation environment that uses text to represent the various intersections and can operate with Gemini 2.5 Flash and LangGraph to implement a “Council of Agents” control architecture. TrafficGen uses a semantic translation layer to generate human-readable chain-of-thought reasoning logs for each signal decision, enabling it to manage complex traffic patterns. In empirical stochastic evaluations, TrafficGen was tested using four custom stress scenarios and 30 independent runs, yielding a mean reduction of 46.9% ± 2.8% in Wait Burden Score across all scenarios and runs (from 6,370 points to 3,380 points per run, compared to standard fixed-time signal controllers). Additionally, 100% of the Green Corridor maintained a success rate for dynamic emergency vehicle pre-emption. Architecturally, the system achieved a 100% decision override latency of 1,556 ms and a Green Corridor success rate of 100% for life safety evaluations. The quantitative results show the effectiveness of zero-shot semantic LLM orchestration compared to other LLM controllers with heuristic rules. These results come from a controlled single intersection environment and may not guarantee similar performance when deploying traffic management in municipalities generally, but TrafficGen paves the way for traffic management to move beyond black-box numerical paradigms and into explainable decision-making. Finally, this paper is an initial step towards adaptive traffic management for smart urban mobility.

Introduction

Background

The rapid pace of urbanization worldwide has transformed modern transportation, stretching current infrastructure to its physical and operational limits. By 2026, the concept of the Smart City has evolved from a theoretical model into a data-rich reality, with billions of IoT sensors, connected vehicles (CVs), and smart infrastructure generating a torrent of high-velocity data. The critical question is: how can we turn this big data stream into dynamic, secure, and effective mobility?

Traditionally, traffic control relied on past trends and localized control. We are now entering an era where generative AI and multi-agent systems (MAS) can generate and anticipate complex human behavior even before it manifests on the road. TrafficGen represents this technological transformation, shifting towards an active, self-negotiating ecosystem rather than passive, reactive stop-and-go logic. By applying the machine learning agent concept to each object, such as a delivery drone or a commuter car, we can transition to more complex automation and achieve genuine urban orchestration.

Urban mobility is characterized by non-linearity and stochasticity. In a metropolitan gridlock, a single event, such as a double-parked delivery truck or a heavy rainstorm, can trigger a butterfly effect that spreads across the entire city and causes gridlock. Current systems and existing solutions

Existing traffic management systems can be broadly grouped into three categories, each with its limitations:Rule-Based Signal Control (e.g., SCATS, SCOOT): These industry standards utilize inductive loop sensors to detect the presence of vehicles and adjust signal timing based on set thresholds. They are reliable but reactive, unable to anticipate an influx of traffic until it occurs, and lack the capability to make complex trade-offs between different modes of transport (e.g., between a bus and five cars).Microsimulation Tools: Urban planners use these tools to simulate traffic flows. They operate as closed-loop systems; they can predict potential outcomes using past data but struggle to incorporate real-time behavior over time.Early-Stage AI and Single-Agent RL: In recent years, reinforcement learning (RL) has been introduced to traffic management. Nevertheless, most implementations are single-agent systems where a central AI attempts to manage an entire city. This creates a single point of failure and requires substantial centralized computing resources. Moreover, these models often lack a generative component, meaning they can only maximize scenarios they have encountered in their training data and are fragile when faced with unique real-world anomalies.

The general background and related context on smart mobility are provided by standard deep reinforcement learning (DRL) architectures, as well as generative diffusion models. TrafficGen examines LLM-driven semantic agent orchestration as an alternative smart mobility control paradigm. The proposed framework is based on zero-shot semantic reasoning and natural language state representations, enabling explainable and real-time traffic signal optimization and dynamic life-safety pre-emption, rather than relying on black-box numerical policy maps or complex continuous state-space optimizations.

Motivation

main driving force behind trafficking is the necessity to bridge the gap between fixed city planning and the reality of mobile mobility. Traffic management in cities is approaching zero margins as urban density increases. The potential of Generative AI as a crystal ball, enabling the simulation of millions of what-if scenarios in a digital twin environment, and only altering a single signal in the physical world, motivates this model.

Additionally, the transition to autonomous and connected cars requires a system capable of communicating in their language. TrafficGen aims to develop a so-called Universal Traffic Language through which infrastructure and vehicles negotiate space and time. This collaborative effort is essential for transitioning from egoistic driving (where each car attempts to save time) to socially optimal driving (where the entire network travels faster).

The environmental impact is negligible. We can significantly reduce the carbon footprint of each trip by minimizing the stop-and-go nature of urban driving through adaptive signal control and smart routing.

Moreover, we are driven by safety. A high proportion of accidents in urban areas occur at intersections due to conflicting intentions. Utilizing pedestrians as agents in the generative model allows it to anticipate perilous interactions in advance, modifying signal timings or notifying connected vehicles to decelerate ahead of time.

Although urban signal optimization directly impacts vehicle emissions, the environmental benefits in this study—including lower fuel consumption and reduced carbon footprint from decreased vehicle idling durations (Wait Burden Score reductions)—are modeled as derived theoretical estimates rather than directly measured tailpipe emissions.

Finally, this research is fueled by the democratization of smart mobility. TrafficGen can be implemented in new Smart Cities where decades of historical data required by traditional systems may not be available, by generating models that can be trained with minimal real-world data to extrapolate the rest.

Research work description and goals

Literature review

From basic mechanical timers to highly adaptive systems powered by artificial intelligence, the evolution of traffic signal control (TSC) systems illustrates an ongoing quest to improve urban mobility. This literature review summarizes this trend and identifies the main challenges encountered in the existing SOTA reinforcement learning methods, while also laying the groundwork for the use of generative AI and large language models (LLMs) in cyber-physical infrastructure.

Early work in intersection management primarily focused on the fixed-time deterministic approach, often implemented through Webster’s formula for determining cycle lengths to minimize delay (Webster, 1958). Although these methods were essential, they were inflexible and unable to cope with the stochastic variations in traffic volume. Physical induction loops were deployed, and manuals were developed for traffic engineering to standardize actuated control (Roess et al., 2004; Koonce et al., 2008). In the late 1980s, adaptive traffic control systems (ATCS) were designed to address the non-stationary traffic of urban networks, such as the Split Cycle Offset Optimization Technique (SCOOT) (Hunt et al., 1981) and SCATS (Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System) (Lowrie, 1990). These systems use heuristic algorithms to dynamically change signal phases based on actual sensor data. However, they cannot respond to micro-level, zero-shot anomalies like sudden multi-vehicle incidents.

The introduction of deep learning has led to a paradigm shift from traditional model-based methods to reinforcement learning (RL) for traffic optimization in control systems (El-Tantawy et al., 2013). RL agents learn optimal policies by continuously interacting with simulated environments based on Markov decision processes (Sutton and Barto, 2018). As the size and complexity of intersection networks increased, the use of isolated RL was found insufficient, leading to the introduction of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) (Chu et al., 2020).

In recent years, MARL has emerged as the SOTA for traffic signal control, as evidenced in the literature. Wei et al. presented PressLight, which optimized signals by computing the “max-pressure” between incoming and outgoing lanes (Wei et al., 2019a). Subsequently, CoLight used graph attention networks for spatial communication between adjacent intersection agents, achieving significant improvements in network-level flow (Wei et al., 2019b). Meta-Learning, developed by MetaLight, addresses the generalization problem across numerous intersection topologies within MARL frameworks, enabling agents to learn quickly for new traffic distributions (Zang et al., 2020). Other notable frameworks, such as FRAP (Zheng et al., 2019), utilized phase competition modeling to enhance agent learning.

While MARL systems may perform well in simulation, they face significant deployment challenges (Dafoe et al., 2021). They suffer from the “curse of dimensionality”, necessitating vast numbers of compute nodes (GPU clusters) to train in continuous state spaces (Chu et al., 2020). Most importantly for public infrastructure, MARL networks are “black boxes” (Guidotti et al., 2018). If an RL agent makes an erroneous action or decision due to an accident, it becomes challenging for human auditors to comprehend the rationale behind the decision based on the underlying neural weights (Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020).

The emergent capabilities of natural language processing (NLP) models, typically trained to generate text, have shown that large language models (LLMs) can possess emergent reasoning and logic abilities (Brown et al., 2020; Bubeck et al., 2023) to overcome the constraints of RL. LLMs can break down complex, multi-step optimization problems into logical, sequential deductions through various methods such as chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022).

LLMs can now interact with the outside world, reasoning and acting as represented in the reasoning and acting (ReAct) paradigm (Yao et al., 2023). This ability has been advanced by models, including Google’s Gemini, which deliver ultra-low latency and multimodal inference essential for real-time applications (Gemini Team et al., 2023). LLMs are currently being studied for use in autonomous driving systems and transportation routing (Xu et al., 2024), where semantic reasoning shows great promise for understanding complex spatial rules without specialized mathematical training.

Recently, the concept of single LLMs has been extended into multi-agent systems (MAS), where multiple personality-specific LLMs collaborate to achieve complex tasks (Wang et al., 2024). For instance, AutoGen and LangGraph enable decentralized negotiation, with agents exchanging contextual states, discussing results, and reaching a consensus based on algorithms (Wu et al., 2023; Dafoe et al., 2021). This multi-agent semantic architecture can be directly applied to urban traffic, with specialized agents (standard optimizers vs. emergency pre-emptors) performing functionally on the right-of-way using deterministic logic (without a probabilistic reward) (Han et al., 2016).

Explainable AI (XAI) is a significant benefit of LLM-powered control systems. In the RL domain, explainability is a complex post-hoc surrogate model (Lin et al., 2024), while for LLL, explainability is built into the LLM’s capability to produce natural language logs that describe its cognitive reasoning before taking action, which is necessary for civil infrastructure auditing. Additionally, the literature suggests that Edge-AI deployment addresses the latency and connection concerns of cloud-based APIs. Recent studies have demonstrated that powerful LLMs can now run locally on low-resource edge devices, delivering deterministic, low-latency performance in decentralized settings, using activation-aware weight quantization (AWQ) and four-bit model compression (Khetan et al., 2025).

The proposed TrafficGen system fills the gaps in the literature by replacing the opaque and heavy computation of MARL models with a lightweight, transparent, and linguistically reasoned “Council of Agents”.

Objectives

The main objective of the TrafficGen research work is to develop a powerful, generative, and collaborative multi-agent system that can maximize urban mobility. Problem statement

Urban centers are experiencing a Mobility Paradox: the volume of vehicles and pedestrians is growing exponentially, yet physical infrastructure (roads and intersections) is not. Conventional traffic management systems are reactive and rule-based; they can only respond to congestion after it has already occurred. These systems rely on timers with fixed cycles or simple loop detectors that do not differentiate between an ambulance, a bus, or a private car, creating significant inefficiencies.

Moreover, existing traffic simulators cannot replicate the long-tail distribution of human behavior. Uncommon but significant incidents, such as a pedestrian jaywalking on a highway or a car stalling in a tunnel, cause systemic failures in current AI models due to their inability to generate such events. This results in:Economic Loss: Billions of dollars in lost productivity because of traffic delays.Environmental Degradation: Overproduction of CO2 by stop-and-go traffic.Safety Hazards: A high incidence of accidents at intersections due to a lack of coordination between motorized and non-motorized agents.

Technical specification

This section outlines the technical details of the traffic management system, generative AI, multi-agent system, and traffic forecasting. It addresses the technical specifications, architectural parameters, and feasibility constraints of the proposed generative AI (GAI)-based multi-agent traffic management system (TrafficGen). The system requirements reflect a new paradigm of semantic reasoning, explainable AI (XAI), and orchestration of the state-graph, contrasting with the previously discussed multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) paradigms, which were computationally intensive.

The system requirements are divided into functional and non-functional categories. Functional requirements specify the deterministic behavior of the multi-agent environment, while non-functional requirements outline the necessary levels of performance, security, and scalability for deploying the system in an urban setting. Non-functional requirements

The non-functional requirements outline the constraints and quality attributes to consider when designing the TrafficGen architecture, particularly in a mission-critical urban environment.

Ultra-Low Latency & Throughput: It must be possible to ingest telemetry, use the LLM, and then actuate the tool within acceptable human-reaction time limits. The prototype phase utilizes cloud-based APIs (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Flash) and will be followed by eventual 4-bit/8-bit edge quantization, which will reduce decision latency to less than 100 ms.

Deterministic Fallback (Reliability): The system should ensure that it is consistently up to date and available at 99.999% with a Rule-Based Round-Robin controller instead of the LLM whenever the API is rate-limited, fails, or hallucinates. A road traffic stop cannot be caused by software failure.

Scalability (Decentralized Negotiation): The architecture needs to be horizontally scalable. Eventually, agents at a particular intersection must be able to pass a small amount of token state information to adjacent intersections to facilitate “proactive green waves”.

Data Privacy & Compliance: The system should not collect raw visual data to comply with urban surveillance laws. Only anonymized telemetry (car count) should be retained, and the physical identifier should be removed as soon as possible within the context of federated learning.

Feasibility study

A thorough feasibility study was conducted to assess the transition from MARL to an LLM-based Council of Agents. Technical, economic, and operational considerations were examined.

Technical feasibility

Technically, the research is eminently solvable due to the latest developments in generative AI inference speed and state-graph frameworks. The “curse of dimensionality” in traditional multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) makes it challenging to simulate traffic control problems with continuous state space, such as those from MegaDrive simulations, without using large clusters of GPUs. We aimed to convert a continuous physics problem for the study into a discrete semantic logic puzzle by utilizing LLMs to replace the complex mathematical models in the research. The software architecture is highly feasible, supported by existing libraries, such as LangChain and StateGraph, that enable LLM reasoning to be bound to real hardware, with the usage tool being a Python execution program (set_light_green), thus avoiding the significant hardware dependency of Phase 1 MARL approaches.

Multi-agent workflow specification

The decision-making process is based on a deterministic state machine rather than a black-box neural network.Intake: This is where all data is directed to the SupervisorAgent.Supervisor: The Supervisor classifies the data into either STANDARD_ROUTING or EMERGENCY_ROUTING.Standard: The IntersectionAgent is given the state and uses the optimization prompt to balance the North, South, East, and West queues, writing the duration to the set_light_green tool as a variable <duration>. System design

This section outlines the architecture design and detailed design for each subsystem of TrafficGen. The system design elaborates on the theoretical requirements outlined in Section 3, detailing the computational framework required to implement a multi-agent Generative AI traffic management system. The design methodology is not based on black-box neural networks like traditional MARL but instead features an architecture that is decoupled, transparent, and linguistically driven, relying on a state-machine methodology.

System architecture

The macro-architecture of TrafficGen, as seen in Figure 1, is composed of four pipeline levels, with a separation between the physical layer and cognitive layer with memory. Each AI agent operates in a semantic space and is completely unaware of the various traffic sensor hardware manufacturers.Layer 1: The Physical & Telemetry Layer (Perception Input): The first layer consists of a decentralized hardware network comprising vehicle-to-everything (V2X) receivers, inductive loops, and Edge-AI vision cameras. This graph is utilized to exhibit data, which informs action intents and reasoning logs.Layer 4: The Actuation & Safety Layer: The Actuation & Safety Layer is the fourth layer. The final level of architecture provides semantic output concerning the real world. It helps map the text output of the LLM onto executable Python functions (set_light_green, override_signal). LLM multi-agent ontology

The core of TrafficGen’s knowledge is the ontology of specialized agents. Each persona serves as a different prompt for each problem, significantly impacting both the number of tokens used and the accuracy.

The first agent is the SupervisorAgent, which functions as the system’s classifier. Its main purpose is swift triage. It is configured via its system prompt to process incoming telemetry context for anomalies. Its role is not to stop traffic but to relay information. It is a simple binary classifier tasked with determining, based on contextual information, whether to run a normal workflow or an emergency workflow.

Following the SupervisorAgent is the IntersectionAgent, succeeded by the IntersectionOptimizer (standard optimizer). The IntersectionOptimizer is the default optimizer used after the IntersectionAgent. Its primary objective is to balance loads daily and prevent lane starvation. This agent acts in the capacity of a civil engineer, as designated by the system prompt. It must strike a reasonable trade-off between longer wait times (fairness to individual drivers) and high queues (greater traffic efficiency). It will produce a logical structure that outlines its reasoning before executing the standard Python script to turn the lane light green for a set duration.

Finally, the EmergencyVehicleAgent is “pre-empted” by the system. It is designed to provide assistance in life-saving and conflict resolution situations. It’s a system prompt that instructs it to disregard the default fairness measures it would typically use with the Intersection Agent. It merely advises how to create a “clear green” for emergency vehicles. In more complicated situations, such as two simultaneous emergencies with ambulances approaching from opposite directions, it will prompt the user to consider the conflicting variables between the lanes: Max Wait Time and Queue Length, and determine the most critical scenario before applying the signal override tool.

Emergency false alarm sub-routine and failure recovery

The detailed design includes a fallback mechanism in the emergency state handler to avoid excessive network disruptions from spurious and canceled priority requests. If an EmergencyVehicleAgent detects an approaching emergency vehicle, it transmits a high-priority interrupt signal to the IntersectionAgent, which immediately overrides the current phase and “gives” the emergency vehicle a green corridor. At the same time, a timer is set (Time_verify = 15 s) to trigger secondary spatial verification systems, such as the vision feeds from the intersection cameras or an uninterrupted V2X beaconing signal indicating that the vehicle is actually moving through the specific lane. If the vehicle changes roads before reaching the detection threshold, does not activate its siren or emergency status due to a canceled call, or if there is no match in the area of the vehicle before the end of Time_verify, then the vehicle triggers a false alarm. If a false alarm flag is raised, the system immediately switches to a different state; the priority green phase override is turned off with the standard yellow-red transition periods, control is returned to the IntersectionAgent to allow adaptive scheduling, and a natural language message is structured and written to the chain-of-thought (CoT) log, including details of the false alarm and the time and purpose for turning off the priority green phase override to meet civil governance auditing requirements.

Methodology and testing

The method developed for the TrafficGen system, as shown in Figure 2, represents a paradigm shift from conventional stochastic modeling to a semantic generative AI orchestration approach and multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). It describes the empirical research design, data serialization techniques for data input, concrete implementation of the state-graph architecture, and extensive stress-testing protocols to benchmark the GenAI system against a deterministic rule-based control system.

Methodology.

Research design and framework

The research paradigm assumes that traffic in the city can be reduced to a multi-objective, multi-state optimization problem, characterized by rapidly changing states and unpredictable edge cases (such as emergency vehicles with conflicting requirements). Traditional MARL struggles to process zero-shot edge cases due to the “curse of dimensionality” associated with continuous states.

To address this, we adopt a discrete semantic optimization strategy in the research framework. In contrast to continuous numeric weights that are optimized using gradient descent, this framework enables the execution of deductive logic operations over discrete semantic representations of traffic states using the pre-trained reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs).

The model is intended to be a state machine that functions as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). A state dictionary is a centralized dictionary that is replicated across a network of specialized LLM agents. Each agent is an individual cognitive node that contains heuristic prompts and deterministic execution tools. The system is designed to meet three operating objectives:Triage: Classify the environment between a standard congestion state and a critical safety anomaly.Negotiation: Employ a dynamic human-like reasoner to resolve conflicting lane demands, rather than using a cyclic timer-based approach.Transparency: Ensure explainable AI (XAI) by recording the intermediate steps of token generation (chain of thought) before the actuation of the physical hardware.

Stochastic simulation profiles and scenario setup

To evaluate the adaptive control performance of TrafficGen under more realistic traffic variations, test cases A, B, C, and D were transitioned from static, single-pass tests to stochastic simulation profiles. Instead of counting the number of vehicles at each intersection, the number of vehicles arriving at each intersection is modeled using a Poisson arrival process. (1) where λ is the stochastic arrival rate parameter and is randomly selected over the course of the simulation for each iteration based on the stress profile of the scenario.

Scenario A (Balanced Baseline Flow): This scenario represents symmetric traffic flows on all four approaches of each intersection with equal volumes, establishing a stable baseline for normal traffic operations.

Scenario B (Unbalanced Directional Surge): An unbalanced distribution of vehicles arrives at one major arterial corridor, characterized by a high volume of vehicles during peak hours and a low volume in the cross streets.

Scenario D (Dynamic Emergency Corridor Pre-emption): This scenario introduces low-frequency, high-priority emergency vehicle arrivals at random times and locations amidst high background traffic flow with stochastic characteristics. Each scenario was replicated N = 30 times with unique random seeds for vehicle generation and arrival intervals to ensure statistical significance. Results from all runs were combined to calculate the mean scores and variance bands of the evaluations.

Performance metrics and validation strategy

Empirical performance metrics were defined before testing the TrafficGen framework to rigorously validate its effectiveness. The validation strategy is based on running identical deterministic scenarios through the fixed rule-based algorithm (Phase 1) and the GenAI Council of Agents (Phase 2).

These are the main quantitative indicators:Wait Burden Score (WBS): A custom score developed to measure overall intersection inefficiency, with a strong emphasis on long delays. It is calculated as the sum of the product of the queue length and maximum wait time for all lanes l at intersection I, as shown in Equation 2. WBS=∑{l∈L}(ql×wl) (2) where ql represents the queue length (number of waiting vehicles) in lane l , and wl represents the cumulative wait time elapsed for the leading vehicle in lane l .

Linear queue metrics or average delay are common metrics used in conventional traffic management studies, but these metrics do not account for non-linear delay growth and extreme lane starvation. Linear metrics reduce a long queue with small queueing delays to the same value as a short queue with severe delays due to starvation. WBS, on the other hand, heavily penalizes long waiting periods. For instance, if there are two vehicles in the lane waiting for 400 s (w = 400 s), then the WBS is 800. If there are 10 vehicles in the lane waiting for only 20 s (w = 20 s), then the WBS is 200. The second scenario has five times more vehicles, but WBS is used as a compound metric that places a higher value on equity and starvation prevention (along with raw throughput).

To thoroughly evaluate the cognitive decision-making capabilities of TrafficGen, two baseline controllers are set up for comparison:Fixed-Time Round-Robin Baseline: A fixed, non-adaptive controller that alternates a 60-s green phase for each lane, without consideration of real-time queue accumulation or emergency conditions.Greedy Rule-Based Baseline (Max-WBS Controller): A heuristic baseline that dynamically computes the WBS value at each instant and gives the green-light priority to the lane with the highest current WBS. This baseline compares the ability of zero-shot semantic LLM orchestration to a pure mathematical, localized greedy heuristic for contextual reasoning.

Results and discussion

TrafficGen evaluation is a step in the development of urban AI. In 2026, as cities become automated in orchestration, the measures of success have shifted from simple throughput to encompass scenario resilience and multi-modal harmony. This chapter takes a closer look at the experimental results, where the generative multi-agent system is compared to the systems of the 20th century.

Hardware-driven strategic pivot and methodology justification

During the architectural design stage of TrafficGen, several candidate methodologies were thoroughly assessed but ultimately excluded due to key computational, hardware, and interpretability limitations. Among those considered and rejected was multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL).

Typical MARL approaches rely on expensive clusters of GPUs for offline policy optimization during training, struggle to converge in high-dimensional state space explosions, and are essentially decision networks that are ‘black-box’ in nature. The resulting numerical state vectors offer no transparency, making it impossible for civil infrastructure operators to audit and explain specific signal control actions. Likewise, although Latent Diffusion models provide excellent generative scene capabilities, their iterative sampling loops incur high computational costs and latency, rendering them prohibitive for real-time control loops.

To overcome these hardware burdens and limitations of explainability, TrafficGen made a strategic shift to LLM-driven agentic orchestration by combining Gemini 2.5 Flash with LangGraph. This paradigm offers two important technical benefits:Zero-Shot Semantic Adaptability: The framework converts the traffic state into a natural language context, enabling zero-shot reasoning over complex and long-tail traffic anomalies and emergency lane pre-emption without the need for multi-day GPU retraining or fixed pre-trained policies.Native Explainable AI (XAI) Auditing: The architecture generates human-readable chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning logs for each signal decision, rather than only providing output in the form of numerical state vectors. This meets the civil engineering demands for clear, verifiable, and auditable municipal infrastructure.

Latency trade-off analysis and edge AI deployment strategy

Empirical results have demonstrated an operational latency balance between semantic agentic orchestration and rule-based approaches concerning context reasoning. For the proof-of-concept of the digital twin based on text, the decision-making cycle of each phase evaluation included a delay ranging from 1,500 ms to 6,371 ms. Within a simulated environment, this latency is completely acceptable and suitable for macro-level signal phase adjustments (where signal phases last for 30–60 s or more). However, in the context of real-time, high-speed physical intersections, this needs to transition from a cloud-based inference model to a localized edge-based actuation model.

The framework was able to eliminate the compute cost of training the LLM offline on a GPU and utilize cloud APIs for zero-shot LLM reasoning with policy optimization as a continuous multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) process. However, cloud API network round trips create decision latencies in microsecond physical actuator safety loops of 1,500 ms – 6,371 ms, which are insufficient for fine-grained real-time execution boundaries (< 100 ms).

To achieve sub-100 ms latency in production, the deployment architecture must transition from models running in the cloud to models deployed at the Edge and processed locally. It can deploy quantized small language models (SLMs) on edge devices such as NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin NPUs, avoiding cloud transmission bottlenecks, by using quantization techniques like Activation-aware weight quantization (AWQ) or vLLM. This edge deployment model allows for inference latency < 100 ms, provides deterministic signal switching in microseconds, and guarantees the full operability of municipal networks during connectivity downtimes.

Impact of LLM zero-shot reasoning on decision robustness

This transition to LLMs demonstrated that zero-shot reasoning can replace months of RL training on certain urban mobility issues. The agents can extrapolate logic from the telemetry data to address “Edge Case” scenarios that they were not explicitly trained on.

The significance of this capability is that it has shown that smart cities can implement adaptive traffic logic much more quickly by employing generative models as the reasoning engine, rather than synthesizing localized models that adjust to the geometry of each intersection.

Computational efficiency and resource trade-offs

The pivot represents a valid engineering trade-off confirmed by the experimental results.

The system used 0 tokens in processing some local metrics, as indicated by the logs, and concentrated the costs on the reasoning phase. This renders the TrafficGen Prototype very practical in resource-limited contexts, including municipal governments that do not have access to large university clusters of GPUs but need to enhance immediate traffic flow.

Scaling potential and real-world generalizability

The final benchmark results show that TrafficGen achieved a 46.9 percent reduction in wait time, far exceeding the initial research goal of 20–30 percent. This suggests that the” Council of Agents” framework is highly scalable.

Because the system uses natural language reasoning to process telemetry, it can be generalized to any intersection layout—roundabouts, 4-way stops, or complex interchanges—without requiring a new reward function or structural change to the AI. This modularity positions TrafficGen as a flexible, next-generation solution for smart-city infrastructure.

While LLM reasoning is more advanced, the latency remains a bottleneck compared to traditional controllers operating at the millisecond level, making local model quantization the next step toward real-world deployment.

Derived environmental impact and practical infrastructure integration

To ensure clarity of the methodology, the reduction in fuel consumption and carbon output from vehicles (tailpipe emission reduction) is calculated based on an estimated decrease in cumulative idling time (WBS), without requiring measurements from a tailpipe sensor or a micro-scale model of chemical combustion. The system reduced the Wait Burden Score by a mean of 46.9% in stochastic test scenarios, indicating that it directly decreases excessive engine idling, a widely used proxy for urban fuel consumption and local emissions reductions.

Operationally, TrafficGen does not require any special hardware changes to connect with existing physical civil infrastructure and functions through software-powered LLM reasoning. A functioning pipeline would take a real-time stream of raw perception data (such as image frames, induction loops, or even LiDAR frames at intersections) and continuously convert that data into structured low-bandwidth state summaries in the form of text. These text representations are then passed into the LLM agent, which generates a reasoning log (chain of thought (CoT)), a natural language version of the reasoning, and a list of standardized API function calls like set_light_green(phase_id). Standard municipal signal controllers are seamlessly fed structured API outputs that enable flexible integration within a high-level language and traditional traffic hardware.

Conclusion and further enhancements

We are now on the brink of a new era in urban engineering as we conclude the documentation of TrafficGen. The shift from the 20th-century paradigm of reactive control to the 21st-century paradigm of generative orchestration is no longer a theoretical aspiration but a technological reality. This chapter summarizes the impact of the research work and outlines the roadmap for its development through 2030 and beyond.

Conclusion

This research presented a proof-of-concept software prototype, TrafficGen, to assess the feasibility of using a large language model (LLM) for agentic orchestration in adaptive traffic signal control. TrafficGen introduces a new paradigm for traffic control beyond numerical approaches by leveraging a deterministic, single-intersection text-based digital twin and an AI-based semantic Council of Agents framework, developed with the help of Gemini 2.5 Flash and LangGraph.

Unlike standard reinforcement learning methods, which rely on opaque numerical state vectors, TrafficGen uses a special semantic translation layer to generate human-readable chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning logs for each signal phase decision. This capability provides civil engineering operators with native explainability (XAI) and auditable decision histories. In empirical experiments, using a baseline controller with a rigid round-robin strategy (3,380 points), TrafficGen performed, on average, 46.9% better than the baseline controller (WBS = 6,370 points). Additionally, at the architectural level, in life-safety analyses, it achieved a 100% green corridor success rate (GCSR) with a 1,556 ms decision override latency (DOL).

These proof-of-concept empirical results are in a controlled single-intersection environment and do not claim to demonstrate the general effectiveness of zero-shot semantic reasoning for city-wide deployment of municipal systems but indicate the effectiveness of TrafficGen compared with black-box numerical controllers. This work is therefore a stepping stone towards a transparent, explainable, and adaptive smart mobility infrastructure.
