Predictive value of preoperative esophagram in postoperative outcomes after antireflux surgery
Authors: Adam De Jesus, Brooklynn Rylee Rickett, Matthew Smith, Sean Phillip Duminie, David Harris, Amber Shada, Anne Lidor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) significantly impairs quality of life. Antireflux surgery (ARS) improves symptoms when medical therapy fails, but postoperative recurrence and dysphagia remain concerns, emphasizing the need for improved preoperative risk stratification. This study evaluates whether esophageal transit time (ETT) measured on standard preoperative esophagram can predict postoperative outcomes after ARS and serve as an accessible method to characterize esophageal motility on esophagram and assist in preoperative decision-making.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 174 patients underwent Nissen or Toupet fundoplication (± hiatal hernia repair) between 2014–2025 at a tertiary academic center. Patients were required to have preoperative esophagram and ≥ 1 postoperative Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQL) follow-up; 92 patients also had high-resolution manometry. Preoperative esophagrams were independently reviewed by a subspecialized radiologist blinded to clinical outcomes. ETT was correlated with manometric findings and postoperative HRQL scores.ResultsReceiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis identified ETT threshold ≥ 5.375 s as associated with greater long-term symptom burden across all patient-reported outcome domains at 3–5 years (p < 0.05). Longer ETT was associated with more failed contractions and weaker distal esophageal contractions on manometry, and ETT showed sensitivity of 75–83%, specificity of 35–46%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 15–55%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 71–94%, positive likelihood ratio (LR +) (1.22–1.54), and negative likelihood ratio (LR-) (0.37–0.65).ConclusionETT greater than 5.375 s was associated with greater long-term symptom burden across all patient-reported outcome domains. Longer ETT correlated with higher percentages of failed contractions and lower distal esophageal contraction amplitude on manometry supporting physiologic relevance. While specificity is low, high NPV suggests ETT less than 5.375 s may serve as a simple, noninvasive screening to help exclude severe dysmotility and reduce reliance on manometry in selected patients. These findings support using ETT as an adjunctive risk-stratification measure to assist in preoperative decision-making rather than a standalone prognostic test.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13126-4.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a highly prevalent condition that significantly impairs quality of life, affecting an estimated 10–20% of the population and more than 75–150 million individuals in Europe and the United States [1]. Symptoms like heartburn, regurgitation, chest pain, and dysphagia can be life-altering [2]. Beyond symptom burden, chronic acid exposure may lead to long-term esophageal injury, including erosive esophagitis, Barrett’s esophagus, strictures, and adenocarcinoma [3].

Antireflux surgery (ARS), including Nissen and Toupet fundoplication with or without hiatal hernia repair, mechanically reinforces the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) to reduce pathologic gastroesophageal reflux and serves as an important alternative to medical management for appropriately selected patients [4, 5]. Current clinical guidelines support ARS for patients with medically refractory GERD or those who prefer surgical intervention [5, 6]. However, a substantial subset of patients continues to experience suboptimal outcomes, including persistent or recurrent GERD, dysphagia, gas bloat symptoms, continued need for proton pump inhibitors, or the need for additional medical or surgical therapy like repeat imaging studies, esophageal dilation, and repeat operation [4, 7, 8]. These varied postoperative courses highlight the need for improved preoperative risk stratification to identify patients most likely to benefit from ARS.

Currently, there is no unanimous consensus established to guide preoperative workup while ensuring optimal postoperative outcomes following ARS. Although current recommendations include esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), manometry, and pH testing in the preoperative evaluation of GERD, the most recent multi-society SAGES guideline noted in Key Question 1 that, while manometry may be helpful, a formal GRADE recommendation could not be made because of insufficient evidence [9]. High-resolution manometry (HRM) provides a detailed assessment of esophageal motility and categorizes dysfunction according to the Chicago Classification, including disorders of esophagogastric junction (EGJ) outflow and peristalsis [10]. HRM is routinely performed before ARS to exclude achalasia and other motility disorders that may worsen following surgery [11]. Despite its routine use, the utility and necessity of routine HRM remain debated, given the cost, patient discomfort, and limited impact on surgical planning when the esophagram is normal [12]. Some investigators argue that HRM should not be mandatory before ARS [13–15]. Several studies indicate that no HRM parameter reliably predicts postoperative symptom recurrence or the need for reintervention [16, 17].

In contrast, the barium esophagram is a non-invasive, inexpensive, and safe method which provides structural and functional information, enabling dynamic visualization of bolus transit, hiatal hernia anatomy, and the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) location, while also aiding in the detection of motility disorders such as achalasia and distal esophageal spasm [18]. Esophageal transit time (ETT), the duration required for a swallowed bolus to pass from the first rib to the gastroesophageal junction, quantifies the effectiveness of esophageal peristalsis and clearance function, enabling assessment of esophageal motility [19, 20]. Multiple studies have shown that prolonged ETT is associated with esophageal dysmotility and correlates with clinical symptoms. Patients with motor disorders such as achalasia, diffuse esophageal spasm, and scleroderma demonstrate significantly delayed transit [21]. Current SAGES recommendations do not include esophagram in the preoperative evaluation of GERD, but studies have found that when patients present with preoperative dysphagia and have delayed esophageal transit on barium esophagram, it is prognostic of poor postoperative outcomes [16, 22]. Overall, inconsistent radiographic definitions, variable recommendations, and term-to-term validation have been lacking, reinforcing our stance that the workup for patients with surgical GERD has many unanswered questions [9]. Measuring with a standard-view esophagram may offer a practical, reproducible metric for assessing motility and identifying patients at increased risk of postoperative complications. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to determine whether ETT correlates with HRM findings and predicts postoperative outcomes— measured by Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQL) questionnaire scores—following ARS, with the aim to reduce reliance on preoperative esophageal manometry if ETT is normal.

Material and methods

Patient population

This study is a retrospective cohort study of a prospectively maintained database of all adult patients who underwent Nissen or Toupet fundoplication, with or without concurrent hiatal hernia repair, at the University of Wisconsin between 2014 and 2025. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent a preoperative esophagram and completed at least one postoperative HRQL assessment at 6 weeks, 1 year, 3 years, or 5 years. All patients considered for antireflux surgery have an esophagram performed routinely. Fewer patients had HRQL data available at longer follow-up, so the 3– and 5–year time points were combined, and for each patient, the most distant available HRQL assessment was used. Postoperative reinterventions were also assessed through the most distant available follow-up time point and included any reintervention, endoscopic dilation, reoperation, and repeat imaging. A subset of patients who had also undergone preoperative esophageal manometry was analyzed separately. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of achalasia or had previously undergone operative foregut surgery All patient data were de‑identified and stored securely in REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) to ensure confidentiality. The study was approved by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (IRB) which included a waiver of informed consent.

Symptoms assessment

Patient‑reported outcomes were evaluated using the Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Health-Related. Quality of Life (GERD–HRQL) questionnaire, Eckardt score, Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI), and Reflux Symptom Index (RSI), all validated tools for assessing symptom changes following medical or surgical therapy. The GERD–HRQL instrument quantifies typical GERD‑related symptoms— including heartburn, dysphagia, odynophagia, gas bloat—as well as the impact of medications and overall patient satisfaction [23]. Each item is rated on a six‑point Likert scale: 0 = no symptoms; 1 = noticeable but not bothersome; 2 = noticeable and bothersome but not daily; 3 = bothersome daily; 4 = symptoms interfere with daily activities; and 5 = symptoms are incapacitating. A final question evaluates overall condition satisfaction and is categorized as “satisfied,” “neutral,” or “dissatisfied.” Total scores range from 0 (no symptoms) to 50 (severe symptoms).

The Eckardt score measures achalasia severity across four domains: dysphagia, regurgitation, chest pain, and weight loss [24]. Symptom frequency for the first three components is scored from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates absence, 1 occasional symptom, 2 daily symptoms, and 3 symptoms with every meal. Weight loss is scored as 0 for none, 1 for < 5 kg, 2 for 5–10 kg, and 3 for > 10 kg. Total scores range from 0 to 12, with values < 3 indicating treatment success and values > 3 suggesting suboptimal outcomes.

GCSI measures gastroparesis severity across three subscales: post-prandial fullness/early satiety (4 items); nausea/vomiting (3 items), and bloating (2 items). Each item is rated on a six‑point Likert scale: 0 = no symptoms; 1 = noticeable but not bothersome; 2 = noticeable and bothersome but not daily; 3 = bothersome daily; 4 = symptoms interfere with daily activities; and 5 = symptoms are incapacitating [25].

RSI is a validated patient-reported questionnaire used to assess laryngopharyngeal and extra-esophageal reflux symptoms, including hoarseness, throat clearing, excess throat mucus or postnasal drip, dysphagia, cough, globus sensation, and reflux-related throat or chest symptoms. Each item is scored from 0 to 5, with total scores ranging from 0 to 45; higher scores indicate greater symptom burden, and scores > 13 are generally considered abnormal in validation studies [26].

Radiographic methods

Radiological assessment of preoperative esophagram studies was retrospectively re-evaluated by two radiologists blinded to patient information and outcomes. The motility series is one of the most important standard components of the esophagram and allows assessment of the esophageal function, performed with a single swallow in the prone oblique position to avoid gravity assist. A common protocol includes ingestion of a low density barium suspension during high frame rate fluoroscopy (7.5–15 frames/second). Statistical methods

Analyses were performed in a Julius AI-assisted Python environment and reviewed by the authors with University of Wisconsin statistical support. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Welch t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Correlations were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients with 95% confidence intervals, and receiver operating characteristic analyses were used to evaluate candidate ETT cutoffs across long-term symptom outcomes. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis without imputation, all tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. [27]. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STROBE Statement to promote transparent reporting of this retrospective cohort study [28].

Results

Cohort assembly and baseline characteristics

A total of 367 patients were eligible for review. Of these, 78 underwent outside-institution esophagram studies, and 76 of those were excluded because the studies lacked appropriate functional motility imaging. Among 289 in-institution studies, 116 were excluded because of missing motility data, non-visualization of the LES to calculate ETT, premature termination of the fluoroscopic video sequence, or late cine start time. The final analytic cohort therefore included 174 patients, of whom 92 had preoperative manometry available and 82 did not (Fig. 1). The cohort had a mean age of 58.7 ± 14.4 years and mean BMI of 30.5 ± 4.6 kg/m2. Most patients were female and underwent minimally invasive Toupet or Nissen fundoplication, usually with concurrent hiatal hernia repair. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by ETT

When stratified by ETT threshold, 113 patients (64.9%) had ETT ≥ 5.375 s and 61 patients (35.1%) had ETT < 5.375 s. Baseline demographic and operative characteristics were broadly similar between groups, including age, sex distribution, BMI, weight, operative approach, and fundoplication type. The main differences were dyspnea, which was more frequent in patients with ETT ≥ 5.375 s, and hiatal hernia type distribution, with type I hernias more common in the prolonged ETT group and type III/IV hernias more common in the shorter ETT group. Detailed ETT-stratified baseline characteristics are shown in Supplemental Fig. 3.

Correlation analyses between ETT, manometric variables, and symptom scores

Among patients with available manometric data, longer preoperative ETT was associated with markers of impaired esophageal motility. Specifically, longer ETT correlated with a higher percentage of failed contractions and lower average distal esophageal contraction amplitude (Fig. 4; Supplemental Fig. 2). The strongest associations were between ETT and percent failed contractions (r = 0.444, R2 = 0.197, n = 63), ETT and average distal esophageal contraction amplitude (r = –0.327, R2 = 0.107, n = 51), percent failed contractions and 1-year Eckardt score (r = 0.453, R2 = 0.205, n = 23), and percent simultaneous contractions and 1-year Eckardt score (r = 0.48, R2 = 0.227, n = 18). Test characteristics of manometry and esophagram for long-term symptom outcomes

Test characteristics for poor long-term postoperative symptom outcomes were compared between percent failed contractions on manometry and ETT on esophagram using the 5.375-s threshold. Overall, ETT demonstrated higher sensitivity across symptom domains, ranging from 75–83%, compared with 30–67% for manometry, while manometry generally demonstrated higher specificity, ranging from 59–69%, compared with 35–46% for ETT. The negative predictive value of ETT was highest for GCSI and Eckardt outcomes, reaching 87% and 94%, respectively, suggesting that shorter ETT may be more useful for identifying patients less likely to develop poor long-term symptom burden. However, ETT had limited specificity and modest positive likelihood ratios across outcomes (LR + 1.22–1.54), indicating limited utility as a standalone rule-in test. Preoperative ETT was not associated with reintervention

Preoperative ETT was not significantly associated with postoperative reintervention, including any reintervention, endoscopic dilation, reoperation, or repeat imaging. Mean ETT was similar between patients who did and did not undergo any reintervention (6.09 vs 6.05 s, p = 0.509), endoscopic dilation (5.96 vs 6.07 s, p = 0.965), reoperation (6.89 vs 6.03 s, p = 0.150), or repeat imaging (6.02 vs 6.09 s, p = 0.848). Although patients requiring reoperation had numerically longer ETT, this difference was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that ETT was more strongly associated with long-term patient-reported symptom burden than with downstream procedural outcomes in this cohort (Supplemental Fig. 4).

Postoperative outcomes stratified by manometric thresholds

ROC analysis demonstrated that percent failed contractions had limited ability to discriminate worse long-term postoperative symptom outcomes (Supplemental Fig. 5). Across symptom domains, AUC values were approximately 0.58–0.60, suggesting poor discriminatory performance. These findings further support that conventional manometric thresholds alone were limited predictors of long-term symptom burden in this cohort.

Multivariate analysis of long-term symptom scores

We performed an adjusted linear regression model including ETT group, baseline symptom severity, age, BMI, and hiatal hernia type. We found that ETT ≥ 5.375 s remained significantly associated with higher long-term RSI and GCSI scores. Specifically, ETT ≥ 5.375 s was associated with a 5.61-point higher long-term RSI score (95% CI 0.84 to 10.38, p = 0.021) and a 5.09-point higher long-term GCSI score (95% CI 1.51 to 8.67, p = 0.005). Associations with GERD–HRQL (β = 2.32, 95% CI −1.49 to 6.13, p = 0.233) and Eckardt score (β = 0.81, 95% CI −0.24 to 1.86, p = 0.131) were not statistically significant after adjustment. These findings suggest that prolonged ETT is independently associated with selected domains of long-term symptom burden (Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients undergoing antireflux surgery, preoperative ETT was associated with several postoperative symptom outcomes and correlated with select manometric measures of esophageal function. Longer ETT was associated with higher percent failed contractions and lower average distal esophageal contraction amplitude on preoperative manometry, and patients with longer preoperative ETT demonstrated greater symptom burden at later postoperative follow-up across several patient-reported outcome measures.

These findings are clinically relevant because current GERD guidelines continue to emphasize upper endoscopy, manometry, and pH testing in the preoperative evaluation of medically refractory reflux, while also acknowledging ongoing uncertainty regarding the strength of evidence supporting specific components of that workup [9]. Concurrently, the ACG guideline states that barium esophagram should not be used solely as a diagnostic test for GERD [29]. Our goal was therefore not to suggest esophagram as a replacement diagnostic test for GERD, but rather to study if an esophagram-derived metric (ETT) could serve as an adjunctive screening tool during preoperative assessment. Given the lack of consensus regarding which preoperative tests are necessary, we sought to determine whether a normal ETT on esophagram might help identify selected patients in whom routine manometry could be deferred.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings suggest that ETT may help identify patients in whom normal esophageal transit makes severe underlying dysmotility less likely. Because the negative predictive value of ETT < 5.375 s was relatively high across symptom domains, a normal ETT may have value as a screening marker to help determine when routine preoperative manometry might be safely deprioritized in selected patients, particularly when the esophagram is otherwise normal and there is no clinical concern for a major motility disorder [30]. This idea is consistent with prior studies that critique the universal necessity of manometry before antireflux or hiatal hernia surgery, particularly when other elements of the preoperative evaluation are reassuring [12, 13, 17]. Importantly, our data does not support disuse of preoperative manometry altogether, since it remains critical for exclusion of achalasia and other important motility disorders.

An important finding of this study is that the relationship between ETT and outcomes was more apparent at longer-term follow-up than in the early postoperative period. This pattern may indicate that delayed esophageal clearance has greater relevance to persistent or recurrent symptom burden over time than to short-term postoperative recovery. The separation observed in later Eckardt, GCSI, RSI, and GERD–HRQL trajectories suggests that ETT may capture an aspect of esophageal function that is not fully reflected by static anatomic findings alone. This longer-term association distinguishes our study from prior work that has focused more on early postoperative dysphagia or immediate perioperative outcomes and represents a strength of our analysis [16, 22].

The ROC analyses identified 5.375 s as the threshold used for subsequent analyses because it performed consistently across multiple symptom domains. Even so, the predictive performance of ETT as a standalone test was modest. Specificity and positive predictive values were limited, and the likelihood ratios were not strong enough to support ETT as an independent rule-in test for poor postoperative outcome. In contrast, the higher negative predictive values suggest that shorter ETT may be more useful in identifying patients less likely to experience poor long-term symptom burden. Moreover, ETT was more associated with long-term patient-reported symptom burden rather than with harder downstream events such as dilation, repeat imaging, or reoperation. This likely reflects the multifactorial nature of reintervention after foregut surgery and the low event rate in our cohort. Accordingly, ETT may be more useful as a tool for counseling patients about expected symptom trajectories than as a predictor of procedural failure requiring additional intervention.

The correlation between longer ETT and higher percent failed contractions supports the physiologic relationship between delayed fluoroscopic transit and impaired esophageal clearance. The inverse correlation between ETT and distal contraction amplitude further suggests that prolonged transit may reflect weaker distal esophageal body function. Although these correlations were modest, they provide internal consistency between the radiographic and manometric datasets and support the concept that ETT may function as a convenient and cost-effective fluoroscopic marker of esophageal motility.

The manometry analyses in this cohort were less robust than the ETT analyses, but these findings should be interpreted in context. Only a subset of patients had manometry available, and abnormal manometric subgroups were small for several variables. In addition, patients with achalasia and re-operative foregut surgery were excluded, narrowing the physiologic range of motility abnormalities in the final cohort. As a result, the study may have been underpowered to detect clinically meaningful associations between several conventional manometric thresholds and postoperative outcomes. The weaker performance of manometric thresholds in this analysis therefore should not be interpreted that manometry lacks clinical value, particularly when exclusion of major motility disorders before ARS remains the primary purpose of manometry.

A substantial proportion of potentially eligible esophagram studies, especially from outside institutions, could not be analyzed because of insufficient images, which ultimately limited the statistical power of the study but also highlights an important limitation of current esophagram practice: lack of standardized acquisition for motility assessment. These findings emphasize its usefulness depends heavily on how the study is performed and recorded. If ETT is to be used more broadly in preoperative risk stratification, standardized acquisition and interpretation protocols will be necessary. This is an area of future study and validation.

In our adjusted multivariable model accounting for baseline symptom severity, age, BMI, hiatal hernia type, and prolonged ETT remained significantly associated with higher long-term RSI and GCSI scores, while associations with GERD–HRQL and Eckardt score were attenuated. These findings suggest that ETT may capture a physiologic component of postoperative symptom burden, particularly extra-esophageal/reflux-related and nausea/vomiting/gastroparesis-type symptoms, but does not uniformly predict all long-term outcome domains. Therefore, ETT should be interpreted as an adjunctive risk-stratification marker rather than a standalone predictor of postoperative outcomes.

Several additional limitations should be considered. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study and is therefore subject to inherent selection bias, incomplete data capture, and heterogeneity in clinical decision-making. Second, there was loss to follow-up in postoperative HRQL data over time, which necessitated combining the 3- and 5-year follow-up windows and using the furthest available time point for each patient. Although this approach preserved longer-term data, pooling scores beyond 3 years may have introduced heterogeneity in follow-up timing and influenced outcomes if the distribution of 3– and 5–year data was unequal.

Third, normative thoracic ETT thresholds are not well established, and the cutoff used in this study was derived from internal ROC analyses rather than external validation. While this represents a reasonable threshold based on manometric studies defining normal distal latency (DL) as < 8.8 s from pharyngeal swallow to the LES using the 95th percentile [31] and considering that the thoracic esophagus comprises approximately 80% of total esophageal length, the lack of external validation limits generalizability and introduces a risk of overfitting. Additionally, fluoroscopic transit measurements are technique-dependent and may be influenced by factors such as contrast volume, patient positioning, frame rate, and reader interpretation. This study did not assess the interobserver variability in the ETT measurement, which will be addressed in a future study to assess the reproducibility of this measurement.

In summary, these limitations suggest potential challenges regarding generalizability and applicability in routine clinical practice without prospective validation. The specificity and modest likelihood ratios also limit the clinical utility of ETT as a predictive tool, underscoring the need for further investigation. Additionally, our multivariable analysis is limited by the modest complete-case sample size and the low explanatory power of the overall models, with adjusted R2 values indicating that much of the variation in long-term symptom scores remains unexplained by ETT, baseline symptom severity, age, BMI, and hiatal hernia type. Finally, postoperative symptom questionnaires are inherently patient-reported and may be influenced by factors beyond esophageal physiology alone.

Despite these limitations, this study supports further investigation of ETT as a simple and potentially useful component of the preoperative workup for antireflux surgery. We hope our findings can help create a template for standardizing how UGIs are performed. Esophagram is widely available, less invasive than manometry, and already provides important anatomic information. If standardized and prospectively validated, ETT may provide additional functional information to help identify patients at higher or lower risk of persistent postoperative symptoms.
