Fostering human learning is crucial for boosting human–AI synergy
Julian Berger, Jason W. Burton, Ralph Hertwig, Thomas Kosch, Ralf H. J. M. Kurvers, Benito Kurzenberger, Christopher Lazik, Linda Onnasch, Tobias Rieger, Anna I. Thoma, Dirk U. Wulff, Stefan M. Herzog

Abstract
The collaboration between humans and artificial intelligence (AI) holds the promise of achieving superior outcomes compared to either acting alone. Nevertheless, our understanding of the conditions that facilitate such human–AI synergy remains limited. A recent meta-analysis showed that, on average, human–AI combinations do not outperform the better individual agent, indicating overall negative human–AI synergy. We argue that this pessimistic conclusion arises from insufficient attention to human learning in the experimental designs used. To substantiate this claim, we re-analyzed all 74 studies included in the original meta-analysis, which yielded two new findings. First, most previous research overlooked design features that foster human learning, such as providing trial-by-trial outcome feedback to participants. Second, our re-analysis, using robust Bayesian meta-regressions, demonstrated that studies providing outcome feedback show relatively higher synergy than those without outcome feedback. Crucially, when feedback is paired with AI explanations we tend to find positive human–AI synergy, while AI explanations provided without feedback were strongly linked to negative synergy, indicating that explanations are useful for synergy only when humans can learn to verify the AI’s reliability through feedback. We conclude that the current literature underestimates the potential for human–-AI collaboration because it predominantly relies on experimental designs that do not facilitate human learning, thus hindering humans from effectively adapting their collaboration strategies. We therefore advocate for a paradigm shift in human–AI interaction research that explicitly incorporates and tests human learning mechanisms to enhance our understanding of and support for successful human–AI collaboration.

Introduction

Combinations of humans and artificial intelligence (AI) have the potential to achieve better outcomes than either independently, a phenomenon called human-AI synergy (Fig.
1
A). However, understanding when and why this human–AI synergy occurs is currently limited. Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
recently conducted a preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis of 74 studies comparing the performance of humans, AI and human–AI combinations on the same task. The studies span a wide range of tasks, such as classifying images, predicting house prices or writing code. On average, human–AI combinations performed worse than the best of humans or AI alone (i.e., negative human–AI synergy). Positive human–AI synergy emerged when humans outperformed AI alone, but synergy was negative when AI outperformed humans alone. Other, plausible moderators could not explain synergy differences across studies. For instance, neither AI confidence nor AI explanations (helping humans understand how a model arrived at its output) led to human–AI synergy. Here, we argue that human learning is a crucial, but overlooked, facilitator of human–AI synergy (Figure
1
B and C). Re-analysing all studies covered in Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
, we show that the few studies that gave humans feedback tended to report positive human–AI synergy—especially when AI explanations were also provided. We argue that neglecting human learning leads to underestimating the potential for human–AI synergy. We therefore call for a greater focus on human learning to better understand and foster successful human–AI collaboration.

Conclusions about human performance and rationality can drastically depend on how a phenomenon is empirically studied
[
2
,
7
,
9
,
14
]
. For example, when people can sample — and thus experience — the probabilistic structure of a task environment through repeated interactions with the task, their inferences and decisions are close to what probability theory would suggest
[
14
]
. The same does not hold in setups where participants are merely given textual descriptions of the task
[
14
]
. Could Vaccaro et al.’s
[
18
]
disheartening findings of negative human–AI synergy be partly due to the way human–AI collaboration is typically conceptualized and empirically studied in the synthesized literature?

Learning, experience, and feedback are crucial for successful human judgment and decision making
[
11
,
14
]
. For example, learning from outcome feedback (i.e., being told the correct response after each case) helps people identify reliable sources of high-quality information

[
3
,
16
]
.
It can also help humans assess the accuracy of their own decisions and of AI input, and how best to integrate these insights into their final judgments
[
10
]
. Also, guiding humans’ attention to fewer, relevant task features (e.g., a predictive subset of medical diagnostic indicators) and indicating how to use those features to predict the outcome can further improve human judgments
[
11
]
.
Explainable AI may likewise help humans focus on relevant task features—and even indicate how the AI uses those features to predict the outcome—and thus facilitate human learning
[
8
]
. Such guidance should be particularly useful when humans and AI excel at different kinds of cases and humans need to learn to adaptively rely on their own judgment or AI’s advice
[
17
]
.
AI explanations, expressions of AI confidence and other meta information can thus serve as additional task features that can help humans develop adaptive strategies for when (not) to rely on AI.

To investigate the extent to which human learning and feedback are recognized in the literature on human–AI synergy, we coded and re-analyzed the studies reviewed by Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
, quantifying design factors likely to benefit or hinder human learning (e.g., provision of feedback or practice trials; coded separately for each experimental condition, see SI Extended Methods for a full list of codes and descriptive statistics).
Across all studies reviewed, participants typically took part in a single session with no practice trials and a median of 30 trials in the main task. In most cases, no outcome feedback was provided. Thus, the typical study in the review lacks design features known to facilitate human learning and decision making.
Focusing on the 10 (14%) of 74 studies that included experimental conditions with outcome feedback (see SI Extended Methods for details), only three studied how humans’ use of AI assistance evolved across trials; the rest reported average performance across trials. None of the studies experimentally varied whether outcome feedback was provided or not.

To test whether outcome feedback—especially when combined with AI explanations, which may guide humans’ attention during learning—fosters human–AI synergy, we ran a series of robust Bayesian meta-regressions
[
4
]
modeling human–AI synergy depending on whether outcome feedback and AI explanations were provided (see SI Extended Methods for details). We adopt Vaccaro’s et al.
[
18
]
approach to quantify human–AI synergy using Hedge’s
g
effect size to measure the standardized difference in performance between the human–AI combination and whichever of humans or AI alone performed better. That is, positive (negative) estimates indicate that the human-AI combination performed better (worse) than either humans or AI alone.

In sum, our re-analysis of Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
showed that the studies meta-analyzed largely neglect human learning. This finding is consistent with another recent review
[
13
]
surveying 124 studies on human–AI decision making, which found that only 6 (5%) studies offered outcome feedback—a finding those authors did not discuss.
Crucially, both reviews
[
18
,
13
]
focused on a subset of research fields that investigate how humans interact with AI and related decision support systems. Lai et al.
[
13
]
explicitly focused on human–computer interaction (HCI) research published after 2018.
Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
informed us that many studies after their literature search had to be excluded as they did not report all performance metrics necessary to compute the effect sizes for human–AI synergy, yielding a set of studies consisting primarily of HCI research after 2020 (personal communication, August 10, 2025). To more comprehensively assess the role of human learning in human–AI collaboration, our call for future systematic reviews and meta-analyses is thus twofold.
First, we highlight Vaccaro and colleagues’
[
18
]
call to develop and report standardized performance metrics, even when performance is not a study’s primary outcome (but, e.g., self–reported trust in AI). This would allow third parties to use the data for analyses such as those reported here.
Second, using broader search terms and including studies prior to 2018 would likely better represent further relevant fields such as human factors
[
17
]
and judgment and decision making (e.g., algorithm aversion versus appreciation
[
6
]
), thus broadening our understanding of how human learning contributes to successful human–AI collaboration.

Recently, Hullman et al.
[
9
]
concluded that many human–AI decision making studies do not provide enough information for participants to identify the best response, potentially resulting in a literature riddled with invalid—and overly pessimistic—claims about the rationality of using AI assistance. Moreover, within the broad design space of possible human–AI decision-making experiments, providing feedback or other learning opportunities is just one of many key choices. Task selection, timing and format of AI advice, incentive schemes, participant populations, and evaluation metrics all jointly define the region of the design space from which data are drawn—and thus shape the conclusions drawn
[
2
]
.
We thus advocate for a more systematic and theory-informed exploration of the design space of possible human–AI modes of interaction and of the corresponding empirical studies
[
2
]
(including experimentally varying the presence and type of feedback
[
1
]
).

Notwithstanding the neglect of human learning in the studies reviewed by Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
, our re-analysis suggests that outcome feedback—especially when paired with AI explanations—plays a crucial role in human–AI collaborations, potentially leading to positive human–AI synergy.
Considering also the broader literature on human learning
[
11
,
14
,
3
,
16
]
and the importance of study design
[
2
,
7
,
9
,
14
]
, these meta-analytic results highlight the need for the explicit study of when and how fostering human learning boosts human–AI synergy.

Supplementary Information

Methods

Data

Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
provided average human-only, AI-only and human–AI performance over all trials of an intervention. While it would be ideal to test our research questions about human learning as a function of how many trials a participant has already engaged with, only three of the 10 studies with feedback reported results as a function of trial number. Thus, the data aggregated by Vaccaro et al. does not allow for such detailed analysis of potential learning effects.

Robust Bayesian Model Averaging

We used a Robust Bayesian Model Averaging (RoBMA; Rpackage version 3.5) approach for our meta-regressions evaluating moderation effects
[
4
,
5
]
. This approach runs many models, systematically varying assumptions (e.g., absence versus presence of main or moderator effects, absence versus presence of publication bias) and then calculates a posterior effect-size estimate based on the average of all models, weighted by how well the models account for the data.
The resulting meta-regression models quantify the evidence for moderation using Bayes factors (
BF_{inclusion}
) and make it possible to calculate the conditional marginal posterior effect-size estimate for different moderator levels (i.e., feedback versus no feedback).

We used R 4.4.2. for all analyses. Our RoBMA models have the same three-level hierarchical model structure originally used by Vaccaro et al.
[
18
]
, nesting effect sizes within experiment and experiments within study. We relied on default priors as specified by the RoBMA authors
[
5
]
. See the model outputs below for detailed information on the models.

We fit all models using the spike-and-slab algorithm. Models were run for at least 45,000 samples after 15,000 burn-in and 10,000 adaptive samples. In case models did not converge, burn-in and adaptive samples were increased up to 25,000 and 20,000 respectively. Convergence was checked using the Gelman–Rubin criterion
\hat{R}

<
1.05, relying on the automatic convergence checks implemented in RoBMA.

To calculate the contrast between effect-size estimates of Hedge’s
g
for studies with versus without feedback, we extracted the conditional marginal posterior distributions from the RoBMA models and summarized the resulting posterior distribution of the difference in effect-size estimates using
tidybayes::median_qi()

[
12
]
. We also describe the posterior difference using the probability of direction, using
bayestestR::p_direction()

[
15
]
to quantify how much of the posterior distribution of the difference is greater than 0.
