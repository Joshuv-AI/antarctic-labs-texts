Disability prevalence and associated factors in Nigeria: policy implications and the path towards inclusive development
Authors: Gbenga P. Ishola, Joseph Kuye, Según Bakare, Kola A. Oyediran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with disabilities are a marginalized group, facing significant socio-economic and public health challenges in low- and middle-income countries like Nigeria. Understanding their characteristics is important for developing effective policies and interventions that promote inclusion and well-being.MethodsThis analysis drew data from the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey, a nationally representative survey that included a disability module. The analytical sample comprised 67,663 household members aged 15 and older. Disability was assessed using the Washington Group Short Set (WGSS) questions. Descriptive analysis was used to determine the prevalence of disability among household members, and a logistic regression model examined the associated socio-demographic factors.ResultsNearly 12% of Nigerians aged 15 and older living in households have some level of disability, with 2% experiencing severe disabilities. The most prevalent types of disability were visual (8.8%) and mobility-related (3.7%). Disability prevalence varied by state and region, with Imo State reporting the highest rate (26.4%) and Kebbi State the lowest (2.6%). Factors such as those age 65+ (aOR; 23.79, 95% CI: 20.22–27.97), primary (aOR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.27) and post-secondary education (aOR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.59–1.99), lower wealth (first quintile aOR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.99), residing in the Southeast (aOR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.13–1.61) or South-South region (aOR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.43–2.05), being widowed (aOR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.36–1.71), living in female-headed households (aOR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.06–1.33), and household size (aOR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98) were significantly associated with disability.ConclusionsThe study underscores the necessity of tailored policies and programs to meet the diverse needs of people with disabilities in Nigeria. Interventions should prioritize equitable access to education, healthcare, employment, and public spaces, specifically addressing vulnerable subgroups. To achieve inclusion and well-being, in line with the Sustainable Development Goals, it is essential to address socio-economic disparities, promote inclusive practices, and strengthen support systems.

Background

People with disabilities represent a significant, yet often marginalized, segment of the global population, and this is particularly true in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Nigeria [1, 2]. Epidemiological data reveal a significant disability burden in Nigeria. The 2018 General Household Survey Panel (GHSP) found that 6.9% of adults (aged 15+) experience functional difficulty, with a higher prevalence among women (7.5%) than men (6.3%) [3]. Prevalence is also influenced by location, with rural areas reporting a higher rate (7.5%) than urban areas (5.8%) [3]. Earlier, the World Report on Disability 2011 estimated that nearly 25 million people in Nigeria (about 13% of the population) live with at least one disability [2]. Furthermore, Thompson (2020) estimated that nearly 40% of people with disabilities in Nigeria have multiple impairments [4]. Specific health conditions significantly contribute to disability in Nigeria. For example, research by Oyewole and colleagues found a high prevalence of disability among Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) patients, with one study reporting 50.6%–63.0% experiencing functional disability and 12.3% experiencing global disability [5]. Similarly, 39.5% of people living with HIV (PLWH) in Nigeria experience disability, with marital status and education levels being significant associated factors [6]. In Nigeria, a country with over 200 million people, the prevalence of disability creates a complex socio-economic and public health challenge [7, 8]. Its diverse socio-cultural landscape also shows various ways in which individuals, groups, and families interact with people with disabilities [9, 10]. Understanding the unique characteristics of individuals with disabilities in this context is therefore essential for developing effective policies and programs that promote inclusion and well-being.

The concept of disability extends beyond physical impairments to include a broad spectrum of conditions, such as sensory, intellectual, and psychosocial disabilities [11, 12]. In Nigeria, as in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, traditional beliefs often associate disability with stigma and social exclusion [1, 13]. Studies from other African countries like South Africa, Uganda, and Kenya show how these cultural attitudes significantly impact the lives of people with disabilities, restricting their access to education, employment, and social participation [14, 15]. These findings emphasize the importance of understanding the experiences of people with disabilities within their unique social and cultural contexts.

Moreover, the Nigerian healthcare system, characterized by inadequate infrastructure and resource constraints, often fails to meet the specific needs of individuals with disabilities [16]. This lack of access to rehabilitation services, assistive technologies, and specialized care further restricts their potential for full social participation. Additionally, in Nigeria, adequate attention has not yet been given to developing a systematic process for including PWDs in political, social, and economic activities to promote their full participation and ensure their equal and equitable access to available resources and opportunities. Furthermore, the absence of comprehensive data on the profile of people with disabilities hampers the development of evidence-based interventions. Recent legislative efforts, such as the Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act of 2018 [17], demonstrate a growing recognition of the rights and needs of individuals with disabilities in Nigeria. However, effectively implementing these policies remains a significant challenge, and the availability of data and information is key to this effort.

Few studies have examined disability prevalence and associated factors in Nigeria, despite numerous global studies. Prevalence estimates vary widely, from 8.3% in Nigeria to 26.3% in Uganda among household members aged 5 years and older [1], while the WHO estimates that 1 in 6 people worldwide has a disability [18]. Additionally, Mitra et al. [19] assessed the global prevalence of adults with disabilities at 14% based on data from the World Health Survey (WHS) conducted between 2002 and 2004 [20]. Studies have found significant differences in disability prevalence based on socio-demographic, economic, and health-related factors, including age [1, 2, 21–27] household wealth [1, 21, 23, 28], place of residence [21–23] sex [2, 27], ethnicity [1, 19], religion [1], education [2], and marital status [2]. For instance, in Nigeria, disability prevalence was twice as high among married and never-married individuals aged 15 and older compared to those who were previously married [2]. These findings highlight how demographic factors are closely linked with disability rates and access to services.

Despite growing recognition of the rights and needs of individuals with disabilities in Nigeria, there remains a lack of comprehensive, up-to-date data detailing their unique characteristics within this specific socio-cultural context. Such data are crucial for translating legislation into effective, targeted programs. This study profiles individuals with disabilities in Nigeria using data from the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS). By examining their demographic, socio-economic, and health-related characteristics, the research aims to generate evidence to support the development of policies and programs that promote inclusion, empowerment, and an improved quality of life for this population in Nigeria.

Methods

Study setting and population

This is a cross-sectional study that further analyzes the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) [8], a nationally representative survey that included a disability module. Due to its cross-sectional design, causality cannot be determined. The 2018 NDHS was conducted to provide policymakers and program implementers with data to assess and develop strategies and interventions to improve Nigerians’ health and well-being. Data collection took place from August to December 2018 across Nigeria’s thirty-six states and the Federal Capital Territory [8]. The survey achieved a response rate of 99% [8]. It also provides indicators relevant to Nigeria’s progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Notably, including the disability module allows an evaluation of progress toward including people with disabilities, since disability is referenced in five of the seventeen SDGs, specifically in education (SDG4), economic growth and employment (SDG8), reducing inequalities (SDG10), accessible human settlements (SDG11), and data collection and monitoring (SDG17). Although this was the fifth wave of the survey conducted in Nigeria, it was the first to collect data on disability and the disability status of all individuals aged 5 years and older. The disability module was embedded in the household questionnaire and administered to the head of each household for each household member aged 5 and above. The household questionnaire listed all de facto residents, meaning those who spent the night before the survey in the household.

Study Design and Sampling

For the 2018 NDHS, a stratified, two-stage sampling process was used to select respondents. Nigeria consists of thirty-six states and the Federal Capital Territory, each of which contains both urban and rural areas. To ensure the sample accurately represented the population, a two-stage sampling method was applied. In the first stage, 1,400 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected from both rural and urban regions, with probability proportional to their size. While the NDHS (2018) collected data from 101,739 respondents aged 5 years and older, this analysis focused only on 67,663 individuals aged 15 years and above. This restriction aimed to improve the accuracy of disability reporting within households, reducing the tendency to hide disabilities among younger people due to stigma and discrimination. Additionally, limiting the data to those aged 15 and older directly connects the findings to the working-age population, enabling an assessment of their functional productivity and socioeconomic contributions. For detailed information on the 2018 NDHS sample design, response rates, and sampling errors, please refer to the technical report [8].

The study’s limitations must be recognized. Cross-sectional data prevent establishing cause-and-effect relationships. Additionally, relying on the Washington Group Short Set (WGSS) to assess functional limitations may not fully encompass the broader concept of disability, which includes social and environmental barriers. Reporting biases might occur because data were self-reported by household heads due to the sensitive nature of disability and related stigma. Lastly, sample weights were used to ensure the data’s representativeness (considering state-level allocation and different response rates), which was especially important given the very high overall response rate of 99%.

Data collection

Disability measures

The NDHS disability module evaluated difficulties in seeing, walking, hearing, remembering, communicating, and self-care using the Washington Group Short Set (WGSS) of six questions [29], a standardized tool designed for cross-population comparisons of disability. One benefit of the WGSS questions is that they are self-reported rather than based on clinical assessments, which can be costly to administer and are prone to non-response due to the stigma and discrimination often associated with disabilities [30]. The module was given to the head of household, who listed all household members; however, the disability module was administered only to those aged 5 years and older. The module asks whether there is no difficulty, some difficulty, a lot of difficulty, or cannot do at all in six areas of disability: seeing, hearing, walking or climbing steps, remembering or concentrating, washing all over or dressing, and communicating [29].

Following the Washington Group on Disability Statistics guidelines, responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” were used to indicate the presence of a disability for this analysis [1, 29, 30]. As a result, a dichotomous disability variable was created, classifying individuals as either “without disability” or “with disability.” Household members reporting “no difficulty” or “some difficulty” across all six domains were categorized as “without disability.” Conversely, individuals reporting “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in at least one of the six domains were categorized as “with disability” [1]. The data enabled the examination of the prevalence of disability across six domains (seeing, hearing, walking, cognition, self-care, and communication). It also allows for analysis by disability type and severity compared with individuals without disabilities. Severity is determined by a response of “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in any domain. Each domain is scored as “0” for no severe disability and “1” for severe disability. The total of these domain scores is then converted into a binary variable, indicating “severity of disability.” A score of “0” signifies no severe disability, while a score of “1” indicates severe disability in at least one domain.

Data analysis

Variables measurement

Outcome Variable

Disability status is binary: 1 indicates a household member with a disability, and 0 indicates a household member without one. The construction of the disability variable is described in detail above.

Explanatory Variables

Based on existing literature regarding factors associated with disability [1, 21, 28] and the availability of relevant data within the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) household member dataset, the following variables were included as potential predictors of disability: age, head of household sex, marital status, education, place of residence, household wealth, region, and number of household members. Age was categorized into six groups: 15–24 years, 25–34 years, 35–44 years, 45–54 years, 55–64 years, and 65 years and above. The head of household’s sex was defined as either male or female. Education was categorized into four groups: no education/preschool, primary, secondary, and post-secondary. Marital status categories were never married/single, married/cohabiting, widowed, divorced, and separated. Household wealth, a composite measure of socioeconomic status derived from household assets and characteristics, was divided into five quintiles, ranging from the lowest (first quintile) to the highest (fifth quintile). Geographical location was represented by place of residence (urban or rural) and region of residence (North-central, Northeast, Northwest, Southeast, South-South, and Southwest). Household size was included as a continuous variable in the logistic regression model.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate analyses were performed. The descriptive analysis aimed to understand the distribution of the study population, the prevalence of disability across different domains, and the types and severity of disability. For bivariate analysis, the chi-square test was used to examine associations between disability measures and the study population’s characteristics. We found that certain characteristics showed similar associations with disability for both men and women. Therefore, their data were combined for the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Regarding missing data, explanatory variables with more than 5% missing values were excluded from the multivariate analysis. It’s important to note that, overall, independent variables with missing cases had 30 or fewer missing instances. Given the dichotomous nature of the disability variable, a multivariate logistic regression model was employed to evaluate the effects of selected predictors. Binary logistic regression was conducted to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals for individuals aged 15 years and older with disabilities. The multivariate model included statistically significant predictors (p < 0.05) in the univariate analysis. The analysis accounted for the survey sampling weights and design. All statistical analyses were performed in Stata version 16, and visualizations were created in R.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

After reviewing our research project, the DHS Program granted us access to the data. Ethical approval for the 2018 NDHS was obtained from both the National Health Research Ethics Committee of Nigeria (NHREC) and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the ICF. Therefore, no additional ethical approval was needed for this study since the data are secondary and publicly accessible. For more information on the ethical standards of DHS data, please visit:https://dhsprogram.com/Methodology/Protecting-the-Privacy-of-DHS-Survey-Respondents.cfm.

Results

Prevalence of disability by type and States

The findings indicate that nearly one in ten household members (12%) aged 15 or older experience some level of disability in at least one functional domain: vision impairment, hearing, communication, cognition, mobility, or self-care. Additionally, 2% of the study population have severe disabilities in at least one domain. Seeing (8.8%) was the most common disability among Nigerian household members aged 15 and above, followed by mobility (3.7%). Disabilities in self-care, hearing, cognition, and communication were less common among those aged 15 and above in the sample population (see Table 1). Household wealth quintiles show that disability prevalence among household members increases with wealth for both men and women. For example, disability affects 12.8% of men in the poorest quintile compared to 13.9% in the wealthiest. For women, the corresponding rates are 19.8% and 21.6%, respectively. The findings indicate that rural residents have a slightly lower percentage of disability (11.9%) compared to urban residents (12.2%). Regional differences in disability prevalence are also noted, with the Southeast and South-South regions showing higher percentages. Specifically, the Southeast has 17.5% and the South-South has 17.7%. Women who are widowed (41.3%) or divorced (18.8%) have a higher percentage of disability compared to those who are single (4.1%) or currently married or cohabiting (12.5%). Household member sex also shows a small difference, with women having a slightly lower percentage of disability (11.8%) than men (12.3%). Lastly, the sex of the household head is strongly linked to disability prevalence. Households headed by females have a higher percentage of members with disabilities (19.4%) compared to male-headed households (10.8%).

Factors influencing disability among Nigerians 15 years and above

Table3 shows a logistic regression model assessing socio-demographic predictors of disability among Nigerians aged 15 and older, presenting both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (OR, aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Discussion

This analysis aimed to describe the characteristics and prevalence of disability among individuals aged 15 and older in Nigeria, using the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS). This source, which included a specific disability module for the first time, remains the most comprehensive and nationally representative dataset available, utilizing the standardized Washington Group Short Set (WGSS) to ensure reliable comparison across populations. The results show that about one in ten people (12%) in the target age group reports some form of disability, with 2% classified as severe. Visual impairments (8.8%) were the most common condition, followed by mobility issues (3.7%). These rates are consistent with regional estimates and the World Health Organization (WHO) global projection that 15% of the population live with a disability [1, 18]. However, interpreting these results requires understanding certain methodological limitations: the cross-sectional nature of the data prevents establishing cause-and-effect relationships, and since the data are self-reported, there is potential respondent bias, especially given cultural stigma. Additionally, the analysis only includes individuals aged 15 and older, excluding the important pediatric population, and the WGSS mainly assesses functional limitations rather than wider social or environmental barriers. Despite these limitations, this research offers essential foundational evidence for policy development and explicitly links disability inclusion with the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

The prevalence of disability increases with age, as this study demonstrates a positive link between age and disability rates. Specifically, both men and women face higher rates of disability as they grow older. For instance, among those 65 and older, 47% of men and 48.6% of women report having a disability. This corresponds with the decline in brain function that happens with aging. The brain’s capacity to remember and think reduces as people age. This pattern aligns with global trends and previous research in other countries, including Malaysia and Nepal, where disability rates are significantly higher among older adults [1, 21, 23, 28].

However, unlike a similar study in Nepal among older adults [28], this study reveals a somewhat complex relationship between education and the prevalence of disability. In this study, higher education is associated with a slight increase in odds (aOR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.27) compared to no education, whereas post-secondary education is associated with significantly higher odds (aOR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.59–1.99). The relationship between education and disability prevalence among household members here aligns with findings from Rwanda and Uganda, where lower education levels were linked to a higher risk of disability [1]. This suggests that education may serve as a protective factor against disability, potentially through enhanced access to information, healthcare, and improved living conditions.

There is also a common link between poverty and disability, as families with PWDs often have lower wealth accumulation compared to wealthier families. Poor families are more vulnerable to financial risks because the onset of disability can lead to additional financial commitments. Studies confirm that at any given time, persons with disabilities are more likely to live in low-income households, possess significantly lower overall net worth and non-housing assets, and experience notable reductions in wealth after disability begins [31, 32]. Unlike some studies, this research found a slightly higher rate of disability in households within the wealthiest quintile. The higher reporting of disabilities in more affluent households could result from increased awareness and access to diagnoses, suggesting that reported disability rates may not directly reflect the true relationship with poverty. Disability often significantly diminishes household financial resources, especially in resource-constrained countries lacking government support systems to cushion the economic impact, which could also lead to under-reporting of disability among poor households.

The prevalence of disability in Nigeria varies widely by state and region. Imo State has the highest rate at 26.4%, followed by Akwa Ibom at 20.1%. In contrast, Kebbi State reports the lowest prevalence at 2.6%. Differences in disability prevalence may be linked to socio-cultural factors and challenges in accessing healthcare related to disabilities [8, 9, 33]. The findings support social constructivist theories, indicating that disability is shaped by societal attitudes and perceptions within a specific context, rather than being solely an inherent trait [9, 10]. This suggests that how households and family members report impairments can differ across regions depending on local acceptance and views of people with disabilities.

This study revealed significant regional disparities in disability prevalence across Nigeria, with the Southeast (17.5%) and South-South (17.7%) regions showing notably higher rates and odds of disability (aOR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.13–1.61 and aOR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.43–2.05, respectively) compared to the Northwest. These differences highlight the complex factors influencing regional health outcomes, including socioeconomic conditions, where less developed regions often have limited healthcare access; uneven healthcare quality and resource availability, which disproportionately impact specific communities and hinder access to essential rehabilitation services; environmental and occupational risks, as regions with hazardous farming or industrial practices may experience more injury-related disabilities; cultural attitudes and stigma, which can influence reporting rates; and, to a lesser extent, conflict and insecurity, which can lead to increased injuries and mental health issues. This finding aligns with the relationship between disability and household wealth, as both the Southeast and South-South regions are more economically developed than the Northwest.

The study found a slightly lower self-reported disability prevalence in rural areas (11.9%) than in urban areas (12.2%), although the adjusted odds ratio for urban residence was not statistically significant (aOR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.89–1.09). This result contrasts with some literature suggesting higher disability rates in rural areas due to limited access to services and can be attributed to several factors [3, 23, 25]. One factor is the potential for underreporting in rural communities, possibly because of stronger social stigma surrounding disability or less exposure to public health awareness campaigns. Additionally, differences in disability types, lifestyles, and individual perceptions of"difficulty" in daily activities between rural and urban settings could significantly influence these findings. Moreover, limited access to healthcare and diagnostic services in rural areas might lead to the underdiagnosis of less severe disabilities [23, 25].

Another key factor to consider is selective migration, where individuals with severe disabilities may relocate to urban centers in search of better medical care and support. This can lead to higher disability rates in cities while decreasing reported rates in rural areas. Additionally, a “healthy survivor” effect in rural regions, where those with severe disabilities face greater survival challenges due to limited healthcare, may also influence the observed patterns. The unexpected urban-rural differences highlight the complexity of disability prevalence and indicate the need for more detailed research. Future studies could employ qualitative methods to explore perceptions of disability in different environments or conduct in-depth analyses of specific disability types and their environmental factors in both urban and rural settings. Such research would help provide a clearer understanding of these regional and residential disparities in Nigeria.

The findings show that the prevalence of disability was significantly higher among widows compared to those who are currently married or cohabiting (aOR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.36–1.71). This is consistent with previous research [1, 28]. It could be related to the stress and social isolation linked to widowhood, as well as the higher likelihood of older age and associated health conditions in this group. Household head sex also emerged as a significant factor, with female-headed households displaying a greater percentage of members with disabilities. This may reflect the socio-economic vulnerabilities faced by female-headed households, which could encounter more challenges in accessing resources and support for members with disabilities.

The slight decrease in disability odds with larger household size (aOR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98) is an interesting finding that deserves careful consideration alongside existing research. Although previous studies have shown a higher prevalence of household disability in smaller households [34, 35], this study presents the opposite. Possible explanations include a dilution effect, differences in household age composition, or increased informal support in larger families [36–38]. Conversely, research on the economic impact of disability [39, 40] may not directly address how household disability odds vary by size. The small effect size (aOR: 0.96) is statistically significant but also raises questions about its practical importance.

Policy and Programmatic Implications

Analysis of the 2018 NDHS data on disability in Nigeria highlights significant policy and program implications. Findings emphasize a critical need to change policies and societal attitudes to fully include and support people with disabilities, aligning with the SDGs. First, ensuring equitable access to essential aspects of life, such as education, healthcare, employment, and public spaces, is essential for the active participation of people with disabilities. This involves creating inclusive infrastructure, support services, and urban planning that benefit both individuals with disabilities and society.

Secondly, interventions must be tailored to address the diverse needs of vulnerable subgroups within the disability community, such as older adults, widowed individuals, and residents of specific regions. Contextualized approaches that use participatory methods to identify needs are essential for promoting well-being and social inclusion. Thirdly, protecting rights and fostering inclusion require reviewing and reforming legal frameworks, along with a fundamental shift in societal attitudes. Effective implementation and enforcement of laws like the Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act, 2018, combined with public awareness campaigns to combat stigma, are vital.

The study aligns with social constructivist perspectives, highlighting that disability is influenced by societal attitudes, policies, and environmental barriers rather than being solely a medical problem. This underscores the importance of development and humanitarian organizations prioritizing inclusive programs that address these social and environmental factors. Improving coordination platforms is crucial for promoting better inclusion. Development partners play a vital role by providing technical support through partnerships, advocacy, resource mobilization, and capacity building.

Although Nigeria enacted the Persons with Disabilities Act (2018), its effective implementation remains a challenge. Collective efforts are needed to gather support from government officials at all levels to turn legislation into real action. Accurate disability data is vital for effective policymaking and targeted programs. Investing in strong national data-collection systems and using standardized tools such as the WGSS is essential for making evidence-based decisions, allocating resources, and tracking progress toward the SDGs.

This study highlights the importance of a multi-faceted approach to tackling socio-economic disparities, fostering inclusive practices, and strengthening support systems for people with disabilities. Implementing evidence-based policies and programs will help Nigeria make significant progress toward achieving the SDGs and ensuring the inclusion and well-being of all its citizens.

Conclusions

This study shows that a significant number of Nigerians aged 15 and older have disabilities, highlighting the complex relationship between various demographic, socio-economic, and health-related factors. The findings offer evidence to support the creation of policies and programs that foster inclusion, non-discrimination, empowerment, and an improved quality of life for this population in Nigeria. Additionally, it highlights the importance of the government effectively enforcing the Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act, 2018 by the government, and the need for consistent public awareness campaigns to combat prejudice.
