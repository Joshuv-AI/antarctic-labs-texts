Lactoferrin-Modified Ultra-Sustained Release Propofol Nanoparticles for Multi-Pathway Therapy of Depression via Intraperitoneal Injection
Authors: Zhenyi Wu, Junze Deng, Shunlian Li, Lu Yu, Hang Zhang, Yufeng Xun, Jing Bian, Jing Liu, Jianbo Zhang, Pu Lei, Qingyan Ma, Yajuan Fan, Zai Yang, Feng Zhu, Xiancang Ma, Daocheng Wu, Shuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression imposes a significant burden on global public health, and traditional antidepressants are limited by slow onset, low efficacy, and high recurrence rates. Intraperitoneal injection of propofol can rapidly exert anti-anhedonic effects by regulating the VTA–NAc dopaminergic and GABAergic signaling pathways. However, its application in depression treatment is limited by rapid systemic clearance, poor brain permeability, and significant systemic toxicity. Moreover, the simple lipophilic structure of propofol and the limited drug-loading and long-term controlled-release performance of conventional physically encapsulated nanocarriers hinder the development of long-acting propofol formulations.MethodsLactoferrin-functionalized PVA-propofol nanoparticles (LF-PVA@Pro NPs) were prepared based on the strong intermolecular affinity between PVA and propofol, as validated by molecular dynamics simulations. Their physicochemical properties, in vitro release behavior, brain-targeting efficiency, biosafety, and in vivo antidepressant efficacy were systematically characterized using CRS-induced depressive mouse models through multiple behavioral and histological assessments.ResultsLF-PVA@Pro NPs achieved a drug loading of 19.3% and exhibited sustained in vitro release for 100 h. Following intraperitoneal injection, the formulation maintained therapeutic brain concentrations for 100 h (6–8-fold longer than free propofol) and increased cerebral drug accumulation by 2.5-fold. Its LD50 was 2802 mg/kg, 2.36-fold higher than that of free propofol, ensuring safety at the therapeutic dose of 40 mg/kg. In vivo studies demonstrated that LF-PVA@Pro NPs markedly alleviated depressive- and anxiety-like phenotypes, reduced VTA/NAc neuronal injury, and restored c-Fos, GABA, and DAT expression, thereby reactivating reward circuits.ConclusionLF-modified ultra-sustained brain-targeted nanoparticles enable multi-pathway long-term depression intervention, offering a translatable strategy to repurpose propofol as a rapid-onset antidepressant for chronic stress-related depression.

Introduction

Depression is a common and highly disabling mental disorder, mainly characterized by persistent low mood, emotional blunting, cognitive dysfunction, and suicidal ideation. It has become a major global public health concern.1,2 Although a variety of antidepressants and psychotherapeutic approaches are currently available in clinical practice, conventional therapeutic strategies remain limited by delayed onset, low remission rates, high recurrence rates, and the development of treatment tolerance in some patients.3,4 In recent years, research on the pathophysiological mechanisms of depression has extended beyond the monoamine deficiency hypothesis to focus on synaptic plasticity impairment, neuroinflammation, and imbalances in excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitter release (such as GABA).5,6 Among these mechanisms, anhedonia, a core symptom of depression, is closely associated with functional abnormalities in the mesolimbic dopamine system, particularly the reward pathway connecting the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the nucleus accumbens (NAc).7–9 Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic strategies capable of rapidly regulating neurotransmitter release, restoring synaptic function, and enhancing reward-circuit activity represents an important direction in depression research.

A popular intravenous anesthetic with quick onset, quick metabolism, and accurate pharmacological control is propofol (Pro). Its anesthetic effects are primarily mediated through enhancement of GABA receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission.10,11 However, recent studies have shown that intraperitoneal administration of propofol not only modulates GABAergic neurotransmission but also influences glutamatergic transmission, dopaminergic signaling, neuroinflammatory responses, and synaptic plasticity. This administration route can significantly prolong the in vivo retention of propofol while reducing systemic toxicity.12 In particular, recent studies have demonstrated that propofol can rapidly improve apathetic-like behaviors induced by chronic stress by regulating dopamine transporter function, increasing dopamine levels in the ventral tegmental area, and activating reward-related neuronal circuits.13–16 These findings suggest that propofol has considerable potential as a rapid-acting antidepressant candidate. A prerequisite for achieving these therapeutic mechanisms is maintaining an effective concentration of propofol in the brain over an extended period, which is essential for improving therapeutic efficacy. However, few studies have addressed this issue because intraperitoneal administration of propofol still faces several major challenges. As a small-molecule drug, propofol is widely distributed throughout the body and undergoes rapid systemic distribution and clearance after administration, resulting in limited sustained brain exposure and a relatively short duration of action following a single dose. In addition, the systemic toxicity of propofol limits the administrable dose. Repeated administration to maintain antidepressant efficacy may increase the treatment burden and the risk of propofol-related adverse reactions.17 Therefore, reducing systemic toxicity, prolonging the retention time of propofol, and improving its brain-targeted delivery efficiency are essential for maximizing its antidepressant efficacy and facilitating its clinical translation.

The development of nanomedicine-based delivery systems offers promising solutions to address these challenges. Well-designed nanoparticles can improve the aqueous dispersibility and stability of hydrophobic drugs, regulate drug release behavior, and optimize drug distribution in vivo. Although numerous studies have reported the application of nanoparticles for depression treatment,18–20 there have been very few reports on propofol-based nanoparticles for depression therapy. Owing to its simple molecular structure and strong lipophilicity, propofol lacks functional groups suitable for stable coordination, self-assembly, or efficient cross-linking. To date, most propofol nanoparticles have relied on physical encapsulation strategies, such as liposomes, emulsions, and polymer microgels. However, these n nanoparticles generally suffer from drug leakage, poor storage stability, rapid drug release, and low drug-loading capacity.21 Kazi et al developed a lipid-based self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery system (SNEDDS) for intravenous delivery of propofol; however, the propofol loading capacity was only 5% (w/w).22 Moreover, Chen et al constructed propofol nano-microemulsions based on a COO-PEG-b-PDLA/PLDA diblock copolymer, which improved the aqueous dispersibility, blood compatibility, and pharmacokinetic behavior of propofol. However, the structural stability of these microemulsions remained limited. Experimental results showed that the microemulsions remained stable for only approximately 50 min, and drug release occurred rapidly. At pH 4.5, nearly complete release occurred within 30 min, whereas at pH 7.4, the cumulative release reached approximately 97% within 120 min.23 Therefore, polymer microemulsion-based propofol nanoparticles still face challenges related to colloidal stability and insufficient long-term controlled-release capability. Additionally, Husna et al systematically reviewed the stability and compatibility of propofol injection emulsions, reporting that the stability of propofol emulsions is generally less than 24 h, whereas the physical stability of opened MCT/LCT emulsions is approximately 6 h and that of EDTA-containing LCT emulsions is approximately 24 h. Furthermore, propofol emulsions are incompatible with many intravenously administered medications, which may result in precipitation, oil droplet enlargement, phase separation, or phase inversion. Therefore, although conventional propofol emulsions can overcome the poor aqueous solubility of propofol, they still exhibit significant limitations in physical stability, storage stability, and particularly in achieving long-term sustained release following intraperitoneal administration.24 Thus, developing high-performance propofol nanoparticles capable of ultra-sustained release and efficient brain targeting to maintain long-lasting therapeutic concentrations has become a key challenge for improving the antidepressant efficacy of propofol.

Given that conventional physical encapsulation strategies cannot satisfy the requirements for propofol-based depression therapy, we sought to develop high-loading propofol n nanoparticles based on molecular interactions. In our previous study, the introduction of glyceryl monooleate (GMO) promoted the co-assembly of propofol and etomidate within a nanosystem, achieving a total drug loading of approximately 20%, including approximately 10% propofol. However, this strategy relies on the synergistic assembly of multiple drugs with a bridging molecule and is therefore not fully applicable to the stable encapsulation of propofol alone. We hypothesized that if the carrier material itself exhibited strong molecular interactions with propofol, both the drug-loading capacity and sustained-release performance could be markedly improved. Therefore, molecular simulations were performed to investigate the interactions between propofol and different carrier materials. The results revealed that poly (vinylalcohol) (PVA) exhibited a relatively high binding free energy with propofol (−482.43 kcal/mol), suggesting that allowing PVA to fully interact with propofol prior to nanoparticle formation could substantially increase propofol loading and prolong its sustained release in vivo. Based on this rationale, we developed lactoferrin-modified brain-targeting propofol nanoparticles capable of prolonged propofol release, efficient brain-targeted delivery, and sustained improvement of depressive behaviors (Scheme1). In this system, PVA served as the drug-encapsulation matrix, first stabilizing propofol through intermolecular interactions before nanoparticle solidification to produce PVA@Pro nanoparticles. Subsequently, lactoferrin, a brain-targeting ligand, was surface-coated onto the nanoparticle surface to generate LF-PVA@Pro NPs (Scheme 1). The resulting nanoparticles achieved a propofol loading of 19.3% and remained stable for up to 6 months after freeze-drying. Moreover, it significantly enhanced brain delivery efficiency, increasing cerebral drug accumulation by approximately 2.5-fold compared with free propofol. More importantly, following intraperitoneal administration, LF-PVA@Pro NPs maintained therapeutic brain concentrations for up to 100 h, representing a sustained-release duration 6–8-fold longer than that of free propofol. Compared to free propofol, its LD50 was 2.36-fold greater at 2802 mg/kg (Table S3-S5). As a result, the study’s therapeutic dose of 40 mg/kg propofol showed a very good safety profile. Following treatment with LF-PVA@Pro NPs, the experimental results showed significant improvements in depression-related phenotypes. To varied degrees, the chronic restraint stress (CRS) model’s behavioral impairments were mitigated by both free propofol and LF-PVA@Pro NPs. However, the elevated plus maze (EPM, p < 0.01) and open field test (OFT, p < 0.05) showed that free propofol only slightly reduced CRS-induced anhedonia, behavioral despair, and anxiety-like behaviors. In contrast, treatment with LF-PVA@Pro NPs significantly decreased immobility time in the tail suspension test (p < 0.01), increased sucrose preference (p < 0.001), and improved exploratory behavior in the center region of the OFT (p < 0.0001) and the open arms of the EPM (p < 0.001), suggesting a significant attenuation of CRS-induced behavioral despair, anhedonia, and anxiety-like behaviors. Free propofol reduced neuronal damage in the VTA and NAc by around 40% and 30%, respectively, when compared to the CRS group, according to histological examination based on H&E staining injury ratings and Nissl-positive neuron counts. Nissl staining showed partial restoration of neuronal populations, with Nissl-positive cell numbers recovering to approximately 1.66-fold and 1.20-fold of those in the CRS group in the VTA and NAc, respectively, indicating a limited neuroprotective effect. In contrast, LF-PVA@Pro NPs markedly reduced tissue injury in both brain regions, decreasing H&E injury scores by approximately 60% and 55% in the VTA and NAc, respectively. Consistently, Nissl staining demonstrated a greater recovery of neuronal integrity, with Nissl-positive cell numbers reaching approximately 1.92-fold and 1.34-fold of those in the CRS group. Additionally, immunofluorescence research revealed that while free propofol enhanced aberrant neuronal function to some extent, LF-PVA@Pro NPs had far greater effects. Both the VTA and NAc showed a large rise in c-Fos fluorescence intensity (p < 0.0001) and a considerable recovery of GABA expression (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, recovery of c-Fos neuronal activity, GABAergic functional remodeling, and dopamine transporter (DAT)-associated reward-circuit regulation were all more successfully supported by LF-PVA@Pro NPs, suggesting improved modulation of important brain regions and superior central antidepressant efficacy. Collectively, this study demonstrates brain-targeted nanoparticles capable of sustained intracerebral delivery with multitarget therapeutic effects, providing long-acting neuroprotection and coordinated repair of the VTA–NAc dopamine/GABA signaling pathways. These findings indicate that intraperitoneally administered ultra-sustained-release propofol nanoparticles represent an effective strategy for depression treatment with considerable potential for future clinical translation.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Profofol (Pro), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), Tween 80, glutaraldehyde, hydrochloric acid, anhydrous ethanol and fluorescent colourant IR780 iodide were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Carbon-supported copper networks were acquired from Beijing Zhongjing Kaiyi Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cut-off threshold (MWCO) of 3500 Da were provided by MYM Biotechnology (Maryland, USA). The isoflurane was acquired from RWD Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China); the primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining - including anti-DAT, anti-GABA and anti-c-Fos - were purchased from Hangzhou Hua’an Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China); unless otherwise stated, all chemicals and reagents were of analytical or higher quality and were used without additional purification.

Cells and Animal’ s Model

Mouse hippocampus neuronal cells (HT22) were acquired from Puno Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured in the modified Dulbecco culture medium for pulmonary epithelium incubation (DMEM), supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum (FBS). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a moistened incubator containing 5% CO2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Thermo Forma 311, United States). Male mice of strain C57BL/6 (age: 7–8 weeks; weight: 20–25 g; SPF class) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Centre of the Faculty of Medicine of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The mice were raised in controlled environmental conditions (25 ± 2 C, 30 ± 10% relative humidity), with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and received a standard powdered feed as well as drinking water ad libitum. Unless otherwise stated, the mice were deprived of food for 24 hours before the experiments, while maintaining free access to water.

Molecular Simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were realised using GROMACS software (version 2025). Under the GAFF2 force field, the complex was positioned in the centre of a rectangular simulation box, with a minimum distance of 10 Å between its surface and the walls of the box. The system was solvated according to the TIP3P water model, and Na+ and Cl− ions were added randomly in order to neutralise the system and reach a physiological ionic concentration of 150 mM. Periodic boundary conditions were applied throughout the simulations. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Ewald Particle Mesh (PME) method, and the cut-off distance for short-range electrostatic and van der Waals interactions was set at 14 Å. The system first underwent 5000 steps of energy minimisation using the algorithm of the greatest difference in height, followed by 100 ps of molecular dynamics simulations with position constraint, carried out both in the NVT set and in the NPT set. Then, simulations of molecular dynamics of production were carried out under conditions without constraints. All covalent bindings were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, with an integration step of 2.0 fs. The pressure in the NPT set was maintained at 1 atm using the Berendsen barostat, while the temperature was regulated to 300 K using the speed rescaling thermostat. The production simulations were performed for at least 100 ns in the NPT set. The binding free energies of the carrier–propofol complexes were calculated from the equilibrated MD trajectories using the molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method. The corresponding GROMACS analysis modules were used to calculate the mean quadratic deviation (RMSD), the rotation radius (Rg), the average quadratic fluctuation (RMSF), the solvent-accessible surface (SASA) and other structural parameters.

Preparation of LF-PVA@Pro NPs

The LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles were prepared according to the following steps: first, 100 mg of poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) were dissolved in 10 mL of deionised water (ddH2O), in order to obtain a 1% (w/v) PVA solution; this solution was then heated and stirred at a temperature between 80 and 90 °C until the PVA was completely dissolved. Then 50 μL of Tween 80 to 0.5% were added to improve the stability of the emulsion, then the solution was cooled to room temperature. At the same time, 40 μL of propofol were dissolved in 2 mL of ethanol to prepare the organic phase. The EDC and the NHS were then added to the aqueous solution of PVA in a molar ratio of 1.5: 1. The organic phase containing propofol was gradually added to the aqueous phase under high-speed stirring (800–1200 rpm) in order to obtain a pre-emulsification, followed by an ultrasound for 30–90 s to obtain the PVA@Pro nanoparticles. Then, 80 μL of 25% glutaraldehyde and 100 μL of hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 1 mol/L were added to the mixture, which was continuously stirred for 24 hours to complete the reticulation and hardening. Following cross-linking and curing, the resulting PVA@Pro NPs were mixed with 5 mg of lactoferrin (LF) and stirred at 200 rpm for 3 h at room temperature. During this process, LF was coated onto the nanoparticle surface through non-covalent interactions rather than a separately established covalent conjugation reaction. The resulting LF-PVA@Pro NPs were subsequently purified by dialysis against deionized water using a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 3500 Da for 4 h, with the external medium replaced every 2 h. The purified nanoparticles were then lyophilized for long-term storage and subsequent experiments. The surface loading and serum stability of LF were further evaluated using an LF-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Characteristics of LF-PVA@Pro NPs

The hydrodynamic diameter and ζ potential of the nanoparticles were measured using a Zetasiser Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., United Kingdom). The morphology and size of the particles were characterised by a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; JEM-F200, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The chemical composition of the LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles was analysed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (T60, PG Instruments Ltd., China) as well as by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Tensor 27, Bruker Optics, Germany). The crystalline structure was characterised using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker Scientific Instruments Ltd., Germany). The modification of the surface by lactoferrin was confirmed by electrophoresis on polyacrylamide gel in the presence of sodium sulphate (SDS-PAGE).

The LF binding efficiency and loading capacity were determined using an LF-specific ELISA by quantifying the unbound LF in the supernatant and washing solutions after centrifugation. To evaluate the serum stability of the LF coating, LF-PVA@Pro NPs were incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS at 37 °C for 0, 1, 4, 8, and 24 h, with PBS as the serum-free control, and the amount of nanoparticle-associated LF at each time point was quantified by ELISA to calculate the LF retention rate. In vitro Release Study of PVA@Pro NPs

PVA@Pro NPs and LF-PVA@Pro NPs were prepared prior to the release experiments. A standard curve for propofol was established by UV–Vis spectrophotometry. Briefly, a series of propofol solutions with different concentrations were prepared in ethanol, and the absorbance at the characteristic wavelength was measured. Linear regression was performed using propofol concentration as the independent variable and absorbance as the dependent variable to generate the calibration curve. For the release study, 5 mL of each nanoparticle suspension was transferred into dialysis bags (MWCO = 3500 Da), sealed, and immersed separately in deionized water or PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Parallel samples were prepared for each condition, resulting in four independent release systems. The release experiments were conducted at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 200 rpm. At predetermined time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 48, 72, and 100 h), 200 μL of the external release medium was collected, and an equal volume of fresh medium pre-equilibrated to the same temperature was immediately added to maintain a constant volume. Each collected sample was mixed thoroughly with 2.8 mL of anhydrous ethanol and transferred to a quartz cuvette for absorbance measurement using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. In vivo Fluorescent Imaging

To directly visualize the brain-targeting capability of the nanoparticles, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using an in vivo imaging system (Tanon ABL-X5) equipped with IR780 fluorescence and X-ray imaging functions. Prior to imaging, the hair covering the mouse head was first trimmed using electric clippers, followed by complete hair removal with Veet depilatory cream. The skin was then thoroughly rinsed with normal saline. After a 2 h recovery period, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 per group) and intraperitoneally injected with 200 μL of free IR780, PVA@IR780 NPs, or LF-PVA@IR780 NPs (IR780 dose: 5 mg/kg), respectively. Following administration, fluorescence signals were monitored under isoflurane anesthesia using the in vivo imaging system at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 100 h. At 100 h after administration, the mice were euthanized, and the brain together with the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested, washed with saline, and subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was evaluated using double fluorescence staining based on Calcein AM and EthD-1. The colouring solution contained 1 μM of Calcein AM and 4 μM of EthD-1. In summary, 0.25 μL of stock solution of Calcein AM (4 mM) and 2 μL of stock solution of EthD-1 (2 mM) were added to 1 mL of PBS (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2–7.4), mixed carefully by vortexing, then used immediately after preparation.

For flow cytometry analysis, the cells were collected and then washed two to three times with PBS. Approximately 5×105 cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of colouring solution and incubated at room temperature, in the dark, for 15 to 20 minutes. Cellular viability was assessed within a maximum of 30 minutes using a flow cytometer. Calcein AM and EthD-1 were excited by a 488 nm laser; fluorescence emissions were captured respectively at 530 nm wavelengths for Calcein AM and 617 nm for EthD-1. For fluorescence microscopy, the adherent HT22 cells were washed two to three times with PBS before staining, in order to eliminate any residual esteratic activity. For example, in a 24-well plate, 0.5 mL of colouring solution was added to each well containing between 1 and 5×105 cells. After incubation at room temperature and in dark conditions for 15 to 20 minutes, the coloured cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Pharmacokinetic Assessment of Propofol and LF-PVA@Pro NPs

Twenty-six C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University School of Medicine and randomly assigned to two groups. Free propofol and LF-PVA@Pro NPs were administered by intraperitoneal injection at an equivalent propofol dose of 40 mg/kg, with the injection volume controlled between 200 and 300 μL.

Blood samples were taken at the following times: 0 h, 5 min, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, 72 h and 100 h after administration. For the collection of the serum, the blood was taken by final orbital blood sample. A blood sample was taken for each group at each specific time. After sampling, the whole blood samples were placed at a 45° angle and allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 30 to 45 minutes. The samples were then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10–15 minutes at 4°C, then the supernatants were recovered. The supernatants were centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C in order to obtain the serum, which was then kept at −80 °C until the analysis. The concentration of propofol in the serum was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), using a mixture of acetonitrile and water (68:32, v/v) as a moveable phase, with a detection wavelength set at 275 nm. Under these chromatographic conditions, the retention period of propofol was 10.73 min.

Chronic Restraint Stress (CRS) Procedure

Mice were housed under standard animal facility conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 30 ± 10% relative humidity) and acclimated for 7 d under a reversed light/dark cycle. Subsequently, chronic restraint stress (CRS) was induced by placing each mouse individually into a 50 mL centrifuge tube with multiple ventilation holes. Cotton was inserted behind the tail to restrict movement, and the tube cap was securely fastened to prevent escape while maintaining adequate ventilation. The mice were restrained in the tubes for 6 h each day. After each restraint session, the mice were returned to their home cages, and the restraint tubes were cleaned thoroughly. During the restraint period, the tubes were covered with black plastic sheets to maintain a dark (“night”) environment. Food and water were withheld throughout each 6 h restraint session. After 21 consecutive days of CRS, mice that successfully developed depression-like behaviors were selected for subsequent behavioral assessments.

Behavioral Experiment

All behavioural tests were carried out during the dark phase, in low light conditions (<20 lx), in order to simulate the period of natural nocturnal activity of the mice. The behaviour of the mice was recorded and then analysed using the ANY-maze behavioural tracking system (Stoelting Co., United States). In order to minimise the stress induced by manipulations, all the mice were gently handled by the same experimenter once a day for three consecutive days before the behavioural tests. This acclimatisation procedure reduced stress responses during formal experiments and minimised the influence of handling on behavioural results. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. XJTUAE2026-1736) and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892–2018).

Open Field Test (OFT)

The open field test (OFT) was performed to evaluate spontaneous locomotor activity and exploratory behaviour in mice. The open field test device included a square chamber measuring 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm, with a video camera installed directly above the test area to record behaviour. Before the test, the mice were transferred to the behavioural test room at least 24 hours before the start of the tests, to allow them to acclimatize to the environment. The test room was kept calm throughout the experiments. At the beginning of each test, each mouse is gently placed in the centre of the arena, with its head turned towards the wall, then allowed to explore freely for 8 minutes. Mouse behaviour is recorded using an infrared camera. The parameters recorded include the total distance travelled, the average speed of movement, the time spent in the central area and the distance travelled within this area. After each test, the mouse was returned to its original cage, and the experimental space was thoroughly cleaned using 75% ethanol to eliminate the olfactory indices and maintain uniform experimental conditions for the following animals. Elevated Cross Maze Experiment (EPM)

The higher labyrinth test (EPM) was carried out based on the innate tendency of rodents to avoid open spaces and to favour closed environments. This behavioural paradigm is widely used to assess behaviours associated with anxiety and depression, as well as the effects of stress and environmental factors on the emotional state. The device included a more-shaped labyrinth located about 50 cm above the ground. The device included four arms of the same length and width, connected by a square centre platform; the adjacent arms were arranged at a 90° angle, with two opposite closed arms and two opposing open arms. Before the test, all mice were transferred to the behavioural test room at least 24 hours before the test, to ensure their adaptation to the environment. During the test, each mouse was gently placed on the central platform, turned to one of the open arms, and then allowed to freely explore the labyrinth for 8 minutes. The behaviour of the mouse was recorded using an infrared camera, and parameters such as the time spent in the open and closed arms, as well as the distance travelled in the open arms, were analysed. After each test, the mouse was returned to its original cage, then the labyrinth was thoroughly cleaned using 75% ethanol to remove urine, faeces and residual olfactory cues before testing the next animal.

Sucrose Preference Experiment (SPT)

The sucrose preference test (SPT) was used to evaluate anhedonia based on the innate preference of rodents for sweet solutions and is widely applied for assessing depression-like behavior. Before testing, each mouse was provided with two identical drinking bottles, one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other containing drinking water. Both bottles were placed in the home cage, allowing the mice free access to food and water. To eliminate side preference, the positions of the two bottles were exchanged every 12 h. One day before the formal test, both food and water were removed simultaneously, and the mice were deprived of food and water for 24 h. At the beginning of the experiment, the bottles containing sucrose solution and water were weighed and randomly placed in each cage. The mice were allowed to drink freely, and the bottles were weighed again after 3 and 6 h to determine the consumption of sucrose solution and water. Tail Suspension Test (TST)

The tail suspension test (TST) was performed to evaluate behavioral despair, a characteristic feature of depression-like behavior in rodents. Experiments were conducted using a suspension rack positioned approximately 60 cm above the floor in a quiet testing environment. During the test, adhesive tape was attached approximately 1 cm from the tip of the tail, and the opposite end of the tape was secured to the suspension rack, allowing the mouse to hang freely without contacting the floor. Opaque partitions were placed between adjacent animals to prevent visual interference. Each test lasted 8 min, with the first minute regarded as an acclimation period during which the mice typically struggled to adapt to the suspended posture. The immobility time during the test was recorded and analyzed. After the experiment, the tape was carefully removed, and the mouse was returned to its home cage.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia. Brain tissues were first transcardially perfused with 0.9% normal saline, followed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were then collected and fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4°C, followed by sequential immersion in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions until the tissues completely sank, indicating adequate cryoprotection. Coronal brain sections (30 μm thick) encompassing the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAc) were subsequently prepared using a cryostat (DAKEWE CT520). The sections were permeabilized with PBST (0.1 M PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100) for 30 min and then blocked with PBST containing 5% normal goat serum at room temperature for 30 min to minimize nonspecific antibody binding. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the corresponding primary antibody (Cat. No. 12410-1-AP). On the following day, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each and then incubated with the fluorescent secondary antibody (Cat. No. HA500007) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, the sections were washed three additional times with PBS (5 min each) to remove excess antibody and minimize background fluorescence. The sections were subsequently counterstained with DAPI staining solution (Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) for 15 min. Finally, an antifade mounting medium was applied, and the sections were coverslipped carefully to avoid air bubbles. Fluorescence images were acquired and quantitatively analyzed using an Axioscan 7 panoramic tissue imaging system. Histopathological evaluation of adjacent brain sections was performed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Nissl staining.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all data are expressed in the form of the mean ± standard deviation (SD), calculated from three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between two groups were made using the unpaired Student’s t test. For comparisons between several groups, a one-factor variance analysis (ANOVA) was used, followed, where appropriate, by the least significant difference post hoc test (LSD). Statistical analyses were carried out using the corresponding software, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Simulation

To evaluate the molecular interaction between propofol and different carrier materials, liposomes were selected as the representative control. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to investigate the self-assembly behavior of liposomes or poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) with propofol at a mass ratio of 2:1 in a 15 × 15×15 nm3 cubic water box over 100 ns at ambient temperature. The representative conformations of the complexes at 0, 50, and 100 ns are shown from left to right in Figure 1A and B. During the simulation, the liposome molecules gradually aggregated by hydrophobic interactions between their lipid tails, eventually surrounding the propofol molecules and forming a relatively stable complex. However, due to the strong hydrophobic interactions between the lipids themselves, the majority of propofol molecules remained adsorbed on the surface of the liposome, while only a small fraction penetrated the interior of the liposomal aggregates. Therefore, the propofol binding capacity of the liposomal system was lower than that of the PVA system. As shown by the detailed analysis of the interactions presented in the additional information (Figure S1), propofol interacts with liposomes mainly by π–π stacking interactions. In Figure S1, the pink dotted lines indicate these π–π stacking interactions between atoms located in the region of the hydrophobic tail of liposomes and the benzene ring of propofol. The analysis of hydrogen bonds (Figure 1C and E) also showed that liposomes as well as PVA are capable of forming hydrogen bonds with propofol, each system forming about ten hydrogen bonds after balancing. However, the liposome-propofol system systematically has a lower number of hydrogen bonds compared to the PVA-propofol system. These hydrogen bond interactions help maintain the encapsulation structure surrounding propofol molecules. Interaction energy analysis (Figure 1D and F) showed that the liposome-propofol and PVA-propofol systems both had favourable non-covalent interactions throughout the simulation. In the liposome-propofol system, the LJ-SR interaction energy (short-range Lennard-Jones interaction) played a more significant role than the short-range Coulombian interaction energy (Coul-SR), thus suggesting that van der Waals interactions played an important role in the stabilisation of propofol within the liposomal structure. The LJ-SR energy remained around −2500 kJ/mol, confirming strong attractive interactions between propofol and liposomal components.

According to the analysis of the free binding energy presented in the additional information (Table S1), the MM/PBSA analysis yielded a total calculated binding free energy of −482.43 kcal/mol for the PVA–propofol system, compared with −416.38 kcal/mol for the liposome–propofol system. These values represent the total binding free energies of the simulated carrier–propofol complexes rather than values normalized per individual propofol molecule or PVA chain. In both systems, the van der Waals energy term (vdwaals) was the dominant contribution to the global binding free energy. The results obtained by simulation for other carrier materials show a similar trend. As a whole, these simulations demonstrate that PVA interacts more strongly with propofol and has a greater encapsulation capacity than that of liposomes. The stronger hydrogen-bonding interactions and van der Waals forces enabled PVA to encapsulate propofol molecules more completely while maintaining a more stable complex structure. Therefore, PVA exhibited superior encapsulation performance compared with the liposome system as a carrier for propofol.

Characterization of LF-PVA@Pro NPs

In this study, a unique strategy was developed to prepare long-acting propofol nanoparticles, in which propofol was stably encapsulated within a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) matrix through strong intermolecular interactions to form PVA@Pro NPs. Subsequently, lactoferrin (LF), a brain-targeting ligand, was coated onto the nanoparticle surface to construct LF-PVA@Pro NPs with brain-targeting capability. The morphology of the nanoparticles was first characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure S2). In addition, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis showed that the major protein band of the molecular aggregates corresponded well with the molecular weight of lactoferrin (approximately 70–100 kDa), confirming the successful incorporation of LF into the nanoparticles (Figure S3). ELISA analysis revealed an LF binding efficiency of 67% and a loading capacity of 33.5 μg/mg nanoparticles. Furthermore, the LF surface retention rates after incubation in 10% FBS at 37 °C were 94.37%, 90.82%, 87.66%, and 72.94% at 1, 4, 8, and 24 h, respectively (Table S6). These results demonstrated appreciable LF surface stability under serum-containing conditions, providing additional support for the LF-assisted brain-targeted delivery strategy.

As shown in Figure 2A, PVA@Pro NPs exhibited a relatively uniform particle size of approximately 80 nm. The nanoparticles displayed an internal aggregate-like structure, which may result from the intermolecular interactions between propofol and the PVA matrix. After LF modification, LF-PVA@Pro NPs exhibited a spherical morphology with an increased particle diameter of approximately 120 nm. In addition, the surface of the particles has become significantly smoother than that of the unmodified nanoparticles (Figure 2B), suggesting the formation of an outer protein layer that could further improve the structural stability and dispersibility of nanocures. Dynamic light diffusion (DLS) analysis showed that the hydrodynamic diameters of PVA@Pro nanoparticles and LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles were respectively 120.7 ± 1.13 nm and 152.6 ± 1.93 nm (Figure 2C). These values were slightly higher than those observed by TEM due to the hydration layer and the effects of the solvent in the aqueous dispersion. The zeta potential analysis also showed that LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles had a negatively charged surface (Figure 2D). As illustrated in Figure 2E, free propofol has a characteristic ultraviolet absorption peak at 272 nm, and a distinct absorption peak at this same wavelength is also observed in PVA@Pro nanoparticles, indicating a successful encapsulation of propofol within these nanoparticles. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis further demonstrated that the drug’s loading capacity reached 19.3%, indicating that the PVA matrix could effectively encapsulate propofol while providing the necessary basis for prolonged release of the drug. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was then used to study intermolecular interactions within therapeutic nanocarries. Free propofol has a characteristic O–H traction vibration at 3574 cm−1, while this peak disappears after the formation of nanoparticles in the PVA@Pro NPs and LF-PVA@Pro NPs systems. In addition, the characteristic C–H traction vibration located nearly 2800 cm−1 moves after the formation of nanoparticles, which reinforces the presence of intermolecular interactions between propofol and the PVA matrix (Figure 2F). The long-term stability of the nanocharacterisations was then evaluated. As illustrated in Figure 2G, the hydrodynamic diameters of PVA@Pro nanoparticles and LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles remained virtually unchanged after lyophilisation and storage of at least six months, which shows excellent storage stability.

In vitro release studies demonstrated that both PVA@Pro NPs and LF-PVA@Pro NPs exhibited sustained release profiles in deionized water and PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), with complete drug release occurring only after more than 100 h. Slight differences were observed between the two released media. One possible explanation is that albumin in FBS interacts with PVA@Pro NPs or LF-PVA@Pro NPs through electrostatic and/or hydrophobic interactions, thereby promoting particle swelling or subtle structural changes that facilitate the release of encapsulated propofol (Figure 2H). Pharmacokinetic analysis further demonstrated that LF-PVA@Pro NPs exhibited a prolonged time to peak concentration, a higher maximum plasma concentration, a markedly slower systemic clearance rate, and substantially increased overall drug exposure compared with free propofol. Moreover, the bioavailability of LF-PVA@Pro NPs was approximately fourfold higher than that of free propofol, clearly demonstrating the excellent ultra-sustained-release characteristics of the nanoparticles (Figure 2I and Table S2).

The Biological Safety of PVA@Pro NPs

In the development of psychoactive and anesthetic agents, reducing drug-associated toxicity remains a major objective. Therefore, minimizing the potential cytotoxicity of propofol was an important consideration in the present study. The cytotoxicity of PVA@Pro NPs and free propofol was first compared using a cell viability assay (Figure 3A and B). As the concentration of free propofol increased, cell viability gradually decreased and was significantly reduced to concentrations above 50 μg/mL, indicating that high doses of free propofol induce significant cell damage. On the other hand, PVA@Pro nanoparticles have a significantly lower cytotoxicity than that of free propofol, which can be explained by their prolonged release properties. At the same equivalent concentration of propofol, the cells treated with PVA@Pro NPs systematically had a much higher viability than that of cells treated with free propofol, indicating that nanoparticle delivery effectively mitigates the cytotoxicity induced by propofol while preserving its pharmacological activity. As shown in the Figure S4, at all tested concentrations, the cytotoxicity of LF-PVA Pro nanoparticles is not higher than that of non-targeted PVA Pro nanoparticles (p > 0.05). Both formulations exhibit excellent biocompatibility, maintaining relatively high cell viability even at the highest concentration. This indicates that the surface modification with LF does not introduce additional toxicity. A colouring to identify live and dead cells was then performed at a propofol equivalent concentration of 20 μg/mL to further evaluate cytotoxicity. As illustrated in Figure 3B, intense green fluorescence was observed in the control group, indicating that the majority of cells remained viable. In contrast, in the group treated with free propofol, a significantly higher number of red fluorescent cells was detected, reflecting significant cell death and relatively high cytotoxicity. In comparison, a significantly lower number of red fluorescent cells was detected in the group containing PVA@Pro NPs, which further confirms that nanoparticle delivery effectively reduces the cytotoxic effects of propofol.

Flow cytometry analysis provided additional quantitative data confirming better biosafety of PVA@Pro nanoparticles. As illustrated in Figure 3C, only a small proportion of dead cells was detected in the control group, while the group treated with free propofol showed a marked increase in cell death. Although cell death was also observed after treatment with PVA@Pro nanoparticles, this percentage was significantly lower than that observed in the free propofol group. Quantitative analysis also showed that the proportion of dead cells in the free propofol treated group was 1.57-fold higher than that observed in the PVA@Pro nanoparticle group and 2.18-fold that of the control group (Figure 3D). As a whole, these results demonstrate that the encapsulation of propofol in PVA-based nanocarries significantly mitigates its cytotoxicity.

Since propofol is widely used as an intravenous anaesthetic in clinical practice, its administration is frequently associated with adverse effects such as hypotension, respiratory depression and pain at the injection site. Therefore, careful monitoring of physiological parameters is essential when using propofol in experimental models related to depression or in therapeutic studies, in order to ensure safety and minimise the risks associated with treatment. In the present study, systolic blood pressure and heart rate were continuously monitored using a physiological monitoring system for small animals. The measurements were performed at several time points (0–240 min) after administration in each experimental group (Figure 3E–G). Throughout the observation period, the mice in the control group maintained stable physiological parameters, with a systolic blood pressure of about 119.40 ± 3.14 mmHg and a heart rate of 548.70 ± 1.32 beats/minute. Intraperitoneal administration of subanesthetic doses of propofol did not produce significant alterations in hemodynamic parameters or autonomic nervous system function. During the entire monitoring period, no statistically significant differences were observed in blood pressure or heart rate between all the experimental groups and the control group.

Body weight was also monitored throughout the treatment period. As shown in Figure 3H, mice in both the LPS and CRS model groups exhibited gradual weight loss before treatment. Following drug administration, body weight progressively increased in all treatment groups, suggesting that the therapeutic interventions did not adversely affect appetite or normal growth. Acute toxicity studies further demonstrated that the LD50 of PVA@Pro NPs following intraperitoneal injection was 2802 mg/kg, which was 2.36-fold higher than that of free propofol (Table S3-5). Therefore, the therapeutic dose of 40 mg/kg propofol used in this study exhibited a wide safety margin.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that PVA@Pro NPs significantly reduced propofol-induced cytotoxicity compared with free propofol, highlighting the advantage of nanoparticle-mediated delivery in improving the biosafety profile of propofol. Furthermore, in vivo safety evaluation showed that neither PVA@Pro NPs nor free propofol produced significant changes in vital signs under the experimental conditions. Combined with the markedly increased LD50 of the nanoparticles, these findings indicate that PVA@Pro NPs possess an excellent safety profile while maintaining the therapeutic potential of propofol.

Brain-Targeting Ability and in vivo Distribution

To exert therapeutic effects in the treatment of central nervous system disorders, propofol must effectively cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB). In the present system, lactoferrin (LF), a well-established brain-targeting ligand, was introduced to facilitate the transport of drug-loaded nanoparticles across the BBB, thereby enhancing brain accumulation, while the PVA matrix provided sustained drug release for prolonged therapeutic action.

To evaluate the brain-targeting capability of the nanoparticles, three groups of healthy mice (n = 3 per group) were randomly assigned to receive intraperitoneal injections of free IR780, PVA@IR780 NPs, or LF-PVA@IR780 NPs. Real-time in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 100 h after administration (Figure 4A). Free IR780 exhibited rapid systemic distribution and clearance, resulting in relatively weak and transient fluorescence signals in the brain, indicative of rapid in vivo elimination. In contrast, PVA@IR780 NPs displayed prolonged brain retention, with fluorescence signals persisting over an extended period. More importantly, LF-PVA@IR780 NPs exhibited markedly stronger and more sustained fluorescence signals in the brain than both free IR780 and non-targeted nanoparticles, demonstrating that LF modification substantially enhanced brain-targeting efficiency. The sustained-release behavior of the nanoparticles were further evidenced by the persistent brain fluorescence signal that remained detectable even at 72 h after administration. Compared with the other two formulations, LF-PVA@IR780 NPs exhibited earlier brain accumulation, stronger fluorescence intensity, and markedly greater brain exposure throughout the observation period. Notably, a relatively high fluorescence signal was still detected in the brain at 100 h, indicating efficient brain targeting together with prolonged in vivo retention, both of which are expected to contribute to sustained therapeutic efficacy.

Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity (n = 3) further demonstrated that brain accumulation reached its maximum at approximately 36 h after administration. At this time point, the fluorescence intensity of the LF-PVA@IR780 NPs group was 2.50-fold higher than that of the free IR780 group, whereas the PVA@IR780 NPs group exhibited a 1.80-fold increase (Figure 4C). These results indicate that free IR780 exhibits rapid systemic distribution and elimination, while its encapsulation in the PVA matrix significantly prolongs its in vivo retention. In addition, the modification by LF significantly improves the accumulation in the brain, thus obtaining the highest intensity of fluorescence and the longest retention time among all the groups studied.

In order to more precisely assess tissue distribution, ex vivo fluorescence imaging of the brain and main organs was performed 72 h after administration (Figures 4B and S5). The LF-PVA@IR780 nanoparticles showed the most intense fluorescence signal in the brain, while much less strong signals were observed in the peripheral organs, thus indicating an effective delivery specifically targeting the brain with limited off-target accumulation. A quantitative analysis of isolated brain tissue confirmed these results (Figure 4D). The average fluorescence intensity of the LF-PVA@IR780 NPs and PVA@IR780 NPs groups was 2.38-fold and 1.84-fold higher respectively than that of the free IR780 group. More importantly, pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrated that, following intraperitoneal administration, LF-PVA@Pro NPs maintained therapeutically effective propofol concentrations in the brain for up to 100 h, representing a 6–8-fold longer duration than that achieved with free propofol.

In summary, the LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticle system significantly extends the residence time of propofol in vivo while significantly increasing its accumulation in the brain. At the same time, this formulation reduces non-specific distribution to peripheral tissues, thus improving brain targeting efficiency. By prolonging the duration of exposure to the drug in the brain and improving the effectiveness of its administration, these nanodrugs have the potential to reduce the required therapeutic dose of propofol, while strengthening its antidepressant effectiveness and minimising adverse effects associated with systemic drug exposure.

Behavioral Evaluation in CRS Model

To evaluate the antidepressant efficacy of LF-PVA@Pro NPs, a chronic restraint stress (CRS)-induced mouse model of depression was established. As illustrated in Figure 5A, C57BL/6 mice were acclimated for 7 days and then subjected to CRS for 21 days.25 Following model establishment, mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of free propofol or LF-PVA@Pro NPs. The single-dose paradigm was used to capture the rapid behavioral effects of propofol while minimizing potential confounding effects associated with repeated dosing, including drug accumulation and adaptive neurobiological changes. Based on previous evidence of a rapid anti-anhedonic effect of propofol, behavioral assessments were performed 4 h after administration, a time point also selected to reduce potential interference from acute sedative/anesthetic effects.13 Depression- and anxiety-like behaviors were subsequently evaluated using the SPT, TST, EPM, and OFT, with representative behavioral trajectories shown in Figure 5B and C. Compared with the control group, mice in the CRS group exhibited markedly reduced exploration of the open arms in the EPM and displayed a pronounced preference for the peripheral region in the OFT, indicating that chronic stress induced significant anxiety- and depression-like behavioral abnormalities. In contrast, treatment with LF-PVA@Pro NPs substantially restored exploratory behavior, and the movement trajectories became more similar to those of the control group. Quantitative behavioral analyses further supported these observations (Figure 5D–G). Compared with the control group, CRS mice exhibited significant impairments in all behavioral assessments, including SPT, TST, EPM, and OFT (p < 0.001), confirming the successful establishment of the depression model. Free propofol and LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles have mitigated behavioural deficits induced by CRS to varying degrees. After free propofol treatment, the preference for sucrose was significantly increased compared to the CRS group at 3 hours (p < 0.05); the downtime during the stress test trial (TST) was significantly reduced (p < 0.05); and the exploratory behaviour in the EPM and OFT tests partially recovered (EPM, p < 0.01; OFT, p < 0.05). These results indicate that free propofol partially alleviates chronic stress-induced anhedonia, behavioural despair and anxious-type behaviours. However, the therapeutic effect remained limited, and several behavioral parameters did not fully recover to normal levels. Treatment with PVA@Pro NPs also partially improved CRS-induced behavioral abnormalities, as reflected by increased sucrose preference, reduced immobility time in the TST, and improved exploratory behavior in the EPM and OFT. However, these improvements were generally less pronounced than those observed following LF-PVA@Pro NPs treatment.

By comparison, treatment with LF-PVA@Pro NPs produced significantly greater behavioral improvement. These nanoparticles markedly increased sucrose preference (p < 0.001), reduced immobility time in the TST (p < 0.01), and significantly enhanced exploration of the open arms in the EPM (p < 0.001) and the central region in the OFT (p < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that LF-PVA@Pro NPs effectively ameliorated CRS-induced anhedonia, behavioral despair, and anxiety-like behaviors.

Direct comparison of the two treatment strategies further demonstrated the superior therapeutic efficacy of LF-PVA@Pro NPs (Table S7). In the EPM, mice treated with LF-PVA@Pro NPs spent significantly more time in the open arms than those treated with free propofol. Similarly, the OFT showed significantly greater exploration of the central area in the LF-PVA@Pro NPs group than in the free propofol group. These findings indicate that LF-PVA@Pro NPs further enhanced the antidepressant and anxiolytic efficacy of propofol. Importantly, no significant differences in total locomotor distance were observed among the experimental groups in the OFT, indicating that the behavioral improvements produced by LF-PVA@Pro NPs were not attributable to alterations in locomotor activity but instead reflected improvements in emotional behaviors.

Behavioral data from the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depression model presented in the Supporting Information (Figure S6) further demonstrated that LF-PVA@Pro NPs exhibited therapeutic efficacy in multiple depression models. However, behavioural improvements were more marked in the CRS model than in the LPS model, suggesting that LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles could be particularly effective in treating chronic stress-related depression.

As a whole, these behavioural results show that the CRS paradigm succeeded in inducing in mice phenotypes marked as depression and anxiety, phenotypes that were significantly attenuated after treatment with LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles. Compared to free propofol, LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles have consistently produced greater improvements in terms of preference for sucrose, duration of immobility during the temporal stress test (TST), exploration in the open weapon according to the EPM test, as well as activity in the central area of the OFT test, with particularly marked therapeutic benefits in the CRS model. In addition to the behavioural results obtained in the LPS model, these data indicate that LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles not only surpass free propofol in the current experimental conditions, but also have an increased therapeutic potential in depression induced by a Chronic stress, thus providing complete behavioural evidence that delivery via nanoparticles improves the antidepressant and anxiolytic efficacy of propofol. Although CRS is a well-established model of chronic stress-induced depression-like behaviors, it cannot fully replicate the complexity of human depression. Compared with LPS and CUS models, CRS provides a standardized approach to inducing chronic psychological stress. Nevertheless, the long-term efficacy of LF-PVA@Pro NPs following repeated administration requires further investigation in additional depression models.

The Regulatory Effect of LF-PVA@Pro NPs on the VTA-NAc Brain Region

To investigate the neural mechanisms by which LF-PVA@Pro NPs ameliorate CRS-induced depressive-like behaviors, histopathological and neurofunctional analyses were performed in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAc), two key brain regions involved in emotional regulation and reward processing (Figure S7 and S8). As shown in Figure 6A–6D, chronic restraint stress induced pronounced neuronal injury in both the VTA and NAc, as evidenced by increased histopathological damage scores in H&E staining (VTA: p < 0.05, NAc: p <0.01) and a significant reduction in the number of Nissl-positive neurons (VTA: p <0.0001, NAc: p <0.0001). Treatment with free propofol partially alleviated tissue injury in both brain regions, reducing the H&E injury scores by approximately 40% and 30% in the VTA and NAc, respectively, compared with the CRS group. Consistently, Nissl staining demonstrated partial restoration of neuronal integrity, with the numbers of Nissl-positive neurons recovering to approximately 1.66-fold and 1.20-fold of those in the CRS group in the VTA and NAc, respectively. These findings indicate that free propofol partially attenuated chronic stress-induced neuronal damage, although its neuroprotective efficacy remained limited. In contrast, LF-PVA@Pro NPs produced substantially greater neuroprotection. Histological damage was markedly reduced, with H&E injury scores decreasing by approximately 60% in the VTA and 55% in the NAc. Correspondingly, Nissl staining revealed a more extensive recovery of the neural structure: the number of neurons positive for Nissl marking increased to about 1.92-fold and 1.34-fold that observed in the CRS group, respectively in the VTA and Nac. These results show that LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles effectively protect against chronic stress-induced neuronal damage and promote structural recovery in the brain regions associated with the reward. In order to further evaluate the restoration of neuronal circuit function, immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine neuronal activity and markers related to neurotransmitters (Figure 6E–J and Figure S9, S10). Compared to the control group, the CRS strongly reduced the expression of c-Fos in the VTA and NAc regions (p < 0.0001), indicating a marked suppression of reward circuit activity following prolonged exposure to stress. In addition, GABA expression was significantly reduced in both brain regions (p < 0.0001), suggesting a disturbance in the homeostasis of inhibitory neurotransmitters. In addition, dopamine carrier immunofluorescence (DAT) was significantly decreased in CRS mice, which confirms a dopaminergic signalling deficit associated with emotional regulation. Next, the effects of free propofol and LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles on these CRS-induced neurofunctional abnormalities were evaluated in detail. Compared to the CRS group, free propofol significantly restored c-Fos expression in NAc (p < 0.0001), accompanied by partial recovery of GABA expression (VTA, p < 0.05; NAc, p < 0.01). In addition, immunofluorescence for DAT showed moderate recovery after treatment with free propofol, with an increase in DAT expression of about 15% in VTA and NAc compared to the CRS group. These results indicate that free propofol partially restores dopaminergic function altered by chronic stress; however, its regulatory efficacy remains limited.

On the other hand, LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles have induced a much more pronounced neurofunctional recovery. The treatment strongly increased the expression of c-Fos in the VTA and NAc regions (VTA, p < 0.0001; NAc, p < 0.0001), indicating a robust restoration of neuronal activity within the reward circuit. Consistently, anti-GABA immunofluorescence showed significant recovery of GABA expression in these two regions (VTA, p < 0.0001; NAc, p < 0.0001). Given that propofol mainly exerts its central pharmacological effects by potentiating inhibitory neurotransmitter transmission mediated by GABA receptors, these results suggest that LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles could more effectively restore the excitation-inhibition balance altered by chronic stress, thanks to a lasting increase in GABAergic signalling. Furthermore, DAT expression was significantly increased following LF-PVA@Pro NPs treatment (VTA, p < 0.001; NAc, p < 0.01), indicating more effective restoration of dopaminergic neurotransmission and reward circuit function, thereby providing additional mechanistic support for its antidepressant efficacy. PVA@Pro NPs also partially alleviated histopathological injury and improved c-Fos, GABA, and DAT expression in the VTA and NAc. However, these improvements were generally less pronounced than those observed following LF-PVA@Pro NPs treatment, highlighting the potential contribution of LF modification to enhanced neuroprotective and neuromodulatory effects.

Direct comparison of the three treatment strategies further demonstrated the superior neuroregulatory capacity of LF-PVA@Pro NPs. Although free propofol partially ameliorated CRS-induced neurological dysfunction, LF-PVA@Pro NPs produced significantly greater neuroprotective and neuromodulatory effects. Histological analysis showed that LF-PVA@Pro NPs increased the numbers of Nissl-positive neurons by approximately 15% and 18% in the VTA and NAc, respectively, compared with free propofol. More importantly, LF-PVA@Pro NPs produced greater restoration of c-Fos activity, GABAergic neurotransmission, and DAT-associated dopaminergic signaling than free propofol, indicating that nanoparticle-mediated delivery substantially improves the neuroregulatory efficiency of propofol in key reward-related brain regions and consequently enhances its central antidepressant efficacy. Further analysis of the LPS model data presented in the Supporting Information (Figure S11 and Table S8) demonstrated that LF-PVA@Pro NPs also alleviated neurofunctional impairment induced by neuroinflammation. However, compared with the LPS model, the therapeutic benefits were more pronounced in the CRS model, particularly with respect to restoration of GABAergic function and remodeling of the VTA–NAc rewar dcircuitry. These findings suggest that LF-PVA@Pro NPs may be especially effective for depression associated with chronic stress-induced neural dysfunction. Inaddition, Compared with small molecule drugs and other free propofol and nano-drug delivery systems (Table S9), LF-PVA@Pro NPs demonstrated more comprehensive behavioral improvement effects in depression disease models, which could simultaneously alleviate anhedonia, behavioral despair, and anxiety-like behaviors. Multiple behavioral indicators such as SPT, TST, EPM, and OFT all showed significant improvements, especially in the recovery of anxiety-related behaviors.26–29 Moreover, LF-PVA@Pro NPs further promoted the recovery of depression-related neural functions by enhancing c-Fos-mediated neuronal activity and synergistically regulating GABAergic signals and dopamine transporter (DAT) functions, suggesting that it can promote the remodeling of neurotransmitter homeostasis and the restoration of the reward neural circuit. In summary, LF-PVA@Pro NPs not only enhanced the brain therapeutic efficacy of propofol but also achieved the synergistic effect of behavioral improvement and neural circuit regulation, demonstrating superior antidepressant treatment potential compared to traditional free drugs.

In summary, H&E and Nissl staining demonstrated that propofol alleviates neuronal injury in the VTA and NAc induced by both CRS and LPS. By enhancing brain-targeted delivery and prolonging intracerebral drug exposure, LF-PVA@Pro NPs more effectively restored c-Fos-associated neuronal activation, GABAergic neurotransmission, and DAT-mediated dopaminergic signaling within the VTA–NAc reward circuit than free propofol. Collectively, these findings suggest that the antidepressant-like effects of LF-PVA@Pro NPs are associated with coordinated neuronal protection, restoration of GABAergic homeostasis, recovery of dopamine transporter function, and reactivation of the mesolimbic reward circuit.

Conclusion

We successfully developed a lactoferrin (LF)-modified polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)-based ultra-sustained, brain-targeted propofol nanoparticles (LF-PVA@Pro NPs) that addresses three major barriers limiting the translational application of propofol as a rapid-acting antidepressant: insufficient brain accumulation, short intracerebral therapeutic duration, and the systemic toxicity of free propofol, as well as the low drug loading capacity and poor long-term release performance associated with conventional physically encapsulated propofol nanoformulations. Unlike conventional lipid-based carriers that rely primarily on passive physical encapsulation, this nanoplatform exploits strong hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions between PVA and propofol, as validated by molecular dynamics simulations. This intermolecular interaction-driven strategy enabled a high propofol loading capacity of 19.3%, sustained in vitro drug release for up to 100 h, and excellent storage stability after lyophilization for at least 6 months. Furthermore, surface modification with LF conferred efficient blood–brain barrier penetration, increasing brain accumulation to 2.5-fold that of free propofol and maintaining therapeutic intracerebral drug concentrations for up to 100 h following intraperitoneal administration, representing a 6–8-fold extension compared with free propofol. In addition, the 2.36-fold increase in LD50 substantially broadened the therapeutic safety window at the working dose of 40 mg/kg.

In the chronic depression stress model induced by the CRS, LF-PVA@Pro nanoparticles produced robust therapeutic effects on several targets. These nanotherapies have effectively attenuated the characteristic behaviours of depression and anxiety, reduced neuronal damage within the VTA-NAc reward circuit, and restored the expression of c-Fos, GABA and DAT, thus reactivating dysfunctional dopaminergic and GABAergic signalling. Compared to free propofol and non-targeted PVA@Pro nanoparticles, the LF-functionalised nanosystem has superior neuroprotective efficacy and more effective regulation of reward circuit function, thus highlighting the synergistic benefits offered by the combination of prolonged drug release and targeted delivery to the brain.

In summary, this study presents an innovative intermolecular interaction-driven nanoencapsulation strategy for propofol that overcomes key limitations of existing propofol nanocarriers. By integrating ultra-sustained drug release with efficient brain-targeted delivery, the LF-PVA@Pro nanoplatform enables long-term, multi-target modulation of central nervous system function in stress-related depression and provides a promising translational strategy for repurposing propofol as a rapid-acting antidepressant. Future studies should further investigate the regional distribution of propofol within different brain nuclei, clarify the causal relationship between neurotransmitter modulation and behavioral improvement, and comprehensively evaluate long-term safety, repeated-dose administration, dose–response relationships, and therapeutic efficacy across additional depression models to facilitate future clinical translation for neuropsychiatric disorders.
