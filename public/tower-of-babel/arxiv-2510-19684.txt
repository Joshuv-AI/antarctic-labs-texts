Addressing spins at their clock transition with a frequency- and bandwidth-tunable superconducting resonator Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yutian Wen, V. Ranjan, T. Lorriaux, D. Vion, B. Huard, A. Bienfait, E. Flurin, P. Bertet

Abstract
Solid-state spin ensembles addressed via superconducting circuits are promising candidates for quantum memory applications, offering multimodal storage capability and second-long coherence times at their clock transition. Implementing practical memory schemes requires dynamic control over both the resonator frequency and bandwidth. In this letter, we report measurements of a superconducting resonator whose frequency can be tuned by passing a DC current through the high-kinetic-inductance thin film, and whose bandwidth can be tuned by parametric coupling to a low-Q buffer resonator. Using this resonator, we address an ensemble of bismuth donors at their clock transition, measuring a Hahn-echo coherence time of 450 ms. We demonstrate RF driving of the bismuth donor hyperfine transitions, as well as dynamic bandwidth control of the resonator.



Despite continuous improvements, the limited coherence time remains a key challenge for superconducting quantum processors, constraining both noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) algorithms
Preskill 2018
and quantum error correction schemes
Fowler et al. 2012
.
A promising solution
Gouzien and Sangouard 2021
relies on dedicated quantum memory units capable of parallel storage of multiple qubit states over extended periods.
Amongst various platforms explored to this end
Craiciu et al. 2021
;
Businger et al. 2020
;
Hann et al. 2019
;
Zhong et al. 2017
;
Freer et al. 2017
;
Ranjan et al. 2020
,
solid-state spin ensembles stand out for their multimodal storage capability
Schuster et al. 2010
;
Grezes et al. 2016
;
Probst et al. 2017
;
Ranjan et al. 2020
;
O’Sullivan et al. 2022
and second-long coherence times
Wolfowicz et al. 2013
.
An example of architecture consists in encoding the qubit states in single- or few-photon microwave pulses to be stored in and retrieved from the spin ensemble memory through refocussing sequences
Afzelius et al. 2013
;
Julsgaard and Mølmer 2012
;
Julsgaard and Mølmer 2013
;
Bernád et al. 2025
.
One difficulty of this approach is to achieve efficient absorption of the wave packet in the spin ensemble. Even with the use of high-quality-factor resonators to enhance the spin-photon interaction strength, efficient photon absorption requires relatively large spin density, which leads to broad ensemble spin linewidth and reduced spin coherence time due to dipolar interactions
Wolfowicz et al. 2012
.

One possible way to solve this issue is to bias spins at specific magnetic fields where their mean magnetic dipoles vanish, allowing for long coherence times even at large spin concentrations. These so-called clock transitions (CTs), or zero first-order-insensitive (ZEFOZ) points, occur in coupled electron-nuclear spin systems with large hyperfine interactions. Enhanced coherence times at CTs have been observed in donors in silicon
Wolfowicz et al. 2013
, molecular spins
Shiddiq et al. 2016
, and rare-earth-ion-doped crystals
Alexander et al. 2022
;
Tiranov et al. 2025
.
Because CTs occur only at specific magnetic fields, they require resonators tuned precisely to the corresponding transition frequency.
While this can be achieved via trial and error with fixed-frequency resonators
Ranjan et al. 2020
, a more general and scalable approach uses resonators whose frequency can be tuned in-situ and dynamically to the CT frequency. Additionally, several quantum memory protocols
Julsgaard et al. 2013
;
Bernád et al. 2025
also require dynamical control of the resonator bandwidth, to avoid maser emission when the spin ensemble is inverted by the control pulses.

Aluminium-based Josephson devices offer many tools to design resonators with tunable characteristics
Scarlino et al. 2022
, but they are usually incompatible with the application of magnetic fields above
\sim 10\,\mathrm{mT}
that are necessary to bias the spins. Another mechanism, compatible with large magnetic fields, arises from the quadratic dependence of the kinetic inductance of a superconducting wire,
L_{k}(I)=L_{k0}+\alpha I^{2}
on the current
I
passing through it. Field-resilient kinetic inductance (KI)-based frequency-tunable resonators have been demonstrated
Annunziata et al. 2010
;
Vissers et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2025
and used for interfacing spins at
\sim 200-500\,\mathrm{mT}
fields
Asfaw et al. 2017
. Here, we design and fabricate a frequency- and bandwidth-tunable resonator based on KI. We demonstrate its suitability as a spin-ensemble quantum memory interface by addressing bismuth donor spins in silicon (Bi:Si) at their CT where we measure extended coherence times, and we perform a spectroscopy of Bi:Si sub-gigahertz transitions by electron nuclear double resonance (ENDOR).

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{Q_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}{2C_{\mathrm{a}}}-\frac{Q_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}}{2C_{\mathrm{b}}}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{2},

(S7)

where
Q_{\mathrm{a}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{b}}
are the charge on each capacitor. We consider that only
L_{\mathrm{a}}
and
L_{\mathrm{c}}
are non-linear inductors, so that their values depend on the currents flowing through them (respectively
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{c}}
) as described above.

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}+c_{1}^{a}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+c_{2}^{a}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}

(S8)

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}+c_{1}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})+c_{2}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{2}.

(S9)

The Hamiltonian of the circuit is given by:

H=\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}}-\mathcal{L},

(S10)

where the phase operators can be evaluated through
\Phi_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial Q_{i}}
, yielding:

\displaystyle\Phi_{a}

\displaystyle=(L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0})\dot{Q_{\mathrm{a}}}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}\dot{Q_{\mathrm{b}}}+\frac{3}{2}c_{1}^{a}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}+\frac{3}{2}c_{1}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{2}+2c_{2}^{a}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}^{3}+2c_{2}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{3}

(S11)

\displaystyle\Phi_{b}

\displaystyle=(L_{\mathrm{b}}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0})\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}\dot{Q_{\mathrm{a}}}+\frac{3}{2}c_{1}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{2}+2c_{2}^{n}(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}}+\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})^{3}.

(S12)

Expressing the Hamiltonian purely as a function of (
\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}
,
\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}
,
Q_{\mathrm{a}}
,
Q_{\mathrm{b}}
) requires inverting Eq.
S12
to express
\dot{Q}_{a/b}
as a function of
\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}
. We do so by defining the vectors
\Phi=(\Phi_{\mathrm{a}},\Phi_{\mathrm{b}})
and
\dot{Q}=(\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{a}},\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{b}})
, and express Eq.
S12
as:

\Phi=\Lambda\dot{Q}+R,

(S13)

where
\Lambda
is a 2-by-2 matrix independent of
\dot{Q}_{a/b}
and
R
contains the terms in
\dot{Q}_{a/b}^{k}
with
k>1
. We now have:

\dot{Q}=\Lambda^{-1}\Phi-\Lambda^{-1}R.

(S14)

One can then express the higher terms contained in
R
by substituting them by Eq.
S14
. This creates a new expression for
\dot{Q}
. Performing this process iteratively enables to express
\dot{Q}_{a/b}
as a function of
\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}
,
\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}
, and higher order cross-products, and these expressions can then be trimmed at a given order before injection into the Hamiltonian. Cutting-off the resulting expressions at the third order (up to
\Phi_{a/b}^{3}
), we find :

H=H_{0}+g_{1,1}\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}+g_{2,1}\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}+g_{1,2}\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}+g_{3,0}\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}^{3}+g_{0,3}\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}^{3}

(S15)

where:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}{2C_{\mathrm{a}}}+\frac{Q_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}}{2C_{\mathrm{b}}}+\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}{2\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{a}}}+\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}}{2\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{b}}},

(S16)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}\frac{1}{1+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}/L_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}},

(S17)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{b}}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{b}}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}\frac{1}{1+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}/L_{\mathrm{a}}}

(S18)

\displaystyle g_{1,2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{a}}}c_{1}^{a}\left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}}{L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}L_{\mathrm{b}}(1+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}/L_{\mathrm{ab}})}\right)^{2}-\frac{3}{2}c_{1}^{n}\frac{1}{(L_{\mathrm{b}}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0})^{2}(L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}+L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0})}\frac{L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}L_{\mathrm{b}}}{\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{a}}\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{b}}}

(S19)

and others
g_{i,j}
are geometrical terms that can be expressed as a function of
L_{\mathrm{c}}^{0}
,
L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}
,
L_{\mathrm{b}}
,
c_{1}^{a}
,
c_{1}^{n}
, but do not involve
c_{2}^{a}
and
c_{2}^{b}
. We defined
L_{\mathrm{ab}}=\frac{L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}L_{\mathrm{b}}}{L_{\mathrm{a}}^{0}+L_{\mathrm{b}}}
. We now introduce the annihilation operators
\hat{a}=(\Phi_{\mathrm{a}}+iZ_{\mathrm{a}}Q_{\mathrm{a}})/\sqrt{(2\hbar Z_{\mathrm{a}})}
and
\hat{b}=(\Phi_{\mathrm{b}}+iZ_{\mathrm{b}}Q_{\mathrm{b}})/\sqrt{(2\hbar Z_{\mathrm{b}})}
, where
Z_{\mathrm{a}}=\sqrt{\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{a}}/C_{\mathrm{a}}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{b}}=\sqrt{\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{b}}/C_{\mathrm{b}}}
are the impedances of the coupled resonators. The Hamiltonian
S15
can be re-expressed as a function of these operators.

A.3.3
Three-wave mixing for dissipation engineering

We now wish to derive the effective three-wave Hamiltonian, and show that it can be used to control the dissipation rate of resonator
A
. We thus consider the action of a pump tone of amplitude
\xi
with frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{a}}-\omega_{\mathrm{b}}
applied on the buffer port, as well as a drive tone applied at
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{a}}
. We accomplish this by considering the interacting Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}=UHU^{\dagger}-i\hbar U\frac{dU^{\dagger}}{dt}
calculated using the following evolution operator

U=e^{i\omega_{\mathrm{d}}t\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}e^{i(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}-\omega_{\mathrm{p}})t\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}e^{-\tilde{\xi}\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\tilde{\xi}^{*}\hat{b}}.

(S20)

where we remove the classical displacement due to the application of pump on the buffer flux. The displacement
\tilde{\xi}=\xi e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{p}}t}
can be related to the rf drive current flowing through the inductor by
\xi=\tilde{L}_{\mathrm{b}}I_{\text{rf}}/\sqrt{2\hbar Z_{\mathrm{b}}}
. In our experiment, owing to the Bragg mirror, this current can be safely related to the input pump power on the buffer port
P_{\text{in}}
as
\sqrt{P_{\text{in}}/Z_{0}}
, since the pump tone is applied far below the Bragg mirror bandpass frequency range.

Keeping only terms of
H_{\text{int}}
which are non zero after a time-average over one drive period, we effectively perform the rotating-wave approximation and we find:

H_{\text{RWA}}=\hbar\delta_{\mathrm{a}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hbar\delta_{\mathrm{b}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\hbar g_{\text{3WM}}(\hat{a}\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b})

(S21)

where we have
\delta_{\mathrm{a}}=\omega_{\mathrm{a}}-\omega_{d}
,
\delta_{\mathrm{b}}=\omega_{\mathrm{b}}+\omega_{p}-\omega_{d}
and:

\hbar g_{\text{3WM}}=4\xi\sqrt{(2\hbar)^{3}Z_{a}Z_{b}^{2}}g_{1,2}=12k\frac{I_{\mathrm{dc}}I_{\mathrm{rf}}}{I^{*2}}\hbar\sqrt{\omega_{a}\omega_{b}},

(S22)

with
k\approx L_{k,n}^{0}/\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{a}}L_{\mathrm{b}}}
and where we have neglected the contribution of
L_{\mathrm{a}}
to the third-order non-linearity.

We now derive the quantum Langevin equations from Eq.
S21
, including the dissipation on modes
a
and
b
, as well as a drive of strength
\alpha_{\text{in}}
applied to mode
a
:

\displaystyle\dot{a}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}+i\delta_{\mathrm{a}}\right)a-ig_{\text{3WM}}b+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{c}}}\alpha

(S23)

\displaystyle\dot{b}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}+i\delta_{\mathrm{b}}\right)b-ig_{\text{3WM}}a

(S24)

In the steady-state, whenever
\delta_{\mathrm{b}}=0
, we find:

a=\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{c}}}\alpha_{\text{in}}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}+\frac{4g^{2}_{\text{3WM}}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}}+2i\delta_{\mathrm{a}}},

(S25)

where we can identify that through the three-wave mixing interaction, the buffer mode creates an additional decay channel for mode
a
of rate
4g^{2}_{\text{3WM}}/\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}
.
