Retinal microvascular changes in patients with ankylosing spondylitis receiving biologic therapy: a cross-sectional OCTA study
Authors: Rumeysa Samanci, Bayram Meydan, Kuddusi Teberik, Sumeyye Ucar Meydan, Safinaz Ataoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate retinal microcirculation in biologic-treated ankylosing spondylitis (AS) using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) and its association with disease activity.MethodsFifty-seven AS patients (31 active, 26 inactive) under biologic therapy and 57 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were included. Right eyes underwent 10°×10° macular OCTA imaging (Heidelberg Spectralis). Vessel area density (VAD), foveal avascular zone (FAZ) metrics, and vessel density within a 300-µm-wide ring surrounding the FAZ (FD-300) were quantified for the superficial and deep vascular complexes (SVC and DVC). Structural OCT provided foveal and parafoveal retinal thickness, peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), and subfoveal choroidal thickness. Biologic therapy details and disease activity indices (Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score–C-reactive protein [ASDAS-CRP], Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index [BASDAI], Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index [BASFI]) were obtained from records. OCTA and structural parameters were compared among active AS, inactive AS, and control groups.ResultsParafoveal SVC VAD was reduced only in the active AS group (p = 0.007), whereas DVC total/parafoveal VAD and FD-300 values were lower in both AS groups compared with controls (all p < 0.01). Retinal thickness, RNFL, and choroidal thickness were similar across groups (all p > 0.05). For distinguishing AS patients from controls, DVC metrics showed the highest performance (AUC ≈ 0.70–0.73; 0.63 for SVC), while the combined model including age, sex, and OCTA parameters achieved an AUC of 0.77. For differentiating disease activity, DVC-FAZ alone showed limited performance (AUC = 0.67). A parsimonious multivariable model including age, sex, BASFI, and DVC FAZ area yielded an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.80–0.97).ConclusionsIn AS patients receiving biologic therapy, retinal microcirculatory alterations were detected—particularly within the DVC—while retinal and choroidal structural measurements appeared to remain largely preserved. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Ankylosing spondylitis, Retinal microcirculation, Optical coherence tomography angiography, Biologic therapy

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic spondyloarthritis primarily affecting the sacroiliac joints and spine, characterized by inflammatory back pain and progressive spinal stiffness [1]. Chronic systemic inflammation in AS extends beyond the musculoskeletal system, contributing to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD); indeed, epidemiological data indicate that the incidence of CVD in AS is approximately 1.5 times higher than in the general population [2, 3].

While evidence supports accelerated atherosclerosis at the macrovascular level in AS, data regarding microvascular alterations remain limited [4]. Most available studies rely on techniques such as capillaroscopy or laser Doppler flowmetry, which are easily influenced by external factors such as blood pressure or temperature [5–7]. However, endothelial dysfunction and microvascular remodeling are known to precede clinically overt macrovascular CVD and may contribute to the pathogenesis of cardiac ischemia. Therefore, early and objective evaluation of microcirculation holds substantial clinical significance [8, 9].

The retinal microcirculation offers a unique and noninvasive window into systemic microvascular health [10, 11]. Because retinal blood flow is largely autoregulated, measurements are relatively less affected by external environmental factors [12, 13]. Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) enables the quantitative assessment of flow and vessel density within the superficial and deep retinal capillary plexuses [14, 15]. Deviations in retinal vascular architecture are thought to reflect underlying pathophysiological processes, including systemic inflammation [16]. Moreover, a considerable proportion of AS patients experience acute anterior uveitis episodes during their lifetime, placing them under regular ophthalmic follow-up an opportunity for early and practical evaluation of microcirculation [17].

Current treatment modalities for AS, including biologic agents such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, interleukin-17 (IL-17) inhibitors, and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, not only suppress disease activity but may also mitigate the deleterious vascular effects of chronic inflammation, thereby improving microvascular function. Indeed, anti-TNF therapy has been shown to significantly enhance endothelium-dependent vasodilation capacity and capillary perfusion in AS patients [5, 6, 18].

Despite the increasing use of OCTA in systemic and inflammatory diseases, published data evaluating retinal microcirculation in AS with this modality remain limited. Recent studies have reported reduced retinal vessel density in patients with axial spondyloarthritis, particularly in the deep capillary plexus, and suggested that some microvascular alterations may not fully normalize despite treatment [19]. Similarly, other investigations have shown decreased parafoveal and perifoveal capillary density compared with healthy controls, with these changes appearing to correlate with disease duration rather than disease activity [20]. Retinal imaging studies beyond OCTA have also identified microvascular alterations associated with cardiovascular risk profiles in AS [9]. Nevertheless, several aspects of the relationship between OCTA-derived microvascular architecture and disease activity remain insufficiently characterized.

The present study aims to investigate the retinal microvascular architecture in AS patients receiving biologic therapy and to assess the association between OCTA-derived measurements and disease activity. We hypothesize that this approach may facilitate the early detection of microvascular dysfunction in AS, support the use of retinal microcirculation as a potential biomarker, and contribute to a more accurate prediction of cardiovascular risk.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, observational clinical study aimed to evaluate the impact of biological therapies on ophthalmological findings in patients with AS. The study protocol was approved by the Non-Interventional Health Research Ethics Committee of Duzce University (Approval No:2025/82; Date:10.03.2025) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and participants

Patients with AS who presented to the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (PMR) outpatient clinic of the Duzce University Faculty of Medicine, Research and Training Hospital between January 2023 and January 2025 were included. AS patients evaluated in the PMR outpatient clinic were advised to undergo regular ophthalmological examinations to monitor for ocular involvement.

Patients who had completed routine ophthalmological examinations in the Department of Ophthalmology and were receiving biological therapy (including anti-TNF agents, IL-17 inhibitors, or JAK inhibitors) were assigned to the study group. The control group consisted of healthy volunteers with normal ophthalmic screening and no systemic inflammatory disease, recruited from the ophthalmology outpatient clinic.

Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of AS according to the Modified New York Criteria [21], age between 18 and 75 years, and ongoing biological therapy. Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, a history of retinal disease or glaucoma, spherical equivalent refractive error ≥ ± 4 diopters, poor-quality images, presence of any concomitant rheumatologic disease other than AS, and a history of significant systemic illness (e.g., cerebrovascular event, diabetes, or kidney disease).

Ophthalmological examinations

All participants had undergone comprehensive ophthalmological examinations. Refractive error measurements were obtained using an autorefractometer (Topcon RM-8800, Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Intraocular pressure (IOP) and pachymetry were measured with a non-contact tonometer-pachymeter (Canon TX-20P, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Axial length was measured using optical biometry (IOLMaster 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany). Retinal evaluations, including optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCTA, were performed with the same device (Heidelberg Spectralis OCT, Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany).

Macular OCT scans were acquired using a 30°×25° (8.9 × 7.4 mm) scan area and analyzed according to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid. Foveal thickness was obtained from the central 1-mm circle, and parafoveal thickness was calculated within the 1–3 mm annulus.

Choroidal thickness was defined as the vertical distance between the outer border of the hyperreflective retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) band and the hyporeflective sclerochoroidal junction. In archived OCTA images, subfoveal choroidal thickness was manually measured using the caliper tool at the foveal center.

Peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was measured using a circular B-scan (3.5 mm diameter, 768 A-scans) centered on the optic disc. RNFL thickness was defined as the distance between the inner border of the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and the outer boundary of the RNFL. Automatic segmentation was performed using the device software (Spectralis software, version 7.0.4).

OCTA image analysis

Archived OCTA images acquired using a fovea-centered 10°×10° volume scan (512 A-scans/B-scan; 512 B-scans/volume) were analyzed. En-face images of the macular superficial vascular complex (SVC) and deep vascular complex (DVC) were generated through automatic retinal layer segmentation using the device software (Fig. 1). The en-face OCTA images were exported in TIFF format and analyzed with the open-source OCTAVA software through a standardized workflow consisting of preprocessing, segmentation, and skeletonization steps (Fig. 2). OCTAVA is an image analysis tool optimized for OCTA images obtained from different devices and provides multiple quantitative metrics for assessing the retinal microvascular network [22, 23].

The following quantitative metrics were computed: foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area (mm²) and FD-300, representing vessel density within a 300-µm annulus surrounding the FAZ. Quantitative vascular evaluation was performed by calculating vascular area density (VAD, %) for both the entire scan area (total VAD) and the parafoveal region. After each analysis, segmentation and skeletonization outputs were visually inspected, and poor-quality images were excluded from the study.

All measurements were performed by an experienced ophthalmologist using identical devices and standardized imaging protocols. Due to the high interocular correlation of OCTA parameters reported in the literature [24], only the right eye was analyzed.

Clinical and laboratory data

Sociodemographic characteristics and AS-related clinical data (including disease duration, human leukocyte antigen-B27 (HLA-B27) status, extra-articular manifestations, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels measured at the study visit, and disease activity parameters) were obtained from patient records. The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI), Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score based on CRP (ASDAS-CRP), and Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) were recorded. Disease activity was assessed using BASDAI and ASDAS-CRP, with ASDAS ≥ 1.3 or BASDAI ≥ 4 defined as active disease [25]. The biological agents used by the patients included adalimumab (Humira; AbbVie, Inc., North Chicago, IL, USA), etanercept (Enbrel; Pfizer, Inc., New York, NY, USA), golimumab (Simponi; Merck Sharp & Dohme Limited, Hoddesdon, UK), infliximab (Remicade; Janssen Biotech, Inc., Horsham, PA, USA), secukinumab (Verxant; Farmanova Limited, Istanbul, Turkey), and certolizumab pegol (Cimzia; UCB Pharma Limited, Slough, UK).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (version 31.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Comparisons among the three groups (active AS, inactive AS, and healthy controls) were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons between two independent groups, the independent samples t-test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. To control for the potential confounding effects of age and sex, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for OCTA-derived microvascular and structural retinal parameters. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction for variables that showed significant differences in the ANCOVA analysis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of OCTA parameters, and area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals were reported for each variable. Univariate ROC analyses were conducted for individual OCTA parameters, whereas multivariate ROC analyses were based on predicted probabilities derived from logistic regression models including age, sex, clinical variables, and significant OCTA parameters.

To assess the potential confounding effect of biologic treatment duration, associations between treatment duration and OCTA parameters were examined using Spearman correlation analysis. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed by adding biologic treatment duration as a covariate to the multivariate logistic regression models.

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1) with α = 0.05 and power = 0.80. As no direct OCTA data were available in biologic-treated AS patients at the study design stage, effect size estimates were derived from a pilot OCTA study conducted in biologic-treated Crohn’s disease patients with comparable chronic inflammatory and treatment-related characteristics [26]. Accordingly, at least 105 participants (35 per group) were required. In addition, a post hoc sensitivity power analysis for the active versus inactive AS subgroup comparison showed that, with the achieved sample sizes (31 vs. 26), the minimum detectable effect size for two-sided independent-samples t tests was Cohen’s d = 0.76.

ROC analysis demonstrated that DVC parameters provided the best discrimination between eyes with AS and controls. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values were 0.729 (SE = 0.047, 95% CI = 0.637–0.821, p < 0.001) for DVC VAD, 0.729 (SE = 0.047, 95% CI = 0.637–0.821, p < 0.001) for parafoveal DVC VAD, and 0.703 (SE = 0.049, 95% CI = 0.607–0.799, p < 0.001) for DVC FD-300. The parafoveal SVC VAD showed moderate diagnostic ability (AUC = 0.629, SE = 0.053, 95% CI = 0.526–0.732, p = 0.014), whereas the DVC FAZ area was not significant (AUC = 0.533, SE = 0.055, 95% CI = 0.426–0.641, p = 0.543) (Fig. 4A).

A multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, DVC VAD, parafoveal DVC VAD, parafoveal SVC VAD, DVC FD-300, and DVC FAZ area was statistically significant (omnibus χ² = 26.7, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.279) with a classification accuracy of 68.4%.The predicted probabilities from this model yielded an AUC of 0.767 (SE = 0.044, 95% CI = 0.681–0.853, p < 0.001), indicating improved discrimination compared with individual OCTA metrics alone (Fig. 4B).

Among AS patients, univariate ROC analysis revealed that only the DVC FAZ area significantly differentiated active from inactive disease (AUC = 0.672, SE = 0.071, 95% CI = 0.534–0.811, p = 0.015). Other OCTA parameters, including DVC VAD, parafoveal SVC VAD, and DVC FD-300, were not significant (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 4C).

A multivariable logistic regression model incorporating the same OCTA variables (adjusted for age and sex) was not significant overall (omnibus χ² = 8.83, p = 0.183; Nagelkerke R² = 0.192; accuracy = 63.2%), although its predicted probabilities showed a moderate discriminative ability (AUC = 0.696, SE = 0.069, 95% CI = 0.561–0.831, p = 0.005) (Fig. 4D).

To evaluate the additional diagnostic contribution of OCTA parameters beyond clinical variables, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. The clinical model including age, sex, and BASFI score (Model A) was significant (χ² (3) = 20.886, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.410; classification accuracy = 77.2%). In this model, only BASFI was independently associated with active disease (OR = 3.45, p = 0.001). In a sensitivity analysis, when biologic treatment duration was added as a covariate (Model A′), model performance improved (Nagelkerke R² = 0.481; classification accuracy = 84.2%; AUC = 0.871, 95% CI: 0.776–0.966). BASFI remained the strongest independent predictor (adjusted OR = 3.85, 95% CI: 1.69–8.77, p = 0.001), whereas biologic treatment duration showed only a modest independent association (adjusted OR = 1.023/month, 95% CI: 1.000–1.046, p = 0.046).

When OCTA parameters (DVC VAD, parafoveal DVC VAD, DVC FD-300, and DVC FAZ area) were added, the expanded Model B demonstrated a marked improvement in model fit and explanatory power (χ² (8) = 33.908, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.599; −2LL = 44.672), with higher discriminative performance (AUC = 0.898, 95% CI: 0.819–0.977). In this model, a higher BASFI score (OR ≈ 5.76, p = 0.001) and a smaller DVC FAZ area (p = 0.033) remained independently associated with active disease, whereas the other OCTA parameters were not significant (all p > 0.05).When biologic treatment duration was added to the full model (Model B′), the main findings were unchanged; BASFI (p = 0.003) and DVC FAZ area (p = 0.031) retained their independent associations. This model showed high discriminative ability (AUC = 0.928, 95% CI: 0.865–0.991).

Because Model B included 8 variables despite only 26 events in the smaller subgroup, an additional parsimonious model (Model C) was constructed to reduce the risk of overfitting. This model included age, sex, BASFI, and only DVC FAZ area. Model C was significant (χ² (4) = 32.26, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.578; classification accuracy = 77.2%). BASFI (OR = 5.33, 95% CI: 2.00–14.18, p < 0.001), smaller DVC FAZ area (p = 0.005), and younger age (OR = 0.886, 95% CI: 0.799–0.982, p = 0.021) were independently associated with active disease. The AUC of Model C was 0.882 (95% CI: 0.798–0.966), indicating comparable discriminative performance to Model B with fewer variables.

To further assess the potential confounding effect of biologic treatment duration on microvascular findings, associations between treatment duration and major OCTA parameters were additionally examined in AS patients. All correlations were weak and statistically non-significant (DVC VAD: ρ = 0.130, p = 0.336; parafoveal DVC VAD: ρ = −0.056, p = 0.679; DVC FD-300: ρ = −0.005, p = 0.972; DVC FAZ area: ρ = −0.116, p = 0.389; parafoveal SVC VAD: ρ = −0.178, p = 0.186), suggesting no meaningful monotonic relationship between treatment duration and OCTA findings.

Discussion

The principal findings of this study indicate that, even under biologic therapy, patients with AS exhibit discernible alterations in retinal microcirculation compared with healthy controls. In particular, vessel density within the DVC was significantly lower in the AS group. This microvascular impairment appears largely independent of disease activity; notably, patients with inactive AS still demonstrated a comparable reduction in vessel density relative to healthy individuals. In the active-disease subgroup, the unexpectedly smaller FAZ is noteworthy. However, the FAZ difference within the DVC did not remain statistically robust after Bonferroni correction in the three-group comparison and should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating, requiring confirmation in larger cohorts. The diagnostic performance of OCTA metrics was modest; in ROC analyses, retinal vessel-density measures distinguished AS eyes from healthy eyes with only moderate accuracy (best AUC ~ 0.73). Collectively, these findings suggest an association between AS and alterations in retinal microcirculation; however, given the cross-sectional design and absence of an untreated comparison group, no conclusions can be drawn regarding causality or the effects of biologic therapy on these changes.

Our results are broadly consistent with the literature reporting microvascular disturbances in AS. Karataş et al. observed significantly reduced vessel densities at both the SVC and the DVC in patients with axial spondyloarthritis, with the most pronounced decrease at the DVC. Although anti-TNF therapy partially improved these parameters, normalization was not achieved, implying persistent structural alterations at the microvascular level despite treatment [19]. Similarly, another study reported significantly reduced parafoveal and perifoveal capillary plexus density in patients with axial spondyloarthritis compared with healthy controls, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the deep capillary plexus. Capillary density was inversely associated with disease duration, whereas no significant relationship was found with current disease activity [20]. In addition, Batko et al., using laser Doppler flowmetry, demonstrated delayed post-occlusive hyperemia and reduced peak flow in active AS, with partial recovery of these functional parameters after anti-TNF therapy. The lack of complete structural normalization alongside functional improvement suggests that functional gains may be constrained by underlying structural damage [5].

Kal et al. reported an increase in choroidal thickness and an accompanying decrease in RNFL and ganglion cell complex thickness (GCC) in patients with AS [27]. However, the preservation of retinal and choroidal thickness parameters in our study may be related to the fact that all patients in our group were receiving biological therapy. On the other hand, a study conducted in patients with HLA-B27-associated anterior uveitis reported an increase in vessel density in the deep capillary plexus and a decrease in the superficial plexus; furthermore, the observation of these changes in the clinically unaffected fellow eyes supports the role of systemic inflammation [28]. In our findings, a decrease was detected particularly in DVC vessel density, and these differences may stem from the disease phenotype in the study population, the presence of uveitis, and the imaging parameters used.

Previous investigations have also revealed that retinal and choroidal structures exhibit concomitant changes with microvascular alterations in AS. Yavuz et al. reported significantly decreased RNFL, GCC and total retinal thickness in AS patients receiving long-term adalimumab compared with controls, suggesting that neurosensory tissues are susceptible to chronic inflammation. The presence of reduced peripapillary RNFL and macular thickness despite biologic therapy further supports the notion that inflammation-related neural injury may not be fully reversible [29]. In a comprehensive analysis, Karataş et al. further showed that choroidal thickness and choriocapillaris vessel density were reduced in axial spondyloarthritis compared with controls; anti-TNF therapy afforded partial protection but did not restore normal values [19]. Together, these observations indicate that retinal and choroidal microcirculatory reductions may coincide with structural thinning at neural and stromal levels. In our cohort, retinal thickness and RNFL values remained within normal limits, which may reflect a partially protective effect of biologic therapy; the absence of a significant difference in choroidal thickness is likewise plausibly attributable to suppression of inflammation with chronic treatment. Overall, the findings support a dynamic process within the retina–choroid complex in AS, wherein neural structures may be particularly vulnerable to the cumulative effects of inflammation.

We believe that the more pronounced involvement of DVC metrics relative to the SVC in our cohort is biologically plausible and may stem from the structural and physiological characteristics of the DVC. Situated at the inner nuclear–outer plexiform layer interface in close proximity to photoreceptor synapses, the DVC has comparatively high oxygen and glucose demands. Yet, unlike the SVC, it is not as close to arteriolar inflow and is supplied predominantly by a venous-sided capillary network. Its fine-caliber, more terminal flow architecture with limited collateralization through vertical anastomoses likely reduces perfusion reserve under conditions of inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, predisposing the DVC to capillary rarefaction [30–33]. In addition, TNF-α/IL-17–mediated disruption of endothelial–pericyte interactions may accelerate capillary loss within the deep plexus, while concomitant choriocapillaris dysfunction reported in axial spondyloarthritis could secondarily constrain oxygen delivery to the DVC [19, 34, 35]. Taken together, these factors provide a coherent mechanistic rationale for why vessel-density loss manifests earlier and more prominently in the DVC than in the SVC, supporting the notion that the DVC may constitute an “early-warning” window for microvascular compromise in AS.

The potential mechanisms underlying the observed microvascular changes in AS can be framed along the axis of systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [5, 36]. Chronic elevations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17) compromise endothelial integrity and favor a vasoconstrictive, prothrombotic milieu within the microcirculation [37–40]. These mediators contribute to endothelial dysfunction by reducing nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, enhancing oxidative stress, and activating vasoconstrictive and prothrombotic pathways; as a result, microvascular homeostasis is disrupted [41]. In the retinal microcirculation, these processes may manifest as decreased capillary perfusion and reduced vessel density, reflecting the susceptibility of the retina to systemic inflammatory and oxidative insults [14, 42]. Consistent with this pathophysiological framework, in both AS and other inflammatory diseases, chronic inflammation has been shown (by OCTA) to be associated with reduced retinal or choroidal vascular density [19, 35, 43, 44]. Indeed, Karataş et al. reported decreased retinal vessel density in AS patients despite biologic therapy [19]. Similarly, Ferreira et al. observed reduced superficial and deep capillary plexus perfusion in systemic lupus erythematosus patients [45]. In addition, psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis cohorts have also demonstrated retinal microvascular rarefaction associated with systemic cytokine burden [43, 44]. Consistent with this pathophysiology, Bodnár and colleagues reported strong inverse correlations between flow-mediated dilation (FMD) and carotid intima–media thickness (cIMT) in AS, indicating that endothelial dysfunction emerges early at both micro- and macrovascular levels [36]. Although suppression of chronic inflammation can partially restore microvascular function, the long-term consequences of cumulative structural injury may persist—offering a cogent explanation for the persistence of microcirculatory abnormalities even in patients receiving biologic therapy.

Although biologic treatment duration was longer in the active AS subgroup than in the inactive subgroup, correlation analyses did not demonstrate a significant association between treatment duration and the principal OCTA parameters. In sensitivity-adjusted multivariable models, treatment duration showed only a modest independent association with active disease status (Model A′: p = 0.046; Model B′: p = 0.024). However, this finding likely reflects a selection effect rather than a direct vascular influence of prolonged therapy. In clinical practice, biologic agents are typically initiated in patients with higher disease activity, more advanced structural involvement, or inadequate response to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, meaning that individuals receiving long-term biologic therapy may inherently represent a more severe baseline disease phenotype [46]. Accordingly, the observed relationship between treatment duration and active disease in our cohort may be better interpreted as a surrogate marker of baseline severity and persistent inflammatory burden rather than a direct effect of biologic exposure on retinal microvasculature. Importantly, the main clinical predictor (BASFI) and the principal OCTA discriminator (DVC FAZ area) remained independently associated with active disease after adjustment for treatment duration (Model B′: p = 0.003 and p = 0.031, respectively), supporting the robustness of these associations and arguing against substantial confounding by treatment exposure.

The clinical relevance of our findings can be considered on two fronts. First, retinal microcirculatory abnormalities may serve as a subclinical indicator of the well-recognized elevation in cardiovascular risk in AS. A standardized retinal OCTA protocol could be integrated into the routine ophthalmologic follow-up of AS patients, thereby providing clinically actionable data in a practical manner for both rheumatologic and cardiovascular risk management. The retina is uniquely suited for direct, non-invasive visualization of the systemic microvasculature; in this context, van Bentum et al. demonstrated reduced retinal arteriolar tortuosity and increased vessel density in AS, linking these parameters to cardiovascular risk profiles [9, 11]. In our data, DVC VAD and FD-300 did not provide stand-alone diagnostic discrimination but appear to offer complementary value when integrated with routine clinical and biochemical markers. Indeed, in our parsimonious multivariable model (Model C), the combination of BASFI and DVC FAZ area achieved an AUC of 0.882 for discriminating active from inactive AS, supporting the potential incremental value of OCTA-derived metrics within an integrated clinical assessment. Second, for distinguishing active from inactive disease, the DVC-FAZ area displayed limited discriminatory power when used in isolation; however, when interpreted alongside clinical activity indices and acute-phase reactants, it may function as a supportive parameter for monitoring disease activity. Within this framework, retinal OCTA could be incorporated into clinical decision-making as a complementary biomarker panel: DVC vessel-density measures capturing the accumulation of chronic vascular injury, coupled with DVC-FAZ dynamics that are more sensitive to short-term inflammatory fluctuations. This combined approach may enhance risk stratification and longitudinal assessment in AS beyond conventional evaluations.

This study has several limitations. Because it has a cross-sectional design, the causality of the observed microvascular changes and their evolution over time cannot be evaluated. The relatively small sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect differences, particularly between the active and inactive subgroups. Notably, although the a priori power analysis indicated that 35 participants per group were required, only 26 patients could be included in the inactive AS subgroup, which may have limited the detection of small-to-moderate subgroup differences. Since all participants were receiving biologic therapy, the distinct effects of different agents (such as IL-17 or JAK inhibitors) on the microcirculation could not be differentiated. In addition, OCTA image quality and segmentation errors are factors that may lead to technical variation in the measurements. Moreover, the fact that all measurements were performed by a single observer without formal intra- or inter-observer reliability assessment (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis) may increase the risk of observer-related bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, all data were acquired using the same device and analysis protocol to minimize this effect. Future prospective and multicenter studies will more robustly elucidate the prognostic value of retinal microvascular parameters in disease progression and treatment response.

Conclusions

Retinal OCTA analysis suggests that persistent microvascular changes may continue—particularly at the DVC level—in patients with AS undergoing biologic therapy, whereas retinal thickness, RNFL, and CCT appear to remain largely preserved. Although vessel density and FAZ-based indices of the DVC demonstrate limited discriminative power individually, they may provide complementary information regarding both the presence and activity of the disease when assessed alongside other OCTA parameters. These findings indicate that subtle microvascular alterations may persist in some biologic-treated patients. Therefore, DVC-focused OCTA metrics may offer additional value to clinical decision-making when integrated into multiparametric evaluations rather than used as standalone diagnostic tools. These findings highlight the need for larger, longitudinal studies to further clarify the clinical significance of OCTA-based microvascular alterations in AS.
