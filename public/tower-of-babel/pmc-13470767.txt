A Practical and Efficient Multiplex qPCR Assay for Simultaneous Detection of HLA Cross‐Reactive Groups and Human Platelet Antigens
Authors: Huimin Ji, Kaihao Feng, Yunping Xu, Le Chang, Ying Yan, Jie Ma, Yi Liu, Jinfeng Zeng, Huizhen Sun, Lunan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmune‐mediated platelet transfusion refractoriness (IPTR) remains a major clinical challenge, with immune alloimmunization against HLA and HPA as the predominant causes. Although HLA cross‐reactive groups (CREGs) provide a practical alternative to allele‐level matching, methods for simultaneous HLA‐CREG and HPA genotyping remain limited despite their potential to improve platelet matching.MethodsSpecific U‐anchoring primers (UAPs) and amplification refractory mutation system (ARMS) primers were designed and optimized based on one or more SNPs within HLA‐CREG subgroups and HPA alleles. Reaction conditions were optimized, and analytical performance was evaluated. A total of 200 donor DNA samples were tested and compared with NGS and a commercial HPA sequencing kit, with discrepant results verified by Sanger sequencing.ResultsWe developed and optimized a novel multiplex real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay integrating UAPs and ARMS primers for simultaneous detection of HLA‐A/B CREGs and major HPA alleles in 12 reaction tubes. The assay demonstrated an overall limit of detection (LOD) of 0.838 ng/μL and the intra‐/inter‐assay coefficients of variation were both below 5%, indicating high reproducibility. HLA‐CREG typing results were 100% concordant with next‐generation sequencing. Sixteen discrepant HPA genotyping results were identified compared with the commercial kit, all of which were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and were consistent with the multiplex qPCR assay.ConclusionsThis multiplex qPCR assay enables rapid, accurate, and cost‐effective simultaneous detection of HLA‐CREGs and HPA alleles, providing a practical tool for comprehensive platelet compatibility assessment and improved transfusion matching.

Introduction

Platelet transfusion is a critical therapeutic strategy for the prevention and treatment of hemorrhage in patients with thrombocytopenia or platelet function disorders. However, approximately 5%–34% of patients experience platelet transfusion refractoriness (PTR), a condition in which transfused platelets fail to adequately increase the patient's platelet count [1]. In patients with bone marrow failure (aplastic anemia, acute leukemia, and myelodysplastic syndrome), the incidence of PTR can be as high as 30%–70% [2]. While various nonimmune factors such as fever, infection, and splenomegaly may contribute to PTR, alloimmune causes, particularly alloimmunization against human leukocyte antigens (HLA) and human platelet antigens (HPA), are major contributors to immune‐mediated PTR (IPTR) [3]. HLA antigens are widely expressed on nucleated cells and platelets and account for the majority of immune‐mediated PTR cases, whereas HPAs are platelet‐specific polymorphic antigens that can also induce clinically significant alloantibodies and transfusion complications.

Among HLA molecules, cross‐reactive groups (CREGs) represent clusters of shared epitopes among different HLA‐A or HLA‐B antigens [4, 5]. CREGs are clinically relevant because alloantibodies formed against a given HLA antigen can react with other antigens within the same CREG group [6]. Therefore, CREG‐based matching is considered a feasible and efficient alternative to high‐resolution HLA matching in clinical platelet transfusion and kidney transplantation practices [7, 8, 9, 10]. In addition to HLA incompatibility, mismatches in the HPA system are also closely associated with immune complications such as PTR, neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (NAIT), and post‐transfusion purpura (PTP) [11, 12, 13]. These associations highlight the critical importance of accurate and comprehensive HPA genotyping in ensuring optimal transfusion outcomes.

Despite the clinical importance of HLA‐CREG and HPA genotyping, currently available methods, including PCR‐SSP, PCR‐SSO, and NGS, are still limited by low throughput, labor‐intensive protocols, prolonged turnaround times, high costs, and substantial technical demands, thereby limiting their routine implementation in clinical laboratories and blood centers [14, 15, 16].

To overcome these limitations, we developed a novel multiplex real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay that integrates U‐anchoring primers (UAPs) with amplification refractory mutation system (ARMS) primers. This dual‐primer design enables precise single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) discrimination while maintaining high specificity, sensitivity, rapid turnaround, and low cost. Unlike previously reported molecular assays that generally focus on either HLA or HPA genotyping alone, our method enables the simultaneous detection of HLA‐A/B CREG groups and 14 clinically relevant HPA alleles within a single workflow. The assay was validated using whole‐blood samples from 200 blood donors and compared with an NGS‐based HLA typing kit and commercial HPA sequencing kits. It demonstrated excellent accuracy, reproducibility, and compatibility with conventional qPCR platforms. By combining broad multiplexing capability with rapid turnaround, low cost, and compatibility with conventional qPCR platforms, this assay provides a practical solution for comprehensive platelet compatibility assessment in routine transfusion settings.

Materials and Methods

Plasmid Construction for HLA and HPA Targets

Based on the grouping of HLA‐A and HLA‐B alleles according to CREGs and sequence homology, these loci were further classified into 22 representative subgroups. At least one HLA gene sequence was chosen from each subgroup to serve as its representative. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from peripheral blood using the Magnetic Serum/Plasma DNA Kit commercial kit (Tiangen, China). Full‐length HLA‐A and HLA‐B genes were amplified by PCR (Table S1) and cloned into the pEASY‐Blunt Cloning Vector (Tran, China). For low‐frequency alleles, gene synthesis was used. For HPA alleles, gDNA was extracted from relevant samples, and 500–1500 bp fragments flanking the SNPs were amplified and cloned into blunt‐end vectors. Site‐directed mutagenesis was applied to construct low‐frequency HPA alleles. Details of all recombinant plasmids are listed in Table S2.

Design and Optimization for Specific Primers and Probes

Specific primers were designed based on one or more SNPs within HLA‐CREG subgroups and HPA alleles. Two primer types were used: UAPs and ARMS primers, each targeting specific SNPs at their respective loci (Figure S1).

UAPs consist of a long 5′ region and a short 3′ region, connected by a poly(A) spacer. The SNP is positioned in the middle of the 3′ segment. Initial UAPs were 15–30 bp in length, with Tm values of 55°C–65°C and GC content of 30%–80%. The 5′ region was designed with a higher Tm than the 3′ region. Primer optimization involved adjusting the 3′ region and spacer length while keeping the 5′ region constant. For well‐performing primers, further 5′‐end optimization was carried out to enhance amplification efficiency.

ARMS primers were designed to match the target SNP at the 3′ terminal base, exploiting Taq polymerase's lack of 3′ → 5′ exonuclease activity to prevent non‐specific extension. This principle has been widely applied in allele‐specific PCR assays for HLA genotyping, including approaches developed for the accurate detection of the HLA‐B*15:02 allele [17]. Design criteria included a 15–30 bp length, a Tm of 60°C–65°C, and a GC content of 30%–80%. A deliberate mismatch was introduced at the −2 position from the 3′ end to improve SNP discrimination. If needed, a second mismatch at the −3 position was added. For highly specific primers, final adjustments were made to optimize overall Tm and performance.

After identifying specific primers, corresponding probes were designed. Primer‐probe sets were selected based on the following criteria: Ct ≤ 30 for the target (1 × 104 copies/μL), no amplification or Ct > 35 for non‐targets (1 × 104 copies/μL), and ΔCt > 9 between matched and mismatched templates. The final sequences of UAP and ARMS primers used in this study are listed in Table S3.

Multiplex PCR Reaction Conditions and Optimization

Multiplex PCR reactions were conducted in a final volume of 40 μL using the Genome Multi PCR Kit (Genome, China). Key reaction components and thermal cycling parameters were optimized for efficiency and specificity. MgCl2 (4–20 mM), dNTPs (0.2–1.0 mM), and Taq DNA polymerase (0.8–4.0 U) were tested, along with annealing temperatures (56°C–64°C) and durations (15–75 s). The final reaction mix included the following: 20 μL of 2 × PCR Mix, 4 μL of dNTPs (2.5 mM), 0.2 μmol/L of each primer, 1 μL of DNA polymerase, 5 μL of gDNA, and 7.8 μL of water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 94°C for 3 min (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. PCR amplifications were performed using the SLAN‐96H (Hongshi Technology, China).

Internal Control and Quality Control Strategy

To prevent false negatives and ensure the effectiveness of both gDNA extraction and qPCR amplification, an internal control targeting a conserved HLA‐B region was designed using UAPs and a specific probe. This control showed similar amplification efficiency to that of the other targets and was included in the first 11 PCR tubes for each sample. A Ct value ≤ 32 was considered indicative of valid extraction and amplification; otherwise, the result was considered invalid. To monitor amplification integrity and exclude false‐positive results, an exogenous adenovirus‐based control was introduced. This pseudovirus contained a synthetic, nonhuman DNA sequence, hereafter referred to as random DNA, and was co‐extracted with test samples. The resulting extract was added to the 12th PCR tube, which served as the negative control and contained primers and probes for both the Random DNA sequence and the HLA‐B internal control. The assay was valid if: (1) the HLA‐B internal control in tubes 1–11 had Ct ≤ 32; (2) the Random DNA target in the negative control tube had a Ct ≤ 32; and (3) the HLA‐B internal control in the negative control tube showed no amplification. Primer and probe sequences are listed in Table S4.

Limit of Detection (LOD) and Reproducibility Evaluation

The LOD of the multiplex qPCR assay was determined by analyzing the target with the lowest amplification efficiency. gDNA samples were serially diluted 2‐fold and tested seven times per day for three days, with more than 20 measurements per concentration. Positive detection rates were analyzed using a probit regression model to identify the LOD (≥ 95% detection).

For reproducibility, two known‐genotype gDNA concentrations were tested: one at three times the LOD and one at typical clinical levels. Each was measured seven times per day for three days (n > 20). Intra‐ and inter‐assay precision were assessed based on Ct values.

Interpretation of HLA‐CREG and HPA Genotyping Results

HLA‐CREG grouping was determined based on the amplification status of the corresponding CREG‐specific primer/probe sets. Amplification in a given reaction tube (Ct ≤ 35) was interpreted as the presence of the corresponding CREG subgroup, whereas the absence of amplification was interpreted as negative. For HPA genotyping, allele‐specific amplification patterns generated by the ARMS/UAP primer sets were used for genotype assignment. Amplification of only one allele‐specific reaction was interpreted as a homozygous genotype (aa or bb), whereas amplification of both reactions was interpreted as a heterozygous genotype (ab).

Validation With Clinical Samples

A total of 200 whole‐blood samples were collected from healthy blood donors at Shenzhen Blood Center for gDNA extraction. The extracted gDNA samples were tested using the multiplex qPCR assay developed in this study. Concurrently, HLA‐A and HLA‐B genotyping was performed using the GENDX NGS HLA Typing Kit (BFR, Beijing, China), while HPA genotyping was conducted using the commercial HPA [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 15, 18] Antigen Sequencing Kit (Weihe Biotechnology Inc., Jiangsu, China). For samples with discrepant results between the multiplex qPCR assay and either sequencing or the commercial kit, Sanger sequencing was employed as the reference method for further validation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (Approval No. 2024BJYYEC‐KY316‐02) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because all samples were anonymized residual specimens from routine blood donation screening.

Results

Schematic Mechanism of the CREG Grouping and HPA Typing Assay

The workflow of this assay for testing HLA‐CREGs and HPA alleles is shown in Figure 1. Briefly, gDNA was extracted from whole‐blood samples, while in parallel a pseudovirus solution containing the random DNA sequence was extracted to serve as the negative control. The DNA from whole blood was dispensed into the first 11 qPCR tubes, while the random DNA was added to the 12th tube. Each tube contained a preloaded, target‐specific qPCR reaction mix. After thorough mixing, the tubes were subjected to amplification using the SLAN‐96H for approximately 40 min. Following amplification, the results were interpreted based on Ct values from the 12 tubes, with two quality control (QC) criteria required for result validation: (a) In the 11 reaction tubes targeting HLA‐A, HLA‐B CREG groups, and HPA genotypes, the internal control had to show a Ct value ≤ 32 with a typical sigmoidal (“S”‐shaped) amplification curve; (b) In the negative control tube, the internal control showed no amplification, while the random DNA control showed a Ct value ≤ 32 with a standard “S”‐shaped curve. Only results meeting both criteria were considered valid. For valid results, targets with a typical amplification curve and Ct ≤ 35 were interpreted as positive; those without a curve or with Ct > 35 were considered negative.

Primer and Probe Screening and Optimization

HLA‐A and HLA‐B alleles were grouped based on CREGs and sequence homology, resulting in 22 subgroups (Figure 2). Specific primers were designed to target subgroup‐specific SNPs within HLA‐CREGs and HPA alleles, followed by systematic screening and validation (Figure 3). Taking the 1C1 subgroup as an example (Figure 3), the specific SNP site for the 1C1 subgroups is “T,” and the penultimate nucleotide is “C.” We first designed ARMS and UAP primers targeting this site for the initial round of screening. In this first round, the penultimate nucleotide of the ARMS primers was designed as C, T, or A, and the 3′‐end lengths of the UAP primers were designed as 10, 8, and 6 bp, respectively. The primers were tested against both 1C1‐specific and non‐1C1 plasmids of 1 × 104 copies/μL. The results showed that the ARMS primer with a penultimate base of “C” and the UAP primer with a 6‐bp 3′ end yielded a single melting‐curve peak with the 1C1‐specific template while producing either lower or different melting peaks with non‐specific templates or ddH2O. These primers were selected for further optimization.

In the second round, the penultimate nucleotide of the ARMS primer was fixed as C, while the third‐to‐last base was further modified to C, G, or T. Simultaneously, the UAP primers varied in the number of ‘a’ units in the linker region (ranging from 3a to 9a). Corresponding probes were also designed for fluorescence‐based qPCR. The ARMS primer with a ‘G' at the third‐to‐last position and the UAP primer with 8 ‘a’ units showed superior specificity and were selected for further optimization.

In the third round, the 5′‐end lengths of both ARMS and UAP primers were adjusted in length. The UAP primer with an 18‐bp 5′ end demonstrated the best specificity, with a maximal ΔCt value of 17.5. The Ct value for the 1C1‐specific template was ≤ 30, while the Ct value for non‐1C1 templates was > 35, meeting the predefined selection criteria.

Based on this strategy, ARMS and UAP primers for all targets were designed accordingly. The final specific ARMS and UAP primers for each target are listed in Table S3.

Optimization of Reaction Conditions

Using the previously optimized UAP and ARMS primers for the 1C2 subgroup as a foundation, this study systematically optimized the reaction system and amplification conditions for detecting the 1C2 subgroup. Initially, key reaction components, such as MgCl2 (4–20 mM), dNTPs (0.2–1.0 mM), and Taq DNA polymerase (0.8–4.0 U), were adjusted. The results showed that the optimal detection specificity, reflected by the highest ΔCt value, was achieved when the final concentrations were 8 mM MgCl2, 0.4 mM dNTPs, and 1.6 U Taq DNA polymerase per reaction (Table 1 and Figure S2).

Annealing temperature optimization showed that lower temperatures (56°C and 58°C) caused non‐specific amplification, whereas higher temperatures (62°C and 64°C) inhibited amplification (Table 1 and Figure S2). Optimization of the annealing time revealed that longer durations (≥ 45 s) promoted binding of primers to non‐specific templates, resulting in non‐specific amplification with Ct values < 35. Conversely, overly short annealing times caused low amplification efficiency of the target, with Ct values for specific amplification >°30 (Table 1 and Figure S2).

Considering these results, an annealing temperature of 60°C and an annealing time of 30 s were selected as the optimal amplification conditions.

The Detection of Clinical Samples

gDNA was extracted from 200 whole‐blood samples collected from voluntary blood donors and analyzed using the multiplex qPCR assay developed in this study. In parallel, HLA‐A and HLA‐B genotyping was performed by sequencing, while HPA genotyping was conducted using a commercial kit from Weihe Bio (Beijing, China). Discrepant results were further validated by Sanger sequencing. The HLA‐CREG grouping results from the multiplex qPCR assay were 100% consistent with NGS sequencing (Table 4). However, discrepancies were found in 7 cases (3.5%) for HPA‐3 and 9 cases (4.5%) for HPA‐15 compared with the commercial kit (Table 5). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the results obtained using our multiplex qPCR assay were consistent with the Sanger sequencing data. Figure 4 presents the sequencing results for HPA‐3 and HPA‐15 discordant samples, including cases in which homozygous samples were misclassified as heterozygous and, conversely, heterozygous samples were misclassified as homozygous by the commercial kit. Additionally, Figure 5 presents representative amplification curves from the multiplex qPCR assay for one of the analyzed samples.

Discussion

IPTR is one of the most complex and challenging complications encountered in transfusion medicine. It is characterized by the failure to achieve satisfactory post‐transfusion platelet count increments despite transfusion of appropriate platelet doses. Studies have shown that immune factors are responsible for a significant proportion of PTR cases, among which more than 80% are attributed to alloimmunization against HLA class I molecules, particularly HLA‐A and HLA‐B antigens expressed on platelet surfaces, and approximately 10% are caused by alloantibodies against HPA [19]. Notably, HLA‐C antigens are typically not expressed on platelets and are therefore less involved in immune‐mediated PTR [20].

Repeated exposure to allogeneic antigens through transfusions is a key driver of alloimmunization. Once IPTR develops, patients may experience reduced transfusion efficacy, persistent or worsening thrombocytopenia, and heightened risk of severe bleeding. This condition often leads to a cascade of adverse consequences including prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, greater demand on blood product resources, and in severe cases, a decline in patient survival. Moreover, repeated transfusion attempts in sensitized individuals can further exacerbate alloimmune responses, creating a vicious cycle of immunologic resistance and clinical deterioration [21].

Traditionally, HLA antigen matching has been regarded as the gold standard for mitigating immune‐related PTR. However, because of the high genetic polymorphism of HLA, fully matched donors are extremely rare. It has been estimated that at least 5000, 18,000, and 25,000 donor candidates are required to identify just five fully compatible donors in Japanese, European Caucasoid, and North American Caucasoid populations, respectively [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that matching based on shared HLA epitopes—commonly referred to as CREGs—can achieve comparable clinical outcomes [22, 23]. These findings suggest that CREG‐based strategies could serve as a clinically effective and logistically simpler alternative to allele‐level matching, particularly in settings where high‐resolution genotyping is not feasible.

Although several molecular methods have been developed for HLA or HPA genotyping, most existing PCR‐ and qPCR‐based approaches focus on a single antigen system or require multiple independent assays to obtain comprehensive compatibility information. In contrast, the assay presented here integrates HLA‐A/B CREG grouping and major HPA genotyping into a single multiplex workflow. By simultaneously assessing the two principal alloimmune determinants of immune‐mediated platelet transfusion refractoriness, this approach provides a more comprehensive and practical strategy for platelet compatibility evaluation while maintaining the advantages of rapid turnaround, low cost, and compatibility with standard qPCR platforms.

In this study, we developed a multiplex qPCR assay that enables simultaneous detection of HLA‐A/B CREG groupings and key HPA alleles. This system is based on a dual‐primer strategy that integrates UAPs, which ensure efficient amplification across multiple SNPs, and ARMS primers, which provide high specificity for SNP discrimination. Through systematic optimization of reaction conditions—such as MgCl2 and dNTP concentrations, annealing temperatures, and primer concentrations—the assay demonstrated high sensitivity, reproducibility, and stability in our validation experiments. Typically, gDNA extracted from 200 μL of whole‐blood yields a concentration of approximately 50 ng/μL; therefore, the detection sensitivity of 0.838 ng/μL achieved by this method is fully sufficient for routine clinical testing. Importantly, this assay does not require high‐end instrumentation and can be performed on conventional qPCR platforms commonly available in clinical laboratories. Similar real‐time PCR‐based approaches have previously been reported for rapid HLA genotyping, such as melting curve analysis for HLA‐B27 detection [24], supporting the feasibility of qPCR‐based strategies for routine clinical HLA testing.

Its cost‐effectiveness and ease of implementation may significantly lower the barrier to broader adoption of HLA‐CREG grouping and HPA genotyping, enabling pre‐transfusion matching in a timely and scalable manner. Specifically, the per‐sample testing cost of this method is less than 50 RMB, with a turnaround time of only 40 min. In contrast, NGS‐based approaches typically exceed 200 RMB per sample per locus and require substantially longer processing times. Other methods such as SSP are relatively low‐cost as well, but they involve additional downstream steps like electrophoresis, which increases the overall time burden. From a practical implementation perspective, the assay workflow is relatively simple and does not require specialized sequencing instruments, post‐PCR electrophoresis, or complex bioinformatics analysis. The multiplex design allows simultaneous assessment of multiple HLA‐CREG groups and HPA alleles within a single testing workflow, reducing hands‐on time and reagent consumption. Because the assay is fully compatible with conventional real‐time PCR platforms commonly available in hospital laboratories and blood centers, it can be readily integrated into existing diagnostic workflows. These characteristics may facilitate broader adoption in transfusion services, particularly in resource‐limited laboratories where NGS‐based approaches remain difficult to implement.

This study summarizes key principles for optimizing ARMS and UAP primer design. Thermodynamic differences among mismatches critically influence primer specificity: mismatches such as “GA(AG),” “CT(TC),” “TT,” “CC,” “AA,” and “GG” exhibit higher stability, whereas “CA(AC)” and “GT(TG)” represent weaker mismatches [25]. Accordingly, in ARMS primers, strong SNP‐complementary mismatches require only a single weak or strong additional mismatch at the penultimate position, whereas weak mismatches necessitate the introduction of stronger destabilizing mismatches at the penultimate or antepenultimate positions. Compared with ARMS primers, UAP primers provide greater flexibility and specificity in two scenarios. First, when multiple SNPs cluster near the 3′ end, ARMS primer design demands multiple mismatch manipulations, substantially increasing design complexity, while UAP primers can simultaneously cover these sites by tuning the bridging structure and “a” base length. Second, ARMS primers are limited in regions prone to secondary structures or with high GC content, whereas UAP primers circumvent such constraints through their bridging sequence, preserving target discrimination. Collectively, these findings refine primer design strategies, highlighting the context‐dependent advantages of ARMS and UAP approaches, and offering a framework applicable to diverse SNP genotyping settings.

During validation with clinical samples from 200 blood donors, the assay showed complete concordance with sequencing results for HLA‐CREG classification. For HPA genotyping, some discrepancies were noted when compared with results from a commercial detection kit. Specifically, inconsistencies were observed in HPA‐3 (3.5%) and HPA‐15 (4.5%) typing. Sanger sequencing of these discrepant samples confirmed the accuracy of our multiplex qPCR assay. The observed discrepancies may reflect differences in assay design, target selection, and allele discrimination strategies between the two methods. HPA‐3 and HPA‐15 genotyping are based on SNPs that may be particularly sensitive to primer specificity and mismatch discrimination efficiency. Variations in primer‐binding regions, amplification efficiency, or allele‐calling algorithms could therefore contribute to discordant results. Although Sanger sequencing supported the accuracy of our assay for all discrepant samples, the exact causes of disagreement could not be definitively determined because the detailed design and analytical parameters of the commercial kit are proprietary. Further evaluation using larger and genetically diverse cohorts would be valuable to better characterize the performance differences between these methods.

Antibodies against HPA‐3 and HPA‐15 have well‐established clinical implications. Previous studies showed that antibodies targeting HPA‐3a were detected in approximately 2% of FNAIT cases, highlighting the pathogenic potential of this antigen system [26]. For HPA‐15, several reports have documented serious adverse outcomes: one case involved maternal anti‐HPA‐15b alloimmunization resulting in severe neonatal thrombocytopenia (platelet count 3 × 109/L), purpura, and extensive hematomas [27]; another case implicated anti‐HPA‐15b antibodies in hydrops fetalis, accompanied by pathological findings of inhibited angiogenesis and increased endothelial apoptosis [28]. These findings illustrate that accurate HPA‐3 and HPA‐15 genotyping is not only essential for methodological reliability but also critical for transfusion safety—particularly in vulnerable populations such as neonates, pregnant women, and patients requiring repeated platelet transfusions.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, the validation cohort consisted exclusively of healthy blood donors, without inclusion of patients with confirmed PTR or those receiving platelet transfusions. Therefore, we were unable to directly correlate our findings with clinical transfusion outcomes such as corrected count increment or bleeding resolution. Second, although we included several clinically important HPA alleles, rare alleles and subtypes were not fully represented in the sample pool, limiting the sensitivity assessment.

In summary, we report the development and validation of a novel, multiplex qPCR‐based assay capable of simultaneously detecting HLA‐CREG groups and major HPA alleles. This method is rapid, specific, cost‐effective, and compatible with standard laboratory infrastructure. It has the potential to improve the efficiency of platelet matching, reduce the incidence of IPTR, and enhance overall transfusion safety. With further clinical validation, this approach may serve as a valuable tool for personalized transfusion strategies and precision medicine in transfusion‐dependent populations.
