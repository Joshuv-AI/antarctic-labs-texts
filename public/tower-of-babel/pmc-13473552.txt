Amelioration of cadmium toxicity in Vicia faba via modulation of caffeic acid O-methyltransferase gene expression by biochar and nanobiochar
Authors: Sherifa F.M. Dawoud, Medhat Rehan, Fatmah Ahmed Safhi, Esraa M. A. El-Shazly, Nora M. Al Aboud, Noha A. Sukar, Amany A. El Sadawy, Safaa A. El-Shazly, Abdelrahman A. Awad, Mohamed Elzarei, Amina M. G. Zedan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium (Cd) is considered one of the most mobile and toxic environmental contaminant on all organisms. This study aimed to remove the toxicity effect of Cd with important natural materials without harmful side effect on the living organisms. Two concentrations (1% and 2%) of biochar and nano-biochar (with an average particle size of 15.13 nm) were applied to Vicia faba L. under Cd stress (200 µM) by adding them to Hoagland’s nutrition solution. Results indicated that Cd significantly decreased plant growth parameters (root and shoot length, fresh and dry weights of shoot and root and leaf area) and chlorophyll levels under Cd stress. Cd elevated the antioxidant enzymes and proline levels. The genes that are engaged in the melatonin pathway (N-acetylserotonin, methyltransferase, Serotonin N-acetyltransferase slightly interacted (if any) with Cd stress. Caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT) only showed a significant response to Cd, with a 3.74-fold increase in expression at the first time-point. At the same time, biochar and nano-biochar effectively reduced the harmful effects of Cd on plant growth, improved physiological responses, and modulated the expression of the COMT gene. Nano-biochar exhibited particularly strong improvements across various growth parameters. This is the first time studying these genes in Vicia faba plants under any stress. COMT is involved in other pathways such as lignin pathway and might be expressed in response to Cd stress as a part of this pathway.

Introduction

Heavy metal pollution is a worldwide agricultural problem that affect the soil and human health1. It represents a major global challenge, owing to its severe effects on crop production, ecological balance. Heavy metals, such as cadmium, copper, lead, chromium and mercury are major environmental pollutants, particularly in areas with high anthropogenic pressure2. Cadmium (Cd) is discharged into the surrounding environment due to both natural and intensified activities among humans. These include mining, industrial processes, the elimination of waste comprised of metals, including but not limited to sludge, batteries, excessive use of fertilizers, and the utilization of items associated with cadmium3. Compared to the other heavy metals, cadmium has greater mobility in soil. It risks the health of plants, animals, and humans by accumulating in soil and vegetation4. Crop growth are impacted by Cd, which also lowers crop quality and yield5. By altering redox regulation in mitochondria, Cd-induced toxicity produces reactive oxygen species (ROS), which affected the plasma membrane lipids and causes disruption in plant metabolism6. Cd affects on the physically and chemistry nature of soil, depletion of natural resources, Cd also adversely impacts the composition and activities of soil microorganisms7,8.

The melatonin biosynthesis pathway in plants involves four enzymatic pathways, each requiring at least one enzyme, including serotonin N-acetyltransferase (SNAT) and N-acetylserotonin methyltransferase (ASMT), to produce melatonin from tryptophan9. Melatonin plays a crucial role in helping plants overcome Cd stress. Exposure to Cd enhances the expression of melatonin biosynthesis genes like SNAT, ASMT, and COMT in Arabidopsis thaliana and other plant species10.

Caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT) is mainly known for its role in lignin biosynthesis. Still, it can also function as N-acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase (ASMT), catalyzing the final step of melatonin production in plants. Melatonin, in turn, is crucial for regulating various plant processes and enhancing their ability to remedy stress11. COMT is involved in several plant functions, including helping plants respond to abiotic stresses and playing a role in phytohormone signaling.

Biochar (BC) is a type of charcoal produced by pyrolysis, a process that involves heating the material to a high temperature in the absence of oxygen to sufficiently carbonize it. Wood, other biomass sources, and agricultural wastes were among the materials employed in several research to prepare Biochar12. BC is a prospective modification that has the potential to reduce the bioavailability of heavy metals as well as their toxicity potential in soil due to its alkalinity, high cationic exchange capability, broad surface area, rich porous structure, and surface groups with a negative charge13–15. Furthermore, its capacity to adsorb contaminants will help with environmental cleanup, tackling soil and water pollution16. It has been discovered that BC is a powerful adsorbent for Cd, decreasing its bioavailability to plants and minimizing the risk of damage to living organisms17.

Using mechanical milling, chemical activation, or sonication, pyrolyzed biomass is further processed to create nano-biochar, a nanoscale variant of conventional biochar that produces particles with increased surface area, reactivity, and adsorption capacity18. It is a flexible material for environmental remediation due to its distinct physicochemical characteristics, which include high porosity and functionalized surfaces. It removes organic contaminants, heavy metals, and microplastics from soil and water19. According to Wang et al., 2020 20, nano-biochar optimizes soil-microbe interactions and moisture retention in agriculture, improving nutrient delivery, boosting plant development, and mitigating abiotic stressors like salt or drought.

So, knowing how biochar and nano-biochar affect plant growth when plants are under stress, as well as how application rates affect this, is very important for future field applications. We posited that both biochar and nano-biochar would promote plant growth, boost physiological responses, and modulate gene expression in response to cadmium stress. Besides, we looked at how Cd stops plants from growing. We investigated the efficacy of biochar and nano-biochar in promoting growth and regulating various physiological and molecular processes under cadmium stress. This study examined the subsequent research questions: What is the impact of cadmium stress on the growth, pigment content, antioxidant activity, and gene expression of Vicia faba L.? Can biochar and nano-biochar lessen the harmful effects of Cd toxicity? What is the role of Cd in the modification of genes responsible for melatonin expression? A controlled experiment examined physiological characteristics, antioxidant enzyme activities, and gene expression in Vicia faba subjected to cadmium stress, utilizing biochar and nano-biochar treatments. Thus, the study aims to understand the genetic mechanism involved in Cd tolerance and explore the impact of biochar and nano-biochar as soil amendments in reducing the effect of Cd on bean seedlings at the molecular level in the genes for melatonin expression.

The effect of Cd, biochar and nanobiochar on the plant growth parameters

Data on morphological attributes showed that Cd toxicity significantly decreased plant growth and biomass. In comparison to the group that served as the control, the reductions in the length of the shoot, the length of the root, the fresh weight of the shoot, the fresh weight of the root, the dry weight of the shoot, the dry weight of the root, and the area of the leaf were 56.1%, 58.8%, 49.3%, 58.3%, 53.2%, 78.4%, and 72.9% under the Cd treatment, respectively (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6). On the other hand, adding biochar and nano-biochar improved plant growth by reducing Cd toxicity and causing immobilization. Biochar increases the previous measurement parameters with values reached 73.77%, 67.34%, 46.68%, 128.23%, 45.04%, 69.28% and 51.63%, respectively, when compared with the control. Nano-biochar can effectively decrease the toxicity of Cd, with improvements in the previously measured parameters calculated 136.1%, 124.5%, 78.5%, 283.2%, 130.5%, and 177.5% compared to the control, respectively. The increment in biochar or nano-biochar doses had a positive effect on plant growth and health.

The pigments evaluation in plants under Cd stress with remediation by biochar and nanobiochar

Chlorophyll a and b with carotenoid concentrations were measured in the leaves of the plants. These pigments significantly decreased with Cd stress. Conversely, the application of biochar and nano-biochar significantly (p ≤ 0.05) increased the chlorophyll and carotenoid contents compared to the control and Cd treatments. The maximum rate was observed in nano-biochar treatments, which records a high percentage compared to the control. Cd reduced the total chlorophyll and carotenoids by 84.11% and 75.16%, respectively; biochar improved the total chlorophyll and carotenoids by 53.28% and 29.74% over than the control, respectively, whereas nano-biochar improved the total chlorophyll and carotenoids by 126.44% and 62.57% more than the control treatment, respectively (Fig. 7).

Discussion

As a heavy metal (HM), cadmium (Cd) is present in the environment due to both increased human activity and natural processes3. Plants are toxically affected by heavy metals like Cd, which negatively alter their morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics21. The findings of our study illustrate the detrimental effects of Cd on the growth parameters of bean plants, including the lengths of the shoot and root, the fresh weights of the shoot and root, the shoot and root dry weights, and the leaf area. These results agree with Ren et al. (2021)22, who reported that cadmium inhibited tobacco growth. Additionally, Ahmad et al. (2024)23 demonstrated that cadmium adversely affected plant growth by decreasing shoot and root length, root fresh and dry weight, shoot fresh and dry weight, spike length, number of tillers, and grain weight in Oryza sativa L plants. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2019)1 highlighted the harmful impact on plant quality, yield, and overall growth. The negative effects on growth may be linked to the higher mobility of cadmium as it moves to the aboveground parts of the plant24. The fundamental mechanism of these effects may be ascribed to the high mobility of cadmium within the plant system, promoting its accumulation in the aerial parts of plants. This buildup could interfere with growth by affecting important physiological and biochemical processes like nutrient uptake, photosynthesis, and water balance. Cadmium-induced oxidative stress may contribute by producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in cellular damage and disrupting normal metabolic functions. These stress responses could help explain why biomass and morphological growth have slowed down.

Using biochar (BC) and nano-biochar can enhance the growth of bean plants. Nano-biochar particles were characterized by the TEM and FTIR which provided insights into particle size and surface functional groups, whereas critical parameters for adsorption, such as specific surface area (e.g., via BET analysis), surface charge (zeta potential), and bulk elemental composition, were not measured. The incorporation of biochar demonstrates a reduction in the toxic effects of cadmium on plants while simultaneously promoting their growth25,26. The application of biochar effectively mitigated the inhibitory impact of cadmium22. Furthermore, through the enhancement of soil’s physical and chemical properties, reduction of nutrient loss, and facilitation of nutrient absorption in plants, biochar contributes to plant growth and development. This, in turn, helps mitigate the adverse effects of Cd stress on the growth of tobacco plants22. The combined use of biochar and plant growth regulators has demonstrated effective outcomes in removing the harmful effects of Cd stress on maize growth and minimizing Cd absorption by maize27. Prior research indicates that incorporating biochar into Cd-contaminated soils can enhance plant growth and mitigate Cd toxicity. This is attributed to improved nutrient availability and reduced concentrations of bioavailable Cd ions, leading to beneficial outcomes for plant development28.

Conversely, Cd treatment decreased the content of photosynthetic pigments (Chl-a, Chl-b, and carotenoids) in bean leaves. This finding is in agreement with the results reported by Waheed et al., (2022)29 who also highlighted similar physiological disruptions under cadmium stress. The fundamental mechanism of these effects may be ascribed to the accumulation of cadmium in plants resulted in the degradation of chloroplast structure hastening the decomposition of photosynthetic pigments, and reducing the overall photosynthetic pigment content22.

Biochar and nano-biochar enhanced chlorophyll a, b, and carotenoids in the presence of cadmium exposure. These results in cooperating with many findings that indicated the concentrations of chlorophyll b, a, and carotenoids in the foliage of tobacco, rice, and maize plants subjected to biochar treatment were increased22,23,27. Also, Bashir, et al. (2021)30 reported that applying ZnO nanoparticles with biochar improved wheat plant growth, increased biomass, elevated chlorophyll content, and improved the ratio of antioxidant enzymes. The addition of biochar demonstrates the potential to mitigate the harmful effects on plant cells by Cd stress, safeguard the structure of chloroplasts in tobacco leaves, and enhance the amount of photosynthetic pigments22. This could be associated with the improvement in mineral content resulting from biochar application and that mentioned by Abideen et al.31. Furthermore, when biochar nanoparticles were mixed with SiO2 nanoparticles, the highest reduction in Cd uptake occurred, followed by improvement in plant development characteristics32. The exposure to Cd stress manifested elevated levels of H2O2, MDA, proline, and sucrose, along with increased activity of POD, CAT, and SOD in the leaves of bean seedlings33. Trovato et al.,34 indicated that proline plays a role in responding to environmental stress conditions. Reports suggest that Cd induces oxidative damage in plants by elevating free oxygen radicals or diminishing the levels of non-enzymatic or enzymatic antioxidants in conditions of Cd toxicity35,36. Excessive levels of cadmium have been documented to cause genotoxic effects in various crops37.

Mixing biochar and nano-biochar under Cd stress notably enhanced antioxidant parameters and proline levels. BC is essential in mitigating oxidative stress in plant cells, thereby safeguarding the photosynthetic machinery from impairment resulting from Cd-induced stress38. The findings of Haider et al.27 said that using biochar on plants experiencing Cd stress notably enhanced enzyme activity in maize plants. The observed phenomenon may be attributed to the ability of biochar applications to eliminate detrimental consequences of Cd stress. Furthermore, biochar exhibits robust interaction mechanisms with active surfaces, encompassing ionic and covalent bonds39. Their functional groups can bind to heavy metals due to their surface properties, as demonstrated by Regmi et al. and Southavong et al., 40–42, which helps in mitigating the toxic effects of these metals. Throughout the growth period, the activities of antioxidant enzymes in tobacco leaves, including catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase, as well as the increase in malondialdehyde, were observed following the application of biochar22.

Numerous studies have shown that plants’ capacity to defend against damage induced by Cd is improved by producing extra antioxidants, which is supported by the integration of biochar and NPs composites43,44. Numerous experimental studies demonstrate that integrating nanoparticles can enhance cadmium’s phytoremediation from contaminated soil. This is achieved by improving the phytoremediation capabilities of crops, boosting crop health through increased tolerance to heavy metal toxicity, enhancing the properties of soil, and fostering the proliferation of beneficial microbes essential for effective phytoremediation45,46.

COMT gene regulates plant lignin production. In addition, the COMT gene produces melatonin, which improves Arabidopsis thalia’s salt tolerance47. It is widely known that greater COMT expression increases melatonin, which protects against abiotic and biotic stressors such as salt, drought, cold, heavy metal stress, and high temperatures9,48. Melatonin significantly improves plants’ stress tolerance49. In this study, we can observe that one of the studied genes reacts with Cd stress. The biosynthetic pathway of melatonin in plants begins with tryptophan and progresses through four steps, mirroring the process in animals. This pathway requires the sequential action of tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC), tryptamine 5-hydroxylase (T5H), followed by serotonin N-acetyltransferase (SNAT), and finally by N-acetylserotonin methyltransferase (ASMT)50,51. ASMT serves as the final enzyme in the biosynthesis of melatonin and may represent the rate-limiting step within this biosynthetic pathway52,53. In our study, we observed that the genes involved in the melatonin pathway didn’t respond to Cd stress. Therefore, we could conclude that the COMT gene in this study responded to Cd stress through an alternative pathway. This gene may be associated with another biosynthetic pathways such as lignin and/or flavonoid pathways). Recent studies have shown that the multifunctional enzyme COMT, previously considered to play a role in lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis, is indeed involved in these processes. Additionally, it facilitates the final steps of melatonin biosynthesis in plants51. Lignin not only contributes to plant development but also plays a crucial role in managing many biotic and abiotic stresses, facilitating plant adaptation to environmental conditions54.

In this study, we can conclude that the genes participating in the melatonin pathway didn’t react to Cd stress, whereas the COMT gene, involved in many pathways, was overexpressed in response to Cd stress. The biochar and nano-biochar recover the normal expression of COMT gene.

Materials and methods

Biochar and nano-biochar Preparation

Biochar and nano-biochar are made from date palm seeds. Following collection, they underwent cleaning, distilled water washing, and oven drying at 105 °C. In a porcelain airtight container, the samples were pyrolyzed for four hours at 500° in a furnace (ZBX1, China) with minimal oxygen to create biochar according to Mahdi et al. (2017)55 Elbasiouny et al.(2023)26. Samples of nano-biochar were made by manually grinding them into nanoparticles using a ceramic mortar for approximately nine hours. The samples were then preserved in hermetically sealed containers until they were analyzed and used for the experiment.

Nano-biochar characterization

The transmission electron microscope, abbreviated as ‘’TEM’’. The JEOL JEM2100, located in Tokyo, is utilized to investigate nanobiochar’s structure by measuring the particle size under the microscope. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of specimens were recorded by Jasco FT/IR 4100 spectrometer in the wavelength range of 4000–400 cm − 1.

Plant experimental design

A completely randomized design was utilized to create the current experiment. The experiment used Vicia faba plants (Sakha 1, acquired from the Center of Agricultural Research at Sakha Station, Kafr El-Sheikh, Egypt) seeds, which were organized into identical sizes. 2.5% sodium hypochlorite was used to sterilize their surfaces for 2 min, followed by a triple wash with sterile distilled water. After being soaked for 24 h and kept covered until they sprouted. Biochar, nano-biochar (concentrations of 1% and 2% (w/v56) and cadmium (200 µM57) were added to Hoagland’s nutrition solution and thoroughly mixed. In trays, sprouting seeds were submerged in half-strength Hoagland’s solution, which contains cadmium, biochar, and nano-biochar. The experiment was conducted with grown plants and divided into six groups (with three replicates in each group): control, 200 µM Cd, 200 µM Cd + biochar (1%), 200 µM Cd + biochar (2%), 200 µM Cd + nanobiochar (1%), and 200 µM Cd + nanobiochar (2%) under controlled conditions (temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity: ~60%, photoperiod: 16 h light / 8 h dark, and light intensity: approximately 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Plants were harvested for growth assessment, physiological analysis, and genetic research 21 days after treatment.

Growth characteristics

The parameters of growth included shoot lengths (cm), fresh weights (g), shoot dry weights (g), and leaf area (cm²), which were evaluated and assessed. Leaf area was calculated as described by Peksen(2007)58.

Chlorophyll pigments and carotenoids

Chlorophyll pigments and carotenoids were assessed in the stressed and non-stressed treatments. The leaf biomass weighted 0.1 g was extracted with 20 mL of 90% methanol using the spectrophotometric (Jenway 6305 UV/Visible) method following59, to quantify the amounts of carotenoids and chlorophyll (a, b). Chlorophyll was expressed as µg/ml methanol by the equation in Nofal et al. 60.

Antioxidant enzymes

The antioxidant enzymes include catalase (CAT), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and peroxidase (POD) activity and proline content were evaluated as follows: 0.2 g of the fresh leaves were crushed in liquid N2, mixed in 4 mL of the buffer that contains ice-cooled K-phosphate at pH 7, and centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min to extract the enzyme. The extracted crude was then used to test antioxidant enzyme activity (CAT, PPO, and POD). The enzymatic activity of catalase was assessed by quantifying the H2O2 rate breakdown61. The process was initiated by adding the enzyme extract (0.1 mL) to a reaction mixture containing five mM of buffer, comprising K-phosphate at pH levels of 7 and 10.5, and 10.5 mM of hydrogen peroxide. The extinction coefficient, which is 40 mM/cm, was used to assess the CAT activity by recording the decline in absorbance at 240 nm. Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) catalyzes the oxidation of gallic acid to yield purpurogallin. A 0.5 ml of the extract of the enzyme was incorporated into the reaction mixture consisting of pyrogallol (2 mM ) dissolved in K-phosphate buffer “0.1 M” at pH 6), employing the technique described by 62 to assess PPO activity. Following five minutes of incubation at 25 °C, 2.5 N sulfuric acid was introduced to end the reaction. Purpurogallin absorbance was measured at 420 nm, and the extinction value of 26.40 M[-1 cm[-1 was utilized to determine the activity of PPO. The POD assay depended on the enhanced absorption caused by guaiacol oxidation mediated by guaiacol peroxidase in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. The reaction mixture, consisting of 11.8 mM H2O2, 7.2 mM guaiacol, and 100 mM K-phosphate buffer at pH 5.8, was combined with 0.1 ml of the extract for enzyme to initiate the reaction.

Assay of proline

Two hundred milliliters of fresh leaf samples were pulverized with liquid nitrogen and extracted using a pestle in an ice-cold mortar containing ten milliliters of 5-sulfosalicylic acid 3%. The homogenate was filtered using filter paper, and the resulting filtrate was utilized for analysis. Free proline was quantified using an acidic ninhydrine reagent composed of glacial acetic acid “30 ml”, 20 ml of H3PO4 (6 ml), and 1.25 g of ninhydrine, as described by Bates et al. 63.

RNA extraction with gene expression

The total RNA was isolated from plant samples by TRIzol™ LS Reagent (Cat. No. 10296010, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA was quantified by NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The extracted RNA was employed to generate the cDNA for gene expression through qRT-PCR. SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix (Invitrogen) was used to amplify the cDNA as described in the manufacturer’s guide. QRT-PCR was performed using QuantiTect SYBR® Green RT-PCR, as described in the kit guide (Cat. No. / ID: 204243, Qiagen). The qPCR conditions involved activation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Four primers (Table 1) were used to detect gene expression differentiation under the various treatments, while the actin gene was used as a normalization control. The gene expression was calculated from two biological samples of three technical triplicates.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, data were examined to ensure compliance with the assumptions of parametric tests, including normality of distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). Differences among treatments were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, Duncan’s multiple range test was applied to compare treatment means at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

Conclusions

This study shows that Vicia faba L. plants’ growth, pigment content, antioxidant responses and gene expression are all severely impacted by cadmium stress. The application of both biochar and nano-biochar effectively mitigated these adverse effects by enhancing physiological performance, modulating antioxidant enzyme activity, and influencing melatonin and lignin biosynthesis pathways. Additionally, both treatments contributed to the regulation of the COMT gene expression, implying that they play a role in stress adaptation. Due to limitations in BET surface area measurements, zeta potential analysis, or elemental composition in this work, the future work will incorporate these analyses to provide a more complete characterization of nano‑biochar. According to the current results, nano-biochar with high concentrations is offering an economical and environmental approach in removing heavy metals for sustainable crop production, enhancing food security and environmental protection.
