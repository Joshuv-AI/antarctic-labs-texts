COMPLETE CHARACTERIZATION OF
𝟐

Abstract

Abstract

Let
\sigma(n)
be the sum of the positive divisors of
n
. A positive integer
n
is said to be
2
-near perfect when
\sigma(n)=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}
, where
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are distinct positive divisors of
n
. We show that there are no odd
2
-near perfect numbers with exactly two prime factors, and that all even
2
-near perfect numbers (i.e. those of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
, where
p
is an odd prime) belong to a specific family, provided that
m
is at least 3. In combination with prior work, these results produce a complete characterization of
2
-near perfect numbers with exactly 2 prime factors.

1
Introduction

A classical object of study in number theory is the perfect number, an integer
n
such that the sum of its divisors,
\sigma(n)
, equals
2n
. This concept has been generalized in various ways. One classic generalization is to pseudoperfect numbers. A number
n
is said to be pseudoperfect if
n
is equal to the sum of some subset of its proper divisors.

Pollack and Shevelev
[
8
]
broke the pseudoperfect numbers down further. They defined a number to be a
k
-near perfect number if
\sigma(n)=2n+\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
for
k
distinct positive divisors
d_{i}
of
n
. A number
n
is said to be
abundant
if it satisfies
\sigma(n)>2n
. If
n
is
s
-near perfect for some
s>0
, then
n
must be abundant, but the converse does not hold; 70 is a counterexample. Numbers which are abundant but not
k
-near perfect for any
k
are said to be
weird
and were first investigated by Benkowski and Erdős
[
3
]
. There is a classic conjecture that all weird numbers are even.

Recently, Jeba S, Roy, and Saikia
[
7
]
introduced the related idea of a
k
-facile number which is a number
n
such that
\sigma(n)=2n+\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
where for
1\leq i\leq k
the
d_{i}
are distinct divisors of
n
.

In the same paper where Pollack and Shevelev introduced
k
-near perfect numbers, they constructed the following three distinct families of 1-near perfect numbers:

1.

2^{t-1}(2^{t}-2^{k}-1)
where
2^{t}-2^{k}-1
is prime. Here
2^{k}
is the omitted divisor.

2.

2^{2p-1}(2^{p}-1)
where
2^{p}-1
is prime. Here
2^{p}(2^{p}-1)
is the omitted divisor.

3.

2^{p-1}(2^{p}-1)^{2}
where
2^{p}-1
is prime. Here
2^{p}-1
is the omitted divisor.

Ren and Chen
[
9
]
showed that all near perfect numbers with two distinct prime factors must be either 40, or one of the three families found by Pollack and Shevelev. Hasanalizade
[
6
]
gave a partial classification of near perfect numbers which are also Fibonacci numbers or Lucas numbers. Das and Saikia
[
5
]
described near perfect numbers which have certain other forms related to the Fibonacci sequence but which are not directly Fibonacci numbers themselves. Tang, Ma, and Feng
[
10
]
showed that the only odd near perfect number with four or fewer distinct prime divisors is
173369889=(3^{4})(7^{2})(11^{2})(19^{2}).
Adajar
[
1
]
also proved the same result as Tang, Ma and Feng, and also showed that, given a positive integer
k
, there are only finitely many odd near perfect with
k
distinct prime factors.

In the other direction, Cohen, Cordwell, Epstein, Kwan, Lott, and Miller
[
4
]
showed that for any fixed
k\geq 4
that there are infinitely many
k
-near perfect numbers and gave an asymptotic expression for their density. Thus,
k
-near perfect numbers are well-understood for
k=1
and
k\geq 4
with
k=2
or
k=3
being more open.

This paper focuses on
2
-near perfect numbers, that is numbers satisfying
\sigma(n)=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}
, where
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are distinct positive divisors of
n
. Prior work by Aryan, Madhavani, Parikh, and the penultimate author of this paper
[
2
]
gave a complete classification of
2
-near perfect numbers of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
where
p
is an odd prime and
m\in\{1,2\}
. In particular, their paper proved the following two theorems.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n=2^{k}p
be
2
-near perfect with
p
an odd prime. Then exactly one of the following statements is true:

•

p=2^{k}-1
, and the omitted divisors are 1 and
p
.

•

p=2^{k+1}-2^{a}-2^{b}-1
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, and the omitted divisors are
2^{a}
and
2^{b}
.

•

p=\frac{2^{k+1}-2^{a}-1}{1+2^{b}}
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, and the omitted divisors are
2^{a}
and
2^{b}p
.

•

p=\frac{2^{k+1}-1}{1+2^{a}+2^{b}}
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, and the omitted divisors are
2^{a}p
and
2^{b}p
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{2}
be
2
-near perfect with
p
an odd prime. Then
n\in\{18,36,200\}
.
[
2
]

Aryan, Madhavani, Parikh, and the penultimate author of this paper also conjectured the following.

Conjecture 1
.

There were only finitely many
2
-near perfect numbers of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
for
m\geq 3
.

Our main results disprove Conjecture
1
, subject to the standard conjecture that there are infinitely many Mersenne primes. More concretely, we give a complete classification of
2
-near perfect numbers of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
where
m\geq 3
. In particular, we intend to show the following.

Theorem 3
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
p
an odd prime and
m\geq 3
. Then
m=3
,
p=2^{k+1}-1
, and the omitted divisors are
p
and
p^{2}
.

Note that Theorem
3
implies that there are infinitely many
2
-near perfect of the form
n=2^{k}p^{m}
with
m\geq 3
if and only if there are infinitely many Mersenne primes. Since it is a standard conjecture that there are infinitely many Mersenne primes, the same conjecture should apply here.

1.1
Odd
2
-near perfect numbers

The above results give a complete classification of
2
-near perfect numbers with 2 prime factors. This is because there are no odd
2
-near perfect numbers with 2 prime factors.

To prove this result, we recall the following basic properties of the
\sigma(n)
function.

Lemma 1
.

Let
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
, where
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
are distinct primes and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}
are positive integers. Then

\sigma(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+p_{i}+p_{i}^{2}+\cdots+p_{i}^{a_{i}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}-1}{p_{i}-1}.

We will frequently use Lemma
1
without stating explicitly that we are using it. We also require the following standard upper bound on
\sigma(n)
.

Lemma 2
.

If
p
is prime and
m
is a positive integer, then
\frac{p}{p-1}>\frac{\sigma(p^{m})}{p^{m}})
.

Proof.

\frac{\sigma(p^{m})}{p^{m}}=\frac{1+p+\ldots+p^{m}}{p^{m}}=\frac{p^{m+1}-1}{p^{m}(p-1)}<\frac{p^{m+1}}{p^{m}(p-1)}<\frac{p}{p-1}.

∎

Note that the upper bound in Lemma
2
is, in a certain sense, the best possible bound. If one knows only the set of prime divisors of a number
n
, but not the corresponding exponents in its prime factorization, then no upper bound sharper than the one given in Lemma
2
can be obtained.

We are now equipped to prove the result.

Proposition 1
.

There are no odd
2
-near perfect numbers with exactly 2 prime factors.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
n=p^{m}q^{s}
is
2
-near perfect, where
p
and
q
are distinct odd primes and
m
and
s
are positive integers. By definition,
\sigma(n)=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}
. So:

\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}>2

which implies that

\frac{15}{8}=\frac{3}{3-1}\frac{5}{5-1}\geq\frac{p}{p-1}\frac{q}{q-1}>\frac{\sigma(p^{m})}{p^{m}}\frac{\sigma(q^{s})}{q^{s}}>2,

which is a contradiction. Note that at the third step above we are using here that
\frac{x}{x-1}
is a strictly decreasing function.
∎

Therefore, if
n
is
2
-near perfect and has exactly 2 prime factors, then
n
must be even. The vast majority of this paper will concern this situation.

1.2
Introductory Lemmas

We begin by establishing several basic properties of even
2
-near perfect numbers with two prime factors (those of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with omitted divisors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Then

(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}.

Proof.

Since
n
is
2
-near perfect, we have
\sigma(n)=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}
. Using the multiplicativity of
\sigma
and the formula for geometric series, we compute

\displaystyle\sigma(n)

\displaystyle=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle\sigma(2^{k})\cdot\sigma(p^{m})

\displaystyle=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle(1+2+\ldots+2^{k})(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle=2n+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}.

, which proves the desired result.
∎

The equation in Lemma
3
can be manipulated to yield a more convenient form. By factoring out
p^{m}
from the geometric series and rearranging terms, we obtain the following equivalent characterization.

Lemma 4
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with omitted divisors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Then

(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})=1+d_{1}+d_{2}.

Proof.

Starting from the equation in Lemma
3
, we factor the geometric series:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)\left[(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})+p^{m}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})+(2^{k+1}-1)p^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})

\displaystyle=p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}.

We can rewrite the left-hand side using the geometric series formula
1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1}=\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}
:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}

\displaystyle=p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{m}-1)}{p-1}

\displaystyle=p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}.

(2)

Multiplying both sides of Equation
2
by
(p-1)
and rearranging we get the desired result.
∎

The parity structure of the omitted divisors depends on the parity of the exponent
m
, as the following result demonstrates.

Lemma 5
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with omitted divisors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. If
m
is odd, then
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
have the same parity. If
m
is even, then
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
have opposite parities.

Proof.

From Lemma
4
, we have
(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})=1+d_{1}+d_{2}
. Since
p
is an odd prime and
2^{k+1}
is even, the factor
(2^{k+1}-p)
is odd.

The sum
(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})
consists of
m
terms, each of which is odd (since
p
is odd). Therefore, this sum is odd if
m
is odd, and even if
m
is even.

Since
(2^{k+1}-p)
is odd, the left-hand side
(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})
has the same parity as
(1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1})
. That is, the left-hand side is odd when
m
is odd, and even when
m
is even.

Therefore,
1+d_{1}+d_{2}
is odd when
m
is odd and even when
m
is even. This means
d_{1}+d_{2}
is even when
m
is odd and odd when
m
is even. The conclusion follows immediately from the parity of the sum
d_{1}+d_{2}
.
∎

2
The Case
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1

In this section, we establish that
2
-near perfect numbers of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
with
m\geq 3
cannot satisfy
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. This constraint is paramount for our classification of such numbers.

Throughout this section, we adopt the following conventions:
d_{1}=2^{a}p^{b}
denotes the greater omitted divisor, and
d_{2}=2^{c}p^{d}
denotes the lesser omitted divisor, where
j
is the unique integer satisfying
2^{j}<p<2^{j+1}
. Our primary goal for this section is to establish the following constraint:

Theorem 4
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
p
an odd prime and
m\geq 3
. Then
2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
.

We prove this result by contradiction, assuming
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
and deriving a contradiction through a sequence of increasingly restrictive conditions on the parameters.

2.1
Lower Bounds and Divisibility Constraints for the Exponent
m

Under the assumption
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
, we first establish the structure of the omitted divisors.

Lemma 6
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then the greater omitted divisor equals
\frac{n}{2^{j}}
, and the lesser omitted divisor is at least
\frac{n}{2^{j}p}
.

Proof.

This follows from the contrapositive of Proposition
2
combined with Proposition
3
, both established below.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
. If
\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}<2+\frac{2}{p}
, then
2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
.

Proof.

Suppose
\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}<2+\frac{2}{p}
. Using the formula for
\sigma(n)
when
n=2^{k}p^{m}
,

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{m+1}-1)}{2^{k}p^{m}(p-1)}

\displaystyle<\frac{2p+2}{p}

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{m+1}-1)}{2^{k}p^{m}(p-1)}

\displaystyle<\frac{2(p+1)}{p}.

Since
m\geq 3
, we can have two cases, when
m=3
or when
m>3
. If If
m>3
then since
m
is odd, we factor
p^{m+1}-1=(p^{4}-1)(p^{m-3}-1)
when
m>3
, or if
m=3
then we have that
p^{m+1}-1=p^{4}-1
. For the case
m=3
:

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{4}-1)}{2^{k}p^{3}(p-1)}

\displaystyle<\frac{2(p+1)}{p}

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{2}+1)(p+1)}{2^{k}p^{3}}

\displaystyle<\frac{2(p+1)}{p}

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{2}+1)}{2^{k}p^{3}}

\displaystyle<\frac{2}{p}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(p^{2}+1)

\displaystyle<2^{k+1}p^{2}

\displaystyle 2^{k+1}(p^{2}+1)-(p^{2}+1)

\displaystyle<2^{k+1}p^{2}

\displaystyle 2^{k+1}

\displaystyle<p^{2}+1.

For
m>3
, the proof is similar.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
. If
\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}\geq 2+\frac{2}{p}
, then the greater omitted divisor equals
\frac{n}{2^{j}}
and the lesser omitted divisor is at least
\frac{n}{2^{j}p}
.

Proof.

The divisors of
n=2^{k}p^{m}
are ordered as follows.

\displaystyle n>\frac{n}{2}>\frac{n}{2^{2}}>\ldots>\frac{n}{2^{j}}>\frac{n}{p}>\frac{n}{2^{j+1}}>\ldots.

Since
\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}\geq 2+\frac{2}{p}
, we have
d_{1}+d_{2}\geq\frac{2n}{p}
. Since
d_{1}>d_{2}
, we get
d_{1}>\frac{n}{p}
, which implies
d_{1}\geq\frac{n}{2^{j}}
.

To show
d_{1}\leq\frac{n}{2^{j}}
, we use the upper bound on
\sigma(n)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\frac{p}{p-1}

\displaystyle d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n}{p-1}.

Since
d_{1}
is the largest omitted divisor and
d_{1}<\frac{n}{2^{j-1}}
(as this would be included), we must have
d_{1}=\frac{n}{2^{j}}
.

For the lesser divisor, we have:

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2n}{p}-\frac{n}{2^{j}}=\frac{n(2^{j+1}-p)}{2^{j}p}\geq\frac{n}{2^{j}p}

since
2^{j+1}>p
.
∎

2.2
Constraints on the Exponent
m

We now derive several constraints on the exponent
m
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then
d\geq m-1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
,
d_{2}\geq\frac{n}{2^{j}p}>\frac{n}{p^{2}}=2^{k}p^{m-2}
. Suppose
d\leq m-2
. Then

\displaystyle d_{2}=2^{c}p^{d}

\displaystyle>2^{k}p^{m-2}

\displaystyle 2^{c}p^{m-2}

\displaystyle>2^{k}p^{m-2}

\displaystyle 2^{c}

\displaystyle>2^{k}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle>k

but this contradicts the constraint that
c\leq k
from the structure of the divisors of
n
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then
p^{m-1}\leq 2^{k+1}-1
.

Proof.

We have that
(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})=2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2}
and from Proposition
4
, we have
p^{m-1}|d_{2}
. Therefore:

\displaystyle p^{m-1}

\displaystyle|(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle p^{m-1}

\displaystyle|(2^{k+1}-1)

since
\gcd(p^{m-1},1+p+\ldots+p^{m})=1
. Thus
p^{m-1}\leq 2^{k+1}-1
.
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then
m
is odd.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
,
d_{1}=2^{k-j}p^{m}
and
d_{2}\geq 2^{k-j}p^{m-1}
. Suppose
m
is even. By our earlier parity result, exactly one of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
is odd.

If
d_{1}
is odd, then
k-j=0
, so
k=j
. But then
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1>2^{2j}=2^{2k}
, implying
k+1>2k
or
1>k
, which is impossible since
k\geq 1
.

If
d_{2}
is odd, then
c=0
and
d_{2}=p^{d}
. From
d_{2}\geq 2^{k-j}p^{m-1}
:

\displaystyle p^{d}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{k-j}p^{m-1}

\displaystyle p^{d-m+1}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{k-j}

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\geq 2^{\frac{k-j}{d-m+1}}.

Since
d\geq m-1
, we have
d-m+1\geq 0
. If
d>m-1
, then this leads to
p\geq 2^{k-j}>2^{j}
, contradicting
p<2^{j+1}
. If
d=m-1
, then
p^{m}\geq 2^{k-j}p^{m-1}
gives
p\geq 2^{k-j}
, again leading to contradiction.
∎

2.3
2
-Adic Valuations and Additional Restrictions

We now examine how large powers of 2 divide various quantities related to
n
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then
d_{2}\neq 2^{k-j}p^{m-1}
.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
d_{2}=2^{k-j}p^{m-1}
. Then
d_{1}=2^{k-j}p^{m}
by Lemma
6
. From Lemma
3
:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{m}+2^{k-j}p^{m}+2^{k-j}p^{m-1}

\displaystyle=2^{k-j}p^{m-1}(2^{j+1}p+p+1).

Since
m
is odd by Proposition
6
, we have
(1+p)|(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})
. Since
\gcd(p,1+p+\ldots+p^{m})=1
:

\displaystyle(1+p)

\displaystyle|2^{k-j}(2^{j+1}p+p+1)

\displaystyle(1+p)

\displaystyle|2^{k+1}p.

This implies
(1+p)|2^{k+1}
, so
1+p
is a power of 2. Since
2^{j}<p<2^{j+1}
, we have
1+p=2^{j+1}
.

Substituting back:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})

\displaystyle=2^{k-j}p^{m-1}(2^{2(j+1)})

\displaystyle=2^{k+j+2}p^{m-1}

This gives
(2^{k+1}-1)|p^{m-1}
, so
2^{k+1}-1\leq p^{m-1}
. Combined with Proposition
5
, we get
p^{m-1}=2^{k+1}-1
.

Since
m
is odd,
m-1
is even, so
p^{m-1}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. But
2^{k+1}-1\equiv 3\pmod{4}
for
k\geq 1
, giving a contradiction. ∎

Proposition 8
.

Let
n=2^{k}p^{m}
be
2
-near perfect with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}\geq p^{2}+1
. Then
v_{2}(m+1)+v_{2}(p+1)-1=\min[a,c]
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3
, we need to compare
v_{2}(1+p+\ldots+p^{m})
with
v_{2}(2^{k+1}p^{m}+d_{1}+d_{2})
.

For the left side, using
1+p+\ldots+p^{m}=\frac{p^{m+1}-1}{p-1}
and writing
m+1=2^{x}y
where
y
is odd:

\displaystyle v_{2}\left(\frac{p^{m+1}-1}{p-1}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{2}\left(\frac{p^{2^{x}}-1}{p-1}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{2}((p^{2^{x-1}}+1)(p^{2^{x-2}}+1)\cdots(p+1))

\displaystyle=(x-1)+v_{2}(p+1)

\displaystyle=v_{2}(m+1)+v_{2}(p+1)-1.

1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1}\mid 1+d_{1}+d_{2}=1+2^{a}p^{b}+2^{c}p^{m}.

We can simplify this relation:

\displaystyle\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}

\displaystyle\mid 1+2^{a}p^{b}+2^{c}p^{m}

\displaystyle\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}

\displaystyle\mid 1+2^{a}p^{b}+2^{c}p^{m}-2^{c}(p^{m}-1)

\displaystyle\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}

\displaystyle\mid 1+2^{a}p^{b}+2^{c}.

This implies the inequality
1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1}\leq 1+2^{a}p^{b}+2^{c}
.

First, consider the case where
b=0
. The inequality becomes:

\displaystyle 1+p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2^{a}+2^{c}

\displaystyle p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a}+2^{c}\leq 2^{k}+2^{k}=2^{k+1}.

Since
m\geq 3
, we have
p+p^{2}\leq p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{m-1}
. Combining this with the hypothesis
2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
:

p+p^{2}\leq 2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1.

This simplifies to
p<1
, which is impossible for a prime
p
. Thus,
b\neq 0
, and we must have
b=1
.

Now, with
b=1
, the inequality is
1+p+\ldots+p^{m-1}\leq 1+2^{a}p+2^{c}
. This leads to:

\displaystyle p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a}p+2^{c}\leq 2^{k}p+2^{k}=2^{k}(p+1)

\displaystyle\frac{p(p^{m-1}-1)}{p-1}

\displaystyle<\frac{p^{2}+1}{2}(p+1)

\displaystyle 2p(p^{m-1}-1)

\displaystyle<(p-1)(p^{2}+1)(p+1)=(p-1)(p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1)

\displaystyle 2p^{m}-2p

\displaystyle<p^{4}-1.

If
m\geq 4
, then
2p^{4}-2p\leq 2p^{m}-2p<p^{4}-1
, which implies
p^{4}<2p-1
. This is impossible for any prime
p\geq 3
. Therefore,
m
must be less than 4. Since
m\geq 3
, we must have
m=3
.

We now have
n=2^{k}p^{3}
, with omitted divisors
d_{1}=2^{a}p
and
d_{2}=2^{c}p^{3}
. From Lemma
3
:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+p^{2}+p^{3})

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}p^{3}+2^{a}p+2^{c}p^{3}

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-1)(p+1)(p^{2}+1)

\displaystyle=p(2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{2}).

Since
\gcd(p,p^{2}+1)=1
, it follows that
p^{2}+1\mid p(2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{2})
, and thus
p^{2}+1\mid 2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{2}
. This allows us to write:

\displaystyle p^{2}+1

\displaystyle\mid(2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{2})-(2^{k+1}+2^{c})(p^{2}+1)

\displaystyle p^{2}+1

\displaystyle\mid(2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{2})-(2^{k+1}p^{2}+2^{k+1}+2^{c}p^{2}+2^{c})

and thus

p^{2}+1\mid 2^{a}-2^{k+1}-2^{c}.

(3)

Thus, Equation
3
implies that
p^{2}+1\mid|2^{a}-2^{k+1}-2^{c}|=2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}
. If
c\leq a
, then
2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}\leq 2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
. For divisibility to hold, the only possibility is
2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}=0
, which is impossible. Therefore, we must have
c>a
.

Since
m=3
is odd, by Lemma
5
,
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
must have the same parity. Since their exponents of 2,
a
and
c
, are different, they cannot both be odd. Thus, they are both even, which means
a\geq 1
and
c\geq 1
. As
c>a
, we have
c\geq 2
.

From the divisibility
p^{2}+1\mid 2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}
, let
x(p^{2}+1)=2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}
for some positive integer
x
. We can bound

:

x(p^{2}+1)=2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}<2^{k+1}+2^{c}\leq 2^{k+1}+2^{k}=3\cdot 2^{k}.

And since
2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
, we have
2^{k}<(p^{2}+1)/2
.

x(p^{2}+1)<3\cdot\frac{p^{2}+1}{2}\implies x<1.5.

As
x
is a positive integer,
x=1
.
So,
p^{2}+1=2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}
. Considering this equation modulo 4:

\displaystyle p^{2}+1

\displaystyle\equiv 2^{k+1}+2^{c}-2^{a}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle 1+1

\displaystyle\equiv 0+2^{c}-2^{a}\pmod{4}\quad(\text{since }k\geq 1\text{ and }c\geq 2)

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\equiv-2^{a}\pmod{4}.

For this congruence to hold with
a\geq 1
, we must have
a=1
which contradicts the fact that
c>a
.

The assumption that
2^{c}p^{m}
is an omitted divisor leads to a contradiction. The result follows.
∎

Proposition 17
.

If
n=2^{k}p^{m}
is a
2
-near perfect number with
m\geq 3
and
2^{k+1}<p^{2}+1
, then neither omitted divisor can be a power of 2.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that one of the omitted divisors is a power of 2. Let this divisor be
d_{1}=2^{a}
for some
0\leq a\leq k
, and let the other be
d_{2}=2^{c}p^{d}
.

First, we establish a bound on
p
. Using the equivalent form from Lemma
4
, we have
(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})=1+d_{1}+d_{2}
. If we assume

:

\displaystyle(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})

\displaystyle>(2^{k+1}-2^{k})(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})

\displaystyle 2^{k}(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})

\displaystyle<1+d_{1}+d_{2}=1+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{d}.

Since the divisors must be distinct,
d_{2}
cannot also be a power of two, so

. From Proposition 5, we know

is not of the form
2^{c}p^{m}
, so

.

\displaystyle 2^{k}(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})

\displaystyle<1+2^{k}+2^{k}p^{m-1}

\displaystyle 2^{k}+2^{k}p+\dots+2^{k}p^{m-1}

\displaystyle<1+2^{k}+2^{k}p^{m-1}

\displaystyle 2^{k}p+\dots+2^{k}p^{m-2}

\displaystyle<1.

This is a contradiction for any prime
p
and
k\geq 1
. Therefore, we must have
p>2^{k}
.

Next, from the equation in Lemma
3
, we take the congruence modulo
p
:

(2^{k+1}-1)\sigma(p^{m})\equiv 2n+d_{1}+d_{2}\pmod{p}\implies 2^{k+1}-1\equiv 2^{a}\pmod{p}.

This means
p
divides
2^{k+1}-1-2^{a}
. Let
x
be the integer such that
xp=2^{k+1}-1-2^{a}
. Given that
p>2^{k}
, if we assume
x\geq 2
, then
2p>2^{k+1}
, which leads to the contradiction:

2^{k+1}<2p\leq xp=2^{k+1}-1-2^{a}<2^{k+1}.

Thus,
x
must be 1, and we have the exact relation
p=2^{k+1}-1-2^{a}
.

With these facts established, we now analyze the situation by splitting it into two cases.

Subcase 1: The omitted divisor is
2^{a}
with
a>0
.

From Lemma
4
and the fact that
2^{k+1}-p=1+2^{a}
, we have:

(1+2^{a})(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})=1+2^{a}+2^{c}p^{d}.

This simplifies to
(1+2^{a})(1+p+\dots+p^{m-2})=2^{c}p^{d-1}
, which implies that
1+2^{a}\mid 2^{c}p^{d-1}
. We analyze the greatest common divisor of
1+2^{a}
and
p
:

\gcd(1+2^{a},p)=\gcd(1+2^{a},2^{k+1}-1-2^{a})=\gcd(1+2^{a},2^{k+1}).

Since
a>0
, the term
1+2^{a}
is an odd integer greater than 1. Its gcd with any power of 2 must be 1. Thus,
\gcd(1+2^{a},p)=1
.

From the divisibility condition
1+2^{a}\mid 2^{c}p^{d-1}
and
\gcd(1+2^{a},p)=1
, it must be that
1+2^{a}\mid 2^{c}
. Since
1+2^{a}
is odd, this is only possible if
1+2^{a}=1
, which means
2^{a}=0
. This is impossible for any integer
a
, and contradicts our case assumption that
a>0
. Thus, the case
a>0
is impossible.

Subcase 2: The omitted divisor is
2^{0}=1
(i.e.,
a=0
).

If
a=0
, the equation for
p
becomes:

p=2^{k+1}-1-1=2^{k+1}-2=2(2^{k}-1).

Since
p
is an odd prime, this equation presents an immediate contradiction, as the right-hand side is an even number. Alternatively, for the equation to hold,
p
must divide
2^{k}-1
, which implies
p\leq 2^{k}-1
. This directly contradicts our established fact that
p>2^{k}
.

Thus, the case
a=0
is also impossible.

Since both possible cases (
a>0
and
a=0
) lead to contradictions, the initial supposition is false. Therefore, neither omitted divisor can be a power of 2.
∎

Proposition 18

If

Let

d_{2}=2^{c}p^{d}

Our first step is to determine the exponents of

•

If

Proposition 19
.

If
n=2^{k}p^{m}

4
2-deficient perfect numbers

Definition 1
.

A positive integer
n
is called
2-deficient perfect
if there exist two distinct positive divisors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
of
n
such that:

\sigma(n)=2n-d_{1}-d_{2},

where
\sigma(n)
denotes the sum of all positive divisors of
n
.

For this analysis, the preliminary lemmas from the introduction apply with a sign change. Specifically, Lemma
4
becomes
(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})=1-d_{1}-d_{2}
.

Proposition 21
.

If
n=2^{k}p^{m}
is a 2-deficient perfect number, then

(a)

The prime
p
must satisfy
p>2^{k+1}
.

(b)

At least one of the omitted divisors,
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
, must be a power of 2.

Proof.

(a) From the modified Lemma
4
, we have
(2^{k+1}-p)(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})=1-d_{1}-d_{2}
. Since
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are distinct positive divisors,
1-d_{1}-d_{2}
is a negative integer. The sum
(1+p+\dots+p^{m-1})
is positive. Therefore, the term
(2^{k+1}-p)
must be negative. As it is an integer,
2^{k+1}-p\leq-1
, which implies
p\geq 2^{k+1}+1
. Thus,
p>2^{k+1}
.

(b) Suppose for contradiction that
p
divides both
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Then
p
also divides their sum,
d_{1}+d_{2}
. From the definition
\sigma(n)=2n-(d_{1}+d_{2})
, it follows that
d_{1}+d_{2}=2n-\sigma(n)
. We have:

d_{1}+d_{2}=2\cdot 2^{k}p^{m}-\sigma(2^{k})\sigma(p^{m})=2^{k+1}p^{m}-(2^{k+1}-1)(1+p+\dots+p^{m}).

Taking this modulo
p
, we get
d_{1}+d_{2}\equiv-(2^{k+1}-1)(1)\pmod{p}
. Since
p\mid d_{1}+d_{2}
, it must be that
p\mid-(2^{k+1}-1)
, which means
p\leq 2^{k+1}-1
. This contradicts part (a), which showed
p>2^{k+1}
. Thus, the assumption is false, and at least one divisor is not divisible by
p
, meaning it must be a power of 2.
∎

Theorem 5
.

An integer
n=2^{k}p^{m}
with

an odd prime is 2-deficient perfect if and only if
m=1
and
p
is a prime of the form

We prove the theorem by considering the cases
m=1
and
m\geq 2
separately.

. Let

. The omitted divisors
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are divisors of

. By Proposition

, at least one divisor is a power of 2; let

. The other divisor

or

.

Suppose

. The modified Lemma

2^{k+1}-p=1-2^{a}-2^{c}p\implies p(2^{c}-1)=2^{k+1}-1-2^{a}.

,

. Since

. This means the left side,

. This is a contradiction.

must also be a power of 2, say

Since the divisors must be distinct,

. We will show that no solutions exist in this case. By Proposition

, one divisor must be a power of 2,
d_{1}=2^{a}
. The other is
d_{2}=2^{c}p^{d}
.
We first show

(i.e.,

p\mid p-2^{k+1}
, implying
p\mid 2^{k+1}
, which is impossible. So
d_{2}
must be a power of 2,
d_{2}=2^{c}
. But then an odd integer greater than 1,
(p-2^{k+1})
, divides a power of 2, which is impossible. Thus,
a>0
.

Now, we use the divisibility argument:
1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}\mid d_{1}+d_{2}-1=2^{a}+2^{c}p^{d}-1
. From
p>2^{k+1}
, we get
2^{k}<p/2
.

1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}\leq 2^{a}+2^{c}p^{d}-1\leq 2^{k}+2^{k}p^{d}-1<\frac{p}{2}+\frac{p}{2}p^{d}-1=\frac{p(1+p^{d})}{2}-1.

The leading term on the left is
p^{m-1}
and on the right is
p^{d+1}/2
. So,
p^{m-1}<p^{d+1}/2
.
If
d\leq m-2
, this gives
p^{m-1}<p^{m-1}/2
, a contradiction.
Therefore, the only possibility is
d=m-1
.

With
d=m-1
, we have
1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}\mid 2^{a}+2^{c}p^{m-1}-1
.
This implies
1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}
also divides
(2^{a}+2^{c}p^{m-1}-1)p=2^{a}p+2^{c}p^{m}-p
.
We also know
p^{m}\equiv 1\pmod{1+...+p^{m-1}}
. Thus:

1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}\mid 2^{a}p+2^{c}(1)-p=(2^{a}-1)p+2^{c}.

So,
1+p+\dots+p^{m-1}\leq(2^{a}-1)p+2^{c}\leq(2^{k}-1)p+2^{k}<(\frac{p}{2}-1)p+\frac{p}{2}=\frac{p^{2}}{2}-\frac{p}{2}
.
The leading term on the left,
p^{m-1}
, must be less than
\frac{p^{2}}{2}
. This implies
m-1<2
, so
m<3
.
Since we assumed
m\geq 2
, the only possibility is
m=2
.

Finally, we test
m=2
. The divisors are
d_{1}=2^{a}
and
d_{2}=2^{c}p
. Since
m=2
is even, by Lemma
5
,
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
must have opposite parity. As we showed
a>0
,
d_{1}=2^{a}
must be even. Thus
d_{2}=2^{c}p
must be odd, which implies
c=0
and
d_{2}=p
.
The divisibility becomes
1+p\mid d_{1}+d_{2}-1=2^{a}+p-1
. This implies
1+p\mid(2^{a}+p-1)-(p+1)=2^{a}-2
.
So,
p+1\leq 2^{a}-2
. But
p>2^{k+1}
and
a\leq k
.

2^{k+1}+1<p+1\leq 2^{a}-2\leq 2^{k}-2.

This gives the contradiction
2^{k+1}<2^{k}
. Thus, no solution exists for
m=2
.

Since no solutions exist for
m\geq 2
, the theorem is proven.
∎

5
Future Work

The complete characterization of
2
-near perfect numbers with two prime factors opens several potential paths for further investigation. Below are some results, that, while mostly computational, could open up future exploration in this field.

5.1
Computational Results

Below is the C++ code we used to search for
2
-near perfect numbers. The program implements arithmetic operations for handling large numbers up to
2^{64}
. The core of the program uses safe multiplication with overflow detection, making use of 128-bit integers where available:

uint64_t safe_multiply(uint64_t a, uint64_t b) {
// Early exit for 0 to avoid unnecessary __int128 calculation
if (a == 0 || b == 0) return 0;
// Check if multiplication would obviously overflow based on leading bits
// Simple check: if a > max/b roughly
if (b > 0 && a > numeric_limits<uint64_t>::max() / b) return 0;

// Use 128-bit integer if available for a precise check
#ifdef __SIZEOF_INT128__ // Check if the compiler supports 128-bit integers
unsigned __int128 result = (unsigned __int128)a * b;
if (result > numeric_limits<uint64_t>::max()) {
return 0; // Indicate overflow
}
return (uint64_t)result;
#else
// Fallback if 128-bit integers aren’t available (less precise)
// Already checked a > max/b above, which is a decent heuristic
return a * b;
#endif
}

The program tests numbers of the form
2^{k}p^{m}
by computing divisor sums and checking if
2
-near perfect property. It includes optimizations for handling large exponents and prime powers, with bounds checking to prevent integer overflow. The implementation also supports testing hybrid forms like adding one omitted divisor and subtracting another and includes a command-line interface for exploring different number patterns. Results are then outputted into the CSV format.

5.4
Hybrid Forms

Beyond the standard definition, we identified several variants for future research:

1.

Hybrid
2
-near Perfect Numbers:
Numbers where one divisor is added and one is subtracted, satisfying
\sigma(n)=2n+d_{1}-d_{2}
. Our initial computations suggest these might be more numerous than standard
2
-near perfect numbers.

2.

2-Deficient Perfect Numbers:
Numbers where both divisors are subtracted, satisfying
\sigma(n)=2n-d_{1}-d_{2}
.
