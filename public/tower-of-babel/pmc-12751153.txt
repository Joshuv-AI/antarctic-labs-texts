Morphine use and clinical outcomes in acute pancreatitis: a tertiary-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Abdulrahman Ibn Awadh, Renad Meshal Alharbi, Mohannad Abuobaid, Musthafa Chalikandy Peedikayil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe safety of morphine in acute pancreatitis (AP) remains controversial due to theoretical risks of exacerbating complications. This study evaluated whether early morphine use correlates with clinical deterioration in AP.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 154 patients with AP (diagnosed per Revised Atlanta Criteria) treated at a tertiary center (2012–2024) were stratified by morphine use within 48 h of admission. The primary outcome was a composite of clinical deterioration (30-day mortality, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, surgery, prolonged hospitalization, CRP doubling, or new organ failure). Associations were assessed using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, admission severity, and inflammatory markers.ResultsOf 154 patients, 53 received early morphine. Baseline age and sex distributions were similar between groups, but morphine recipients had lower mechanical ventilation rates (17.0% vs. 35.6%, p = 0.02) and higher hemoglobin (131.0 vs. 122.0 g/L, p = 0.01). Clinical deterioration occurred in 49.1% (morphine) vs. 62.4% (non-morphine), with no significant difference (adjusted OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.29–1.28, p = 0.19). High-dose opioids (MME ≥ 100, n = 24) showed no association with deterioration (adjusted OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.33–2.54, p = 0.87). Severity-adjusted analyses confirmed these findings.ConclusionsIn this cohort, early morphine and opioid use were not associated with clinical deterioration in acute pancreatitis. These findings support the safety of early opioid analgesia for pain control in AP, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. Prospective studies are needed to confirm causality and inform guideline recommendations.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Morphine, Clinical deterioration, Opioid safety, Retrospective cohort study, Analgesia, Opioid

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a leading gastrointestinal cause of hospitalization worldwide, characterized by significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden [1–3]. Effective pain management remains a cornerstone of AP treatment, yet the optimal analgesic strategy remains debated [4]. Opioids, particularly morphine, are widely used for pain relief but have historically raised concerns due to theoretical risks of exacerbating pancreatic inflammation, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, or systemic complications [5].

Prior studies on opioid safety in AP have yielded conflicting results, with some suggesting potential harm and others reporting no adverse effects [6–10]. This ambiguity underscores a critical knowledge gap: whether early morphine administration influences clinical trajectories, including organ failure, prolonged hospitalization, or mortality [10–12]. Addressing this question is essential to balance adequate pain control with theoretical safety concerns, particularly in a condition where uncontrolled pain may itself drive complications.

In this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the association between early morphine use (within 48 h of admission) and clinical deterioration in patients with AP. Leveraging two decades of data from a tertiary care center, we aimed to clarify whether opioid analgesia, specifically morphine, correlates with adverse outcomes. We hypothesized that early morphine administration would not be associated with clinical deterioration in patients with AP.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis (AP) at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre (KFSHRC), a tertiary care center, between 2012 and 2024. Participants were identified through electronic health records and required a contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan or follow-up CT within one year of diagnosis.

Participants

AP diagnosis required meeting two of three criteria: (1) characteristic epigastric pain radiating to the back, (2) serum amylase/lipase > 3× the upper limit of normal, or (3) imaging findings consistent with AP. Exclusion criteria included incomplete records, chronic pancreatitis, or age < 14 years. All consecutive eligible patients presenting between 2012 and 2024 were included. No formal sample size calculation was performed a priori.

Diagnostic and severity criteria

Disease severity was classified using the revised Atlanta classification

Mild AP: No organ failure or local/systemic complications.

Mild AP: No organ failure or local/systemic complications.

Moderately Severe AP: Transient organ failure (< 48 h), local complications, or exacerbation of comorbidities.

Moderately Severe AP: Transient organ failure (< 48 h), local complications, or exacerbation of comorbidities.

Severe AP: Persistent organ failure (> 48 h).

Severe AP: Persistent organ failure (> 48 h).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome, clinical deterioration, was defined as meeting ≥ 1 of the following: 30-day mortality, invasive mechanical ventilation, vasopressor requirement, emergent abdominal surgery, hospitalization > 15 days, doubling of C-reactive protein (CRP), or new-onset organ failure.

Data collection

Demographics, laboratory values (CRP, amylase, lipase), imaging findings, treatments (opioid use, morphine dose, paracetamol), and outcomes were extracted by trained reviewers. Morphine Milligram Equivalents (MME) were calculated using standard CDC conversion factors (e.g., oxycodone ×1.5, hydromorphone ×4, fentanyl patch ×2.4 per mg oral morphine equivalent).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS v28 and Jamovi v2.3. Continuous variables (median [IQR]) were compared via Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; categorical variables (n [%]) via chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Continuous covariates, including age, C-reactive protein (CRP), and total morphine milligram equivalents (MME), were modeled as linear continuous variables after verifying the assumption of linearity in the logit using graphical inspection. Missing data, primarily for CRP, were handled using complete-case analysis, which excluded 28 cases (18% of the cohort) from adjusted models. Logistic regression models assessed associations between pain management variables (morphine, opioids, MME, paracetamol) and deterioration, adjusting for age, sex, admission severity, and CRP. Missing data (> 20% for albumin/urea) were excluded; complete-case analysis was applied for adjusted models. A two-sided α = 0.05 defined significance.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre (IRB# 2231257). A waiver of informed consent was granted due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Of 154 eligible patients with AP, 53 (34.4%) received early morphine within 48 h of admission, and 101 (65.6%) did not. The median age did not differ significantly between the morphine and non-morphine groups (50.0 [32.0–62.3] vs. 54.0 [38.0–64.3] years, p = 0.23), and the proportion of males was similar (52.8% vs. 56.4%, p = 0.67). Disease severity at presentation differed significantly (p = 0.01): moderate acute pancreatitis was more frequent among morphine recipients (11.3% vs. 1.0%), whereas mild and severe cases were comparably distributed between groups (79.2% vs. 82.2% and 9.4% vs. 16.8%, respectively).

Etiologic categories, including gallstone (26.4% vs. 21.0%), idiopathic (22.6% vs. 27.7%), malignancy (5.7% vs. 11.9%), alcohol (7.5% vs. 5.0%), and other causes (37.7% vs. 34.7%)—showed no significant differences (p = 0.58).

Laboratory parameters were broadly comparable, although patients who received morphine had higher hemoglobin levels at diagnosis (131.0 [111.2–146.8] g/L vs. 122.0 [102.0–137.3] g/L, p = 0.01). White blood cell count (10.7 [7.4–13.1] vs. 9.5 [6.4–13.6] × 10⁹/L, p = 0.34) and CRP (21.5 [8.3–90.1] vs. 30.7 [7.2–138.5] mg/L, p = 0.59) did not differ significantly.

Clinical outcomes were generally comparable between groups. There was no statistically significant difference in clinical deterioration between morphine and non-morphine groups (49.1% vs. 62.4%, p = 0.11; Table 1). At baseline, invasive mechanical ventilation was less common among patients who later received morphine (17.0% vs. 35.6%, p = 0.02), and ICU admission was numerically lower but not statistically significant (18.9% vs. 31.7%, p = 0.08). Median hospital stay was 9.0 (6.0–14.7) days in the morphine group and 12.0 (5.0–25.0) days in the non-morphine group (p = 0.23). The composite outcome of clinical deterioration occurred in 49.1% of patients receiving morphine compared with 62.4% of those who did not (p = 0.11).

Regression analysis

In univariate analyses (Table 2), invasive mechanical ventilation was significantly less common among patients who received morphine (unadjusted OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.16–0.84, p = 0.02), indicating that morphine was more frequently administered to patients not requiring ventilatory support. Other baseline factors, including age, sex, etiology, WBC, CRP, and ICU admission, were not significantly associated with morphine use.

After adjustment for potential confounders: age, sex, admission severity and CRP, the association between morphine use and mechanical ventilation was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (adjusted OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.17–1.03, p = 0.06). None of the other variables showed significant independent associations with morphine administration. These findings suggest that early morphine use was not independently predicted by demographic or biochemical factors but may have been influenced by clinical judgment and perceived illness severity at presentation.

In logistic regression analyses (Table 3), neither early morphine use nor overall opioid exposure within 48 h of admission was associated with an increased risk of clinical deterioration. In univariate models, morphine use was associated with a non-significant lower odds of deterioration in univariate analysis (unadjusted OR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.30–1.13, p = 0.11). After adjustment for age, sex, admission severity and CRP, early morphine use remained unassociated with clinical deterioration (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.29–1.28, p = 0.19).

Similarly, total opioid use and high-dose exposure (MME ≥ 100) were not significantly related to clinical deterioration in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses (adjusted OR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.28–1.25, p = 0.17, and adjusted OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.33–2.54, p = 0.87, respectively). Overall, these results indicate that neither early administration of morphine nor higher cumulative opioid dosing was associated with worsened outcomes in acute pancreatitis after controlling for confounding clinical factors.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 154 patients with acute pancreatitis, no significant association was found between early morphine use, opioid exposure (including high-dose MME ≥ 100), or paracetamol administration and clinical deterioration. These results mitigate long-standing concerns about morphine-induced worsening in AP.

The possibility of confounding by indication must be considered when interpreting our results. The observed lower baseline rate of mechanical ventilation in the morphine group (17.0% vs. 35.6%) suggests that clinicians may have selectively administered morphine to patients perceived as less critically ill. This selection bias could mean that the morphine group was inherently at a lower risk for deterioration at baseline. While our multivariable models adjusted for major severity indicators, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, the lack of association between morphine and clinical deterioration may partly reflect this initial risk profile. This underscores the need for future studies to employ methods like propensity score matching in larger cohorts to better account for these baseline differences.

When contextualized with the recent large, international cohort study by Pandanaboyana et al. (2024), our findings offer a complementary perspective on the timing of opioid exposure [5]. Their study found that opioid administration after the admission day or for a prolonged duration (≥ 6 days) was independently associated with an increased risk of moderately severe or severe acute pancreatitis, whereas opioids given on the admission day were not. Our study, focusing specifically on early analgesic exposure, aligns with this temporal distinction. We found no significant association between morphine or opioid use within the first 48 h of admission and clinical deterioration. This convergence suggests that the potential risk of opioids may be more closely linked to sustained use in a deteriorating patient (confounding by indication) rather than a direct harmful effect of early, short-term administration. Our results, therefore, reinforce the notion that early opioid analgesia was not associated with increased harm in initial pain management strategies.

Similarly, a systematic review by Basurto Ona et al. (13) found no difference in complications between opioids and alternative analgesics in AP, and Moggia et al. [14] reported no adverse effects of morphine on disease progression. Our findings reinforce these conclusions, as even high-dose opioid exposure (MME ≥ 100) showed no association with deterioration [15].

While our study is smaller than recent multi-center cohorts, its strength lies in providing detailed, severity-adjusted analyses from a distinct single-center population. Strengths also include comprehensive case ascertainment, uniform diagnostic criteria, and inclusion of all eligible patients over a 12-year study period. A small sample size, especially in the high-dose group (MME ≥ 100, n = 24), lowers statistical power and might have led to wide confidence intervals. Nonetheless, the consistent absence of a signal toward harm supports the growing consensus that early morphine use does not appear to exacerbate clinical outcomes in acute pancreatitis. Our findings complement larger datasets by offering granular insight into early analgesic exposure within a well-characterized local cohort.

Our analysis lacked the use of standardized pain assessment data. As a retrospective study, pain intensity was not consistently recorded, precluding quantitative correlation between pain severity, analgesic dosing, and outcomes. This introduces potential confounding by indication, as morphine may have been preferentially prescribed to patients experiencing greater pain. Nevertheless, our findings provide valuable real-world insight into analgesic safety under routine clinical conditions.

The single-center design and homogeneous population (100% Saudi) limit the external generalizability of our findings. However, this uniformity also minimizes variability in practice patterns. Given that data from Middle Eastern populations are underrepresented in the literature, our findings add important regional evidence. Future multi-center studies across diverse healthcare systems are needed to confirm the reproducibility of these results.

The composite primary outcome of ‘clinical deterioration’ was designed to reflect the multifactorial progression of acute pancreatitis. We recognize that CRP doubling is not equivalent in clinical significance to organ failure or mortality. Therefore, the composite should be viewed as an overall indicator of disease worsening. Importantly, no adverse association with morphine use was observed, suggesting that the overall conclusion of no increased risk is robust.

The lack of harm associated with paracetamol in our cohort is consistent with guidelines recommending it as a first-line analgesic [7, 16, 17]. However, the 2020 American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guidelines highlight limited high-quality evidence for opioid safety, underscoring the need for further research [7]. Our study addresses this gap by providing robust observational data adjusted for key confounders.

Contribution to clinical practice

The absence of increased deterioration risk with morphine or opioids suggests that pain management in AP should prioritize adequate analgesia without undue hesitation. However, it is critical to distinguish between a lack of harm and a proven benefit. Our findings are particularly notable because morphine was preferentially used in moderately severe cases, the population most susceptible to progression. The fact that we found no increased risk of deterioration in this higher-risk group provides reassuring evidence against historical concerns about opioid-induced pancreatic complications.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design introduces potential unmeasured confounders, such as pain severity or provider prescribing biases. Second, the homogeneity of the cohort limits generalizability. Third, missing CRP data and the small high-dose opioid subgroup reduced statistical power, meaning a modest effect cannot be excluded. Fourth, pain severity and adequacy of analgesia could not be evaluated. Finally, the composite outcome combined heterogeneous components, which may dilute associations with individual endpoints.

Conclusion

In this cohort, early morphine and opioid use were not associated with clinical deterioration in acute pancreatitis. These findings support the safety of early opioid analgesia for pain control in AP, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. Prospective studies are needed to confirm causality and inform guideline recommendations.
