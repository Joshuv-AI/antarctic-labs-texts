A Gap Between the Gaussian RKHS and Neural Networks: An Infinite-Center Asymptotic Analysis
Akash Kumar, Rahul Parhi, Mikhail Belkin

Abstract
Recent works have characterized the function-space inductive bias of infinite-width bounded-norm single-hidden-layer neural networks as a kind of bounded-variation-type space. This novel neural network Banach space encompasses many classical multivariate function spaces, including certain Sobolev spaces and the spectral Barron spaces. Notably, this Banach space also includes functions that exhibit less classical regularity, such as those that only vary in a few directions. On bounded domains, it is well-established that the Gaussian reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) strictly embeds into this Banach space, demonstrating a clear gap between the Gaussian RKHS and the neural network Banach space. It turns out that when investigating these spaces on unbounded domains, e.g., all of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , the story is fundamentally different. We establish the following fundamental result: Certain functions that lie in the Gaussian RKHS have infinite norm in the neural network Banach space. This provides a nontrivial gap between kernel methods and neural networks by exhibiting functions that kernel methods easily represent, whereas neural networks cannot.

Introduction

In supervised learning, we observe samples with corresponding labels, which may represent classes or continuous values. Our primary objective is to construct a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
based on these observations that can accurately predict labels for new, unseen data points. Traditionally, reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) have provided a principled framework for this task, offering both theoretical guarantees and practical algorithms. Their power stems from the representer theorem, which ensures that optimal solutions can be expressed as combinations of kernel functions centered at the training points.

However, the landscape of machine learning has evolved significantly with the emergence of neural networks, which have demonstrated remarkable success across diverse applications over kernel methods. The simplest neural architecture—the single-hidden layer network—builds upon the concept of ridge functions, which map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
via the form
\bm{x}\mapsto\sigma(\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{x})
, where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a univariate function and
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. In practice, these networks combine multiple ridge functions:

\bm{x}\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}\,\sigma(\bm{w}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}-b_{k}),

(1)

where
K
represents the network width,
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
are weights, and
b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
are biases. While RKHS methods suffer from the curse of dimensionality, neural networks can overcome it by learning effective low-dimensional representations
(
Ghorbani et al., 2021b
;
von Luxburg and Bousquet, 2004
)
.

A fundamental question is to compare the approximation capabilities of neural networks with those of RKHS corresponding to different kernels. For example,
Mei et al. (2016)
showed that if the target function is a single neuron, neural networks can learn efficiently using roughly
d\log d
samples, whereas the corresponding RKHS requires a sample size that grows polynomially in the dimension
d
(see also
Yehudai and Shamir (2019)
;
Ghorbani et al. (2019)
).

Recent work
(
Parhi and Nowak, 2021
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2023a
)
has studied the Banach-space optimality of single-hidden-layer (shallow ReLU) networks over both bounded and unbounded domains
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There, the authors established a representer theorem which demonstrates that solutions to data-fitting problems in these networks naturally reside in a kind of bounded variation space, referred to as the second-order Radon bounded variation space
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
. These spaces, in turn, contain several classical multivariate function spaces, including certain Sobolev spaces as well as certain spectral Barron spaces
(
Barron, 1993
)
. For instance,
Parhi and Nowak (2023a)
have shown that the Sobolev space
H^{d+1}(\Omega)
embeds into
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
for any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, on any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Gaussian reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\textsf{Gauss}}(\Omega)
is known to embed into the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s>0
(see Corollary 4.36 of
Steinwart and Christmann (2008)
). This observation appears to highlight limitations of Gaussian kernel machines when compared to neural networks on
bounded domains
. Consequently, a natural question arises.

Are Gaussian kernel machines restrictive in approximating general functions?

Conversely, one may also ask the following question.

To what extent can we approximate functions using shallow neural networks?

To that end,
Ghorbani et al. (2021a)
demonstrated that the gap between neural network approximations and kernel methods can be narrowed when the intrinsic dimensionality of the target function is well captured by the covariates of the data. In this paper, we take a different perspective: While the Gaussian RKHS may seem rather limited in a bounded domain, we show that on unbounded domains, in particular, on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with fixed dimension
d
, there exist functions in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\textsf{Gauss}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with unbounded
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
-norm.

The key idea behind our analysis is that, in the regime of kernel machines with infinite centers on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist functions of the form

f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot)
with bounded RKHS norm, but the infinite sequence
\{\alpha_{i}\}
has an
unbounded

\ell_{1}
-norm (see Example
5
in Section
3
). This fact can be exploited to design a sequence of functions
\left\{f_{n}\right\}
whose
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
-norm is diverging as
n\to\infty
(see Theorem
9
in Section
5
). An important step in this study is we compute an explicit form for
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
-norm of a Gaussian kernel machine, and further simplify the form using well-known Hermite polynomials. This form provides an interpretable characterization of these kernel machines, which is of independent interest for future studies.

Related Work

Approximability with Kernel Methods

Bach (2017)
studied various classes of single-/multi-index models with low intrinsic dimension and bounded
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
-norm. In contrast,
Ghorbani et al. (2019)
showed that if the covariates have the same dimension as the low intrinsic dimension of the target function, kernel and neural network approximations can be competitive. Empirically, some works show that the curse of dimensionality with kernel methods can be handled with an appropriate choice of dataset-specific kernels
(
Arora et al., 2019
;
Novak et al., 2018
;
Shankar et al., 2020
)
or mirroring neural network training dynamics closely to kernel methods
(
Mei et al., 2018
;
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2020
;
Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden, 2022
;
Chizat and Bach, 2018
)
. Furthermore,
Petrini et al. (2023)
showed that compared to a network that learns sparse representations while the target function is constant or smooth along certain directions of the input space, lazy training (via random feature kernel or the NTK) yields better performance.
But a wide body of work has also shown a gap in approximation with neural networks capturing a richer and more nuanced class of functions compared to kernel methods (see
(
Allen-Zhu and Li, 2019
;
Mei et al., 2018
;
Yehudai and Shamir, 2019
;
Ghorbani et al., 2019
)
). In our work, we show that while Gaussian RKHS is embedded within neural networks in bounded domains, in the unbounded regime there exists a non-trivial gap between
{\mathcal{H}}^{\textsf{Gauss}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.

Function Spaces of Shallow Networks

The function space
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
naturally characterizes the function approximation and representation capabilities of shallow ReLU neural networks
(
Ongie et al., 2020
)
.
Parhi and Nowak (2021)
established a
representer theorem
, showing that solutions to variational problems over
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
correspond to single-hidden layer ReLU networks with weight decay regularization. Unlike RKHSs

\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
can efficiently represent functions with a low-dimensional structure. Moreover, neural networks trained with weight decay achieve near-minimax optimal estimation rates for functions in
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
, while kernel methods provably cannot
(
Parhi and Nowak, 2023a
)
. This suggests that on bounded domains, RKHSs are quite restrictive, while
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
provides a more expressive framework.
For further details see
(
Ongie et al., 2020
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2021
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2022
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2023a
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2023b
;
Bartolucci et al., 2023
;
Parhi and Unser, 2025
)

Embeddings of RKHSs and
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})

For any bounded Lipshitz domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is well-known that the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega)
is (equivalent to) an RKHS if and only if
s>d/2
. For example, the Laplace and Matérn kernels are associated with Sobolev RKHSs
(see, e.g.,
Kanagawa et al., 2018
, Example 2.6)
. In contrast,
Zhou (2003)
and
Steinwart and Christmann (cf., 2008, Corollary 4.36)
showed that the Gaussian RKHS
\mathcal{H}^{\text{Gauss}}(\Omega)
is contained in
\mathcal{H}^{\text{Gauss}}(\Omega)\subset H^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\geq 0
. Recent work has further demonstrated that the RKHSs of typical neural tangent kernel (NTK) and neural network Gaussian process (NNGP) kernels for the ReLU activation function are equivalent to the Sobolev spaces
H^{(d+1)/2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
and
H^{(d+3)/2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, respectively
(
Bietti and Bach, 2021
;
Chen and Xu, 2021
)
.
Steinwart et al. (2009)
has shown that an optimal learning rates in Sobolev RKHSs can be achieved by cross-validating the regularization parameter. On another front, embedding properties relating Sobolev spaces and the second-order Radond-domain bounded variation space has been explored. For example,
Ongie et al. (2020)
showed that
W^{d+1}(L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
embeds in
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. More recently,
Mao et al. (2024)
established a sharp bound by proving that
W^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
with
s\geq 2+(d+1)/2
for
p\geq 2
embeds in
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\Omega})
for bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Problem Setup and Preliminaries

3.1
Gaussian Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space

We begin by defining a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with a Gaussian kernel on an infinite domain. For a given positive definite Mahalanobis matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\text{Sym}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, we define the Gaussian kernel
k_{\mathbf{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

k_{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(2)

where
\sigma>0
is a fixed scale parameter and the Mahalanobis distance is defined as

\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}=(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}).

(3)

The corresponding RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the closure
1
1

1

With respect to the norm topology on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
of the linear span of kernel functions:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}:=\textsf{cl}\left({\left\{f:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}\,\bigg\lvert\,n\in\mathbb{N},f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\cdot k_{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot),\,\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}}\right),

(4)

where the (squared) RKHS norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}
of a kernel machine
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
is defined as
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{\bm{M}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
. Alternately, we can write
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}=\alpha^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}\alpha
where
\mathbf{K}=(k_{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}))_{i,j}
is an
n\times n
matrix.

3.2
Separated Sets and Function Spaces

For our analysis, we introduce two key definitions of separated sets that play a crucial role in our theoretical development.

Definition 1

(
(\bm{\beta},\delta)
-separated set)
.

For any given scalar
\delta>0
and a vector
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a
(\bm{\beta},\delta)
-separated subset of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(\bm{\beta},\delta):=\left\{\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\}\,\middle|\,\,\forall\,i,j,\,|\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{j}|\geq\delta\right\}.

(5)

This could be further generalized to the notion

Definition 2

(
(\bm{\beta},\delta,\eta)
-separated set)
.

For any given scalars
\delta,\eta>0
and a vector
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a
(\bm{\beta},\delta,\eta)
-separated subset of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(\bm{\beta},\delta,\eta):=\left\{\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\}\,\middle|\,\,\forall\,i,j,\bm{\beta^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\geq\eta\left\|\bm{\beta}\right\|\left\|\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\right\|,|\bm{\beta}^{\prime\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\beta}^{\prime\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{j}|\geq\delta\right\}.

(6)

Example 3
.

Let
\bm{\beta}=(1,0,\dots,0)
. For all
\eta_{0}\geq\eta
, pick
\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\;=\;\bigl(\eta_{0},\sqrt{1-\eta_{0}^{2}},0,\dots,0\bigr)

so that

\|\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\|=1\;\;\text{and}\;\;\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\;=\;\eta_{0}\geq\eta
. Now define

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}:=(i-1)\,\delta\,\bm{\beta}^{\prime},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(7)

For
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\bm{\beta}^{\prime\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\beta}^{\prime\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{j}\bigr|=|(i-j)|\,\delta\,\|\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\|^{2}=|i-j|\,\delta\,\geq\,\delta.

(8)

Hence,
\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\}
is in
(\bm{\beta},\delta,\eta)
-separated subset of size
n
.

Definition 4

(Unbounded Combinations)
.

For a kernel machine
f\in\mathcal{H}
with representation
f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot)
, we say the coefficient vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is unbounded with respect to
f
if
\|\alpha\|_{\ell_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\alpha_{i}|=\infty
.

Example 5
.

Consider a kernel machine
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
corresponding to the combination
\alpha=(a_{n})
defined by
a_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence of centers
(\bm{x}_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
i,j
,
\left\|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}\right\|\geq|i-j|\delta
for some fixed scalar
\delta>0
. For this construction, Gaussian RKHS norm
\alpha^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})<\infty
, but
\|\alpha\|_{\ell_{1}}
is unbounded.

We provide the proof of the statement in the example above in Appendix
D
.

3.3
Probabilist’s Hermite Polynomials

Probabilist’s Hermite polynomials
(
Szegő, 1975
)
, denoted by
\text{He}_{d}(z):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, are defined by the generating function

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(zt-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Bigr)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\text{He}_{d}(z)\frac{t^{d}}{d!},

(9)

and they are orthogonal with respect to the standard normal density

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{He}_{d}(z)\,\text{He}_{d^{\prime}}(z)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\,dz=d!\,\delta_{dd^{\prime}},

(10)

where
\delta_{dd^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta. We use the notation
H_{d}
to denote the polynomial unless stated otherwise.

3.4
Radon Transform and the Second-Order Radon-Domain Bounded Variation Space

Radon Transform

For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, its Radon transform

\mathcal{R}\{f\}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\{f\}(\bm{\beta},t)=\int_{\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}=t\}}f(\bm{x})\,ds(\bm{x}),

(11)

where
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
ds(\bm{x})
is the

(d-1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on the hyperplane

Radon Bounded Variation Space

We define the second-order Radon bounded variation space
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
as:

\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ is measurable}:\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f})<\infty,\\
&\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(\bm{x})|(1+\|\bm{x}\|)^{-1}<\infty\end{aligned}\right\},

(12)

where the second-order Radon total variation norm
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f})
is a seminorm defined by

\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f})=c_{d}\|\partial_{t}^{2}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}f\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}.

(13)

Here,
\Lambda^{d-1}=(-\partial_{t}^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
,
c_{d}^{-1}=2(2\pi)^{d-1}
is a dimension-dependent constant, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}
denotes the total variation norm in the sense of measures supported on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. Note that all operators must be understood
in the distributional sense (see
Parhi and Nowak (2021)
;
Parhi and Unser (2024)
for more details). The seminorm in
Eq.
(
13
) exactly coincides with the representational cost of a function realized as a single-hidden-layer bounded-norm infinite-width network and coincides with the
{\mathcal{R}}
-norm
introduced by
Ongie et al. (2020)
.

\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of a Kernel Machine

In this section, we study the
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of kernel machines in the RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}
. We show that one can write an explicit computable form for the case when the input dimension
d
is odd.
Consider the underlying matrix
{\mathbf{M}}\succ 0
for the Gaussian kernel
k_{{\mathbf{M}}}
has the following Cholesky decomposition

{\mathbf{M}}={\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(14)

Since
{\mathbf{M}}
is full rank and is in
\text{Sym}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
this decomposition is unique. With this we state the following result on
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f})
of a kernel machine
f\in{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the proof in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that the input dimension
d
is odd.
For a kernel machine
f\in{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the form

\displaystyle f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}k_{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot),

(15)

the
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of
f
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(f)=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta},

(16)

where we have used the decomposition
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}
.
Furthermore, this can be extended to the case when
f
has a representation with infinite kernel functions by taking limits.

Proof.

Outline

The proof proceeds in three main steps:
First, we leverage the factorization
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}
to express the Gaussian kernel for a single center
\bm{x}_{0}
as

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{|\mathbf{L}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})|^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(17)

Next, we compute its Fourier transform using the change-of-variables formula to obtain

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\bm{\omega})=\exp\Bigl(-\mathrm{i}\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}\Bigr)\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}|^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(18)

Finally, we apply the Fourier slice theorem
(
Ramm and Katsevich, 1996
)

to connect the one-dimensional Fourier transform of
\mathcal{R}\{g\}(\bm{\beta},t)
(with respect to
t
) with
d
-variate Fourier transform evaluated on one slice:
\hat{g}(\omega\bm{\beta})
. By inverting this transform, we derive the explicit expression for
\mathcal{R}\{g\}(\bm{\beta},t)
.
For odd dimensions
d
, the second-order Radon total variation of smooth functions is characterized by the
L_{1}
-norm of the
(d+1)
th
t
-derivative of
\mathcal{R}\{g\}(\bm{\beta},t)

(
Ongie et al., 2020
;
Parhi and Nowak, 2021
, cf.,)
. The result then readily extends to any finite kernel machine

\displaystyle f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}k_{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot)

(19)

through the linearity of both the Fourier and Radon transforms.

Assumption 1

Lemma 8

Let

Theorem 9

(Diverging
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
)
.

Consider the Gaussian RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as defined in

(
4

\displaystyle\rho:=\int_{[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}\left|H_{d+1}\left(y\right)\,e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy.

(36)

Fix a unit vector
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\eta\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, and
\delta=3\max\{\epsilon,\delta_{0}(\rho),\delta^{\prime}\}
where
\delta_{0}(\rho)
is chosen as per Lemma
8
, and
\delta^{\prime}(d)
as per Assumption
1
. Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{\infty}=\left\{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an infinite sequence such that any subsequence
\Gamma_{n}=\left\{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\right\}

(\bm{\beta},\delta,\eta)
-separated set of size
n
. Define a function
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}_{\infty}
that has a representation with an
f

\alpha_{f}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({\{f_{n}\}})\to\infty,

(37)

as

.

Proof.

First, we rewrite
Eq.
(
28
) for the
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of the function
f_{k}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f_{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|dy\,d\bm{\beta},

(38)

with the inner integral

\displaystyle I_{\textsf{inner}}(\bm{\beta})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy,

(39)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}=0,\,\,\Delta_{i}=\frac{a_{1}-a_{i}}{\sigma}=\frac{\bm{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}}{\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\|}\quad\text{for }i=2,3,\ldots,k.

(40)

First, define the cone
{\mathcal{K}}
wrt
\bm{\beta}
and
\eta
as stated in the theorem statement, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}:=\left\{\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\>\middle|\>\bm{\beta}^{\prime\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\geq\eta\right\}.

(41)

Note that the volume
\textsf{vol}({{\mathcal{K}}})>0
implying that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\,d\bm{\bm{\beta}}\geq\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\,d\bm{\beta}=(\star).

(42)

Note that
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}
. We assume that the
\mathbf{M}
is symmetric and PSD, implying that singular values of
\mathbf{L}
are
exactly
the square root of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{L})=\sqrt{|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{M})|}.

(43)

But, since
\mathbf{L}
is invertible implying the singular values if
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
are inverses to singular values of
\mathbf{L}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{L}^{-1})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{L})}
.
Thus, we can rewrite
Eq.
(
42
) as

\displaystyle(\star)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{L}^{-1})^{d+1}}\,d\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{L})^{d+1}\,d\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}\,d\bm{\beta}=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}\textsf{vol}({{\mathcal{K}}})>0.

(44)

Now, we will show for any
\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, there is a non-trivial lower bound on
I_{\textsf{inner}}(\bm{\beta}^{\prime})
. Note that by definition,
\Gamma_{n}
is in the
(\bm{\beta}^{\prime},\delta)
-separated set.

Hence, for all
i,j=2,3,\ldots

\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}|\geq\delta.

(45)

Define the neighborhoods
\left\{N_{i}\right\}
for the safe constant
\epsilon
as follows

\displaystyle N_{i}:=\left[-\Delta_{i}-\epsilon,-\Delta_{i}+\epsilon\right].

(46)

Now, consider the integral on the neighborhood

\displaystyle\int_{N_{i}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{N_{i}}\left|\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy-\underbrace{\int_{N_{i}}\left|\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\alpha_{j}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy}_{=:\large{\theta_{i}}}.

(47)

The second line follows from the triangle inequality. Now, change of variable simplifies the first equation as

\displaystyle\int_{N_{i}}\left|\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy\geq|\alpha_{i}|\int_{[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}\left|H_{d+1}\left(y\right)\,e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy\geq|\alpha_{i}|\rho.

(48)

In the last equation, we used the definition of
\rho
.

Now, summing over each

, we get

\displaystyle I_{\textsf{inner}}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{N_{i}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy\geq\rho\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}.

(49)

Now, we will show how to bound the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}
.

Bounding
\theta_{k}

First note that, we can bound each
\theta_{i}
as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{N_{i}}\left|\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\alpha_{j}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\int_{N_{i}}\left|\alpha_{j}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

(50)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\int_{\left[-\epsilon,\epsilon\right]}\left|\alpha_{j}\,H_{d+1}\left(-\Delta_{i}+y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(-\Delta_{i}+y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}|\alpha_{j}|\int_{\left[-\epsilon,\epsilon\right]}\left|H_{d+1}\left(|i-j|\delta\right)\,e^{-\frac{(|i-j|\delta)^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

(52)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}|\alpha_{j}|\left|H_{d+1}\left(|i-j|\delta\right)\right|\,e^{-\frac{(|i-j|\delta)^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}|\alpha_{j}|\left|H_{d+1}\left(|i-j|\delta\right)\right|\,e^{-\frac{(|i-j|\delta)^{2}}{2}}.

(53)

Eq.
(
50
) is a straight-forward application of triangle inequality. In
Eq.
(
51
), we simplify
Eq.
(
50
) via change of variable. In
Eq.
(
52
), we use the assumption of
\delta
-peak. To simplify

for a choice of

, we assume that indices of the projections
\Delta_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots
are arranged in ascending order in their values on the real line. Since each consecutive projections are at least
\delta
apart, we can bound
|-\Delta_{i}+y+\Delta_{j}|>(|i-j|-1/3)\delta
. Since Hermite polynomials in even dimension, i.e.
d+1
, are even

\displaystyle\left|H_{d+1}\left(-\Delta_{i}+y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(-\Delta_{i}+y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\leq\left|H_{d+1}\left((|i-j|-2/3)\delta\right)e^{-\frac{((|i-j|-2/3)\delta)^{2}}{2}}\right|.

(54)

For simplification, we have omitted the
-(2/3)\delta

Eq.
(
53
) follows as
\epsilon\leq 1/2
.

Summing over each

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{N_{i}}\left|\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\alpha_{j}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{j}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{j})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}|\alpha_{j}|\left|H_{d+1}\left(|i-j|\delta\right)\right|\,e^{-\frac{(|i-j|\delta)^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left({\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|H_{d+1}\left(j\delta\right)\right|\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|.

(55)

Using Lemma
8
, we can rewrite
Eq.
(
49
) as

\displaystyle I_{\textsf{inner}}\geq\rho\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|-2\left({\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left|H_{d+1}\left(j\delta\right)\right|\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|\geq\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|.

(56)

Now, note that using
Eq.
(
42
) and
Eq.
(
44
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f_{k}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|dy\,d\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{{\mathcal{K}}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\left({\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|}\right)\,d\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\left({\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}\textsf{vol}({{\mathcal{K}}})}\right)\cdot\left({\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|}\right).

Now, in the limiting case

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f_{k}})

\displaystyle\geq\left({\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}\textsf{vol}({{\mathcal{K}}})}\right)\lim_{k\to\infty}\left({\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})^{\frac{(d+1)}{2}}\textsf{vol}({{\mathcal{K}}})}\right)\cdot\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|\alpha_{f}\right\|\to\infty.

Hence the claim of the theorem has been proven.
∎

6
Conclusion

In this work, we showed that if we allow
unbounded
domains, there exist functions that cannot be represented within
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{BV}^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, but can be represented within the Gaussian RKHS. This analysis reveals a nontrivial gap between kernel methods and neural networks by exhibiting functions that kernel methods can represent, whereas neural networks cannot.
On that note, this observation motivates further investigation of what this gap entails in a learning setting. This observation also motivates investigating if a similar gap exists between kernel methods and
deep
neural networks. We leave the details to future work.

In this Appendix, we provide the proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}\right\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}{2}}\right).

(59)

Also, define the
\bm{0}
mean identity covariance Gaussian

\displaystyle g_{0}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(60)

If we can write
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}\right\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{L}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})\right\|^{2},

(61)

in which case

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}\bm{x}-\mathbf{L}\bm{x}_{0}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(62)

We have the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{0}(\bm{\omega})=\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\bm{\omega}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(63)

We have the equality
g(\bm{x})=g_{0}(\mathbf{L}\bm{x}-\mathbf{L}\bm{x}_{0})
. Using the change of variables formula for the Fourier transform, we have

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\bm{\omega})

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{L}\bm{x}_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\hat{g}_{0}(\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega})

(64)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(66)

The Fourier slice theorem
(
Ramm and Katsevich, 1996
)
says that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}\{\mathcal{R}\{f\}(\bm{\beta},\cdot)\}(\omega)=\hat{f}(\omega\bm{\beta}).

(67)

If we evaluate
\hat{g}
at
\bm{\omega}=\omega\bm{\beta}
in the

(

), we find

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\omega\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}(\omega\bm{\beta})}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}(\omega\bm{\beta})\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}(\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})\omega}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\exp\left({-\frac{\left|\omega\right|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(69)

The 1D inverse Fourier transform of this is the Radon transform of

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\{g\}(\bm{\beta},t)=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right).

(70)

If

L_{1}
-norm of
(d+1)
derivatives in

of this quantity (see Equation (28) in
Parhi and Nowak (2021)
). That is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}.

(72)

This gives the stated expression on
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}({f})
for one center.
∎

A.1

For a kernel machine with

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\alpha_{i}\cdot\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}\bm{x}-\mathbf{L}\bm{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

Denote by

.

Now, the Fourier transform of
g
can be written for the extended case, noting the linearity of the transform,

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\bm{\omega})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{g}_{i}(\bm{\omega}).

(74)

This implies that

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\bm{\omega})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp\left({-\mathrm{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}}\right)\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\exp\left({-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\omega}\right\|^{2}}{2}}\right).

(75)

Now, computing the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{g}
wrt
\bm{\omega}
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\{g\}(\bm{\beta},t)=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right).

(76)

As before the
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of
g
, i.e. the second-order Radon domain total variation for odd values of
d
is the
L_{1}
-norm of
(d+1)
derivatives in
t
of this quantity. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(g)=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}.

(77)

This expression can be extended to the case of kernel machines with infinite centers by taking limits. In particular, it is guaranteed to be finite for the case when the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coefficients
\alpha
is finite since

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\leq C\cdot\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|,

(78)

where it is straightforward to show that
\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)
is bounded by a universal constant
C>0
for all choices of
\bm{x}_{i}
.

Appendix B
Change of Variables For Multiple Centers

In this Appendix, we provide the proof of Lemma
7
.

Proof.

Previously, we computed the
\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}
of a general kernel machine as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(g)=\frac{1}{\left|\det\mathbf{L}\right|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{{\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left({\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left({-\frac{(t-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta})^{2}}{2\left\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\right\|^{2}}}\right)}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}.

(79)

Now, we can rewrite the (
d+1
)-th derivative of the involved exponential as follows

\frac{\partial^{d+1}}{\partial t^{d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{(t-a_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)=(-1)^{d+1}\sigma^{-(d+1)}H_{d+1}\left(\frac{t-a_{i}}{\sigma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(t-a_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

where we define

\displaystyle a_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma=\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\|.

(80)

Substituting the expression for Hermite polynomial (see Section
3
) into the integral
I
gives

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}H_{d+1}\left(\frac{t-a_{i}}{\sigma}\right)e^{-\frac{(t-a_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|dt\,d\bm{\beta}.

To simplify the inner integral, we can perform the following change of variable centered at
a_{1}=\bm{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle y=\frac{t-a_{1}}{\sigma}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t=\sigma y+a_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad dt=\sigma\,dy.

(81)

We can express all
y_{i}
in terms of
y
as follows

\displaystyle y_{i}=\frac{t-a_{i}}{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma y+a_{1}-a_{i}}{\sigma}=y+\Delta_{i},

(82)

where we define

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}=\frac{a_{1}-a_{i}}{\sigma}=\frac{\bm{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}}{\|\mathbf{L}^{-\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}\|}\quad\text{for }i=2,3,\ldots,k,

(83)

and
\Delta_{1}=0
.

With this change of variable into the inner integral we have the stated final form

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{L}|\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|dy}_{\text{define $I_{\textsf{inner}}$}}\,d\bm{\beta}

(84)

\displaystyle I_{\textsf{inner}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\,H_{d+1}\left(y+\Delta_{i}\right)\,e^{-\frac{(y+\Delta_{i})^{2}}{2}}\right|\,dy.

(85)

∎

Appendix C
A Useful Property of Hermite Polynomials

In this Appendix, we provide the proof of Lemma
8
.

Proof.

Since
H_{d+1}(y)
is a polynomial of degree
d+1
, there exists a constant
C>0
(depending only on
d
) such that

\displaystyle\Bigl|H_{d+1}(y)\Bigr|\leq C\,(1+|y|)^{d+1}\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathbb{R}.

(86)

Hence, for any
\delta>0
and any integer
j\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|H_{d+1}\bigl(j\delta\bigr)\Bigr|\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}\leq C\,(1+j\delta)^{d+1}\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}.

(87)

Now, we define

\displaystyle S(\delta):=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(1+j\delta)^{d+1}\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}.

(88)

Note that we we can upper bound as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\Bigl|H_{d+1}\bigl(j\delta\bigr)\Bigr|\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}\leq C\,S(\delta).

(89)

For each fixed
j\geq 2
, notice that
(1+j\delta)^{d+1}\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}

decays exponentially in
j
(since the exponential term
e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}
dominates the polynomial growth of
(1+j\delta)^{d+1}
). Moreover, for fixed
j\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to\infty}(1+j\delta)^{d+1}\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}=0.

(90)

Thus, the series
S(\delta)
converges for every fixed
\delta>0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to\infty}S(\delta)=0.

(91)

Hence, by the definition, there exists some
\delta_{0}>0
such that for all
\delta\geq\delta_{0}
we have

\displaystyle S(\delta)<\frac{\rho}{4C}.

(92)

It follows that for every
\delta\geq\delta_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\Bigl|H_{d+1}\bigl(j\delta\bigr)\Bigr|\,e^{-\frac{(j\delta)^{2}}{2}}\leq C\cdot S(\delta)<C\cdot\frac{\rho}{4C}=\frac{\rho}{4}.

(93)

Thus for this choice of
\delta_{0}
we achieve the statement of the lemma.
∎

Appendix D
A Sequence With Diverging
\ell_{1}
-Norm and Converging RKHS Norm

In this Appendix, we provide the proof of Example
5
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
and suppose that the points
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}\|\geq|i-j|\delta\quad\text{for some }\delta>0\text{ and for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

(94)

Then the function

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,k(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{n}),

(95)

with the Gaussian kernel

\displaystyle k(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr),

(96)

has finite RKHS norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{ij}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})<\infty,

(97)

even though

\displaystyle\|\alpha\|_{\ell_{1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}=\infty.

(98)

Proof.

We begin by splitting the double series defining the RKHS norm into diagonal and off–diagonal parts:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{ij}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}).

(99)

Since
k(\bm{x},\bm{x})=1
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the diagonal contribution is

\displaystyle S_{\text{diag}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}},

(100)

which converges (indeed,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}=\pi^{2}/6
).

For the off–diagonal part, define

\displaystyle S_{\text{off}}=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{ij}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}).

(101)

By symmetry and non-negativity of
k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
, we can write

\displaystyle S_{\text{off}}=2\sum_{i>j}\frac{1}{ij}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}).

(102)

For
i>j
, the separation condition implies

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}\|\geq(i-j)\delta,

(103)

so that

\displaystyle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{((i-j)\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr).

(104)

Setting

\displaystyle k=i-j\quad(k\geq 1)

(105)

and writing
i=j+k
, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{\text{off}}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(k\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j(j+k)}.

(106)

We now analyze the inner sum. Using partial fractions,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j(j+k)}=\frac{1}{k}\Bigl(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+k}\Bigr).

(107)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j(j+k)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+k}\right).

(108)

The telescoping sum yields

\displaystyle H_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+k}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j},

(109)

where
H_{k}
is also known as the
k
th harmonic number. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j(j+k)}=\frac{H_{k}}{k}.

(110)

It follows that

\displaystyle S_{\text{off}}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(k\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{H_{k}}{k}.

(111)

For large
k
, it holds that

\displaystyle H_{k}=\ln k+\gamma+o(1),

(112)

where
\gamma
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Moreover, the Gaussian factor

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(k\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)

(113)

decays exponentially in
k
. Therefore, the series

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(k\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{H_{k}}{k}

(114)

is bounded, where we note that
\tfrac{H_{k}}{k}
is bounded from above by
1
(for all
k
taken sufficiently large). Combining the diagonal and off–diagonal parts, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{ij}\,k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})

(115)

\displaystyle=S_{\text{diag}}+S_{\text{off}}

(116)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(k\delta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{H_{k}}{k}

(117)

\displaystyle<\infty.

(118)

∎
