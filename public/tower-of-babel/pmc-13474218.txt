Acute Klebsiella pneumoniae infection of the knee joint following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction in a young adult: A case report
Authors: Zong-Han Lin, Chang-Hao Lin, Yu-Sian Ding, Chen-Hao Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute postoperative infection caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction in young, immunocompetent individuals is exceedingly uncommon. An immunocompetent man in his early 20s developed fever and progressive swelling of the knee 5 days after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated inflammatory markers, including a C-reactive protein level of 18.36 mg/dL and synovial fluid white blood cell count of 62,667/mm3. Culture of aspirated synovial fluid confirmed infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae, establishing a diagnosis of acute postoperative septic arthritis of the knee. The patient underwent prompt arthroscopic debridement with biofilm debulking. Empirical intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam was administered for 3 days, followed by targeted intravenous levofloxacin for 5 days, and oral levofloxacin to complete an 8-week antimicrobial course. Infection was successfully controlled without graft or implant removal. The patient exhibited clinical improvement with resolution of fever and inflammatory signs following treatment. This case highlights that acute Klebsiella pneumoniae infection of the knee joint can occur even in young, immunocompetent patients shortly after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, without an identifiable remote infectious focus. Early recognition, prompt arthroscopic intervention, minimal implant burden, and appropriate targeted antibiotic therapy may enable successful infection control in selected patients.

Introduction

Primary anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is commonly performed to restore knee stability after ligament injury. Postoperative joint infection is a rare but serious complication of ACL reconstruction, with a reported incidence of 0.6% (range, 0.15%–2.44%). The most common pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and coagulase-negative staphylococci. 1

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a gram-negative, encapsulated opportunistic pathogen that is a leading cause of hospital-acquired infections. It commonly colonizes the gastrointestinal tract and is most frequently associated with pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and bloodstream infections. Urinary tract infection is the most common clinical presentation, and bacteremia often follows secondary dissemination from a primary focus. 2

Management of K. pneumoniae infections remains challenging because of problems such as multidrug resistance, biofilm formation protecting bacteria from host defenses and antibiotics, and intestinal colonization that can precede invasive, hematogenously disseminated infection. 3 Although these infections are more common in immunocompromised patients, they can also develop postoperatively in otherwise healthy individuals in both community and postoperative settings.4,5 Established risk factors include preexisting colonization, invasive procedures, and chronic comorbidities.4,5

Despite the clinical significance of K. pneumoniae, musculoskeletal involvement remains uncommon, and septic arthritis caused by K. pneumoniae has rarely been reported, particularly in knee joints.6–9 In this report, we present a rare case of K. pneumoniae infection following ACL reconstruction. This case highlights the microbiological characteristics and therapeutic considerations associated with this uncommon but clinically relevant pathogen.

Case presentation

This case report was prepared in accordance with the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 10 Only de-identified, patient-specific information was used in this study to ensure confidentiality. Written informed consent for the surgical procedures was obtained as part of routine clinical care. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2024). The study design was approved by the institutional review board of Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia-Yi Christian Hospital (approval no.: 2025135), which waived the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study.

A man in his early 20s was admitted to Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia-Yi Christian Hospital, Chiayi, Taiwan, in October 2025 for left knee arthroscopic ACL reconstruction using semitendinosus and gracilis tendon autograft (6-stranded construct, 9 mm in diameter), pretensioned at 20 lb for at least 10 min, along with bilateral menisci repair to address a complete ACL rupture and bilateral menisci tears. Femoral fixation was achieved using an adjustable-loop cortical suspensory device (UltraButton; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA). On the tibial side, a rigid hybrid fixation was performed using an intratunnel bioabsorbable interference screw (BioSure; Smith & Nephew) supplemented by an extratunnel backup tie-on-post with a stainless-steel cortical screw and washer. Standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis was administered with intravenous cefazolin (2 g) 10 min before the start of surgery because the body weight of this patient was >80 kg, followed by intravenous cefazolin (2 g every 8 h) for the first 24 h postoperatively. 11 The wound healed well, and the patient was able to ambulate with the left knee in full extension by using a knee brace without assistance on postoperative day 2 and was subsequently discharged. He had no known immunosuppressive conditions or diabetes. On postoperative day 5, he developed fever, followed by redness and swelling over the surgical wound, prompting him to visit the emergency department. Although no obvious infectious source was identified, the early postoperative timing and unusual pathogen posed a diagnostic challenge.

Laboratory studies revealed considerably elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level (18.360 mg/dL). Influenza A + B antigens and a coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) antigen rapid test were negative. Upon readmission day 1, empirical antibiotic therapy with intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam (4500 mg q8 h) and teicoplanin (600 mg q12 h) were initiated. Computed tomography (CT) of the left knee (with and without contrast) demonstrated synovial thickening with increased enhancement and joint effusion involving the suprapatellar pouch and anterior compartment (Figure 1). Physical examination revealed mild serous discharge from the proximal tibial wound, whereas the arthroscopic portal sites appeared unremarkable (Figure 2(a)). Joint aspiration of the left knee yielded approximately 10 mL of amber–orange serosanguinous and turbid fluid (Figure 2(b)). Synovial fluid analysis revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 62,667/mm3, which is highly suggestive of postoperative joint infection. Differential diagnoses included postoperative inflammatory effusion, hemarthrosis, and septic arthritis. On readmission day 2, K. pneumonia was identified through microbiological culture of the joint aspirate by using the VITEK mass spectrometry system (MALDI-TOF MS; bioMérieux, France), confirming septic arthritis. Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion testing of antimicrobial susceptibility indicated susceptibility to piperacillin–tazobactam, levofloxacin, cefotaxime, cefepime, amikacin, ertapenem, and meropenem and resistance to ampicillin/sulbactam, cefazolin, gentamicin, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. Teicoplanin was discontinued, and piperacillin–tazobactam was continued.

Arthroscopic examination, debridement, and biofilm debulking were performed on readmission day 2. Arthroscopic examination revealed hypertrophic synovitis and blood clots and biofilm predominantly located within the anterior compartment. The reconstructed ACL and repaired bilateral menisci remained structurally intact (Figure 3). Based on the recommendations of an infectious disease specialist, intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam was administered for 3 days and subsequently discontinued. The patient received targeted intravenous levofloxacin (750 mg daily) from readmission days 4–8. Urinalysis and urine culture revealed no evidence of urinary tract infection, and chest radiography indicated no signs of pneumonia. On readmission day 8, the treatment was switched to oral levofloxacin (750 mg daily). After 2 days of observation, the wound had remained stable without notable discharge, warmth, or tenderness, and the patient was discharged on day 10. Oral therapy was continued post discharge for a full 8-week course of antimicrobial treatment.

Postoperative inflammatory markers were closely monitored. His CRP level was 4.810 mg/dL on postoperative day 5 and decreased to 0.098 mg/dL by readmission day 35. Functional knee recovery was satisfactory at the 6-month follow-up, with full range of motion and no pain during daily activities. No clinical signs of infection were observed at follow-up, and no adverse or unanticipated events were observed. The patient completed the full antibiotic course without adverse reactions, and no treatment-related adverse events were observed. The isolation of K. pneumoniae in this postoperative setting was an unexpected finding.

Discussion

Our case involves several clinical features that are distinct from those in previously reported cases of K. pneumoniae infection following orthopedic procedures of the knee (Table 1). To our knowledge, previously reported cases of K. pneumoniae knee infections have predominantly involved prosthetic knee joints, particularly following total knee arthroplasty. In contrast, the present case occurred after ACL reconstruction in a young immunocompetent patient without a joint arthroplasty implant. Furthermore, most cases have been reported in older adults and been accompanied with comorbidities, whereas our patient was a young, immunocompetent man without underlying diseases.

In native septic arthritis, S. aureus is the most common causative organism across all age groups, followed by streptococcal species. 12 A retrospective epidemiological study of knee septic arthritis by Helito et al. reported that S. aureus accounted for 72.1% of culture-positive cases, whereas gram-negative organisms were identified much less frequently. 13 In contrast, septic arthritis following ACL reconstruction is most commonly caused by S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci, particularly S. epidermidis. 1 Therefore, the identification of K. pneumoniae in the present case represents an uncommon microbiological finding.

Graft choice has been indicated as a potential factor influencing infection risk following ACL reconstruction. A meta-analysis including 21 level-I and level-II studies has reported an overall low incidence of deep infection after ACL reconstruction (pooled incidence of approximately 0.12%). 14 In subgroup analysis, bone–patellar tendon–bone autografts were associated with a lower risk of deep infection compared with hamstring autografts (relative risk, 0.23; 95% confidence interval, 0.097–0.54), whereas no significant difference was observed between autografts and allografts. 14 The authors indicated that differences in graft characteristics and tissue handling may contribute to this variation, although the absolute difference in infection risk remained small. In the present case, a hamstring tendon autograft was used; therefore, graft selection alone is unlikely to fully account for the development of acute postoperative septic arthritis.

The timing of infection onset is also distinct. A delayed infection developing months to years after surgery has been reported in previous studies; however, our patient developed acute infection within 3 days postoperatively. This difference may be explained by several factors. First, most previous cases have involved total knee arthroplasty or revision procedures, which introduce a large volume of prosthetic material and extensive bone–implant interfaces that facilitate biofilm formation and prolong infection. 15 Consequently, these infections often present months to years postoperatively as delayed or chronic periprosthetic joint infections. In contrast, ACL reconstruction involves a substantially smaller implant and is primarily a soft tissue procedure. Thus, any contamination from infection is more likely to manifest acutely.

Second, several previously reported cases involved remote infectious sources, further supporting a delayed mode of infection through a hematogenous pathway. In our patient, the absence of an identifiable remote infectious focus indicates a lower likelihood of hematogenous spread; however, the source of K. pneumoniae remains unclear. The patient was hospitalized for routine postoperative care until postoperative day 2; however, no specific nosocomial source was identified. Therefore, a hospital-acquired origin cannot be completely excluded. Although the infection manifested acutely through pronounced elevation of inflammatory markers, hypervirulent strains were not assessed, and we cannot definitively determine whether the strain possessed hypervirulent characteristics. Finally, host-related factors may have contributed to the rapid clinical presentation. Young, immunocompetent patients may mount a robust inflammatory response, leading to earlier symptom manifestation and markedly elevated inflammatory markers, whereas older adults with comorbidities may exhibit a more insidious clinical course.

The treatment strategies applied in our case also differ from those previously reported. Most previously reported patients have been reported to require prolonged antibiotic therapy, implant retention or staged implant removal, and additional surgical interventions for remote infectious sources. In contrast, our patient was successfully treated with a single arthroscopic debridement and biofilm debulking followed by appropriate antibiotic therapy, without the need for additional surgical procedures or long-term suppressive antibiotics. Consistent with the less invasive treatment approach, our patient achieved relatively short time to symptom resolution. In contrast to previous studies that have reported symptom resolution timelines range from several weeks to years, our patient achieved clinical resolution within 35 days after antibiotic therapy initiation.

Graft retention versus removal remains a key decision in the management of septic arthritis following ACL reconstruction. Current evidence from systematic reviews suggests no clear superiority of graft removal over graft retention in terms of clinical outcomes and indicates that early postoperative infections can often be successfully managed with graft-preserving arthroscopic debridement combined with targeted antibiotic therapy.16,17 However, graft removal may be considered in cases of persistent or recurrent infection, gross graft contamination, graft loosening, or failure of repeated debridement. 18

The current report is limited by the fact that it presents a single case, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, although the patient achieved satisfactory short-term clinical and laboratory outcomes after an 8-week course of antimicrobial therapy, the lack of long-term follow-up limits our ability to draw conclusions regarding the durability of infection eradication and potential late complications. Finally, differences in patient populations, surgical techniques, and reporting practices may influence the observed differences in outcomes between our case and those reported previously.

Conclusion

This case indicates that early diagnosis, prompt arthroscopic intervention, minimal implant burden, and the absence of medical comorbidities may enable effective antibiotic treatment in selected young patients with acute Klebsiella pneumoniae infection of the knee joint. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution given the single-case nature of this report.
