Hidden cardiometabolic risk: normal weight central obesity in a nationally representative study from Türkiye
Authors: Hamit Harun Bağcı, Şefik Yurdakul, Mehmet Enes Gökler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the prevalence of normal-weight central obesity (NWCO) in Türkiye and its associations with cardiometabolic diseases. The analyses utilized data from the Türkiye Household Health Survey (STEPS 2023), which was conducted in accordance with the World Health Organization’s STEPwise approach.MethodsA total of 6,017 individuals participated in the survey; 4,991 with complete anthropometric data were included in the primary analytic sample. Weighted prevalence estimates, multivariable logistic regression, and population attributable fraction (PAF) analyses were performed, accounting for the complex sampling design.ResultsThe prevalence of central obesity in Türkiye was 62.0%. Among individuals with a normal BMI, 25.2% had central obesity, and the prevalence of normal weight central obesity (NWCO) in the overall population was 8.8%. The prevalence of NWCO was higher in women than in men (11.9% vs. 5.7%; p < 0.001). In the NWCO group, the prevalence of hypertension (21.3%), diabetes (10.4%), and hypercholesterolemia (28.1%) was significantly higher compared to individuals with a normal BMI and no central obesity (p < 0.001). In multivariable analyses, NWCO was associated with hypertension (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.27–3.80) and hypercholesterolemia (OR 2.43, 95% CI 1.06–5.59) in women and with diabetes in men (OR 4.19, 95% CI 1.13–15.54), although some subgroup-specific estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously. PAF estimates suggested that NWCO may have meaningful population-level relevance; however, the wide confidence intervals surrounding several estimates indicate substantial uncertainty.ConclusionThese findings indicate that abdominal fat accumulation is associated with adverse cardiometabolic profiles even among individuals with a normal BMI. In Türkiye, incorporating waist circumference alongside BMI into routine clinical assessment, particularly within primary care, may enhance identification of individuals with potentially elevated cardiometabolic risk not captured by BMI alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27865-5.Keywords: Cardiometabolic risk, Central obesity, Normal-weight central obesity, STEPS, Turkish population, Waist circumference

Background

Obesity, a global public health issue, continues to cause serious health problems worldwide. According to data from the World Health Organization (WHO), one in eight people worldwide had obesity in 2022, and the proportion of overweight individuals is also quite high [1]. In the WHO European Region, nearly 60% of adults are affected by overweight or obesity [2].

While obesity poses a serious threat to the health of individuals and societies, the fat distribution in the body plays a crucial role in identifying this threat [3, 4]. Central obesity can be defined as the accumulation of body fat concentrated in the abdominal region. Numerous studies have shown that central obesity is a stronger predictor of cardiometabolic risks compared to Body Mass Index (BMI) [3, 5]. Large-scale studies have shown that central obesity is associated with increased all-cause mortality, even among individuals with normal weight [6, 7].

Waist circumference is the most commonly used indicator of central obesity. Waist circumference is a practical indicator of abdominal fat distribution and is recommended by the WHO with thresholds of ≥ 94 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women in Europid populations [8]. However, there are few studies evaluating the discrepancy between BMI and central obesity indicators in nationally representative samples and the association of this discrepancy with cardiometabolic outcomes.

Although BMI is the most widely used measure of obesity, classifications based solely on BMI may fail to capture individual cardiometabolic risk. It is increasingly emphasized in the literature that individuals within the normal BMI range who exhibit abdominal adiposity constitute a group carrying “hidden cardiometabolic risk” [8–11].

Identifying individuals with normal BMI but excess abdominal adiposity is therefore becoming increasingly important in public health research. This group may be overlooked in clinical practice and also indicates the presence of a subpopulation that poses a “hidden risk” within the general population [9, 12]. However, there is limited nationally representative evidence in Türkiye regarding normal-weight central obesity (NWCO) and its cardiometabolic effects. NWCO is increasingly recognized as a distinct cardiometabolic phenotype, reflecting a mismatch between overall body mass and abdominal fat distribution.

In this study, data from the Türkiye Household Health Survey (STEPS 2023) were used to examine the prevalence of NWCO in Türkiye and its association with cardiometabolic risk factors. The aim of the study was to determine the prevalence of individuals with central obesity despite having a normal BMI and to elucidate the association of this phenotype with cardiometabolic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia. These findings may also have relevance for other populations facing a growing burden of chronic disease.

Although recent multinational STEPS-based analyses have demonstrated that NWCO is associated with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes across diverse populations, Türkiye was not included in these pooled analyses. Consequently, nationally representative evidence regarding NWCO prevalence and cardiometabolic correlates in the Turkish population has remained limited. The present study addresses this gap by providing contemporary nationally representative estimates from Türkiye using the most recent STEPS 2023 data.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted using anonymized secondary data and was carried out in accordance with the ethical approval and institutional permissions obtained for STEPS 2023. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (No. 08/1469, October 23, 2025), and institutional permission was obtained on November 11, 2025 (No. E-49654233-604.02-294009322).

The 2025 ethics approval obtained for this study specifically covered the present secondary analysis of previously collected anonymized Türkiye STEPS 2023 survey data, for which data-use permission was granted by the Ministry of Health.

Study design and data source

Data were obtained from the Türkiye Household Health Survey—“Prevalence of Risk Factors for Noncommunicable Diseases 2023”—conducted in accordance with the WHO STEPwise approach (STEPS). STEPS 2023 was implemented across Türkiye’s 12 statistical regions between September and November 2023, ensuring national representativeness with data collected from 6,017 individuals aged 15 and older. The Türkiye STEPS 2023 survey aimed to recruit 8,650 individuals, of whom 6,017 participated, corresponding to an overall response rate of 80.9%. Participants missing data required for obesity phenotype classification or relevant cardiometabolic outcome/covariate variables were excluded from the corresponding analyses under a complete-case approach. The survey followed the standard three-step WHO methodology that includes behavioral factors (tobacco, alcohol, diet, physical activity), physical measurements (height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure), and biochemical markers (fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid profile) [13, 14]. Pregnant women were excluded from anthropometric analyses in accordance with the Türkiye STEPS 2023 protocol.

Covariates for multivariable models were selected a priori based on previous literature and conceptual relevance to the association between body composition phenotype and cardiometabolic outcomes. Age, educational level, current smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity were included because they are established determinants of both adiposity patterns and cardiometabolic risk. Region was accounted for through the stratification variable in the complex survey design. Urban/rural residence was not available in the analytic dataset. Additional socioeconomic variables were not included in the primary models because the selected model was designed to incorporate core demographic and behavioral confounders commonly used in comparable STEPS-based analyses while avoiding overparameterization. Socioeconomic measures in the analytic dataset were also limited in granularity: household income was reported only as nominal earnings without adjustment for household composition or inflation context, and employment status was captured only as a binary working/non-working variable. Given these limitations, such variables were not considered sufficiently robust for inclusion in the primary models. Residual socioeconomic confounding cannot be excluded.

The Türkiye STEPS 2023 survey includes individuals aged 15 years and older in accordance with the national surveillance protocol. Among participants in the 15–29-year age group, 8.9% were aged 15–17 years, corresponding to 1.2% of the total analytic sample. To evaluate the potential impact of including adolescents, sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating the primary analyses after restricting the sample to participants aged 18 years and older while retaining the original survey design specifications.

STEPS data were collected using a complex sampling design with weighting and calibration to ensure national representativeness [15, 16].

Definition of obesity phenotypes

NWCO is defined by a discrepancy between BMI and waist circumference. Among individuals with a BMI in the range of 18.5–24.9 kg/m² (normal weight), those with a waist circumference of ≥94 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women—based on the threshold values recommended by the WHO for European populations—are classified as having normal-weight central obesity. These definitions are based on criteria recommended by the WHO and the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [8]. Waist circumference was selected as the indicator of abdominal obesity because it is the predefined anthropometric marker used in the WHO STEPS surveillance framework and remains the most widely applied measure in international NWCO surveillance studies, allowing comparability with prior literature. WHO Europid waist circumference thresholds were used because Türkiye is generally classified within the WHO European Region and no nationally validated alternative waist circumference cutoffs for NWCO surveillance currently exist for the Turkish adult population. Overweight and obesity categories were combined into a single “overweight-obesity” category for phenotype construction to preserve analytic parsimony and because obesity without central obesity was rare in the study population, resulting in limited stability for separate subgroup analyses.

Cardiometabolic outcomes

The primary outcomes were hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia.

Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mmHg, or self-reported current use of antihypertensive medication.

Blood pressure was measured according to the standardized WHO STEPS protocol after seated rest using repeated measurements at standardized intervals. The mean of the repeated blood pressure measurements was used for hypertension classification.

Diabetes was defined as a venous plasma glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL, an HbA1c level of ≥6.5%, or self-reported current treatment for elevated blood glucose/diabetes.

Hypercholesterolemia was defined as a total cholesterol level of ≥190 mg/dL or self-reported current lipid-lowering treatment.

All cardiometabolic outcome definitions and threshold values were applied in accordance with the standardized WHO STEPS surveillance framework and the predefined Türkiye STEPS 2023 protocol to maintain consistency with the original survey methodology and national surveillance reporting standards.

Biochemical outcome definitions were based on fasting venous blood samples collected according to the standardized STEPS 2023 protocol.

Explanatory variables

Covariates were grouped into sociodemographic and behavioral factors. Sociodemographic variables include age, sex, and educational level.

Behavioral risk factors include current tobacco use, alcohol consumption, level of physical activity, and daily fruit and vegetable intake. Tobacco use was defined as current smoking, and alcohol consumption was defined as any intake reported within the past 30 days. Consuming five or more servings of fruits and vegetables per day was considered adequate consumption.

Physical activity level was assessed using the World Health Organization’s Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ). Physical activity was quantified using metabolic equivalent of task (MET) values. Participants were classified as sufficiently active if they reported at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity per week, or an equivalent combination totaling at least 600 MET-minutes.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 27 and R version 4.5.2. Survey-weighted logistic regression analyses accounting for the complex sampling design were conducted in SPSS Complex Samples. Data visualization was performed in R. Participants’ sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics are presented as weighted frequencies, percentages, and 95% confidence intervals.

Since the Türkiye STEPS study was conducted using a multistage cluster sampling method, the complex sampling design was taken into account in the analyses. Sampling weights were used to ensure national representativeness, and all frequency estimates were calculated using these weights.

The distribution of BMI–central obesity phenotypes according to sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics is presented using weighted prevalence values and 95% confidence intervals. Group differences were assessed using a design-adjusted F test.

The associations between BMI–central obesity phenotypes and cardiometabolic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia) were evaluated using multivariate logistic regression models. Models were adjusted for age (continuous), educational level, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and physical activity level. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) was calculated as a model-based descriptive measure to estimate the theoretical proportion of disease burden statistically associated with NWCO under causal assumptions [17]. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, these estimates should not be interpreted as causal or preventable fractions. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Survey-weighted logistic regression analyses were performed using the Complex Samples module of IBM SPSS Statistics version 27, incorporating the multistage survey design through specification of sampling strata (STRATUM), primary sampling units (PSU), and the appropriate survey weights (WSTEPS1, WSTEPS2, or WSTEPS3, depending on variable availability). The most specific available survey weight was applied according to the variables included in each analysis: WSTEPS1 for questionnaire-based analyses, WSTEPS2 for analyses involving physical measurements, and WSTEPS3 for analyses involving biochemical variables. Because completion rates differed across STEPS modules, minor differences in weighted estimates across analyses may partly reflect module-specific weighting and corresponding analytic subsamples. Variance estimates were obtained using Taylor series linearization. A complete-case approach was used for all analyses. Participants missing data required for obesity phenotype classification were excluded from phenotype analyses, and those missing outcome or covariate data were excluded from the corresponding outcome-specific regression models. Biochemical outcomes were available only for participants completing the STEPS biochemical assessment module.

Results

A total of 6,017 individuals participated in the Türkiye STEPS 2023 survey. Of these, 4,991 participants with complete anthropometric data required for obesity phenotype classification were included in the primary analytic sample. A participant flow diagram summarizing analytic sample derivation and outcome-specific model inclusion is provided in Supplementary Figure 1. Women accounted for 50.3% of the sample. The weighted mean age of participants was 42.5 years, and participants ranged in age from 15 to 105 years. The weighted prevalence of central obesity in the overall Turkish population was 62.0% (95% CI, 59.9–64.1). Among individuals with a normal BMI, 25.2% (95% CI, 22.0–28.6) had central obesity (Table 1).

The distribution of phenotypes showed a significant difference by sex (p < 0.001). The overweight-obesity/central obesity phenotype was more prevalent in women, whereas the normal BMI/non-central obesity phenotype predominated in men. The NWCO phenotype was also more common in women than in men (12.9% vs. 6.0%).

Phenotype distribution also varied significantly across age groups (p < 0.001). The normal BMI/ no central obesity phenotype was more common in younger age groups, whereas the prevalence of overweight-obesity/central obesity phenotype increased with age.

Phenotype distribution also differed significantly by educational level (p < 0.001). The overweight-obesity/central obesity phenotype was observed at higher rates among individuals with a low educational level, whereas the normal BMI/non-central obesity phenotype was found to be higher among individuals with secondary and tertiary educational levels.

When examined in terms of behavioral factors, significant associations were observed between current tobacco use (p < 0.001), alcohol consumption (p=0.024), and physical activity level (p < 0.001) and BMI–central obesity phenotypes. The normal BMI/non-central obesity phenotype was observed at a higher rate among individuals who used tobacco and alcohol, whereas the overweight-obesity/central obesity phenotype was found to be higher among those who did not use these products. Additionally, although the difference was modest, the overweight-obesity/central obesity phenotype was found to be higher among individuals with insufficient physical activity. No significant association was observed between daily fruit and vegetable intake and phenotype distribution (p=0.302). Although central obesity was observed in 25.2% of individuals with a normal BMI, the prevalence of NWCO in the general population was 8.8% (95% CI: 7.6–10.2). NWCO prevalence was significantly higher in women than in men (11.9% vs. 5.7%; p < 0.001). NWCO prevalence also varied significantly by educational level (p=0.002), ranging from 6.0% among primary school graduates to 12.5% among those with tertiary education. NWCO prevalence did not differ significantly by age group, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, or daily fruit and vegetable intake (p=0.234, p=0.211, p=0.061, p=0.160, and p=0.538, respectively). The prevalence estimates in Table 3 reflect the overall weighted population prevalence of NWCO within each subgroup, whereas Table 2 presents within-group phenotype distributions; therefore, these values are not directly comparable. The prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia were higher among individuals with NWCO than among those with a normal BMI and no central obesity. The prevalence of hypertension was 21.3% (95% CI: 17.0–26.4) in the NWCO group, whereas it was 11.8% (95% CI: 9.6–14.5) in individuals with a normal BMI and no central obesity (design-based F=82.842; p < 0.001). Similarly, diabetes prevalence increased from 4.0% to 10.4%, and hypercholesterolemia prevalence increased from 14.4% to 28.1% (both p < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment for age, educational level, smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity, sex-stratified logistic regression analyses showed that NWCO was associated with diabetes among men (OR = 4.19; 95% CI, 1.13–15.54), although this estimate was imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously given the wide confidence interval and limited subgroup event counts. In women, NWCO was significantly associated with hypertension (OR=2.20; 95% CI, 1.27–3.80) and hypercholesterolemia (OR=2.43; 95% CI, 1.06–5.59). In individuals who were overweight or had central obesity, the risks of hypertension and diabetes were found to be significantly increased in both men and women. In a separate model-based analysis, cross-sectional PAF estimates were calculated to explore the potential population-level relevance of statistically significant associations. Model-based cross-sectional PAF analyses suggested that NWCO was statistically associated with 13.4% of the observed hypertension burden and 15.6% of the observed hypercholesterolemia burden in women under theoretical causal assumptions. However, formal interaction testing did not demonstrate statistically significant effect modification by sex for the association between BMI–central obesity phenotype and hypertension, diabetes, or hypercholesterolemia (all interaction p values > 0.05). Discussion

Using nationally representative Türkiye STEPS 2023 data, this study evaluated the prevalence of central obesity in Türkiye and its associations with chronic disease risk among individuals with normal-weight central obesity. The results indicate that central obesity is highly prevalent in the Turkish population aged 15 years and older, with an estimated prevalence of 62%. In addition, the detection of central obesity in 25.2% of individuals with normal BMI and in 8.8% of the overall population indicates the presence of a NWCO group of considerable size in Turkish society. Although differences were observed in analyses by sex, increased frequencies of hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia were found in the NWCO group. Together, these findings suggest that abdominal adiposity may confer meaningful cardiometabolic risk even among individuals with a normal BMI, supporting the clinical relevance of the NWCO phenotype. Accordingly, the principal contribution of this study lies in providing contemporary Türkiye-specific estimates that may inform national surveillance, clinical awareness, and population-level risk assessment.

Reported NWCO prevalence varies widely across studies, ranging from 5% to 45% depending on the definitions used and the populations studied [18]. In a large-scale analysis covering adult populations from different geographies using STEPS data, Ahmed et al. reported the prevalence of central obesity among individuals with normal BMI as 21.7% [19]. In this context, the 25.2% prevalence observed in our study indicates that a substantial proportion of individuals with normal BMI in Türkiye have central obesity. NWCO is increasingly recognized as a distinct cardiometabolic phenotype, highlighting that individuals with normal BMI may still have unfavorable abdominal fat distribution and elevated metabolic risk. This underscores the limitations of relying on BMI alone to assess cardiometabolic risk. The determinant role of risk factors defined for general obesity phenotypes, such as lack of physical activity, consumption of refined carbohydrates, sedentary life, and low educational level, in NWCO is still controversial in the literature [19–23]. Indeed, in our study, most measured behavioral and sociodemographic variables were not significantly associated with NWCO. This may suggest that the factors assessed in the present analysis do not fully explain variation in NWCO status, although null findings should be interpreted cautiously given potential measurement limitations and residual confounding.

In this study, the prevalence of NWCO in the general population was found to be 8.8%, and this rate was approximately two times higher in women compared to men (11.9% vs. 5.7%). This finding is consistent with previous studies showing a higher prevalence of NWCO in women [21, 23, 24]. Potential explanations may include hormonal influences, sex-related differences in fat distribution, and menopausal changes, although these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study. On the other hand, no significant relationship was found between NWCO prevalence and age group, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, or fruit and vegetable consumption. These results suggest that although behavioral factors may be associated with broader obesity phenotypes, they do not clearly distinguish NWCO from other phenotypes in this dataset. The significant associations observed for tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and physical activity were evident when all four phenotypes were analyzed together, but these relationships were not apparent when NWCO was examined separately. This may indicate that NWCO is not fully captured by the behavioral factors measured in this study, or that other unmeasured determinants may contribute to this phenotype.

A significant relationship was found between educational level and NWCO, and it is noteworthy that the highest prevalence of NWCO was observed among university graduates. This finding suggests that higher educational attainment does not necessarily correspond to a more favorable body composition profile. Previous studies have suggested that occupational and lifestyle factors associated with higher educational attainment, including sedentary work patterns, may contribute to abdominal adiposity [22, 23]. In a study conducted in the USA by Choi et al., it was shown that especially sedentary work environments and low physical workload significantly increased the risk of central obesity [25]. However, the present study did not directly assess the mechanisms underlying this association, and the observed pattern may reflect unmeasured socioeconomic, occupational, or lifestyle factors [26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that NWCO may occur across a broad range of socioeconomic and educational groups rather than being confined to traditionally defined high-risk populations. This pattern may support consideration of broader screening approaches rather than limiting NWCO assessment to narrowly defined risk groups.

In our study, cardiometabolic diseases were found to be markedly higher in the NWCO group. Compared with individuals with normal BMI and without central obesity, the prevalence of hypertension in the NWCO group increased by approximately two times (21.3% vs. 11.8%). Similarly, the prevalence of diabetes increased from 4.0% to 10.4%, and the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia rose from 14.4% to 28.1%. These findings are consistent with previous studies. In the global study conducted by Ahmed et al. and in the meta-analysis conducted by Khonsari et al., it was determined that all three diseases were increased in the NWCO group compared with the normal population [18, 19]. Moreover, large-scale studies suggest that the NWCO phenotype is associated not only with these risk factors but also with cardiovascular mortality. For example, in a study by Sahakyan et al., it was shown that NWCO status significantly increased the risk of cardiovascular mortality [6]. Similarly, in the study conducted by Coutinho et al., it was stated that the presence of central obesity may be a stronger indicator than BMI in determining cardiovascular risk [7]. All these findings suggest that accepting individuals within the normal BMI range as healthy may be misleading. These findings support the clinical relevance of NWCO and suggest that this phenotype may warrant consideration in cardiometabolic risk assessment.

Although some differences were observed in sex-stratified point estimates, formal interaction testing did not support statistically significant effect modification by sex; therefore, these apparent differences should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, exploratory sex-stratified analyses suggested potentially differing association patterns across men and women. In a US study, Li et al. found that NWCO in women predicted cardiovascular events and diabetes-related mortality. The same study also suggested an increased diabetes-related risk associated with central obesity [27]. Similarly, in a study conducted by Pluta et al., it was shown that the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia was higher in women with NWCO [28]. Global-scale analyses also report that there is a significant relationship between NWCO and hypertension in women. In the study by Khonsari et al., it was shown that the risk of hypertension increased by approximately 1.25 times in women [18], while in the analysis carried out by Ahmed et al. using STEPS data from different countries, this risk was found to increase by 1.66 times [19]. In our study, NWCO was found to increase the risk of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia in women by more than two times. Among men, NWCO was statistically associated with diabetes in adjusted analyses; however, this estimate was highly imprecise and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. Although prior multinational analyses have also reported an association between NWCO and diabetes in men [19], the wide confidence interval in the present study precludes precise estimation of the magnitude of this association. Confirmation in larger studies with greater statistical power is warranted. Overall, the findings show that abdominal adiposity may play an important role in cardiometabolic risk and that classifications based solely on BMI may be insufficient to reflect individuals’ true metabolic profiles. The PAF analyses conducted to assess the population-level relevance of these risks suggested that NWCO was associated with 13.4% of the observed hypertension burden and 15.6% of the observed hypercholesterolemia burden in women. The corresponding PAF estimate for diabetes in men should be interpreted cautiously given the imprecision of the underlying association estimate. More broadly, the wide confidence intervals surrounding several PAF estimates indicate substantial uncertainty and limit firm conclusions regarding population-level burden. When studies conducted on general central obesity are examined, in a meta-analysis by Wang et al. in China investigating the burden of diabetes in the society, central obesity was evaluated and the PAF was found to be 20.9% [29]. In a cohort study conducted by Cirera et al. in Spain, the PAF value of the presence of central obesity for all-cause mortality in men was found to be 7% [30]. To our knowledge, population-level PAF estimates specifically for NWCO are scarce, suggesting that our findings help address an important gap in the literature. Because NWCO and cardiometabolic outcomes were assessed contemporaneously, these PAF estimates should be interpreted as descriptive model-based cross-sectional estimates rather than causal estimates of preventable disease burden.

Our findings suggest that reliance on BMI alone may be insufficient for cardiometabolic risk assessment in the Turkish population. The literature shows that the indicators used in the detection of central obesity are particularly important in identifying “hidden risks” [11, 31]. For this reason, our findings suggest that incorporating waist circumference measurement alongside BMI in primary health care may improve the identification of individuals with potentially underrecognized cardiometabolic risk. In countries such as Türkiye, where the burden of chronic diseases increases year by year, it is considered that approaches such as waist circumference, which is also accepted by the WHO [8], may be effective in screenings and in the development of protective and preventive health policies for chronic diseases. Through this approach, earlier detection of cardiometabolic diseases in society can be achieved and may inform future preventive strategies pending confirmation in longitudinal studies.

Among the strengths of the study are that it is a secondary analysis of data obtained with the STEPwise methodology standardized by the WHO and that national representativeness for Türkiye was able to be ensured. The use of data obtained through the STEPS methodology increases the comparability of the results with international studies. The use of complex sampling and weighted prevalence estimates enhances the generalizability of the findings to the Turkish population. Sociodemographic and behavioral variables were used together in obtaining the adjusted OR values in the study. Another strength of the study is that it is the first study in Türkiye to perform a PAF analysis specific to NWCO and one of the limited studies in the world.

This study has several limitations. First, some variables in the STEPS dataset—including medication use and behavioral factors—were based on self-report and may therefore be subject to recall error or misclassification. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and reverse causality cannot be excluded, as individuals with established cardiometabolic disease may have changed their lifestyle or lost weight while retaining central adiposity. Third, biochemical parameters such as blood glucose and cholesterol were based on single measurements, which may have introduced outcome misclassification due to within-person biological variability or measurement error. Because a complete-case approach was used, selection bias cannot be excluded if missingness was not completely at random. Within the STEPS framework, biochemical measurements are obtained only from participants completing the biochemical assessment module; therefore, participants with missing biochemical, anthropometric, or covariate data may have differed systematically from those included in the analytic sample, potentially influencing the reported estimates. Adult BMI and waist circumference thresholds were applied across the full sample, including participants aged 15–17 years; however, sensitivity analyses restricted to participants aged 18 years and older yielded materially similar findings, suggesting minimal impact of adolescent inclusion on the reported estimates. Although WHO Europid waist circumference thresholds were used in accordance with international guidance, alternative waist circumference thresholds were not explored in sensitivity analyses. The most appropriate cutoffs for the Turkish population remain uncertain and warrant further validation in nationally representative studies. Additionally, the generalizability of these findings beyond the Turkish population may be limited, as body composition patterns, waist circumference distributions, and cardiometabolic risk profiles may differ across populations. Waist circumference is also an indirect measure of abdominal adiposity and cannot distinguish visceral from subcutaneous fat. In addition, combining overweight and obesity into a single category improved statistical stability but may have reduced the clinical interpretability of phenotype-specific comparisons by obscuring potential differences between overweight and obese individuals. The cross-sectional design also limits causal interpretation of the reported PAF estimates. Some subgroup-specific estimates were imprecise, and non-significant findings in smaller strata may partly reflect limited statistical power. Finally, because multiple statistical comparisons were performed without formal adjustment, borderline significant findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of the potential for type I error.

Future studies may also benefit from incorporating continuous or alternative measures of abdominal adiposity to further explore dose-response relationships beyond phenotype-based classifications.

Conclusions

This study shows that approximately one-quarter of individuals with a normal body mass index in Türkiye have central obesity, corresponding to an overall NWCO prevalence of 8.8%. The findings indicate that individuals with NWCO show significant associations with cardiometabolic diseases. Analyses by sex suggested that NWCO is significantly associated with hypertension and hypercholesterolemia in women, and particularly with diabetes in men.

Model-based cross-sectional PAF analyses suggest that this phenotype may represent a potentially important population-level marker of cardiometabolic burden. These findings suggest that normal BMI alone may not reliably indicate low cardiometabolic risk. Particularly in countries such as Türkiye, where the burden of chronic diseases is steadily increasing, routinely measuring waist circumference alongside BMI in cardiometabolic risk assessment may assist in identifying individuals who are considered healthy based solely on BMI but who carry hidden cardiometabolic risk. This approach may provide useful information for future screening and prevention strategies, pending confirmation in longitudinal studies.
