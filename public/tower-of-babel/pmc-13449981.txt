Protocol for multiplexed quantification of fluorescent biosensor FRET efficiency using barcoded cells
Authors: Jhen-Wei Wu, Yichu Xu, Yun Chen, Jr-Ming Yang, Chuan-Hsiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenetically encoded fluorescent biosensors frequently use Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) between donor and acceptor fluorescent proteins (FPs) as readouts for monitoring molecular activities in live cells. Here, we present a protocol for determining FRET efficiency through the simultaneous imaging of multiple FRET biosensors alongside donor and acceptor FPs using spectrally orthogonal fluorescent cell barcodes. We describe steps for culturing and transfecting cells, acquiring and analyzing images, and calculating FRET efficiency. This approach facilitates simultaneous analysis of multiple FRET biosensors.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Wu et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol describes a method to simultaneously measure FRET efficiency of three CFP-YFP constructs in HeLa cells using multiplexed biosensor barcoding. The constructs include the CytoFAK biosensor2 and two FRET standards with high and low efficiency.1 This approach is scalable to accommodate additional FRET biosensors and adaptable to other cell lines.

Innovation

FRET biosensors exploit changes in energy transfer efficiency between FPs to detect molecular interactions, conformational changes, or biochemical activities in living cells.3,4,5,6 FRET efficiency can be determined in several ways, such as spectral imaging of donor and acceptor, acceptor photobleaching, fluorescence lifetime imaging, and polarization-resolved imaging. A commonly used and convenient readout for genetically encoded biosensors is the acceptor-to-donor emission ratio, but this ratio is not the true FRET efficiency. Accurate determination of FRET efficiency values require correction for donor bleed-through into the acceptor channel, direct excitation of the acceptor by the donor excitation wavelength, and instrument-specific detection efficiencies. This typically relies on sequential imaging of donor-only, acceptor-only, and at least two constructs with different FRET levels under donor or acceptor excitation.7,8,9 This multi-step workflow is laborious and susceptible to variability in imaging conditions over time, which can introduce errors in calculated efficiencies.

We previously developed a cell barcoding strategy for highly multiplexed, simultaneous biosensor imaging. Cells expressing different biosensors are labeled with fluorescent barcode proteins that are spectrally distinct from the biosensors and targeted to specific subcellular compartments. These barcoded populations are then pooled and imaged together, with the unique barcode pattern in each cell identifying which biosensor it expresses.10,11,12,13 Applying this method, donor-only (CFP), acceptor-only (YFP), and CFP–YFP constructs with different FRET efficiencies can be imaged in the same field of view and under identical settings. To ensure that two constructs with different FRET levels are included, we introduced FRET-ON and FRET-OFF, which exhibit high and low FRET efficiencies, respectively.7,8,9 FRET-ON was derived from the CytoFAK biosensor2 by mutating the substrate tyrosine to phenylalanine (Y349F), which prevents FAK phosphorylation and locks the sensor in a high-FRET state. FRET-OFF was derived from the H3K9me3 biosensor14 by first using the nonresponsive W45A version and then deleting the histone H3 domain to remove nuclear localization, yielding a low-FRET, nonresponsive construct.

Although the mathematical basis for calculating FRET efficiency follows established principles, our strategy eliminates the need for sequential acquisition of separate samples. Consequently, FRET efficiencies of multiple biosensors can be determined concurrently, under consistent imaging conditions, reducing variability and substantially improving experimental throughput and reproducibility.

Microscope

For this study, we used a Zeiss AxioObserver equipped with a 780-Quasar confocal module and a 34-channel, high-sensitivity GaAsP spectral detector controlled by Zen software. The protocol requires three excitation lasers: 458 nm, 514 nm, and 633 nm. A motorized stage enables multi-position imaging, while a Definite Focus nosepiece provides rapid autofocus to correct focus drift during time-lapse acquisition.

In general, to accurately separate the emission spectra and determine the subcellular localization of distinct barcoding proteins, the microscope should include: (1) a confocal module, (2) appropriate excitation laser lines, and (3) a spectral detector. Live-cell experiments additionally require an environmental chamber to maintain temperature, CO2, and humidity.

Expected outcomes

For both barcode and biosensor images, individual cells should be well-separated to allow segmentation. In barcode images, individual cells should be assigned a single color (mCherry, mCardinal, or iRFP), and display either a nuclear or cytoplasmic pattern (Figure 2A). During image acquisition, about 20 cells expressing the barcodes and biosensors are typically found in a 40× viewing field. With 30 viewing fields, about 300 cells can be used for analysis.

For calculation of C2/1 and Y2/4, the values of CH2/CH1 for CFP (Equation 1) and CH2/CH4 for YFP (Equation 2) are expected to be highly consistent across cells. In our experience, the coefficient of variation (S.D./mean) is typically less than 2%. Outliers should be discarded from the calculation.

For different FRET constructs (FRET-ON, FRET-OFF, and CytoFAK in this protocol), CH2/CH4 vs. CH1/CH4 values should show a linear relationship (Figure 3). This is expected because the decrease in the donor fluorescence (CH1/CH4) leads to a proportional increase in acceptor fluorescence (CH2/CH4) during energy transfer.

Limitations

This protocol for simultaneous determination of FRET efficiency of multiple biosensors in barcoded HeLa cells is readily adaptable to larger biosensor panels and different cell lines. While the barcodes described here comprise a single fluorescent protein (FP) targeted to either the cytoplasm or nucleus, substantially greater multiplexing capacity can be achieved by using pairs of FPs targeted to additional subcellular locations (e.g., nuclear membrane, plasma membrane).10 However, several considerations are important when applying this method to additional sensors and cell lines.10

First, the transient transfection protocol described here may not be equally effective across all cell lines. Commercial transfection reagents optimized for specific cell lines are available and should be considered. For difficult-to-transfect cells, alternative delivery methods such as lentiviral transduction or electroporation may be required.

Second, this strategy requires a 1:1 stoichiometry of FRET donor and acceptor. While this applies to the majority of genetically encoded fluorescent biosensors with a unimolecular design (where the FRET donor and acceptor are contained within the same polypeptide chain), some applications employ FRET donor and acceptor as separate proteins.17 The strategy described here is not compatible with such bimolecular sensors.

Third, all FRET constructs must use the same donor-acceptor pair. While various CFP and YFP variants have been used as FRET pairs in biosensor construction, biosensors with different FRET pairs cannot be measured simultaneously unless the corresponding CFP-only and YFP-only constructs are included.

Fourth, fluorescent biosensors often vary in sensitivity and dynamic range. Therefore, biosensors should be validated in the specific cell line before experimental use (e.g., by confirming responses to known activators or inhibitors) to ensure reliable reporting of biochemical activities.
