Transition from Population to Coherence-dominated Non-diffusive Thermal Transport
Laurenz Kremeyer, Bradley J. Siwick, Samuel Huberman

Abstract
Deviations from diffusive heat transport in high thermal conductivity crystalline insulators are generally understood within the framework of the phonon Boltzmann Transport Equation. However, for low thermal conductivity materials with large primitive cells or strong anharmonicity, the recently developed Wigner Transport Equation is more appropriate as it includes tunnelling between overlapping phonon bands. In this work, via solutions to the Wigner Transport Equation, we develop a scheme to obtain the dynamics of the phonon populations and coherences as a function of an arbitrary heat source. The approach is applied to predict size effects and dynamical thermal conductivities in CsPbBr 3 and La 2 Zr 2 O 7 using first-principles data as input. We predict significant deviations from the bulk thermal conductivity in these materials at length scales on the order of hundreds of nanometers to a few microns at room temperature, well within the reach of direct observation using current experimental techniques.



I
Introduction

Diffusive heat transport in crystalline insulators occurs when the relevant transport space and/or time scales are much larger than the mean free paths (MFPs) and/or lifetimes of phonons.
As the system size approaches the MFPs, or the timescale associated with the heat source approaches phonon lifetimes, deviations from diffusive transport are expected.
Canonical examples are the reduction in thermal conductivity as the system size decreases
Ju and Goodson 1999
;
Liu and Asheghi 2006
;
Cheaito et al. 2012
;
Scott et al. 2021
(sometimes referred to as the size effect) or as the size of the thermal excitation decreases
Johnson et al. 2013
;
Hu et al. 2015
;
Cuffe et al. 2015
;
Zeng et al. 2015
;
Huberman et al. 2017
.
A more exotic, but unambiguously non-diffusive, behavior is phonon hydrodynamic transport, where momentum conserving phonon scattering processes dominate over resistive or boundary scattering and the spectacular phenomena of second sound can occur
Huberman et al. 2019
;
Jeong et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
;
Kremeyer et al. 2024
.
In these non-diffusive regimes, the phonon Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) is typically used and has been shown to accurately model a range of transport behaviors.
While the BTE is suitable for high thermal conductivity materials, where the phonon linewidths are narrow and interactions are dominated by scattering mechanisms (i.e., phonon-phonon, boundary, defect etc.) with rates
\Gamma_{s}(\bm{q})
that are much less than the phonon frequency
\omega_{s}(\bm{q})
, the BTE fails to capture the contribution of phonon wave-like tunnelling between branches that can occur in low thermal conductivity solids.
Tunnelling-mediated inter-branch phonon transport becomes relevant when phonon linewidths are on the order of the inter-branch spacing (i.e.,
(\Gamma_{s}(\bm{q})+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}(\bm{q}))/2\approx(\omega_{s^{\prime}}(\bm{q})-\omega_{s}(\bm{q}))
). This condition is generally satisfied in anharmonic materials with a large number of atoms in the unit cell, but also for glasses where the Brillouin zone reduces to the gamma point and the same condition applies,
(\Gamma_{s}(\vec{0})+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}(\vec{0}))/2\approx(\omega_{s^{\prime}}(\vec{0})-\omega_{s}(\vec{0}))
.
When such tunnelling is significant, the density matrix describing the phonon system cannot be assumed to be diagonal.
Phonon coherences, not just populations, become important and interference phenomena play an important role in determining transport in this regime.
It should be noted that in all cases discussed here, the vibrational modes do not fall below the Ioffe-Regel limit (
\Gamma_{s}(\bm{q})=\omega_{s}(\bm{q})
).
Recent work derived an expression for thermal conductivity which includes this coherence contribution based on the Wigner Transport Equation (WTE)
Simoncelli et al. 2019
.
Here we generalize these results to investigate the case of non-diffusive transport in the limit where inter-branch tunnelling is significant.
We begin by proposing an extension to the WTE to encompass the effects of a space- and time-dependent heat source, which connects the framework to time-resolved experimental approaches that can be used to test the predictions of this theory. Following the same notation as
Simoncelli et al. 2019
, the WTE is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)+\mathrm{i}\Big[\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}(\bm{q}),\bm{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{R},\bm{q},t)\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}(\bm{q}),\vec{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\bm{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{R},\bm{q},t)\Big\}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{R},{\bm{q}},t)\bigg|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t),

(1)

where
\bm{\mathsf{N}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)
is the generalization of a one-phonon distribution (from a rank 1 to a rank 2 tensor) obtained via Wigner transformation of the time-evolution of the one-phonon density matrix.

\bm{R}
and
t
are spatiotemporal coordinates and
\bm{q}
is the phonon wave vector.
The collision term
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)\big|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}
describes phonon scattering and tunnelling processes.
The diagonal elements,
\mathsf{N}_{s,s}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)
, correspond to the conventional phonon populations of mode
s
described by the BTE and the off-diagonal elements correspond to phonon coherences between modes
s
and
s^{\prime}
.
The tensor
\bm{\mathsf{V}}(\bm{q})
is a generalization of a phonon’s group velocity and
\mathsf{\Omega}_{s,s^{\prime}}(\bm{q})=\omega_{s}(\bm{q})\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}
.
As was done previously when solving the BTE
Chiloyan et al. 2021
, we have added the term
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)
which describes the heat source.
For the purposes of generality,
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)
is a matrix-valued function, where the diagonal terms correspond to the production of phonon populations and the off-diagonal terms allow for the production of phonon coherences.
To solve Eq.
1
, we take the following steps. First, we Fourier transform the expression in space (
\bm{R}
) and time (
t
), denoted by
\mathscr{F}
, to obtain Eq.
1
where
\omega
and
\bm{k}
are the temporal and spatial Fourier variables:

-\mathrm{i}\omega\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}({\bm{k}},{\bm{q}},\omega)+i\Big[\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}(\bm{q}),\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}({\bm{k}},{\bm{q}},\omega)\Big]+\frac{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}}{2}\Big\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}(\bm{q}),\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}({\bm{k}},{\bm{q}},\omega)\Big\}=\mathscr{F}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{q}},t)\bigg|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}\right\}+\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}({\bm{k}},{\bm{q}},\omega).

(2)

In the following, we drop the explicit dependence on
\bm{R},\bm{q},t
or
\bm{k},\bm{q},\omega
, and instead indicate it by the use of a tilde above the respective symbol.
Following the operation of
\mathscr{F}
, spatial and temporal derivatives transform into simple algebraic terms and the WTE now takes the form
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a linear operator combining all terms on the left-hand side and the collision operator on the right-hand side of Eq.
2
.
We will touch on the Fourier-transformed collision term later and discuss different assumptions and models that can be used to describe scattering in the system.
Regardless of the details of the chosen scattering operator, one can introduce a Green’s function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},\omega)
defined by

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}=\bm{\mathsf{I}}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}=\bm{\mathsf{I}}\delta(\bm{R})\delta(t),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathsf{I}_{s,s^{\prime}}=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}.

(3)

The Green’s function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
encodes how the phonon system responds to an impulsive excitation that is localized in space and time.
Once
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
is obtained by inversion of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
, the solution for an arbitrary heat source
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}
can be directly computed as
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}
for the pair of
(\bm{k},\omega)
for which
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
was inverted.
Computing the system’s Green’s function for a range of frequencies
\omega
at a specific
\bm{k}
provides a frequency-response picture of the system and allows us to recover the Wigner distribution at fixed wavevector
\bm{k}
via the inverse Fourier transform in time

\bm{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},\omega).

From the real-time Wigner distribution of the system, many experimentally accessible observables can be computed.
Pump-probe experiments are the first that come to mind, since they involve an ultrashort excitation of the studied system that very closely resembles the underlying Dirac distribution
\delta(t)
of the Green’s function approach.
Thus, the frequency-domain Green’s function offers an intuitive, quantitative connection between this theory and time-resolved pump-probe data.

Explicitly calculating the inverse operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
to obtain
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
can be computationally very expensive—especially for systems with complex unit cells.
It is faster to use solvers that do not explicitly compute the full Green’s function while still solving a system of the form
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}
, with the drawback that the system is only solved for a specific source term.
Accordingly, if responses are needed for many different sources, forming
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
can be more efficient overall; if only a few sources are of interest, solving
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}
per source is preferable.
The calculations presented here were performed using a mix of both approaches, as the code implementation supports easy switching between methods.

II
Theory

We first discuss the theory of calculating dynamical thermal conductivities and then move on to solving the WTE for arbitrary source terms.
Throughout this work, we invoke small deviations from equilibrium and linearize the local-equilibrium distribution around a reference temperature
T_{0}
, which is the system base-temperature before photoexcitation in a pump-probe experiment.
To first order, this means

\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{l}}=\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{0}}\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\omega)+\delta\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}},

(4)

\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{0}}\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\omega)+\delta\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}},

(5)

with the diagonal matrix
\delta\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}}{\partial T}\big|_{T_{0}}\Delta T
with its non-zero elements
\delta\tilde{\bar{\mathsf{N}}}_{s,s}=\frac{\mathcal{V}N_{\mathrm{C}}}{\hbar\omega_{s}}c_{s}\Delta T
.
The delta-distributions arise from the Fourier transform of the time- and space-independent equilibrium distribution.
All material parameters have been calculated only at the background temperature
T_{0}
.
It is assumed that those do not change significantly for
\Delta T\ll T_{0}
and that
\delta\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\ll\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{0}}\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\omega)
.

II.1
Dynamical Thermal Conductivity

For the collision term we choose a closed-form expression in the relaxation-time approximation (RTA)
Callaway 1959
;
Allen 2013
that follows from the linearization about the equilibrium temperature
T_{0}
and is expressed in terms of one-body quantities as

\begin{split}\mathscr{F}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{q})\bigg|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}\right\}{=}-(1-\delta_{s,s^{\prime}})\frac{\Gamma_{s}+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}}{2}\tilde{\mathsf{N}}_{s,s^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\\
-\frac{\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{V}N_{\mathrm{C}}}{\sum\limits_{{s^{\prime\prime}}{\bm{q}^{\prime\prime}}}}\mathsf{A}^{T_{0}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q},{\bm{q}^{\prime\prime}})\left(\tilde{\mathsf{N}}_{{s^{\prime\prime}},{s^{\prime\prime}}}(\bm{q}^{\prime\prime})-\mathscr{F}\left\{\bar{\mathsf{N}}^{T_{l}}_{s^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q}^{\prime\prime})\right\}\right),\end{split}

(6)

where
\mathcal{V}
is the unit cell volume,
N_{\mathrm{C}}
is the number of unit cells,
\mathscr{F}\left\{\bar{\mathsf{N}}^{T_{l}}_{s}({\bm{q}})\right\}{=}\mathscr{F}\left\{\left({\exp\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}(\bm{q})}{k_{B}T_{l}(\bm{R},t)}\right]{-}1}\right)^{-1}\right\}
is the Fourier transform of the Bose-Einstein distribution at local temperature
T_{l}(\bm{R},t)
,
\Gamma_{s}(\bm{q})=(\mathcal{V}N_{\mathrm{C}})^{-1}\mathsf{A}^{T_{0}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{q})\delta_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}
is the phonon linewidth and
\mathsf{A}^{T_{0}}_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime\prime})
is the standard collision operator that accounts for anharmonicity and isotopic disorder
Fugallo et al. 2013
;
Cepellotti and Marzari 2016
.
One can see that the collision operator does not couple populations to coherences.
Additionally, note that the collision term only annihilates coherences, while it both creates and annihilates populations.
Coherences are only created by the off-diagonal entries of the generalized velocity operator
\bm{\mathsf{V}}
.
In the RTA, the collision term can be further simplified to

\mathscr{F}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}\bigg|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}\right\}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{l}}\right\},

(7)

with
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{s,s^{\prime}}=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}\Gamma_{s}
, where the first term damps the non-equilibrium Wigner distribution, while the second term ensures relaxation towards the
local
equilibrium at temperature
T_{l}
.
Away from the zero-mode, for any
(|\bm{k}|,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
, we can simplify to

\mathscr{F}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\mathsf{N}}\bigg|_{\mathrm{H}^{\mathrm{col}}}\right\}=-\frac{\Gamma_{s}+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}}{2}\,\tilde{\mathsf{N}}_{s,s^{\prime}}+\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}\,\Gamma_{s}\delta\tilde{\bar{\mathsf{N}}}_{s,s},

(8)

To compute the dynamical thermal conductivity as a function of
\bm{k}
and
\omega
, we study the linear response to a harmonic temperature field

T_{l}(\bm{R},t)=T_{0}+\Delta T\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}((k\,\bm{e}_{\beta})\cdot\bm{R}-\omega t)}\quad\text{with}\quad|\Delta T|\ll T_{0}.

(9)

Greek indices
\alpha,\beta\in\{x,y,z\}
denote Cartesian components of vectors and tensors.
We drive the system with a spatial harmonic along the direction
\beta
, i.e.
\bm{k}=k\bm{e}_{\beta}
.

We must choose a source term that (i) produces a response proportional to
\Delta T
, (ii) does not inject net power, and (iii) vanishes for a uniform
\Delta T
(i.e.,
\bm{k}=0
).
A convenient choice that meets all three requirements is

\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}_{\nabla T}^{\beta}=\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{2}\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{\beta},\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{0}}\}\Delta T-\delta\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}.

(10)

The first term is the drift term of the local-equilibrium distribution under a gradient, i.e., the generalization of the Peierls-Boltzmann drift term, and the second term cancels the inhomogeneous part of the scattering operator in the RTA.
First, the chosen
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}_{\nabla T}
is proportional to
\Delta T
and by linearity of the WTE the response is also proportional to
\Delta T
.
Second, the second term of the source cancels the
\delta\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
term in the RTA scattering operator; and since
\sum_{\bm{q}}\Tr\left[\hbar\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}^{\beta}\right]=0
, no net power is injected.
Last, the net source, after cancellation with the RTA term, vanishes for
\bm{k}=0
and we recover the expected equilibrium solution without drive.
Plugging Eq.
8
and
10
into Eq.
2
yields

\begin{split}-\mathrm{i}\omega\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}+\mathrm{i}\left[\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right]+\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{\beta},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\}\\
=\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{2}\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{\beta},\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{0}}\}\Delta T\end{split}

(11)

From the resulting distribution
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}(\bm{k},\omega)
we can calculate the thermal conductivity as

\kappa^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\left[\frac{\sum_{\bm{q}}\Tr\left[\{\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}(\bm{q}),\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{\alpha}(\bm{q})\}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}^{\beta}(k,\omega,\bm{q})\right]}{\frac{2\mathcal{V}N_{\mathrm{C}}}{\hbar}\,\mathrm{i}k\Delta T}\right].

(12)

Note that by choice of the gradient source term
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}_{\nabla T}
, we constructed a purely linear, inhomogeneous system with a known right-hand side.
The temperature field
T_{l}
does not need to be determined self-consistently, as it is an input to the problem.
Without loss of generality, we can choose an arbitrary
\Delta T
and directly obtain the thermal conductivity.

II.2
Arbitrary Source Terms

The key difference in solving the WTE for arbitrary source terms is that net power can be injected into the system.
Plugging Eq.
8
into Eq.
2
gives the WTE in the RTA for arbitrary source terms

\begin{split}-\mathrm{i}\omega\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}+\mathrm{i}\left[\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right]+\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{\beta},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\}\\
=\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\bar{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}^{T_{l}}\right\}.\end{split}

(13)

Consequently, the local temperature
T_{l}(\bm{R},t)
is no longer an input and we solve for it self-consistently to enforce energy conservation via

\sum_{{s}{\bm{q}}}\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{\mathcal{V}N_{\mathrm{C}}}\left(\tilde{\mathsf{N}}_{s,s}(\bm{q})-\delta(\omega)\delta(\bm{k})\bar{\mathsf{N}}^{T_{0}}_{s}({\bm{q}})\right)=C\Delta T(\bm{k},\omega),

(14)

with the total heat capacity
C=\sum_{s,\bm{q}}c_{s}(\bm{q})
.
More details on the implementation can be found in Appendix
B
.

III
Numerical Results

As a benchmark, we start by calculating static (
\omega=0
) thermal conductivities of silicon, CsPbBr
3
and La
2
Zr
2
O
7
using the gradient source term
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}_{\nabla T}
from Eq.
10
in the macroscopic regime with a macroscopic grating period, before turning to dynamical thermal conductivity and microscopic grating sizes that simulate, for example, geometries of transient thermal grating experiments.
Note that all thermal conductivities are reported along the thermal grating.
We use the same unit cell convention as
Simoncelli et al. 2022
and report
\kappa=\kappa^{xx}
along the direction of the shortest unit cell axis in CsPbBr
3
.
The results are shown in Fig.
1
a.
In silicon, we observe that contributions from coherences are negligible compared to populations and the thermal conductivity is in good agreement with literature.
Inter-branch degeneracies are rare and the velocity operator is close to diagonal in the phonon eigenbasis, so the anticommutator drive creates a mostly diagonal response.
Off-diagonal coherences dephase rapidly due to the commutator term
\mathrm{i}[\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}},\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
at splittings
\Delta\omega_{ss^{\prime}}
that far exceed the damping rate
\frac{\Gamma_{s}+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}}{2}
.
In CsPbBr
3
and La
2
Zr
2
O
7
, by contrast, dense clusters of flat optical branches create many quasi-degenerate pairs
(s,s^{\prime})
with small
\Delta\omega_{ss^{\prime}}
.
The source’s anticommutator directly pumps coherences, and the RTA damping competes less effectively when
\Delta\omega_{ss^{\prime}}\sim\frac{\Gamma_{s}+\Gamma_{s^{\prime}}}{2}
.
Therefore, the coherence contribution is significant and even becomes the dominant contribution to the thermal conductivity above 240 and 800 K, respectively.
The total thermal conductivity exactly matches the results obtained from
phono3py

Caldarelli et al. 2022
and the good experimental agreement of those results has been previously discussed.
In La
2
Zr
2
O
7
, the contributions from populations and coherences are also in good agreement with previous results; however, in CsPbBr
3
we observe a minor deviation, with a larger contribution from coherences and a smaller contribution from populations across the whole temperature range
Simoncelli et al. 2019
;
Caldarelli et al. 2022
.

Further, we can study size effects by varying the grating period
\Lambda=2\pi/k
.
Finite
\bm{k}
filters carriers by mean free path: modes with
\Lambda_{\mathrm{mfp}}\gg\Lambda
cannot support the spatial modulation and are suppressed by the convective term
\tfrac{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}}{2}\left\{\bm{\mathsf{V}},\cdot\right\}
, even at
\omega=0
.

In La
2
Zr
2
O
7
, populations and coherences both contribute significantly to the thermal conductivity in the bulk.
The population contribution mainly stems from a few long mean free path acoustic phonon modes close to the Brillouin zone center in the static case.
As the grating period is reduced, these modes are suppressed and the population contribution to the thermal conductivity drops quickly, starting at around 10 µm.
The coherence contribution is more evenly distributed across the phonon spectrum; many more modes contribute and their effective mean free paths are shorter.
As a result, the onset of the suppression of the coherences via size-effect filtering does not start until around 50 nm.
The results for room temperature and 700 K are shown in Fig.
1
b.
Note that the onset of the size effect shifts to smaller grating periods at higher temperatures as phonon mean free paths decrease with increasing temperature.
In CsPbBr
3
, the phonon mean free paths of the zone-center acoustic modes are only an order of magnitude larger than the mean free paths of higher-frequency modes
Simoncelli et al. 2022
.
Size effects start to appear on the scale of 50 nm for both populations and coherences.
With extreme ultraviolet thermal grating techniques reaching grating periods in the sub-50 nm regime, these size effects are experimentally accessible
Bencivenga et al. 2015
;
Chergui et al. 2023
.

Having investigated thermal conductivities and size effects under static conditions, we now move on to dynamical thermal conductivities.
From molecular dynamics simulations
Volz 2001
and linearized BTE calculations
Chaput 2013
, it is understood that the population thermal conductivity is strongly suppressed as the excitation frequency approaches the material’s phonon lifetimes.

Fig.
1
c shows the dynamical thermal conductivity of La
2
Zr
2
O
7
at 500 K for two different grating periods.
For the bulk grating period of 63 µm, we observe that the real part of the thermal conductivity starts to decrease at around 1 GHz, which coincides with the onset of a peak in the imaginary part.
The peak marks a
\pi/2
phase lag of the heat flux behind the drive and occurs where
\omega
matches the effective relaxation rate of the dominant heat-carrying phonons.

To quantify the character of the thermal conductivity, we define a dimensionless parameter

\chi=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{C}}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{C}}},

(15)

which we call the transport character.
Fully population-dominated transport
\chi
takes a value of 1, fully coherence-dominated transport
-
1 and 0 for equal contributions from populations and coherences.
Fig.
2

Zr
2

.
The thick black contour indicates equal contributions from populations and coherences.
One can see that coherences dominate the thermal conductivity at experimentally accessible grating periods on the micron scale at moderately elevated temperatures.
A second cut through the
\chi(T,k,\omega)

2

The intersecting line of the two planes in Fig.

As is seen in the BTE, increasing

Coherence channels—fed by many near-degenerate optical pairs with finite

IV
Conclusion

We developed a frequency-domain formulation of the phonon Wigner transport equation that incorporates general space-time sources and yields a linear problem.
In this framework, a gradient-type drive produces the dynamical thermal conductivity
\kappa(\bm{k},\omega)
without requiring a self-consistent temperature field, while arbitrary sources are accommodated through an energy-conserving closure.
We implemented two numerical strategies to solve the resulting linear problem, based on either direct inversion or iterative solvers.
Applied to CsPbBr
3
and La
2
Zr
2
O
7
, our framework predicts static and dynamical thermal conductivities in addition to size effects. Based on our calculations, we predict deviations from bulk diffusivities at experimentally accessible length and time scales. The decomposition of the thermal conductivity into populations and coherences across the Brillouin zone reveals that the character of thermal transport within a phonon branch (i.e. population dominated or coherence dominated) does not depend on the grating period or drive frequency.

Beyond this work, explicit Green’s function inversion enables systematic studies of system responses to a variety of source terms and to different forms of the collision operator. For instance, it would be of interest to consider a collision operator that is not restricted to the annihilation of coherences. Additionally, specific parts of the Brillouin zone can be targeted, where the results of electron-phonon coupling studies can be used to construct source terms that mimic the effect of ultrafast laser pulses.
