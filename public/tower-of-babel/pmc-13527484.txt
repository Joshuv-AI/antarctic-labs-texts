Extra-articular rheumatoid arthritis: example of RA-associated interstitial lung disease
Authors: Jeffrey A. Sparks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease that primarily affects the joints. However, extra-articular manifestations are common and can be serious. These include cutaneous rheumatoid nodules, Sjögren's disease, vasculitis, Felty syndrome, and pulmonary manifestations such as interstitial lung disease (ILD), bronchiectasis, obliterative bronchiolitis, nodules, and pleural effusions. The prevalence of most extra-articular RA manifestations has decreased over the past few decades with expanded treatment options, with the notable exception of RA-ILD. Clinically apparent RA-ILD affects about 10% of people with RA and is characterised by pulmonary fibrosis and/or inflammation. RA-ILD is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Since screening for ILD is not routinely performed, an additional 10% to 30% of people with RA may have subclinical ILD characterised by chest imaging abnormalities without symptoms that do not reach clinical attention. Research efforts are ongoing to identify risk factors and optimise screening strategies to identify early RA-ILD. Biomarkers for RA-ILD risk and progression may inform pathogenesis and prevention strategies. Treatment of active RA with anti-inflammatory medications may also treat ILD. Several antifibrotics may also slow the progression of ILD. However, more trials are needed to optimise the treatment of RA-ILD and improve outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Although rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterised by a destructive polyarthritis, this is a systemic disorder that can affect almost any organ system [1]. Although most RA therapies have focused on articular outcomes, there has been a decline in extra-articular manifestations of RA over calendar time (particularly cutaneous rheumatoid nodules), likely due to expanded treatment options and improved RA disease control [2]. A notable exception to this improvement is RA-related lung disease, which includes interstitial lung disease (ILD), bronchiectasis, obliterative bronchiolitis, pleural effusions, and pulmonary nodules [3]. This Advances in Targeted Therapies topic will focus on RA-ILD as one of the few RA-related outcomes not improving even with expanded treatment options.

RA-ILD EPIDEMIOLOGY

A recent large meta-analysis reported that the pooled prevalence of ILD in RA is 11% [4]. This contrasts with higher prevalences of ILD in systemic sclerosis (SSc; 47%) and idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM; 41%) [4]. However, most rheumatologists screen for ILD in people with SSc and IIM, unlike for RA. Considering undiagnosed and subclinical ILD and the larger denominator of people with RA, this condition may rival the prevalence of ILD in SSc and IIM. Another population-based retrospective study reported that 15.3% (about 1 in 8) of patients with RA will develop ILD by 20 years of disease duration [5]. This study also showed that people with RA can develop ILD before articular onset and during early RA, contrary to previous notions where ILD only develops in undertreated RA of long duration [5].

The prevalence of RA-ILD also seems to be increasing over calendar time. A large Danish study showed that the prevalence of RA-ILD has increased nearly 2-fold from 2004 to 2016 [6]. The reasons for this are likely multifactorial, related to increased awareness, wider availability of advanced chest imaging, improvements in mortality, and perhaps due to factors such as specific disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs).

RA has a predilection for usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) on chest computed tomography (CT) imaging. UIP and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis have indistinguishable imaging and pathologic characteristics and are both clinically characterised by progressive pulmonary fibrosis with poor outcomes. In a meta-analysis, about 50% of patients with RA-ILD had the UIP pattern [4]. This is in contrast to SSc-ILD and IIM-ILD, of which about 70% of patients have the nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) pattern characterised by inflammation [4]. The RA-specific factors that lead to UIP are currently being investigated.

RA-ILD OUTCOMES

Patients with RA have about a 2-fold increased risk for respiratory mortality, which is more pronounced for seropositive RA and for ILD-related mortality [7,8]. Although total, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality are all improving over calendar time, respiratory and infection mortality has not improved even with the advent of expanded treatment options [8]. An early study showed that the median survival of RA-ILD was only 2.6 years, though many may have been diagnosed later in their ILD course [9]. A more contemporary study showed that survival after RA-ILD is now about 8 years, but the hazard ratio for mortality remains 2 to 3-fold higher than RA without ILD [5,10].

Patients with RA-ILD and the UIP pattern have worse survival compared with those with the NSIP pattern in several studies [11,12]. A recent prediction model showed that UIP pattern, low diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide, high-titre cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP), high Krebs von den Lungen-6 (a pulmonary damage biomarker), and great extent of ILD were all predictive of progressive pulmonary fibrosis in RA-ILD [13].

RA-ILD RISK FACTORS

There has been major progress in identifying risk factors for ILD in RA. This includes older age, male sex [14], smoking, obesity, the MUC5B promoter variant, and RA characteristics such as rheumatoid factor, CCP, and high disease activity [15,16]. The MUC5B promoter variant is the only currently identified genetic risk factor for RA and is specifically strongly associated with the UIP pattern as well as older age of RA onset and earlier onset of ILD [17,18]. Of note, methotrexate has not been associated with a higher risk of ILD in RA [19], though it can rarely cause drug-induced hypersensitivity pneumonitis [19].

SCREENING FOR RA-ILD

Recent guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) recommend screening patients with RA for ILD if they have risk factors [20,21]. However, this was based on expert consensus, and they may lack specificity. A risk score for subclinical ILD in RA composed of age, sex, disease activity, and the MUC5B promoter variant demonstrated accuracy and ability to stratify [22]. The multicentre Study of Inflammatory Arthritis in Early RA (SAIL-RA) study also performed external validation of several proposed screening strategies in early RA (<2 years duration) and demonstrated accuracy and efficiency [23]. The international ANCHOR-RA study is ongoing with a goal of developing a clinical model to screen for ILD in RA [24]. There are also efforts to use quantitative assessment of chest CT imaging to screen and monitor for RA-ILD as well as identify novel cellular biomarkers [15,25].

RA-ILD TREATMENT

Recent guidelines also addressed treatment for RA-ILD. However, much of this was based on observational data, expert opinion, or extrapolated from trials in other diseases such as SSc-ILD. Only 4 trials have randomised a total of 335 patients with RA-ILD [26]. The ACR/CHEST guideline recommends mycophenolate, azathioprine, and rituximab as first-line options with consideration for cyclophosphamide for severe disease [27]. They recommend nintedanib, tocilizumab, and pirfenidone as additional options for people with progression [27]. In contrast, the ERS/EULAR guideline first recommends treatment of active articular RA with any DMARD. If ILD is still active, they also recommend Janus kinase inhibitors and abatacept as options, while also considering antifibrotics for progressive pulmonary fibrosis and/or UIP pattern [21]. After the guidelines for treatment of RA-ILD were published, favourable results for nerandomilast were released, which may be an option for patients with RA-ILD with progressive pulmonary fibrosis [28].

CONCLUSIONS

Extra-articular RA manifestations are common but decreasing with expanded treatment options. However, the prevalence of RA-ILD is increasing and is one of the few outcomes not improving in the biologic era of expanded treatment options. RA-ILD is heterogeneous with a variety of patterns and propensity for progression. However, the UIP pattern is most common and has the poorest prognosis. Progress is being made in identifying risk factors for RA-ILD. Some subgroups of patients with RA may benefit from screening. Recent guidelines have been released for screening, monitoring, and treatment. Given the rising prevalence of ILD in RA, more work is needed to develop accurate screening strategies. Trials are needed to understand optimal treatment approaches for RA-ILD.
