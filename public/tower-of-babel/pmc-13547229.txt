Music–taste interactions enhance gustatory and sensorimotor brain activity
Authors: Alberte B. Seeberg, Ana Teresa Lourenço Queiroga, Patricia Alves Da Mota, Henrique Miguel Fernandes, Peter Vuust, Paul Smeets, Qian Janice Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eating is one of the most fundamental multisensory experiences, yet the neural mechanisms that integrate auditory and gustatory inputs in humans remain underexplored. This study examined how music influences the brain’s encoding of taste. We collected functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and behavioural data from 28 participants across three conditions: taste only, music only, and music–taste combinations. In the scanner, participants received sweet and sour aqueous solutions, accompanied by musical soundtracks composed to convey sweetness or sourness. In a behavioural session, participants rated each stimulus or combination for pleasantness, intensity, and sound–taste congruency. fMRI results revealed that sweet and sour music and taste stimuli engaged overlapping anatomical regions, particularly within peri-rolandic and medial prefrontal areas. Although voxel-wise conjunction did not identify suprathreshold overlap for sweet stimuli, regional co-localisation was observed in gustatory and reward-related regions, including the rolandic operculum, postcentral gyrus, and gyrus rectus. For sour stimuli, a small but significant voxel-wise conjunction emerged in the right postcentral gyrus. Crucially, concurrent music–taste stimulation elicited stronger activation in gustatory and sensorimotor cortices compared to taste alone, indicating multisensory enhancement. Direct contrasts between congruent and incongruent pairings revealed limited but selective congruency effects: when sweet taste was paired with congruent versus incongruent music, increased activation was observed in bilateral superior temporal gyri and rolandic operculum. Overall, these findings suggest that neural enhancement was primarily driven by the presence of simultaneous multimodal input, with additional modulation under specific congruent pairings. Behaviourally, participants perceived congruent music–taste pairings as more congruent and more pleasant, with modest trends toward increased taste intensity in the presence of music. These findings provide novel insight into the neural basis of sound–taste interactions and contribute to our understanding of how auditory cues can shape flavour perception through multisensory integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53065-7.

Introduction

To survive as both individuals and a species, we must eat and reproduce. Our sensory system supports these fundamental behaviours by identifying, evaluating, and predicting environmental stimuli1. To optimise information processing, the brain integrates input from multiple sensory modalities2. This principle of multisensory integration is well-illustrated in the experience of flavour, a complex synthesis of taste, smell, the trigeminal system, touch, vision, and audition. Flavour, then, can be understood as a multisensory system for nutrient prediction and evaluation3.

While the crossmodal effect of vision and smell on taste perception is well studied4, 5, a growing body of empirical research has demonstrated that what we hear can also influence how we experience taste6, 7. For example, studies have demonstrated that artificially amplifying the chewing sound of potato crisps and apples can increase the perception of crispiness8, 9. Beyond eating sounds, environmental noise can alter sensitivity to sweetness and saltiness 10, 11 and liking (or disliking) for background music can also influence the taste of foods12, 13.

In addition, crossmodal correspondences between auditory and gustatory stimuli are now well established, showing that specific acoustic features systematically evoke specific taste qualities14, 15. For instance, high-pitched, legato, and consonant sounds tend to be associated with sweetness, while lower-pitched, dissonant, and percussive sounds are more often linked with sourness or bitterness.

Building on this work, the phenomenon of sonic seasoning has been introduced to describe how music or soundscapes can be intentionally composed or selected to correspond with, and subsequently modify, the perceived taste, aroma, mouthfeel, or flavour of foods and beverages16, 7, 17. For example, listening to soundtracks with auditory attributes that correspond to sweetness18 can also alter the sweetness level of the food tasted19. Furthermore, especially composed or selected soundscapes have also been shown to influence ratings of bitterness16,17, sourness10, crunchiness20, food choice21, odour pleasantness22, odour sensitivity23, and odour discrimination24.

Beyond ample behavioural evidence detailed above, it remains unclear how music changes the perception of taste. While food is a primary reward, music is also known to elicit reward-related responses in the brain25. Brain areas such as the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) are associated with both food and music listening pleasure cycles26. A recent meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies on food- and music-induced pleasure identified specific brain areas that were consistently engaged when participants experienced pleasure from food and/or music27. The results showed a common reward system involving the ventral striatum, the insula, and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Furthermore, the authors also found reward-specific (food or music) brain activations, with the left insula, bilateral putamen and right amygdala showing higher activation in response to food compared to music-induced pleasure. On the other hand, the right ventromedial striatum, right superior temporal gyrus and the anterior prefrontal cortex were found to have higher activation during/in response to music compared to food-induced pleasure. Beyond pleasure, the OFC is also known to serve as a crucial site for convergence28, 29 when it comes to the integration of taste and smell30. This role in processing multisensory reward suggests a shared substrate where different stimuli, including auditory cues, could modulate the integrated olfactory-gustatory signals that form our perception of flavour28.

Furthermore, recent neuroimaging studies have revealed that contextual cues (albeit non-sound based) can modulate sensory processing at both early and late stages of information processing, via cognitive processes such as attention, expectation, or mental imagery31, 32. For instance, participants who were told to expect a very sweet drink when given a less sweet drink showed greater taste cortex activation, compared to those who received the same drink without this expectation20. Furthermore, reading the word salt has been shown to activate many of the same brain areas as the actual taste of salt33. Avery et al.34 found that the gustatory cortex was activated both when participants were tasting a liquid or viewing pictures of food. These examples demonstrate the extent to which gustatory-related brain regions can be modulated by cognitive and contextual inputs. Yet, despite this growing evidence, relatively little is known about how auditory cues, particularly those involving sound-taste congruency, might engage similar mechanisms in the brain.

Neuroscientists have begun to investigate the role of congruency between music and food. For example, Callan et al.35 demonstrated preliminary evidence of how music modulates the neural mechanisms underlying the processing of taste-specific food imagery. They found that playing sweet music resulted in higher activation of the posterior insula when participants were looking at sweet dessert pictures, compared to looking at pictures of savoury meat dishes. However, this study still relied on mental imagery rather than real taste experience.

In the present study, we investigated the neural underpinnings of crossmodal interactions between taste and sound using actual taste and sound stimuli during functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI). Participants experienced sweet and sour tastes and their corresponding soundtracks, presented both separately (sound only, taste only) and in combination. Sweet and sour were selected because they represent two taste qualities with well-established yet contrasting auditory correspondences, which makes them particularly suitable for examining sound–taste integration14, 36.

The study was driven by two main hypotheses. First, we expected that auditory stimuli associated with sweetness or sourness would elicit behavioural correspondences and activate taste-related brain regions even in the absence of gustatory input, as previous studies have shown that mental imagery and semantic information can engage corresponding sensory pathways (e.g.,33). Second, we hypothesized that congruent sound–taste combinations would result in increased activation in gustatory cortices and enhance perceived taste intensity and pleasantness, especially for positively valenced sweet stimuli, consistent with behavioural evidence that matching auditory cues can amplify flavour perception and hedonic experience19, 17.

Results

Manipulation check

Can people associate soundtracks with basic tastes?

First, we verified that participants accurately matched the sweet and sour soundtracks with the corresponding sweet and sour tastants. According to the soundtrack evaluations (Fig. 1A), the sweet soundtrack was strongly associated with sweetness (p < .001 for all comparisons), whereas the sour soundtrack was linked primarily to sourness and bitterness (p < .001 for all comparisons, see Appendix D for more detail). The association for the sour soundtracks to evoke both sourness and bitterness is consistent with previous work18, illustrating the common confusion between the two taste qualities37.

Beyond semantic associations, sound-taste congruency ratings from the tasting trials revealed that the purely sweet tastant was rated as more congruent with the sweet soundtrack than the sour soundtrack, and the purely sour tastant was rated as more congruent with the sour soundtrack compared to the sweet (Fig. 1B). In fact, sound-taste congruencies matched ratios of sucrose and citric acid in the tastants, with the 75/25 solution more congruent with the sweet soundtrack and 25/75 solution more congruent with the sour soundtrack (p < .001 for all comparisons, see Appendix D for more detail). It was only the 50/50 solution that was rated as equally congruent with both soundtracks.

Music-taste overlap

Does listening to sweet or sour soundtracks activate corresponding taste areas in the brain?

To assess whether congruent music and taste stimuli engaged shared neural substrates, we performed voxel-wise conjunction analyses between sweet music (MSw) and sweet taste (TSw), and between sour music (MSo) and sour taste (TSo). Conjunctions were defined as voxels that survived cluster-corrected thresholding (Z > 3.1, p < .05) in both contrasts. In addition, regional overlap was examined descriptively at the anatomical level using the automated anatomical atlas 3(AAL3)38 labelling, considering the full spatial extent of significant clusters rather than only peak coordinates.

Sweet music and sweet taste

The voxel-wise conjunction analysis between MSw and TSw revealed no overlapping suprathreshold voxels at the cluster-corrected threshold (Z > 3.1, cluster-corrected p < .05). Thus, no significant shared activation was observed at the voxel level for sweet music and sweet taste.

Although no voxel-wise conjunction clusters were detected, both contrasts independently engaged similar anatomical regions, including the right and left postcentral gyrus, right precentral gyri, left gyrus rectus, and left ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Fig. 2; Table 1). This pattern reflects regional co-localization of suprathreshold activation but did not survive voxel-wise conjunction criteria. Additionally, activation of the rolandic operculum was seen in both conditions, though not in the same hemisphere (L in MSw, R in TSw; see Table 1).

Sour music and sour taste

The voxel-wise conjunction analysis between sour music (MSo) and sour taste (TSo) revealed a single overlapping cluster comprising three voxels (peak Z = 11.7; Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinates: 60, −14, 36; located in the right postcentral gyrus). This cluster survived the cluster-corrected threshold (Z > 3.1, p < .05), indicating spatially restricted but significant shared suprathreshold activation between sour music and sour taste conditions.

In addition to the postcentral gyrus in the right hemisphere, descriptive regional overlap activations between MSo and TSo (each contrasted against an implicit baseline) further indicated overlapping suprathreshold activation in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, right supramarginal gyrus, and left gyrus rectus (Fig. 3; Table 2).

Music-taste interactions

How does congruent sound plus taste experiences compare to taste alone?

The effects of combining music and taste are shown in Fig. 4; Table 3, highlighting brain regions that exhibited greater activation during concurrent auditory–gustatory stimulation compared to taste alone. For the sweet condition (MSwTSw > TSw; Fig. 4A), increased activation was observed in multiple regions, with cluster peak activations in the right precentral gyrus, left postcentral gyrus, and left precuneus. Additional relevant local maxima within clusters included the right inferior frontal gyrus (opercular part), and left rolandic operculum (for full list of areas, see Table 3).

For the sour condition (MSoTSo > TSo; Fig. 4B), cluster peak activations were identified in the right postcentral gyrus, left precentral gyrus, left lingual gyrus, right calcarine fissure and surrounding cortex, and left cerebellar lobule VI. Within these clusters, additional relevant local maxima were observed in the right rolandic operculum, left supramarginal gyrus, and right precuneus (for full list of areas, see Table 3).

Congruent vs. incongruent music-taste combinations

To determine whether neural enhancement during music–taste interaction was driven by congruency per se, or whether enhancement was due to concurrent music-taste presentations, we contrasted congruent and incongruent pairings while holding taste constant. When sweet taste was paired with sweet versus sour music (MSwTSw > MSoTSw), increased activation was observed in the superior temporal gyrus and rolandic operculum (Fig. 5, Table 4). When sour taste was paired with sour versus sweet music (MSoTSo > MSwTSo), no significant clusters were identified.

Additional analyses, including taste-only and music-only contrasts, direct music–taste versus unimodal comparisons, and conjunction analyses assessing overlap between interaction and taste-only conditions, are reported in Appendix B.

Behavioural results

Behavioural results presented here come from the subset of 28 participants from whom completed neuroimaging and behavioural data were obtained. Results for the full set of 48 participants who completed the behavioural task can be found in Appendix C.

How does sound affect taste pleasantness and intensity?

The mean values of the participants’ taste solution evaluations in all three sound conditions (silent, sweet soundtrack, sour soundtrack) are shown in Fig. 6. An RM-MANOVA revealed a significant main effect of sound condition (F(6, 22) = 2.96, p = .028, Wilks’ Λ = 0.55) and of tastant type (F(12, 16) = 62.02, p < .001, Wilks’ Λ = 0.02) when considered over all three measurement types (pleasantness, sweetness, sourness). There was no significant interaction effect between sound condition and tastant type (F(24, 4) = 2.00, p = .264, Wilks’ Λ = 0.08).

In terms of pleasantness ratings, there was a significant main effect of soundtrack (F(2,54) = 3.85, p = .027, ηp2 = 0.13), where the tastant solutions tasted during the sweet soundtrack were significantly more pleasant compared to the silent condition (Msweet_soundtrack(SE) = 4.44(0.25), Msilence(SE) = 4.15(0.24), p = .016), and a trend where the sweet soundtrack tended to be more pleasant compared to the sour soundtrack (Msour_soundtrack(SE) = 4.16(0.24), p = .097). There was also a significant main effect of tastant type (F(4,108) = 19.95, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.43), where the 100% sour solution was the least pleasant (p < .01 for all comparisons), followed by the 25–75 solution (p < .01 for all comparisons), followed by the 100% sweet, 75 − 25, and 50–50 solutions which were equally pleasant.

In terms of sweetness ratings, we did not observe a significant main effect of sound (F(2,54) = 0.48, p = .623, ηp2 = 0.02). There was, as expected, a significant main effect of tastant type (F(4,108) = 175.84, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.87), whereby an increasing percentage of sucrose in the tastant solution resulted in higher sweetness ratings (all p < .001).

In terms of sourness ratings, we did not observe a significant main effect of sound (F(2,54) = 1.99, p = .147, ηp2 = 0.07). There was, as expected, a significant main effect of tastant type (F(4,108) = 177.67, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.87), whereby increasing percentage of citric acid in the tastant solution resulted in higher sourness ratings (all p < .001).

Discussion

The present study was designed to test two main hypotheses: first, that auditory cues associated with sweetness or sourness would elicit behavioural correspondences and engage taste-related brain regions even without gustatory input, and second, that congruent sound–taste combinations would enhance perceived intensity through greater activation in gustatory cortices. Our results partially support these hypotheses. We found that sweet and sour soundtracks engaged regions involved in taste processing, and concurrent auditory-gustatory stimulation enhanced neural responses relative to taste alone. However, evidence for congruency-specific modulation was limited and emerged primarily when sweet taste was paired with congruent music.

Neural overlap between music and taste

In this study, we observed overlapping brain activations elicited by sweet and sour music and taste stimuli, particularly within peri-rolandic and medial prefrontal regions. Consistent with previous neuroimaging research localising gustatory cortex to the insula and overlying operculum30, 39, the rolandic operculum was engaged during both sweet taste and sweet music conditions. Although voxel-wise conjunction did not reveal suprathreshold overlap for sweet stimuli, regional overlap at the anatomical level was evident in the rolandic operculum, postcentral gyrus, gyrus rectus, and medial orbital frontal cortex.

The postcentral gyrus, which processes somatosensory information such as mouthfeel40 and integrates gustatory input41, 42, showed overlap between sour music and sour taste, including a small but significant voxel-wise conjunction in the right hemisphere. This finding suggests that auditory cues associated with sourness may engage oral somatosensory representations even in the absence of direct taste input. Given that gustatory perception is intrinsically multisensory and embodied, it is plausible that sound–taste correspondences operate partly through the activation of shared sensorimotor representations.

Activation in medial orbital prefrontal cortex and gyrus rectus was observed across both music and taste conditions. As part of the OFC, these regions play a central role in integrating sensory inputs to generate hedonic evaluation43, 44, 28, 45. Rather than reflecting sweetness-specific effects, this shared engagement may indicate that both music and taste recruit common valuation circuits, consistent with evidence that reward-related regions respond to both food and music27.

Sour relative to sweet taste elicited stronger recruitment of cerebellar regions (lobules IV/V, VI, VIII; vermis VI; Crus II) and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Lobules IV/V and VIII are typically associated with sensorimotor processing, whereas lobule VI and Crus regions have been implicated in higher-order integration46, 47. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex has been linked to cognitive control and top-down regulation of behaviour48. This pattern may suggest greater sensorimotor engagement and increased control demands during sour processing compared to sweet, possibly due to the salivating effects of the sour taste solution.

Behavioural findings further support the above neuroimaging findings: participants reliably matched the sweet soundtrack with sweetness and the sour soundtrack with sourness (Fig. 1). Notably, the degree of perceived congruence between each soundtrack and the tastant mixtures closely followed the ratio of sweet to sour components. In other words, the sweeter a solution was, the more congruent it was perceived to be with the sweet soundtrack, and vice versa for the sour soundtrack. This graded pattern of correspondence demonstrates that sound–taste associations are not binary but continuous, reflecting sensitivity to subtle changes in taste composition. Such findings extend previous behavioural work by showing that participants can finely tune their crossmodal judgements in proportion to the sensory balance of a mixture, further reinforcing the robustness and perceptual precision of sound–taste correspondences.

Music-Taste interactions enhance neural and perceptual processing perceptual experience

Relative to taste alone, congruent music–taste stimulation activated opercular gustatory cortex as well as peri-central and parietal regions. The rolandic/opercular cortex has consistently been identified as part of human gustatory cortex39, suggesting that auditory context may modulate neural processing within established taste-responsive regions. Activity observed in pre- and postcentral gyri likely reflects the sensorimotor components inherent to oral processing and auditory-motor coupling49, 50. Furthermore, in the sweet congruent condition, but not in the sour condition, increased activation was observed in the inferior frontal gyrus relative to sweet taste alone. This region has been associated with affective and multisensory aspects of flavour processing51, suggesting that congruent music may influence hedonic evaluation in the sweet condition.

We had speculated that congruent stimuli may lead to greater processing fluency52, whereas incongruent stimuli may be more distracting and demanding53, 54. Direct contrasts between congruent and incongruent pairings yielded partial validation of our hypothesis. When sweet taste was paired with congruent sweet music (MSwTSw > MSoTSw), increased activation was observed in bilateral superior temporal gyrus and, importantly, the rolandic operculum. Because the taste stimulus was identical across conditions, these effects reflect modulation by auditory congruency. Enhanced activation in superior temporal gyrus, as part of the auditory cortex55, likely reflects stronger perceptual coherence under the congruent sound-music pairing, while increased activation in the rolandic operculum is consistent with the possibility that congruent music may facilitate gustatory processing. However, we did not observe a similar activation when looking at congruent versus incongruent sour taste and music pairings (MSoTSo > MSwTSo).

Taken together, this suggests that the difference between multimodal compared to monomodal taste conditions may reflect both general enhancement arising from concurrent music-taste stimulation, as well as in specific instances, the effect of music–taste congruence. This aligns with evidence that multisensory integration in these regions can occur whenever inputs are temporally and contextually coherent, even when the semantic or affective congruence between modalities is not strong (e.g., Calvert et al.56).

Behaviourally, listening to sweet soundtracks increased the perceived pleasantness of tastant solutions, suggesting a transfer of positive valence across modalities via a halo effect57, 31. This finding aligns with previous research showing that positive auditory stimuli can influence hedonic evaluations of taste15, 18. Moreover, there was limited evidence that, in line with the neuroscientific findings above, the presence of taste-associated soundtracks had a significant effect of increasing taste ratings (Appendix C). More specifically, listening to the sweet soundtrack resulted in higher sweetness ratings for all solutions tasted, compared to listening to the sour soundtrack or in silence. This significant effect was nevertheless only observed in the full set of participants (N = 48) and not in the subset of participants with complete neuroimaging data (N = 28), suggesting limited power in the current analysis.

Limitations

While our findings on the neural mechanisms underlying sound–taste interactions are insightful, several important areas remain for further exploration. Although our sample of 28 young, right-handed adults offered the advantage of experimental control, its homogeneity limits the generalisability of our results. Future research should examine more diverse populations to assess the robustness of these effects across age groups, handedness, and other demographic variables. The role of individual factors, such as musical training, cultural context, or personal taste preferences, could also be explored in future studies, given their potential to shape and modulate the observed effects. Moreover, as shown in Appendix C, behavioural effects of sonic seasoning emerged more clearly in the larger behavioural sample (N = 48), suggesting that these effects may be relatively modest in size. While this calls for cautious interpretation, even small crossmodal effects can accumulate meaningfully in real-world multisensory experiences, such as immersive dining environments7. Future studies with larger sample sizes will be important for better estimating effect sizes and clarifying the practical relevance of these findings.

From a methodological perspective, although fMRI, with its high spatial resolution, provided insights into the localisation of crossmodal integration, future investigations integrating magnetoencephalography (MEG) or electroencephalography (EEG) with fMRI could add information on the temporal dynamics of these effects. Moreover, the interpretation of co-activation using univariate fMRI analysis is limited. While our results identify brain regions that show suprathreshold activation in both auditory and gustatory contrasts, this co-activation does not imply that the underlying neural response patterns are similar or shared. It is possible that distinct neural populations within these regions respond separately to each modality. To make stronger claims about shared neural coding, pattern-based analyses such as multivariate pattern analysis would be required.

Additionally, the present study employed a fixed task order in which the music–taste interaction run preceded the unimodal taste and music runs. Although this design minimized potential carryover of unimodal expectations into the multisensory condition, it also means that interaction > taste contrasts may partly reflect differences in novelty or habituation across runs. Future studies could counterbalance task order to disentangle multisensory enhancement from potential sequence effects.

Furthermore, although swallowing and rinsing periods were modelled explicitly as nuisance regressors, subtle orofacial motor preparation and intra-oral adjustments may still have contributed to peri-rolandic activation. Given the close coupling between gustatory, somatosensory, and motor processes, it remains difficult to fully separate sensory enhancement from embodied motor dynamics in fMRI paradigms involving liquid delivery.

Finally, the present study focused on a limited range of auditory and gustatory stimuli. Incorporating more diverse tastes and musical genres in subsequent work would yield a finer-grained understanding of crossmodal interactions. Moreover, the current study used basic taste solutions involving sucrose and citric acid. While this provided experimental control, sonic seasoning effects have generally been observed in complex food stimuli rather than in basic taste solutions7, which could further explain the relatively modest behavioural effects observed here.

Future directions

Looking ahead, our results emphasise the significance of contextual auditory cues in shaping multisensory experiences, opening new possibilities in areas such as sensory marketing and product design. The present findings suggest that concurrent auditory context can enhance gustatory and hedonic processing. For example, customised soundscapes may modulate the perceived quality and pleasantness of food and beverages in the hospitality sector.

Moreover, the capacity of auditory and gustatory inputs to interact at both behavioural and neural levels holds promise for supporting individuals with altered or reduced taste function, suggesting potential applications in healthcare and dietary interventions. Future research could further explore how different types of auditory structure, cultural familiarity, and learning history shape these crossmodal effects. Overall, these findings highlight the role of crossmodal correspondences and multisensory integration in sensory perception, offering a foundation for future research and applied innovations that leverage the synergy between music and taste.

Methods

The study consists of a neuroimaging session followed by a behavioural session, to develop a comprehensive model of how sound contributes to flavour perception in healthy participants.

Participants

A total of 48 healthy participants (27 female, average age = 27.5 years, SD = 7.0) were recruited for the study. Inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 55 years; right-handed; able to speak Danish or English; with normal health (no neurological or current psychiatric illness); and normal or correct-to-normal senses of vision, hearing, smell, and taste. Due to technical issues experienced early in the study, complete MRI data were acquired from 28 participants (16 female, average age = 27.8 years, SD = 6.5) while behavioural data were collected from all 48 participants.

All participants gave informed consent before participating, and the study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. This project was approved by the Danish Health Research Ethics Committee of the Midtjylland Region (no. 75299).

Auditory stimuli

The auditory stimuli consist of 45 s of musical soundtracks adapted from stimuli previously validated in crossmodal research on sound–taste correspondences36, 18. The “sweet” soundtrack incorporated high pitch, legato articulation, and consonant harmony, while the “sour” soundtrack featured high pitch, sharper attacks, and mild dissonance. These features were selected to evoke perceptual associations reflecting empirical mappings between acoustic and gustatory dimensions. Importantly, sound–taste congruence in this context does not solely reflect matched affective valence. Rather, it is based on systematic crossmodal correspondences between specific acoustic features (e.g., pitch, timbre, consonance, articulation) and basic taste qualities, as demonstrated across multiple behavioural paradigms14, 18, 36. Although shared affective dimensions such as valence and arousal partly mediate these correspondences, congruence is defined more broadly as alignment between learned auditory–gustatory mappings at both perceptual and affective levels.

Participants listened to the two soundtracks (sweet and sour), both while undergoing MRI scanning, as well as in a post-scan behavioural session. The soundtracks are available at https://soundcloud.com/janicewang09/sets/mfib.

Taste stimuli

Participants received two tastant solutions in the pre-screening taste session and during scanning: Sweet (138.1 g/L = 0.406 M sucrose, off-the-shelf) and Sour (2.4 g/L = 0.011 M citric acid, Sigma-Aldrich CAS #5949-29-1); and the same tastants with five different concentrations (100% sweet, 75% sweet + 25% sour, 50% sweet + 50% sour, 25% sweet + 75% sour, and 100% sour) in the after-scan behavioural study. The behavioural study included the same sweet and sour solutions as used during scanning, along with a series of partial mixtures between them, to capture finer perceptual variations while maintaining comparability with the imaging conditions. The solutions were served/delivered at room temperature (20 °C). The concentrations were determined by using intensity-matched intensities at a perceptibly strong concentration level36 such that each tastant would be easily identifiable. We used single-compound taste solutions rather than complex food stimuli to maintain experimental control and isolate basic neural mechanisms of auditory–gustatory integration.

Gustometer

An MRI-compatible multistimulator (OG001, Burghart, Germany) with three taste modules was used to deliver water, sweet, and sour tastants during the MRI scanning. The multistimulator delivers defined amounts of liquids via three tubes directly to the subject´s tongue. Tastant solutions were kept at room temperature in pressurized syringes, and liquid delivery was controlled by a computer located in the MRI control room, to release the solutions on the participant’s tongue using an in-house 3D printed mouthpiece and holder (https://osf.io/g9fhc/). The mouthpiece was made of Polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG), with a groove to accommodate the top front teeth; the flexible mouthpiece holder is printed with thermoplastic elastomer (TPE) material (images in Appendix A). The purpose of the mouthpiece and holder setup is to ensure a stable tongue position so that the liquid can be delivered uniformly across participants in a comfortable and controlled manner.

Procedure

This auditory-taste protocol was investigated first inside the MRI scanner (session 1), and then validated via a sensory evaluation (session 2), where participants made explicit evaluations while being presented with the same set of sound/taste stimuli (Fig. 7A). Participants were asked to avoid eating or drinking (except water) at least two hours before the study.

Session 1 - MRI

Pre-screening tests were followed by the acquisition of both anatomical and functional MRI images. All participants underwent three functional MRI sessions. Inside the scanner, participants were first presented with a multisensory interaction task, followed by gustatory and auditory tasks (see more details below). The gustatory and auditory tasks were presented in random order. The experiment was controlled by a PsychoPy3 (PsychoPy®) script. An MRI-compatible screen was used to project the instructions, which participants viewed via a mirror attached to the head coil. Participants were instructed about the conditions before entering the scanner and practiced receiving water via the gustometer to become familiar with swallowing while lying down. Inside the scanner, participants received visual information about which condition would take place. Gustatory stimuli were delivered through a MRI-compatible gustometer OG001 (Burghart, Wedel, Germany) at a flow rate of 150 µl/s for 3 s at a time. Auditory stimuli were delivered by OptoACTIVE noise-cancelling headphones. The MRI session had a total duration of 59 min.

Multisensory interaction task

Four conditions were presented to the participants: sweet soundtrack (music; MSw) and sweet taste (TSw) - MSwTSw; sweet soundtrack and sour taste - MSwTSo; sour soundtrack and sweet taste - MSoTSw; sour soundtrack and sour taste - MSoTSo. In each of the four conditions, music and tastants were presented simultaneously. The 4 conditions (MSw + TSw, MSw + TSo, MSo + TSw, MSo + TSo) were presented to the participants eight times in random order for a total of 32 trials. Each condition lasted for 45 s, and participants received it eight times. In total, the music-taste interaction paradigm lasted for 24 min (6 min each condition) (Fig. 7B).

Gustatory task

The gustatory (taste) session consisted of two conditions: sweet and sour. Each condition was repeated 8 times with a duration of 35 s. The taste-only session lasted for a total of 10 min (Fig. 7C).

Auditory task

The auditory stimuli session also consisted of two conditions: sweet and sour soundtracks (each condition lasting for 45 s). Each condition was presented to the participants 8 times. The auditory-only session lasted for a total of 12 min (Fig. 7D).

Session 2 – behavioural sensory evaluation

Immediately after the MRI scanning session, participants then participated in a behavioural session where they evaluated the same sound stimuli and an expanded set of 5 taste stimuli (100% sucrose and 100% citric acid, as in the neuroimaging session, plus 25–75, 50–50, and 75 − 25 ratios of sucrose and citric acid). The extended mixture set allowed for capturing more nuanced perceptual and judgment differences, which were not possible in the scanner due to time constraints. The sound stimuli were presented over closed-ear headphones connected to an iPad running a Qualtrics questionnaire. Taste solutions (5 mL) were served in 20 mL transparent cups labelled with random 3-digit identifiers.

Participants first experienced a total of 15 tasting trials, covering full factorial combinations of 3 sound conditions (sweet soundtrack, sour soundtrack, silence) and the 5 taste concentrations. To ensure a consistent tasting procedure, participants listened to a 30-second soundtrack for each trial, which included the verbal instruction “taste now” at 10 s, and “swallow now” at 25 s. After tasting, participants evaluated tastant pleasantness, sweetness intensity, sourness intensity, and sound-taste congruency on 9-point scales with anchor points at one (“not at all”) and nine (“very much”).

After the tasting trials, participants listened to the two soundtracks again and evaluated soundtrack pleasantness as well as how much they associated the sound with each basic taste, again via 9-point scales. The behavioural session lasted approximately 20 min (Fig. 7E).

MRI image acquisition and processing

All participants underwent the same imaging protocol using a 32-channel head coil in a Siemens 3T Prisma magnetic resonance scanner located at Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical scans and T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences for functional MRI were collected for each participant.

Anatomical scan acquisition

A MPRAGE sequence was acquired to provide an anatomical reference for the functional images, with the following parameters: voxel size of 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.90 mm3, 192 sagittal slices, echo time (TE) of 2.87 ms, repetition time (TR) of 5000 ms, flip angle (α) of 4, field of view (FOV) of 230 mm (readout) × 215.7 mm (phrase, 93.8%).

fMRI acquisition

Three EPI-sequences were acquired with the following parameters: voxel size of 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm3, TE = 30 ms; TR = 1000 ms; flip-angle (α) of 60; FOV of 192 mm (readout) × 192 mm (phase); matrix size = 76 × 76; GRAPPA parallel imaging with an in-plane acceleration factor of 2; multiband acceleration factor = 3. Slice orientation was axial (transversal), automatically aligned to the anterior–posterior commissure plane using AutoAlign. Fat suppression was applied (fat sat). Three separate fMRI runs were conducted, consisting of 1440 volumes for the multisensory (music–taste) interaction task, 740 volumes for the music task, and 580 volumes for the gustatory task.

Data analysis

fMRI analysis

All fMRI data analysis was carried out using FEAT (FMRI Expert Analysis Tool) Version 6.00, part of FSL (FMRIB’s Software Library www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl).

Significance thresholds were cluster-based, using a cluster-forming threshold offset to Z > 3.1 and a family-wise error corrected cluster significance with an alpha level of 0.05. For mapping significant activation clusters to anatomical brain areas, AAL3 was used38. Group-level maps have been uploaded to Neurovault at https://neurovault.org/collections/ECYFQMPR/.

fMRI data preprocessing

The pre-processing steps included: correction for motion using MCFLIRT58, slice-timing correction using a custom slice timings file, removal of non-brain tissues using BET59, spatial smoothing employing a Gaussian kernel with a Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of 5 mm, normalisation of the entire 4D dataset by a single multiplicative factor based on grand-mean intensity, and high-pass temporal filtering using Gaussian-weighted least-squares straight line fitting with a sigma value of 50.0s.

Functional images were registered to each participant’s structural image and subsequently to standard space using FLIRT with FSL’s default normal search settings.

First-level analysis

Once the data were pre-processed, the modelling of the hemodynamic response for each of the tasks (music-taste interaction, taste only and music only tasks) was carried out at the individual participant level using a General Linear Model (GLM). For each of the three tasks, experimental conditions (music-taste interaction: MSwTSw, MSoTSo, MSwTSo and MSoTSw; taste only: TSw and TSo; music only: MSw and MSo) were modelled as explanatory variables (EVs) convolved with a single-Gamma hemodynamic response function.

For the music-taste interaction task, each experimental condition was modelled as a separate EV representing sustained 43-second trials of music playing (including the three nested tasting periods occurring while the music was playing). These trials were presented in an alternating fashion, separated by a 2-second baseline period. Swallowing and rinsing periods were modelled explicitly as nuisance explanatory variables. Swallowing events were modelled with a duration of 2 s, and rinsing events with a duration of 5 s. These periods were included to account for possible movement- and artefact-related signal changes associated with orofacial motion and liquid intake and were therefore excluded from the implicit baseline. GLM contrasts were defined to estimate condition-specific activation relative to baseline for both congruent (MSwTSw> baseline [ 1 0 0 0 0 0] and MSoTSo> baseline [0 1 0 0 0 0]) and incongruent (MSwTSo> baseline [0 0 1 0 0 0] and MSoTSw> baseline [0 0 0 1 0 0]) music-taste pairings. In addition, pairwise differential contrasts were specified to directly compare experimental conditions within the music-taste interaction task, specifically congruent versus incongruent pairings (MSwTSw> MSoTSo [1–1 0 0 0 0], MSoTSo> MSwTSw [-1 1 0 0 0 0] and MSwTSw> MSoTSw [ 1 0 0–1 0 0], MSoTSo> MSwTSo [0 1–1 0 0 0]).

Within the taste only task, swallowing and rinsing periods were also modelled explicitly as nuisance explanatory variables. Contrasts were defined to estimate individual condition effects relative to baseline (TSw> baseline [1 0 0 0] and TSo> baseline [0 0 1 0]), and differential activation between tasting conditions (TSw > TSo [1 0–1 0] and TSo > TSw [-1 0 1 0]).

Finally, for the music only task, contrasts were set to estimate individual condition effects relative to baseline (MSw> baseline [1 0 0 0] and MSo> baseline [0 0 1 0]), as well as differential activation between music conditions (MSw > MSo [1 0–1 0] and MSo > MSw [-1 0 1 0]).

Taking into account the design matrix for each participant’s task block, the analysis of time-series statistics was conducted using FILM, incorporating local autocorrelation correction as proposed by60. Gaussianised T/F statistic images (Z) were subjected to thresholding, with clusters determined by Z > 3.1 and a Gaussian random field corrected cluster significance threshold set at p = .0561.

Second-level analysis

To make it possible to compare neural activation across different blocks of tasks, a second-level analysis was conducted to directly compare the conditions from the music only task (MSw and MSo), the taste only tasks (TSw and TSo), and the music-taste interaction task (MSwTSw, MSoTSo, MSoTSw and MSwTSo) for each participant. We took the within-subject results from the first-level analysis and computed additional across-task contrasts. Specifically, the analysis was performed using FSL FEAT. For each of the participants, first-level contrast images, representing specific effects of MSw and MSo from the music only run, the corresponding contrast images of TSw and TSo from the taste only run, and condition-specific effects from the music-taste interaction run (MSwTSw, MSoTSo, MSoTSw and MSwTSo) were used as inputs.

A within-participant GLM was constructed for each of the comparisons of interest. Specifically, for the sweet music and taste, we defined the MSw > TSw and MSw < TSw contrasts to compare music only and taste only comparisons. For the sour stimuli, analogous contrasts were specified, namely MSo > TSo and MSo < TSo. Finally, we contrasted the taste only conditions with the corresponding congruent conditions from the music-taste interaction task to assess the effect of musical context on taste processing (MSwTSw > TSw and MSoTSo > TSo).

For all mentioned contrasts, a fixed effects model was employed by setting the random effects variance to zero through the use of FLAME (FMRIB’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects)62, 63, 64. The Z (Gaussianised T/F) statistical images were then subjected to thresholding, with clusters determined by Z > 3.1 and a Gaussian random field corrected cluster significance threshold set at p = .0561.

Group-level analysis

For the group-level analysis, R contrast of parameter estimate (COPE) images from each participant’s first- and second-level analyses were carried forward into a higher-level mixed-effects model results for each of the contrasts specified in first- and group-level analyses served as input to obtain the group-level activation estimates for each contrast of interest. Group-level analyses were performed using FLAME (FMRIB’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects), employing stages 1 and stage 262, 63, 64. The resulting Gaussianised T/F statistic images (Z-maps) were subjected to thresholding using a primary voxel-wise threshold of Z > 3.1 and a Gaussian random field-corrected cluster significance threshold set at p = .0561.

Thresholded statistical maps were anatomically characterised using the AAL3 atlas38. Significant clusters and their local maxima were identified, and peak MNI coordinates were used to assign clusters to anatomical regions. See Fig. 8 for visualization of first-, second- and group-level analyses.

Voxel-wise conjunction analysis

To formally assess spatial overlap between conditions, voxel-wise conjunction analyses were conducted for selected pairs of contrasts. Conjunction maps were computed as the intersection of cluster-corrected, thresholded group-level Z-maps (Z > 3.1, cluster-corrected p < .05). Specifically, thresholded statistical maps were multiplied voxel-wise using FSL (fslmaths -mul), yielding maps representing voxels that survived correction in both contrasts. The resulting conjunction maps were subjected to cluster analysis using the same primary threshold (Z > 3.1), and cluster size, peak Z values, and MNI coordinates were extracted using FSL’s cluster tool (mm-space coordinates). These analyses provide a conservative estimate of shared suprathreshold activation and do not constitute a formal test of the conjunction null hypothesis.

Conjunction analyses were conducted for theoretically motivated pairs of contrasts to examine shared neural activation patterns. These included crossmodal comparisons between music-only and taste-only conditions, matched according to established sound-taste correspondences (MSw and TSw; MSo and TSo), which are reported in the main text. Additional conjunction analyses, reported in the Supplementary Materials (Appendix B), comprised within-modality comparisons (TSw and TSo; MSw and MSo), comparisons between taste-only conditions and their corresponding congruent music–taste interaction conditions (MSwTSw and TSw; MSoTSo and TSo), and comparisons between the two congruent music–taste interaction conditions (MSwTSw and MSoTSo).

Regional overlap for visualization

Because voxel-wise conjunctions are highly conservative and sensitive to spatial variability across participants, overlap was additionally characterised at the anatomical level for visualization purposes. Specifically, AAL3 regions containing suprathreshold activation in multiple contrasts were identified, and shared regional involvement was illustrated in the figures.

This regional overlap approach does not constitute voxel-wise conjunction inference but provides a descriptive summary of common anatomical engagement across conditions.

Behavioural data analysis

First, sound-taste associations from the soundtrack evaluation block, as well as sound-taste congruency ratings from the testing trials, were assessed as a manipulation check, in order to validate that people did indeed perceive the sweet and sour soundtracks to match the sweet and sour tastants, respectively. Next, to assess the overall effect of sound, multivariate repeated-measures analysis of variance (rm-MANOVA) was conducted with sound (sweet, sour, silence) and tastant solution type (100-0, 75 − 25, 50–50, 25–75, 0-100 proportions of sucrose-citric acid) as within-subject factors, and with tastant pleasantness and tastant intensity (sweet, sour) as measures (SPSS 28).
