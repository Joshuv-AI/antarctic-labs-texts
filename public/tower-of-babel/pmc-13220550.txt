Facilitators, barriers, and factors determining the willingness to donate human milk among postpartum mothers in a tertiary women’s hospital in Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chandra Singh Sejuwal, Bishow Kandel, Samikshya Badal, Dinesh Raj Neupane, Sunny Kumar Yadav, Rojina Basnet, Khem Bahadur Karki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreastfeeding is universally acknowledged as the most effective and efficient means of ensuring optimal growth, development, and survival of infants. However, in rare cases where breastfeeding is medically contraindicated or the mother’s milk is insufficient, donor human milk from a milk bank serves as a critical alternative. At a time when Nepal’s first milk bank is struggling to supply adequate milk for newborns, this study aimed to identify the facilitators, barriers, and factors determining willingness to donate human milk among postpartum mothers in a tertiary women’s hospital in Nepal.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among 318 postpartum women aged 18 years and above at Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital, Nepal. Data were collected from February to March 2025 using semi-structured interview. Systematic random sampling was used. Binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed, and factors with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant at the 95% confidence level.ResultsOnly 23.3% had heard about milk banks. Overall, 61.6% of postpartum mothers were willing to donate human milk. Background

Breastfeeding is universally acknowledged as the most effective and efficient means of ensuring optimal growth, development, and survival of infants, particularly in the first two years of life. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, emphasising that infants should receive only breast milk and no other liquids or solids, not even water [1]. Breast milk is a vital source of nutrition, essential growth hormones, enzymes, and immunological components that support development and immune protection, especially for those born prematurely, small for gestational age, or in resource-limited settings [2–4]. Breastfeeding offers protective effects against many childhood and adolescent health conditions such as overweight/obesity, high blood pressure, type-2 diabetes, and increases cognitive performance and the lack of breastfeeding is linked to significant global economic losses due to reduced intelligence and higher disease burdens [5–7]. The most natural and common way of providing breast milk to babies is through Mothers’ Own Milk (MOM), either directly or expressed. However, in rare cases where breastfeeding is medically contraindicated or the mother’s milk is insufficient, donor human milk from a milk bank serves as a critical alternative [8, 9].

Donor breast milk is a practice dating back to ancient times providing vital nutrition for infants when their mother cannot produce her own milk. Despite its life‑saving importance, many vulnerable infants still lack access to breast milk, either because MOM is unavailable due to maternal illness, death, abandonment, or delayed lactation, or because donor human milk supplies from milk banks are insufficient to meet demand [4]. Currently there are more than 700 human milk banks operating in more than 60 countries, mostly in Europe, United States, Asia and Brazil. Unfortunately, Human milk banking facilities are not available in most of the South Asia and Africa countries where childhood malnutrition and diseases are a major cause of childhood mortality leaving significant coverage gap [10, 11].

India established Asia’s first human milk bank in November 1989 at the Lokmanya Tilak Municipal General Hospital in Mumbai. In contrast, Nepal only initiated serious policy discussions on human milk banking in 2020 [12]. Which led to the establishment of Nepal’s first human milk bank, Amrit Kosh, at Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital in Kathmandu in 2022. This milestone was achieved through a partnership between the Government of Nepal, the European Union, and UNICEF [13]. Amrit Kosh has facilities to collect, pasteurise, test, and store safe donor human milk from lactating mothers and then provide it to infants in need [12]. Amrit Kosh has emerged as a critically important and well-functioning facility; however, it is currently unable to meet the full daily demand for donor human milk as the amount of milk collected in donation, pasteurised and dispensed has not increased significantly [12–14]. The establishment of Nepal’s first human milk bank directly supports the country’s national and global development goals. It is a key intervention for achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2 (improved nutrition), SDG 3 (good health and well-being), and SDG 4 (supported cognitive development), while also advancing the objectives of Nepal’s Multi-Sector Nutrition Plan [15, 16].

The concept of a human milk bank is still new in Nepal. Understanding the facilitators, barriers and associated factors influencing mothers’ willingness to donate human milk is crucial for ensuring the long-term sustainability and expansion of such services. Therefore, this study aims to identify facilitators, barriers and factors determining willingness to donate human milk among postpartum mothers in a tertiary women’s hospital in Nepal.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a quantitative, cross-sectional study conducted at Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital in Kathmandu, Nepal. The hospital was purposively selected as it houses Nepal’s first human milk bank (Amrit Kosh).

Study population

The study population consisted of postpartum mothers aged 18 years and above who were admitted to the postdelivery ward of Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital during the study period. Mothers facing complications during delivery or those unable to communicate due to hearing or speech problems were excluded. All participants were within seven days of delivery and admitted to the post-delivery ward.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula for estimating proportions: (n=Z2pq/d2)

Assuming a proportion (p) of 25% [17], a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a margin of error (d) of 5%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 289. After adjusting for a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was set at 318 participants.

Sampling technique

Systematic random sampling was used to enrol postpartum mothers aged 18 years or older who delivered at Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital during the study period. A daily list of eligible mothers was created from admission log of postdelivery ward. Using a randomly determined starting point, every second mother (sampling interval = 2) was selected.

Data collection tools and procedures

Face-to-face interviews were conducted by the principal investigator to collect responses from the participants using a semi-structured interview schedule. Data were collected from February to March 2025. Those who had recently undergone caesarean section and were unwilling to participate at the time of consent were approached the next day. The tool was adapted from previously validated instruments used in Nepal and similar settings [18, 19]. Minor modifications were made to suit the local context, language, and target population. The questionnaire included three sections: sociodemographic information, knowledge, attitudes and practice towards human milk donation, and willingness or unwillingness to donate breast milk.

Validity and reliability

Content validity was assessed through expert consultation with five professionals in the relevant field. Reliability was established through pretesting the instrument with 10% of the sample size at a non-study site. Internal consistency of the scale was measured using cronbach’s alpha, which demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.78).

Data management and analysis

Collected data were coded, cleaned, and entered into EpiData version 3.1. The data were then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ characteristics. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between independent variables and willingness to donate breast milk. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant at a 95% confidence interval. Variables having p value less than 0.25 were considered for the multiple logistic regression model [20]. Variables with variance inflation factor less than 2 were considered for multivariable logistic regression model.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of the Institute of Medicine (Ref. No. 222(6–11, E2), 081/082). Approval was also taken from the Institutional Review Committee of Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Mean age of respondents was 26.41 years, with the standard deviation of 4.9 years ranging from 18 years to 39 years. More than half of the respondents were Janajati (54.1%), followed by Brahmin/Chhetri (24.5%) and Dalit (13.5%). Hinduism (72.0%) was the predominant religion among respondents, followed by Buddhism (17.3%) and few represented Christianity, Kirat and Islam as shown in the Table 1. Slightly more than half of the respondents belonged to nuclear families (51.6%), while 32.1% belonged to joint families and 16.0% to extended families. Educational status showed that most of the participants were literate (95.3%), with only 4.7% being illiterate. Among the literate respondents, most had completed higher secondary education (55.4%) while a smaller proportion had attained tertiary education or above (14.5%). In terms of occupation, almost half of the respondents were homemakers while only 23% were involved in service.

Knowledge, perceptions, and practices related to human milk banking

In terms of current caretaker of mother, husbands were the primary caretakers (67.3%), followed by other family members (18.6%), mothers-in-law (7.9%), and parents (5.7%). Permission for milk donation was most often required from husbands (58.5%), followed by other family members (23.9%). A smaller proportion of respondents (16.0%) felt that no permission was required. Almost half of the respondents perceived society’s view on milk donation as good (46.2%), although 31.4% perceived it as bad. Mothers were identified as the key decision-makers in milk donation (58.2%) though husband were the main permission giver.

Facilitators and barriers to willingness to human milk donation

A majority 196 (61.6%) of respondents expressed willingness to donate breast milk, while 122 (38.4%) reported that they were not willing to donate. Facilitators included having an adequate milk supply (44.7%), a desire to meet the nutritional needs of infants lacking sufficient milk (18.6%), altruism or a desire to help other infants (13.2%), earning spiritual or religious merit (9.1%), and a desire to avoid infant formula feeding for recipients (0.9%), while 13.5% reported they don’t know any specific facilitator.

As shown in the Table 4, barriers included a lack of information about the donation process (21.4%), fear of producing insufficient milk for one’s own infant (24.5%), the requirement for family permission (5.0%), lack of personal interest (4.1%), cultural beliefs or norms (1.9%), concerns about effects on the donor’s own baby (2.2%), and limited availability of milk bank services (0.3%); 40.6% were unable to identify a specific barrier.

Discussion

In this study, nearly one-fifth of the respondents reported hearing about human milk bank. This finding is consistent with a global trend of low awareness in regions where concept of human milk banking is relatively new such as China (17%) [21], Turkey (25.1%) [22], South Africa (18.9%) [23], and Kenya [24]. This lack of information related to human milk banking is a bottleneck to donor recruitment and willingness to donate. For the minority who were aware, health personnel were the major source of information. This highlights the healthcare system’s important role as a source of most trusted information in our study setting. Similarly, health care providers were major source of information related to human milk bank in Iran [25] and South Africa [23]. However, it contrasts with studies where social media or the internet were primary sources in China and Turkey [22, 26], suggesting use of context specific health education and promotion strategies.

In our study, almost two-third of respondents were willing to donate breast milk which aligns with findings from other studies conducted in Turkey (57.9%) [22], and slightly lower than report from Southeast China (73.4%) [21], and Kenya (78.1%) [24]. However, it stands in contrast to a lower willingness (39.9%) reported in Nigeria [27], highlighting potential regional variations influenced by culture. In the Nepali context, the high willingness to donate human milk may be attributed to mothers’ recognition of its importance for infant health and nutrition, as well as the existing practice of informal breast milk sharing among relatives and neighbours [28].

This expressed willingness, however, contrasted with the actual donation practice as only 4% of the mothers were donors. Similar kinds of patterns can be seen in other studies highlighting a significant gap between intention and action [25]. The reasons for this gap may be linked to limited knowledge, restricted accessibility, and societal factors. Our study revealed a substantial knowledge gap regarding human milk banking, with only 23.3% of participants having ever heard of it. Accessibility also remains a major challenge in Nepal as there is only one operational human milk bank, limiting opportunities for donation [29]. Beyond these barriers, cultural taboos and societal beliefs continue to discourage milk donation [28]. A study conducted in Rajasthan, India, similarly reported barriers such as religious stigma and cultural beliefs, including concerns about the transfer of hereditary traits and fears of diminished mother–child affection, which act as strong deterrents to donation [30].

The foremost facilitator for willingness to donate was having an adequate supply of breast milk (44.7%), which was also reported as a primary facilitator for willingness to donate [31–33]. Altruistic desires, including helping other infants and earning spiritual merit, were also commonly cited, which was in line with the studies from Brazil and South Africa [23, 32]. The most common barrier perceived by the mothers was fear of producing insufficient milk for own infant (24.5%) and lack of information about the donation process (21.4%). These types of barriers relating to insufficient milk supply to own infant and informational gaps are commonly reported in other studies from China and South Africa [23, 26]. Public health programs must overcome these types of barriers through educational interventions. The requirement for family permission, though cited by a smaller percentage (5.0%), aligns with the identified importance of familial support in decision-making [23, 25].

In bivariate logistic regression, awareness was found to be a statistically significant factor associated with willingness to donate human milk. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ghana [34] and China [21], which also identified awareness as a strong positive correlate of willingness to donate milk. A positive personal opinion regarding the concept of human milk banking was a strong predictor of willingness to donate human milk. This finding is supported by other studies where attitude regarding human milk banking was associated with donation intention [26, 35]. Similarly, the perception of a positive societal attitude was strongly associated with willingness to donate breast milk (OR = 4.54, p < 0.01). This finding aligns with evidence from studies in Iran [25], Nigeria [17], and South Africa [23], which highlight the significant influence of subjective norms, family support, and spousal attitudes on donation decisions. Knowledge of eligibility criteria also significantly increased willingness (OR = 3.29), aligning with studies that identify knowledge as a factor predicting milk donation [17, 21].

Multivariable logistic regression showed positive personal opinion and positive perception of society as independent and strongest predictors determining the willingness. This result supports the constructs from behavioural theory, where attitude and subjective norms are key determinants of intention [35]. It indicates that while knowledge is necessary as a primary driver of change, the translation of knowledge into willingness is mediated by internalized positive beliefs and the perceived support of one’s social environment. Other socio-demographic and clinical variables including age, ethnicity, education, occupation, and delivery type showed no significant association. Evidence from a qualitative study conducted in central Nepal further supports the role of sociocultural context in shaping attitudes toward breastmilk donation. Participants in that study expressed concerns regarding potential infectious disease transmission, reduced mother–infant bonding, religious beliefs, stigma, and the perceived quality and compatibility of donor milk [28]. This diverges from studies where factors like age [23, 24] or occupation [21] were significant, suggesting that in this population, psychosocial factors are key determinants of willingness than demographic characteristics. While this study provides valuable insights into mothers’ awareness, willingness to donate, and factors influencing donation, the findings may not be generalizable to mothers in rural areas or those in community-based settings, as it was conducted in a tertiary women’s hospital located in an urban area where the human milk bank is established.

Conclusions

Although the concept of human milk donation is a new concept in Nepal, majority of the respondents expressed willingness to donate milk. The facilitators for milk donation included having an adequate milk supply and altruistic motives to help other infants. The main barriers were fear of producing insufficient milk for one’s own infant and lack of information about the donation process. The determining factors for willingness to donate were positive personal opinion toward milk banking and positive perception of society’s attitude toward milk donation. Other factors such as age, occupation, baby’s health, decision-maker, and knowledge of eligibility criteria were not statistically significant in the final model. Our finding suggest that healthcare providers should take the lead in educating mothers to increase awareness and address barriers, such as fear of insufficient milk and lack of knowledge. Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) activities should be carried out in the community, especially among mother groups, to build a positive attitude toward human milk donation. Efforts should also be made to promote positive social perceptions and normalize milk donation through community participation.
