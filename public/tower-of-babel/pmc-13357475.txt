Garin3 regulates sperm-oocyte fusion and male fertility
Authors: Zhixian Chen, Siyuan Xie, Guowei Zou, Hongying Zhu, Lei Wang, Xiaoxi Sun, Jian Mu, Qing Sang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interaction between sperm and the zona pellucida (ZP), which involves the binding of sperm surface ligands to complementary carbohydrates on the ZP, is the initial direct contact between gametes and is crucial for subsequent gamete fusion and successful mammalian fertilization. In this study, we show that Garin3 is a critical gene for sperm–oocyte ZP binding. Garin3 expression begins during the acrosomal stage of spermatogenesis, and the gene is highly expressed in the male reproductive system. We created a Garin3 gene-knockout mouse model, and spermatozoa lacking Garin3 exhibited only mild morphological changes and showed no deficiency in motility; however, their ability to bind to the oocyte ZP was severely impaired and this ultimately resulted in failure of in vitro fertilization. These findings suggest that Garin3 is not directly involved in sperm–ZP binding and that it instead indirectly affects such binding by regulating the expression and localization of acrosome-related proteins, including RAB2, SPACA1, ADAM2, and ACR. Notably, fertilization failure was effectively rescued by bypassing the ZP-binding step by removing the ZP altogether or by performing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). In human sperm samples, the expression levels of GARIN3 were significantly reduced in sperm with poor fertilization potential compared to sperm with normal fertilization potential, and this was consistent with the phenotypic observations in animal models. Our findings highlight the crucial role of Garin3 in fertilization and the potential molecular mechanism behind its effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06225-x.

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the inability of a couple to conceive within one year of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse. Approximately 15% of the global reproductive-age population is affected by infertility, with male factors contributing to nearly half of all cases [1]. Male infertility can be clinically classified as azoospermia, oligospermia, asthenospermia, or teratozoospermia [2], and these result from genetic mutations, lifestyle factors, medical conditions, and medications [3]. Of these, about 15%–35% of male infertility cases are due to genetic causes [4]. Despite extensive research into the etiology of male infertility, the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying most cases remain poorly understood.

Spermatogenesis is a complex process that occurs in the seminiferous tubules of the testis and involves a total of 16 steps in mice, including nuclear elongation, acrosome formation, and tail formation [5]. Collectively, these processes guarantee the structural integrity of the sperm and support normal fertilization. The acrosome is a distinct Golgi-derived structure in the sperm head, and it contains high concentrations of acid hydrolase and is crucial for sperm-oocyte fusion [6]. Acrosomal reactions are characterized by exocytotic events, including the release of enzymes stored in the acrosome that help the sperm cross the oocyte’s zona pellucida (ZP) and the migration of receptors that facilitate sperm–oocyte fusion [7]. Based on changes to the morphology of the acrosome and the nucleus, spermatogenesis can also be divided into four stages, including the Golgi stage, cap stage, acrosome stage, and maturation stage [8]. However, research into the formation, function, and clinical relevance of the acrosome is limited.

The RAB protein family, a subset of the Ras superfamily, is a small GTP-dependent protein family composed of around 70 different types of proteins [9]. These proteins are mostly expressed in membrane-bound cellular components in eukaryotic cells, and they play crucial functions in membrane transport and the development of Golgi structures and regulate Golgi trafficking [10]. Because the acrosomes in male germ cells are derived from the Golgi apparatus, members of the RAB protein family have been reported to be involved in acrosomal biogenesis and structural modification through their influence on membrane transport [11]. Among them, RAB2 has been suggested to play roles in the acrosomal response, sperm capacitation, autophagosome maturation, and Golgi function [12, 13]. The Golgi-Associated RAB2 Interactors (GARINs), also known as the FAM71 protein family, all possess RAB2-binding domains. Previous studies have shown that the loss of GARINs have varying effects on spermatogenesis and male fertility [14], but whether these members act independently or in coordination remains unclear. It has been reported that GARIN1A and 1B are not expressed in mature sperm [15], whereas other GARINs are present, suggesting that their functions are not redundant. Knockout studies suggest that GARIN3 is essential for male fertility [14]. Nevertheless, GARIN3’s expression pattern, subcellular localization, and molecular function remain poorly understood.

In this study, we show that Garin3 deficiency results in only slight sperm deformities but still results in total fertilization failure. Garin3 knockout mice displayed normal development and behavior, and sterility was observed only in males. Garin3 was expressed from the acrosome phase through sperm maturation, and our findings suggest that it is essential for maintaining the integrity of acrosome function and sperm–ZP binding. Garin3 deficiency in sperm led to decreased expression levels and aberrant localization of RAB2, SPACA1, ADAM2, and Acrosin (ACR). Each of these proteins is crucial in regulating the acrosomal reaction. In addition, we successfully fertilized an oocyte by removing the ZP and then performing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), and this suggests a novel strategy for treating male infertility. Notably, we found that GARIN3 expression was decreased in human sperm with poor fertilization following IVF, indicating the importance of this protein in mammalian reproductive function. Our results suggest a previously unrecognized role and mechanism of Garin3 in regulating male reproduction, and they provide new insights into the genetic causes of male infertility.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with animal testing guidelines and regulations and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical College of Fudan University (Approval number 2021JSIBS-007).

Antibodies

The primary antibodies were anti-HA (CST, 3724T), anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, F7425), anti-GARIN3 (Abclonal, E24999), anti-Vinculin (CST, 1390T), anti-α-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T6199), anti-RAB2 (Proteintech, 15420), anti-SPACA1 (Proteintech, 12829), anti-ADAM2 (Merck, 3927174), anti-Acrosin (Novus, 14620), anti-GAPDH (Abclonal, AC033), and rabbit IgG nonspecific antibody (Abmart, B30011). The secondary antibodies were goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Abmart, M21001), goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Abmart, M21002), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-11008), and Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-21422).

Identification of RAB2-interacting proteins by mass spectrometry

Proteomic analysis was performed using sperm from the cauda epididymis of 2-month-old male wild-type (WT) mice. Briefly, ultrasonic lysis was performed with NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 7.5) supplemented with a mixture of 1% protease inhibitor (Bimake) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor (Bimake). The lysis buffer was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 25 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant protein. The protein samples were separately incubated with 6 µg IgG antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abmart) and 6 µg RAB2 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Proteintech, 15420-1-AP). The samples were then digested with trypsin and analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an Easy-nLC 1000 nanoflow liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The raw MS data were searched against the UniprotKB mouse protein database, and the global false discovery rate cutoff for peptide and protein identification was set at < 1%. Proteins were considered identified when they were allocated in at least two replicates and were supported by three unique peptides. Additionally, proteins with fold change values greater than 2 and a P-value < 0.01 were considered to exhibit significant differential expression. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was conducted using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (accessible at https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). This analysis covered three main aspects: biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied, while other parameters were set to their default values. For in-depth analysis, the top-10 most highly enriched terms in each of the BP, CC, and MF categories were selected.

Construction of expression vectors and cellular transfection

The coding sequences of the target genes were amplified from mouse tissue cDNA templates. FLAG and HA tags were then fused to the amplified sequences, and these were inserted into the pCMV6 plasmid. This cloning procedure successfully generated the recombinant plasmids pCMV6-Garin3-FLAG and pCMV6-Rab2-HA. The HEK-293T cell line was procured from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). For cell cultivation, HEK-293T cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (F2442; Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco). The cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Plasmid transfection was performed using PolyJet Transfection Reagent (SL100688; SignaGen Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Co-immunoprecipitation

HEK-293T cells were transfected with the plasmid and cultured for 36 h and then lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. Similarly, testicular tissues were lysed with the same buffer to extract proteins. The lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected as total protein samples. For the immunoprecipitation procedure, the total protein from testicular tissues was equally divided into two aliquots. One aliquot was incubated overnight with 2 µg of IgG antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abmart), while the other was incubated with 2 µg of GARIN3 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abclonal). Additionally, 10% of the protein samples derived from both cells and testicular tissues were set aside as the “input” controls. The remaining proteins underwent incubation at 4 °C for 2 h with 15 µL of FLAG gel, HA beads, or Protein A/G beads depending on the experimental design and requirements. Following incubation, the beads were washed four times to remove excess buffer. Finally, the immunoprecipitated proteins bound to the gel or beads were resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and analyzed by Western blot assay alongside the input samples.

Human sperm sample collection

Fresh human sperm samples were obtained from clinical donors. Each sample was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 1 min, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. The sperm pellet was then resuspended in PBS and washed by centrifugation for 2–3 times. The resulting sperm pellets were stored at − 80 °C for subsequent Western blot analysis. Samples were divided into two groups according to clinical IVF outcomes: the normal fertilization group (patients with normal routine sperm parameters and IVF fertilization rates ≥ 60%) [16] and the poor fertilization group (patients with normal routine sperm parameters, IVF fertilization rates < 30%, and normal ICSI outcomes after exclusion of female factors) [17]. The study involving human sperm samples was conducted in strict accordance with ethical standards. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Shanghai Institute of Planned Parenthood Research (Approval No. PJ2021-26). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Western blot

Tissues from the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, testis, and epididymis of mice, along with capacitated sperm harvested from the epididymal cauda and collected human sperm samples, were all lysed in RIPA buffer (WellBio) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected as total protein extracts. Protein concentrations were measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membranes were probed with secondary antibodies for signal detection. Protein bands were visualized using ECL western blotting substrates (Tanon, 180–5001) and chemiluminescence imaging. Band intensities were analyzed and quantified using ImageJ/FIJI software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Immunofluorescent staining

Testes from adult WT male mice were collected and fixed overnight in 4% Bouins solution at 4 °C. The fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and dewaxed and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were blocked with an antibody dilution buffer containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GARIN3 (1:100 dilution, Abclonal). Following the primary antibody incubation, the sections were washed four times with PBST (Phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20). The sections were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:500 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and lectin PNA (1:500 dilution, Sigma) at 37 °C for 2 h. DNA labeling was performed by incubating the specimens with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000 dilution, Abcam) at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, the stained sections were visualized using a Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Sperm samples were collected from the cauda epididymidis of Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− male mice. The specimens were fixed by incubation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon Biotech, A500684) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by permeabilization in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The sperm were then blocked with a buffer solution (3% BSA in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h. The sperm were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the samples were incubated with corresponding fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies and lectin PNA (1:500 dilution, Sigma) at room temperature for 2 h. DNA labeling was then performed by incubating the specimens with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000 dilution, Abcam) at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, immunofluorescence-stained samples were imaged using a Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Generation of Garin3 knockout mice using CRISPR/Cas9

The Garin3 knockout mouse model in the C57BL/6J background was generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing technology by Cyagen Biosciences (China). Briefly, four distinct guide RNA (gRNA) sequences targeting Garin3 were selected: gRNA1 (TGG AAC TGA AGA CCG CAA CTT GG), gRNA2 (TGC ACC AGT ATT AGC TTG TCA GG), gRNA3 (GGG TGT TGG TCT GAT TTG AGG GG), and gRNA4 (TGA CCC ATG ACC TCC CCT TAG GG). These gRNAs were designed to induce the deletion of exons 1 and 2 of the Garin3 gene. Cas9 mRNA and the four gRNAs were co-injected into fertilized C57BL/6J mouse oocytes to create targeted gene-edited offspring. F0 founder mice underwent genotyping using PCR followed by Sanger sequencing. Subsequently, the founders were bred through successive generations until homozygous Garin3 knockout mice were obtained. For routine genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies. Two allele‑specific primer sets were used: a mutant‑specific pair (F1: 5′‑TAAAATGCTAGCCTTGACACATCAC‑3′ and R1: 5′‑ACTTGCCCCTACAGTTTAAGAAACA‑3′) yielding a 637‑bp product, and a wild‑type‑specific pair (F2: 5′‑TGGAGAAGATCATCCTTGAAACCA‑3′ and R1: 5′‑ACTTGCCCCTACAGTTTAAGAAACA‑3′) yielding a 534‑bp product.

Fertility assessment

To evaluate fertility, three-month-old WT (Garin3+/+) and homozygous (Garin3−/−) male mice were paired with adult WT female mice for mating. Upon detection of vaginal plugs, female mice were isolated and maintained until parturition. Subsequently, the total number of newborn pups was determined and recorded for each group.

Sperm parameter analysis

To assess sperm parameters, 3-month-old Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− male mice were sacrificed, and spermatozoa were isolated from the cauda epididymidis. The collected sperm samples were incubated in human tubal fluid (HTF) medium (Merck Millipore, Germany) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO₂ for 45 min. Following incubation, sperm motility and kinematic parameters were evaluated using computer-assisted sperm analysis (Hamilton Thorne IVOS II, Beverly, MA, USA).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and scanning electron microscopy

For HE staining, mouse testes were fixed in a testes fixative solution (Servicebio, G1121). The samples underwent a series of processing steps, including dehydration with graded ethanol solutions, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. The sections were first deparaffinized for 10 min, followed by rehydration in decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%) for 5 min each. After a 1-minute wash with distilled water, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min. Subsequently, eosin staining was performed for 1–2 s, and the sections were immediately dehydrated in anhydrous ethanol for 1–2 min. Finally, the sections were cleared in xylene and observed under a microscope.

For scanning electron microscopy analysis, sperm samples from adult Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− male mice were prepared as follows. After washing, the sperm were evenly spread onto 2 mm × 3 mm glass slides and air-dried at room temperature. Subsequently, the slides were immersed in 2.5% (vol/vol) glutaraldehyde solution and fixed overnight at 4 °C to preserve cellular structures. Following triple washing with PBS, the specimens underwent a stepwise dehydration process using an ascending series of ethanol concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%). After dehydration, a CO₂ critical-point dryer was used to remove residual solvents without causing structural distortion. The dried samples were then mounted on aluminum stubs and coated with a thin layer of gold for 3 min using a sputter coater to enhance conductivity. Scanning electron microscopy images were acquired using an SU8010 microscope (HITACHI, Japan) according to standard operating protocols, enabling high-resolution visualization of sperm morphology and surface features.

In vitro fertilization

Female mice aged 7 to 8 weeks underwent superovulation. Initially, 10 international units (IU) of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; Ningbo Second Hormone Factory) were intraperitoneally injected. After 46 to 48 h, 10 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG; Ningbo Second Hormone Factory) were administered. After 12 h, sperm were harvested from the cauda epididymis of Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− male mice and incubated for capacitation in separate droplets of HTF at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 1 h.

For conventional fertilization, cumulus-intact oocytes retrieved from superovulated females were transferred into a fresh HTF droplet followed by the addition of capacitated sperm. In the case of ZP-free oocyte fertilization, capacitated sperm were first transferred into a new HTF droplet to ensure even dispersion. Oocytes were collected from superovulated females, and the cumulus cells were removed by treating them with hyaluronidase (0.3 mg/ml; H4272, Sigma-Aldrich). The oocytes were then transferred into acidic Tyrode’s solution (T1788, Sigma-Aldrich) to dissolve the ZP. The resulting ZP-free oocytes were then placed in an HTF droplet containing capacitated sperm.

In both fertilization conditions, oocytes and sperm were co-incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h. Following incubation, the sperm were removed, and two-pronuclear (2PN) zygotes were selected and transferred into KSOM medium (M1430, EasyCheck) for blastocyst culture at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Sperm–ZP binding

To evaluate sperm–ZP binding ability, cumulus-oocyte complexes were initially treated with 0.3 mg/ml hyaluronic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, H4272). After a 2-minute incubation, the cumulus cells were removed and the denuded oocytes were co-incubated with capacitated sperm for 30 min. The samples were then washed three times, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, and stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000 dilution, Abcam) for 15 min in the dark to fluorescently label sperm DNA bound to the ZP. Finally, the total number of sperm attached to the oocytes was counted under a fluorescence microscope to quantify sperm–ZP binding capacity.

We next used a pre-incubation strategy with antibodies to determine whether Garin3 modulates sperm-ZP binding via direct action or indirect regulation of other molecules. Sperm isolated from the cauda epididymidis of WT male mice were pre-incubated separately with IgG (1:100 dilution, Abmart), GARIN3 (1:100 dilution, Abclonal), and SPACA1 (1:100 dilution, Proteintech) antibodies in a humidified atmosphere of 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 30 min. These antibodies were then added to HTF drops at a 1:1000 dilution for fertilization. Capacitated sperm, pre-incubated with antibodies, were then co-cultured with granulosa-cell-free normal oocytes in the fertilization droplets for 30 min. The subsequent experimental procedures were consistent with those of the standard ZP-binding assay.

Acrosome reaction assay

Sperm were collected from the cauda epididymidis of Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− male mice and incubated in HTF medium at 37 °C for 5 min and 60 min to assess spontaneous AR before and after capacitation, respectively. For induced AR, after capacitation, 500 µL of the upper sperm suspension was collected and divided into 50 µL aliquots. Sperm were treated with 15 µM calcium ionophore A23187 to induce the AR and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Following treatment, 20 µL of sperm suspension was spotted onto glass slides, air-dried, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and washed with ddH2O. Acrosomal status was determined by Coomassie Blue staining (B104237, Aladdin). Slides were imaged, and the percentage of acrosome-reacted sperm was quantified. At least 200 sperm were evaluated per experimental condition.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection

After obtaining granulosa-free oocytes and capacitated sperm by the aforementioned method, ICSI was performed. Sperm heads were detached from their tails via ultrasonication using a Skymen JP020S device (China). Using a microinjection system, a single spermatozoon was injected into each oocyte. The injected oocytes were transferred to KSOM (M1430, EasyCheck) medium and cultured in a cell incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Statistical analysis

Data from at least three independent experiments were included in the analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism GraphPad 9 software. For data comparison, two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was applied, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Garin3 binds to Rab2 and is expressed from the acrosomal phase of spermatogenesis

Previous studies have shown that Garin1a and Garin1b of the GARIN family cooperatively regulate sperm acrosome formation during step 5 (cap phase) but are not expressed in the acrosomal and maturation phases [15]. To further explore whether additional proteins collaborate with the RAB family to regulate sperm function during and after sperm maturation, we separately incubated IgG antibodies and RAB2 antibodies with sperm obtained from the epididymis of WT mice and performed interaction mass spectrometry to identify the key proteins that regulate RAB2 in mature sperm. A total of 10,980 peptides and 1,706 proteins interacting with RAB2 were identified, 72 of which were differentially expressed proteins, including 62 upregulated proteins and 10 downregulated proteins in sperm incubated with RAB2 antibody (fold change > 2 and p < 0.01) (Fig. 1A). Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was then conducted on the 62 significantly up-regulated proteins. The GO analysis showed that these differentially expressed proteins were highly enriched in acrosome assembly and in processes related to sperm head formation. Notably, members of the GARIN family were significantly enriched among these proteins (Fig. 1B). Among the GARIN family, GARIN3 was most highly expressed in mature sperm, and interaction mass spectrometry also showed that GARIN3 was the most enriched protein (Figure S1A-B). Thus, Garin3 likely plays a major role in sperm maturation. In order to further verify the interaction between GARIN3 and RAB2, we performed co-immunoprecipitation analyses in HEK-293T cells and in mouse testes. The results confirmed the interaction between GARIN3 and RAB2 (Fig. 1C-D, Figure S1C).

To further investigate the specific role of Garin3 in male fertility, we first examined its expression and localization. Garin3 was predominantly expressed in the testes and epididymis of mice, with minimal expression in other tissues, as shown by immunoblot (Fig. 1E). We further assessed the expression and localization of GARIN3 throughout the course of spermatogenesis using immunofluorescence. The acrosomes were labeled with PNA staining to distinguish different spermatogenic stages. GARIN3 was found to be expressed in the acrosomal and maturation stages, but not in the Golgi and cap stages (Fig. 1F). Given that sperm acrosome formation occurs during the acrosomal stage and that fertilization capacity is acquired during the maturation stage, we hypothesized that Garin3 participates in physiological processes associated with acrosomes, which in turn affect sperm fertilization capacity.

Garin3−/− male mice are infertile

The above results suggested that Garin3 plays an important role in spermatogenesis, and Garin3-knockout mice (Garin3−/−) were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 to investigate its function in male fertility (Figure S1D). PCR genotyping demonstrated the effective knockout of Garin3 in the targeted mice (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis confirmed that GARIN3 was completely deleted in Garin3−/− mice (Fig. 2B). When Garin3+/+ and Garin3−/− mice were mated with WT female mice, the Garin3−/− males were infertile (Fig. 2C). Meanwhile, matings between heterozygous males and homozygous females were fertile, producing an average litter size of 6.4 ± 1.3 pups (Figure S2A). These results indicate that Garin3 heterozygous male mice and homozygous female mice exhibit normal fertility and are capable of producing viable offspring through mating. The testis size, testis weight, body weight, and the ratio of testis weight to body weight in Garin3−/− males were comparable to those in Garin3+/+ males (Fig. 1C-D, Figure S2B-C). Next, we evaluated the sperm quality in Garin3−/− mice. We first analyzed the physiological parameters of the sperm in the cauda epididymis using computer-assisted sperm analysis. Compared to Garin3+/+ mice, the sperm concentration of Garin3−/− mice was decreased by approximately 40% (Fig. 1F). However, no significant abnormalities were observed in sperm motility, progressive motility, or other kinematic parameters (Fig. 1G, Figure S1E). Histologically, Hematoxylin-Eosin-Saffron staining of testis sections revealed that the progression of spermatogenesis in Garin3−/− male mice was mostly intact (Fig. 1H). Scanning electron microscopy showed that, unlike Garin3+/+ spermatozoa, which featured a sharply defined and slender hook structure at the sperm head, spermatozoa from Garin3−/− mice displayed a little more blunted appearance in the hook region. Moreover, Garin3⁻/⁻ sperm exhibited generally normal morphology, with no evidence of structural abnormalities (Fig. 1I).

Garin3−/− sperm lead to failed IVF due to the inability to bind to the ZP

Next, we explored why Garin3−/− male mice are infertile. We performed in vitro fertilization (IVF) experiments using sperm from Garin3+/+, Garin3+/− and Garin3−/− male mice paired with oocytes from WT female mice. The results showed that almost no 2PN zygotes or 2-cell embryos were formed with Garin3−/− sperm (Fig. 2A), wherCh Garin3+/− spCin3+/+ males (Figure S2D-F). Furthermore, we assessed whether Garin3 deficiency affects female reproduction. Oocytes from Garin3−/− females exhibited normal fertilization rates and supported subsequent embryonic development comparable to those from Garin3+/+ controls (Figure S2G-I ), suggesting that Garin3 is dispensable for female fertility. Thus, the observed impairment in fertility is specific to Garin3−/− males. We next removed the oocyte ZP and performed IVF, after which Garin3−/− male mice showed fertilization rates that were comparable to Garin3+/+ male mice (Fig. 3B-C). Moreover, embryos derived from ZP-free IVF using Garin3−/− sperm developed normally to the 2-cell stage and even to the blastocyst stage, with no significant differences in developmental capacity compared with the Garin3+/+ group (Fig. 3D-E). Because the fertilization ability of Garin3−/− sperm could be restored by oocyte ZP removal, we further conducted a sperm-ZP binding assay to evaluate the ability of Garin3-deficient sperm to bind to the ZP. The results showed that the number of sperm from Garin3−/− mice that bound to each oocyte was significantly decreased (Fig. 3F-G). The acrosome reaction (AR) is a critical process that allows sperm to penetrate the ZP and fuse with the oocyte. To investigate whether Garin3 deficiency affects the AR in mouse sperm, we examined the spontaneous and induced AR rates in both WT and Garin3−/− sperm. A23187, a calcium ionophore widely used to trigger the AR in mammalian sperm [18], was employed to induce the AR. Sperm were capacitated in HTF medium, and AR was assessed at 5 min and 60 min under spontaneous conditions or following A23187 induction. Acrosome status was evaluated by Coomassie Blue staining, followed by imaging and quantitative analysis. Compared with WT sperm, Garin3−/− sperm showed no significant difference in spontaneous AR rates. However, the A23187-induced AR rate was significantly reduced in Garin3−/− sperm (Figure S2J-K). These findings suggest that, in addition to impaired sperm‑ZP binding, Garin3‑deficient sperm also exhibit defective AR, which further compromises their ability to penetrate the ZP and undergo subsequent fusion with the oocyte. Consequently, Garin3 deficiency prevents sperm from completing the secondary binding with the oocyte, impairs ZP penetration, and ultimately leads to complete infertility.

Garin3 is not directly involved in sperm–ZP binding

We then investigated the role of Garin3 in sperm–ZP binding. First, we determined whether Garin3 is a direct participant in the process. Antibody-blocking sperm–ZP binding assays have been widely used to evaluate whether a protein is directly involved in sperm–ZP binding. We incubated WT male mice sperm with a GARIN3 antibody, SPACA1 antibody, and IgG separately to evaluate the impact of these antibody incubations on sperm–ZP binding, aiming to clarify the role of Garin3 in this process. The results showed that sperm incubated with SPACA1 antibodies exhibited a significantly reduced binding capacity to the oocyte ZP compared with the control group incubated with IgG antibodies, but sperm incubated with GARIN3 antibodies did not (Fig. 4A-B). These findings suggest that GARIN3 is not directly involved in sperm–ZP binding.

Garin3 deficiency affects the expression and localization of RAB2, SPACA1 , ADAM2, and ACR

Our results showed that although Garin3−/− male mice exhibited only mild oligospermia and sperm head deformities, they were completely infertile. Previous studies have shown that mild oligospermia and morphological abnormalities are not enough to cause complete fertilization failure [19, 20]. Thus, we hypothesized that Garin3 may indirectly regulate spermatogenesis by regulating other acrosome-associated proteins. We found that Garin3 was continuously expressed from the acrosome stage, thus Garin3 might affect sperm–ZP binding by regulating the expression and localization of proteins related to this process. After reviewing the literature, we selected RAB2, a known interacting partner of GARIN3, as well as three proteins involved in sperm-oocyte binding–SPACA1, ADAM2, and ACR [21–23]–to investigate whether these proteins are affected by Garin3 deficiency.

We first detected the expression of these proteins. Immunoblotting showed a significant decrease in RAB2, SPACA1, and ADAM2 expression, whereas ACR levels were not significantly reduced in Garin3−/− sperm (Fig. 4C-D). Additionally, when examining testicular tissues, RAB2, SPACA1, ADAM2, and ACR levels in Garin3−/− mice were all significantly lower than those in Garin3+/+ mice (Figure S3A-B).

In addition to expression level changes, we further investigated the effect of Garin3 on the localization of these proteins. Immunofluorescence staining was used to observe the localization of these proteins in sperm from Garin3−/− mice before and after the acrosome reaction. In non-acrosome-reacted sperm, compared with Garin3+/+ mice, the locations of RAB2 and SPACA1 in Garin3−/− sperm were changed. Compared with the nuclear area of ​​the sperm head, the localization areas of RAB2 and SPACA1 increased, while the area of ​​ADAM2 decreased. These results indicated that the subacrosomal layer of Garin3-deficient sperm expands to the post-acrosomal sheath region (Fig. 4E-F). Additionally, the ACR signals at the acrosome shifted significantly forward, resulting in a decrease in the ratio of their fluorescence area to the nuclear fluorescence area (Figure S3C-D). In acrosome-reacted sperm, with the exception of ACR, which was not detected, the localization patterns of other proteins in Garin3−/− mouse sperm were similar to those observed in non-acrosome-reacted sperm (Figure S4A-B). These results suggested that Garin3 affects sperm–ZP binding by regulating the localization of acrosome-associated proteins.

ICSI can reverse the fertilization failure of Garin3−/− male mice

Given that sperm from Garin3−/− male mice failed to penetrate the ZP, we performed ICSI to determine whether this could overcome the fertilization failure in these mice. The results showed that Garin3−/− male mouse sperm successfully fertilized WT oocytes through ICSI. Moreover, the embryos generated from ICSI developed into blastocysts, with no significant differences observed compared to embryos produced by Garin3+/+ sperm (Fig. 5A-B). These results suggested that ICSI might be an effective therapeutic strategy for male infertility associated with Garin3 deficiency.

GARIN3 expression is downregulated in sperm from patients with poor fertilization

Our findings showed that male mice with Garin3 deficiency suffer from fertilization failure. To further validate the relevance of Garin3 in human reproductive function, we collected sperm samples from individuals with normal fertilization rates and individuals with poor fertilization rates after IVF and quantified the levels of GARIN3 expression. The results showed that GARIN3 protein levels were significantly decreased in sperm from individuals with poor fertilization compared to those with normal fertilization, and this was consistent with the phenotypic observations in knockout mice (Fig. 6A-B). These findings suggest that Garin3 plays a key role in human reproductive capacity.

Discussion

In this work we performed a systematic study on Garin3 and showed that it plays a critical function in spermatogenesis and male fertility. In a previous study, Wang et al. [14] showed that members of the Golgi-associated RAB2 interactor protein family (GARINs), including Garin3, actively influence male reproductive capacity in mice. They attributed this phenotype to abnormalities in head structure and PNA morphology. Notably, Garin4-deficient sperm exhibit normal fertility despite having the most pronounced defects in terms of head structure, the greatest proportion of head abnormalities, and the most aberrant PNA morphology among all members of the GARIN family. At the same time, they found that the Garin3-deficient phenotype was the most severe, showing complete infertility. The authors attributed this to the reduced expression of ADAM family proteins. However, they did not conduct a detailed study on the expression, location, and physiological role of Garin3, and thus it is still unclear whether Garin3 directly participates in or directly affects sperm–ZP binding.

In this study, we conducted an in-depth investigation of Garin3 to determine its potential regulatory mechanisms. We found that Garin3 is expressed from the initiation of acrosome formation during spermatogenesis and persists through to the sperm maturation stage. Garin3 had the highest expression level in mature sperm. Spermatozoa lacking Garin3 showed no changes in motility and displayed only mild morphological defects; however, their capacity to bind to the oocyte ZP was greatly weakened, ultimately resulting in IVF failure. The expression pattern of Garin3 strongly suggests its involvement in acrosome biogenesis, the regulation of sperm development, and epididymal sperm maturation, potentially modulating acrosomal enzyme synthesis, transport, storage, or activation and thus influencing sperm penetration and ZP binding. Furthermore, we observed that Garin3−/− sperm exhibited a significantly reduced ability to undergo the AR following A23187 induction. This finding suggests that the absence of Garin3 not only impairs sperm-ZP recognition and binding but also compromises the subsequent AR via disrupting the expression and localization of acrosome-associated proteins, which is essential for penetrating the ZP after initial binding. Together, these two defects—impaired ZP binding and compromised AR—underlie the reduced sperm-ZP adhesion and the consequent failure in IVF. Notably, this fertilization defect could be rescued by ZP removal, further supporting that the observed impairments are specific to ZP interaction and penetration. We found that Garin3 is not directly involved in sperm–ZP binding and that the fertilization failure associated with Garin3 deficiency primarily results from altered expression and localization of acrosome function-related proteins, including SPACA1, ADAM2, and ACR. To explain these distinct localization changes, we consider that Garin3 deficiency disrupts the structural integrity of the acrosome. In Garin3−/− sperm, the acrosomal structure becomes loose and abnormally expanded, which may physically push the equatorial segment posteriorly. As a result, the spatial arrangement of acrosomal and equatorial proteins is altered in a coordinated manner. SPACA1 normally translocates from the equatorial region to the anterior acrosome during epididymal maturation [24]. ADAM2 is localized in the post-acrosomal region [25]. RAB2 localizes to the acrosomal membrane and perinuclear theca and helps direct vesicle trafficking [15]. ACR resides within the acrosomal matrix [26]. The location of these proteins is strictly limited. In Garin3-/- sperm, vesicle trafficking is disrupted, causing proacrosomal vesicles to accumulate around the perinuclear region instead of being properly targeted to the anterior nucleus. Consequently, SPACA1 and RAB2, which are normally located in the anterior part of the sperm head, expand posteriorly, while ADAM2, situated in the post-acrosomal region, becomes compressed. The reduced ACR signal likely reflects shrinkage or collapse of the acrosome itself due to defective assembly, rather than a primary mislocalization. Collectively, these localization changes are not independent events but rather coordinated consequences of a common structural defect. These findings support an essential role of Garin3 in maintaining the spatial architecture of the sperm head. We found that Garin3 localizes to the sperm acrosome through its interaction with RAB2. Based on our results, we propose that Garin3, which is highly expressed from the acrosome phase through maturation, modulates the expression or localization of other acrosome-associated proteins through RAB2, thereby affecting sperm–ZP binding.

We found that fertilization failure due to Garin3 deficiency could be effectively overcome by bypassing the ZP-binding step through ZP removal or by performing ICSI. The embryos that were generated from these fertilizations could develop into blastocysts. These results thus suggest that Garin3 primarily functions in sperm–ZP binding without affecting sperm–oocyte fusion, fertilization, or later stages of embryonic development.

Additionally, we compared GARIN3 expression levels between human sperm samples from fertilization-normal and fertilization-poor IVF outcomes. Notably, although no classical Mendelian diseases caused by coding mutations in Garin3 have been recorded in the OMIM database to date. We observed a reduction of GARIN3 in IVF poor patients; however, this represents a correlative finding rather than evidence of causality. The reduced GARIN3 expression observed in sperm from IVF fertilization-poor patients is more likely to arise from complex and multifactorial regulatory abnormalities rather than single inactivating mutations. Based on previous studies, we speculate that such decreased expression may be attributed to multiple mechanisms, including aberrant epigenetic regulation such as abnormal DNA methylation in the gene promoter region [27] or dysregulated histone modifications [28], disrupted upstream signaling pathways such as excessive oxidative stress in semen that may suppress its expression [29], dysregulation of the transcription factor network [30], genomic copy number variations affecting regulatory regions [4], and abnormal post-translational modifications and protein stability [31, 32], including dysregulated phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and SUMOylation. These observations provide important directions for future studies aimed at performing comprehensive genetic and epigenetic analyses of GARIN3 regulatory regions in infertile populations. We further explored potential clinical interventions for Garin3-related infertility, such as ZP removal to facilitate fertilization or the application of ICSI. This cross-species consistency suggests that Garin3 plays a conserved role in male reproduction.

In summary, we demonstrate that Garin3 deficiency results in male infertility, and we have elucidated its physiological mechanism. Our study shows that GARIN3 acts as a regulatory protein for the expression and localization of acrosome-associated proteins from the acrosome stage to mature sperm. In addition, we found that GARIN3 expression is decreased in human sperm with poor fertilization outcomes in IVF, implying the conserved importance of GARIN3 in mammalian reproductive function. This study highlights the critical role of Garin3 in regulating sperm–ZP binding and thus provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms behind male infertility. Investigating the role of Garin3 and its associated regulatory pathways might therefore pave the way for novel diagnostic approaches and therapeutic interventions.
