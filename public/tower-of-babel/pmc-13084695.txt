Neuroprotection trial design in progressive supranuclear palsy: challenges and solutions
Authors: Lawrence I Golbe, Ronald G Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe review the specific challenges posed to neuroprotective trial design by progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), a rare disorder difficult to diagnose and to assess quantitatively.METHODSFocusing on reducing the size and cost of trials, we review elements of PSP neuroprotection trial methodology and formulate a new minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for PSP to be used as a potential outcome milestone.RESULTSWhile the original, 28‐item version of the PSP Rating Scale (PSPRS) remains the best‐validated and most widely published clinical outcome measure, promising improvements include the PSPRS‐15 and ‐10; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) volumetry; wearable movement‐sensitive devices; platform, futility, basket, factorial, adaptive, and multistage designs; customized endpoints; baseline predictor stratification; and the replacement of a continuous disability scale with attainment of milestones, possibly including our newly calculated MCID.DISCUSSIONThe specific assessment tools we deem most valuable could be used individually or in combination to reduce the required N and cost of PSP neuroprotection trials.Keywords: adaptive trials, baseline predictor stratification, basket trials, customized endpoints, factorial trials, futility trials, minimum clinically important difference, movement‐sensing wearable devices, MRI volumetry, mult‐stage trials, neuroprotection trials, platform trials, progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), PSP Rating Scale, rating scales

INTRODUCTION

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) has been viewed as a test bed for trials of disease‐modifying treatments for other tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), but it is a devastating condition deserving study in its own right.

Neuroprotective treatment remains elusive. This review discusses the current standard and special challenges in PSP trial design. It also suggests improvements to reduce the N and/or double‐blind treatment durations and to better reflect the goals of patients and families. Use of minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs), the theme of this journal issue, is one potential route to more efficient trial design in PSP.

CLINICAL FEATURES OF PSP RELEVANT TO TRIAL DESIGN

Natural history, etiology, and pathogenesis

PSP produces severe disability via imbalance with falls, frontal dementia, affective changes, insomnia and daytime somnolence, impaired eye movement, dysarthria, dysphagia, generalized rigidity and bradykinesia, and urinary incontinence. The quality of life over much of the short disease course is poor. Its prevalence rate of 6.9 per 100,000 1 exceeds that of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a better‐known and better‐studied condition. Its median onset age is in the mid‐60s, and its average survival of approximately 7 years 1 is far less than the 19 years for the general US population of the same initial age. PSP is only slightly more aggressive than Parkinson's disease (PD), where the mean survival just before the levodopa era was 9.2 years, 2 but PSP responds at best modestly and transiently to dopaminergic medications and even less so to deep‐brain stimulation.

The etiology of PSP, like that of the other sporadic neurodegenerative diseases, has both a genetic component, with 14 known risk allelic variants, and some acquired factors, the best confirmed being lesser educational attainment, with some evidence for metals exposure and dietary exposure to mitochondrial toxins. 3

Its pathogenesis, like that of other non‐Mendelian neurodegenerative diseases, is far from fully understood. The cellular damage starts in the glia of the subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus externa, and substantia nigra. It spreads to the neurons and to other brain areas across synapses and through the action of microglia, although the importance of these relative to other mechanisms is not known. Eventually, no brain area or neurotransmitter system is spared. The agent of disease spread appears to be misfolded, aggregated tau protein that can template its folding pattern onto normal tau in a prion‐like manner. The pathologic hallmark of PSP is tau aggregates of various morphologies in the glia and neurons. Other contributing cellular derangements are impairments in mitochondrial function, protein homeostasis, neuroinflammation, epigenetic genomic DNA modifications, and DNA transcription.

Presymptomatic period

As long as 7.8 years before the appearance of more specific symptoms, patients in the UK Biobank who eventually developed PSP were found to have deficits in reaction time, hand grip, and a variety of cognitive tasks. 4 Close relatives of patients with PSP have deficits in motor, behavioral, cognitive, and olfactory function more often than relatives of controls, and presymptomatic PSP has also been demonstrated by autopsy in a large, unselected population, where 6% of neurologically asymptomatic individuals were found to have early histological PSP changes. 5 Designers of neuroprotection trials will find this an obstacle to their goal of starting the treatment very early in the pathogenetic process. However, if sufficiently sensitive and convenient trait and state markers become available, presymptomatic cases would provide a large population in which to test and prescribe neuroprotection when it may be most effective.

Phenotypes

The canonical phenotype described in 1964, now called PSP‐Richardson syndrome (PSP‐Richardson), accounts for about half of all PSP. A second phenotype, PSP‐Parkinsonism (PSP‐P), was described in 2005 and accounts for an additional third, 6 with a more prolonged course of approximately 9 years. Most cases of PSP‐P are misdiagnosed as PD until highly predictive features, such as the poor levodopa response or the downgaze abnormality, emerge. More recently, eight other phenotypes have been described, most with survival durations longer than that of PSP‐Richardson. 6 , 7 Each is distinguished by one of the components of PSP‐Richardson as its first and worst feature. However, most patients converge on a PSP‐Richardson phenotype after a mean of 2.9 years 8 and share a unitary cellular pathology with varying anatomic emphasis.

For statistical convenience, the 10 PSP phenotypes are often binned into three neuroanatomically oriented groups: PSP‐Richardson; PSP‐cortical (speech/language, frontal behavioral, and corticobasal phenotypes); and PSP‐subcortical (Parkinsonism, progressive gait freezing, ocular motor, cerebellar, postural instability, and primary lateral sclerosis phenotypes). 9 In early disease stages, most patients fulfill criteria for multiple phenotypes. 10 As will be explained, the diversity of phenotypes presents significant challenges to clinical trial design.

Advantages of PSP as a tauopathy test bed

The use of PSP as a surrogate for the more common tau‐based disorders is prompted by certain practicalities: the absence of a second major aggregating protein analogous to amyloid beta of AD; its relatively rapid progression; the specificity of both the 1996 and 2017 criteria for PSP‐Richardson; and the availability of the PSP Rating Scale (PSPRS) as clinical outcome measure with good inter‐rater reliability, sensitivity to progression, and ample field experience. 11 , 12 , 13

TRIAL DESIGN CHALLENGES IN PSP

Diagnostic criteria

Most studies of PSP prevalence and incidence use criteria the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke—Society for Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (NINDS‐SPSP) published in 1996, 14 well before the non‐Richardson phenotypes were recognized. It delineates only what the 2017 criteria (the Movement Disorders Society [MDS] criteria for PSP) define as PSP‐Richardson. The newer criteria also address some of the recently described phenotypes and improve early‐stage sensitivity by adding a new category, “suggestive of PSP,” to the “possible PSP” and “probable PSP” categories shared with the 1996 criteria. The point of the “possible” category is to increase sensitivity while accepting a loss of specificity. The “suggestive of” definition raises the overall sensitivity to a very useful 84%, but at the cost of reducing the specificity to 40% 15 (Table 1).

Clinical trials in PSP could, in theory, balance diagnostic accuracy with recruitment needs by allowing both “possible” and “probable” criteria, with an acceptably low 16% false positive rate. However, there are few patients with “possible PSP” at any given time. In a study at the Mayo Clinic, only 5% of individuals with PSP qualified as “possible PSP” at the initial visit, with 47% “probable,” 36% “suggestive of,” and 12% unclassifiable. 15 A similar result occurred at a consortium of centers in Germany at 3 years after onset, when clinical trial enrollment would be most likely. Of those 204 patients, only six (3%) qualified as “possible” PSP. 8

The “suggestive of” definition, with only 60% positive predictive value, should be confined to evaluations of diagnostic markers or epidemiological studies seeking complete population ascertainment with subsequent application of a more specific marker. About half of patients with “suggestive of” PSP convert to “possible” or “probable” PSP within 12 months. 16 Such patients could therefore be enrolled in PSP trials and included in the primary outcome analysis if and only if their diagnoses had progressed to “possible” or “probable” before the data closeout date.

Dropouts

An important challenge to 12‐month PSP trials is dropouts. Twelve‐month dropout rates were 23% in the davunetide trial 17 and 35% in the tideglusib trial. 18 The latter includes a 5% mortality rate. By comparison, unpublished data (D. Hengartner et al.) shows that for neuroprotective trials in PD with a mean duration of 19.6 months, the mean dropout rate was only 15%.

The true causes of dropout in PSP trials are multifactorial and difficult to ascertain. They include treatment‐emergent adverse effects, difficulty traveling to the study site, multiple hours of bedside testing, and frustration at a lack of absolute symptomatic drug benefit. Experience suggests that even patients or caregivers who understand the distinction between symptomatic and protective treatment cannot gauge a slowing of progression relative to what would have occurred under standard care. The resulting suspicion that the subject had been assigned to placebo further contributes to dropout risk.

Enrollment challenges

Disease‐related obstacles

Slow enrollment in PSP trials is a function of low disease prevalence, rapid progression through the trial‐eligible stage, insufficient awareness of PSP among potential referring clinicians and the public, and the absence to date of a sensitive diagnostic marker in the early stages. False positive diagnoses arise from the absence to date of a diagnostic marker with 100% positive predictive value. Early‐stage patients are difficult to identify and to enroll because existing diagnostic criteria and markers are insufficiently sensitive, especially for the half of all patients with non‐PSP‐Richardson.

A long latency from symptom onset to a correct PSP diagnosis is the result of the low index of diagnostic suspicion among clinicians, the non‐specific nature of the first symptoms, the absence of early‐stage markers, and the relatively high population prevalence of PD, a frequent false diagnostic alternative. A typical average latency from onset to a correct PSP diagnosis is 3 years. 19 , 20 This means that for PSP‐Richardson, over half of the total disease duration from onset to death has elapsed before the patient can be enrolled in a trial. By that time, the disease process may have passed the window in which the patient is both susceptible to the proposed treatment mechanism and able to participate to the end of a 12‐month trial.

Trial design‐related obstacles

PSP trial inclusion criteria typically allow only PSP‐Richardson and are biased toward subjects sufficiently early in their course to minimize dropout and to maximize ability to cooperate with the protocol. To this end, trials typically require patients to be able to walk five or 10 steps with minimal assistance. Trials also typically impose a maximum symptom duration of 5 years to exclude patients with very slowly progressive conditions with greater likelihood of diagnoses such as vascular parkinsonism or normal pressure hydrocephalus. Cognitive disability sufficient to impair trial compliance is typically minimized by imposing a minimum Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) or Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) in the low 20s.

A longitudinal observational study 21 analyzed a subgroup meeting typical trial inclusion criteria, finding these “trial‐ready” subjects to progress more quickly than others as measured by the PSPRS‐28 (Section 5.1) change and by survival duration. This result provides a warning that a clinical trial result may not apply to all patients with PSP‐Richardson, much less those with the nine trial‐ineligible phenotypes. The reason for that result is not clear, but we point out that cognitive changes contribute only 8% of the PSPRS‐28 (behavioral changes contribute 12%), while motor changes contribute 64%. Patients brought to neurological attention by early‐appearing cognitive loss rather than early motor features are likely to have motor features that, while meeting PSP diagnostic criteria, are only mild and slowly progressing.

Patient disability‐related obstacles

Certain clinical features of PSP may introduce statistical noise, requiring a larger N and/or trial duration. Leading this list is the frontal dementia of PSP, which prompts patients to underreport or impulsively to overreport adverse effects. Impairment of insight into one's disabilities is a challenge to the incorporation of patient preferences into PSP trial design and to subjective outcome reporting. The high trial dropout rate was discussed earlier. Eye movement difficulties can interfere with assessment instruments requiring visual tasks. The limb rigidity and bradykinesia produce difficulty with handwriting that can affect some cognitive test items. The dysarthria and bradyphrenia prolong verbal interactions with study personnel, producing frustration and fatigue. Apathy and anxiety, two common features of PSP, further interfere with bedside and imaging procedures.

Potential trial subjects with more than a mild degree of cognitive loss, with a MMSE or MoCA score below approximately 23, should also be excluded, as such patients may have difficulty reporting adverse effects reliably and otherwise cooperating with the complex demands of the study. This constraint on the range of cognitive disability represented in a trial, while necessary, may limit the generalizability of the study's results.

Gait impairment and general disability‐related obstacles

The early and rapidly progressive gait abnormalities of PSP are also a consideration in designing subject inclusion criteria. Most PSP trials require the patient at screening to demonstrate the ability to walk with no more than minimal assistance. Gait and balance decline rapidly in PSP, and those items comprise a large fraction of the PSPRS, 18 out of 100 points. For these reasons, enrolling patients requiring moderate gait assistance would risk allowing most of them to reach maximal gait disability before the end of the trial. 22 That would introduce Type II error by obscuring any subsequent slowing of progression that might have occurred before the end of the trial.

The same rationale applies to the PSPRS‐28, where a total score of 40 or 50 has been imposed in some trials as a maximum at screening. For patients with a baseline score of 20 to 29, the likelihood of retaining some gait ability 12 months later is 76.4% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 67.5 to 86.5) and only slightly less for scores of 30 to 39 (72.4%, 95% CI: 62.7 to 83.7). But for baseline scores of 40 to 49, the likelihood declines to 51.7% (95% CI: 40.0 to 66.9). 23 It is therefore advisable to impose a maximum baseline PSPRS of 40 or 45.

An alternative is to impose a maximum duration of symptoms at screening, typically 5 years. With a median survival of about 6 years for PSP‐Richardson, allowing more advanced patients into a trial risks excessive dropout due to death or advanced disability. The mean delay from onset to diagnosis of approximately 3 years 24 , 25 leaves a window of only a scant 2 years during which the average patient with PSP‐Richardson can enter a trial.

PHENOTYPIC VARIATION

PSP‐Parkinsonism

Confining trials to PSP‐Richardson slows recruitment and risks constraints on the product's labeled indication. By far the most common non‐Richardson phenotype, PSP‐P, accounts for 35% to 40% of all PSP. 25 In the tideglusib trial, the 82 subjects with PSP‐Richardson progressed 14.62 (± 9.88) PSPRS points at 60 weeks, and the 56 with PSP‐P progressed only 9.83 (± 7.33) points, although the two groups’ mean PSPRS scores did not differ at study baseline. 10 Similar results were obtained for other outcome variables. The conclusion is that the additional variance arising from including subjects with non‐Richardson subtypes would probably increase the required N to a point that would defeat the advantage of access to a larger candidate pool.

Corticobasal degeneration (CBD)

This is a phenotypically diverse, pathologically defined condition with 10% to 20% of the prevalence of PSP but a similar onset age and survival. Its pathology is most commonly a 4‐repeat tauopathy, as in PSP, but the pathognomonic cellular change is astrocytic plaques rather than the tufted astrocytes of PSP. A minority have transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43), as opposed to tau, pathology. 26 , 27

The most common clinical expression of CBD is corticobasal syndrome (CBD‐CBS), featuring highly asymmetric motor, sensory, and cognitive deficits of both cortical and basal ganglia origin. Most prominent are rigidity, bradykinesia, and tremor as in PD, but with even more asymmetry and a poor levodopa response. CBD‐CBS also features variable dystonia, apraxia, myoclonus, pyramidal signs, and impairment of spatial perception. Features of PSP such as postural instability, bulbar signs, vertical gaze palsy, and frontal cognitive loss occur in a minority phenotype called CBD‐PSP. Other phenotypes are CBD‐frontal behavioral‐spatial syndrome (CBD‐FBS) and CBD‐non‐fluent/agrammatic variant of primary progressive aphasia. 28

Conversely, CBS is the phenotypic expression of multiple pathologies including CBD itself in a third to half of cases. A close second is PSP pathology, followed by AD, dementia with Lewy bodies, and, rarely, Creutzfeldt‐Jakob disease or others. 29

CBD can be indistinguishable from PSP, and the CBD‐PSP versus PSP‐CBS distinction is difficult in living patients with current clinical tools. For this reason, the condition in living patients, no matter how typical, is referred to as CBS, with CBD being reserved for autopsy‐proven cases. For the same reason, analysis of patient cohorts in PSP should be corrected for a misdiagnosis of CBD in about 10% of cases. 26 Confining those cohorts to PSP‐Richardson reduces that misdiagnosis rate but at the cost of constraining ascertainment to only half of the PSP population.

A better solution is to study PSP and CBD together as “4R tauopathies.” The 2017 PSP criteria 30 provide such a definition, subsequently validated in autopsy‐confirmed cases with specificity of 99% in the first year and 88% at the last visit. 31 To date, none of the few known molecular differences between PSP and CBD pathologies discourages that strategy.

The dataset used to validate the PSPRS, having been collected from 1994 to 2005, applied the 1996 criteria for “probable PSP,” which is equivalent to the MDS PSP criteria's definition of “probable PSP‐Richardson” (its most accurate rating short of autopsy). Rating scales for the other nine phenotypes have not been published and the specificity of their MDS PSP diagnostic criteria are far inferior to those for PSP‐Richardson. Thus, the design and analysis of a trial for “probable 4R tauopathy” would have to recognize the existence of two categories of patients: those who do and do not meet probable PSP‐Richardson criteria.

MSA

Another potential PSP lookalike, especially in the early stages of PSP‐P, is a multiple system atrophy of the Parkinsonian phenotype (MSA‐P), an α‐synucleinopathy.

The Neuroprotection and Natural History in Parkinson Plus Syndromes (NNIPPS) study

In an early example of a basket design, the NNIPPS study trialed riluzole in both PSP and MSA, analyzing the two conditions together and separately. 32 Its primary outcome measure was a lengthy, newly created scale validated for both disorders. One rationale for the basket design was the clinical similarity between PSP (particularly PSP‐P) and MSA (particularly MSA‐P) in the early stages, when trial enrollment would be most desirable. Another was that the mechanism of riluzole's efficacy in ALS, a TDP‐43‐opathy, was unknown, suggesting both α‐synucleinopathies and tauopathies as additional potential beneficiaries.

Diagnostic differentiation of MSA from PSP

The NNIPPS trialists designed a set of diagnostic criteria specifically to distinguish PSP from MSA. 33 Of the 256 patients entering the trial with a clinical diagnosis of PSP, only 10 (4%) had their clinical diagnosis changed after 3 years’ follow‐up. Only two of those changes were from PSP to MSA. The NNIPPS Criteria in the patients who reached autopsy during the observation period achieved 84% specificity. 33

The 2017 MDS PSP criteria also have been independently confirmed as valid in excluding MSA. In a series of 66 autopsy‐confirmed cases of PSP at the Mayo Clinic, none had been diagnosed as MSA during life by the Mayo neurologists using those criteria, although two had met criteria for “suggestive of PSP” early in the course. 15

Skin biopsy seed amplification assay for tau has 87.5% sensitivity and 95% specificity for distinguishing advanced or autopsy‐proven PSP from MSA and other synucleinopathies. 34 This test is now becoming widely available and may soon supplement clinical evaluation in PSP trial enrollment criteria. A trial comparing the two is eagerly awaited. Unfortunately, the state of such assays for early or equivocal cases is not nearly as advanced.

Phenotypic subtype‐related variation in progression

Differences in disease progression across PSP phenotypes both help and hinder the trial designer. A longitudinal observation found that PSPRS‐28 scores in patients with PSP‐Richardson progressed more quickly (8.7 points per year) than in other phenotypes (6.3), and survival from onset was shorter (5.6 vs 7.3 years). 21 This means that trials must either exclude a large fraction of potential subjects or use a larger N to correct for these differences statistically. The latter approach may allow sponsors to avoid a potential labeling restriction.

CLINICAL RATING SCALES

The PSPRS

The PSPRS reached its present form and was made widely available in 1995 as conference presentations but was further validated and formally published in 2007. 11 That validation involved 162 patients with PSP‐Richardson who received the scale at each visit from 1995 to 2005. It was designed to cover as many features of PSP as practical within the time constraint of a routine clinical visit and was intended to be used both as a convenient rubric for clinical recordkeeping and as an interventional outcome measure.

PSPRS design

The scale's original and still most commonly used form, herein called the PSPRS‐28, comprises 28 items, of which six are rated on a three‐point scale and 22 on a five‐point scale. The items are divided into six domains: Daily Activities by History (seven items, 28 points), Mental (four items, 16 points), Bulbar (two items, 8 points), Ocular Motor (four items, 16 points), Limb Motor (six items, 16 points) and Gait/Midline (five items, 20 points). Each item is accompanied by a list of rules and tips for administration that should be immediately visible to the examiner on the same screen or page. The list published with the scale in 2007 11 has since been expanded to respond to users’ experience. 35 PSPRS‐28

PSPRS progression

Validation of the PSPRS‐28 is partly via demonstration of the score's progression over time, which is approximately linear, at a mean rate (aggregating all phenotypes) of 11.3 points per year. 11 This is the case for the stage of PSP where most clinical drug trials occur, and baseline score does not correlate with subsequent 12‐month rate of progression, at least in a population suitable for clinical neuroprotection trials. 17 , 36 But there is preliminary evidence that the rate of progression is slower in the first year or two 37 and also toward the end of the course, starting with the sixth year of illness. 38 The latter could be the result of a ceiling effect, where some patients never reach maximal severity in a few scale items or even fail to develop those features at all. This effect could become important for the design of future long‐term, open‐label trial extensions. Similarly, the slower apparent progression in the first 2 years, while not important given our current diagnostic insensitivity to early‐stage PSP, may well affect the design of future trials using improved biomarkers.

While the progression rate's standard deviation (SD) of 11.0 points 11 provides sufficient statistical power to support a Phase 3 trial of practical size, 39 the required N for PSP trials remains an important concern and finding ways to reduce it is the principal goal of this review (Figure 1). Careful training of site examiners to achieve uniform application of the PSPRS across sites and study visits may improve the within‐rater variance and inter‐rater reliability (IRR), thereby improving statistical power, but no data exist to confirm or quantify this expectation.

PSPRS drawbacks

The scale's developers measured IRR at .86 (95% CI: .65–98). For the sake of uniformity, they used video exams, which required omitting a few of the 28 items that were difficult to videotape. This has prompted the opinion that the scale's IRR has not been clearly proven. 12 A subsequent formal analysis 13 of the scale's 16 motor items (the bulbar, ocular motor, limb motor, and gait/midline domains) found the items for limb dystonia and tremor, and possibly those on limb rigidity and dysphagia, to be statistically redundant and/or clinically unimportant. Others have criticized the PSPRS for the subjectivity of some of its scoring definitions and the non‐uniformity of maximum point values available for various clinical features.

Adoption of the PSPRS‐28 by neurologists as a data‐gathering tool in routine clinical practice has been hampered by the administration time, 15 to 20 m depending on the patient's degree of disability and the examiner's experience with the instrument. Despite its shortfalls, the statistical power of PSPRS, to a similar degree in both its original and modified versions, has been shown to match or exceed those of all other available PSP clinical outcome measures other than magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) volumetry (Table 2). 39

Modified versions of PSPRS

PSPRS‐10

Although the PSPRS has become the standard primary outcome measure in interventional trials and one of several important variables in observational studies, in 2018, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) created the first modified version, referred to here as the PSPRS‐10 after the number of items [FDAFDA URL] The European Medicines Agency has informally indicated that the PSPRS‐28 remains satisfactory for trials in its jurisdiction, and the United Kingdom's Medicines for Human Use Regulatory Authority has continued this policy in the post‐Brexit era.

The FDA eliminated items from the PSPRS‐28 that it considered to be insufficiently related to daily disability, too subjective, unclearly worded, or possibly secondary to the disability (such as withdrawal or insomnia). It also changed some of the wording slightly and collapsed most of the remaining 10 items’ scoring choices on grounds that the original five points recognized gradations of little clinical relevance. This rescored version, called the rPSPRS‐10, has 29 points as the theoretical maximum and includes no items from the original mental, ocular motor, or limb motor domains. Longitudinal Item Response Theory analysis 40 shows that the rPSPRS‐10 retains 76% of the information in the PSPRS‐28.

The FDA has not issued formal guidance regarding the PSPRS‐10 or rPSPRS‐10, preferring to discuss the primary outcome measure separately with each trial sponsor at a pre‐Investigational New Drug meeting. [FDA URL] In response, many designers of pivotal PSP trials currently use their own negotiated version of the PSPRS‐10 as the primary outcome and the original PSPRS as one of the secondary outcomes. In this way, their data can be used in supplementary analyses relying on previous PSPRS‐28‐based research. Most earlier‐phase trials and observational studies continue to use the PSPRS‐28 alone.

The slight wording changes in at least one of the FDA's versions make it inadvisable for pivotal trials simply to administer the original PSPRS‐28 alone and define the primary outcome measure as the subset corresponding to the PSPRS‐10 items and its collapsed scoring definitions. Other modifications of the PSPRS‐28 have been developed to suit specific purposes, though none has yet reached frequent use.

PSPRS‐14

Data from the tideglusib trial 18 and two longitudinal studies in Germany have been used to validate another abridged version. The PSPRS‐14 41 comprises those items best reflecting functionally relevant disease milestones, each item rated on a three‐point scale. This version suits regulatory agencies’ preference for patient‐centered outcome measures while retaining the information on eye movement, mentation, and limb movement deleted by the FDA's 10‐item versions. The sensitivity of the PSPRS‐14 to progression and its statistical power nearly match those of the original PSPRS‐28 (Table 3), and the ceiling effect of the original was absent. Another group using its own cohort confirmed that result but found that the PSPRS‐14 performed unsatisfactorily in non‐RS phenotypes. 42

PSPRS‐21 and 25

Two PSPRS versions prompted by the COVID pandemic removed items from the PSPRS‐28 unsuitable for video evaluations. 43 They comprise a 25‐item, 88‐point version omitting the items testing limb rigidity, neck rigidity, and postural stability and a 21‐item, 72‐point version additionally omitting the ocular motor and limb dystonia items. Each provides nearly as much power as the original. The per‐arm sample sizes needed to detect a 50% improvement over 6 months using data from one trial cohort and one observational database were PSPRS‐28: 50, PSPRS‐25: 52, and PSPRS‐21: 58. The video‐friendly versions are suitable not only for use in home‐bound patients, but can also greatly ease the transportation burden for all patients in clinical trials, potentially reducing dropout rate and financial costs.

The PSPRS‐15

A 15‐item version 44 using data from two drug trials and two observational databases retains elements of all six original domains and, per FDA advice, focuses on patient‐centered variables. [FDA URL] It also retains those PSPRS‐28 items with best responsiveness to change and statistical independence. The result slightly improves upon the statistical power of the PSPRS‐28, reducing the required N by approximately 10%. It seems the best candidate to replace the PSPRS‐28 in clinical trials based on its careful design, relative ease of administration, and power to detect change. However, prospective field testing and retrospective evaluation of its performance in additional longitudinal datasets are needed. The PSP Trial Platform 45 (Section 7.5) has secured FDA approval to use the PSPRS‐15 as the primary outcome measure for its Phase 2 trials.

Other PSP‐specific rating scales

Cortical basal ganglia functional scale (CBFS)

This very convenient instrument was published in 2020 and has been validated in both PSP and CBS. 46 Perhaps more than any form of the PSPRS, the CBFS directly addresses the need for a patient‐centered, patient‐reported measure of daily activities. It can be completed at home or in the clinic waiting room by the patient and caregiver on a paper or digital form with minimal introductory instructions from clinic staff. This provides a convenient solution to the requirement of all the PSPRS versions for direct or video contact with a well‐trained clinician. The CBFS comprises a page of lay‐language instructions and 14 motor and 17 non‐motor items, each rated on a five‐point scale.

For patients with PSP, the CBFS correlates with the PSPRS‐28's annual progression r = 0.44, p = 0.02 and cross‐sectional scores (r = 0.71, p < 0.003). 20 However, its statistical power is inferior to that of the PSPRS‐28 (Table 2). The CBFS is the only published rating scale for the full range of disabilities in CBS. Therefore, a basket trial comprising PSP and CBS, the major 4R tauopathies, could well consider using it as a primary outcome measure.

Progressive Supranuclear Palsy Clinical Deficits Scale (PSP‐CDS)

This assays daily‐activity‐relevant deficits using seven variables revealed by a search of the PSP clinicopathologic literature as the most consistent correlates of PSP's anatomic pathology: akinesia‐rigidity, bradyphrenia, communication loss, dysphagia, eye movement abnormalities, loss of finger dexterity, and gait/balance loss. 47 It correlates closely with the PSPRS‐28 (r = 0.88 for all phenotypes, 0.86 for PSP‐Richardson), uses a four‐point scale for each item, is clinician‐administered, and requires an average of only 4 min to complete. Its statistical power is good, requiring a sample size of 65 to demonstrate 50% slowing and 177 for 30% slowing (per arm for a two‐arm, 12‐month trial with 80% power). 47 The equivalent sample size for the PSPRS‐28 from the same analysis for 50% slowing is only 40 and for the PSPRS‐14 is 45.

PSP Quality‐of‐Life Scale (PSP‐QoL)

This lengthy scale's 46 items are all self‐administered by the patient and/or caregiver. 48 It requires about 30 min and uses a 0 to 4 rubric, for a maximum of 180 points. Twenty‐two of its items are motor, including ocular motor, 23 are cognitive and behavioral, and one is a 0 to 100 Likert scale on overall life satisfaction. Correlations between the total PSP‐QoL and total PSPRS‐28 are good (r = 0.65, p < 0.001), but the PSPRS‐28 domains for bulbar, ocular motor, and limb motor functions do not correlate significantly with the total PSP‐QoL. 49 For subjects with PSP‐Richardson, correlation with the PSPRS‐28 is weaker than for the non‐Richardson phenotypes but still satisfactory. 49

The annualized progression of the PSP‐QoL in a trial‐eligible cohort of subjects with PSP‐Richardson is 16.1 (±19.8) points. 20 As expected from so great a SD relative to the mean, the PSP‐QoL requires N's approximately three times those of the PSPRS‐28 20 but has gained use as a secondary measure in some large PSP treatment trials.

PSP Short Quality‐of‐Life Scale (PSP‐ShoQoL)

In response to criticism of the time required to complete the PSP‐QoL, clinicians at 22 centers in Germany 50 created a much shorter PSP‐ShoQoL. It comprises 12 of the original's best‐performing items while retaining a balance of mental and physical components. It provides the same statistical power, including sensitivity to progression over 12 months, but further experience, especially in other countries, is awaited.

PSP cognitive composite battery

This was developed 51 using baseline Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), letter‐number sequencing and phonemic fluency (Section 5.4.5) data from the PASSPORT trial 52 but to date has not been validated prospectively. It comprises 11 of the 12 RBANS subtests, omitting picture naming, and adds letter‐number sequencing and phonemic fluency. Its mean/SD ratio for 52‐week change is 0.78, far superior to 0.42 for the RBANS. Its administration time is not specified but would, if anything, exceed that of the RBANS itself.

The short cognitive and neuropsychiatric scale

This recent addition 53 uses a 0 to 3 scoring rubric to rate five common frontal deficits of PSP: bradyphrenia, apathy, aphasia, executive loss, and disinhibition. Its mean annualized progression in PSP‐Richardson is 1.0 (of a total of 15), with a relatively unfavorable mean/SD ratio of 0.31. The ShoCo Scale, if validated prospectively, could prove a useful supplement to the various PSPRS modifications that omit the cognitive and behavioral items of the PSPRS‐28.

Relevant rating scales not specific to PSP

MDS‐sponsored revision of Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)

The MDS‐UPDRS 54 is the world's standard clinical scale for PD. Its sections are as follows: I: non‐motor aspects of daily activities (by history); II: motor aspects of daily activities (by history); III: motor exam; and IV: motor complications (by history). Sections II and III have been found, albeit inconsistently, to provide good statistical power in PSP. Sample sizes required to detect a 30% change in progression over 12 months for the trial‐eligible subjects in a large observational cohort 20 were 86 for the PSPRS‐28, 114 for the MDS‐UPDRS II, and 347 for the MDS‐UPDRS III. However, data from the TAUROS trial's 18 placebo group analyzed retrospectively 39 found better power for the UPDRS II, requiring 113 subjects, compared to 138 for the PSPRS‐28.

Schwab‐England Activities of Daily Living (SEADL)

This popular measure of overall disability is an 11‐point scale scored from 100% (unimpaired) to 0% (fully dependent) in increments of 10%. It describes each score level in only a few words and requires only a few seconds to administer. Originally developed and validated in PD and widely used in such trials, it has been well validated in PSP as well. Despite its coarseness and subjectivity, it boasts good statistical power to detect slowing of progression in a 12‐month trial in trial‐eligible subjects. This far exceeds the power of the PSP‐QoL and UPDRS III, but not those of the CBFS, UPDRS II, or PSPRS‐28. 20

Clinical Global Impression of disease severity (CGI‐S)

A seven‐point scale developed in psychiatry 55 but valid in other disorders, the CGI‐S is frequently used as a secondary outcome measure in PSP trials. The clinician poses the following question: “Considering your total clinical experience with this particular population, how ill is the patient at this time?” The available responses are normal, not at all ill, borderline ill, mildly ill, moderately ill, markedly ill, severely ill, among the most extremely ill. Despite its subjectivity, the CGI‐S provides good statistical power to detect progression in PSP. Analysis of the davunetide (AL‐108‐231) 17 and TAUROS 18 trials’ placebo groups found a required sample size of 81 per arm for a 50% slowing of progression after 12 months at 80% sensitivity, comparable to 51 for the PSPRS‐28 and 70 for the SEADL scale (Section 5.4.2). 39

Clinical Global Impression of change (CGI‐C)

The CGI‐C 56 is similar to the CGI‐S and is as frequently used in trials in neurodegenerative disease. It assigns one to seven points according to the clinician's impression of the subject's change since the study's baseline, using the rubric: very much improved, much improved, minimally improved, unchanged, minimally worse, much worse, very much worse. As explained below (Section 7.12), the CGI‐C has been used to generate a minimal clinically important difference in PSP. 56

Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status

This is a general test of cognition and behavior suitable for multiple disorders or healthy populations. 57 In PSP, all six of its subindices (immediate memory, visuospatial/constructional, language, attention, delayed memory, and total scale) progressed to a statistically significant degree over 12 months and the magnitude of progression exceeded that of age‐matched healthy controls. 58

The RBANS is included in many PSP trials but is cited by study personnel as an important cause of patient fatigue over the course of a trial visit. Its developers give the administration time as 20 to 30 min in healthy persons, but subsequent literature suggests a time of 30 to 45 min in impaired patients. In subjects with PSP, the bradyphrenia, apathy, inattention, bradykinesia, dysarthria, impaired ocular fixation, and easy fatigability typically prolong the RBANS administration even further.

Montreal Cognitive Assessment

The MoCA is widely used in clinical practice and research. Modeled closely after the MMSE, it emphasizes frontal function, only a minor component of the MMSE. Like the MMSE, it does not include items on mood or behavior and requires non‐cognitive actions frequently impaired in PSP involving eye movement and manual dexterity. Although the MoCA correlates well with the RBANS in PSP‐Richardson in cross‐sectional data, 59 its sensitivity to change is modest at best. 20 , 59 In PSP clinical trials, it is more useful as a screening tool, with minimum acceptable scores ranging from 20 to 23, but no validation of any specific screening cut‐off has been attempted.

Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ)‐39 and 8

These scales were developed to measure quality of life in PD but have been found useful in PSP as well and have been part of a few PSP studies. 60 Its score correlates with the PSPRS‐28 in subjects with PSP‐Richardson (r = 0.621). A version selecting only eight representative items, the PDQ‐8, requires proportionately less time and performs nearly as well. 60

Parkinson's Disease Caregiver Burden Questionnaire (PDCB)

Caregiver burden has rightly become an outcome of formal interest for clinical trials. One study 61 found that caregivers of people with PSP with a mean PSPRS‐28 score of 38 and a MoCA score of 22 (both typical for a trial‐ready cohort) spent a mean of 7.4 h per day in that task. The PDCB, comprising 20 items, each rated 0 to 4, has been validated in PSP, correlating well with PSPRS‐28 and other accepted measures. It also has excellent internal validity, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.803. 61

EuroQol 5‐Dimension 5‐Level questionnaire (EQ‐5D‐5L)

This brief, self‐completed quality‐of‐life scale (originally called the European Quality‐of‐Life Scale) and its variants have reached widespread use in trials across a broad range of disorders. The scale rates each of five activities of daily living (mobility, self‐care, work/study/housework/family/leisure, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) on a five‐point scale. In its lone appearance in a PSP trial, 18 the EQ‐5D‐5L was the sole clinical outcome measure to show a statistically significant improvement relative to placebo. A 2015 review of scales for PSP suggests (but does not recommend) its use. In the decade since, no further data on this scale's performance in PSP trials have appeared.

OTHER CURRENTLY AVAILABLE MARKERS

MRI

The MDS PSP diagnostic criteria 30 mention midbrain atrophy on MRI as an informal, “supportive” feature. The mandatory diagnostic features list MRI findings of non‐degenerative and prion diseases as exclusionary.

As a quantitative state marker, structural MRI has been a secondary or exploratory endpoint in half of the treatment trials for PSP over the past two decades. 62 As a measure of disease progression over a 12‐month trial, volumetric measures of regional brain atrophy easily outperform any instrument measuring neurological impairment or subjective symptoms. As shown in Table 2, for each level of change of progression rate, ventricular volume as measured on T1 MRI images has an effect size about 25% greater than that of the next most sensitive measure, the PSPRS‐28. As an example of the reduction in required sample size, for a 50% slowing of progression (with α = 0.05, 80% sensitivity, 1:1 randomization, and 12‐month duration), a trial‐eligible cohort evaluated with the PSPRS‐28 requires 32 patients per arm, while the ventricular volume requires only 14. 20 This may result from reduced interindividual variation of the measure, where the SD/mean ratio for the PSPRS‐28 is 0.78 and for the volumetry, 0.63. This difference may in part be an artifact of the clinical evaluations being decentralized over multiple sites and examiners, while the MRI readings were performed centrally.

Other anatomic brain area volumes perform as well as ventricular volume or nearly so. Also requiring fewer than 20 patients under the specifications above are measures of midbrain, pons, “central” areas (mostly basal ganglia), frontal lobe, and cingulate. 62 The sum of the volume changes of midbrain, frontal lobe, and third ventricle provided better statistical power than any individual anatomical area and planimetric MRI measurements such as midbrain area and magnetic resonance Parkinsonism index (MRPI and MRPI 2.0) and are most sensitive in detecting subtle atrophy at baseline. This suggests that a two‐dimensional measure is preferable for diagnosis at enrollment, but volumetric measures are preferable for measuring longitudinal progression. 62 Unlike AD and multiple sclerosis, PSP trials probably have little to fear from the “pseudo‐atrophy” artifact, where a true neuroprotective effect of an anti‐inflammatory drug in a progressive, inflammatory condition is misinterpreted as continuing atrophy, masking a neuroprotective effect. Unlike MS, the inflammation in PSP is subtle, and, unlike the amyloid beta in AD, the tau burden in PSP is minimal. Still, the issue of pseudo‐atrophy in PSP deserves study before an anti‐inflammatory trial is mounted.

Wearable devices

The cognitive difficulties of PSP and interexaminer variability present a need for more objective measures of activities of daily living between trial visits. Devices that can be attached and operated by the patient or caregiver would be optimal. 63 An initial step toward that ideal has been reported, although its hardware and software setup and data acquisition and exporting still require more training than feasible for a typical PSP trial population or their likely caregivers.

Two studies of a system (Mobility Lab from APDM, Inc.) have shown good correlation with the PSPRS‐28. Both a six‐sensor (wrists, ankles, chest, and lumbar) 64 and three‐sensor protocol from a different center 65 showed interindividual variation small enough to detect worsening in some of the gait variables over shorter periods than necessary using the PSPRS‐28. However, the device's statistical performance has not been compared directly to that of the PSPRS‐28's gait items, the best‐performing of the six PSPRS‐28 domains, 39 and the most relevant comparator for a gait‐sensing hardware system.

Eagerly awaited are simplifications and refinements in this promising approach, including an assessment of falling. However, in PSP, most patients’ falling frequency diminishes as their reliance on helpers, gait devices, and wheelchair increases, potentially creating a falsely favorable report. The transition to wheelchair may even occur in the first half of the disease course, when trial enrollment is most common. The PSPRS‐28's item on falling frequency by history (item 5) recognizes this conundrum by requesting a hypothetical estimate of the fall frequency in an unassisted state, an approach with its own downside. Obviously, remote assessment of unassisted gait in PSP is not feasible for reasons of safety.

INNOVATIONS IN TRIAL DESIGN

Six‐month designs

Some sponsors have considered using 6 months rather than 12 as the duration of the double‐blind phase of a pivotal trial. As an example, the N required to detect 50% slowing of progression rate in a 6‐month trial using a trial‐ready cohort and the PSPRS‐28 as the primary outcome would be 100 per arm, 20 while the corresponding N for a 12‐month trial is 32 per arm. A similar tripling of the required N applies to other percentage outcome changes. Furthermore, the cost of onboarding the number of study sites needed to recruit the additional subjects and the time needed to do so could defeat the financial and temporal advantages of the 6‐month design. Another disadvantage of a large number of trial sites is the relative inexperience of the additional sites’ staff in PSP, especially if other PSP trials are concurrently competing for sites.

Futility designs and historical placebo groups

By contrast, futility trials in PSP can be accomplished with a 6‐month treatment period, even without a concurrent placebo group. The best‐known example in PSP is the basket trial of salsalate and young plasma, which shared a control group of 305 subjects with PSP‐Richardson from the davunetide trial and 48 from a longitudinal observational study. 66 Remarkably, the futility design with power to detect a failure to achieve 40% slowing of progression in the PSPRS‐28 required N of only 10 for salsalate and 5 for young plasma. These small Ns were made feasible by powering the study to detect a relatively large treatment effect of 40%, by using a large control group, and by the relatively low intersubject variance of progression of the PSPRS‐28, SEADL, and the other outcome measures in PSP‐Richardson. The expense and other practical obstacles of placebo‐controlled pivotal trials in PSP can be minimized by subjecting candidate treatments to such a protocol. 67 Comparator cohorts publicly available from the FDA‐funded Critical Path Institute [CPI] at present include 12‐month placebo group data from the davunetide and tilavonemab trials and longer‐term PSPRS‐28 data on 414 patients with PSP‐Richardson seen by author LIG in the course of routine care from 1994 to 2020.

Like any trial relying on data gathered from a previous cohort of external controls, PSP futility trials must consider differences in enrollment selection, evolving standards of medical care, the limited experience with futility trials in PSP to date, and the demonstrated low sensitivity of available biomarkers to clinical change over periods shorter than 12 months.

We have constructed power curves comparing the power of a futility trial to detect a true positive with the size of a historical control group. Figure 2 shows such curves for trials of 12, 26, and 52 weeks’ duration and for active‐drug groups ranging from 100 to 500. A 52‐week study provides a power of approximately 0.80, even with only 100 subjects on active drug and 250 controls. However, for a 26‐week study, the same power requires 300 on active drug and 500 controls.

These power curves for neuroprotection trials in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)‐Richardson syndrome of 12, 26, and 52 weeks’ duration use historical control subjects from the davunetide, TAUROS, PASSPORT, and ARISE trials. Effect size is set at 35% slowing of progression of the PSP Rating Scale‐28 at p < 0.05. Note that a 52‐week study of only 100 active‐drug subjects requires fewer than 250 historical controls, a number readily obtained from published sources. Power for a 52‐week study improves with 200 patients but not further with more. A 26‐week study may attain 80% power with 200 active‐drug subjects and approximately 1000 historical controls, a number not presently available.

Simon's two‐stage design

Further efficiency in futility trials can be achieved by scheduling an interim analysis to create the possibility of an earlier stopping point for inefficacious treatments. This approach, called Simon's two‐stage design,“ is commonly used in oncology. It would have reduced the Ns of two neuroprotective trials in early PD, Deprenyl and Tocopherol Antioxidant Therapy of Parkinsonism (DATATOP) and PRECEPT, by 75%. 68 Two‐stage designs also offer the ethical benefits of minimizing subjects” exposure to ineffective drugs and allowing them to move on to other trials before their eligibility window closes. One important caveat is that while the risk of missing an efficacy signal is low, its downside, the removal of a useful drug from its development pipeline, is large. 69 Remedies include slight expansion of the N from its theoretically optimal level and using an endpoint that is quantitative and normally distributed rather than dichotomous. 70

Non‐Richardson phenotypes in neuroprotection trials

A variant on the basket design is to enroll multiple PSP phenotypes in one trial. Although the PSPRS‐28 was designed for PSP‐Richardson before the other phenotypes were recognized, the scale actually provides greater statistical power for the cortical phenotypes than for PSP‐Richardson 20 (Table 3). The obstacles to including non‐Richardson phenotypes in trials include (1) the insufficient validation of the PSPRS‐28 and other critical outcome measures in those groups, 21 (2) the heterogeneity among the subtypes in their rates of progression and mix of clinical features, (3) the lesser specificity and positive predictive values of the non‐Richardson diagnostic criteria, and (4) the theoretical possibility of heterogeneity with regard to the pathogenetic feature at which the study drug is targeted. The first two of these issues could be addressed by normalizing the progression rates for the grouped non‐Richardson variants using data from ongoing, longitudinal, observational cohorts or by validating instruments such as the CBFS for those variants.

Basket designs

PSP is well suited for trials of multiple disorders in a trial of a single drug. In the earliest disease stages, where neuroprotective treatment seems most likely to be effective, PSP can be difficult to distinguish from some forms of PD, MSA, and CBD. Over the period of the trial, most of the participants are likely to “declare themselves” as one of the included diseases, or as neither, allowing a retrospective reclassification or exclusion of patients before performing the statistical analysis. Another virtue of basket trials is the opportunity to include multiple, rare disorders sharing a common druggable target, where none on its own would be sufficiently common to attract adequate funding or enrollment. This could allow trials of non‐Richardson phenotypes together in a single trial using an outcome measure that may be moderately suitable for all PSP phenotypes but far from optimal for PSP‐Richardson itself.

Platform designs

Platform trials compare multiple drugs to the same placebo group using similar protocols and often, overlapping biomarkers. 45 Cost savings result not only from the reduced N but also from economies of scale, with only one center for recruitment, coordination, monitoring, and data management for all the drugs. 69 The high active:placebo randomization ratio may make participation more attractive for patients. If the placebo group is fully or mostly recruited along with the first‐joining drug group, an interim analysis for a later‐joining drug will enjoy greater statistical power.

Drawbacks of the platform model include initial setup time and costs, the difficulty in simultaneously recruiting the requisite number of sponsors willing to use a similar protocol, evolution in standards of symptomatic care between recruitment intervals of the placebo group and trial drug groups, and making placebo masking applicable to the range of all trial drugs’ administration routes and expected side effects.

A platform infrastructure specific for PSP‐Richardson, based at the University of California, San Francisco, University of California, San Diego, and Massachusetts General Hospital, was funded by the National Institutes of Health in 2024. 45 The $75.4 million cost for the first five years will be supplemented by fees from Pharma sponsors. It will involve up to 70 sites across the United States, and initial enrollment is expected in late 2026. Its plan to compare three active drug groups to one placebo group is expected to reduce the total required N by a third and costs by half.

Factorial designs

A popular variant that simultaneously considers multiple active drugs in a single trial is a factorial design. A two‐drug trial would randomize subjects into four groups: both active drugs, drug 1 plus placebo resembling drug 2, drug 2 plus placebo resembling drug 1, and double placebo. A three‐drug trial would create six such groups. A multidrug approach lends itself well to PSP, where neuroprotection may require two or more drugs addressing multiple pathogenetic contributors acting in parallel. This design does not reduce the required N of separate, traditional 1:1 studies but provides additional information on any synergistic effects on efficacy. It saves administrative costs and its attractive active:placebo randomization ratio of 3:1 (for a 2×2 design) may facilitate enrollment and reduce dropout.

Multi‐arm multistage trials (MAMS)

This modification, combining any of several forms of multi‐arm design with an interim analysis, saves time and costs by allowing only the best‐performing treatment(s) in a Phase 2 trial to advance seamlessly to Phase 3. The approach has been used in PD 70 but not to date in PSP or other atypical Parkinsonian disorders, even in retrospective database analyses.

Stratification on baseline predictors

Improvement in statistical power may in theory be achieved by stratifying trial participants on baseline variables known to correlate with outcome variable progression rate over the next 12 months.

A post hoc analysis of the davunetide trial's placebo group 71 adjusted for baseline PSPRS‐28, age, gender, disease duration, treatment group assignment, tau haplotype, CoQ10 use, and MMSE found statistically significant effects on PSPRS‐28 progression rate over the 12 months of the study to be disease duration, total raw RBANS, Geriatric Depression Scale, and color trails tests 1 and 2. However, these effects are weak, and multivariate predictive models combining these were no better at predicting PSPRS‐28 progression than the individual variables.

A prospectively designed assessment of baseline predictors of 12‐month progression from the Mayo Clinic's longitudinal observational cohort 72 found that only 18F‐flortaucipir positron emission tomography (PET) and not any MRI or clinical measures predicted subsequent progression rate. They unexpectedly found that baseline PSPRS scores had an inverse relationship to progression rate. The authors explain this as a ceiling effect artifact wherein some scale items had reached a maximum score before baseline or soon thereafter. Additional evidence against a predictive value of baseline MRI in PSP is provided by a retrospective analysis of five large trials. 73

PI‐2620 and APN‐1607 are the two best‐studied second‐generation tau PET ligands and those closest to possible clinical approval for PSP. Neither has yet been evaluated as baseline predictors of 12‐month progression.

More recent work has supported fluid markers as predictors of progression over the subsequent 12 months. Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a measure of damage to large, myelinated axons, occurs in many neurological disorders. In the davunetide trial cohort at baseline, serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) elevations and lower CSF phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) each correlated with PSPRS‐28 progression rate and a ratio of those performed better still. 74 Serum NfL by single molecular array (SiMoA) has shown excellent correlation with CSF NfL. 75 As a secondary outcome measure, it could be used as a covariate to improve statistical power. Standard PSP trial design now includes assays of NfL in CSF at least at baseline and endpoint, and in serum at more frequent intervals.

PET measures of subcortical inflammation using the microglial ligand 11C‐PK11195 and of subcortical tau deposition using 18F‐AV‐1451 predict subsequent PSP progression. 76 The relationships in that small trial were substantial but require confirmation at other centers. Neither PET tracer is commercially available at present.

Study of genetic variables as predictors of progression has yielded little to date. A group of 940 patients pooled from three previous US/UK PSP cohorts found the A allele at the single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs2242367 to associate with shorter survival. 77 Mean survival durations for deceased individuals with PSP‐Richardson and the AA genotype was 5.86 (SD 1.71) years, the AG genotype, 5.97 (SD 2.00), and the GG genotype, 6.64 (SD 2.23). The A allele's hazard ratio for death was 1.37 (95% CI: 1.25 to 1.51, p = 1.3×10−10). That SNP is most closely linked to the gene for leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), in which point mutations are known to cause Mendelian forms of PD and, far less commonly, PSP and MSA. No other known PSP risk genes are known to be associated with survival duration. 78 We conclude that while mortality‐based outcomes may benefit slightly from stratification on the rs2242367 genotype, progression‐based trials as yet receive no guidance from genetics.

Remote visits

As mentioned in Section 3.2, an impediment to PSP trial recruitment and retention is the need for motorically impaired patients to travel periodically to the study site. The video‐based PSPRS‐21 and PSPRS‐25 show little loss of statistical power relative to the PSPRS‐28. 45 The deleted items were those expected in a video setting to be either risky (balance, dysphagia for liquids) or difficult to evaluate (eye movements). If the PSPRS‐28 is required or preferred as the primary outcome measure, it could be performed only at baseline and endpoint, with most interim visits for futility monitoring purposes using video evaluations. Validation of the PSPRS‐21 and 25 have been performed only as retrospective analyses of data (not video) from archived subsets of the PSPRS‐28 items administered in person. Prospective validations of the new versions performed via video are yet to be reported. Such efforts must also assess the reliability and practicality of an online exam performed via video on a patient with PSP assisted only by an often‐elderly caregiver in the home environment.

Also, as mentioned in Section 6.2, wearable monitors to detect gait speed, falls, and even details of gait physiology may soon allow some interim visits to be replaced by long‐term, continuous measurements transmitted from home. However, this awaits validation in a clinical trial setting.

Customized endpoints

Even among patients with PSP‐Richardson and certainly across other PSP phenotypes, the rate of progression varies regardless of how it is measured. To reduce the resulting statistical noise and the N required, an expected rate of progression could be calculated for each patient based on their progression rate since symptom onset. This has started to be modeled in PSP. 23 , 79 An obvious source of error is the uncertainty of symptom onset for cognitive or behavioral symptoms, as opposed to falls, where the onset date can be specified by most patients or their families.

Time to milestones or death as an outcome variable

Time to disability milestones or death is a way to focus PSP trials on patient‐centric outcomes. It lends itself to customization of endpoints or to stratification.

One resource 23 uses a longitudinal PSPRS‐28 database of 417 patients with PSP‐Richardson to calculate median time to disability milestones. For example, the median time to requiring help arising from a chair for patients with baseline PSPRS‐28 of 25 was 2.5 years, while for those with a baseline score of 45, the median was 1.8 years.

Although baseline PSPRS has an inconsistent effect on its subsequent progression rate, a few studies have found a correlation with survival. 11 , 23 , 78 One meta‐analysis 78 found survival longer in patients with early onset of cognitive loss, dysphagia, or falls. Only for the last factor was there a calculation of a multivariate hazard ratio: 2.32 (95% CI: 1.94 to 2.77).

Survival can be analyzed as a secondary outcome, with a separate measure of disability progression as the primary. But survival can also be combined with disability as the primary outcome in a so‐called combined assessment of function and survival (CAFS). This approach was developed for trials in rapidly progressive, life‐limiting diseases such as ALS, AIDS, and certain malignancies. CAFS has been applied retrospectively in PSP 80 as the Joint Rank Test, which integrates survival with PSPRS‐28 progression, in this case over a 2‐year double‐blind period. It and a follow‐on analysis 81 concluded that CAFS is unlikely to reduce the required N and, depending on the mortality rate, may actually double the required double‐blind time if few deaths occur before a 12‐month interim analysis.

Minimum clinically important difference

Another potential route to enhancing the patient‐centric emphasis of trial outcomes is to compare the percentages of each treatment arm attaining at least one MCID in disability, or the hazard ratio between groups for the time to acquisition of such a difference. The lone published attempt to measure PSP disability in that way 56 retrospectively analyzed the davunetide placebo group data, 17 which included both PSPRS‐28 and CGI‐C, a seven‐point scale (Section 5.4.4). The analysis calculated the mean change in the PSPRS‐28 in subjects whose CGI‐C was rated by the investigator as “minimally worse” than at baseline. The result 56 after correction for covariates was 5.7 points (95% CI: 4.83 to 6.51). Our recalculation for the same dataset but without covariate correction was 6.22 points (SD 7.4) for the CGI‐C and 7.71 (SD 7.5) for the CGI‐S.

That MCID calculation uses essentially a two‐point scale and one trial's dataset. For a potentially more refined MCID, we anchored the PSPRS‐28 change to the SEADL, which ranges from 0% to 100% in steps of 10%, for a total of 11 points. We used data from five PSP studies of 12 months or more (Table 4), defined the MCID as the number of PSPRS‐28 points corresponding to one step in the SEADL, and weighted the trials by their Ns. The resulting mean is 6.56 PSPRS‐28 points (95% CI: 4.07 to 9.40). Details are in Table 4. Omitting the upper and lower outliers (the PROSPERA 82 and ARISE 83 trials, respectively) raised the weighted mean to 6.92 points.

CONCLUSIONS

There is no shortage of druggable targets and potential neuroprotective drugs in PSP. However, PSP drug developers may be deterred from bringing their products to clinic by the prospect of excessive costs and slow recruitment related to strict enrollment criteria, dropout related to long trial duration, and advancing disability. Large Ns are required by suboptimal positive predictive values of existing diagnostic criteria and insensitivity of standard outcome measures of disease progression. Enrolling patients at early stages to maximize the likelihood of drug efficacy is difficult because of insufficient sensitivity of available diagnostic markers in equivocal cases and insufficient familiarity with PSP among referring neurologists.

We have reviewed solutions in both qualitative and quantitative ways, presenting methods of improving statistical power via reducing the number of subjects required to demonstrate efficacy in slowing disease progression. In theory, these could be used in combination. Of these, the most feasible and effective are probably platform trials, adaptive designs merging Phases 2 and 3, stratification on baseline serum NfL, and conversion from a continuous disability scale to disability milestone attainment.

One such milestone is the MCID, which we have calculated as points in the original,100‐point, 28‐item PSPRS corresponding to one of the 11 steps in the widely used SEADL. Our N‐weighted mean of five studies produced an MCID of 6.56 points (95% CI: 4.07 to 9.40; Table 4).

We hope that further work will maximize efficiency of upcoming PSP neuroprotection trials by modeling our suggestions in existing longitudinal, observational databases, and in drug trial placebo groups.
