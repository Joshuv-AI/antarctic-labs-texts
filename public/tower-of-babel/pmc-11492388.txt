Limosilactobacillus-related 3-OMDP as a potential therapeutic target for depression
Authors: Qi Zhong, Wentao Wu, Jing Xie, Jiao-lin Wang, Ke Xu, Yi Ren, Jianjun Chen, Peng Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGut microbiota was closely involved in the pathogenesis of depression, but the underlying molecular mechanisms in depression remained unclear. This study was conducted to investigate the relationship between neurotransmitters/inflammatory factors and gut microbiota in depressed mice.Materials and methodsA chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) depression model was established. Gut microbial composition was detected in faeces, neurotransmitters were detected in faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus, and inflammatory factors were detected in hippocampus. After a key neurotransmitter was identified, intervention experiment was conducted to explore whether it could improve depressive-like behaviours.ResultsSix differential genera in faeces, 14 differential neurotransmitters in gut-brain axis, and two differential inflammatory factors (interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6)) in hippocampus were identified in depressed mice. There were significant correlations among differential genera, differential neurotransmitters and IL-1β/IL-6. Among these differential neurotransmitters, 3-O-Methyldopa (3-OMDP) was found to be consistently decreased in faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus, and 3-OMDP was significantly correlated to Limosilactobacillus and IL-1β. After receiving 3-OMDP, the depressive-like behaviours in depressed mice were improved and the increased IL-1β/IL-6 levels were reversed.ConclusionsThese results indicated that gut microbiota might affect host’s inflammation levels in brain through regulating neurotransmitters, eventually leading to the onset of depression. ‘Limosilactobacillus-3-OMDP-IL-1β/IL-6’ might be a potential pathway in the crosstalk of gut and brain, and 3-OMDP held the promise as a therapeutic target for depression.

Introduction

Depression is a common mood disorder that affects physical and mental health [1,2]. This disease is characterized by pessimism, loss of interest, sleep disturbances and even suicidal ideation [3]. It is the fourth most common disease worldwide, currently affecting more than 350 million people, and this number is increasing at an alarming rate [4]. Although significant progresses in the aetiology and treatment of depression have been made in recent decades, the underlying mechanisms of depression remain unclear [5,6]. This dilemma causes huge challenges for the prevention and treatment of depression. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore the potential novel pathogenesis of depression to develop more effective treatments.

Human intestine harbours nearly 100 trillion microbes, which are closely involved in many host’s functions, such as regulating digestion, immune system and secreting neurotransmitters [7–9]. In recent years, gut microbiota has emerged as a significant factor in the development of depression [10–12]. Our previous studies showed that depression patients had obviously different gut microbiota compositions compared to healthy controls [13–16]. Other studies have also found that the gut microbiota of patients with depression was significantly different from healthy people [17,18]. Briefly, the diversity of gut microbiota decreases and the number of harmful bacteria increases in depression patients [19,20]. Disordered gut microbiota had also been considered to affect intestinal barrier function and lead to an increased inflammatory response, which might eventually influence the onset and progression of depression [21,22]. Recent studies reported that brain activity and anxiety behaviour in mice were altered by a gut-derived metabolite [23,24]. However, the specific mechanisms of the crosstalk between gut microbiota and depression are still needed further investigation.

Gut microbiota can produce various metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, neurotransmitters and inflammatory factors [25,26]. These metabolites have important roles in the pathogenesis of neuropsychiatric diseases, including depression [13,27]. Evidence showed that neurotransmitters could be helpful for maintaining the chemical balance in the brain [28,29]. In our previous studies, we found that the levels of some neurotransmitters were significantly altered in depressed mice, such as tryptophan and tyrosine [23,30,31]. Meanwhile, Morais et al. reported that gut microbiota could establish a communication system with the brain (known as the ‘gut-brain axis’) via neurotransmitters and inflammatory factors [32]. In addition, it became evident that gut microbiota could affect the central nervous system (CNS) physiology and inflammation [33,34]. These results indicated that gut microbiota, neurotransmitters and inflammation might be closely related to the onset of depression.

Therefore, the present study was conducted to further explore the relationships between gut microbiota and depression using chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) depression model. Neurotransmitters in gut-brain axis (faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus) were detected, and four inflammatory factors in hippocampus were also detected. We focused on neurotransmitters in the GABAergic and catecholaminergic pathways here. By integrating these data, we aimed to reveal that gut microbiota could play an important role in depression by regulating neurotransmitters or inflammatory factors. Moreover, if a key neurotransmitter was identified here, intervention experiment would be conducted to explore whether it could improve depressive-like behaviours. Our results would provide novel clues to reveal pathogenesis of depression and develop effective methods for treating depression.

Methods and materials

Ethics statement

This study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Animal protocols and experiments were reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China; 2017013). Male C57BL/6J mice (7–8 weeks of age) were obtained from ENSIWEIER Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Chongqing, China). Retired CD1 breeders (7 months old, 30–35 g) were obtained from the Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). All mice were maintained in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act and Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals: Eighth Edition. At the end of the experiment, according to the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association, all mice were cardiac-perfused after anaesthesia.

CSDS depression model construction

To obtain a CSDS depression model, mice were randomly assigned into control (CON) group and CSDS group based on body weight and sucrose preference (SP). Each group had eight mice, which was decided according to our previous studies [23,30,31]. Mice in the CSDS group were individually exposed to different CD1 aggressors for 5–10 min daily, along with 24 h of sensory contact. The CSDS procedure was continued for 10 consecutive days. Mice in CON group were individually housed under the standard conditions. The mice were kept in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 am) and had ad libitum access to food and water.

Subsequently, behavioural tests were performed to assess whether the CSDS depression model was successfully built. The following behavioural tests were conducted in this study: sucrose preference test (SPT), which was a widely used method to evaluate anhedonia in depression; SP was viewed as the evaluation index; open field test (OFT), which was performed to evaluate the anxiety- and exploration-like behaviours of the mice; centre distance (CD%) or centre time was viewed as the evaluation index; forced swim test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST), which were commonly used to evaluate behavioural despair in mice; and immobility time was viewed as the evaluation index. The procedures for these behavioural tests were identical to those used in our previous study [20].

Gut microbiota, neurotransmitters and inflammatory factors detection

Samples were rapidly collected and then stored at −80 °C, including faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus. Faecal samples were used for gut microbiota detection. The faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus were used for neurotransmitter detection via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. The procedures for gut microbiota and neurotransmitter detection were conducted in accordance with the procedures in our previous studies [23,30,35]. The levels of alpha 1-antitrypsin (AAT), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α) in hippocampus were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd). First, a tissue homogenate was prepared by weighing the brain tissue, and then sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS) was added at a 1:9 ratio. The supernatant was carefully collected for ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm × 15 min.

3-O-Methyldopa (3-OMDP) as intervention drug

3-OMDP was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-113468A, MedChemExpress, USA) and dissolved in a vehicle (sterile PBS) at a dosage of 3 mg/kg. The dosage of 3-OMDP was determined based on previous study [36]. First, the CSDS depression model was built. Secondly, the mice in CSDS group were randomly assigned into two groups with body weight- and SP-matched: one group was received sterile PBS by intraperitoneal injection (n = 8), and another group was received 3 mg/kg/day 3-OMDP by intraperitoneal injection (n = 8). Mice in CON group were also randomly assigned into two groups with body weight- and SP-matched: one group was received sterile PBS by intraperitoneal injection (n = 8), and another group was received 3 mg/kg/day 3-OMDP by intraperitoneal injection (n = 8). The entire intervention process was lasted 10 days. Finally, behavioural tests were conducted to assess whether 3-OMDP had antidepressant effects, and the related inflammatory factors were detected in the hippocampus.

Statistical analysis

Only the two correspondence authors knew the group allocation at the different stages of the experiments. All data analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0, Cytoscape 3.10.0, and R 4.0.5. Student’s t-test, non-parametric tests, and Spearman’s correlation analysis were used when appropriate. Benjamini and Hochberg False Discovery methods were used to conduct multiple testing corrections. Four parameters (ace, chao, Simpson and Shannon) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) were used to evaluate alpha and beta diversity, respectively, between the two groups. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) Effective Size (LEfSe) was used to identify the differential genera between the two groups (LDA > 2.0, p < 0.05). The correlations between differential behaviours, genera, neurotransmitters, and inflammatory factors were assessed to identify potential contacts between the gut and brain.

Results

Behavioural characteristics in CSDS mice

At baseline, body weight and SP were not significantly different between the two groups. However, after 10 days of stimulation, all CSDS-exposed mice showed distinct depression-like behaviours. For instance, the body weight was significantly lower in CSDS mice (n = 8) than in CON mice (n = 8), and SP was also significantly lower in CSDS mice than in CON mice (Figure 1). Meanwhile, in the FST, we found that CSDS mice had significantly higher immobility time (IT) compared to CON mice (Figure 1). In the OFT, we found that both CD% and the number of centre zones entering (ET) were significantly lower in CSDS mice than in CON mice (Figure 1). These results demonstrated that CSDS procedure successfully induced depression-like behaviours in mice.

Depressive-like behaviour in CSDS mice. Compared to CON mice, CSDS mice had the significantly lower body weight, sucrose preference (%), centre distance (%), and number of centre zones entering, as well as higher immobility time. Each group had eight mice.

Differential gut microbiota compositions CSDS mice

No significant differences in alpha diversity were found between the two groups (ace, p = 0.87; chao, p = 0.75; Simpson, p = 0.07; Shannon, p = 0.11; n = 8 in each group; Figure 2(A)). The relative abundances of gut microbiota at phylum level in the two groups were shown in Figure 2(B). Firmicutes and bacteroidetes were the two dominant bacterial taxa in the faecal microbiota of both groups. Meanwhile, there was a significant difference in gut microbiota compositions between the two groups, as shown by the PCoA plot in Figure 2(C). The LDA score results highlighted the specific contributions of the different bacterial taxa in the CON and CSDS mice (Figure 2(D)). Briefly, Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus and Acetivibrio were the prominent bacterial taxa in CON mice, while Phocaeicola, Rodentibacter and Parabacteroides were the prominent bacterial taxa in CSDS mice. In addition, we found that there were close relationships between depressive-like behaviours and five differential bacterial taxa: body weight was significantly correlated with four different genera, including Rodentibacter, Phocaeicola, Parabacteroides and Limosilactobacillus; CD% was significantly correlated with genera Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus; SP, ET and IT were significantly correlated with genera Rodentibacter and Lactobacillus, respectively (Figure 2(E)).

Differential gut microbiota compositions between CON mice and CSDS mice: (A) No significant differences in alpha diversity were found between the two groups; (B) relative abundances of gut microbiota at the phylum level showed that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the two dominant bacterial taxa in the two groups: (C) PCoA plot showed that there were significant differences in the compositions of gut microbiota between the two groups; (D) there were six different bacterial taxa identified by LEfSe (LDA > 2.0 and p < 0.05); (E) close relationships between depressive-like behaviours and differential bacterial taxa were observed. Each group had eight mice.

Differential neurotransmitters in CSDS mice

Compared to CON mice (n = 8), CSDS mice (n = 8) had the significantly higher levels of 3-OMDP, phenylethylamine (PEA), GABA, Ornithine (Orn), and 5-Guanylic acid (5-GMP) were significantly lower, whereas the level of 4-Hydroxy-3-methoxymandelic acid (VMA) in the faeces (Figure 3(A)). In the colon, the levels of 3-OMDP, L-aspartic acid (Asp), and spermine (Spn) were significantly lower in CSDS mice than in CON mice (Figure 3(B)). In the hippocampus, the levels of 3-OMDP, 5-GMP, norepinephrine (NE), and 1,4-Diamino­butane (Pun) were significantly lower in CSDS mice than in CON mice (Figure 3(C)). In the blood, compared to CON mice, CSDS mice had significantly lower levels of 3-OMDP, levodopa (L-DP), dopamine (DA), GABA, Orn, and glutathione (GSH), along with higher levels of Asp and L-glutamic acid (Glu) (Figure 3(D)). Among these differential neurotransmitters, 3-OMDP, PEA, VMA, NE, L-DP and DA belonged to the catecholaminergic pathway, whereas GABA, Orn, 5-GMP, Asp, Spn, Pun, GSH and Glu belonged to the GABAergic pathway.

Differential peripheral and central neurotransmitters in CSDS mice: (A) six differential neurotransmitters in the faeces of CSDS mice were identified; (B) three differential neurotransmitters in the Colon of CSDS mice were identified; (C) four differential neurotransmitters in the hippocampus of CSDS mice were identified; (D) eight differential neurotransmitters in the blood of CSDS mice were identified. Among these differential neurotransmitters, 3-OMDP, PEA, VMA, NE, L-DP, and DA belonged to the catecholaminergic pathway, and GABA, Orn, 5-GMP, Asp, Spn, Pun, GSH and Glu belonged to the GABAergic pathway. Each group had eight mice.

Correlations between differential gut microbiota and neurotransmitters

Correlation analysis showed that six differential genera were closely correlated with 14 differential neurotransmitters in gut-brain axis (faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus; n = 8 in each group), and Limosilactobacillus might have the greatest influence on these differential neurotransmitters compared to other genera (Figure 4(A)). Moreover, we found that 3-OMDP in blood was significantly correlated with five of the six differential genera, and Limosilactobacillus was significantly correlated with 3-OMDP in blood and 3-OMDP in hippocampus (Figure 4(A)). Further analysis revealed that peripheral differential neurotransmitters were closely related to central differential neurotransmitters: central 5-GMP, NE, Pun and 3-OMDP were significantly correlated with 10, 2, 6 and 8 peripheral neurotransmitters, respectively (Figure 4(B)). All of these central differential neurotransmitters were significantly correlated with at least one type of depressive-like behaviour (Figure 4(B)). These results indicated that the interactions between peripheral neurotransmitters and central neurotransmitters might be closely involved in the onset of depression.

Correlations between differential gut microbiota and neurotransmitters: (A) close relationships were observed between differential genera and differential neurotransmitters. The number of neurotransmitters closely related with Limosilactobacillus was biggest, and 3-OMDP in blood was significantly correlated with five of six differential genera; (B) correlation analysis showed that the differential peripheral neurotransmitters could significantly affect central differential neurotransmitters, especially 5-GMP and 3-OMDP. Each group had eight mice.

Differential inflammatory factors in CSDS mice

Four inflammation-related cytokines were detected in hippocampus: AAT, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6. As shown in Figure 5(A), CSDS mice had significantly higher levels of IL-1β and IL-6 compared to CON mice (n = 8 in each group). Although the AAT and TNF-α levels showed an increasing trend, the differences were not statistically significant. These results indicated that mice with CSDS-induced depression had increased inflammation levels in brain. Furthermore, we found that IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were negatively correlated with Limosilactobacillus, and IL-1β was positively correlated with Phocaeicola (Figure 5(B)). The correlations between differential inflammation-related cytokines and neurotransmitters were also explored. The results showed that IL-6 also correlated with Glu (r = 0.609, p = 0.047), GSH (r=-0.636, p = 0.035), and VMA (r = 0.657, p = 0.020), and IL-1β was correlated with VMA (r = 0.72, p = 0.018) and 3-OMDP (r=-0.601, p = 0.039). Taken together, these results suggested that the disordered gut microbiota might cause the increased inflammation levels in brain by regulating the host’s peripheral and central neurotransmitters.

Correlations between differential gut microbiota and inflammatory factors: (A) AAT and TNF-α were not significantly higher in CSDS mice than in CON mice, and IL-1β and IL-6 were significantly higher in CSDS mice than in CON mice; (B) Phocaeicola was significantly correlated with IL-1β, and Limosilactobacillus was significantly correlated IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. Each group had eight mice.

Potential ways in gut-brain axis

In this study, the level of 3-OMDP was found to be consistently decreased in faeces, colon, blood and hippocampus of CSDS mice compared to CON mice (n = 8 in each group, Figure 3). Moreover, we found that 3-OMDP in feces was significantly correlated with 3-OMDP in colon (r = 0.53, p = 0.04), 3-OMDP in colon was significantly correlated with 3-OMDP in blood (r = 0.56, p = 0.03), and 3-OMDP in blood was significantly correlated with 3-OMDP in hippocampus (r = 0.85, p = 0.00006). These results indicated that the 3-OMDP level in gut could obviously affect 3-OMDP level in brain, and 3-OMDP might be a vital neurotransmitter in depression. Meanwhile, we found that Limosilactobacillus was closely correlated with peripheral and central 3-OMDP levels (Figure 4(A)). Disturbances in the neurotransmitter balance were often accompanied by the release of inflammatory cytokines. As our study showed, the decreased 3-OMDP level in hippocampus of CSDS mice was accompanied by the increased IL-6 and IL-1β levels. Therefore, we deduced that the decreased Limosilactobacillus caused the disturbances of peripheral and central 3-OMDP, which resulted in the increased IL-6 and IL-1β levels in brain, eventually leading to the onset of depression (Figure 6).

Limosilactobacillus-3-OMDP-IL-1β/IL-6 as potential way in gut-brain axis. Here, the level of 3-OMDP was consistently decreased in faeces, Colon, blood and hippocampus of CSDS mice, and significant correlations were found between 3-OMDP in faeces and 3-OMDP in Colon, between 3-OMDP in Colon and 3-OMDP in blood, between 3-OMDP in blood and 3-OMDP in hippocampus. Meanwhile, we found that Limosilactobacillus was closely correlated with peripheral and Central 3-OMDP, and IL-1β was correlated with 3-OMDP. These results indicated that Limosilactobacillus might affect IL-6 and IL-1β levels in brain via peripheral and Central 3-OMDP, and then eventually leading to the onset of depression.

Antidepressant effects of 3-OMDP

To investigate the antidepressant effect of 3-OMDP (n = 8 in each group), we injected 3-OMDP intraperitoneally into CSDS mice and then performed behavioural tests, including SPT, OFT, and TST. Firstly, we assessed the effects of 3-OMDP on depressive-like behaviours. As shown in Figure 7, after ten days of intervention, CSDS + 3-OMDP mice had significantly higher SP (p = 0.015, Figure 7(A)), lower IT (p = 0.008, Figure 7(B)) and higher CD% (p = 0.043, Figure 7(C)) compared to CSDS+PBS mice, but had similar SP, IT and CD% compared to CON+PBS mice (Figure 7). Meanwhile, the SP, IT and CD% in CON+PBS group were similar to CON + 3-OMDP group, but significantly different to CSDS+PBS group (p = 0.004, p = 0.012 and p = 0.014, respectively, Figure 7). These results indicated that 3-OMDP could significantly improve the depressive-like behaviors in CSDS mice.

Effects of 3-OMDP on depressive-like behaviours in CSDS mice: (A) sucrose preference (%) was significantly improved in CSDS mice receiving 3-OMDP than in CSDS mice receiving PBS; (B) immobility time was significantly decreased after 3-OMDP intervention in CSDS mice; (C) centre distance (%) was significantly increased after 3-OMDP intervention in CSDS mice. The similar sucrose preference (%), immobility time and centre distance (%) in CON+PBS group were similar to both CON + 3-OMDP group and CSDS + 3-OMDP group, but significantly different to CSDS+PBS group. These results indicated that 3-OMDP could significantly improve the depressive-like behaviours in CSDS mice. Each group had eight mice.

Secondly, we assessed the effects of 3-OMDP on two differential inflammation factors in CSDS mice (n = 8 in each group). As shown in Figure 8, CSDS + 3-OMDP mice had significantly lower IL-1β (p = 0.035) and IL-6 (p = 0.021) levels compared to CSDS+PBS mice but had similar IL-1β and IL-6 levels compared to CON+PBS mice (Figure 7). Meanwhile, the IL-1β and IL-6 levels in CON+PBS group were similar to CON + 3-OMDP group, but significantly lower to CSDS+PBS group (p = 0.031 and p = 0.049, respectively, Figure 8). These results indicated that 3-OMDP could significantly decrease the higher IL-1β and IL-6 levels in the hippocampus of CSDS mice.

Effects of 3-OMDP on IL-1β and IL-6 in CSDS mice. After 3-OMDP intervention, the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 were significantly decreased in the hippocampus of CSDS + 3-OMDP group compared to CSDS+PBS group. CON+PBS group had similar levels of IL-1β and IL-6 compared to both CON + 3-OMDP group and CSDS + 3-OMDP group. These results showed that 3-OMDP could reverse the increased levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in the hippocampus of CSDS mice. Each group had eight mice.

Discussion

In this study, we identified six differential genera in faeces and 14 differential neurotransmitters in gut-brain axis between the two groups. Meanwhile, we observed two significantly increased inflammatory factors (IL-6 and IL-1β) in the hippocampus of CSDS mice. Among these differential variables, 3-OMDP was consistently decreased in both peripheral and central tissues. The results of correlation analysis showed that Limosilactobacillus was significantly correlated to 3-OMDP and IL-6/IL-1β. Previous studies reported that chronic stress-induced neurotransmitter disturbances and inflammatory responses might contribute to depression [37,38]. Thus, to further explore the relationships between 3-OMDP and IL-6/IL-1β, we conducted intervention experiments. The results showed that 3-OMDP could improve the depressive-like behaviours as well as the recovery of increased IL-6 and IL-1β levels in hippocampus of CSDS mice. These findings suggested that gut microbiota might be involved in the onset of depression via the modulating effects of neurotransmitters on inflammation level, and ‘Limosilactobacillus-3-OMDP-IL-1β/IL-6’ might be a potential pathway in the crosstalk of gut and brain in depression.

Neurotransmitters were closely involved in the regulation of a variety of mental activities, such as emotions, mindset and cognition [39,40]. DA and NE belonged to catecholamine pathway (important monoamine neurotransmitters), whose depletion was considered as a risk factor for the onset of depression [41]. Here, we found that DA and NE were down-regulated in CSDS mice. PEA and L-DP were catecholamine intermediates. Previous study reported that PEA might be a neuromodulator of aminergic synapses, which could boost energy and elevate mood [42]. L-DP was used in the treatment of Parkinson’s disease in clinical practice, because it could supplement DA [43]. Interestingly, both PEA and L-DP were found to be decreased in CSDS mice. These results indicated that the disturbances of catecholaminergic neurotransmitters might be involved in the onset of depression.

GABA, as the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in CNS, was produced through glutamate metabolism. Evidence demonstrated that depressed patients and mice had the significantly changed GABA levels compared to controls [44,45]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy study also showed that GABA level was generally reduced in depression patients [46]. Here, GABA was found to be decreased in faeces and blood of CSDS mice. In addition, Zomkowski et al. reported that GMP had antidepressant-like effect, which might be due to the indirect serotonin activation resulting from the blockade of N-methyl-D-aspartic acid or N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors [47]. In this study, we found the significantly decreased 5-GMP in faeces and hippocampus of CSDS mice. These findings further demonstrated that the disordered GABAergic neurotransmitters played important roles in the pathogenesis of depression.

As a metabolite of L-DP, 3-OMDP was catalysed by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) [48]. Previous study showed that 3-OMDP could affect the DA level in brain through inhibiting dopamine transporter (DAT) and uptake in both striatal membranes and PC12 cells [49]. DA and its metabolites could play a crucial role in brain behavioural reward system. Moriya et al. reported that DAT binding and dopamine concentration were significantly reduced in patients with depression and anhedonia [50]. Additionally, as an enzymatic substrate for COMT, 3-OMDP might exert negative feedback inhibition of COMT. In other words, high 3-OMDP level might inhibit the catechol translocation of L-DP, promoting the conversion of L-DP to DA, which eventually maintained DA levels in the CNS. Considering the important role of behavioural reward system in depression, we hypothesized that 3-OMDP might be involved in the development of depression by influencing DA levels. Interestingly, in this study, we observed significant down-regulation of DA in blood and 3-OMDP in the serum, hippocampus, colon and faeces of CSDS mice. Moreover, we found that after 3-OMDP intervention, depression-like behaviours in CSDS mice were significantly improved. These results suggested that 3-OMDP held the promise as a potential therapeutic target for depression.

Depression and inflammation were intertwined, and people with chronic inflammatory diseases were more prone to be depressed [51–53]. Previous studies reported that the levels of some inflammatory factors were often increased in the serum of patients with depression, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [54,55]. Genetic studies also revealed the close relationships between IL-6/IL-1β genes and the risk of depression [56,57]. IL-6 and IL-1β were two common pro-inflammatory factors that could cause inflammatory responses in the body. In this study, we observed the significantly increased levels of IL-6 and IL-1β in CSDS mice. Furthermore, many studies have shown that the disturbances of gut microbiota could activate the immune system, which then produced a variety of inflammatory factors [58,59]. Here, we observed the significant associations between Limosilactobacillus/Phocaeicola and IL-6/IL-1β in CSDS mice. These results suggested that gut microbiota might be closely related to depression via regulating inflammatory responses.

Evidence demonstrated that the disturbances of gut microbiota were related with many diseases, and microbiome was consisted of microbes that were both helpful and potentially harmful [60–63]. Limosilactobacillus was a beneficial bacterium in human body [64]. Tyagi et al. reported that the fermented products of Limosilactobacillus reuteri had the potential ability to enhance bioactive compounds and antioxidants, which was involved in antidepressant effects by regulating oxidative stress [65]. Meanwhile, Bron et al. found that the deficiency of folic acid and vitamin B12 could lead to homocysteine levels in the body, which was closely related to the onset of depression; and Limosilactobacillus could promote the production of folic acid and vitamin B12 [66]. These results indicated that Limosilactobacillus might have the antidepressant effects. Interestingly, in this study, we found that the decreased Limosilactobacillus might have the greatest influence on these differential neurotransmitters in gut-brain axis of CSDS mice, especially 3-OMDP, and both increased IL-6 and IL-1β levels were improved after 3-OMDP intervention. Therefore, Limosilactobacillus might play a crucial role in the onset of depression, and ‘Limosilactobacillus-3-OMDP-IL-1β/IL-6’ might be a potential pathway in the crosstalk of gut and brain in depression.

Several limitations of our present study should be mentioned here. Firstly, only four inflammatory factors were detected here; future studies were needed to explore the correlations between other kinds of inflammatory factors and gut microbiota. Secondly, we did not further explore how Limosilactobacillus was involved, directly or indirectly, to the production of 3-OMDP. Future studies should use microbiota transplantation (Limosilactobacillus) to specify the relationships between 3-OMDP and Limosilactobacillus. Thirdly, each group had only eight mice; thus, our conclusions were needed future studies to further validate and support. Fourthly, our study only focused on the intraperitoneal injection of 3-OMDP, future studies should consider rectal or other routes of administration, and check whether there were any metabolites of 3-OMDP produced after administration. Fifthly, only one kind of 3-OMDP dose was used here. Future studies should include different doses of 3-OMDP treatment to establish a dose-response relationship conclusively.

In conclusion, six different genera, 14 different neurotransmitters in central and peripheral tissues, and two inflammatory factors (IL-6 and IL-1β) in hippocampus were identified in CSDS mice. Close relationships were found among the differential genera, neurotransmitters, and inflammatory factors. Further analysis revealed that Limosilactobacillus was closely related to central and peripheral 3-OMDP and both inflammatory factors. The results of intervention experiments showed that 3-OMDP could significantly improve the increased IL-6/IL-1β levels and depression-like behaviours in CSDS mice. These findings suggested that disturbances in gut microbiota might contribute to the occurrence of depression via modulating neurotransmitters to affect inflammation level, and ‘Limosilactobacillus-3-OMDP- IL-1β/IL-6’ might be a potential pathway in depression. Our results could be helpful for the future exploration of the pathogenesis of depression and provide potential therapeutic targets for treating depression.
