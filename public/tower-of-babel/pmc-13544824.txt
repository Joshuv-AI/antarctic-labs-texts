Strangulated right obturator hernia causing small bowel obstruction in an elderly woman with bilateral obturator hernias and a positive Howship–Romberg sign: a case report
Authors: Jose Mauricio Barragan Garcia, Cesar Augusto Marquez Mora, Maria Jose Diaz Amaya, Oscar Blancarte Vidal, Ivan Alfonso Castañeda Oviedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Obturator hernia is a rare but potentially life-threatening cause of mechanical small-bowel obstruction, predominantly affecting elderly women. Delayed diagnosis is common because of a nonspecific presentation, and strangulation frequently necessitates emergency surgery.Case presentation:An 84-year-old woman presented with diffuse abdominal pain, abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, oral intolerance, constipation, and a positive Howship–Romberg sign. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated bilateral obturator hernias, with an incarcerated right-sided small-bowel loop associated with obstruction and free fluid. Emergency surgery was performed through an infraumbilical midline laparotomy. A right obturator hernia containing ischemic distal ileum was identified and manually reduced. Approximately 10 cm of nonviable small bowel was resected, followed by a hand-sewn, end-to-end ileoileal anastomosis in two layers with 3-0 polyglactin 910, which was located approximately 250 cm from the angle of Treitz. The obturator defect, measuring approximately 1 cm, was repaired by primary closure with 3-0 polyglactin 910. Mesh was not used because bowel resection increased the risk of contamination. Histopathology confirmed ischemic intestinal injury with necrosis. The postoperative course was favorable.Clinical discussion:This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing the Howship–Romberg sign and the value of computed tomography for preoperative diagnosis. It also illustrates that an obturator hernia may occur in patients without the classic emaciated phenotype. In the emergency setting, open transperitoneal surgery allows bowel assessment, resection when needed, and definitive repair of the defect.Conclusion:An obturator hernia should be considered in elderly women presenting with intestinal obstruction, particularly when the Howship–Romberg sign is present. Early diagnosis by computed tomography and urgent surgical management are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Obturator hernia is an uncommon pelvic hernia and a rare cause of mechanical small bowel obstruction, accounting for a very small proportion of all abdominal wall hernias. Despite its rarity, it carries disproportionate clinical importance because it is frequently diagnosed only after incarceration or strangulation has occurred, resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates. Elderly women are predominantly affected because of wider pelvic anatomy, progressive laxity of pelvic tissues, and reduction of protective preperitoneal fat[1–5].

Clinical diagnosis is challenging because symptoms are often nonspecific and the physical examination may be unrevealing. One of the best-known clinical clues is the Howship–Romberg sign, caused by irritation or compression of the obturator nerve by the hernia contents. It classically manifests as pain along the medial thigh that may radiate to the knee and can be exacerbated by hip extension, abduction, or internal rotation. Although not universally present, when identified, it may meaningfully raise suspicion for an obturator hernia in the appropriate clinical setting[1,3,6].

Computed tomography is currently considered the diagnostic modality of choice because it can accurately demonstrate the obturator canal, the herniated bowel loop, laterality, associated obstruction, free fluid, and signs of vascular compromise. Preoperative diagnosis is therefore increasingly feasible and facilitates operative planning[1,3,6–8].

We present the case of an elderly woman with bilateral obturator hernias identified on imaging, complicated by right-sided incarceration, strangulation, and small-bowel obstruction, with correlation among clinical findings, annotated computed tomography images, operative findings, and histopathology. This case report is presented in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist.

Presentation of the case

An 84-year-old woman with a medical history of systemic arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and a remote history of left thigh liposarcoma treated with tumorectomy in 1992 presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of diffuse, colicky abdominal pain, progressive abdominal distension, nausea, multiple episodes of vomiting, oral intolerance, and absence of bowel movements. Her approximate body mass index was 31.3 kg/m2.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination showed abdominal distension and a positive Howship–Romberg sign, consisting of pain radiating along the medial thigh that increased with lower-limb movement and was consistent with obturator nerve irritation. Laboratory studies demonstrated renal dysfunction, dehydration, mild hyponatremia, hypoalbuminemia, and anemia.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated bilateral obturator hernias. On the right side, a short segment of small bowel was seen herniating through the obturator canal, located between the pectineus and obturator muscles, and was associated with obstruction and free fluid. The right-sided defect measured approximately 2.2 cm, and a smaller left-sided obturator defect measured approximately 1.3 cm. The axial image demonstrated the right obturator hernia, the incarcerated bowel loop, and free fluid, while also showing the left obturator region for comparison (Fig. 1). Coronal reconstruction confirmed bowel herniation through the right obturator canal and the presence of adjacent free fluid (Fig. 2). Sagittal reconstruction further demonstrated the anatomical course of the herniated bowel through the right obturator canal (Fig. 3). Given the radiological findings and clinical suspicion of strangulation, emergency surgery was indicated. Under balanced general anesthesia, an open transperitoneal approach was performed through an infraumbilical midline laparotomy. Upon entry into the abdominal cavity, the bowel was explored systematically. A right obturator hernia was identified through an approximately 1 cm defect, containing a distal ileal loop with established ischemic changes and necrosis. The herniated bowel was manually reduced from the obturator canal. The operative exposure allowed direct visualization of the pelvic floor and obturator region through the peritoneal cavity.

After reduction, bowel viability was reassessed. Because the involved ileal segment was nonviable, approximately 10 cm of compromised small bowel was resected. A hand-sewn end-to-end ileoileal anastomosis was then performed in two layers with absorbable 3-0 polyglactin 910 (Vicryl®), approximately 250 cm from the angle of Treitz. The obturator defect was repaired with primary suture closure using 3-0 polyglactin 910. No prosthetic mesh was used because intestinal resection increased the risk of contamination and subsequent mesh infection. Although the contralateral side had shown a smaller obturator defect on imaging, no clinically significant incarcerated left-sided hernia requiring repair was identified during the operation.

At the end of the procedure, hemostasis was adequate, the anastomosis was intact, and the remaining bowel appeared viable. The abdominal wall was closed in layers without intraoperative complications.

Histopathological examination demonstrated acute and chronic inflammatory infiltrates, hemorrhage, granulation tissue, and areas of necrosis consistent with ischemic intestinal injury secondary to strangulation.

Postoperatively, the patient progressed favorably, without evidence of an anastomotic leak, recurrent acute abdomen, or a systemic inflammatory response. Intestinal transit was appropriately restored, oral intake was progressively advanced, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 with satisfactory clinical recovery during outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Obturator hernia remains an uncommon but dangerous cause of intestinal obstruction because most patients present only after incarceration or strangulation has developed. Delayed diagnosis is common, which explains the historically high rates of bowel resection and perioperative mortality. Therefore, early recognition of suggestive findings is critical[1,3–5,8,9].

This patient displayed several classic features of obturator hernia, including advanced age, female sex, bowel obstruction, and a positive Howship–Romberg sign. The latter deserves emphasis because it reflects irritation of the obturator nerve by hernia contents and may be one of the few preoperative clues in an otherwise nonspecific clinical presentation. In this case, the clinical sign meaningfully supported clinical suspicion before operative confirmation[1,3,6,9].

An additional point of interest is that the patient did not fit the classic description of a thin, emaciated elderly woman. Her approximate body mass index was 31.3 kg/m2. Although loss of preperitoneal fat is traditionally considered a predisposing factor, obturator hernia should not be excluded solely on the basis of body habitus. Therefore, this case expands the clinical spectrum in which the diagnosis should be considered[1,6,9,10].

Computed tomography played a decisive role in the diagnosis and preoperative planning. The annotated axial, coronal, and sagittal images clearly demonstrated right-sided obturator canal involvement, a herniated bowel loop, and associated free fluid, while also showing the contralateral obturator region. These imaging findings correlated closely with the operative findings and improved the educational value of the report. Intraoperative photographs were unavailable; therefore, annotated radiological images were used to highlight the relevant anatomical landmarks and radiological signs of incarceration, including the obturator canal, bowel loop, and adjacent free fluid[1,3,6,8,9].

The operative technique also merits a detailed discussion. In this emergency setting, an open transperitoneal approach through an infraumbilical midline laparotomy was selected because it provided rapid access to the abdominal cavity, allowed full assessment of bowel viability, enabled safe reduction of the hernia, and permitted intestinal resection and anastomosis when required. The incarcerated bowel was manually reduced, and the nonviable distal ileal segment was resected. Restoration of continuity was achieved with a two-layer hand-sewn end-to-end ileoileal anastomosis using absorbable 3-0 polyglactin 910, approximately 250 cm from the angle of Treitz. The obturator defect, approximately 1 cm in diameter, was repaired with primary suture closure using 3-0 polyglactin 910[2–4,7,9].

The choice to avoid mesh was deliberate. Although tension-free prosthetic repair may be appropriate in selected clean cases, the need for bowel resection raised concerns about contamination and subsequent mesh infection. Under these circumstances, primary closure was considered the safest option. Likewise, although the contralateral side had appeared to be involved radiologically, no contralateral incarcerated hernia requiring repair was treated intraoperatively since the priority was the management of the complicated right-sided lesion in an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities[2–4].

Histopathology confirmed an ischemic, necrotic bowel injury, validating the intraoperative judgment that resection was necessary. Despite advanced age, chronic kidney disease, hypoalbuminemia, and anemia, the patient’s postoperative course was favorable. This outcome underscores the importance of timely diagnosis and urgent surgical intervention[3,4,8,9].

Conclusion

Obturator hernia is a rare but serious cause of mechanical small-bowel obstruction that should be suspected in elderly women presenting with obstructive symptoms, especially when associated with a positive Howship–Romberg sign. Computed tomography is essential for accurate diagnosis, anatomical definition, and operative planning. In emergency cases with strangulation and nonviable bowel, open transperitoneal surgery allows manual reduction, bowel resection when needed, and definitive primary repair of the obturator defect. This case also demonstrates that obturator hernia may occur even in patients without the classic emaciated phenotype[1–4,6,8–10].
