Request for Clarification on Data Presentation in Table 2, Figures 3 and 4 of Onuki et al. (Cancer Science 2020)
Authors: Junying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Letter requests clarification of two data presentation issues in Onuki et al. (Cancer Science 2020): a numerical discrepancy in Table 2 and the unspecified age range in Figure 3 that may explain the 1000‐case difference with Figure 4.

To the Editor,

I have read with interest the article by Onuki et al. [1] “Human papillomavirus genotype contribution to cervical cancer and precancer: Implications for screening and vaccination in Japan” (Cancer Science 2020). The study provides valuable baseline data for cervical cancer prevention in Japan. However, during my reading, I encountered two points in Table 2, Figures 3 and 4 where the denominators and age ranges are not clearly defined. I respectfully request the authors to clarify these points.

To facilitate systematic comparison of the different denominators used across Table 2, Figures 3 and 4, I constructed analytic populations and sample size reconciliation for CIN2‐3/AIS cases (Table S1) and a summary of the data discrepancies (Figure 1). Based on this framework, I identified the following two points that merit the authors' clarification.

Summary of data discrepancies in Onuki et al. (Cancer Science 2020).

Point 1: Numerical Discrepancy in Table 2 Between the Total ICC Cases and the Sum of its Histological Subtypes (Table S2)

Simple arithmetic shows that 672 + 358 = 1030, not 1004. The difference is 26 cases. I respectfully request the authors to confirm which of the two numbers (1004 or 1030) is correct and to correct the table accordingly.

Point 2: The Age Range of the Population Analyzed in Figure 3 Is Not Specified in the Figure Legend

The original Figure 3 legend describes the figure as showing cumulative relative contributions of oncogenic HPV types in cervical cancer and precancer by histology. No age range is mentioned in the legend. In contrast, the legend of Figure 4 explicitly states that the figure includes women aged 20–39 years for the age‐stratified analysis.

Notably, the ICC cases shown in Figure 4 sum to 1004, which matches the ICC total in Figure 3 (1004). This numerical correspondence may lead readers to assume that Figure 3 and Figure 4 share the same age range (20–39 years). However, the precancer cases in Figure 4 sum to 2073 (168 + 679 + 1115 + 111), while Figure 3 reports 3073 cases of CIN2‐3/AIS. The difference is 1000 cases. To clarify this discrepancy, I examined the arithmetic relationships between the analytic populations (Table S1). As derived in Table S1, the arithmetic relationships between the analytic populations show that Population C (Figure 3, unvaccinated, n = 3073) minus the 16–19 age group yields Population D (Figure 4B, 20–39 years, n = 2073). The difference of 1000 cases therefore corresponds precisely to the number of HPV‐positive, unvaccinated CIN2‐3/AIS cases aged 16–19 years.

Given that the mean age of the CIN2‐3/AIS cohort was 32.2 years (SD 4.6) and that the 20–24 age group in Figure 4B comprised only 168 cases, a 16–19 age group of 1000 cases would represent a substantial proportion of the cohort. I therefore respectfully request the authors to confirm whether this derivation is correct and whether any additional exclusion criteria were applied to Figure 4B that are not stated in the legend.

In summary, I respectfully request the authors to clarify the three points summarized in Figure 1: (a) the age range represented in Figure 3; (b) the exact number of vaccinated CIN2‐3/AIS cases excluded from the analysis; and (c) the precise number of CIN2‐3/AIS cases in the 16–19 age group. I also suggest that the authors explicitly state the age range of the population analyzed in Figure 3 within the figure legend to avoid reader confusion.

I thank the authors for their valuable work and look forward to their response.
