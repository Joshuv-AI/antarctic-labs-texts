Prognostic value of RANK signalling in breast cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Linrong Li, Xuejing Wang, Yan Li, Songjie Shen, Yali Xu, Yidong Zhou, Qiang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the prognostic value of osteoprotegerin (OPG), receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) and RANK ligand (RANKL) of the RANK signalling in patients with breast cancer.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesWe searched the PubMed, Embase and Cochrane databases up to October 2024.Eligibility criteriaStudies of patients with primary breast cancer evaluating OPG, RANK or RANKL expression in relation to survival outcomes were included.Data extraction and synthesisData from eligible studies were extracted, and HRs with 95% CIs for overall survival (OS), metastasis-free survival (MFS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were used to assess prognostic value.ResultsA total of 18 studies, comprising 11 141 patients with primary breast cancer, were included in this analysis. Elevated OPG expression was found to be significantly associated with an increase in MFS (HR=0.59, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.80, p<0.001) in patients with breast cancer. Conversely, elevated RANK expression was associated with a statistically significant reduction in MFS (HR=1.74, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.40, p<0.001) and DFS (HR=1.34, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.68, p=0.01). No significant association was found between RANKL expression and MFS, DFS or OS after adjusting for publication bias.ConclusionsDownregulation of the RANK signalling predicts survival benefits in patients with primary breast cancer. Further investigation is warranted to explore the therapeutic potential of inhibiting RANK signalling in breast cancer.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42021234825.Keywords: Meta-Analysis, Breast tumours, Survival

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. It is estimated that over 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer were diagnosed globally, and approximately 665 684 women succumbed to the disease in 2022.1 With advances in treatment, breast cancer mortality rates have declined steadily since their peak in 1989.2 However, rather than primary tumours, metastasis and recurrence are major causes of mortality.3 Bone represents a common site for metastasis, accounting for the site of about 40% of metastasis in breast cancer. More than 70% of patients with metastatic breast cancer eventually develop bone metastases.4

First identified in the late 1990s as an important bone remodelling regulator,5 the receptor activator of nuclear factor (NF)-κB (RANK) signalling has emerged as an important paracrine mediator of hormonal signalling in breast tissue,6 as well as a crucial pathway in the initiation, progression and metastasis of breast cancer.7 8 The RANK signalling consists of three essential molecules: (1) osteoprotegerin (OPG), decoy receptor for RANK, (2) RANK and (3) RANK-ligand (RANKL). On binding to RANKL, the RANK receptor transduces signals that promote proliferation, survival and migration, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition and stemness in mammary epithelial cells, thereby facilitating tumourigenesis and metastasis.9 The underlying signalling pathways include the phosphorylation of the mitogen-activated protein kinases, the NF-κB, the hepatocyte growth factor, as well as the phosphatidylinositol (PI) pathways.10,12 Furthermore, accumulating evidence has indicated a potential correlation between RANK signalling and oestrogen receptor (ER).13 And higher serum concentrations of OPG were associated with significantly increased risk and all-cause mortality in ER-negative breast cancer rather than ER-positive disease.14 15

The skeletal and non-skeletal roles of RANK signalling underscore its potential as a prognostic indicator for breast cancer survival. However, only a limited number of contradictory findings have been reported. To provide more robust evidence, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on these findings to evaluate the prognostic roles of OPG, RANK and RANKL in breast cancer.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.16 Articles were identified through literature search on PubMed, Embase and Cochrane, with no restrictions on publication date or language. The following key terms were used: (“Osteoprotegerin”, “RANK Ligand” or “Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor-kappa B”) and (“Breast Neoplasm”) and (“outcome”, “prognosis” or “survival”). A detailed search strategy is shown in online supplemental appendix 1. All potentially relevant studies were considered for review, regardless of the primary outcome. Reference lists of selected articles as well as relevant conference abstracts were also reviewed to identify additional studies, focusing solely on published data, with no attempts to obtain unpublished information. The systematic review and meta-analysis was concluded in October 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) studies evaluating the correlation of OPG, RANK or RANKL expression with overall survival (OS), metastasis-free survival (MFS) or disease-free survival (DFS) in primary breast cancer and (2) studies with sufficient data to determine an estimate of HR and 95% CIs. Exclusion criteria were: (1) duplicates, animal studies, cell line studies, reviews and meta-analyses and (2) studies with overlapping subjects.

Screening and data extraction

Two independent investigators conducted a review of study titles and abstracts, after which eligible studies were retrieved for a full-text assessment. Zotero V.6.0.37 was used for citation management and duplicate identification. In cases of duplicate publication on overlapping populations, only the most recent or comprehensive report was included. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved by discussion or the involvement of a third reviewer.

Data extracted from each selected study included the first author’s last name, publication year, inclusion period, sample origin, sample size, sample type, testing method and cut-off for the expression of OPG, RANK and RANKL, lymph node status, treatment, molecular subtyping and median follow-up. For each study, HRs and 95% CIs for OS, MFS and DFS were retrieved when provided, or calculated from reported results using the spreadsheet developed by Tierney et al.17 We at first looked for the Observed-to-Expected statistic, 95 % CI for the HR or log-rank statistic with its p value, as these metrics allow for most accurate calculation.18 19 When such data were unavailable, patient counts at risk at specific time points were extracted, or, if less precise, estimated from Kaplan-Meier curves using Engauge Digitizer V.12.1. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death, MFS as the time from diagnosis to first distant metastasis, and DFS as the time from diagnosis to tumour recurrence or death.

Quality assessment

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) was applied to assess certainty of evidence.20 21 The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess risk of bias for each eligible study.22 Each study was evaluated across seven items within three broad categories: selection, comparability and outcome. For selection, representativeness of the cases, case definition and testing method were assessed. For comparability, the lymph node status and ER status were assessed. For the outcome, the follow-up period and outcome adjustment were assessed. Three levels of bias were defined: low risk of bias, unclear risk of bias and high risk of bias. Studies with no more than two items rated as high risk of bias were categorised as overall ‘low risk of bias’ and were included in the meta-analysis. The risk of bias was illustrated using Review Manager V.5.4.1.

Evidence synthesis

For meta-analysis, pooled HR estimates with 95% CIs were calculated using fixed or random-effect models, when appropriate. An HR>1 indicates shorter survival for the group with higher marker expression, or vice versa. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.23 Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and sensitivity analyses were performed to adjust for potential publication bias. Subgroup analyses were conducted by ER status, which was predetermined before data collection, given its potential influence on treatment and survival and the association between RANK signalling and hormone receptor subtype. All statistical analyses were performed using R V.3.6.1,24 employing the ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’ packages. A p-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

This meta-analysis was conducted without direct involvement of patients or the public in the design, conduct or reporting of the study.

Results

Study selection

We identified a total of 7447 records for the systematic review and meta-analysis, sourced from PubMed (n=2018), Embase (n=5412) and Cochrane (n=17). An additional 16 records were identified through other sources. After removing duplicates, 6652 records were screened for title or abstract, and 50 records were selected for full-text review. From the 50 full-text articles retrieved, 32 studies were excluded because they studied stage IV breast cancer (n=5), lacked survival analysis (n=14), were animal studies (n=4), cell line studies (n=8) or had overlapping subjects (n=1). Finally, 18 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the process of study identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion.

The median follow-up time ranged from 36 to 140 months, generally exceeding 5 years except in two studies (by Hatzis et al25 26 and Esserman et al26 27) and in five studies that did not specify follow-up duration. Samples were collected from surgical specimens, fine-needle aspirations, core biopsies or serum. Testing methods included mRNA microarrays, quantitative PCR, quantitative reverse transcription-PCR, ELISA, electrochemiluminescence and immunohistochemistry. Expression levels assessed by mRNA microarray were often dichotomised using bimodal cut-offs, whereas immunohistochemistry was evaluated using scoring systems based on staining intensity and positive area. Two studies specifically evaluated the prognostic role in patients with ER-positive breast cancer. Additionally, one study focused on patients with lymph node-positive breast cancer, and two studies focused on patients with lymph node-negative breast cancer.

Regarding systemic therapy, Timotheadou et al28 focused on patients who underwent anthracycline-based adjuvant chemotherapy. And Hatzis et al25 26 and Esserman et al27 studied patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Unless unspecified, patients in other studies received adjuvant therapies, including chemotherapy, endocrine therapy or radiotherapy, based on their clinicopathological characteristics.

Of the 18 articles included in the meta-analysis, 10, 10 and 7 reported statistics of OPG, RANK and RANKL expression on breast cancer survival, respectively. The primary outcomes reported were OS, MFS and DFS. A detailed summary of both qualitative and quantitative findings from the 18 included studies is shown in online supplemental appendix 3. OPG expression and breast cancer prognosis

Figure 2 presents the meta-analysis results examining the relationship between OPG expression and breast cancer survival outcomes, stratified by ER status. The findings indicated that high OPG expression was not associated with a statistically significant difference in OS for patients with breast cancer patients (HR=0.97, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.47, p=0.88) across the included studies. However, elevated OPG expression was associated with a statistically significant increase in MFS (HR=0.59, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.80, p<0.001) with low heterogeneity (I2=0.0%). In addition, no significant association between OPG expression and DFS was identified (HR=1.01, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.36, p=0.94) with medium heterogeneity (I2=60.9%). A random-effects model was applied due to substantial heterogeneity between studies in calculating HRs for OS (I2=82.5%). The funnel plots showed no obvious publication bias.

RANK expression and breast cancer prognosis

Figure 3 presents the meta-analysis results examining the relationship between RANK expression and breast cancer survival outcomes, stratified by ER status. The findings indicated that high RANK expression was not associated with a statistically significant difference in OS for patients with breast cancer (HR=1.09, 95% CI 0.52 to 2.26, p=0.82) across the included studies. Though one study from Kassem et al29 indicated a reduced OS for patients with high RANK expression in ER-positive breast cancer (HR=2.79, 95% CI 1.20 to 6.49). However, elevated RANK expression was linked to a statistically significant reduction in MFS (HR=1.74, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.40, p<0.001) with low heterogeneity (I2=12.1%) in patients with breast cancer. In addition, elevated RANK expression was associated with a statistically significant reduction in DFS (HR=1.34, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.68, p=0.01) with medium heterogeneity (I2=73.8%). A random-effects model was applied due to substantial heterogeneity between studies in calculating HRs for OS (I2=82.1%). The funnel plots showed no obvious publication bias, except for DFS, which exhibited a trend toward reporting a higher HR.

RANKL expression and breast cancer prognosis

Figure 4 presents the meta-analysis results examining the relationship between RANKL expression and breast cancer survival outcomes. The results showed a statistically significant increase in OS in patients with breast cancer with elevated RANKL expression (HR=0.73, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.96, p=0.03) and medium heterogeneity (I2=53.4%). However, no significant association between RANKL expression and MFS (HR=1.54, 95% CI 0.21 to 11.52, p=0.67) or DFS was found (HR=0.89, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.07, p=0.20). The funnel plots demonstrated acceptable publication bias for reporting the HR for MFS and DFS, while a trend towards reporting a smaller HR for OS was observed. Sensitivity analysis excluding the publication by Owen et al30 yielded an insignificant result (HR=0.83, 95% CI 0.67 to 1.03, p=0.09) for OS and reduced heterogeneity (I2=14.6%) (online supplemental appendix 5).

Discussion

RANK signalling, crucial in bone physiology, has been closely linked to breast cancer development. In our study, elevated OPG expression was associated with a 41% lower rate of metastasis, whereas elevated RANK expression was associated with a 74% higher rate of metastasis and a 34% higher rate of recurrence or death, over a median follow-up time ranging from 36 to 140 months. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the prognostic impact of RANK signalling in breast cancer. Given inconsistent findings from individual studies, our synthesis provides stronger evidence and helps clarify the prognostic role of this pathway.

Our results demonstrate that RANK signalling upregulation is a biomarker of poor prognosis in primary breast cancer. This is consistent with the biological role of RANK in promoting tumour proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition and metastasis. In line with these mechanisms, RANK signalling inhibition has been investigated in preclinical and clinical settings for malignancies.31 For example, it was found that RANK signalling blockade might reduce breast cancer recurrence by inducing tumour cell differentiation.32 The ABCSG-18 trial reported continued improvement in DFS, bone MFS and OS in patients with postmenopausal breast cancer receiving aromatase inhibitors treated with the RANKL inhibitor denosumab.33 However, the GeparX and D-CARE trials failed to demonstrate consistent survival benefits.34 35 In fact, preclinical data showed that denosumab increased tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes but had minimal effect on breast cancer cell survival or proliferation.36 Moreover, high RANKL expression was reported to be associated with downregulation of proliferation and cell cycle-related pathways.37 These findings could partly explain the discrepant clinical findings and our observation that RANKL expression correlated with improved OS before adjusting for publication bias. Together, these results highlight the biological complexity of RANK signalling as well as the need for further investigation.

The relationship between RANK signalling and ER status remains unclear. Our subgroup analyses did not yield definitive results, likely due to the limited number of eligible studies. High RANK expression was reported to be more frequently seen in aggressive, ER-negative tumours,37 consistent with poorer survival in this subgroup,38 while OPG expression has been associated with better prognosis in ER-positive patients,39 suggesting their potential interactions with ER signalling pathways. These findings underscore the potential value of combining RANK signalling markers with hormone receptor status to guide the use of RANK pathway inhibitors. Moreover, combining RANK signalling markers with genomic and immunologic data may help clarify their dual role in tumour progression and immune regulation, thereby supporting rational patient selection for RANK signalling inhibition.40

Limitations of the study include the modest number of eligible studies and the reliance on observational data. As a result, marked heterogeneity was observed in the associations of OPG with OS, RANK with OS and RANKL with MFS. Substantial heterogeneity in our results may have arisen from differences in patient populations and hormone receptor status, as well as variability in sample types, testing methods and cut-offs. For example, using core biopsies (eg, Esserman et al27 and Hatzis et al25) may have underestimated expression because of heterogeneous staining. In addition, studies with limited sample sizes (eg, Kassem et al29) produced imprecise effect estimates, and indirect calculation of HRs and 95% CIs may have introduced estimation error. According to GRADE assessments of certainty, our findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be fully generalisable to all settings. The clinical and methodological variations should be considered when interpreting the pooled results, and future investigation incorporating subgroup analyses (eg, by molecular subtyping or treatment) will be needed as more evidence accumulates.

Conclusions

Downregulation of RANK signalling is associated with improved survival in primary breast cancer. The underlying mechanisms and the potential therapeutic implications of RANK pathway inhibition in breast cancer warrant further investigation.
