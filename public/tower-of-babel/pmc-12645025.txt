Associations of oral hypoglycemic use, fruit intake, and diabetes duration with gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction in type 2 diabetes patients in Zanzibar
Authors: Hassan Thabit Haji, Ashabilan Ebrahim, Ahmed Gharib Khamis, Ramla Muhidin Ali, Kaushik Ramaiya, Fredirick L Mashili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction (GAD) is a common complication of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) leading to gastroparesis, constipation, and gastroesophageal reflux disease, consequently affecting the overall quality of life. However, its prevalence and risk factors remain underexplored, particularly in low-resource settings like Zanzibar. This study assessed the prevalence of GAD and its associations with pharmacological, clinical, and lifestyle factors among T2DM patients in Zanzibar using the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score (COMPASS 31) questionnaire. Methods A cross-sectional study was conducted among 364 T2DM patients attending clinics in Zanzibar. Participants were recruited from local healthcare facilities, and data were collected through structured interviews. The gastrointestinal subdomain of the COMPASS 31 questionnaire quantified symptoms of gastrointestinal dysfunction. Descriptive and inferential analyses were performed to explore prevalence and associated factors. Results Among 364 participants, 140 (38.4%) had GAD. Longer diabetes duration was significantly associated with GAD, with those having diabetes for 7–9 years (adjusted OR: 2.19, p = 0.050) and > 10 years (adjusted OR: 2.04, p = 0.036) at higher risk. Use of oral hypoglycemic agents, more common in those with shorter disease duration, was linked to significantly lower odds of GAD (adjusted OR: 0.43, p = 0.007) compared to insulin alone. Additionally, consuming > 2 portions of fruit per day was associated with reduced risk (adjusted OR: 0.41, p = 0.019). Other factors, including gender and BMI, showed trends but were not statistically significant. Conclusion GAD is common among T2DM patients in Zanzibar, with longer diabetes duration increasing risk, while oral hypoglycemic use and higher fruit intake are linked to lower risk. The protective association of oral hypoglycemics may reflect earlier disease stages, reinforcing the importance of early diagnosis and intervention. Findings highlight the need for integrated lifestyle and pharmacological approaches to mitigate autonomic complications. Future research, including longitudinal studies and clinical trials, should explore causal relationships, underlying mechanisms, and the impact of dietary modifications and pharmacological interventions on GAD. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, leading to various long-term complications affecting multiple organ systems1–4. Among these complications, autonomic neuropathy is particularly concerning due to its impact on the gastrointestinal (GI) system, a condition known as gastrointestinal autonomic neuropathy (GAN)5,6. GAD may results in damage to the autonomic nerves consequently leading to impairments in the normal motility and function of the GI tract7. More than 75% of people living with diabetes are thought to experience gastrointestinal problems linked to diabetes-related neuropathy8–10. GAD affects the entire GI system and its complications significantly diminish quality of life11–14. The insidious nature of GAD symptoms often leads to underdiagnosis, particularly in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients, where chronic hyperglycemia accelerates autonomic nerve damage15.

In the esophagus, disrupted motility often leads to gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and esophageal dysmotility, causing symptoms such as heartburn, regurgitation, and dysphagia19. Delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) affects up to 58% of individuals with diabetes, causes symptoms like nausea, vomiting, bloating, and early satiety and can lead to nutritional deficiencies11,16,20. In the small intestine, motility issues range from abdominal discomfort to severe malabsorption, with hyperglycemia impairing mucosal healing. In the colon, slowed transit leads to constipation in up to 20% of individuals with diabetes16, while rapid transit can cause diarrhea as reported in up to 60% of diabetics17–19. Impaired sphincter control can cause both fecal incontinence and severe constipation, leading to poorer quality of life. The resulting dehydration, poor nutritional status, and unpredictable drug absorption complicates the management of diabetes making glycemic control difficult.

The risk of autonomic nerve damage increases with the duration of diabetes, predominantly affecting individuals with long-standing diabetes1,8,20,21. While previous studies have reported the prevalence and other associated factors of gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction (GAD), there remains limited literature on associated interventional factors that could provide insights into the prevention and management of these complications.

On the other hand, the diagnosis of GAD involves more sophisticated autonomic testing methods such as gastric emptying study. However, symptom-based questionnaires such as the COMPASS 31 can help assess symptom severity and guide further testing, especially in low resource settings such as Zanzibar. The COMPASS 31 (Composite Autonomic Symptom Score 31) questionnaire used in the current study is a validated tool for assessing symptoms of autonomic dysfunction, including those related to the gastrointestinal system22.

Zanzibar is part of the United Republic of Tanzania whereby, the challenges of diabetes management are exacerbated by limited healthcare resources including low access to health care services and a high burden of infectious diseases23. These existing challenges highlight the need for study to explore the prevalence and determinants of GAD in Zanzibar. This study further explored the associations between GAD with lifestyle and pharmacological factors that can be used to inform prevention and control strategies in the country.

While GAD is recognized as a common complication of diabetes, there is limited research on its prevalence and risk factors in regions like Zanzibar. Furthermore, as Zanzibar faces a rising burden of diabetes23, studying the prevalence of GAD is crucial for informing healthcare priorities and resource allocation. The region’s unique genetic and environmental factors, such as dietary habits, may influence the development and severity of GAD, making it a valuable area for further exploration. Additionally, the diagnostic challenges of identifying GAD highlight the need for accessible and reliable diagnostic approaches that can be adapted to local healthcare constraints. This research therefore seeks to contribute to the global understanding of diabetic autonomic neuropathy in the context of Zanzibar and inform clinical practices in similar settings.

Methods

Study Design and Setting A cross-sectional study was conducted at Endocrinology Department in Mnazi Mmoja Hospital (MMH), a major referral hospital in Zanzibar. MMH primarily serves the Unguja Urban and West Region, receiving about 81 inpatients and 223 outpatients every day. Selected health facilities which are part of MMH were also used for patients recruitment.

Participants and recruitment

A systematic random sampling approach was utilized to enroll 364 T2DM patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Table 1) attending the Endocrinology Department in MMH from December 20, 2023, to March 20, 2024.

Data collection tool and procedure

A standardized questionnaire (Attachment 1) was used for data collection. To enhance accessibility and ensure comprehension, the questionnaire was translated into Swahili, the predominant regional language in the study area. A team comprising nurses, a clinical officer, and a physician were recruited and trained as research assistants to facilitate data collection in the selected hospital.

For patients who provided consent, self-reported diabetes treatment history was cross verified with clinic records to ensure accuracy and minimize recall bias. The data collection process was closely supervised by the principal investigator (HT) and other co-investigators (AE, AGK, RMA, and FM) to maintain data integrity and adherence to study protocols.

Anthropometric and clinical measurements

Height, weight, hip, and waist circumferences were recorded from each patient. Each participant’s body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing their weight (in kilograms) by the square of their height (in meters). According to World Health Organization (WHO) standards, BMI classifications were as follows: a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m² was considered underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m² was classified as normal weight, 25–29.9 kg/m² was categorized as overweight, and a BMI of 30 kg/m² or higher was classified as obesity24. Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference of at least 88 cm in women and 102 cm in men. Blood pressure (mmHg) was measured while the patient was seated comfortably using an automated upper arm sphygmomanometer (CH-456, CITIZEN SYSTEMS JAPAN CO., LTD). Arterial hypertension was identified if the participant’s blood pressure exceeded 140/90 mmHg or if they were taking medication for hypertension24,25. The survey data was analyzed using the Kobo questionnaire tool to determine the metabolic equivalent task (MET) for each level of physical activity26.

Biochemical measurements

The measurements were conducted following the standard operating procedure. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured using an automated high-performance liquid chromatography analyzer (Cobas Integra 400, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), in accordance with the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) guidelines at Lancet Laboratories. Random blood glucose levels were also monitored to ensure no instances of hypoglycemia occurred before or during the tests. Additional biochemical parameters measured included, glucose fasting category, S-HDL cholesterol, and S-LDL cholesterol levels.

Measurement of gastrointestinal autonomic function

The measurement of gastrointestinal autonomic function in this study was conducted using the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score 31 (COMPASS 31) questionnaire (Attachment 2), which is a validated tool designed to assess autonomic dysfunction across multiple domains27. Specifically, for GIT dysfunction, COMPASS 31 focuses on symptoms related to gastrointestinal autonomic function, including bloating, vomiting, early satiety, abdominal discomfort, constipation and diarrhoea22. These symptoms are indicative of underlying autonomic nervous system involvement in regulating the digestive system. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by using trained healthcare professionals administered the COMPASS 31 questionnaire. To identify the GAD, each symptom is first scored based on its severity and frequency. Then the total score contributes to the overall GIT subdomain score within COMPASS 31.

Scoring of gastrointestinal autonomic function by using COMPASS 31

After interviews with T2DM patients, the GIT scores were determined and summed up for scoring. The total GIT score was calculated by summing individual symptom scores, and participants were categorized as either having normal GIT function or GIT dysfunction, with a threshold used to differentiate between the two. The higher the score, the greater the degree of autonomic dysfunction. This method provided a standardized approach to assess and categorize gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction in diabetic patients. This approach was previously used by other researchers including Sletten et al.22.

The cumulative score for bloating, vomiting, early satiety, abdominal discomfort, constipation and diarrhea were scored as 0 (Never), indicating the absence of GAD, while 1–3 (Mild), 4–6 (Moderate) and 7–12 (Severe) indicates its prevalence. In order to aid in diagnosis and treatment, this method helps determine the degree of autonomic dysfunction affecting the gastrointestinal system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Software for Data Science (STATA) version 13 and Microsoft Excel datasheet software was used record and analyse the data. Frequency and percentage were used to represent categorical variables. For categorical variables, the significance test was the Chi-square test. Mean and standard deviation were used to depict continuous variables with a normal distribution. We began by summarizing each participant’s characteristics. The prevalence of GAN was then determined by calculating the proportion of patients who satisfied the criteria for GAN. To investigate the determinants of GAN, univariate and multivariate binary (YES/NO) logistic regression was conducted.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in full accordance to the relevant national and international ethical guidelines and regulations including the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonization (ICH), Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and the principles of Good Clinical and Laboratory Practices (GCLP) where applicable. Ethical clearance was obtained from two institutional review boards: The Zanzibar Health Research Ethics Committee (ZAHREC), reference number ZAHREC/05/ST/DEC/2023/184 and the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Institution Review Board, reference number MUHAS-REC-02–2023−1528. Approval to conduct the study was also granted by relevant hospital authorities at Mnazi Mmoja Hospital (MMH). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. Participants’ confidentiality was ensured by assigning unique identification codes at the time of data collection and removing all personally identifying information from the research dataset.

Patterns of nutritional supplementation, fruit consumption, vegetable consumption and traditional herb usage among study participants

Nutritional supplement usage, fruit and vegetable consumption, and the use of traditional herbs provide further insight into the participants’ health habits. The majority of participants 83.0% did not use any nutritional supplements, while 17.0% reported supplement usage. Fruit and vegetable consumption patterns varied among participants. Nearly half (45.0%) of the participants reported rarely consuming fruits, while 25.0% consumed one portion per day, and 30.0% consumed two or more portions daily. Similarly, 42.0% of participants reported consuming at least two portions of vegetables per day, whereas 32.7% consumed one portion, and 25.3% rarely included vegetables in their diet. The use of traditional herbs varied among participants, with 50.0% reporting that they had never used them. Among those who had engaged in traditional herb use, 16.7% reported rare use, 4.6% used them occasionally, and 3.7% reported frequent use. Notably, one-quarter of participants (25.0%) had discontinued herb use, potentially reflecting a shift in health management practices, (Table 2).

Discussion

This study revealed a substantial burden of gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction (GAD) among patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) in Zanzibar, with 38.4% of participants diagnosed with those complications. Importantly, diabetes duration, the use of oral hypoglycemic agents, and fruit consumption were identified as significant factors associated with GAD. These findings provide critical insights into the potential role of early diabetes management and lifestyle interventions in mitigating autonomic dysfunction.

The study’s estimated 38.4% prevalence of GAD is consistent with global data. Previous studies have indicated that gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction affects approximatelly 20% to 50% of diabetic patients, with some estimates suggesting higher rates depending on the specific gastrointestinal symptoms and diagnostic tools used11. Diabetic gastroparesis characterised by delayed gastric emptying of solids or liquids is reported in in up to 50% of individuals with diabetes16,28–30. This delayed emptying also contributes to constipation. Similarly, constipation was the most frequently reported symptom, affecting a substantial proportion (88.6%) of our study participants, followed by diarrhea, vomiting, and bloating. Taken together, our findings, along with previous studies, suggest that GAD is a prevalent complication among individuals with diabetes, with highly variable clinical presentations.

These findings underscore the need for healthcare providers to consider gastrointestinal symptoms as part of the comprehensive management of diabetes, particularly in patients with the disease for a longer duration. Addressing these issues could enhance the quality of life and overall clinical outcomes for patients through managing both T2DM and gastrointestinal complications.

The observed positive association between diabetes duration and GAD is consistent with the established pathophysiology of autonomic dysfunction in diabetes15. The significantly higher odds of GAD among individuals with a diabetes duration of 7–9 years and ≥ 10 years suggest a progressive deterioration of autonomic function over time17,31, sometimes in the absence of obvious symptoms. Chronic hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation have been implicated in the gradual degeneration of autonomic nerve fibers, leading to functional impairment of the gastrointestinal autonomic system15.

While these findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating an increased risk of autonomic neuropathy with longer diabetes duration, some studies reported no significant association between diabetes duration and autonomic dysfunction32. These discrepancies indicate that although prolonged diabetes is frequently linked to GAD, the clinical presentation of symptoms remains highly variable1,8,20,21. One possible explanation is that some patients with advanced neuropathy may not report gastrointestinal symptoms, either due to physiological adaptation, underreporting, or differences in symptom perception1,8,20,21. This variation in symptom manifestation could contribute to inconsistent findings across studies. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of early detection and proactive intervention to delay or prevent the onset of GAD, particularly in individuals at higher risk due to longer disease duration.

A key finding of this study is the inverse association between oral hypoglycemic agent (OHA) use and gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction (GAD), with patients using OHAs exhibiting significantly lower odds of GAD (adjusted OR: 0.43, p = 0.007) compared to those on insulin alone. One plausible explanation is that patients on OHA therapy are at an earlier stage of diabetes, as metformin and other oral agents are typically first-line treatments before transitioning to insulin in cases of disease progression33. Consequently, this finding may reflect a lower cumulative burden of hyperglycemia and metabolic dysfunction in this group, potentially reducing the risk of autonomic complications15.

This observation aligns with findings from the United Kingdom Perspective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), which demonstrated that early and intensive glycemic control (“Hit Early, Hit Hard”) leads to long-term benefits in reducing microvascular complications, including neuropathy and autonomic dysfunction34. The legacy seen in UKPDS suggests that individuals maintaining better glycemic control in the early stages of diabetes continue to experience protective effects even as the disease progresses.

Indeed, in our study, OHA use was most prevalent among those with a diabetes duration of 1 to 3 years, whereas its usage declined progressively in individuals who had the disease for 7 years or more. Furthermore, we observed a consistent downward trend in OHA use as disease duration advanced, accompanied by a parallel increase in insulin and combination therapy.

Beyond its role in early disease management, metformin the most widely prescribed oral hypoglycemic agent has been shown to exert protective effects on autonomic function35. Mechanistically, metformin reduces insulin resistance, improves endothelial function, and modulates inflammatory pathways, which may contribute to its potential neuroprotective effects35,36. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that metformin may promote mitochondrial function and enhance neuronal survival, thereby attenuating autonomic nerve damage37. Despite all these established positive effects of metformin in neural biology, some authors have reported its negative effects on the gastrointestinal system14,32,38. While our study design does not establish causality, these findings reinforce the well-established fact that optimizing pharmacological management in the early stages of diabetes is crucial for reducing long-term complications.

The observed protective association between fruit intake and GAD further highlights the role of lifestyle factors in autonomic dysfunction prevention. Participants who consumed more than two portions of fruits per day had significantly lower odds of GAD (adjusted OR: 0.41, p = 0.019) than those consuming less. Fruits are rich in antioxidants, fiber, and polyphenols, all of which contribute to glycemic control, vascular health, and anti-inflammatory effects, mechanisms that may help mitigate the development of autonomic dysfunction39.

Interestingly, vegetable consumption was not associated with GAD in this cohort, despite being more prevalent than fruit consumption. A greater proportion of participants consumed more than two portions of vegetables daily compared to those consuming similar amounts of fruits, suggesting that vegetable intake was not a limiting factor in this population. While vegetables provide essential nutrients and fiber, the absence of a clear association with GAD may indicate that the protective compounds found in fruits, such as polyphenols and certain micronutrients, play a more significant role in autonomic function40. These findings reinforce the importance of promoting balanced dietary habits, including adequate fruit consumption, in diabetes management.

Collectively, our findings underscore the importance of early screening, diagnosis, and intervention in preventing GAD among T2DM patients. The association between longer diabetes duration and increased GAD risk highlights the progressive nature of autonomic dysfunction, emphasizing the need for routine autonomic screening in clinical settings, particularly for patients with a longer disease duration. Moreover, the lower odds of GAD among those on oral hypoglycemic agents suggest that early pharmacological intervention, particularly with metformin, may play a protective role. The protective association observed with fruit intake further supports the integration of lifestyle modifications alongside pharmacological therapy to reduce autonomic complications.

Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, future longitudinal studies and clinical trials are warranted to establish causal relationships and explore the mechanistic pathways underlying these associations. Further research should assess whether early pharmacological intervention and dietary modifications can slow or reverse autonomic dysfunction in diabetes, providing stronger evidence for targeted prevention strategies. Additionally, the markedly skewed sex distribution, with a higher proportion of female participants, may have influenced the observed sex differences in prevalence and limits the generalizability and interpretability of these findings. While statistical adjustment for sex helped to mitigate this limitation, future studies should consider achieving a more balanced sex representation or report sex-specific outcomes to reduce confounding and enhance the robustness of sex-based comparisons.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high prevalence of gastrointestinal autonomic dysfunction (GAD) among T2DM patients in Zanzibar and its association with diabetes duration and modifiable lifestyle factors. The findings emphasize the importance of early screening and timely pharmacological and lifestyle interventions to mitigate autonomic complications. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, future longitudinal research including clinical trials are warranted to clarify causal relationships and explore intervention strategies for reducing the burden of GAD in diabetic populations, particularly in low-resource settings.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study received ethical approval from the Ministry of Health, the Zanzibar Health Research Institute Ethics Committee (ZAHREC) (Approval No. ZAHREC/05/ST/DEC/2023/184), and the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Institutional Review Board (MUHAS-IRB) (Approval No. MUHAS-REC-02-2023-1528). Permission was also sought from the Department of Internal Medicine, which informed and engaged healthcare professionals at the Mnazi Mmoja Hospital (MMH) diabetes clinic regarding the study procedures. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment and were clearly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. To ensure confidentiality and participant anonymity, unique identification codes (e.g., P1, P2, P3) were assigned instead of personal identifiers. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.
