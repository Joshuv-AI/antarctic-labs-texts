Global Burden of Secondhand Smoke–Attributable ER-Negative Breast Cancer and Exploratory Immune–Metabolic Mediation Using Mendelian Randomization
Authors: Defu Yang, Feng Shang, Xinying Ma, Haibo Zhang, Xue Ren, Xianqing Luan, Jianjing Wang, Ying Li, Tong Wu, Dongyang Lv, Ying Yan, Jing Liu, Ying Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global burden of breast cancer attributable to secondhand smoke (SHS) and its potential biological pathways, particularly for estrogen receptor (ER)-negative disease, remain incompletely characterized. This study aimed to evaluate temporal trends in SHS-attributable breast cancer burden and to explore whether immune and metabolic traits may represent candidate pathways linking SHS exposure with ER-negative breast cancer.MethodsWe analyzed the Global Burden of Disease 1990–2021 data to estimate temporal trends in SHS-attributable breast cancer burden using Joinpoint regression, Bayesian age–period–cohort modeling, decomposition analysis, and autoregressive integrated moving average forecasting to 2050. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was performed using 36–38 single-nucleotide polymorphism instruments for exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home. Exploratory two-step MR mediation analyses screened 637 serum metabolites and 731 immune phenotypes using the product-of-coefficients approach.ResultsGlobally, absolute SHS-attributable breast cancer deaths increased from 4,949 in 1990 to 7,518 in 2021, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) increased from 1.74×105 to 2.57×105. In contrast, age-standardized death and DALY rates declined, with estimated annual percentage changes of −1.31% and −1.22% per year, respectively. Marked disparities were observed across Socio-Demographic Index regions. Projection analyses suggested a plateau around 2030, followed by widening uncertainty. Decomposition analysis indicated that population growth and population aging were major contributors to the increasing absolute burden. In MR analysis, genetically proxied SHS exposure outside the home showed a nominal positive association with ER-negative breast cancer in the inverse variance-weighted model (β = 0.9976; odds ratio = 2.71; 95% confidence interval = 1.08–6.82), although alternative MR estimators showed wider and less consistent estimates. Exploratory two-step MR identified lipid- and energy-related metabolites, including 10-nonadecenoate, eicosenoate, 3-hydroxybutyrate, and dihomo-linoleate 20:2n6, as candidate metabolic signals. A myeloid antigen-presentation phenotype, HLA-DR on CD33dim HLA-DR+ CD11b− cells, was also prioritized as a potential immune-related signal. However, the indirect-effect estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence of mediation.ConclusionThe age-standardized burden of SHS-attributable breast cancer has declined globally, whereas absolute deaths and DALYs have increased due to demographic growth and aging, with persistent SDI-linked disparities. MR analyses provided suggestive genetic-instrument evidence linking SHS exposure outside the home with ER-negative breast cancer, and exploratory mediation analyses prioritized lipid metabolism, ketone-related energy pathways, and myeloid immune phenotypes for future validation. These findings support continued smoke-free policies and highlight the need for larger, diverse, and mechanistically informed studies to validate the potential immune–metabolic pathways implicated in SHS-related ER-negative breast cancer.

Introduction

Secondhand smoke (SHS), comprising sidestream smoke from smoldering tobacco products and exhaled mainstream smoke, is an established human carcinogen and a major preventable cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 Although the causal relationship between SHS exposure and lung cancer is well established,2 the association between SHS and breast cancer has remained less consistent across epidemiological studies, with effect estimates varying according to exposure timing, intensity, duration, measurement method, and tumor subtype. Recent syntheses among never-smoking women have reported positive associations between SHS exposure and breast cancer risk, including dose–response patterns when exposure intensity and duration were more precisely characterized.3 At the population level, contemporary global burden assessments further indicate that SHS remains a persistent risk factor associated with substantial health losses in many regions, despite improvements in age-standardized rates in some higher-income settings.4

The biological plausibility of an SHS–breast cancer relationship is supported by experimental and molecular epidemiological evidence. Tobacco smoke contains genotoxic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, aromatic amines, nitrosamines, and other carcinogenic compounds that can induce DNA adducts, oxidative stress, genomic instability, and inflammatory responses in breast tissue.5 In addition to direct genotoxicity, tobacco exposure may perturb endocrine signaling, immune surveillance, systemic inflammation, and metabolic homeostasis. Multi-omic population studies have documented broad molecular signatures of tobacco exposure, including transcriptomic reprogramming of blood immune pathways and reproducible alterations in lipid, amino acid, and xenobiotic metabolite classes.6,7 These biological axes are relevant to breast tumor initiation, progression, and subtype heterogeneity.

A clearer focus on estrogen receptor (ER)-negative breast cancer is clinically and biologically important. ER-negative tumors are generally more aggressive, lack endocrine therapeutic targets, and are associated with fewer prevention and treatment options than ER-positive disease.8 From a mechanistic perspective, SHS-related oxidative stress, DNA damage, inflammation, immune dysregulation, and metabolic remodeling may be more closely aligned with ER-negative tumor biology than with hormone receptor-driven ER-positive carcinogenesis. Therefore, although SHS may contribute to breast cancer through multiple pathways, a stronger or more detectable association with ER-negative disease is biologically plausible and warrants subtype-specific evaluation.

SHS exposure is commonly defined as involuntary exposure to tobacco smoke generated by other people’s smoking. In epidemiological studies, it is often assessed by self-reported exposure at home, in workplaces, or in public spaces, and in some studies by biomarkers such as cotinine. At the population level, SHS exposure remains widespread and unequally distributed by age, sex, socioeconomic position, household environment, and public-space or workplace exposure.9,10 These inequalities may sustain preventable cancer burden, particularly in settings where demographic growth and population aging offset improvements in age-specific disease rates. However, two important knowledge gaps remain. First, the global and regional burden of breast cancer attributable to SHS has not been comprehensively characterized across the post-1990 epidemiological transition using standardized comparative risk assessment. Second, although immune and metabolic dysregulation are biologically credible systems linking tobacco exposure to breast carcinogenesis, their roles in SHS-related breast cancer risk remain insufficiently defined at the population and genetic-epidemiological levels.

Mendelian randomization (MR) provides a complementary approach for evaluating potential relationships between environmental exposures and disease outcomes by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, thereby reducing some forms of confounding and reverse causation that affect conventional observational studies.11 Nevertheless, MR of SHS exposure requires careful interpretation because genetic instruments may capture susceptibility to environmental or behavioral exposure patterns rather than directly measuring SHS dose, duration, timing, or exposure setting. In this study, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) SHS-attributable burden and the UK Biobank genome-wide association study phenotype “exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home” were treated as related but not identical exposure constructs. The GBD estimates reflect modeled population-level attribution based on comparative risk assessment, whereas the UK Biobank phenotype reflects self-reported outside-home tobacco smoke exposure. Their integration was therefore interpreted as complementary rather than interchangeable evidence.

To address these gaps, this study integrated GBD 2021 estimates from 1990 to 2021 with two-sample MR and exploratory immune–metabolic mediation screening. We first characterized temporal, geographical, and development-level patterns in SHS-attributable breast cancer deaths, disability-adjusted life years, years of life lost, years lived with disability, and age-standardized rates across Socio-Demographic Index strata and 21 GBD regions. We then evaluated the association between genetically proxied exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home and breast cancer outcomes, with particular attention to ER-negative disease. Finally, we screened 731 immune phenotypes and 637 circulating metabolites as candidate immune–metabolic signals that may help generate hypotheses for future mechanistic and translational studies. By combining standardized population burden estimation with genetic-epidemiological analysis and high-dimensional omics screening, this study aims to clarify global SHS-attributable breast cancer patterns and prioritize candidate pathways for further validation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Reporting Framework

This study used an integrated analytical framework combining population-level comparative risk assessment, temporal trend analysis, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), and exploratory immune–metabolic mediation screening to evaluate the global burden of secondhand smoke (SHS)-attributable breast cancer and to prioritize candidate biological signals related to estrogen receptor (ER)-negative disease. The study consisted of three analytical layers (Figure 1). First, Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 estimates from 1990 to 2021 were used to characterize temporal and geographical patterns in SHS-attributable breast cancer deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and age-standardized rates. Second, two-sample MR was performed to evaluate the association between genetically proxied exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home and breast cancer outcomes, with particular attention to ER-negative breast cancer. Third, exploratory two-step MR mediation analyses were conducted to screen immune phenotypes and circulating metabolites as candidate immune–metabolic signals requiring further validation. The GBD-based health estimates were reported in accordance with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER), and the MR component was reported following the STROBE-MR statement and its explanation and elaboration document.12–14 Because this study was not a surgical cohort study, STROCSS was not used as a reporting framework.Figure 1Study design. GBD Data Source and Burden Measures

SHS-attributable breast cancer burden estimates were obtained from the GBD 2021 database through the Global Health Data Exchange/GBD Results Tool.15 The GBD 2021 data were extracted on May 31, 2025. The cause was defined as breast cancer, and the risk factor was defined as SHS exposure according to the GBD comparative risk assessment framework.15 The extracted measures included deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs. Both absolute counts and age-standardized rates were obtained for calendar years 1990–2021. Estimates were extracted at the global level, by Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) quintile, across 21 GBD regions, and across countries and territories where available.16,17

The main comparative burden analysis retained the GBD both-sex category to maintain consistency with the extracted risk-attributable comparative burden outputs and to facilitate comparison across locations and time. Because breast cancer burden and SHS exposure may differ by sex, female-specific estimates were additionally examined as sensitivity analyses where GBD outputs were available. Age-specific estimates were extracted using 5-year age groups, and age-standardized rates were calculated using the GBD standard population.16,17 SDI-stratified analyses were conducted to describe burden patterns across development levels.18 All GBD estimates were reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 posterior draws.15

In the GBD framework, SHS-attributable burden is estimated using a comparative risk assessment approach.19,20 Population-attributable fractions are derived from exposure distributions, theoretical minimum risk exposure levels, and epidemiologically synthesized relative risks, with uncertainty propagated through the modeling process.21 Therefore, the GBD SHS attribution represents modeled population-level burden rather than an individual-level exposure measurement. DALYs, YLLs, YLDs, and age-standardized rates were interpreted according to standard GBD disease-burden definitions.22

Age-Standardized Rates and Estimated Annual Percentage Change

Age-standardized rates were calculated using the direct standardization method according to the GBD standard population.16

ASR_y = [Σ_a r_a,y × w_a/Σ_a w_a] × 100,000

where ASR_y represents the age-standardized rate in year y, r_a,y represents the age-specific rate in age group a and year y, and w_a represents the corresponding age-specific weight from the GBD standard population. Age-standardized death rates and age-standardized DALY rates were expressed per 100,000 population.

Temporal trends in age-standardized rates from 1990 to 2021 were quantified using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC).23 A log-linear regression model was fitted as follows:

ln(ASR_y) = α + βy + ε_y

where ASR_y represents the age-standardized rate in year y, α is the intercept, β is the annual change in the natural logarithm of the ASR, and ε_y is the random error term. The EAPC was calculated as:

EAPC = 100 × [exp(β) − 1]

The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the EAPC was calculated as:

95% CI = 100 × [exp(β ± 1.96 × SE) − 1]

A trend was considered increasing if the lower bound of the 95% CI was greater than 0, decreasing if the upper bound was less than 0, and stable otherwise.

Joinpoint regression was used to identify changes in temporal trends and to estimate annual percentage changes across time segments.23 Model selection was based on permutation tests and goodness-of-fit criteria according to the Joinpoint Regression Program.

Decomposition Analysis

To quantify the drivers of changes in absolute SHS-attributable breast cancer burden between 1990 and 2021, decomposition analysis was performed to partition changes in deaths and DALYs into contributions from population growth, population aging, and changes in age-specific rates.24,25 The total burden in a given year was expressed as:

B_t = Σ_a P_t × S_a,t × R_a,t

where B_t represents the total burden in year t, P_t represents the total population size, S_a,t represents the proportion of the population in age group a, and R_a,t represents the age-specific burden rate in age group a. The total change in burden between 1990 and 2021 was decomposed as:

ΔB = B_2021 − B_1990 = ΔB_population + ΔB_aging + ΔB_rate

where ΔB_population represents the contribution of population growth, ΔB_aging represents the contribution of changes in age structure, and ΔB_rate represents the contribution of changes in age-specific rates. This approach allowed us to distinguish whether changes in absolute burden were primarily driven by demographic expansion, population aging, or epidemiological change.

Forecasting Analysis

Future trends in SHS-attributable breast cancer burden were projected to 2050 using Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) models and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models.26 BAPC models were used to account for age, period, and cohort effects within a Bayesian framework and to project age-specific and age-standardized burden. ARIMA models were used as a complementary time-series forecasting approach based on historical temporal patterns. Because BAPC and ARIMA models rely on different assumptions and input structures, their outputs were interpreted as complementary projections rather than interchangeable estimates. Widening uncertainty intervals in later projection years were interpreted as reflecting increasing uncertainty in long-term forecasts.

GWAS Data Sources for Exposure, Outcomes, and Mediators

Genetic instruments for SHS-related exposure were obtained from the UK Biobank genome-wide association study (GWAS) phenotype “exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home” through the IEU OpenGWAS database.27,28 This phenotype was considered related to, but not identical with, the GBD-modeled SHS attribution. The GBD estimates reflect modeled population-attributable burden based on comparative risk assessment, whereas the UK Biobank phenotype reflects self-reported exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home.15,27 This distinction was considered when interpreting the integrated GBD and MR findings. Breast cancer outcome GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC), FinnGen, and IEU OpenGWAS.28–32 Outcomes included overall breast cancer, ER-positive breast cancer, and ER-negative breast cancer. ER-negative breast cancer was considered the main subtype of interest because of its clinical aggressiveness, lack of endocrine therapeutic targets, and potential biological relevance to inflammation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and metabolic remodeling.8,29 All GWAS datasets were based predominantly on individuals of European ancestry. Mediator datasets included 637 circulating metabolites and 731 immune phenotypes. Metabolite GWAS data were obtained from harmonized Nightingale NMR and Metabolon LC/GC–MS resources available through IEU OpenGWAS and related repositories.33–35 These metabolites covered lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acid traits, glycolysis-related metabolites, ketone-body intermediates, amino acids, bile acids, acylcarnitines, xenobiotics, and other circulating metabolic features. Immune phenotype GWAS data were obtained from large flow cytometry-based GWAS meta-analyses and blood-cell trait GWAS resources and included leukocyte subset abundance, cell-surface markers, activation markers, adhesion markers, and antigen-presentation phenotypes across lymphoid and myeloid cell populations.36–38 Potential sample overlap among UK Biobank, BCAC, FinnGen, metabolite GWAS, and immune phenotype GWAS sources was considered when interpreting MR estimates. Such overlap may bias two-sample MR estimates toward observational associations, particularly when genetic instruments are weak.39,40

Genetic Instrument Selection and Harmonization

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with SHS exposure outside the home were selected as instrumental variables using a suggestive-significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−5. This relaxed threshold was used because genome-wide significant variants for SHS-related exposure were limited, allowing construction of a sufficient instrument set for exploratory two-sample MR analyses. Linkage disequilibrium clumping was performed using r2 < 0.001 within a 10-Mb window to ensure instrument independence.39,41 Instrument strength was assessed using the F-statistic, and SNPs with F-statistics ≤10 were considered potentially weak instruments.39,40 Where necessary, sensitivity analyses using alternative instrument thresholds were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Exposure and outcome datasets were harmonized by aligning effect alleles across GWAS summary statistics.41 Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous allele frequencies were removed when strand alignment could not be reliably determined. SNPs with missing outcome information were excluded or replaced by suitable proxy variants where available and appropriate. A comprehensive supplementary instrument table was prepared, including SNP identifier, chromosome, position, effect allele, other allele, effect allele frequency where available, beta coefficient, standard error, P value, F-statistic, variance explained, harmonization status, and exclusion reason where applicable.

Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization Analysis

Two-sample MR was performed to evaluate the association between genetically proxied SHS exposure outside the home and breast cancer outcomes.11,39 The inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary MR estimator under the assumption that all genetic instruments were valid or that horizontal pleiotropy was balanced. MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode estimators were used as complementary sensitivity methods where the number of instruments was sufficient.39,41 Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO where applicable.42 Heterogeneity among SNP-specific estimates was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic. Leave-one-out analysis was performed to determine whether the overall MR estimate was driven by any single SNP. Directionality was assessed using Steiger filtering where applicable. MR estimates were interpreted according to effect direction, precision, consistency across sensitivity methods, heterogeneity, and pleiotropy tests.39,41,42

Because SHS is an environmental exposure, the MR assumptions require careful interpretation.11,39 The relevance assumption requires that genetic instruments are associated with SHS-related exposure. The independence assumption requires that the instruments are not associated with confounders of the SHS–breast cancer relationship. The exclusion restriction assumption requires that the instruments influence breast cancer only through SHS exposure. Genetic instruments for exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home may reflect behavioral, social, or environmental susceptibility to exposure and may not fully represent real-world SHS dose, duration, timing, source, or setting. Therefore, MR results were interpreted as suggestive genetic-instrument evidence rather than definitive proof of causality.

Statistical power for MR analyses was estimated using the method proposed by Brion et al, with particular attention to subtype-specific outcomes such as ER-negative breast cancer.40 Power estimates were reported in the Supplementary Materials together with the proportion of exposure variance explained by the instruments and the detectable odds ratio under prespecified power thresholds.

Exploratory Two-Step MR Mediation Analysis

Exploratory two-step MR mediation analysis was performed to screen candidate immune and metabolic traits that may lie along the pathway between SHS-related exposure and ER-negative breast cancer.43 This analysis was considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

In the first step, MR was used to estimate the association between genetically proxied SHS exposure outside the home and each candidate mediator. In the second step, MR was used to estimate the association between each mediator and ER-negative breast cancer. The indirect effect was estimated using the product-of-coefficients method, a commonly used approach in mediation analysis.43,44

β_indirect = β_SHS→M × β_M→BC

where β_SHS→M represents the estimated effect of SHS exposure on the mediator, and β_M→BC represents the estimated effect of the mediator on ER-negative breast cancer. The mediated proportion was calculated as:

Mediated proportion = β_indirect/β_total × 100%

where β_total represents the total MR estimate of SHS exposure on ER-negative breast cancer. The standard error of the indirect effect was estimated using the delta method where applicable.44

Given the large number of tested mediators, including 637 metabolites and 731 immune phenotypes, this high-dimensional immune–metabolic screen was considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Nominal P values were reported to prioritize candidate signals for further validation. Because circulating metabolites and immune phenotypes are biologically correlated, nominally associated mediators were not interpreted as statistically confirmed independent pathways. Instead, they were considered candidate immune–metabolic signals requiring external validation. The analytical hierarchy was prespecified as follows: the GBD burden analysis was considered the primary analysis; the two-sample MR analysis was considered a secondary genetic-instrument analysis; and the high-dimensional immune–metabolic mediation screen was considered exploratory. Mediators identified only at nominal significance were interpreted as candidate signals requiring external validation, not as confirmed mediating pathways. Because circulating metabolites and immune phenotypes are biologically correlated, candidate mediators were not interpreted as independent pathways. Because multivariable MR requires sufficient independent instruments for each correlated mediator, multivariable MR was planned for prioritized lipid, ketone-body, and immune candidates where instrument availability permitted; when multivariable MR was not feasible, candidate mediators were interpreted cautiously as correlated exploratory signals.45 Additional sensitivity analyses included pleiotropy-robust MR methods where applicable.42 The mediation findings were interpreted in light of MR assumptions, potential horizontal pleiotropy, sample overlap, phenotype heterogeneity, mediator correlation, and uncertainty from multiple testing.43–45

Statistical Software and Reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, and MRPRESSO packages.41,42 Forecasting analyses were performed using relevant R packages, including BAPC, INLA, and forecast, where applicable.26 Figures were generated using ggplot2 and related visualization packages. Joinpoint regression was conducted using Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.4.0 (Statistical Methodology and Applications Branch, Surveillance Research Program, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA). Processed datasets, statistical scripts, and Supplementary Tables are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Global Breast Cancer Burden Attributable to SHS

From 1990 to 2021, the global number of breast cancer deaths attributable to SHS exposure increased from 4,948.9 (95% UI = −1,226.7 to 11,029.3) to 7,518.1 (95% UI = −1,811.4 to 16,932.3), whereas the corresponding ASDR declined from 0.12 per 100,000 population to 0.09 per 100,000 population, reflecting an EAPC of −1.31% (95% CI = −1.39 to −1.22). Similarly, global DALYs rose from 173,837.6 to 256,510.3, yet the age-standardized DALY rate decreased from 4.01 per 100,000 to 2.96 per 100,000 (EAPC = −1.22%, 95% CI = −1.30 to −1.14), indicating improved age-adjusted outcomes despite the increasing absolute burden. YLLs, as the dominant contributor to DALYs, followed the same declining age-standardized trend (from 3.81 to 2.76, EAPC = −1.29%, 95% CI = −1.37 to −1.20). Conversely, YLDs displayed a much more stable age-standardized rate (0.19 in 1990 vs 0.20 in 2021), with only a marginal decline over time (EAPC = −0.09%, 95% CI = −0.14 to −0.03), suggesting a relatively static burden from morbidity (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 1). Global Breast Cancer Burden Attributable to SHS by SDI Region

Across the five SDI quintiles, the breast cancer burden attributable to SHS demonstrated substantial heterogeneity between 1990 and 2021 (Supplementary Table 1). High SDI regions experienced the most notable improvements, with ASDR declining from 0.12 per 100,000 population to 0.05 per 100,000 population, corresponding to an EAPC of −2.97% (95% CI = −3.05 to −2.89). Similarly, the age-standardized DALY rate dropped over time from 4.10 to 1.77 (EAPC = −2.90%), largely driven by a marked reduction in YLLs (EAPC = −3.06%), reflecting significant gains in early detection, treatment, and overall cancer care. The reduction in YLDs was less pronounced (EAPC = −1.45%), suggesting some persistence of morbidity despite mortality improvements. By contrast, low and low–middle SDI regions exhibited increasing or stable burdens. Notably, low–middle SDI regions displayed a modest rise in ASDR (from 0.09 to 0.10, EAPC = 0.31%) and the DALY rate (from 2.88 to 3.22, EAPC = 0.28%), with a more substantial increase in YLDs (EAPC = 1.31%), indicating a shift toward longer survivorship accompanied by a greater disability burden. Similarly, low SDI regions had relatively unchanged ASDRs (0.07 to 0.07, EAPC = −0.15%) and DALY rates (2.23 to 2.23, EAPC = −0.23%), but YLDs increased significantly (EAPC = 0.41%), suggesting limited access to curative treatment and rising survivorship with disability. Middle SDI regions displayed mixed trends, with declining ASDRs (EAPC = −0.97%) and DALYs (EAPC = −0.86%) but a mild rise in YLDs (EAPC = 1.08%), indicating moderate progress in mortality reduction alongside a growing disability-associated burden. Meanwhile, high–middle SDI regions experienced notable reductions in ASDR (from 0.16 to 0.10, EAPC = −1.70%) and DALYs (EAPC = −1.62%), alongside a relatively smaller increase in YLDs (EAPC = +0.41%). From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of breast cancer attributable to SHS exposure displayed divergent trends across SDI levels. ASDR and YLLs declined significantly in high and high–middle SDI regions, reflecting progress in tobacco control, early detection, and treatment. Conversely, low and low–middle SDI regions exhibited relatively stable ASDRs and YLLs, indicating ongoing challenges in reducing premature mortality. Although DALYs also declined in higher SDI regions, they remained largely unchanged or slightly increased in lower SDI groups. Notably, YLDs increased steadily across all SDI levels, particularly in low-resource settings, suggesting improved survivorship but rising disability burdens.

Geographic Disparities in the Breast Cancer Burden Attributable to SHS Exposure

Marked regional disparities were observed in the global breast cancer burden attributable to SHS between 1990 and 2021 (Supplementary Table 1). High-income regions, including Western Europe, North America, and Australasia, exhibited the most significant declines in ASDRs, with EAPCs of −3.44%, −3.60%, and −4.08%, respectively. These decreases were mirrored by substantial reductions in the age-standardized DALY rate (eg, −3.38% in Western Europe and −3.93% in Australasia), highlighting the impact of sustained tobacco control policies, early screening programs, and advanced oncologic care infrastructure in these settings. The reductions in YLLs in these regions further support improvements in cancer survival, whereas the relatively smaller declines in YLDs suggest ongoing needs for chronic care in long-term survivors. Conversely, several low- and middle-income regions displayed either stagnation or increases in burden. For instance, in North Africa and the Middle East, ASDR rose from 0.06 per 100,000 to 0.09 per 100,000, with a positive EAPC of 1.61%, whereas DALYs increased sharply (EAPC = 1.44%), driven in part by a steep rise in YLDs (EAPC = 3.36%), reflecting improved survival accompanied by persistent disability. Similarly, Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (particularly Southern and Western subregions) experienced increasing or stable ASDRs and DALYs, with positive EAPCs for both YLDs and YLLs, indicating an expanding disease burden that is both fatal and disabling. Global Age-Specific Burden Attributable to SHS in 1990–2021: Regional Heterogeneity in Deaths, YLLs, YLDs, and DALYs

Across the globe and within all GBD regions, the age composition of the SHS-attributable breast cancer burden in various study years (1990, 2000, 2010, and 2021) was dominated by older women, with the ≥70- and 65–69-year-old groups contributing the largest shares to deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs. From 1990 to 2021, nearly every region displayed progressive aging of the burden (Figure 5). The proportional contribution of women aged ≥70 years rose most clearly for deaths and YLLs, indicating that SHS-related fatal outcomes are increasingly concentrated at advanced ages. Conversely, middle-aged women (50–64 years) accounted for comparatively larger proportions of YLDs and DALYs, consistent with prolonged survival accompanied by treatment- and disease-related functional loss. Marked regional heterogeneity was observed. Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly the Central, Eastern, and Southern subregions, retained higher contributions from women aged 45–59 years than high-income regions, suggesting earlier onset and more premature mortality attributable to SHS. High-income regions, including North America, Western Europe, and Asia–Pacific, exhibited right-shifted profiles, with a larger share borne by older women, most pronounced for YLDs, likely reflecting longer survival with chronic morbidity. East and Central Asia exhibited a clear transition from middle-aged to older groups over time, underscoring the role of rapid population aging in reshaping the SHS-attributable breast cancer burden. Socio-Demographic Development and the SHS-Attributable Breast Cancer Burden in 1990–2021: Non-Linear Global Patterns and Regional SDI Trajectories

Across 1990–2021, the relationship between sociodemographic development and the SHS-attributable breast cancer burden exhibited clear, outcome-specific patterns (Figures 6 and 7). For deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, regional trajectories aligned with a pronounced inverted U-shape: the burden rose from low SDI and peaked at middle SDI (0.65–0.70) before declining thereafter (Pearson r ≈ 0.63 for deaths, r ≈ 0.59 for DALYs, r ≈ 0.55 for YLLs, all P < 0.001), with Central/Eastern Europe and parts of Central Asia clustering near the peak and high-income North American regions, Western Europe, and high-income Asia–Pacific regions situated on the descending limb. By contrast, YLDs increased almost monotonically with SDI and plateaued only at the highest SDI (r ≈ 0.90, P < 0.001), indicating larger survivorship populations and sustained morbidity in regions where detection and treatment are strongest. Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia featured steep upward trajectories from low SDI but generally remained below the European middle-SDI maxima, consistent with rising detection alongside constrained treatment capacity. Collectively, these patterns suggest a development transition in the SHS-attributable breast cancer burden: as countries move through middle SDI, fatal outcomes intensify before receding, whereas non-fatal disability accumulates with advancing SDI, implicating the need for stage-matched policies that couple comprehensive smoke-free protections and early detection in mid-SDI settings with survivorship-focused care in high SDI regions.Figure 6Secondhand smoke-attributable breast cancer burden by Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), 1990–2021. Joinpoint Trends and Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) in the Global Burden of SHS-Attributable Breast Cancer

Using joinpoint regression, we quantified temporal changes in the absolute burden of breast cancer attributable to SHS (Figure 8). Globally, deaths increased from 4949 in 1990 to 7518 in 2021, yielding an AAPC of 1.36%/year. The trajectory comprised four phases: an early increase (131.5 deaths/year, 95% CI = 119.8–143.1), two slowdown/decline segments (−56.5 deaths/year, 95% CI = −71.1 to −1.9, and −23.5 deaths/year, 95% CI = −32.7 to −14.4), and a re-acceleration in the most recent period (75.8 deaths/year, 95% CI = 68.5–83.2). DALYs rose from 173,838 to 256,510, corresponding to an AAPC of 1.26%/year. Four phases were evident with breakpoints near 1994, 2001, and 2013: an initial rise (1545 DALYs/year), a steeper rise (2009 DALYs/year), a mid-period dip (−960 DALYs/year), and a renewed increase thereafter (2381 DALYs/year, CIs as annotated in the figure). YLDs approximately doubled (8.6 × 103 to 1.73×104), giving an AAPC of 2.2%/year. Joinpoint analysis indicated an early rapid increase (293 YLDs/year, 95% CI = 260–325), a slower late-1990s phase (85 YLDs/year, 95% CI includes 0), a steady rise in 2001–2013 (78 YLDs/year, 95% CI = 46–111), and post-2013 acceleration (94 YLDs/year, 95% CI = 59–127). YLLs increased from 1.65×105 to 2.39 × 105, giving an AAPC of 1.2%/year. Bayesian Age–Period–Cohort (BAPC) Analysis

The BAPC model reproduced the historical decline in the global age-standardized rate (per 100,000) of SHS-attributable breast-cancer deaths from 0.099 in 1990 to 0.072 in 2021 (95% credible interval [CrI] = 0.070–0.073), a 27.3% overall reduction (AAPC = −1.02%/year; Figures 9 and 10). The decline was steeper in 2000–2010 (AAPC = −1.67%/year) than in 2010–2021 (AAPC = −0.89%/year), consistent with the downward trajectory observed in the left panel of the fan plot Forward projections indicate a near-term plateau/slight dip: the posterior mean is projected to reach its minimum around 2030 at 0.0703 (95% CrI = 0.0558–0.0849, 2021–2030 AAPC = −0.25%/year). Thereafter, the mean is projected to drift upward modestly to 0.0749 by 2050 (95% CrI = 0.00023–0.14964, 2030–2050 AAPC = +0.32%/year). Uncertainty fans out sharply after 2025, with the 95% CrI broadening from 0.069–0.074 in 2022 to 0.000–0.150 in 2050, reflecting increasing sensitivity of long-range forecasts to period and cohort effects. Taken together, the model supports sustained but slowing historical declines up to 2021, a stable trough around 2030, and wide uncertainty thereafter, within which the central tendency suggests low, slowly rising rates through mid-century unless further SHS control and early-detection gains are realized. The BAPC model reproduced the long‐run decline in the global age-standardized DALY rate attributable to SHS-related breast cancer, falling from 3.86 per 100,000 in 1990 to 2.87 in 2021 (95% CrI = 2.86–2.88), representing a 25.7% reduction (AAPC = −0.95%/year over 1990–2021). However, the sensitivity estimators did not consistently corroborate the IVW signal. For the ER-negative endpoint ieu-a-1166, the weighted median estimate was directionally similar but non-significant (OR = 2.43, 95% CI = 0.67–8.81, P = 0.177), and the MR-Egger estimate was imprecise with a wide confidence interval (OR = 5.62, 95% CI = 0.29–110.50, P = 0.264). Mode-based estimators were also non-significant. Therefore, the observed IVW association should be interpreted as suggestive genetic-instrument evidence rather than definitive causal evidence.

For ER-positive breast cancer, point estimates varied in direction and none reached statistical significance. For example, the ER-positive meta-analysis ieu-a-1127 showed no evidence of association in the IVW model (OR = 1.02, 95% CI = 0.68–1.52, P = 0.928), while another ER-positive GWAS showed a directionally positive but non-significant estimate (ieu-a-1167: OR = 1.71, 95% CI = 0.94–3.09, P = 0.078). Sensitivity methods were uniformly non-significant. HER2/HER-defined subtypes in FinnGen, including HER-positive, HER2-negative, and HER-negative endpoints with or without exclusion of other cancers, showed no evidence of association across IVW and robustness estimators. Overall breast cancer endpoints from FinnGen, GWAS Catalog, and IEU OpenGWAS were also null in IVW analyses (ORs = 0.97–1.11, all P > 0.55) and in alternative estimators. Carcinoma in situ showed no evidence of association (IVW OR = 1.53, 95% CI = 0.27–8.74, P = 0.630).

For survival outcomes, IVW analysis suggested a borderline inverse association with ER-positive breast cancer survival (ieu-a-1164: β = −1.81, hazard ratio [HR] = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.02–1.09, P = 0.061). However, MR-Egger, weighted median, and mode-based estimates were imprecise and non-significant. Overall breast cancer survival was null (ieu-a-1165: IVW HR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.16–1.86, P = 0.333). Collectively, these results suggest a possible association between SHS-related genetic instruments and ER-negative breast cancer, but the evidence was limited by nominal significance, wide confidence intervals, and incomplete consistency across MR estimators.

Amino acid and small-molecule traits showed additional nominal associations, including higher histidine (OR = 1.65, 95% CI = 1.07–2.53, P = 0.023), leucine (OR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.02–2.23, P = 0.039), phenylacetylglutamine (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.05–2.06, P = 0.024), O-sulfo-L-tyrosine (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.01–1.33, P = 0.034), and succinylcarnitine (OR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.04–1.38, P = 0.0107), but lower tyrosine (OR = 0.895, 95% CI = 0.803–0.997, P = 0.044). Several fatty acid species, including odd-chain and monounsaturated C19 moieties and 20:2n-6, were inversely associated (ORs = 0.74–0.83, all P ≤ 0.044). Ketone and organic acid intermediates, including 3-hydroxybutyrate, α-ketoglutarate, 2-hydroxybutyrate, glycerate, and vanillin, were also inversely associated (OR = 0.60–0.77, all P ≤ 0.043). Urate showed a strong positive association, although the magnitude of the OR reflected raw-unit scaling and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, the metabolite screen prioritized triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, HDL-related traits, inflammatory markers, glucose-related traits, fatty acid species, and ketone-body intermediates as nominal candidate metabolic features associated with genetically proxied SHS exposure. These findings provide exploratory metabolic signals for future validation rather than definitive evidence of metabolic mediation.

Exploratory Mediation Signals Linking SHS Exposure, Candidate Metabolites, Immune Phenotypes, and ER-Negative Breast Cancer

Because the IVW analysis showed a nominal positive association between genetically proxied SHS exposure outside the home and ER-negative breast cancer (β_XY = 0.9976 on the log-odds scale, SE = 0.4708, P = 0.034; OR = 2.71, 95% CI = 1.08–6.82), we conducted exploratory two-step MR mediation analyses using the product-of-coefficients approach. These analyses focused on selected lipid/energy metabolites and one myeloid antigen-presentation phenotype identified in the preceding screening analyses (Figures 16 and 17). Given the wide uncertainty intervals and non-significant indirect-effect P values, these results should be interpreted as exploratory mediation signals rather than confirmed mediating pathways. For the four candidate metabolites, both the exposure-to-metabolite and metabolite-to-outcome estimates were directionally aligned in a manner that produced positive indirect-effect estimates. The strongest point estimates were observed for MUFA-related markers. For 10-nonadecenoate, the indirect effect was β_indirect = 0.3993 (SE = 0.2322, P = 0.085; OR_indirect = 1.49, 95% CI = 0.95–2.35), corresponding to an exploratory mediated proportion of approximately 40%. For eicosenoate, the indirect effect was β_indirect = 0.3079 (SE = 0.2095, P = 0.142; OR_indirect = 1.36, 95% CI = 0.90–2.05), corresponding to an exploratory mediated proportion of approximately 31%. Dihomo-linoleate (20:2n6) showed a larger but imprecise point estimate (β_indirect = 0.6274, SE = 0.4302, P = 0.145; OR_indirect = 1.87, 95% CI = 0.81–4.35), corresponding to an exploratory mediated proportion of approximately 63%. For 3-hydroxybutyrate, the indirect effect was smaller but directionally consistent (β_indirect = 0.1270, SE = 0.0870, P = 0.145; OR_indirect = 1.14, 95% CI = 0.96–1.35), corresponding to an exploratory mediated proportion of approximately 13%. We next evaluated the immune phenotype HLA-DR on CD33dim HLA-DR+ CD11b− cells as a candidate immune-related mediator. The first-step MR estimate suggested a positive association between genetically proxied SHS exposure and this phenotype (β_XM = 1.8727, SE = 0.8263, P = 0.023; OR = 6.51, 95% CI = 1.29–32.86). Discussion

This study integrated population-level burden estimation, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), and exploratory immune–metabolic screening to evaluate the global burden of breast cancer attributable to secondhand smoke (SHS) and to explore candidate signals related to ER-negative breast cancer. The findings should be interpreted across three distinct evidence layers. First, the GBD-based burden analysis provided the strongest and most consistent evidence. From 1990 to 2021, the absolute number of SHS-attributable breast cancer deaths and DALYs increased globally, whereas age-standardized death and DALY rates declined. This pattern suggests that population growth and aging partly offset improvements in age-specific rates. High-SDI settings showed sustained decreases in deaths, YLLs, and DALYs, whereas low- and low-middle-SDI settings showed flatter or rising trends, particularly for YLDs. These findings are consistent with the broader GBD comparative risk assessment framework, in which exposure distributions, relative risks, population structure, and age-specific rates jointly shape attributable disease burden.15

The temporal analyses further suggested that declines in age-standardized rates have slowed in recent years. Joinpoint analysis identified changes in trend segments and estimated annual percentage changes across time periods.23 BAPC modelling provided a Bayesian age–period–cohort framework for projecting future burden and uncertainty.26 In the present analysis, BAPC and ARIMA projections suggested a possible plateau around 2030 with increasing long-term uncertainty. These results should be interpreted as scenario-based projections rather than precise forecasts, because long-range estimates are sensitive to model assumptions and future changes in exposure patterns, cancer detection, treatment access, and survival. The decomposition analysis indicated that the rise in absolute burden was mainly driven by demographic expansion and population aging, which is consistent with the purpose of decomposition methods in separating demographic and epidemiological contributions to disease burden.24,25

The second evidence layer came from the MR analyses. Genetically proxied exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home showed a nominal positive IVW association with ER-negative breast cancer in one GWAS dataset, with directionally similar but weaker signals in additional ER-negative or overall breast cancer datasets. However, this association was not consistently supported by MR-Egger, weighted median, or mode-based estimators, and several estimates had wide confidence intervals. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as suggestive genetic-instrument evidence rather than definitive causal evidence. This cautious interpretation is important because two-sample MR estimates can be affected by weak instruments, sample overlap, and exposure misclassification, particularly when the exposure is environmental and difficult to measure precisely.39 Horizontal pleiotropy remains another key concern and was therefore assessed using pleiotropy-robust sensitivity analyses where applicable.42

The third evidence layer was the exploratory immune–metabolic mediation screen. Candidate signals included MUFA-related lipids, ketone/energy metabolism markers, and a CD33dim HLA-DR-defined myeloid phenotype. However, the indirect-effect estimates were imprecise, several mediation P values did not reach conventional significance thresholds, and mediated proportions were derived from a nominal total-effect estimate. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as confirmed mediating pathways. Rather, they should be viewed as hypothesis-generating signals for future validation. Because the immune and metabolic screens were high-dimensional and included many correlated phenotypes, nominal associations were not used to define statistically confirmed mediators. This conservative interpretation was adopted to reduce overstatement of potentially false-positive signals in an exploratory analysis. This interpretation is consistent with methodological guidance that MR-based mediation analysis requires careful attention to instrument strength, pleiotropy, mediator correlation, and uncertainty in both exposure–mediator and mediator–outcome steps.43 In addition, mediated proportions estimated using product-of-coefficients approaches require cautious interpretation when pathways are correlated or incompletely measured.44 Multivariable MR can help evaluate correlated candidate mediators when adequate instruments are available, but it also depends on instrument strength and pleiotropy assumptions.45

The population-level findings are broadly consistent with recent global evidence indicating that tobacco-related exposures remain important contributors to disease burden despite progress in tobacco control.46 They are also consistent with contemporary analyses showing persistent regional variation in tobacco exposure and control progress.47 In relation to breast cancer specifically, recent evidence among never-smoking women has suggested that SHS exposure may be associated with increased breast cancer risk, particularly when exposure intensity and duration are better characterized.3 Earlier meta-analytic evidence also suggested an association between active or passive smoking and breast cancer risk, although estimates varied across study designs and exposure definitions.48 Therefore, variation in exposure timing, exposure setting, measurement quality, and tumor subtype may partly explain inconsistent findings across studies.

The exploratory metabolic findings should be interpreted as biologically plausible but not confirmatory. Tobacco exposure has been associated with adverse lipid and lipoprotein profiles, including higher triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and lower HDL-related traits.49 In addition, SHS exposure has been linked to dyslipidemia among non-smoking adults, supporting the plausibility that passive smoke exposure may be associated with systemic metabolic disturbance.50 In the present study, nominal metabolite signals involved triglyceride-rich particles, HDL-related traits, inflammatory markers, glucose-related traits, MUFA-related lipids, and ketone-body intermediates. These findings may reflect broader metabolic perturbation associated with SHS exposure rather than specific causal mediators of ER-negative breast cancer. Although lipid metabolism is relevant to cancer biology, circulating metabolites may not directly represent breast tissue metabolism or tumor-specific metabolic states.51 Experimental evidence that exogenous monounsaturated fatty acids can influence ferroptosis resistance further supports the biological plausibility of MUFA-related signals, but does not establish mediation in the present study.52

Similarly, the exploratory immune findings should be interpreted cautiously. The immune screen identified nominal signals involving T-cell activation, regulatory T-cell phenotypes, antigen-presentation markers, and CD33-related myeloid traits. Prior studies have shown that cigarette smoke can alter immune responsiveness and inflammatory signaling.53 Myeloid-derived suppressor cells and related CD33-positive myeloid populations are recognized as important components of tumor-associated immune regulation.54 Standardized nomenclature and characterization criteria for myeloid-derived suppressor cells further emphasize the complexity and heterogeneity of these populations.55 Therefore, the HLA-DR on CD33dim HLA-DR+ CD11b− signal should be considered a candidate immune feature rather than evidence of a defined immune-mediated mechanism. This cautious interpretation is also important because public immune GWAS resources may vary in gating strategy, phenotype definition, assay platform, and cohort composition.

These findings have several implications. First, the burden analysis indicates that declining age-standardized rates do not necessarily translate into a reduced absolute burden when population growth and aging continue. Strengthening smoke-free policies remains important for reducing SHS exposure at the population level.56 Economic tobacco-control measures, such as tobacco taxation, may also contribute to reducing exposure-related health losses, although their effects will vary by policy implementation, enforcement, and socioeconomic context.57 Second, the increase in YLDs in some regions highlights the importance of survivorship-oriented breast cancer care, including rehabilitation, cardiometabolic risk management, mental health support, and functional recovery.58 Third, the MR and mediation findings suggest candidate immune–metabolic signals for future research, but they are not sufficient to support clinical risk stratification or intervention design at this stage.

This study has several strengths. It combines standardized GBD burden estimation with temporal trend analysis, decomposition, forecasting, two-sample MR, and high-dimensional immune–metabolic screening. It also focuses on ER-negative breast cancer, a clinically important subtype with limited endocrine treatment options and potential relevance to inflammatory, oxidative, immune, and metabolic processes.8 Importantly, the revised interpretation separates population-level burden findings from genetic-instrument estimates and exploratory mediator screening, thereby avoiding overstatement of mechanistic conclusions.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, the GBD estimates are model-based and depend on exposure distributions, relative-risk estimates, population-attributable fractions, and cause-specific disease models. Therefore, uncertainty intervals should be interpreted as reflecting uncertainty in modeled attribution rather than direct measurement error. Second, the GBD SHS attribution and the UK Biobank GWAS phenotype “exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home” are related but not identical exposure constructs. The former represents modeled population-level burden attributable to SHS, whereas the latter reflects self-reported outside-home tobacco smoke exposure obtained from UK Biobank-derived GWAS resources indexed in IEU OpenGWAS. This difference may introduce exposure misclassification when integrating GBD and MR evidence. Third, most GWAS datasets used in the MR and mediation analyses were based predominantly on individuals of European ancestry, which limits generalizability to populations with different SHS exposure patterns, genetic backgrounds, and breast cancer subtype distributions. Fourth, potential sample overlap among UK Biobank, BCAC, FinnGen, metabolite GWAS, and immune phenotype GWAS sources cannot be fully excluded and may bias MR estimates, especially when instruments are weak.39

Fifth, the MR findings for ER-negative breast cancer were nominal and not consistently supported across robustness estimators, so they should be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive. Sixth, the mediation screen involved many correlated metabolites and immune phenotypes. Because these traits may reflect overlapping biological processes, mediated proportions should not be interpreted as additive or independent.43,44 Seventh, the mediator datasets were derived from public GWAS resources with heterogeneous phenotype definitions and assay platforms, and circulating metabolites or peripheral immune phenotypes may not fully reflect breast tissue biology or the tumor microenvironment. These limitations reinforce the need to interpret the immune–metabolic findings as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Future research should validate these findings in larger and more diverse GWAS datasets, especially for ER-negative breast cancer. Multivariable MR, colocalization, and phenome-wide sensitivity analyses may help clarify whether the prioritized lipid, ketone, and myeloid immune signals represent independent pathways or correlated markers of broader biological perturbation.45 Prospective cohorts incorporating objective SHS biomarkers, such as cotinine, together with detailed exposure setting, repeated metabolomic profiling, and immune phenotyping will be important for reducing exposure misclassification and clarifying temporal relationships. Policy-effect evaluations using quasi-experimental designs may further determine whether strengthened smoke-free laws and cessation services reduce SHS-attributable breast cancer burden and survivorship-related disability.

Conclusions

The global burden of breast cancer attributable to secondhand smoke reflects the combined influence of demographic change, uneven tobacco-control progress, and survivorship-related disability. Although age-standardized death and DALY rates declined from 1990 to 2021, absolute deaths and DALYs increased, particularly in settings where population growth and aging offset improvements in age-specific rates. These findings highlight the need to strengthen smoke-free policies, reduce SHS exposure, and integrate survivorship-oriented breast cancer care, especially in regions with persistent or rising disability burden. The genetic analyses provided suggestive genetic-instrument evidence linking exposure to tobacco smoke outside the home with ER-negative breast cancer, but this association was not consistently supported across all MR sensitivity estimators. Therefore, the MR findings should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger, subtype-specific, and ancestrally diverse GWAS datasets. The exploratory mediation analyses prioritized MUFA-related lipids, ketone/energy metabolism markers, and CD33/HLA-DR-related myeloid phenotypes as candidate immune–metabolic signals. However, because the indirect-effect estimates were imprecise and the mediated proportions were derived from a nominal total-effect estimate, these signals should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of confirmed biological mediation. Overall, this study provides a population-level assessment of SHS-attributable breast cancer burden and identifies candidate genetic-epidemiological and immune–metabolic signals for future validation. Coordinated prevention strategies, improved exposure measurement, objective SHS biomarkers, and prospective multi-omic studies will be needed to clarify whether these candidate pathways contribute to ER-negative breast cancer risk and to inform targeted prevention and survivorship interventions.
