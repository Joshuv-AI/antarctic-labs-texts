An imported case of human granulocytic anaplasmosis presenting with altered mental status: A case report
Authors: Shundai Ko, Aki Sakurai, Masakatsu Taira, Harutaka Katano, Ken Maeda, Tadaki Suzuki, Norio Ohmagari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA) is a tick-borne disease endemic to the northeastern United States but remains extremely rare in Japan. We report an imported case of HGA in a 77-year-old man who had recently traveled from the United States. He presented with a 5-day history of fever, lethargy, and loss of appetite, followed by altered consciousness. Laboratory findings revealed thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases, and increased creatine kinase levels. Computed tomography was unremarkable except for mild splenomegaly. Considering his recent stay in Bemidji, Minnesota—a region with a high risk of tick exposure—empirical doxycycline therapy was promptly initiated for suspected tick-borne illness. Although indirect immunofluorescence antibody (IFA) assays for IgM and IgG against Anaplasma phagocytophilum were negative at admission, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of whole blood targeting the groEL gene yielded a positive result. Sequence analysis revealed complete (100%) identity of the amplified 16S rRNA, groEL, and p44 fragments with reference A. phagocytophilum sequences (CP000235). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of HGA. The patient responded rapidly to doxycycline therapy and was discharged on hospital day 5. This case highlights the diagnostic value of molecular methods during the early phase of infection, when serology may remain negative, and underscores the importance of considering travel and tick exposure history when evaluating febrile patients.Keywords: Human granulocytic anaplasmosis, Tick-borne infections, PCR

Introduction

Human granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA) is an emerging tick-borne disease caused by the obligate intracellular bacterium Anaplasma phagocytophilum. It is endemic in the Northeastern and upper Midwest regions of the United States, with transmission occurring primarily through the bite of infected Ixodes ticks [1]. Most cases are reported during the summer months (May–July) [2]. HGA has also been documented in parts of Europe and Asia [3].

Clinically, HGA typically presents with an acute onset of high fever, headache, malaise, and myalgia. Rash and neurological manifestations are uncommon, reported in less than 10% of cases [3], [4], [5]. Laboratory abnormalities frequently include leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and mild to moderate elevations in liver transaminases. Diagnosis can be established through polymerase chain reaction (PCR), serologic testing, or identification of characteristic intracytoplasmic inclusions (morulae) within granulocytes on a peripheral blood smear [3]. Prompt initiation of doxycycline therapy is critical and associated with favorable outcomes [6].

Although HGA is relatively common in the United States, it remains rare in Japan. However, with increasing globalization and a growing volume of international travel, the incidence of imported infectious diseases has been on the rise [7]. Here, we describe an imported case of HGA diagnosed in Japan.

Case report

A 77-year-old White man with a medical history of hyperlipidemia and sleep apnea syndrome was transferred to our hospital with a 5-day history of fever, lethargy, and anorexia. He was a resident of Bemidji, Minnesota, USA, and had traveled to Japan with his family 13 days before the presentation. He had been in his usual state of health until 5 days earlier, when he developed a fever of 39.0°C accompanied by fatigue, vomiting, and watery diarrhea. Two days before the current presentation, he was admitted to a local hospital due to poor oral intake, where intravenous fluid and cefmetazole were initiated for suspected gastroenteritis. Although his gastrointestinal symptoms improved, his fever persisted, and his lethargy worsened, prompting transfer to our hospital. On arrival, his vital signs were as follows: body temperature 40.6℃, blood pressure 160/79 mmHg, heart rate 92 beats per minute, respiratory rate 30 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 92% on 2–3 L/minute of oxygen via nasal cannula. The patient appeared confused and disoriented (E4V4M5 on the Glasgow Coma Scale), with visual hallucinations and irritability, but had no focal neurological deficits. Physical examination revealed conjunctival injection, mild petechiae over the trunk and extremities, and coarse crackle in the left lower lung field. No eschar or evidence of a tick bite was present. Laboratory studies revealed thrombocytopenia (26,000/µl), elevated transaminases (AST 264 U/L, ALT 75 U/L), and elevated lactic acid dehydrogenase (LDH 1083 U/L), with a normal total bilirubin level (0.7 mg/dL). Serum creatinine kinase (CK) was markedly elevated (5606 U/L), as were C-reactive protein (CRP 43.6 mg/dL) and ferritin (20,237 ng/mL). The white blood cell count was 3590/µl with 94% neutrophils, and the lymphocyte count was low (150/μl). Electrolytes, serum glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and hemoglobin levels were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of coagulopathy. A whole-body computed tomography (CT) scan revealed splenomegaly and subtle consolidation in the right lower lobe. Testing for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza was negative. Blood and urine cultures obtained 2 days before the current admission were also negative. A thorough history provided by the patient’s family—since the patient was unable to provide details due to an altered mental status—revealed that he lived in a house surrounded by forest in Bemidji, Minnesota, an area recognized as high-risk for tick-borne diseases. Approximately 3 days before traveling to Japan (i.e., 12 days prior to symptom onset), a tick was found in his underwear near the genital area, although it was unclear whether a tick bite had occurred. While in Japan, there was no known history of tick or mosquito bites, and he had not traveled to other countries recently.

Given his history, laboratory findings, and clinical courses, a tick-borne illness was suspected. Empirical therapy with oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily and intravenous cefepime 1 g every 12 h was initiated. He became afebrile promptly the day after treatment initiation, followed by gradual improvement in mental status and general condition. A blood sample collected on the day of admission was sent to the National Institute of Infectious Diseases for evaluation of tick-borne pathogens and other potential infectious agents.

Genomic and serology testing for A. phagocytophilum and Ehrlichia spp.

Serum and EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood samples collected on the day of admission were used for the following tests. Indirect immunofluorescence antibody (IFA) testing for IgG and IgM against A. phagocytophilum and E. chaffeensis was performed using IFA IgG/IgM Substrate Slides (Focus Diagnostics, CA, USA). Both tests were negative, with IgM < 1:40 and IgG < 1:40.

DNA was extracted from EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood using the High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and subjected to conventional PCR assays specific for A. phagocytophilum, targeting the 16S rRNA, groEL, and p44 genes, as described in the Supplementary Methods. All three targets were successfully amplified, and the PCR products were purified and sequenced (Fig. 1). Sequence analysis demonstrated complete (100%) identity of the 16S rRNA, groEL, and p44 fragments with reference A. phagocytophilum sequences strain HZ (accession no. CP000235) (Table 1, Fig. 2). These molecular findings confirmed the diagnosis of HGA.

Multiple real-time PCR assays against multiple infectious pathogens

Nucleic acids were extracted from serum and whole blood samples using the Quick DNA/RNA Viral Kit (Zymo Research, USA). In-house multiplex real-time PCR assays, targeting multiple infectious pathogens (e.g., bacteria, viruses, fungi, and others), as listed in Supplementary Materials and described previously [8], [9], [10], were performed. For tick-borne infections, the multiplex PCR assays included Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus and Tick-borne encephalitis virus, however; the assay did not include other tick-borne pathogens such as A. phagocytophilum, Borrelia burgdorferi, Babesia microti, and Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis. In the present case, all PCR results were negative.

Clinical course

Given the negative blood culture results, cefepime was discontinued on day 2 of hospitalization, while doxycycline was continued. Although his platelet count continued to decline to 25000/μl until day 3, it subsequently recovered, along with improvements in other laboratory parameters, such as transaminases, LDH, and CK. Morulae were not observed on the peripheral blood smear. His level of consciousness improved on day 2. He was weaned off supplemental oxygen on day 3 and was discharged home in stable condition on day 6 of hospitalization. The A. phagocytophilum-specific PCR results, which became available on day 10 (4 days after discharge), were conveyed to the patient, and follow-up with his primary care physician was recommended.

Discussion

We report a rare imported case of HGA in Japan, diagnosed in a 77-year-old traveler from the United States. The infection was most likely acquired in the patient’s home state of Minnesota, an area endemic for tick-borne diseases, where the reported incidence of HGA ranges from 7.2 to 13.4 cases per 100,000 population [11]. The patient’s history of possible tick exposure, together with the typical HGA incubation period of 5–21 days (most commonly 5–14 days) [12], strongly supports the inference that the infection was acquired in the United States. In Minnesota and other Northeastern states, Ixodes scapularis is the primary vector of HGA. This tick species also transmits several other pathogens, including Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), Babesia microti (babesiosis), Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis (ehrlichiosis), and Powassan virus. Consequently, coinfections are not uncommon, reported in approximately 2.3–10% of HGA cases [13]. Therefore, when HGA is suspected, prompt and simultaneous testing for these Ixodes-borne pathogens should be considered at the initial evaluation, given the possibility of coinfection and differing therapeutic approaches [13].

Although HGA remains rare in Japan, several domestic cases have been reported [14], [15], [16]. A recent study by Kawaguchi et al. reviewed eight Japanese cases diagnosed with HGA, all of which were considered domestically acquired [14]. Notably, several Anaplasma species, including A. phagocytophilum, have been detected in dogs and wildlife across the country, with Haemaphysalis longicornis, Ixodes persulcatus, and Ixodes ovatus recognized as potential vectors of HGA [15], [16]. Among these, H. longicornis is also a known vector for severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) and Japanese spotted fever, while I. persulcatus is the primary vector for Lyme disease. Clinicians should, therefore, consider HGA in the differential diagnosis of acute febrile illness following tick exposure, even in Japan, alongside other endemic tick-borne infections.

Regarding clinical manifestations, the present case was notable for exhibiting an altered mental status. While typical symptoms of HGA include fever, malaise, and myalgia, neurological symptoms such as altered consciousness are rarely reported. This contrasts with SFTS, where central nervous system (CNS) involvement is observed in approximately 20–40% of cases [17], [18]. In a systematic review by Dumić et al., the clinical characteristics of 110 cases of HGA were summarized from 88 publications over 20 years from January 2002 to September 2021 [5]. Among the reported clinical manifestations, confusion was reported in 9 patients (8.2%); however, detailed clinical information for these cases was not provided. To further clarify the neurological features of HGA, we conducted a comprehensive literature search using PubMed/PMC to identify reported cases with neurological involvement. The search utilized keywords such as "Anaplasma phagocytophilum", "altered mental status”, "encephalitis", and "meningitis", with the full search strategy described in the Supplementary Material. After manual screening of primary case reports, seven unique cases with CNS involvement were identified (Table 2) [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Similar to our case, the majority of patients (7 of 8) were older than 60 years and presented with altered mental status or confusion. Three cases were diagnosed with encephalitis (including those with meningeal or spinal cord involvement), two had cerebral infarction, one had subarachnoid hemorrhage, and one was diagnosed with encephalopathy. Clinical improvement was achieved in all cases within 24–72 h following the initiation of doxycycline therapy, although three patients had mild residual neurological sequelae[20], [21], [25]. In the present case, brain MRI and cerebrospinal fluid analysis were not performed because of the patient’s agitation and severe thrombocytopenia. However, given the rapid resolution of neurological symptoms following doxycycline therapy, the condition was considered most consistent with infection-related encephalopathy or encephalitis associated with HGA.

In addition to neurological symptoms, the clinical findings were notable for marked cytopenia, hyperferritinemia, and an elevated CK level. The former findings (cytopenia and hyperferritinemia) were compatible with a hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-like syndrome, while the latter finding (elevated CK level) suggested rhabdomyolysis, both of which have been reported in HGA cases [5], [26], [27]. Furthermore, the patient demonstrated pulmonary infiltrates in the right lower lobe. Although relatively uncommon, pulmonary complications, such as pneumonia and interstitial infiltrates, have been described in HGA [5]. In this case, however, given the patient’s altered mental status, aspiration pneumonia was also considered in the differential diagnosis; therefore, cefepime was initiated in addition to doxycycline. These findings highlight that HGA can present with diverse systemic manifestations beyond its typical clinical features, potentially complicating the differential diagnosis and management.

In the present case, PCR testing using whole blood—collected on day 5 of illness and prior to the initiation of doxycycline—led to the diagnosis of HGA. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that PCR provides the most reliable detection during the early phase of infection (within the first 7–10 days after symptom onset), when antibody titers are often undetectable. The reported sensitivity of PCR during this period ranges from 67% to 90%, with a specificity 98–100% [28]. It should be noted that PCR for HGA and other tick-borne infections, such as human monocytic ehrlichiosis (HME) and babesiosis, should be performed on whole blood samples anticoagulated with EDTA or sodium citrate, rather than on serum. This is because these pathogens are obligate intracellular parasites that reside primarily within white blood cells, making whole blood significantly more sensitive for detection [28]. In fact, PCR using serum has extremely low sensitivity, often below 10%, due to the scarcity of free bacterial DNA in the serum [29]. It is also essential to obtain specimens before starting treatment, as the sensitivity of PCR decreases markedly within 24–48 h of initiating doxycycline or minocycline [30].

The IFA test for IgG against A. phagocytophilum is also useful for diagnosis. It should be performed on paired serum samples: one collected during the first two weeks of illness and another 2–10 weeks later. A fourfold or greater increase in IgG titers confirms recent infection [31]. Morphologic identification of morulae in peripheral blood smears—typically seen during the first week of illness—may also aid diagnosis, although this method has limited sensitivity (20%–60%) and depends heavily on the timing of collection and observer expertise [32]. Nevertheless, as serologic and PCR testing for HGA are not readily available in all institutions, detection of morulae on peripheral blood smears remains clinically important because it is accessible to most healthcare facilities. In this case, no morulae were observed on the blood smear obtained before the initiation of doxycycline, and the seroconversion could not be assessed, as the convalescent serum sample was unavailable due to the patient’s departure from the country. To maximize diagnostic yield, appropriate tests should be selected with consideration of each method’s characteristics and the timing of specimen collection relative to disease onset.

In summary, we presented an imported case of HGA in Japan. This case highlights the importance of obtaining a detailed travel history and considering diseases endemic to the patient’s country of origin in the differential diagnosis. Timely therapeutic intervention and appropriate diagnostic testing were crucial for an accurate diagnosis and favorable outcomes.
