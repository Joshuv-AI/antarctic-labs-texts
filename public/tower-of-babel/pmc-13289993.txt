Clinical and Social Determinants of Quality of Life among People Living with HIV in Jodhpur, Rajasthan: A Cross-Sectional Study using the WHOQOL-HIV BREF
Authors: Neha Mantri, Akhil D. Goel, Manoj K. Gupta, Gopal K. Bohra, Siyaram Didel, Rashmi Rathore, Dharamveer Yadav, Nitin K. Joshi, Srikanth Srinivasan, Naveen Kishoria, Prem P. Sharma, Ravisekhar Gadepalli, Jaykaran Charan, Pankaj Bhardwaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:With HIV increasingly managed as a chronic condition under antiretroviral therapy, assessing quality of life (QOL) has become integral to patientcentered care. This study evaluated QOL across multiple domains and identified sociodemographic and clinical determinants among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in western Rajasthan.Methods:A crosssectional study was conducted (March 2024–2025) among PLHIV attending a government ART center in Jodhpur. QOL was measured using the WHOQOLHIV BREF (Hindi version, pretested for reliability). Determinants were examined using descriptive statistics and logistic regression.Results:Among 551 participants (mean age 42.8 ± 12.4 years), the mean overall QOL score was 14.1 ± 2.0. The physical domain scored highest, while social relationships scored lowest. In adjusted models, age >30 years, occupation, and symptomatic HIV status were significant predictors of poor QOL. Spousal absence showed a particularly strong association with diminished independence, underscoring the importance of social support. Viral suppression was modestly linked to better psychological QOL, though the effect size was small.Conclusion:Clinical stage, age, occupation, and spousal support emerged as key determinants of QOL among PLHIV in Jodhpur. These findings highlight the need for integrated interventions that combine medical management with psychosocial counselling and communitybased support to address the multifaceted needs of PLHIV.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, HIV remains a major public health challenge, though advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have transformed it into a chronic, manageable condition. In line with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), countries have committed to eliminating AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.[1] India, with an adult HIV prevalence of 0.2%—lower than the global average—operates the world’s secondlargest ART programme, supporting over 1.4 million people living with HIV (PLHIV).[2] While ART has significantly improved survival, attention must now extend to the quality of life (QOL) of PLHIV as a key indicator of treatment success in the absence of a definitive cure.[3]

Evidence from highburden settings such as South Africa demonstrates that QOL is shaped by a complex interplay of sociodemographic and structural factors, including age, gender, education, employment, housing, and income.[4] In India and other low- and middleincome countries, these determinants are compounded by health system constraints, limited health literacy, and persistent stigma.[5] The National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5) revealed a low comprehensive knowledge about HIV/AIDS, particularly among women (26.8%), underscoring gaps in prevention and patientcentered care.[6] Despite notable national progress—marked by reductions in new infections (44%) and AIDSrelated mortality (79%)—regional disparities persist.[7] Rajasthan, classified as a moderate priority state, reports HIV prevalence (0.15% among males and 0.10% among females) below the national average (0.2%), yet evidence on QOL outcomes among PLHIV remains scarce.[8]

Within Rajasthan, Jodhpur provides a distinctive context for examining QOL. The region is characterized by traditional norms, conservative social structures, and lower literacy levels, which amplify stigma and gender disparities in healthcare access.[9] As a referral hub for western Rajasthan, Jodhpur’s ART centers serve vast rural catchment areas with limited infrastructure and high patient loads. Epidemiologically, heterosexual transmission predominates, with late presentation to care being common.[10] These cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare delivery challenges make Jodhpur an underrepresented yet important setting for QOL research.

Study aim and objectives

This study aimed to evaluate the quality of life of PLHIV receiving ART in Jodhpur and to identify key sociodemographic and clinical determinants influencing QOL outcomes. Specifically, the objectives were as follows:

To measure QOL across physical, psychological, social, independence, environmental, and spiritual domains using the WHOQOLHIV BREF instrument.To analyze associations between sociodemographic and clinical variables and quality of life (QOL) outcomes.To interpret findings in the context of regional HIV care, highlighting implications for patientcentered support and service delivery.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

This crosssectional study was conducted between March 2024 and March 2025 at two government ART centers in Jodhpur, Rajasthan. These centers function as referral hubs for western Rajasthan, serving both urban and rural catchment areas. Together, they cater to approximately 6000 registered PLHIV and manage a daily outpatient load of 150–200 patients, reflecting the high service demand and diverse patient population in the region.

Sampling and sample size

A consecutive sampling approach was adopted to minimize nonresponse and capture the experiences of PLHIV routinely engaged in care. Sample size was calculated using the singleproportion formula, based on a prior North Indian estimate of poor QOL prevalence (28.75%).[11] At 95% confidence and 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample was 315. After applying a design effect (DEFF) of 1.5 and accounting for anticipated nonresponse and refusal, the final sample size was 551 participants.

Data collection procedures

Rapport was established prior to interviews to enhance trust and engagement. Facetoface interviews were conducted by trained healthcare professionals in Hindi, lasting 20–25 minutes. The data was collected through Epicollect5 software[12] and subsequently imported into IBM Statistical Packages for Social Sciences version 23 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis.

RESULTS

We interviewed 551 PLHIV (320 males, 231 females) with a mean age of 42.85 ± 12.4 years. The majority of them were married (69.1%), living in urban areas (85.1%) and in a nuclear family (63.5%). Nearly half of the participants were illiterate (43.6%), while about 63% belonged to a lower socioeconomic class. A substantial proportion contracted HIV through the heterosexual route (n = 261, 47.3%). The majority of participants (461/551; 83.7%) were asymptomatic (WHO staging I), and comorbidities were present in approximately onefifth of the sample (n = 112, 20.3%). The median CD4 count was 467 cells/mm≥, with an interquartile range of 265.25–678.25. The viral load was undetectable in among 86.8% of participants. Level of independence domain

In the Level of Independence domain, the mean score was 11.5 ± 1.4. Individuals with age >30 years (OR: 1.2, 95% CI: 1.0–1.5, P < 0.033), no formal education had significantly higher odds of poor QOL compared to those with formal education (OR: 2.0, 95% CI: 1.4–2.8, P < 0.00). Being a male (OR: 0.6, 95% CI: 0.4–0.8, P = 0.003), skilled occupation showed a protective effect, with lower odds of poor QOL than their unskilled/semiskilled counterparts (OR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.6–1.0, P = 0.10). Lower socioeconomic status (Class IV/V) was also associated with increased odds of poor QOL (OR: 1.7, 95% CI: 1.2–2.4, P = 0.003).

The marital status emerged as a strong predictor, with those whose spouse were absent having higher odds of poor QOL (OR: 10.1, 95% CI: 6.4–16.0, P < 0.001). In the multivariable logistic regression, after adjusting for age, sex, education, socioeconomic status, comorbidity, and living arrangement, the OR increased to 12.7 (95% CI: 7.5–21.3). This inflation compared to the crude OR suggests that certain covariates acted as negative confounders. Variance inflation factors were <2.1, ruling out multicollinearity, and cell counts were adequate, minimizing sparse data bias.

Among clinical variables, HIV symptomatic status (OR: 2.5, 95% CI: 1.5–4.0, P < 0.001) and ART duration greater than 5 years (OR: 1.6, 95% CI: 1.1–2.3, P = 0.01) were significantly associated with poor QOL. In the adjusted regression model, these variables remained independent predictors: age (aOR: 1.8, 95% CI: 1.0–3.3, P = 0.03); absence of spouse (aOR: 12.7, 95% CI: 7.5–21.3, P < 0.001), symptomatic HIV status (aOR: 4.0, 95% CI: 2.2–7.1, P < 0.001), and ART duration >5 years (aOR: 1.5, 95% CI: 1.0–2.4, P = 0.04) [Table 4].

To summarize, WHO clinical staging [Supplementary File 2] and marital status [Supplementary File 3] consistently emerged as the strongest determinants of QOL across domains. Clinical stability and longterm ART adherence were associated with improved QOL, reinforcing the need for sustained treatment and comprehensive management of comorbidities. Equally, the absence of spousal support was linked to poorer outcomes, emphasizing the critical role of family and communitybased interventions.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that PLHIV reported moderate to good overall QOL scores. These results contrast with findings from Nepal, where restricted access to ART services, socioeconomic disadvantages, and heightened stigma have been associated with poorer QOL.[19] In comparison, our QOL scores were lower than those documented in several African settings, where stronger social support networks, wider ART availability, and comprehensive psychosocial interventions appear to foster better perceived QOL.[20]

The mean overall QOL score among participants was 14.1 ± 2.0, which was marginally higher than that reported in a previous Indian study (13.5 ± 2.7).[21] Within our cohort, the physical domain achieved the highest scores, whereas the social domain, though satisfactory, was comparatively lower. This variation likely reflects underlying sociodemographic factors and differences in access to ART services.

In this study, age emerged as an important determinant of QOL among PLHIV. Individuals aged over 30 years were more likely to report poorer outcomes in the physical and independence domains, reflecting the expected decline in functional well-being with advancing age. Interestingly, older participants demonstrated better scores in the spiritual domain, suggesting enhanced resilience and coping capacity over time. These findings are consistent with evidence from Ethiopia,[22] which highlighted reduced physical capacity among older PLHIV, and with broader literature indicating that younger individuals often experience greater fear of death and uncertainty about the future, shaped by cultural and healthcare contexts.

Male participants exhibited higher odds of poor physical QOL (OR = 1.3), which may be attributed to reduced work capacity and limitations in daily functioning. However, no gender differences were observed in the independence domain (OR = 1.0). Notably, studies from LMICs have reported lower social QOL among women, largely driven by socioeconomic disadvantages and structural constraints.[23]

Participants without a spouse had significantly higher odds of poor QOL in the independence (OR: 12.7), social (OR: 1.6), and environmental (OR: 2.3) domains, reflecting reduced support and greater vulnerability. This aligns with findings from Central India,[24] where spouses often serve as primary caregivers, facilitating adherence, emotional support, and household responsibilities. In their absence, individuals may rely more heavily on external systems of care. Evidence from Ghana further shows that disclosure to partners can mobilize emotional support from family (53.3%), extended family (45.5%), and nonfamily members (1.1%).[25] Conversely, some studies report no association—or even improved QOL—among unmarried individuals, underscoring the role of relationship quality, stigma, and sociocultural dynamics in shaping outcomes.[26]These observations resonate with National AIDS Control Organisation (NACO) policies, which emphasize psychosocial support, positive living, and community-based care through Link ART Centers and Care and Support Centers (CSCs). In Rajasthan, CSCs and community organizations have been mobilized to strengthen family engagement, reduce stigma, and improve adherence. Our findings reinforce the relevance of these strategies, highlighting the need for tailored interventions—including home-based care and peer support—for single, widowed, or socially isolated PLHIV.

Participants without formal education had higher odds of poor QOL across multiple domains, though only the environmental domain remained statistically significant after adjustment (OR = 1.8). This underscores the persistent disadvantage linked to low educational attainment, reinforcing evidence that education improves health literacy, adherence, coping, and engagement with supportive environments.[27]

Unskilled participants had 1.7 higher odds of poor QOL in the physical and environmental domains, reflecting the effects of limited resources, greater physical strain, and adverse living conditions. Conversely, skilled workers showed lower odds of poor QOL in the psychosocial (OR = 0.6) and social (OR = 0.7) domains, likely attributable to employment stability and stronger social networks.[28] Nonetheless, workplace stigma and job insecurity may diminish these advantages, contributing to variability across different contexts.

Participants from lower socioeconomic classes (IV and V) had higher odds of poor QOL across psychosocial (OR: 1.6), independence (OR: 1.7), social (OR: 1.5), and environmental (OR: 1.7) domains, though these associations were not statistically significant after adjustment. While prior studies often link lower SES to poorer QOL through limited resources and support, findings from West Bengal[29] show mixed results. In our context, access to ART and counseling may have mitigated SES-related disparities, highlighting the influence of service coverage and social coping mechanisms.

Rural participants showed higher odds of poor QOL in physical, independence, and environmental domains, while urban participants had a modest protective trend in psychological and environmental domains; however, none were statistically significant. These findings reflect the mixed evidence in existing literature—some studies highlight rural disadvantages due to limited transport and greater distances to care facilities.[30] In contrast, others report comparable or even better outcomes in rural areas, attributed to strong social networks and community support.[31] The lack of significant associations may reflect uniform program coverage and decentralized ART services in the study area.

Family structure was significantly associated with QOL outcomes. Participants from nuclear families had lower odds of poor QOL in the independence and environmental domains (OR: 0.7 each) compared to those from joint families. While larger families may offer emotional support, extended households can also impose role expectations and stigma.[32]

Participants on ART for over five years had significantly higher odds of poor QOL in the independence domain (aOR: 1.5), indicating potential decline in functional well-being. This contrasts with findings from South India, where longer ART duration was linked to improved QOL, possibly due to better psychological adjustment and coping.[28] In contrast, shorter ART duration showed a nonsignificant protective trend in psychosocial and spiritual domains (OR: 0.8 each). These mixed findings highlight the influence of contextual factors like healthcare access and support systems on QOL during long-term ART.

HIV symptomatic status (WHO stages II–IV) was strongly associated with poor QOL across multiple domains. Participants had higher odds of poor physical (OR: 7.7), independence (OR: 4.0), psychosocial (OR: 2.1), and environmental (OR: 2.9) outcomes, reflecting the individuals with HIV perceive their surroundings as less safe, less supportive, and reflect barriers in accessing community resources, especially in settings with inadequate infrastructure.[33] These findings highlight the multidimensional burden of advanced HIV on overall well-being.

Individuals with comorbidities had 2.2 higher odds of poor physical QOL in the unadjusted analysis, reflecting the cumulative impact of chronic illness on mobility and support access. Although this association was not significant after adjustment, the trend suggests mediation by factors like age, socioeconomic status and HIV duration. These findings align with Nigerian literature on the clinical and social burden of comorbidities on well-being.[34]

BMI was not significantly associated with QOL outcomes, contrasting earlier studies linking lower BMI to poor outcomes.[35] Higher BMI showed increased odds of reduced QOL in physical, psychosocial, and environmental domains, while BMI <18.5 appeared to offer modest protective effects. These findings suggest that BMI, though clinically relevant, may not fully capture the multidimensional aspects of well-being in PLHIV.

In our study, CD4 count differences did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that while immunological markers remain essential for clinical monitoring, they may not adequately capture the subjective dimensions of QOL, consistent with previous reports.[36] By contrast, higher viral load was associated with poorer QOL in the physical, environmental, and spiritual domains, underscoring the importance of achieving and sustaining viral suppression to enhance both physical and psychological well-being.

Although HIV status disclosure and perceived stigma are well-recognized determinants of the social relationship domain of QOL, our analysis focused primarily on clinical and sociodemographic factors. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that stigma and disclosure remain critical contextual influences on social well-being among PLHIV.

CONCLUSION

This study found that PLHIV reported moderate to good overall quality of life; however, outcomes were significantly influenced by sociodemographic and clinical determinants. Poorer QOL was more evident among participants over 30 years of age, males, those without formal education, individuals engaged in unskilled occupations, and those living in nuclear families—particularly within the independence, social, and environmental domains. Symptomatic HIV status and absence of a spouse were strongly associated with diminished QOL, underscoring the cumulative burden of disease and challenges in treatment adherence. Persistently lower scores in the social domain highlight the continuing impact of stigma and isolation on the well-being of PLHIV.

These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions that address both clinical management and sociodemographic vulnerabilities, while strengthening psychosocial support systems to improve overall quality of life among PLHIV.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference and prevents assessment of changes in QOL over time. Second, reliance on self-reported data may have introduced social desirability bias, while Neyman bias is possible if severely ill individuals were underrepresented. Although a validated Hindi instrument was used, cultural and linguistic nuances may still have influenced interpretation. Additionally, India’s heterogeneous HIV epidemic limits comparability and generalizability of findings, though regional data provide valuable insights into local epidemiological patterns. Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly in associations such as the strong effect of spousal presence on independence QOL.

Future research should incorporate multicentric or comparative analyses to enhance external validity and policy relevance. Analytical approaches such as linear regression using WHOQOL-BREF scores as continuous outcomes may also provide deeper insights into variability across domains.

Implications for practice and policy

Findings underscore the importance of enhancing personcentered HIV care within India’s national programme. To improve QOL among PLHIV, the following priorities are identified:
