The Inductive Bottleneck: Data-Driven Emergence of Representational Sparsity in Vision Transformers
Kanishk Awadhiya kanishk.awadhiya@gmail.com

Abstract
Vision Transformers (ViTs) lack the hierarchical inductive biases inherent to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), theoretically allowing them to maintain high-dimensional representations throughout all layers. However, recent observations suggest ViTs often spontaneously manifest a “U-shaped” entropy profile—compressing information in middle layers before expanding it for the final classification. In this work, we demonstrate that this “Inductive Bottleneck” is not an architectural artifact, but a data-dependent adaptation. By analyzing the layer-wise Effective Encoding Dimension (EED) of DINO-trained ViTs across datasets of varying compositional complexity (UC Merced, Tiny ImageNet, and CIFAR-100), we show that the depth of the bottleneck correlates strongly with the semantic abstraction required by the task. We find that while texture-heavy datasets preserve high-rank representations throughout, object-centric datasets drive the network to dampen high-frequency information in middle layers, effectively “learning” a bottleneck to isolate semantic features.

Introduction

The shift from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
(

to Vision Transformers (ViTs)
(

While a CNN forces a hierarchical reduction of spatial dimensions via pooling layers, a standard ViT maintains a constant patch number and embedding dimension from input to output. Theoretically, a ViT could maintain maximum information density (rank) across its entire depth
(
8

, leading to the assumption that ViTs are "isotropic" architectures.

However, this isotropic assumption is challenged by recent empirical anomalies. Analysis of self-supervised ViTs often reveals a peculiar phenomenon where the feature rank does not stay constant but instead collapses in the middle layers, only to recover in the deeper layers. We term this phenomenon the
Inductive Bottleneck
. This U-shaped profile suggests that despite having the
capacity
to maintain high-dimensional representations, the network
chooses
to compress information mid-computation.

The central question of this paper is:
Is this bottleneck an inherent property of the Self-Attention mechanism, or is it a dynamic reaction to the statistical properties of the training data?

We utilize the Effective Encoding Dimension (EED)

2
Theoretical Framework

2.1
Effective Dimensionality in Deep Learning

To quantify the “true” degrees of freedom utilized by the model, we adopt the concept of
Effective Dimensionality
(
N_{eff}
). In Bayesian deep learning, this metric is often derived from the eigenvalues of the Hessian to measure parameter well-determinedness. We extend this concept to the activation space to quantify representational capacity.

Let
H^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
be the matrix of token embeddings at layer
l
, and
\Sigma^{(l)}
be its empirical covariance matrix. We define the normalized spectral distribution
p^{(l)}
based on the eigenvalues
\Lambda^{(l)}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{D}\}
:

p_{k}^{(l)}=\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(l)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}\lambda_{j}^{(l)}}

(1)

The
Effective Encoding Dimension (EED)
is defined as the exponential of the Shannon entropy of this distribution:

N_{eff}^{(l)}=\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{D}p_{k}^{(l)}\log p_{k}^{(l)}\right)

(2)

This metric provides a robust, continuous measure of rank. An isotropic representation (white noise) yields
N_{eff}\approx D
, while a representation collapsed to a single feature yields
N_{eff}\approx 1
.

2.2
Theoretical Motivation: Generalization via Effective Dimensionality

The “Inductive Bottleneck”—characterized by the spontaneous reduction of effective dimensionality in intermediate layers—aligns closely with established theories on generalization. We interpret this phenomenon through the lens of the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle
(
10
)
. From this perspective, optimizing a deep network is a balancing act between two competing goals: the internal representation
T
must be rich enough to predict the target
Y
(maximizing mutual information
I(T;Y)
), yet compact enough to ignore irrelevant variations in the input
X
(minimizing mutual information
I(T;X)
).

The generalization gap (
\mathcal{G}
), defined as the difference between training and test error, is theoretically bounded by the complexity of the internal representation relative to the number of training samples
M
. For a representation
T
, this bound scales as:

\mathcal{G}\lesssim\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{I(T;X)}}{M}}\right)

(3)

Crucially, in the linear regime of our covariance analysis (assuming Gaussian codebooks), the mutual information
I(T;X)
is upper-bounded by the effective dimensionality of the representation. Specifically,
I(T;X)\approx\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma)
, which is proportional to our Effective Encoding Dimension (EED) metric,
N_{eff}^{(l)}
. Therefore, we propose that the layer-wise generalization bound is controlled by the effective dimension:

\epsilon_{gen}\propto\sqrt{\frac{N_{eff}^{(l)}}{M}}

(4)

This relationship offers a mechanistic explanation for the “U-shaped” profile observed in our results. By aggressively suppressing
N_{eff}
in layers
L_{1}-L_{4}
(e.g., reducing
N_{eff}
to
\sim 30\%
of physical capacity in ViT-Small), the network explicitly tightens the bound in Equation
4
. We hypothesize that the network utilizes “Phantom Dimensions”—physical dimensions with non-zero but spectrally negligible eigenvalues—to facilitate optimization during early training, pruning them as convergence approaches to satisfy this generalization constraint.

2.3
Spectral Entropy as a Measure of Collapse

Measuring Mutual Information
I(X;T)
in high-dimensional spaces is computationally intractable. Instead, we use
Spectral Entropy
as a proxy for the volume of the effective state space.
For a layer
l
, let
H^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
be the matrix of token embeddings. The covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(l)}=\frac{1}{N}(H^{(l)})^{\top}H^{(l)}
captures the second-order statistics of the feature space.
The eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{D}
of
\Sigma^{(l)}
represent the variance along the principal components.
If the representation has "collapsed" to a subspace, many eigenvalues will be close to zero. The Shannon entropy of the normalized eigenvalue distribution
p_{k}=\lambda_{k}/\sum\lambda_{j}
provides a continuous measure of this rank:

S(\Sigma^{(l)})=-\sum_{k=1}^{D}p_{k}\log p_{k}

(5)

This entropy is maximized when the spectrum is flat (isotropy) and minimized when the spectrum is sparse (anisotropy/collapse).

Methodology

3.1
Architecture

We utilize a
ViT-Small
backbone.

•

Patch Size
:
16\times 16
(adapted to
4\times 4
for CIFAR/TinyImageNet to maintain sequence length).

•

Embedding Dimension (
D
)
: 384.

•

Heads
: 6.

•

Layers (
L
)
: 12.

•

MLP Ratio
: 4.

The choice of ViT-Small is deliberate; it provides enough capacity (
D=384
) to observe non-trivial compression dynamics, unlike Tiny (
D=192
) where the dimensions might be fully utilized simply due to lack of capacity.

3.2
Training Objective: DINO

We employ DINO (Self-distillation with NO labels)
(
1
)
, a self-supervised method. DINO avoids the "collapse" (trivial solution) problem of self-supervision by using centering and sharpening of the teacher outputs.
The loss function is the cross-entropy between the student output probability
P_{s}
and the teacher output probability
P_{t}
:

\mathcal{L}_{DINO}=\sum_{x\in\{g_{1},g_{2}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in V\\
x^{\prime}\neq x\end{subarray}}-P_{t}(x^{\prime})\log P_{s}(x)

(6)

where
V
is the set of global and local crops. The teacher weights are an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student weights.
DINO is critical for this study because it has been shown to produce attention maps that segment foreground objects
(
1
)
without supervision. This implies the network structurally isolates "objects" from "background," a process we hypothesize is mechanically implemented via the Inductive Bottleneck.

3.3
Effective Encoding Dimension (EED)

The EED metric
N_{eff}
is calculated as:

N_{eff}^{(l)}=\exp\left(S(\Sigma^{(l)})\right)

(7)

We normalize this by the embedding dimension
D
to get a percentage score:
EED\%=(N_{eff}/D)\times 100
.

4.1.3
The Reconstruction (Late Layers)

In the object-centric datasets, the EED rises sharply in the final layers (
L_{9}-L_{11}
). In our Tiny ImageNet run, Layer 11 recovers to
92.5%
EED. This suggests the model “expands” the compressed semantic features back into the full dimensional space. This contradicts the "Neural Collapse" theory for intermediate layers; collapse happens
before
the final layer in self-supervised ViTs, while the final layer is actually high-dimensional to accommodate the projection head’s contrastive requirements.

4.2
Qualitative Implication of the Bottleneck

The drop in EED at
L_{2}
corresponds structurally to the mixing of tokens via Self-Attention. A low EED implies that the token embeddings have become highly correlated. In terms of attention maps, this likely corresponds to the phase where the [CLS] token attends broadly to the global image context, or where patch tokens attending to the same object synchronize their representations. The "Bottleneck" is the computational moment where the visual field is segmented into coherent semantic regions, discarding the independent variance of individual pixels.

5
Discussion

5.1
The Complexity Hypothesis

Our results strongly support the
“Complexity Hypothesis.”

The depth of the Inductive Bottleneck is inversely proportional to the importance of texture.

•

Texture Tasks
: High EED. The model needs to perform "matching" of high-frequency patterns. Memory capacity is key.

•

Object Tasks
: Low EED (Bottleneck). The model needs to perform "abstraction" of shape and concept. Compression is key.

5.2
Comparative Architectural Analysis

Conventional wisdom suggests that hierarchical architectures like Swin Transformers
(
7
)
perform better on vision tasks because they enforce a bottleneck via merging interacting windows. Our work shows that vanilla ViTs
emulate
this hierarchy.
However, the vanilla ViT is more flexible. A Swin Transformer
must
compress at fixed stages. A ViT
can
compress at
L_{2}
, or
L_{6}
, or not at all (as seen in UC Merced), depending on the data. This "Soft Inductive Bias" might explain the universality of the ViT architecture across modalities (Image, Audio, Point Clouds) compared to the rigid CNN/Swin hierarchies.

5.3
Implications: Active Compression

The bottleneck is an
active compression
mechanism: the model “decides” that reducing dimensionality is the optimal strategy for the data. This mirrors the saddle point optimization dynamics described by
2
, where the network navigates a complex landscape by temporarily simplifying the problem space.

6
Limitations and Future Work

Scale
: Our experiments were limited to ViT-Small. It remains to be seen if ViT-Large or ViT-Huge exhibit the same relative percentages (e.g., 30%) or if they simply add more "Phantom Dimensions" as hypothesized in earlier iterations of this work.

Task Specificity
: We focused on Classification/DINO. It is unclear if Dense Prediction tasks (Segmentation, Detection) would prevent the formation of a bottleneck to preserve spatial fidelity.

Causal Intervention
: While we observe the bottleneck, we have not casually intervened (e.g., by forcing the rank low) to see if it improves performance. Future work should explore "Spectral Pruning" during training.

7
Conclusion

We have identified and analyzed the “Inductive Bottleneck”—a data-driven emergence of representational sparsity in Vision Transformers. We demonstrated that this phenomenon is not an artifact of the architecture, but a learned strategy to deal with compositional complexity.

1.

For object-centric data (CIFAR, TinyImageNet), ViTs learn a U-shaped rank profile, compressing information to
\sim 30\%
of capacity in middle layers.

2.

For texture-centric data (UC Merced), ViTs maintain high rank, operating as maximum-entropy feature banks.

This adaptability explains the success of ViTs: they are not just "isotropic" networks; they are "dynamic hierarchy" networks that build their own bottlenecks on demand.
