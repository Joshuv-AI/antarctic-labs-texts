Drug Critical Limits for Urgent Physician Notification
Authors: Elina Kuang, Yasmeen Ibrahim, Kyle Cheng, Gerald J Kost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical limits represent quantitative decision thresholds for drugs that require immediate clinician notification and potential life‐saving intervention. United States hospitals lack a national standard for drug critical limits. We collected critical limits from 417 US hospitals across all 50 states and Washington, D.C.; of these, 411 maintained drug critical limit lists. We classified hospitals by US Census division and network‐affiliated vs. independent status. We applied non‐parametric statistical analyses, examined critical limits for 111 drugs, and observed significant inter‐institutional variability. Listing frequencies were highest for digoxin 99.8% (410/411), theophylline 72.3% (297/411), lithium 94.9% (390/411), and acetaminophen 86.9% (357/411). Non‐therapeutic measurands also appeared, led by ethanol 47.2% (194/411). Kruskal–Wallis analysis revealed highly significant differences (P < 0.01) across census divisions. Mann–Whitney U analysis comparing network vs. independent hospitals yielded significant differences (P < 0.05) in 16 analytes. Qualitative critical values were listed for volatile alcohols and methotrexate. All digoxin critical limits exceeded levels associated with increased hazard ratios. Only 33.9% of hospitals aligned with consensus guidelines for acetaminophen poisoning. Vancomycin and aminoglycoside critical limits showed wide ranges, overlapping peaks and troughs, and random values inconsistently aligned with peak and troughs. Immunosuppressant critical limits often exceeded thresholds associated with toxicity. Psychotropic drugs, including antiepileptics, tricyclic antidepressants, and lithium, demonstrated variability and misalignment relative to consensus guidelines. Drugs of abuse did not appear in critical notification lists. Results provide hospitals with references that will enhance hospital notification practices and patient safety. We encourage sharing of critical notification lists to foster future research efforts and enhance standards of care.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

This study enables hospitals to evaluate and align their critical limits to consensus guidelines to reduce risks associated with therapeutic drug monitoring. Results provide hospitals with references that can improve notification practices and patient safety by improving the consistency of urgent clinician notification.

Critical limits set the boundaries for clinical thresholds that require immediate clinician notification and potential intervention. 1 , 2 Timely communication of life‐threatening laboratory results facilitates effective clinical decision making and may be life‐saving. The Joint Commission's National Patient Safety Goals (clause 02.03.01) and the College of American Pathologists (checklist 41320–41340) require hospitals and clinical laboratories to establish policies for promptly reporting critical limits. 3 , 4

United States hospitals lack a national standard for drug critical limits. Hospitals independently set their own critical limits based on local policies, clinician expertise, and available literature. 5 , 6 No extensive nationwide analysis of drug critical limits has been published. Therefore, the goals of this study are to report critical limits for therapeutic drugs in all 50 states and Washington D.C. and to provide first comprehensive national reference.

The results enable hospitals to review and compare their practices with peer institutions. This work encourages broader sharing of critical notification lists to support national review and reduction of risks associated with critical limit practices across institutions and across geographic regions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Definitions

A critical limit is an upper or lower numerical threshold of a diagnostic test that, when exceeded or fallen below, indicates a potentially life‐threatening condition and requires immediate clinician notification. Notifications classified as lower priority are listings not requiring immediate clinician notification and typically reported during routine laboratory hours.

For drugs, critical limits represent concentrations associated with serious toxicity or loss of therapeutic effect. A critical value is defined as a qualitative result that also warrants urgent clinician notification. Critical values for drugs are commonly reported using qualitative notification phrases, indicating detection of a substance. An analyte is defined as a substance measured in a laboratory test.

The generally accepted definition for trough concentrations is defined as the measured drug concentration obtained immediately before the next scheduled dose. Peak is defined as the measured drug concentration obtained at a specified time after drug administration. Random is defined as a drug concentration measured at an unspecified time relative to dosing.

Data collection

Critical notification lists were obtained directly from hospitals and through previously established methods using web‐accessible listings. 7 Hospitals were categorized by network affiliation (network or independent) and by the nine US Census Bureau geographic divisions. Notification lists were obtained from 417 hospitals, only six of which (1.4%, 6/411) did not report urgent notifications for drugs. No outliers were removed from the dataset. Pediatric hospitals and hospitals lacking toxicology‐related critical limits were excluded. Lists reporting only lower‐priority thresholds that did not require urgent notification were excluded from the primary analysis but are summarized in Table S1 .

Geographics

Notification lists were obtained from hospitals spanning all 50 states and Washington D.C. Hospitals were categorized into the nine US Census Bureau divisions. Census divisions (states) and percentages of hospitals comprised, respectively: New England (CT, MA, ME, NH, RI, VT) 3.9% (16/411); Middle Atlantic (NJ, NY, PA) 8.0% (33/411); East North Central (IN, IL, MI, OH, WI) 16.8% (69/411); West North Central (IA, MN, MO, NE, KS, ND, SD) 18.2% (75/411); South Atlantic (DE, FL, GA, MD, NC, SC, VA, WV, DC) 17.0% (70/411); East South Central (AL, KY, MS, TN) 6.1% (25/411); West South Central (AR, OK, LA, TX) 7.1% (29/411); Mountain (AZ, CO, UT, ID, NV, NM, MT, WY) 11.4% (47/411); Pacific (AK, CA, OR, WA, HI) 11.4% (47/411).

Artificial intelligence and web searches

Open‐source artificial intelligence (AI), primarily Perplexity and ChatGPT, and Internet search engines (e.g., Google and PubMed) were used to identify notification lists posted online by hospitals and laboratories and accelerate web and literature searches. Notification lists identified on the Web or located through AI‐assisted searches were verified directly with laboratory personnel via email and telephone. Notification lists were also obtained by contacting hospitals, laboratories, and professional personnel in hospitals throughout the United States. Artificial intelligence was used for grammatical editing.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using JASP (version 0.19.3). We assessed normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Q‐Q plots, and histograms. We observed that data exhibited non‐normal distributions and applied non‐parametric statistical analyses. The Mann–Whitney U test evaluated differences between network‐affiliated and independent hospitals and the Kruskal–Wallis test identified differences in geographic divisions. Significance was set at P < 0.05 (*) and considered highly significant at P < 0.01 (**). Critical limits appeared as conventional units. Conversions to molar SI units followed statistical analysis.

Analysis of dispersion

Due to the non‐normal distribution of our data, percent difference and quartile coefficient of dispersion were used to quantify dispersion. The percent difference was calculated as [(maximum critical limit − minimum critical limit)/minimum critical limit] × 100, representing the relative dispersion of reported critical limits across hospitals. 8 This metric reflects the magnitude of variation between the lowest and highest reported thresholds. A percent difference of 0 indicates identical minimum and maximum values, reflecting complete agreement across institutions, whereas larger values indicate increasing overall heterogeneity. However, this measure is sensitive to outliers and may reflect dispersion driven by a small number of hospitals.

To differentiate broad heterogeneity from outlier‐driven dispersion, percent difference was evaluated alongside the quartile coefficient of dispersion (QCD = (75th percentile − 25th percentile)/(75th percentile + 25th percentile)), measuring variability within the central 50% of reported thresholds. QCD was selected over traditional coefficient of variation measures, as it does not assume normality and is resistant to the influence of outliers. A QCD of 0 indicates identical 25th and 75th percentiles, reflecting central consensus, whereas larger values indicate increasing central heterogeneity. To reflect central percent spread, the QCD was multiplied by 100 and presented as a percentage for interpretative purposes.

Ethics

The UC Davis IRB deemed this research exempt (ID 2078118‐1). No consents were required. Critical limit data were collated and results reported anonymously.

RESULTS

Demographics

We obtained drug critical notification lists from 411 hospitals. These hospitals represented all 50 US states and Washington, DC. Classifications, which are not mutually exclusive, comprised 70.3% community hospitals (289/411), 50.1% teaching hospitals (206/411), and 27.7% university hospitals (114/411). Of these hospitals, 56.9% were designated trauma centers (234/411), including 39.7% Level I (93/234), 16.2% Level II (38/234), 24.8% Level III (58/234), and 19.2% Level IV (45/234) centers. Additionally, 46% of hospitals were identified as Centers of Excellence (189/411), and 7.5% were specialized heart centers (31/411).

Critical limits

We identified 103 critical limits comprising therapeutic drugs (89), alcohol (5), toxins (4), and other toxicology measurands (5). No critical limits were listed for drugs of abuse. We grouped measurands into quartiles by overall frequency of hospital reporting. We placed bottom‐quartile measurands, reported by only one or two hospitals, in Table S2 and retained the remainder in the main tables.

Each table reports conventional and SI units, along with the median, range, interquartile values (25th and 75th percentiles), and percent difference of critical limits. Frequently listed drugs

Within cardiopulmonary drugs, digoxin 99.8% (410/411) and theophylline 72.3% (297/411) were most frequently listed, with other listings appearing in < 30% of hospitals (Table 1 ). In psychotropics/antiepileptics, lithium 94.9% (390/411), phenytoin 90.8% (373/411), carbamazepine 87.6% (360/411), phenobarbital 83.7% (344/411), valproic acid 80.3% (330/411), and phenytoin free 39.2% (161/411) were most common, with others appearing in < 20% of hospitals (Table 2 ).

For antimicrobials, vancomycin (trough) was listed by 67.9% (279/411) of hospitals, followed by gentamicin (trough) 57.9% (238/411) and tobramycin (trough) 54.7% (225/411); peak and random listings were listed less frequently. Immunosuppressants were less frequent overall, with cyclosporine 37.5% (154/411), tacrolimus 32.4% (133/411), and sirolimus 17.8% (73/411). For cyclosporine, most listings were unspecified in blood draw timing, although some hospitals explicitly listed peak, trough, or random categories. Acetaminophen 86.9% (357/411) and salicylate 86.9% (357/411) were commonly listed analgesics (Table 3 ).

Hospital notification lists also included non‐therapeutic listings: alcohols, toxins, heavy metals, and other toxicology measurands. Ethanol 47.2% (194/411), ethylene glycol 20.2% (83/411), lead 20% (82/411), and caffeine 18.7% (77/411) were the most frequently listed non‐therapeutic listings, with other listings appearing in < 15% of hospitals (Table 4 ).

Critical values were used by a subset of hospitals as qualitative notifications with phrases such as “any positive,” “positive,” “large positive,” or “phone all results.” These policies appeared most often for alcohols (acetone, ethanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol, and methanol) and for methotrexate (Table 5 ).

Geographic division and network vs. independent hospital analysis

Kruskal–Wallis tests across US Census divisions were highly significant (P < 0.01) for all analytes, indicating division‐level heterogeneity in reported critical limits. The Mann–Whitney U test comparing network and independent hospitals yielded significant differences (P < 0.05) for five out of 103 critical limits: acetone, phenytoin (free), propylene glycol, tobramycin (peak), and valproic (free). Highly significant differences (P < 0.01) emerged in 11 out of 103 limits: amikacin (trough), caffeine, cyclosporine (unspecified), desipramine, ethylene glycol, gentamicin (peak), isopropanol, lead, primidone, quinidine, and theophylline.

Percent difference and quartile coefficient of dispersion

The greatest percent differences were observed in methotrexate (19.900%) and isopropanol (6.700%). Other large percent differences were seen in acetaminophen (2.400%); gentamicin (random) (2.043%) and tobramycin (random) (2.043%); methanol (1.900%) and propylene glycol (1.900%); amikacin (trough) (1.500%); amikacin (peak) (1.150%) and amikacin (random) (1.150%); tobramycin (trough) (1.100%), thiocyanate (1.100%), and pentobarbital (1.100%).

The greatest quartile coefficient of dispersions was observed in propylene glycol (82%). Other large quartile coefficients of dispersion were seen in cyclosporine (peak) (54%), acetaminophen (50%), amikacin (peak) (39%), amikacin (random) (39%), tobramycin (random) (35%), methotrexate (34%), ethylene glycol (33%), isopropanol (33%), methanol (33%), and vancomycin (peak) (33%).

Lower‐priority notification lists

Table S1 identifies the lower‐priority notification lists. Eight unique analytes were recorded: alprazolam, clonazepam, diazepam, nordiazepam, fluoxetine, trazodone, desmethylclomipramine, and desethylamiodarone. These appeared infrequently and expanded the scope of notifications to include selected benzodiazepines and antidepressant‐related measurands.

DISCUSSION

Our study represents the first large‐scale nationwide analysis of critical limits of therapeutic drugs and toxicology analytes in the United States. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) ensures efficacy and minimizes toxicity by maintaining serum concentrations within narrow therapeutic windows. 9 We identified substantial variability in critical limits of therapeutic drugs reported across 411 US hospitals, notably within digoxin, acetaminophen, antimicrobials, and immunosuppressants.

The College of American Pathologists (CAP) has provided valuable insights into clinical chemistry critical limits through its Q‐Probe studies, establishing an important foundation for laboratory quality improvement. 10 However, these efforts primarily focused on general chemistry analytes, leaving therapeutic drug critical limits less explored at a national scale. Our study represents the first comprehensive nationwide analysis of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) critical limits, aiming to strengthen and standardize critical notification practices across US hospitals.

Earlier US surveys were smaller or institution‐specific, and while international work from the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australasia has noted similar variability, no prior analysis has systematically assessed TDM critical limits nationwide. 5 , 6 , 8 , 11 , 12 An independent ad hoc reference compiles reported critical limits but does not specify how these values were derived or validated and therefore complements rather than replaces a national survey. 11 The United Kingdom study found limited consensus, with thresholds often based on clinician opinion. 5 The Canadian pediatric survey explicitly called for a national survey followed by consensus development informed by clinical outcomes. 8 Similarly, the Australasian Association of Clinical Biochemists (AACB) launched an active quality initiative to harmonize critical result management by developing best practice recommendations and a “starter set” of critical limits informed by clinical outcomes and clinician consensus. 6

Digoxin

Digoxin is widely used to treat heart failure and atrial fibrillation but requires careful dosing because of its narrow therapeutic index. 13 Figure 1 compares hospital‐reported critical limits for digoxin with consensus guidelines and evidence‐based thresholds. All reported critical limits (2.0–4.0 ng/mL) exceed the level associated with a 56% increased mortality risk in atrial fibrillation patients (1.2 ng/mL), as well as serum concentrations linked to rising hazard ratios (1.6–2.0 ng/mL). 14 , 15 The Heart Failure Society of America recommends lower target concentrations (0.5–0.9 ng/mL), citing significantly elevated risks at ≥ 1.2 ng/mL and ≥ 1.6 ng/mL. 16 Despite this, many hospitals and guidelines continue to define the therapeutic range as 0.8–2.0 ng/mL. 17 This misalignment suggests that critical thresholds have not kept pace with emerging evidence, possibly because of practical barriers, local variation, or “clinical inertia,” where outdated practices persist despite updated recommendations. 18

Bars indicate: 2022 Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA) consensus therapeutic range (green), concentration associated with neutral hazard ratio (yellow), concentrations associated with increased hazard ratios (red). Hashed black bar denotes hospital‐reported critical limits.

Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen overdose is a leading cause of acute liver failure and is the most reported non‐opioid analgesic in the ToxIC Core Registry. 19 , 20 In 2023, four toxicology associations issued consensus guidelines for acetaminophen poisoning management. 21 Figure 2 shows the distribution of hospital‐reported critical limits compared to those guidelines. The updated Rumack–Matthew nomogram sets the 4‐hour post‐ingestion treatment threshold at 150 μg/mL, with a high‐risk line at 300 μg/mL. Among hospitals, 51.8% (185/357) report thresholds below the treatment line, 33.9% (121/357) match the recommended line, and 14.3% (51/357) exceed it. Elevated thresholds are clinically concerning because delayed N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) can cause irreversible hepatic injury or death. 22

Antimicrobials

Aminoglycosides exhibit concentration‐dependent killing and therefore require close therapeutic monitoring. Antibacterial efficacy is associated with achieving peak concentrations several fold above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the infecting organism, whereas toxicity is linked to sustained elevations in trough concentrations. 23 , 24 Random concentrations have traditionally been used in extended‐interval dosing strategies, where a single timed level obtained several hours after infusion is interpreted using nomograms to guide dosing interval selection. 25 Therapeutic drug monitoring commonly relies on peak and trough measurements to estimate peak exposure and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) using pharmacokinetic or Bayesian methods. 23

Figure 3 displays the overall distribution of aminoglycosides and vancomycin, highlighting the wide ranges and frequent overlap between peak and trough values. Random concentrations appear more closely aligned with peak distributions than with trough distributions.

In 2020, consensus groups published a position paper on the TDM of antimicrobials, stating > 5 mg/L for amikacin and > 1 mg/L for gentamicin/tobramycin trough levels as toxicity thresholds. 24 Other studies have associated nephrotoxicity with > 10 mg/L for amikacin and > 2 mg/L for gentamicin trough levels. 26 Of amikacin listings, 99.3% (152/153) and 44.4% (68/153) exceeded the > 5 mg/L and > 10 mg/L trough thresholds, respectively. Of gentamicin listings, 99.6% (237/238) and 43.3% (103/238) exceeded the > 1 mg/L and > 2 mg/L trough thresholds, respectively. For tobramycin, 99.1% (223/225) of listings exceeded the > 1 mg/L trough threshold.

Vancomycin is one of the most widely used antibiotics in the United States for treating methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and requires TDM to prevent nephrotoxicity. 27 According to an updated 2020 consensus guideline, previously recommended trough concentrations of 15–20 mg/L are now associated with increased nephrotoxicity risk. 28 Patients with trough levels > 15 mg/L demonstrate higher nephrotoxicity rates, with the greatest risk at > 20 mg/L. 29

Of vancomycin listings, 98.6% (275/279) and 71.3% (199/279) of listings exceeded the 15 mg/L and 20 mg/L trough threshold, respectively. Additionally, monitoring of vancomycin peak concentrations is no longer recommended, as these values do not correlate with efficacy or toxicity, offering no added value in guiding therapy. 28 Yet 72% (201/279) of hospitals that reported troughs also reported peaks. This persistence may reflect historical practices or institutional inertia rather than current evidence‐based standards. As TDM shifts toward AUC‐guided approaches, hospitals may benefit from re‐evaluating the utility of peaks.

Immunosuppressants

Tacrolimus, sirolimus, and cyclosporine are vital in transplant and immune modulation therapies but have narrow therapeutic windows. Figure 4 depicts the distributions of immunosuppressants. In 2017, an updated consensus report regarding tacrolimus was issued, stating the risk of acute kidney injury within the first 2 weeks after transplantation was significantly increased for levels > 15 ng/mL and risk of nephrotoxicity significantly increased for levels > 20 ng/mL. 30 Of tacrolimus listings, 88% (117/133) exceeded > 15 ng/mL and 39.8% (53/133) of listings exceeded > 20 ng/mL. Of sirolimus listings, 79.5% (58/73) exceeded > 15 ng/mL, the threshold linked to thrombocytopenia and hyperlipidemia. 31 Of all cyclosporine listings, 26.1% (46/176) exceeded > 500 ng/mL and 5.7% (10/176) exceeded > 1000 ng/mL, levels associated with seizures and coma. 32

Antiepileptics

Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) represent one of the earliest and most established applications of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), with well‐defined therapeutic and toxic concentration ranges developed over decades of clinical use. 33 Older‐generation agents, such as carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin, and valproic acid, demonstrate high listing frequency and low QCDs, reflecting lower inter‐institutional variation (Table 2 ).

However, comparison with recommended critical limits from the 2017 Consensus Guidelines for Therapeutic Drug Monitoring in Neuropsychopharmacology suggests that low variability does not necessarily equate to alignment with expert recommendations. 34 In comparison with the consensus guidelines, 60.9% (227/373) of phenytoin listings exceeded 25 μg/mL; 94.8% (313/330) of valproic acid listings exceeded 120 μg/mL; 69.2% (36/52) of ethosuximide listings exceeded 120 mg/L; and 66% (2/3) of levetiracetam listings exceeded 50 μg/mL. By contrast, only 0.8% (3/360) of carbamazepine listings exceeded 20 μg/mL, and 1.4% (1/72) of primidone listings exceeded 25 μg/mL.

Newer AEDs such as lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, topiramate, and zonisamide were rarely included in hospital critical notification lists (frequency <1%), despite increasing utilization in clinical practice. Newer AEDs are often considered to have more predictable pharmacokinetics and therefore presumed to require less routine monitoring. However, the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) Commission on Therapeutic Strategies emphasizes the clinical significance of interindividual variability across all AEDs. 35

The concept of an “individual therapeutic concentration” is particularly important in situations associated with altered pharmacokinetics, including pregnancy, drug–drug interactions, pathological states, and in children and the elderly. 36 Recent evidence suggests that appropriate therapeutic drug monitoring of newer AEDs provides clinical benefit to guide epilepsy management in real‐world settings. 25 Consideration should be given to evaluating and incorporating evidence‐based monitoring thresholds for newer AEDs into institutional notification policies as their use continues to expand.

Separately, national laboratory collaboration initiatives provide a model for systematic harmonization. The Norwegian harmonization effort demonstrated that coordinated, evidence‐based alignment of AED reference ranges across laboratories is feasible and can reduce discrepancies. 37 Such efforts highlight the value of structured consensus processes and support the development of national‐level harmonization in the United States to promote consistency in critical notification reporting.

Antidepressants

Therapeutic drug monitoring is strongly recommended for tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), as it reduces toxicity risk and is supported by a well‐established concentration–clinical effectiveness relationship. 34 TCA listings occurred at much lower frequencies compared with antiepileptic agents and demonstrated minimal inter‐institutional variability, with a QCD of 0 for all TCAs except desipramine and amitriptyline + nortriptyline (Table 2 ).

In comparison with critical limits recommended by the 2017 Consensus Guidelines for Therapeutic Drug Monitoring in Neuropsychopharmacology, 100% (45/45) of nortriptyline listings exceeded 300 ng/mL; 100% (44/44) of desipramine listings exceeded 300 ng/mL; and 100% (24/24) of imipramine + desipramine listings exceeded 300 ng/mL. By contrast, 4.5% (1/22) of clomipramine + norclomipramine listings exceeded 450 ng/mL; 0% (0/22) of trimipramine listings exceeded 600 ng/mL; and 0% (0/4) of amitriptyline + nortriptyline listings exceeded 300 ng/mL. 34

Lithium

Therapeutic drug monitoring for lithium has become standard of care due to its narrow therapeutic index. 34 Small increases above the therapeutic range are associated with neurologic, renal, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and endocrine toxicity. 37 Within hospital‐reported notification lists, lithium was among the most frequently reported measurands (Table 2 ). In comparison with laboratory alert values recommended by the 2017 Consensus Guidelines for Therapeutic Drug Monitoring in Neuropsychopharmacology, 98.7% (385/390) of listings exceeded 1.2 mmol/L; however, the guidelines also acknowledge that concentrations up to 1.2 mmol/L may be appropriate in acute treatment. 34

Highly significant differences between network and independent hospitals

Several analytes demonstrated highly significant differences in median critical limits between network and independent hospitals. Network hospitals reported higher median values for caffeine, cyclosporine (unspecified), desipramine, isopropanol, primidone, quinidine, theophylline, and lithium. By contrast, independent hospitals reported higher median values for amikacin (trough), ethylene glycol, and lead. The imbalance in sample size between network and independent hospitals may partially contribute to these observed differences and should be considered when interpreting these findings.

While several of these drugs may carry potential clinical implications, the clinical meaningfulness of any specific threshold ultimately depends on local context and clinical judgment. For example, theophylline has a narrow therapeutic range (10–20 μg/mL), with toxicity generally observed at concentrations exceeding 20 μg/mL. 38 The median of network hospitals (20 μg/mL) aligns with guidelines, whereas the critical limit among independent hospitals of (25 μg/mL) exceeds it.

Analytes with large percent differences

Percent difference and quartile coefficient of dispersion (QCD) were used to assess inter‐institutional dispersion in reported critical limits. High percent differences reflect broad ranges between minimum and maximum values, whereas QCD provides insight into dispersion within the central 50% of reporting hospitals.

Methotrexate demonstrated the largest percent difference (19.900%) and a high QCD (34%) (Table 4 ). Qualitative policies such as “call all” and “phone all results” were reported as well, indicating variability not only within critical limits but also in reporting philosophy (Table 5 ). Unlike many analytes with a single toxicity threshold, methotrexate interpretation is highly dependent on clinical context. Low‐dose regimens for autoimmune disease and maintenance therapy are not routinely guided by therapeutic drug monitoring, whereas high‐dose intravenous methotrexate (HD‐MTX) requires structured pharmacokinetic monitoring due to concentration–time dependent toxicity (> 5–10 μmol/L at 24 hours, > 1.0 μmol/L at 48 hours and > 0.1 μmol/L at 72 hours). 39

Isopropanol demonstrated a large percent difference (6.700%) and a high QCD (33%) (Table 4 ). Qualitative policies such as “any” or “call all” were reported (Table 5 ). Most hospitals reported critical limits within the 0–50 mg/dL range, with only a few institutions reporting thresholds at 100 mg/dL, well below the concentrations associated with increased mortality (150–200 mg/dL). 40 A single hospital reported 340 mg/dL, contributing to the large percent difference. Unlike methotrexate, isopropanol toxicity is typically related to acute ingestion. Therefore, variability in reporting philosophy may reflect differences in institutional priorities, whether notification is triggered to identify any confirmed exposure or reserved for concentrations approaching toxicity.

Pentobarbital and thiocyanate were infrequently reported and each demonstrated a percent difference of 1.100% and QCD values of 0 (Tables 2 and 4 ). A QCD of 0 indicates identical 25th and 75th percentiles, reflecting complete agreement among the central 50% of reporting hospitals (10 mg/L for pentobarbital and 50 mg/dL for thiocyanate). The elevated percent differences were driven by values at the upper and lower ends of the reported ranges (5–60 mg/L for pentobarbital; 5–60 mg/dL for thiocyanate), rather than widespread dispersion across institutions.

Limitations

The national database of 411 hospitals represents a subset of the approximately 6039 hospitals in the United States. Additionally, the national database reflects the methods of data collection which limits generalizability to all US hospital settings.

RECOMMENDATIONS

United States hospitals lack a national standard for drug critical limits. Hospitals independently set their own critical limits based on local policies, clinician expertise, and available literature. Collaborative national or regional laboratory initiatives, such as the harmonization efforts for antiepileptic critical limits in Norway, may serve as practical models for developing consensus‐based critical limits through coordinated expert review and inter‐institutional alignment.

Until national guidelines for drug‐specific critical limits are established, hospitals should align decision thresholds with evidence‐based guidelines to reduce variability and reduce patient risk (Table 6 ). Institutions may periodically benchmark their critical limits against multicenter datasets, including the present analysis, to identify outliers and promote harmonization.

Hospitals are encouraged to maintain transparent notification policies and to provide physician education regarding critical notification protocol. Institutional quality improvement initiatives and professional forums may further support awareness and alignment across health systems.

Incorporation of validated multicenter datasets into AI knowledge bases may improve consistency of information retrieval at the point of care. In this capacity, AI may serve as a dissemination tool for peer‐reviewed data rather than replacing clinical judgment.

CONCLUSION

This study provides a large‐scale, national analysis of hospital notification lists for therapeutic drugs and other toxicology critical limits in the United States. The results reveal the scope and diversity of drug‐specific thresholds used across hospitals and identify key therapeutic areas where variability may affect patient care. Percent difference analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity across multiple therapeutic categories, with marked dispersion in methotrexate, volatile alcohols, acetaminophen, and aminoglycoside limits. Evaluation using the quartile coefficient of dispersion (QCD) provided additional insight into central agreement, distinguishing analytes with narrow interquartile consensus from those with broader central spread.

Several findings raise clinical considerations. All digoxin critical limits exceeded concentrations associated with increased mortality risk. Only one‐third of hospitals aligned with the accepted Rumack–Matthew nomogram for acetaminophen. Aminoglycoside and vancomycin limits frequently exceeded concentrations linked to nephrotoxicity, and peak reporting persisted despite evolving AUC‐based guidance. Immunosuppressant limits often surpassed toxicity‐associated thresholds. Drugs of abuse did not appear on critical notification lists.

Among psychotropics, older antiepileptics demonstrated high listing frequency and low QCDs, yet concordance with consensus laboratory alert concentrations varied by drug. Newer antiepileptics were rarely included despite documented interindividual pharmacokinetic variability. Tricyclic antidepressants appeared infrequently and showed minimal dispersion, although alignment with consensus alert concentrations differed across individual TCAs. Lithium was one of the most frequently reported measurands, with most hospital limits exceeding evidence‐based consensus guidelines.
