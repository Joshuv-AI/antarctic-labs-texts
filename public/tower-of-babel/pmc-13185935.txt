Preparation and characterization of shrimp shell powder enhanced peach gum/sodium alginate ternary composite films
Authors: Lin Wang, Hongqin Guo, Binyu Wang, Dongjing Jiang, Weifei Wang, Shasha Li, Xiangyang Yuan, Wei Wang, Hejun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study used shrimp shell powder (SSP) as a biomass filler for peach gum/sodium alginate (PG/SA) composite matrix to prepare PG/SA/SSP ternary composite films with enhanced performance. The effects of SSP addition (0–10 wt%) on structure, thermal stability, mechanical behavior, barrier performance, and water susceptibility of the films were investigated. SSP incorporation enhanced the mechanical strength and hydrophobicity of the films through mechanisms such as Ca2+ ion cross-linking, hydrogen bonding, and physical filling. At 6 wt% SSP, the film exhibited optimal overall performance: its TS increased by 55%, WVP and OP decreased by about 60% and 67%, along with good UV resistance and thermal stability. Microscopy revealed uniform SSP dispersion, enhancing physical crosslinking density and compactness. Applied to fresh-cut pears, 6% SSP/PG/SA film reduced weight loss to 10.33%, suppressed browning, and delayed TSS and pH fluctuations. This strategy valorizes shrimp shell waste and advances high-performance biodegradable food packaging.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of deepening global plastic pollution control and sustainable development concepts, the development of sustainable, biodegradable packaging constitutes a critical research frontier at the intersection of food science and materials engineering. (Kong et al., 2025). Compared with traditional petroleum-based plastics, bio-based polymer materials, derived from renewable resources, exhibit good biocompatibility and degradability, demonstrating significant application potential (Bukhari et al., 2023; Majumder et al., 2024). Among them, natural polysaccharides have attracted considerable attention due to their wide availability, safety, non-toxicity, and ease of film formation (Xue et al., 2025). Peach gum, a secretion from peach trees and other Rosaceae plants, is a natural hydrophilic colloid rich in polysaccharides. It possesses good film-forming ability, adhesion, and certain biological activities, rendering it highly suitable for use in the realm of food coatings and packaging. (Yin et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2022). On the other hand, sodium alginate (NaAlg), a linear anionic polysaccharide extracted from brown algae, has been widely used in the preparation of edible films and active packaging materials due to its excellent film-forming properties, gel characteristics, and cross-linking ability with polyvalent cations (Köhn et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2025). Blending peach gum with sodium alginate is expected to synergistically leverage the advantages of both, constructing a composite film matrix with more balanced performance, thereby providing a material foundation for the development of novel green packaging (S. Wang et al., 2023).

However, single peach gum or sodium alginate films generally suffer from defects such as insufficient mechanical strength, poor water resistance, and limited functionality, which restrict their practical applications (Liu et al., 2024; Mortalò et al., 2023). Constructing a binary composite system can improve performance to some extent, but the mechanical and barrier properties still struggle to meet the demands for high-strength, high-barrier packaging (Lan et al., 2018). Common enhancement strategies currently include introducing nanofillers (such as nanocellulose, montmorillonite), synthetic polymers, or chemical cross-linking (Karnwal et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). Although these methods can effectively enhance performance, they often come with issues such as increased costs, process complexity, or the introduction of non-natural chemical components, which conflict with the concept of green packaging (Liao et al., 2021; Magnin et al., 2024). Therefore, exploring a green enhancement strategy that is naturally sourced, low-cost, and capable of simultaneously enhancing the mechanical features and functional attributes of the matrix is of substantial practical significance.

With the rapid development of the global aquatic product processing industry, approximately 6 million tons of shrimp shell waste are generated annually from shrimp processing worldwide. These wastes are typically landfilled or discarded directly, not only causing severe environmental burdens but also representing a significant waste of biomass resources (Mathew et al., 2020; Pakizeh et al., 2021). The resource utilization of agricultural and aquatic processing waste, transforming it into high-value-added materials, has become an important pathway toward achieving a circular economy (Nata et al., 2020). Shrimp shells are the main byproduct of shrimp processing, rich in functional components such as chitin (approximately 20–30%), calcium carbonate (approximately 30–50%), and proteins (Rodde et al., 2008; Tkaczewska et al., 2023). The raw material cost of shrimp shell powder (SSP) is extremely low, and it can be obtained through simple cleaning, drying, and crushing processes. Its processing energy consumption is far lower than the chemical extraction process for chitin/chitosan. By replacing purified chitin with SSP, the production cost of composite materials is significantly reduced, demonstrating excellent potential for industrial scale-up and economic feasibility. SSP, produced through simple processing, is not only a natural mineral-organic composite filler but also a potential multifunctional enhancer (Su et al., 2023). Among its components, chitin and its deacetylated derivative chitosan contain abundant amino groups that can interact electrostatically with the carboxyl groups in peach gum or sodium alginate, strengthening interfacial bonding. The calcium carbonate component can slowly release calcium ions (Ca2+) in an acidic microenvironment, forming ionic bridges through ion cross-linking with peach gum and sodium alginate molecules, thereby creating a denser structural network (Jiménez-Saelices et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2022). Additionally, chitin/chitosan and shrimp shell proteins themselves possess certain antibacterial and antioxidant activities (Wen & Liao, 2025). Therefore, introducing SSP into the peach gum/sodium alginate system is expected to comprehensively enhance the mechanical strength, barrier properties, and active functions of the composite film through multiple mechanisms such as physical filling, ion cross-linking, and polyelectrolyte complexation. This represents a feasible approach for the high-value utilization of aquatic waste and the promotion of a circular economy (Fang et al., 2024).

Although previous studies have utilized purified single components such as chitin, chitosan, or calcium carbonate for the enhancement and modification of polysaccharide films, research on the direct use of unpurified natural SSP as an organic-inorganic composite bio-filler remains scarce. Compared to purified components, SSP retains the natural synergistic structure of chitin, proteins, calcium carbonate, and trace astaxanthin in shrimp shells, avoiding solvent consumption and component loss during chemical extraction processes. This offers significant advantages in terms of cost, process greenness, and multifunctionality. However, there is currently a lack of systematic elucidation of the multiple enhancement mechanisms of SSP in the peach gum/sodium alginate (PG/SA) composite system. In particular, the synergistic regulation of the condensed state structure and properties of the film by the in-situ release of Ca2+ from SSP, the interfacial hydrogen bond network between amino/hydroxyl groups and the polysaccharide matrix, and the natural organic-inorganic hybrid structure remains unclear.

Therefore, this study employs unpurified SSP as a biomass reinforcement filler and peach gum and sodium alginate as composite matrices to prepare a series of ternary composite films using an environmentally friendly casting method. The effects of SSP addition (0–10 wt%) on the microstructure, crystal structure, thermal stability, mechanical properties, barrier properties, and water sensitivity of the composite films are systematically investigated. The interaction mechanisms among the components are elucidated using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and other techniques, and their potential as active food packaging materials is evaluated. This study not only provides a new technical pathway and theoretical basis for the low-energy, full-component, high-value utilization of aquatic processing waste but also offers theoretical and technical references for the development of novel green packaging materials with excellent comprehensive properties.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Peach gum polysaccharide (BR) was obtained from Xi'an Youlanda Biotechnology Corporation (Shanxi, China), sodium alginate (SA, analytical grade) was purchased from Chengdu Jinshan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China), and sorbitol was purchased from Chengdu Runze Local Chemical Company (Sichuan, China). Potassium bromide (KBr, spectroscopic grade) and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were both purchased from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China); all other chemicals used were of analytical grade and purchased from commercial suppliers.

Preparation of SSP

Shrimp shells, as waste from aquatic product processing, were collected and first thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove attached impurities. The cleaned shrimp shells were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h until a constant weight was achieved. The dried shrimp shells were ground using a pulverizing grinder and sieved through a 300-mesh standard sieve. The resulting fine powder was stored in a sealed desiccator at room temperature to prevent moisture absorption before use.

Preparation of sodium alginate/peach gum polysaccharide composite films with different mass fractions of SSP

The preparation method for the composite films was adapted from previously reported methods and preliminary experimental results with appropriate modifications (Shi et al., 2024). First, 4.2 g of peach gum polysaccharide and 1.8 g of sorbitol were weighed and dissolved in 40 mL of distilled water to form a homogeneous solution, which was set aside. Then, 1.8 g of sodium alginate was completely dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water at 80 °C. The peach gum polysaccharide solution and sodium alginate solution were mixed, and the combined solution was continuously stirred on a constant-temperature magnetic stirrer at 60 °C and 400 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, SSP with different quality scores (0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, 10% w/w, relative to the total solid content) was added to the peach gum/sodium alginate matrix. The mixed solution was homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer (FJ300-FJ) at 12,000 rpm for 20 min to ensure uniform dispersion of SSP in the film-forming solution. To eliminate residual bubbles, the homogenized film-forming solution was degassed under vacuum for 20 min. During this process, the container was placed in an ultrasonic bath to facilitate bubble removal. Subsequently, the bubble-free film-forming solution was poured onto a horizontal glass film-forming plate and dried it in a constant temperature and humidity chamber at 25 °C and 50% relative humidity for 48 h. Finally, the dried, uniform films were carefully peeled from the plate and stored in a desiccator at room temperature for further characterization.

Characterization of SSP and peach gum polysaccharide composite films

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR-8400S, Shimadzu, Japan) was employed to analyze the chemical structures of SSP and the composite films. SSP samples were mixed with potassium bromide powder at a ratio of 1:100, ground, and pressed into translucent pellets (X. Yu et al., 2022). Peach gum polysaccharide composite film samples were directly analyzed using an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory equipped with a Zn/Se crystal. The spectra were collected at 25 °C employing the FTIR-8400S spectrometer, covering the range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 accumulated scans. (Zolfaghari et al., 2024).

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction was employed to characterize the crystallinity and crystal architecture of SSP and film samples using a diffractometer (DX-2700B, China). Measure using CuK alpha radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm) generated at 40 kV and 30 mA. The diffraction pattern is collected within the 2 θ range of 5° to 80°, with a step size of 0.04° and a counting time of 0.2 s per step. (Aberoumand & Hoseinian, 2024).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analyzer (Netzsch TG209F3) can determine the thermogravimetric curve (TGA) to evaluate the thermal stability of SSP and thin film samples. For each measurement, roughly 5–15 mg of the sample was transferred to an aluminum crucible and heated it from 25 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under a N₂ flow of 25 mL/min, with continuous mass monitoring for thermal stability evaluation. (Huibin Wu et al., 2025).

Scanning Electron microscopy (SEM)

The field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, HITACHI SU8010, Japan) was used to characterize the surface and cross-sectional morphologies of SSP and thin film samples. Samples were mounted on clean silicon wafers or aluminum stubs, and a gold layer was sputtered at an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV using an ion sputtering instrument to enhance electrical conductivity. Representative regions were first located at low magnification, followed by detailed imaging with a scale bar of 200 nm. (X. Wu et al., 2024).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was performed using a DSC instrument (JCR-4, Mettler Toledo) following the method described by Marilia et al. (Horn et al., 2023). Approximately 6 mg of the sample was placed in a sealed aluminum crucible. The instrument was operated under a protective N₂ atmosphere, with a temperature range of 30–250 °C and a heating rate of 10 °C/min. By analyzing the DSC curve, the Tg and melting peak characteristics of the sample were determined, and the correlation between component changes and thermal properties of the material was explored.

Ultraviolet and opacity

The optical properties of the films, including light transmittance and UV barrier performance, were assessed. Lin et al. (Lin et al., 2020) measured the light transmittance of the film in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-2800) to evaluate and analyze the opacity and UV shielding ability of the films. The film was cut into rectangular samples (10 × 40 mm), with five replicate strips prepared for each group. The samples were then fixed onto the surface of a quartz cuvette, and spectral scanning was performed at a sampling interval of 1 nm to obtain the corresponding light transmittance curve. The opacity of the film can be calculated according to formula (1):(1)Opacity=A600/d

Where: A600 is the absorbance measurement value of the film at a wavelength of 600 nm, which is obtained by a spectrophotometer; D represents the thickness of the film, measured in millimeters (mm).

Thickness

The thickness measurement method was adapted from the approach described by Wang et al. (H. Wang, Liu, et al., 2022) with appropriate modifications. A digital micrometer was used to randomly select points on the tested film for measurement. For each film sample, measurements were taken at multiple distinct positions, and the average value was calculated from a total of five data points, expressed in μm.

Color difference

The color change of the film was detected using a high-precision colorimeter (3nh, NR110, China), with a whiteboard as the reference standard (Abedinia et al., 2021). The color change of the film was evaluated using three parameters: L* (brightness), a* (red/green), and b* (yellow/blue). Considering the opaque properties of peach gum polysaccharide composite film, all measurements were conducted using a standard white calibration plate (L0 = 99.48, a0 = −0.06, and b0 = −0.17) as the testing background. Each film was placed on the standard plate and measured five times. The total color difference ΔE was calculated using formula (2):(2)∆E=L∗−L02+a∗−a02+b∗−b02

The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EB) of the composite films were measured following the method of Wang et al. (L. Wang et al., 2024) with modifications. Water contact angle

The composite film's water contact angle was determined with a contact angle measuring instrument under static droplet conditions. A drop of distilled water was placed on the horizontal plane of the film, forming a three-phase boundary between the liquid and the composite film. The intersection angle between the two tangent lines of the gas-liquid interface and the solid-liquid interface is the Wetting Angle, usually denoted by the symbol θ and measured in degrees (°) (Zou et al., 2025).

Water absorption test

The determination of film water absorption was carried out following the method described by Jeroen et al. (van den Brandhof et al., 2026). Prepared film samples (3 cm × 3 cm) were positioned in weighing dishes and oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h before being precisely weighed (W0). They were subsequently stored in a sealed desiccator maintained at 25 ± 2 °C and 90 ± 2% relative humidity. Weigh at 0, 30, 90, 150, 210, 270, 300, 480, 600, and 720 min (Wt). The water absorption rate of the film at time t is calculated using formula (7):(7)WA=Wt−W0W0×100%

Where W0 is the weight of the film before water absorption, and Wt is the weight of the film after the corresponding absorption time. All tests were performed in triplicate.

Water vapor permeability (WVP) and oxygen permeability (OP)

Following the method of Li et al. (M. Li et al., 2025) with appropriate modifications, the water vapor permeability (WVP) of the films was determined using the desiccant method. Silica gel was dried in an oven at 10 °C until constant weight, and 30 g of dried silica gel was weighed into a glass bottle. The test membrane was mounted onto the mouth of a glass bottle and secured with a rubber ring to ensure an airtight seal. After obtaining its initial weight, the assembly was placed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (25 ± 2 °C, 90 ± 2% RH) for a continuous period of 48 h. After 48 h, the weight of the glass bottle was measured again. Each sample group was tested at least three times, and the average value was used to calculate the WVP. The WVP was calculated using the following formula (8):(8)WVP=ΔMxΔPAt

Where: ΔM—mass increase of the glass bottle within time t, g; x—film thickness, μm; ΔP—water vapor pressure difference on both sides of the film at 25 °C, Pa; A—effective area for water vapor transmission through the film, cm2; t—testing time, s.

Referring to the method of Zhong et al. (Zhong et al., 2025), the oxygen barrier property of the film was evaluated using a deoxidant adsorption method. A 3.0 g portion of deoxidant was placed at the bottom of a glass bottle, and the film sample was positioned over the bottle opening, secured with a rubber ring to achieve sealing. After weighing the initial mass, place it in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity: 75 ± 2%) and let it stabilize for 48 h before removing and weighing the glass bottle. Each specimen group underwent no fewer than three repeated trials, and the mean was derived from the obtained data. OP was calculated using the following formula (9):(9)OP=ΔMxΔPAt

In the formula: ΔM—quantity of oxygen taken up by the oxygen scavengerr, g; x—film thickness, μm; ΔP—partial pressure difference of O2 on both sides of the film, Pa; A—active oxygen permeation area of the film, expressed in m2; t—duration of the test, measured in days.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 27 software. Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and differences between means were considered statistically significant at a threshold of p < 0.05. Charts were plotted using Origin 2024 software.

Results and discussion

Characterization of SSP

Particle size and microscopic morphology of SSP

The particle size distribution and microscopic morphology of SSP particles are shown in Fig. 1. The average particle size of SSP is 8.37 ± 0.04 μm, which is similar to the test results reported by Li et al. (T. Li et al., 2024). The particle size test results indicate that SSP is a micron-sized powder, which is beneficial for its interfacial role and performance consistency as a bio-filler in composite material applications. As shown in the SEM image in Fig. 1B, the morphology of SSP is irregular and porous fragments, which is due to mechanical crushing resulting in irregular shapes and small particle sizes of SSP particles. Furthermore, the surface of SSP particles is rough and still retains the natural micropores or layered structure of shrimp shells. This characteristic helps improve wettability and promotes interaction with the interfacial region, thereby enhancing the interfacial bonding strength with the polymer matrix. SSP differs significantly from conventional inorganic fillers; its main components are chitin and calcium carbonate, and it contains approximately 20–30% organic components, such as proteins and astaxanthin (Zhu et al., 2025). These natural organic components can form a flexible interfacial bonding layer between calcium carbonate particles and the polymer matrix, significantly enhancing the compatibility and stress transfer efficiency at the two-phase interface, thereby improving the mechanical performance and functional properties of the composite system.(Jain et al., 2025).Fig. 1Particle size distribution (A) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) morphology of SSP (B).Fig. 1

XRD analysis of SSP

The phase composition and crystallization characteristics of SSP were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), with the XRD pattern shown in Fig. 2B. The diffraction pattern indicates that SSP primarily consists of highly crystalline calcium carbonate and a partially crystalline organic phase. The sharpest and strongest diffraction peak in the spectrum appears at 2θ = 29.58°, corresponding to the (104) crystal plane of the calcite form of calcium carbonate. The remaining diffraction peaks at multiple positions, such as 2θ = 36.1°, 39.66°, 43.38°, 47.0°, and 48.72°, are all attributed to diffraction from different crystal planes of the calcite form of calcium carbonate (; Han et al., 2024). These sharp and continuous diffraction peaks confirm that the calcium carbonate in SSP exists in a highly ordered calcite morphology with good crystallinity. A broad and diffuse diffraction peak is observed near 2θ ≈ 20.15°, which is characteristic of the (110) crystal plane of chitin. The relatively broad peak shape suggests that the chitin component has low crystallinity or small grain size, a phenomenon consistent with the structural characteristics of biogenic chitin (Lv et al., 2023). This result is consistent with the diffraction peak position of the 110 crystal plane of chitin reported by Cai et al. (Cai et al., 2025). The XRD results corroborate the FTIR analysis, collectively confirming that SSP is a binary composite system composed of well-crystalline inorganic calcite and weakly crystalline organic biopolymers. This unique microstructure enables it to exhibit excellent reinforcement effects in composite materials.

Thermal stability analysis of SSP

The thermal degradation behavior of SSP under nitrogen atmosphere was examined via thermogravimetry (TGA) along with its derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles. The outcomes are presented in Fig. 2C and D. The thermal degradation process of SSP exhibits distinct three-stage characteristics, fully demonstrating the stepwise decomposition patterns of its organic and inorganic components. The first stage, from 30 to 163 °C, shows a slow weight loss of approximately 8.85%, with a corresponding small peak in the DTG curve near 65.39 °C, primarily attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water and some bound water on the surface of SSP particles. The second stage, from 163 to 625 °C, is the main weight loss stage, with a weight loss rate as high as approximately 40.82%. In this stage, the DTG curve shows a significant exothermic peak near 305.86 °C, corresponding to the complex decomposition process of organic components. The decomposition in this temperature range mainly arises from the thermal degradation of lipids, small-molecule proteins, and low-molecular-weight organic compounds, as well as the complete thermal cracking and carbonization of high-molecular-weight organic components such as the chitin backbone and residual proteins (Stefanowska et al., 2024). These results are consistent with the study by Cai et al. (Cai et al., 2025), who reported that the weight loss of chitin between 200 and 600 °C is due to the deacetylation of the main chain acetyl groups, the cleavage of glycosidic bonds, and the carbonization of the polysaccharide backbone. The third stage, from 625 to 800 °C, shows a weight loss of approximately 12.83%, with a DTG peak at 693.66 °C. The weight loss at this stage is the inorganic decomposition of calcium carbonate. CaCO3 decomposing into CaO and releasing CO2. The theoretical weight loss rate for the complete decomposition of CaCO3 is 44%, while the experimental value is lower, indicating that the residual carbon generated from the decomposition of organic components in the second stage may encapsulate or interact with the inorganic components.

Thermogravimetric analysis confirms that the SSP material contains both organic and inorganic components. The material exhibits excellent thermal stability below 165 °C, a characteristic that enables it to be compatible with the processing conditions of most common thermoplastic polymers, providing important experimental evidence for its application as a bio-based filler.

Characterization of peach gum composite films

Color analysis of the films

This study prepared PG/SA composite films with different mass fractions of SSP, and their optical properties were quantitatively analyzed using a camera and a colorimeter. The results are shown in Fig. 3. Fig. 3A clearly presents the physical appearance of PG/SA composite films with different SSP contents (0–10 wt%). The visual appearance of the films is primarily influenced by the interaction between the matrix material and the filler. The results indicate that as the mass fraction of SSP increases, the composite films gradually exhibit visual opacity, and the yellow tone significantly deepens (Fig. 3A). This phenomenon is explained by the principle of SSP's inherent color and the inherent yellow of PG. Chromaticity analysis further shows that the brightness parameter L* value continuously decreases with increasing SSP addition. This change is mainly due to the combined effect of SSP's yellow tone and the peach gum/sodium alginate matrix, resulting in a deepening of the overall film tone and an enhanced yellow perception, which reduces the film's brightness. This confirms the trend of increasing overall yellowness in the films (J. Yu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3B, the redness value a* significantly increased (modified for issue one), while the yellowness value b* exhibited a non-monotonic change, initially decreasing and then rising, indicating that the introduction of SSP simultaneously enhanced the color intensity of the film in the red-yellow spectral region. The yellow-blue value (b*) showed a trend of slight initial decline followed by an increase, suggesting that at low addition levels, SSP may have slightly altered the color development characteristics of the PG/SA matrix. As shown in Fig. 3C, with the increase in SSP content, the overall color difference ΔE of the composite film also increased, indicating that at high addition levels, its own yellow contribution became dominant (Khan et al., 2024). These changes in color parameters indicate that the addition of SSP not only affects the transparency of the films but also significantly alters their surface chromaticity characteristics. Microstructure analysis

This research utilized scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine the surface and cross-sectional micromorphology of peach gum polysaccharide/sodium alginate (PG/SA) composite films with different SSP addition amounts, and analyzed the distribution state of SSP within the composite film matrix and its impact on the film's microstructure. The results are shown in Fig. 4A (surface) and Fig. 4B (cross-section). The surface of the PG/SA composite film without SSP was overall relatively dense with low roughness, and the cross-section exhibited a uniform and continuous morphology without obvious phase separation, cracks, or pores, indicating good compatibility between peach gum polysaccharide and sodium alginate, which together formed a structurally intact film matrix. There were slight granular substances locally on the surface, speculated to be caused by local aggregation of polymer chains during the film-forming process. The surface roughness of the film with a 6% SSP addition increased, and slightly dispersed protrusions appeared. Cross-sectional images showed that SSP particles were relatively uniformly embedded in the PG/SA matrix with tight interfacial contact, and no significant agglomeration or interfacial delamination was observed. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

The effect of different SSP addition levels on the molecular structure and intermolecular interactions of peach gum polysaccharide/sodium alginate (PG/SA) composite films was analyzed using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, and the results are shown in Fig. 5A. Changes in the spectral characteristic peaks, such as shifts, intensity, and peak shape, intuitively reflect the evolution of hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, and molecular conformations within the film. The broad, strong peak around cm−1 arises from O–H/N–H stretching, attributed to the abundant hydroxyl groups in the film-forming materials (peach gum polysaccharide, sodium alginate, sorbitol) and the amino groups in SSP. As the SSP addition increases, the absorption peak gradually shifts to lower wavenumbers and its intensity increases, this reveals that a new hydrogen-bond network is formed between the amino groups on the SSP surface and the hydrogen bond network on the molecular chains of peach gum polysaccharide and sodium alginate. This interaction enhances intermolecular cross-linking, thereby affecting the film's compactness and mechanical properties. The mechanism diagram of the interactions between SSP and the components of the film-forming materials is illustrated in Fig. 5B. The spectral peaks in the vicinity of 2885 cm−1 are linked to the stretching vibration of the C—H bond. With increasing SSP addition, this peak undergoes a slight shift, indicating that the introduction of SSP subtly affects the local chemical environment and conformational order of the polysaccharide chains (Ali et al., 2024). The absorption peak near 1607 cm−1 primarily corresponds to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of –COO− in peach gum polysaccharides. With the addition of SSP, the intensity of this peak weakens and gradually shifts to higher wavenumbers, indicating that Ca2+ in SSP may form ionic complexes with the –COO− groups. This demonstrates that Ca2+ acts as a bridging agent, forming ionic sacrificial bonds and chemical cross-linking networks within the system. Additionally, strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding may also be present. Together, these interactions alter the vibrational modes of the carboxylate groups, thereby reducing the peak intensity in this band (J. Li et al., 2018). The absorption peak at 1428 cm−1 significantly increases with higher SSP content. This peak can be attributed to the characteristic vibrations of CO32− in SSP, and its intensity change directly reflects the relative content of SSP in the composite film. The absorption peaks near 1154, 1078, and 1012 cm−1 correspond to C–O–C, C–O–H, and C—O stretching vibrations, respectively. The positions and shapes of these peaks remain relatively stable after SSP addition, indicating that the introduction of SSP does not significantly alter the main covalent structure of the polysaccharide backbone. The above results demonstrate that the addition of SSP primarily combines with the peach gum polysaccharide/sodium alginate matrix through hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions. Hydrogen bond formation dominates the intermolecular cross-linking, while the ionic interaction between Ca2+ and –COO− further strengthens the network structure. Light transmittance and opacity

The optical properties of films, particularly opacity and ultraviolet (UV) barrier performance, are key indicators for evaluating their potential in food packaging applications. Ultraviolet radiation can induce photo-oxidation of lipids in food, accelerating nutrient degradation and quality deterioration. This study measured the transmittance curves of pure PG/SA film and SSP composite film with different ratios in the wavelength range of 200–1000 nm using UV visible spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 5C. The results indicate that the introduction of SSP significantly reduced the light transmittance of the films across the entire wavelength range, with a particularly pronounced shielding effect in the ultraviolet region. This enhanced light shielding effect is primarily attributed to multiple light scattering and absorption caused by SSP particles in the matrix, as well as the densified structure resulting from intermolecular interactions between SSP and PG/SA (Priyadharshee & Preetha, 2023). Good UV barrier performance can effectively prevent oxidation and deterioration of packaged items due to light exposure, thereby extending the shelf life of food (Wang, Yang, et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 5D, the film thickness gradually increased with the addition of SSP, mainly due to the increased solid content in the film caused by the incorporation of SSP. Simultaneously, the opacity of the films also increased with higher SSP concentrations, which is closely related to the dispersion of SSP as insoluble particles in the matrix. SSP particles can act as light scattering centers at visible light wavelengths, hindering light transmission through reflection and scattering, thereby enhancing the opacity of the films (GÜRdal, 2022). This result is connected with the aforementioned color difference analysis. Therefore, the addition of SSP can effectively enhance the shielding ability of PG/SA composite films in both the ultraviolet and visible light regions, endowing the films with superior light barrier properties. This has positive implications for delaying the oxidation and deterioration of light-sensitive foods during storage and maintaining their nutritional and sensory qualities (Zhao et al., 2025).

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the film are one of the core indicators for evaluating its feasibility as a food packaging material. The stress-strain curves and mechanical properties of composite films with different SSP addition amounts are shown in Fig. 6A and B. The tensile strength and elongation at break of the pure PG/SA film were 5.50 ± 0.48 MPa and 7.29 ± 0.48%, respectively. When the SSP addition amount increased to 6%, the TS of the composite film significantly increased to 8.55 ± 0.53 MPa, an improvement of approximately 55%, and the EAB increased to 14.36 ± 0.85%, indicating that the addition of SSP significantly enhanced the load-bearing capacity and structural integrity of the film, while also noticeably improving its flexibility and ductility. This enhancement effect can be attributed to the Ca2+ in SSP particles, which can undergo ionic cross-linking with the –COO− groups on the peach gum polysaccharide chains, forming a more coherent and flexible three-dimensional network (B. Jiang et al., 2018). This ionic bond acts as a sacrificial bond, which preferentially breaks under stress to dissipate energy, thereby enhancing strength while granting the material better ductility, similar to the mechanism studied by Ren et al. (Ren et al., 2022) in the pectin/calcium ion cross-linking system. Secondly, the abundant amino groups on the surface of SSP form a hydrogen bond network with the hydroxyl groups on the molecular chains of the polysaccharide matrix, further enhancing interfacial interactions. Additionally, when the SSP content is low, it exhibits good dispersibility, and an appropriate amount of SSP particles can effectively fill the molecular gaps between the skeletons in the PG/SA matrix, improving the compactness of the film structure (Kasmuri & Muhammad Abu Zait, 2018; Soufiani et al., 2025). However, when the SSP addition amount continued to increase to 10%, both TS and EAB decreased. This may be due to the uneven dispersion and agglomeration of excessive fillers in the film-forming matrix, leading to increased stress concentration points and uneven fracture points, which disrupt the continuity and compactness of the film structure, ultimately resulting in the deterioration of mechanical properties (Vonnie et al., 2022). This phenomenon is consistent with the observed agglomeration of SSP and increased porosity in SEM images, further confirming the critical influence of filler dispersibility and interfacial bonding state on the final mechanical behavior of the composite film. Studies have shown that the addition of SSP can simultaneously improve the tensile properties and ductility of the material within a certain range, achieving an ideal balance between strength and toughness. This is because SSP, through multiple mechanisms such as ionic cross-linking, hydrogen bond reinforcement, and structural filling, synergistically optimizes the molecular arrangement and motion characteristics in the film structure, thereby effectively improving the mechanical properties of the PG/SA composite film. When the SSP concentration exceeds the optimal range, SSP is prone to agglomeration, and its compatibility with the polysaccharide matrix decreases, leading to the formation of an uneven structure and defects within the film, ultimately reducing the mechanical properties of the film material.Fig. 6Tensile stress (σ)-strain (ε) relationship (A) and mechanical performance indicators (B) of the peach gum composite film.Fig. 6

Thermal stability

Thermal stability is a key factor in evaluating food packaging films. The thermal stability of pure PG/SA and PG/SA/SSP composite films was systematically evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), with the results shown in Fig. 7. The TGA and DTG curves of the films are shown in Fig. 7A and B, respectively. All film samples exhibited similar thermal decomposition processes, with three stages of weight loss. The first stage, from 30 to 230 °C, the quality loss is mainly due to the evaporation of free water and bound water in the film. The second stage, from 230 to 360 °C, involved weight loss likely due to the decomposition of small-molecule additives (such as sorbitol), the cleavage of glycosidic bonds, and the degradation of components such as residual proteins in SSP (Santu et al., 2020). The third stage, from 360 to 800 °C, mainly involved the further carbonization and decomposition of the peach gum polysaccharide and sodium alginate backbones, as well as the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate in SSP and the conversion to ash. Compared to the TG of pure SSP, the thermal decomposition temperature of the films increased, this improvement may be attributed to the hydrogen bonding interactions established between SSP and PG/SA, as well as the Ca2+-mediated cross-linking, both contributing to enhanced thermal stability of the films.Fig. 7TG (A), DTG (B), DSC (C), and XRD (D) of the composite films.Fig. 7

TG (A), DTG (B), DSC (C), and XRD (D) of the composite films.

The derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles for the composite films are presented in Fig. 7B, where two prominent characteristic absorption peaks are observed. Compared to the pure PG/SA film, with an increase in SSP content, the onset temperature and the temperature at the maximum degradation rate of the main degradation stage (second stage) in the SSP-added composite films shift toward higher temperatures, indicating enhanced thermal stability. This is mainly due to the uniform distribution of SSP particles in the polysaccharide matrix, which can form effective physical barriers, effectively delaying heat transfer and the diffusion of volatile degradation products (Wu et al., 2020). Additionally, the ionic cross-linking formed between Ca2+ in SSP and the –COO− groups on the peach gum polysaccharide chains, along with the enhanced hydrogen bonding network between the amino groups on the SSP surface and the hydroxyl groups of the polysaccharides, collectively increase the resistance to thermal motion of the molecular chains, making the film network structure more stable (Chu et al., 2025; Prabhakar et al., 2015).

The DSC curves of the composite films are shown in Fig. 7C. All films exhibit an endothermic peak around 120 °C and an exothermic peak around 220 °C. The exothermic peak may be related to molecular chain rearrangement or melting of crystalline regions within the film. With an increase in SSP content, the corresponding characteristic endothermic and exothermic peaks show a clear trend of high-temperature movement. This elevation in thermal transition temperature is attributed to the denser structure of the film resulting from hydrogen bonding and cross-linking between matrix components, which hinders the thermal motion of molecular chains and the phase transition temperature of the composite film has been increased, reflecting improved thermal stability due to strengthened cross-linking and increased intermolecular forces (Alias & Ismail, 2021). This confirms that the addition of SSP can enhance the heat resistance of the film, which is mutually validated with TGA/DTG analysis data.

The comprehensive thermal analysis results demonstrate that improves the thermal stability of PG/SA composite films through ionic cross-linking, enhanced hydrogen bonding, and physical barrier effects. Among them, the film with 6% SSP addition exhibits the most balanced improvement in thermal performance, while higher addition levels may lead to reduced structural homogeneity due to particle agglomeration, thereby limiting further enhancement of thermal stability to some extent. This study provides a theoretical basis and material science strategy for designing bio-based food packaging films with excellent thermal stability.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction analysis can determine the type and crystallinity of polymers, where a higher diffraction peak intensity indicates larger grain sizes in the crystalline regions. The crystallization process and structure of composite films were studied by X-ray diffraction, investigating the effect of SSP addition on the crystal structure of PG/SA composite films. The results are shown in Fig. 7D. All films exhibited a broad diffraction peak at approximately 2θ = 20°, which is a typical characteristic of the amorphous structure of polysaccharides. With the increase in SSP content, the intensity of this diffraction peak significantly increases and sharpens, while its peak position does not show a noticeable shift. This phenomenon indicates that the addition of SSP does not alter the fundamentally amorphous nature of the composite film but effectively induces an increase in ordered structures within the matrix (Mohammadi et al., 2018). SSP particles themselves contain crystalline components, and their inherent crystal structure directly contributes to the diffraction signals. These crystalline regions primarily originate from chitin crystals in shrimp shells and the ionic cross-linking formed between Ca2+ and the carboxyl groups of sodium alginate (egg-box structure). The enhancement in crystallinity implies an increase in the number of ionic bonds within the system, forming a large number of reversible ionic sacrificial bonds. Secondly, the abundant functional groups on the SSP surface generated intermolecular H-bonds and developed electrostatic associations with relevant chemical groups such as -OH and -COO− on the PG/SA molecular chains (K. Zhang et al., 2020). This enhanced interfacial interaction can promote local rearrangement and regularization of the originally disordered polysaccharide molecular chains, forming more microcrystalline regions or enhancing intermolecular ordered packing, thereby increasing the overall diffraction intensity of the films (Vandeginste, 2021). This result corroborates the enhanced hydrogen bonding observed in FTIR analysis and the structural densification observed in SEM. The addition of SSP effectively modulates the condensed state structure of the PG/SA composite films through the dual effects of its own crystalline contribution and the induction of molecular ordering in the polysaccharide matrix. This helps explain, from a micro-mechanistic perspective, why both its mechanical behavior and thermal resistance are improved in parallel.

Surface wettability and water stability analysis

The water content (WC) and solubility (WS) provide an integrated insight into the film's hydrophilic nature and structural integrity, facilitating an assessment of these properties. The relevant data are presented in Fig. 8 A. The pure PG/SA film exhibited high WC (20.58 ± 0.12%) and WS (90.49 ± 1.26%) values, which is attributed to the abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl and carboxyl groups in the molecular chains of peach gum polysaccharide and sodium alginate, thereby enabling easy permeation of water molecules through the film. As the SSP loading rose, both WC and WS showed a gradual decreasing trend. This may be due to the hydrophobic SSP components incorporated into the film, reducing the overall hydrophilic sites available for binding with water molecules. Secondly, SSP particles act as physical fillers in the PG/SA network, densifying the microstructure and reducing the free volume and permeation channels available for water occupation (Nata et al., 2020). The Ca2+ released by SSP undergoes ionic cross-linking with the -COO− groups on the polysaccharide chains, forming a more stable three-dimensional network that is less susceptible to water disruption, thereby significantly inhibiting the swelling and dissolution behavior of the film in water (Araujo et al., 2021).Fig. 8Water content and water solubility (A), water contact angle (B), and water absorption (C),water vapor and oxygen permeability (D) of peach gum composite films.Fig. 8

The water contact angle (WCA) serves as a key parameter for evaluating the surface hydrophobicity of membrane materials. An increase in WCA value indicates increased surface hydrophobicity, and a WCA > 90° indicates high hydrophobicity. The surface wettability and overall hydrophilicity of the films are key factors determining their moisture-proof efficacy and structural stability in humid environments. According to the plotted data (8B), the water contact angle of the neat film was measured to be 71.80 ± 0.14°, revealing a markedly hydrophilic nature. With the rise in SSP concentration, the WCA value of the films significantly increased, reaching 97.13 ± 1.10° at a 10% addition level, indicating that the surface properties had transitioned to hydrophobic. This transformation is primarily attributed to components such as chitin/chitosan in SSP having lower surface energy. These components migrate to or become enriched at the surface during film formation, effectively reducing the surface free energy of the films. Simultaneously, the introduction of SSP particles may moderately alter the surface roughness of the films, further synergistically enhancing their hydrophobicity (Waheed et al., 2020). The improvement in surface hydrophobicity helps reduce the adsorption and spreading of environmental moisture vapor on the film surface, effectively decreasing the adsorption and diffusion of ambient humidity. Appropriate addition of SSP is beneficial for reducing water passage and improving the water vapor barrier properties of the composite films.

In summary, the addition of SSP comprehensively improved the water resistance of the PG/SA composite film by imparting surface hydrophobicity, reducing overall hydrophilicity, and strengthening the network structure. The increase in surface WCA effectively prevents initial water attachment, while the reduction in WC and WS ensures the macroscopic morphological stability and functional durability of the film in humid environments or when in direct contact with aqueous phases.

Water absorption rate

The water absorption characteristics of composite films have a significant impact on their structural stability and application performance in humid environments. Fig. 8C shows the curves of water absorption (WA) over time for pure PG/SA films and composite films with different SSP additions. The water absorption process of all films exhibited a similar trend: WA increased rapidly in the initial stage, then the growth rate gradually slowed, and water absorption equilibrium was gradually reached after approximately 300 min. However, throughout the entire water absorption process, the equilibrium water uptake of the PG/SA/SSP composite film was markedly reduced relative to that of the neat PG/SA film, and this value systematically decreased with increasing SSP addition. The chitin/chitosan components in SSP are more hydrophobic than peach gum polysaccharide and sodium alginate. Their introduction effectively reduces the overall surface hydrophilicity of the film and weakens the binding capacity between water molecules and the film matrix. Secondly, mineral components such as calcium carbonate contained in SSP can form physical barriers within the film, further delaying the penetration and diffusion of water molecules (Iffath et al., 2025). As shown by SEM and XRD analyses, SSP particles are uniformly embedded in the PG/SA polymer network structure. By filling pores and enhancing interfacial interactions, the microstructure of the film becomes more compact and complete. This structural densification directly reduces the pathways and free volume available for water permeation, thereby effectively inhibiting the swelling behavior of the film and improving its water resistance and dimensional stability. The addition of SSP, through multiple pathways such as altering surface properties, providing physical barriers, and promoting structural densification, significantly reduces the hydrophilicity and water absorption capacity of PG/SA composite films. This ensures the maintenance of mechanical integrity and the stability of barrier properties under high humidity conditions.

Barrier properties

The water vapor permeability (WVP) and oxygen permeability (OP) of films are key indicators for evaluating their potential application in food packaging, as they reflect the water permeability and oxygen barrier properties of food packaging films, which are directly related to the film's ability to delay moisture loss and oxidative deterioration in food. By testing the WVP and OP values of pure PG/SA films and composite films with different SSP addition levels, the effect of SSP on the barrier properties of the films was investigated, and the results are shown in Fig. 8D. The unmodified PG/SA film showed the greatest WVP and OP, which were 21.55 × 10−12 g·cm−1·s−1·Pa−1 and 16.36 × 10−15 cm3·mm·m−2·d−1·KPa−1, respectively. With the addition of SSP, both the water vapor permeability and oxygen permeability gradually decreased. The water vapor permeability and oxygen permeability continued to decrease with the increase of SSP addition. When the SSP addition content was 10%, both achieved the lowest values, significantly reducing by about 60% and 67% compared to pure membrane, respectively. This result indicates that the addition of SSP effectively enhanced the barrier properties of the PG/SA composite film against water vapor and oxygen. This may be because SSP, as rigid particles, is evenly distributed throughout the PG/SA matrix, it significantly raises the path tortuosity for the diffusion of water vapor and oxygen, thereby prolonging their permeation paths and time, resulting in a physical barrier effect (Raghuvanshi et al., 2023). Secondly, as confirmed by FTIR and XRD analyses, a denser and more stable three-dimensional network structure was formed between SSP and the polysaccharide matrix through Ca2+ cross-linking and hydrogen bonding. This enhanced intermolecular interaction not only did it effectively reduce the number of free hydrophilic groups, but it also limited the mobility of polymer chains, making it difficult for small-molecule permeants to diffuse within the dense network. Additionally, components inherent in SSP, such as calcium carbonate, may also exhibit certain adsorption or barrier effects against gases (Rong et al., 2022). These results are consistent with the findings of Meng et al. (Meng et al., 2024) in their study on polyvinyl alcohol/silica composite films. Their research indicates that with the addition of silica, the water vapor permeability (WVP) and oxygen permeability (OP) of the composite films significantly decrease. This is primarily due to the interactions between silica and PVA chains, which lead to a more compact molecular arrangement within the film, thereby reducing the adsorption sites for water and oxygen in the film. The barrier properties of the PG/SA film were markedly enhanced by SSP through the combined actions of physical filling and chemical cross-linking. This strategy of structural densification and diffusion path complexity provides an effective approach for designing next-generation high-performance bio-based food preservation packaging materials.

TSS and PH

The total soluble solids (TSS) content is an important indicator for measuring the flavor quality and respiratory metabolism intensity of fresh cut pears. As shown in Fig. 11A, with the increase of storage time, all groups showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. This is due to the hydrolysis of starch in the initial fruit into soluble sugars (such as sucrose, fructose, glucose, etc.) and the occurrence of water transpiration on the incision surface, resulting in an increase in the apparent TSS value. Therefore, the peak value of TSS in the blank group was the highest and changed the fastest, reaching 17.2° Brix on the 5th day, significantly higher than the 0% and 6% SSP/PG/SA composite film groups. In the later stage, with the continuous consumption of the fruit's own respiratory metabolism, a large amount of soluble sugars are oxidized and decomposed, resulting in a decrease in TSS. This indicates that the composite film can alleviate the sugar consumption of the fruit, showing good preservation and anti-aging effects. As shown in Fig. 11B, the pH values of the four groups of samples showed an overall upward trend, mainly due to the respiratory metabolism consumption of organic acids as substrates during the tissue aging process. The pH value of the blank group changed the most, increasing from 5.13 to 5.96, while the pH value of the composite membrane with a 6% addition increased from 5.01 to 5.61, indicating that the addition of SSP can enhance the composite membrane, reduce the decomposition and consumption of organic acids, and inhibit the respiratory metabolism intensity and aging process of cut pears.

Conclusion

This study successfully prepared SSP-reinforced peach gum/sodium alginate ternary composite films and systematically investigated the mechanism by which SSP affects the comprehensive properties of the films. The results indicate that SSP, as a natural, low-cost bio-enhancer, significantly optimizes the structure and properties of PG/SA composite films through multiple mechanisms including ionic cross-linking, hydrogen bonding interactions, and physical filling. The abundant amino, hydroxyl, and calcium carbonate components on the SSP surface can interact with the PG/SA matrix via ionic cross-linking and hydrogen bonding, effectively enhancing interfacial bonding and contributing to the construction of a denser network, SSP incorporation strengthens the composite film's mechanical strength, thermal endurance, and barrier properties, and shifted their surface from hydrophilic to hydrophobic. The moisture content and water solubility were significantly reduced, water absorption was markedly inhibited, while the films demonstrated notably improved UV shielding capacity and opacity. With 6 wt% addition of SSP, the film achieved optimal comprehensive properties, including tensile. Water vapor and oxygen transmission rates were greatly reduced, and the film possessed good UV shielding ability and structural compactness. An appropriate amount of SSP can be homogeneously distributed within the matrix and form a stable cross-linked network, while excessive addition tends to cause agglomeration and performance degradation. This study confirms that SSP, as a natural organic-inorganic composite bio-filler, can effectively enhance the comprehensive properties of peach gum/sodium alginate composite films and has broad application prospects in the field of active food packaging. Fresh-cut pear preservation experiments further confirmed that the 6% SSP/PG/SA composite film can effectively reduce fruit weight loss and browning, delay the loss of total soluble solids and pH increase, maintain tissue plumpness, significantly extend the shelf life, and demonstrate practical application potential in active packaging of fresh-cut fruits and vegetables. This research provides a feasible technical pathway and theoretical support for the resource utilization of aquatic waste and the development of green packaging materials.
