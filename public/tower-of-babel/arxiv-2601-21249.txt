Position: Certifiable State Integrity in Cyber-Physical Systems — Why Modular Sovereignty Solves the Plasticity-Stability Paradox
Enzo Nicolás Spotorno, Antônio Augusto Medeiros Fröhlich

Abstract
The machine learning community has achieved remarkable success with universal foundation models for time-series and physical dynamics, largely overcoming earlier approximation barriers in smooth or slowly varying regimes through scale and specialized architectures. However, deploying these monolithic models in safety-critical Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), governed by non-stationary lifecycle dynamics and strict reliability requirements, reveals persistent challenges. Recent evidence shows that fine-tuning time-series foundation models induces catastrophic forgetting, degrading performance on prior regimes. Standard models continue to exhibit residual spectral bias, smoothing high-frequency discontinuities characteristic of incipient faults, while their opacity hinders formal verification and traceability demanded by safety standards (e.g., ISO 26262, IEC 61508). This position paper argues that the plasticity-stability paradox cannot be fully resolved by global parameter updates (whether via offline fine-tuning or online adaptation). Instead, we advocate a Modular Sovereignty paradigm: a library of compact, frozen regime-specific specialists combined via uncertainty-aware blending, which we term ”HYDRA” (Hierarchical uncertaintY-aware Dynamics for Rapidly-Adapting systems). This paradigm ensures regime-conditional validity, rigorous disentanglement of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties, and modular auditability, offering a certifiable path for robust state integrity across the CPS lifecycle.

The Theoretical Core: Persisting Barriers to Certifiable Lifecycle Modeling

While universal foundation models excel in smooth regimes, their deployment in safety-critical CPS reveals persisting theoretical barriers. In systems governed by advection-dominated transport and high-frequency discontinuities, monolithic architectures struggle to resolve dynamic bifurcations, retain regime knowledge, and satisfy formal certifiability. We analyze these barriers below, emphasizing how the runtime prediction error residual acts as the key discriminator for mode separation.

The Certifiability Gap: Entanglement vs. Modular Assurance.

Beyond approximation, monolithic models face a fundamental certifiability barrier under standards like ISO 26262 and IEC 61508, which demand formal verifiability, traceability, and predictable failure modes. Entangled weights obstruct decomposition into independently certifiable components, complicate tight Lipschitz bound computation (needed for formal guarantees on input sensitivity and worst-case error propagation), and obscure failure origins. Constructing

Type-I (Physics-Derived / H-SS):
Suitable for regimes where high-fidelity data is scarce but first principles are well-defined (e.g., aerodynamics, thermal flow). Specialists are instantiated as

Appropriate for regimes with complex, hard-to-model interactions (e.g., tribology, degradation). Specialists leverage historical fleet data, enabling solutions to inverse and forward problems while ideally incorporating PIML constraints for trustworthiness.

Prior to inclusion, each specialist is subjected to rigorous offline Verification and Validation (V&V). Consequently, the library evolves not by retraining, but by accretion: new operating conditions (such as specific towing configurations) are validated and formally inducted as distinct specialists. This additive approach explicitly prevents catastrophic forgetting while ensuring dense coverage of the nominal manifold. Operationally, Type-I models provide immediate zero-shot baselines, whereas Type-II models facilitate progressive refinement as fleet data accumulates. In hybrid configurations, the Governor dynamically arbitrates between the two, weighting its reliance based on real-time residuals and uncertainty metrics, leveraging the runtime prediction error as the key evidence for mode distinction. Discussion and Future Directions: Toward Certifiable Physical Learning

The transition to Modular Sovereignty is not a solved problem; it is an open invitation to rethink how we structure physical learning. While we have argued for the benefits of specialists, we must also address the critical risks inherent in combining them: the trade-off between
Discrete Switching
and
Continuous Aging

Risks of Output-Space Blending.
Naive output blending (

\hat{y}
is the output,
\pi_{k}
are weights,
S_{k}(x)
are specialist predictions) risks invalid states during bifurcations in system dynamics, as convex combinations of disjoint specialists may produce outputs that exit the physical manifold or violate conservation laws. This ”superposition fallacy” is particularly dangerous in safety-critical transitions (e.g., fault onset), motivating the need for controlled interpolation strategies discussed below.

The Stability of Switching: Avoiding Chattering.
A core critique of hybrid control is chattering, that is, high‑frequency switching that destabilizes the plant. While the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filters in Sec.
3
attenuate stochastic noise, formal certification requires explicit dwell‑time constraints. Although classical LPV proofs relies on bounded parameter variation rates
(

By rejecting transitions that violate this bound, the Governor effectively imposes an exogenous stability constraint on the learned policy. This converts a potentially unstable endogenous signal into one that satisfies standard dwell-time conditions, rendering the system analyzable under established LPV frameworks by deliberately trading transient responsiveness for guaranteed stability.

Open Challenge: The Sparsity-Continuity Tension.
This introduces a fundamental tension in the Governor design.
The Case for Sparsity:
To avoid the Superposition Fallacy during regime shifts, the Governor should enforce a sparse prior (e.g., Dirichlet
\alpha<1

The Case for Continuity:
However, physical aging (e.g., tire wear, battery degradation) is a continuous drift, not a discrete switch. Strict sparsity prevents the system from tracking these smooth transitions, leading to a ”chattering” effect between specialists.
