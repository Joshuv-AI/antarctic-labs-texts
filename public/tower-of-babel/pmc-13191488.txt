Effects of Post-Processing on the Properties of Nanofiber/Carboxymethyl Cellulose-Based Hydrogels
Authors: Sa Rang Choi, Jung Myoung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are functional materials characterized by a porous network structure and a high capacity for water absorption. However, most commercial SAPs are synthesized from petroleum-based polymers, which are major contributors to environmental pollution. This study aims to develop a sustainable absorbent material with excellent mechanical strength and stable water retention capacity. Herein, we compared two production routes for fabricating hydrogels composed of carboxymethyl cellulose nanofibers (CMCNF) and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) cross-linked with acrylic acid. The results demonstrated that homogenizing the cross-linking reactants immediately after the reaction and the addition of CMC powder improved the final water retention capacity of the hydrogels. The incorporation of CNF acted as a reinforcing agent, improving the elasticity, stiffness, and structural stability of the gels, although the swelling ratio decreased with increasing CNF content. Therefore, the addition of additional CMC powder partially compensated for the reduction in swelling, maintaining a balanced water absorption performance.

Introduction

Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are hydrophilic materials with a three-dimensional (3D) polymer network structure formed through physical or chemical cross-linking. SAPs are insoluble in water and can absorb and stably retain large amounts of water-soluble liquids within their structure, even under pressure. They have attracted considerable attention in various applications, including disposable hygiene products such as diapers and sanitary napkins, soil moisture conditioners for agriculture, drug delivery, and food packaging, such as ice packs. − Commercially utilized SAPs are mainly manufactured through the synthesis of petroleum-based polymers such as acrylic resins, poly­(lactic acid), poly­(acrylic acid), methacrylic acid, acrylamide, poly­(vinyl alcohol), and poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide). − However, SAPs are not only an unsustainable resource owing to petroleum depletion, but also suffer from the critical disadvantage of not degrading under natural conditions and, consequently, causing environmental pollution.

With the growing need for sustainable, environmentally sound materials, superabsorbent hydrogels (SAHs) derived from natural polymers, including polysaccharides and polypeptides, are being actively researched. − The most important properties of hydrogels are biodegradability, high swelling properties, and mechanical stability. Hydrogels prepared from natural polymers alone have good biodegradability, but their water absorption properties and mechanical stability are inferior to petroleum-based SAPs. Therefore, methods that combine petroleum-based synthetic polymers with natural polymers (such as chitosan, alginate, starch, carboxymethylcellulose, and cellulose nanofibrils) have been proposed to improve these two properties.

Carboxymethylcellulose, in which some hydroxyl groups of the glucose monomers that compose the cellulose backbone are substituted with carboxymethyl groups (−CH2COO), is a promising option as a raw material for absorbent composites, given its advantages of nontoxicity, biocompatible biodegradability, and high hydrophilicity. Several studies have investigated CMC composite hydrogels. Mishra et al. grafted poly­(acrylic acid) chains (PAA) onto the backbone of CMC to produce a graft copolymer poly­(acrylic acid) -grafted CMC (CMC-g-PAA) through the synthesis of a microwave-initiated method.

Habib et al. developed carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)-sodium alginate blended hybrid hydrogels as 3D printing materials for biomaterials. Divalent cations, such as Ca2+, used as cross-linkers, acted on the alginate-containing carboxyl groups (−COO–) to effectively induce gelation. Wang et al. prepared carboxymethylcellulose-g-poly­(sodium acrylate)/medical stone (CMC-g-Poly­(sodium acrylate) (PNaA)/MS superabsorbent composites via a free radical graft copolymerization reaction between sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), partially neutralized sodium acrylate (NaA), medical stone (MS), and cross-linker N,N-methylene-bis-acrylamide (MBA). The resultant composites showed enhanced swelling rates across various pH values, brines, and hydrophilic organic solvents. However, cellulose-based hydrogels are susceptible to microbial degradation, leading to easy breakage of the polysaccharide chains, and hydrogels swollen in water exhibit relatively soft, brittle properties. − To improve the structural stability and robustness properties of hydrogels, cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs), which have a nanoscale particle size and increased specific surface area compared to conventional cellulose, can be considered.

Cellulose can attain excellent physicochemical properties by being cut into smaller sizes on the order of nanometers, and hydrogels prepared with the addition of CNFs form a nanoscale 3D network structure inside owing to the abundance of hydrogen bonding, which provides high strength and mechanical stiffness. Liu et al. investigated sodium alginate SA/cellulose nanofiber (CNF)/poly­(vinyl alcohol) PVA/nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) nanocomposite hydrogels. The hydrogels were prepared by mixing and cross-linking SA, CNF, and PVA in the presence of NPK fertilizer without toxic solvents. The hydrogels have a microporous core structure reinforced by inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding of CNF, and they show promise as fertilizers and soil/water conditioners. Furthermore, Das’s work prepared biodegradable hydrogels for carboxymethyl cellulose (NaCMC)/hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC)-based soil fertilizers using citric acid (CA) as a cross-linking agent. CNFs enhanced the modulus value of the hydrogel and highly controlled the network structure, effectively providing structural stability. Cellulose nanofiber-based hydrogels can effectively transport water and fertilizer sustainably by gradually releasing them, and biodegradation experiments with soil bacteria demonstrated good biodegradability. CNF-based hydrogels were also prepared by Onyianta et al., and they reported that the elasticity of the gel increased by increasing the CNF content loaded.

Our previous study prepared hydrogels via two routes by mixing CMCNF and CMC in various proportions and grafting acrylic acid onto the cellulose backbone. CMCNF was used as a reinforcing agent to enhance the mechanical stiffness of the hydrogel. In the first method, CMC powder was added to the wet hydrogel prior to drying; in the second, to the dried hydrogel. The results showed that the first method, which added CMC powder to the hydrogel before drying, was more effective at retaining CMC during swelling in water than the second method, which added CMC powder after drying was complete.

This study aims to develop sustainable hydrogels with enhanced swelling performance and mechanical robustness by further improving and optimizing the postprocessing steps of our previous method. In the previous approach, bulk hydrogels were immersed in ethanol for 48 h prior to homogenization; however, the influence of this delayed solvent exchange step on network formation and material performance had not been investigated. Therefore, this study systematically compared two postprocessing strategies: (Method 1) homogenizing and drying the bulk gel after ethanol substitution, and (Method 2) immediately homogenizing the freshly polymerized gel before the ethanol-exchange. By evaluating the effects of these contrasting routes on swelling behavior, water and saline absorption capacity, mechanical strength, and thermal properties, this study establishes an optimized processing strategy for fabricating high-performance CMCNF/CMC hydrogels.

Experimental Section

Materials

CMC in powder form was obtained from Marine Material Co., Ltd. (Republic of Korea); its degree of substitution (DS) and viscosity (viscosity, 1% solution in H2O at 25 °C) were 0.8–0.9 and 5,000 cP, respectively. CMCNF in 2% suspension was purchased from Moorim P&P (Ulsan, Republic of Korea). The physicochemical properties of CMCNF are summarized in the Supporting Information Table S1, while SEM images of CMC and a TEM image of CM-CNF are presented in the Supporting Information Figure S1. Acrylic acid (AA, Guaranteed grade, DAEJUNG Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) was used as the monomer for hydrogel synthesis. In addition, potassium persulfate (KPS, Potassium persulfate 99.0%, Special grade, Samjung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) and N′,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) (NMB, N,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) 97%, Alfa Aesar) were purchased and used as polymerization initiators and cross-linkers. For the ethanol substitution and washing steps of the hydrogel preparation, 99.5% ethanol (Ethyl alcohol anhydrous, Guaranteed, DAEJUNG Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) was used.

Preparation of CNF/CMC-Based Hydrogels

To compare the properties of the hydrogels according to the CMC and CMCNF mixing ratio, CMCNF addition ratios of 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100% were used. The formulation of CNF/CMC hydrogels with varying contents of CNF, CMC, and CMC powder additives is provided in Table S2 in the Supporting Information. The CMC and CMCNF mixed suspensions were diluted to 3 wt % and loaded into a 500 mL three-necked flask equipped with a temperature sensor (K162C, Misung S&I Co., Ltd., South Korea). For the polymerization reaction, the reactor was heated using a heating mantle (MS-DM603, Misung S&I Co., Ltd., South Korea) to maintain a constant set temperature. The reactor was stirred at 400 rpm for approximately 15 min using a stirrer (PL-FS121, Poonglim CO., Korea), and 7 wt % acrylic acid was mixed while the reactor was warmed to 60 °C. Then, at 2–3 min intervals, 0.4 and 0.2 wt % of potassium persulfate and N,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) were added, respectively, and the reactor temperature was raised to 70 °C. The reactor was stirred for 10 min from the time it reached 70 °C for uniform mixing of the chemicals. The reaction was then continued for an additional 80 min without stirring. The overall hydrogel formation mechanism and processing steps are illustrated in Figure . As shown in Figure a, acrylic acid (AA) is polymerized via free-radical polymerization initiated by potassium persulfate under thermal conditions, forming poly­(acrylic acid) (PAA). Subsequently, as depicted in Figure b, the PAA chains are covalently cross-linked by N,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) (MBA), while carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) or CMCNF is incorporated into the network through hydrogen bonding and physical interactions. Excess ethanol was added at the end of the reaction to separate the resulting gel from the reactor, and the mixture was stirred at 600 rpm for approximately 5 min. The process of ethanol displacement and the addition of CMC powder (5–30 wt %) is shown in Figure and was performed in two different ways. Method 1 was conducted as in previous studies: the resulting hydrogel was immediately separated from the reactor and soaked in ethanol for at least 48 h. After ethanol substitution, additional CMC powder (5, 10, 20, and 30 wt %) was added, and the mixture was homogenized for 3 min using a mechanical rotor–stator homogenizer (HG-15A, DAIHAN Scientific Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) to ensure uniform dispersion of the CMC within the gel matrix.

Schematic illustration of the hydrogel formation mechanism and network structure. (a) Free-radical polymerization of AA initiated by KPS under thermal conditions and (b) covalent cross-linking of PAA by MBA and incorporation of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC/CMCNF) through hydrogen bonding and physical interactions. −

Schematic descriptions of fabrication methods for hydrogels.

In Method 2, 5–30 wt % CMC powder was added to the freshly obtained gel immediately after it was removed from the reactor. The gel was then homogenized for 3 min using a homogenizer and soaked in ethanol for at least 24 h. After ethanol exchange, the gel was washed three to four times with ethanol using a 200 mesh filter. The hydrogel was dried in a dryer (JSOP-100, JS Research Inc., Korea) at 50 °C, ground into a powder, and then fractionated into particles ranging in size from 0.6 to 1 mm.

Morphological Characterization

The surface of the dried hydrogel particles or the cross-sectional pore structure of the swollen hydrogel was observed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi SU8220, Japan), operating at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV. The swollen hydrogel samples were prepared by freeze-drying, and all samples were coated with platinum before imaging. The pore size distribution of the hydrogels was analyzed from SEM images using ImageJ (NIH, USA). For each sample, more than 60 pores were randomly selected and measured to ensure statistical reliability. Additionally, the obtained data were further analyzed using Origin software (OriginLab Corp., USA) to calculate the average pore size and standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, and differences between samples were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Absorption Performance

One g of a hydrogel sample with a particle size of 0.6–1.0 mm was placed in a paper tea bag (8 cm × 10 cm) and soaked in an aqueous medium (saline or distilled water) for 24 h at room temperature to evaluate the absorption (mass of water adsorbed relative to the weight of the hydrogel) over time. A saline solution was prepared by dissolving sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥99%, DAEJUNG Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) in deionized water to 0.9 wt %. The ionic strength of the solution was approximately 0.15 mol/L. The water absorption in distilled water and saline water was calculated using the following eq : Absorption(g/g)=Ws−WdWd×100 1 where W s and W d are the masses of the swollen and the dried hydrogels, respectively.

Thermal Properties

Thermal analysis of the hydrogels was performed using a thermal analyzer system (Auto TGA-DSC, Discovery SDT 650, TA Instruments, USA) for thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The initial mass of the hydrogels used in the tests was approximately 10 mg. The tests were performed under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, from room temperature to 500 °C, with a continuous flow rate of 10 mL/min.

Viscoelasticity

A rotational rheometer (Anton-Paar MCR 102e, Austria) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry (25 mm diameter) was used to evaluate the rheological properties of the hydrogels. Dry samples of 0.6–1 mm were swollen to equilibrium in deionized water immediately before the rheological characterization. Strain amplitude sweeps were performed under strains ranging from 0–1000% and angular frequencies of 1 rad/s. All samples were deformed at a constant frequency and amplitude, and three replicates were performed for each sample.

Results and Discussion

Hydrogel Characteristics Prepared by Different Postprocessing Methods

Figure shows the appearance of hydrogels (CNF0/CMC) post-treated using two different routes. The hydrogel prepared according to Method 1 was homogenized after the reaction by immersing it in ethanol for a long time in a block state, whereas in Method 2, the gel was homogenized immediately after immersion in ethanol. Ethanol is a solvent that provides a strong dehydrating effect. This suggests that the interaction between the agglomerated hydrogel and ethanol in Method 1 causes the gel network to rearrange and strengthen the hydrogen bond network structure on the gel surface. This can make the homogenization process difficult. In contrast, in Method 2, the hydrogel was homogenized more uniformly and smoothly than in Method 1 because the homogenization was conducted immediately after the reaction.

Appearance before and after homogenization of gels prepared using Method 1 and Method 2.

Figure characterizes the effect of different post-treatment routes on the uptake of saline and distilled water by hydrogels (CNF0/CMC). Generally, the degree of swelling of a hydrogel depends on the size and porosity of the voids created inside the hydrogel. In addition, the degree of cross-linking during gel formation and the amount of free hydrophilic hydroxyl groups that do not participate in ionic cross-linking affect the pore structure inside the hydrogel.

Effect of postprocessing method on water absorption of CNF0/CMC hydrogels. (a) Saline water and (b) distilled water measured over time.

The saline and distilled water absorption of the hydrogel (CNF0/CMC) prepared using Method 1 was 22 and 70 g/g, respectively, within 30 min, and the maximum absorption was achieved after 24 h, at 28 and 80 g/g, respectively. In contrast, the saline and distilled water absorption of the hydrogel prepared using Method 2 was 30 and 88 g/g, respectively, showing 36 and 26% superior absorption performance compared to Method 1. Furthermore, the maximum saline and distilled water uptake of hydrogels prepared according to Method 2 after 24 h was 35 and 109 g/g, respectively, indicating 28–36% higher uptake than Method 1.

Absorbent polymers prepared by polymerization can exhibit significantly different water absorption properties depending on the solvent substitution and drying method used during fabrication. , Cellulose is very rich in hydroxyl groups, and hydrogen bonding during hydrogel preparation can readily lead to a dense pore structure. Therefore, hydrogen bonding can be prevented and the porous structure maintained by appropriately replacing the water in the gel with a volatile solvent, such as ethanol, prior to drying. In Method 1, the hydrogel was prepared by immersing the gel in ethanol for an extended period. During this process, ethanol likely rearranged the surface network and increased its rigidity. In addition, the strong drying effect of ethanol may have promoted hydrogen bonding between adjacent cellulose hydroxyl groups, strengthening the surface structure. These surface hydrogen bonds could hinder ethanol penetration into the interior of the gel, resulting in incomplete solvent exchange. Consequently, residual water within the gel may induce further hydrogen bonding, leading to partial collapse or distortion of the cross-linked network and reduced swelling capacity. This effect becomes more pronounced under saline conditions. The presence of Na+ ions in the 0.9 wt % NaCl solution screens the electrostatic repulsion between ionized carboxyl groups (−COO–) in the poly­(acrylic acid) network, thereby reducing the osmotic pressure difference and further limiting water uptake. , In contrast, in Method 2, the gel was homogenized with ethanol immediately after polymerization. This process promotes rapid solvent exchange throughout the gel, allowing more effective removal of water from the network. As a result, hydrogen-bond-induced aggregation is suppressed and the cross-linked structure is better preserved. Consequently, a more open and uniform porous structure is maintained, leading to higher swelling performance. Even under saline conditions, the hydrogels prepared by Method 2 retain relatively higher absorption, suggesting that the improved network integrity plays a key role in maintaining swelling behavior in ionic environments.

Effect of CNF Loading Rate on Hydrogel Properties

CNFs, which are rich in hydroxyl groups, are known to increase the mechanical stiffness of composite materials. , Therefore, it is expected that adding CNFs to hydrogel polymers will yield robust gels. Figure shows the surface of dry hydrogel beads for CNF0/CMC and CNF100/CMC. Additional SEM images for intermediate CNF contents are provided in the Supporting Information Figure S2. The lower CNF content resulted in gels with large internal voids. When the CNF loading rate was increased, the gel interior exhibited a smooth surface without voids, due to the strong hydrogen bonding between fibril hydroxyl groups that did not participate in the chemical cross-linking reaction. This structural difference is expected to affect the strength and swelling properties of the gel.

FE-SEM images of hydrogels prepared via Method 2. (a) CNF0/CMC and (b) CNF100/CMC at different magnifications (×60 and ×500).

The thermograms and DTG profiles of CMC, CMCNF, and the synthesized gels are shown in Figure , indicating their pyrolysis characteristics. The poly­(acrylic acid) (AA)-based SAP gel recorded the highest maximum mass loss of 80% up to 500 °C. The CMC and CNF raw materials had a maximum mass loss of 61 and 71% by 500 °C, respectively, and the hydrogels containing these materials recorded a maximum mass loss of 72% by 500 °C (Figure a). The thermal decomposition of polymers involves several key steps (Figure b). The first stage of weight loss occurs because of water evaporation. Highly substituted CMC is highly hydrophilic, so the initial weight loss due to water evaporation at temperatures below 100 °C is significant. In addition, the gradual weight loss that occurs between 100–200 °C is related to the dehydration of carboxyl groups, forming anhydride rings. The major weight loss of CMC and CMCNF, occurring around 250–300 °C, was attributed to cellulose backbone degradation, dihydroxylation, and decarboxylation of AA. The CNF100/CMC hydrogel experienced weight loss due to dihydroxylation and decarboxylation of AA around 245 °C, whereas CNF0/CMC experienced a higher weight loss at around 266 °C. This is believed to be due to the higher loading of CMC with a higher DS, forming a more stable bond with AA. Furthermore, the progressive weight loss around 400 °C seen in the hydrogels and AA (SAP) is attributed to chain scission of the polymerized polymer, which may be due to the cross-linked structure formed by the CNF/CMC and acrylic acid polymerization process, which improves thermal stability, shifting the decomposition temperature to a higher range.

Thermal analysis of materials and hydrogels. (a) TGA and (b) DTG curves of CMC, CMCNF, CNF0/CMC, and CNF100/CMC.

The effect of CMCNF loading amount on the swelling ability of hydrogels is presented in Figure . As mentioned earlier, the swelling capacities of the hydrogels prepared using Methods 1 and 2 differed. First, the 30 min saline uptake of CNF0/CMC and CNF100/CMC in Method 1 was 22 and 15 g/g, respectively, which increased to 28 and 18 g/g after 24 h, respectively. The maximum distilled water uptake of CNF0/CMC and CNF100/CMC was 80 and 55 g/g, respectively, both higher than the uptake in saline. Method 2 showed an increased uptake in all samples compared to Method 1. The saline uptake of CNF0/CMC and CNF100/CMC from Method 2 was 30 and 16 g/g after 30 min, respectively, and 35 and 18 g/g after 24 h, respectively. The distilled water uptake increased from 88 and 47 g/g after 30 min to 109 and 54 g/g after 24 h, respectively. The CNF0/CMC and CNF100/CMC from Method 2 had a higher uptake of distilled water. Regardless of the post-treatment method, the swelling capacity of the hydrogels decreased by approximately 32–47%, depending on the CNF loading.

Effect of CNF loading and postprocessing method on water absorption. (a) saline water for hydrogels prepared via Method 1, (b) saline water of Method 2, (c) distilled water of Method 1, and (d) distilled water of Method 2.

The swelling properties of absorbent materials prepared based on carboxymethyl-treated cellulose are highly dependent on the presence of anionic hydrophilic carboxymethyl groups on the cellulose surface. Alam and Che Nan reported that anionic groups, such as carboxymethyl groups, which do not contribute to ionic cross-linking, cause hydrogels to swell extensively by generating electrostatic repulsion at the fiber surface.

In the case of CMCNF, the DS is approximately 0.2, whereas CMC ranges from 0.8 to 0.9. It was found that hydrogels with better swelling properties were prepared due to the increase in carboxymethyl group content with higher CMC content. In addition, Passornraprasit et al. reported that in the preparation of PAA/CNF composite absorbent materials, the pore size and size distribution inside the cross-linked polymer decreased because of CNF, the stiffness increased owing to the dense network structure, and the water absorption performance decreased, which is consistent with our results. As CNFs typically have a high specific surface area and high hydroxyl content, it is believed that as the CNF loading amount increases, the hydrogel network pore structure becomes denser due to the strengthening of physical cross-linking, such as hydrogen bonding.

To examine the internal pore structure of the hydrogels prepared by Method 2, cross sections of samples swollen in saline for 24 h and subsequently freeze-dried were observed using SEM. The internal morphology of the hydrogels is presented in Figure . All samples have a porous network structure, with all pores forming an interconnected channel structure. The AA-based SAP had a larger pore structure than the CNF/CMC composite hydrogel. It is found that the porous network, which becomes tighter with increasing CNF loading, forms a dense structure with low pore size and pore distribution. The size and porosity of pores within an absorbent polymer are properties directly related to its water absorption performance. The larger the pore size and the more pores, the better the water permeability.

Cross-sectional FE-SEM images of hydrogels after saline absorption (Method 2). (a) AA­(SAP), (b) CNF0/CMC, (c) CNF25/CMC, (d) CNF50/CMC, (e) CNF75/CMC and (f) CNF100/CMC.

Additionally, during the preparation of carboxymethyl cellulose-based hydrogels, the higher the carboxyl group content in cellulose, the stronger the anionic repulsion, resulting in greater swelling. −

Therefore, the higher degree of substitution of CMC (DS 0.8) compared to CMCNF (DS 0.2) may be the reason for its better swelling properties. The swelling of CNF100/CMC hydrogels is limited because of the strong interaction of the hydroxyl groups contained in CNF.

Figure presents the pore size results obtained from the analysis of SEM images (Figure ), while representative SEM images used for pore size measurement are provided in the Supporting Information Figure S3. The SAP sample showed the largest pore sizes, with a broad, irregular distribution, including several large pores. In contrast, the CNF-containing hydrogels had smaller, more uniform pore structures, with CNF100/CMC exhibiting the most uniform distribution. The differences between the samples were statistically significant (p < 0.05). These results are in line with the swelling behavior. Samples with larger, more heterogeneous pores showed higher water uptake (commercial SAP and CNF0/CMC), whereas those with smaller, more uniform pores showed lower water uptake (CNF100/CMC).

Pore size of SAP, CNF0/CMC, and CNF100/CMC hydrogels.

The rheometer measurement results for the hydrogel are presented in Figure . The strain sweep test is a standard rheological test used to characterize hydrogels, which measures oscillatory strain by progressively increasing the strain rate at a fixed frequency. The linear viscoelastic region (LVR) is the region of the sample that can be deformed without destruction. The CNF/CMC hydrogels exhibited a shorter LVR range as the CNF loading rate of the hydrogels increased (Figure a). For all samples, the storage modulus (G′) was significantly higher than the loss modulus (G″), indicating that the samples benefit from cross-linking, rendering them fully gelled viscoelastic solids. Furthermore, the high G′ value of the hydrogels implies a reinforced gel network structure and high structural strength. Therefore, a higher storage modulus for shear strain indicates better mechanical stiffness. − Furthermore, the increase in CNF loading amount significantly affected the improvement of the storage modulus of the hydrogels. CNF100/CMC had the highest modulus, while CNF0/CMC exhibited the lowest. These results demonstrate that CNF makes a positive contribution to the viscoelastic enhancement of the gel. The high storage modulus can also be attributed to the low swelling degree of the hydrogel.

Rheological properties of hydrogels with varying CNF content. (a) storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), (b) loss factor (tan δ), and (c) stress–strain curves.

The point at which the microstructure of the hydrogel is destroyed (G′ = G″) is determined by the intersection of the storage and loss modulus. The loss factor is the tan δ value (G″/G′), defined as the ratio of the loss modulus to the elastic modulus, which means the critical strain rate at which the hydrogel switches from solid-like behavior (elastic behavior) to liquid (viscous behavior). − A tan δ value of less than 1 indicates a viscoelastic solid, while a tan δ value of more than 1 indicates viscoelastic liquid behavior. Therefore, tan δ = 1 is defined as the point at which the solid–liquid phase change occurs because of microstructure breakdown of the hydrogel. For CNF100/CMC, the gel failed at a strain of 43%. In contrast, microstructure failure occurred at strains of 62 and 70% for CNF50/CMC and CNF0/CMC, respectively (Figure b).

The stress–strain curves obtained by amplitude strain sweep are shown in Figure c. CNF0/CMC exhibited a maximum stress value of 549 Pa at 47% shear strain, while CNF50/CMC exhibited a maximum stress value of 862 Pa at 26% shear strain, and CNF100/CMC exhibited 1297 Pa at 14% shear strain. This is a 57 and 136% increase, respectively, compared to the sample without CNF. Similar to the critical strain rate, lower maximum stress values were observed as the CNF loading rate increased. The CNF100/CMC gel with the highest storage modulus is believed to have undergone gel fracture at lower strain rates, as excessive stiffness in the gel structure can reduce the flexibility of the material. − The physical entanglement and/or chemical bonding (hydrogen bonding, cross-linking, and so on) between the particles comprising the hydrogel are known to play an important role in the mechanical properties of the gel. Therefore, Heggset et al. reported that increasing the CNF content in the preparation of CNF/CNC-containing alginate hydrogels increases the viscoelastic and storage modulus of the hydrogel owing to the formation of a network due to fibril entanglement.

Effect of CMC Powder Addition on Hydrogel Properties

To improve the water absorption performance of the hydrogel prepared by Method 2, CMC powder was additionally added at 5, 10, 20, and 30 wt % during the post-treatment process. The effect of adding CMC powder to the hydrogels was compared by measuring the saline uptake over time (Figure ). The detailed statistical analysis is provided in the Supporting Information Tables S3 and S4. The gel was homogenized with ethanol immediately after the reaction, and the CMC powder was added at the same time as the gel dried, so the CMC powder was expected to be physically incorporated into the hydrogel polymer structure during drying. CMC powder improved the saline uptake performance of all samples, with higher additions increasing it by up to 30%. In the case of CNF100/CMC hydrogels, an additional 5% CMC powder increased saline water absorption from 18 to 19 g/g after 24 h, an increase of approximately 6%. When added at 30%, the absorption increased by about 27% to 24 g/g. This demonstrated that the subsequent degradation in absorption performance can be effectively improved by increasing the CNF loading rate.

Saline absorption characteristics of hydrogels with different CMC powder additions. (a) CNF0/CMC, (b) CNF25/CMC, (c) CNF50/CMC, (d) CNF75/CMC and (e) CNF100/CMC.

Figure shows the effect of CMC powder addition on the internal voids of the gel after swelling. No distinct CMC powder particles were observed in the gel microstructure, suggesting that the added CMC was well dispersed within the hydrogel matrix rather than remaining as discrete aggregates. The pore size of the hydrogel increased after the addition of CMC powder, compared with CNF100/CMC, consistent with the water uptake results. This indicates that the postadded CMC plays a role in modifying the internal structure of the hydrogel. Given the high hydrophilicity of CMC, the incorporated CMC chains are likely to enhance water affinity within the network, facilitating increased water penetration and retention. In addition, the CMC introduced during post-treatment is not expected to participate in covalent cross-linking, but rather to be physically incorporated within the network through hydrogen bonding and chain entanglement. This may lead to a loosening of the network structure and the formation of larger pores. As a result, the hydrogel exhibits increased pore size and improved swelling capacity.

Cross-sectional FE-SEM images of hydrogels: CNF100/CMC and CNF100/CMC with 30 wt % CMC addition.

Figure shows the effect of 30% CMC powder addition on the viscoelastic and mechanical properties of the gel. Because the added CMC powder is not chemically cross-linked, the storage modulus decreases with increasing CMC content. However, CNF100/CMC+p30 showed a higher storage modulus value than CNF0/CMC owing to the CNF reinforcement effect. The addition of CMC powder affected the critical strain point (loss factor = 1) of the hydrogels; it shifted the critical point toward lower strain rates. CNF100/CMC, which exhibited a critical strain point at 43% strain, showed a crucial point at an 8% strain rate when 30% CMC powder was added. CNF0/CMC and CNF0/CMC+p30 also showed critical strain points at 70 and 19%, respectively. Furthermore, based on the stress–strain curves, it is confirmed that the addition of CMC powder reduces the hydrogels’ strength. When 30% CMC powder is added to CNF100/CMC and CNF0/CMC, the maximum stress is reduced by 62 and 75%, respectively. However, this decrease reflects the expected trade-off associated with improved swelling performance and enhanced water uptake. Importantly, the presence of CNF mitigates strength loss relative to CMC-only systems, suggesting that combining CNF and CMC powders provides a tunable balance between mechanical integrity and absorbency.

Conclusions

This study polymerized CMCNF/CMC and acrylic acid at various CNF loadings and produced hydrogels via two post-treatment routes. Method 1 involved homogenizing and drying the bulk gel after ethanol substitution, whereas Method 2 involved immediate homogenization of the freshly polymerized gel prior to ethanol exchange. Hydrogels prepared via Method 2, in which rapid solvent substitution occurred owing to immediate homogenization, exhibited a 20–30% higher water absorption capacity than those prepared by Method 1. Regardless of the post-treatment method, the swelling capacity of the hydrogels decreased by approximately 32–47%, depending on the CNF loading amount. However, as the CNF loading rate increased, hydrogels with good stiffness were produced. CNF100/CMC exhibited a high storage modulus, with a 136% increase in maximum stress compared to CNF0/CMC. This study presents processing modifications that enhance both the mechanical strength and water-absorption performance of the hydrogels, highlighting their potential as environmentally friendly alternatives to petroleum-based SAPs.
