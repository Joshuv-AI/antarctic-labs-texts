Factors associated with insecticide-treated net utilization among women of reproductive age in Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Evans A. Opoku, Zahra A. Fazal, Mahsa Adib, Zelalem T. Haile, Prince A. Adu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the highest malaria burden globally, accounting for 95% of all cases and 96% of malaria-related deaths, with an estimated 593,000 fatalities. Among women of reproductive age, malaria not only impacts their health and survival but also leads to adverse birth outcomes. This study aims to examine the factors influencing the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), the primary method of malaria prevention among this high-risk group in Ghana.MethodsThis study used data publicly available from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS). GMIS was originally a cross-sectional study that employed a multi-stage probability sampling technique to select a total of 4114 women between the ages of 15 and 49. A structured interview schedule was used to elicit information on socio-demographic characteristics, ITN ownership, use, knowledge, behavior, and practices. Descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed on GMIS data in this study. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe study revealed that 79% of women of reproductive age in Ghana had at least one ITN, and 56% of women who owned an ITN used it the previous night. Women with children were more likely to use ITN than those without, with the likelihood increasing with the number of children. Compared to nulliparous women, ITN use was higher among primiparous (aOR 1.65; 95% CI 1.17, 2.32), multiparous (aOR 1.94; 95% CI 1.39, 2.71), and grand multiparous women (aOR 2.06; 95% CI 1.34, 3.18). The odds of ITNs’ utilization were lower in women who had three or more ITNs (aOR 0.52; 0.43, 0.63) compared to women who had fewer than three ITNs. Women living in the Brong-Ahafo region (aOR 2.22; 1.42, 3.46) and the Upper West region (aOR 2.59; 1.59, 4.28) had higher odds of ITN utilization compared to women in the Eastern region. All adjusted models controlled for age, education, parity, household wealth, and media exposure.ConclusionAlthough ITN ownership among this key population is promising, utilization remains low. This study highlights the need to consider both individual and community-level factors when designing targeted malaria prevention interventions. Addressing these determinants is essential for ensuring equitable access to ITNs and enhancing their use, ultimately strengthening malaria control efforts in Ghana.

Background

Malaria ranks as the world’s second leading cause of infectious disease-related mortality [1]. It is estimated that 350 to 500 million people are affected yearly, with 2 to 4 million fatalities occurring annually [2]. P. falciparum is the most lethal Plasmodium species, accounting for most of the malaria-related fatalities in the world [3]. Regionally, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) carries the greatest malaria burden, with an estimated 234 million cases (95%) and 593,000 deaths (96%) in 2021 [4].

In Ghana, malaria poses a significant public health problem, accounting for 30% of outpatient visits and 23% of hospital admissions [5]. In 2016, fifteen countries accounted for 80% of the global malaria mortality, with Ghana alone accounting for 4% of the mortality [6]. Hence, a considerable amount of Ghana’s annual budget is spent on treating and controlling the malaria endemic, with the economic impact of malaria in Ghana estimated at 1–2% of the country’s GDP [7]. Despite Ghana’s success in controlling malaria, marked by the 7.8% decrease in annual malaria incidence starting from 2011, the progress was set back to a 2.6% decrease between 2018 and 2020 [8]. This shows that there is more work to be done to reduce the malaria burden in the country.

The current malaria preventive interventions, which are being implemented in Ghana, aim to achieve not only malaria control but also the elimination of the disease in the country. These preventive initiatives include indoor residual spraying (IRS), universal coverage of insecticidal-treated nets (ITN), intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp), larviciding, and vaccination initiatives [9, 10]. However, equitable access to ITNs and a lack of information about ITNs’ use have been identified as significant hurdles to the complete utilization of ITNs in Africa. Evidence suggests that more than 40% of ITNs offered to households are underutilized, potentially jeopardizing the advances made in the prevention and control of malaria over the years [11].

Previous studies have examined ITNs utilization among pregnant women and children under-five, as they are at a higher risk for contracting malaria and experiencing adverse outcomes [12, 13]. These studies have linked certain socio-demographic and behavioral factors with ITN utilization in Ghana and other SSA countries, including age, type of residence, and household wealth index [14]. However, these studies do not account for how utilization changes by the perceived severity of malaria and the knowledge about ITN benefits, which are key to ITN use [11, 15]. To fill this knowledge gap, the present study aims to examine the determinants of ITN utilization among women of reproductive age in Ghana, accounting for these key variables. The investigation was guided by the theoretical underpinning of McLeroy’s socio-ecological model, which posits that behavior affects and is equally affected by multiple factors at different levels [16]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the utilization of ITNs is influenced by the characteristics and behavior of individuals and communities, which, in turn, affect the prevalence of malaria in the community. Several other studies have applied a similar framework to contextualize the health-seeking and preventative behavior against malaria in various countries [17, 18].

Given that ITNs are the most widely accessible malaria prevention strategy and are freely distributed, understanding the determinants for their use in a key population could inform strategies to boost their use in the country. Some studies have reported the effectiveness of ITNs in preventing malaria transmission, particularly in high-burden settings, although some challenges have been reported in their utilization [19]. The findings of this study can provide additional inputs for developing appropriate strategies or strengthening existing strategies to increase the utilization of ITNs in Ghana while accounting for spatial and demographic heterogeneity. A more holistic insight into ITN use and determinants would subsequently allow for targeted approaches to reducing malaria-related morbidity and mortality in Ghana.

Methods

Study aims

This study aimed to examine the factors affecting ITN use among women of reproductive age in Ghana.

Study design and data source

We employed a cross-sectional design using de-identified, publicly available data from Ghana’s 2019 Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS). The GMIS is part of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program, a program that collects, analyzes, and disseminates nationally representative data on population health across more than 90 countries [20]. The GMIS reflects a nationwide sampling of households and assesses demographic and health-related variables. The 2019 GMIS was the second in its series, with the first conducted in 2016. GMIS employs a two-stage, stratified cluster sampling design to ensure national representativeness. To account for this complex survey structure, all analyses applied the appropriate sampling weights (v005), clustering (v021), and stratification (v022 or v023) variables provided in the dataset. The sampling weights were normalized by dividing by 1,000,000, following DHS recommendations. The survey contains questions on bed net ownership, use, and indicators of malaria knowledge, treatment-seeking behavior, prevalence of malaria and anemia, and coverage of key malaria interventions. While the GMIS includes interviews across a total of 5,799 households, 5,181 women aged 15–49 successfully completed individual interviews. The data that was analyzed in this study reflects data from 4114 women who owned at least one ITN out of the 5,181 women who completed the GMIS interview.

Setting

The geography of Ghana is variable, ranging from coastal plains in the south to dry, arid regions in the north. Given the variability in climate and environmental factors, malaria is both endemic and perennial throughout Ghana, with the Eastern, Central, and Volta regions bearing the highest burden [21]. Ghana’s health system operates through a decentralized structure, with national, regional, district, and community-level services coordinated under the Ministry of Health (MoH) and implemented by the Ghana Health Service (GHS). Ghana’s malaria control efforts are guided by the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP), a unit under the GHS. The NMCP leads policy development, coordination, resource mobilization, and implementation of interventions.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable in the study was ITN utilization. This was assessed in the GMIS dataset by a variable that recorded whether women slept under a mosquito bed net the night preceding the survey. This yielded a binary response, yes or no, which was coded as 1 and 0, respectively.

Exposure variables or predictors

The selection of the exposure variables was informed by existing literature, identified as factors influencing the use of preventive malaria interventions at both individual and community levels. The variables for the individual level included age (15–49), education (none, primary, secondary, and higher), parity (nulliparity-no child, primiparity-one child, multiparity-2-4 children, and grand multiparity-5 or more children), and sex of head of household (male or female), ITN media exposure (not exposed, exposed). These variables have been found to be associated with the utilization of malaria preventive interventions in similar studies [22, 23]. The number of mosquito bed nets in the households (0–2, 3–5, and 6 +) was also assessed in the models. This variable was previously examined in related studies [24, 25].

Community-level variables included: residence type (urban or rural) and region (Western, Central, Greater Accra, Volta, Eastern, Ashanti, Brong Ahafo, Northern, Upper East, Upper West). Similarly, these factors have been found to be associated with the utilization of malaria preventive interventions [26].

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the characteristics of the participants by various demographic variables. Rao-Scott Chi-square tests were used to assess the differences in the utilization of ITN by age, education, parity, sex of head of household, type of place of residence, and exposure to malaria media messages. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between the outcome variable (ITN utilization) and exposure variables that were statistically significant in the bivariate analyses. All tests were two-sided, and significance was set at p < 0.05. SAS OnDemand for Academics was used for all statistical analyses. All analyses incorporated complex sample design elements of the 2019 GMIS.

Ethical considerations

This study involved the analysis of publicly available secondary data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS). As such, Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required for this analysis. The original GMIS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ghana Health Service Ethical Review Committee and the Inner-City Fund (ICF). The dataset used for this study was fully anonymized, and all personal identifiers were removed prior to access.

Participants with three or more ITNs reported a higher ITN use (63.7%) compared to those with one or two ITNs (45.8%). Knowledge about ITNs was significantly associated with their use (p = 0.002), with participants who were knowledgeable about ITNs showing a higher utilization (58.1%) compared to those without this knowledge (52.3%). Discussion

In this large population-based cross-sectional study, we investigated the factors associated with ITN utilization among women of reproductive age in Ghana. We found that 79% of the study population had at least one ITN, but only 56% of ITN owners had used it the night prior to the survey. Comparable to the 79% ITN ownership and the 56% utilization found in this study, higher ITN ownership has been reported in other countries, including Sierra Leone (87.6%), Togo (96.7%), and Ethiopia (91.0%). Similarly, these countries have reported higher utilization rates compared to Ghana, with 65.0%, 68.3%, and 76.5% reported in Ethiopia (2009), Togo (2011), and Sierra Leone (2012), respectively [27–30]. This finding highlights a major need for Ghana to boost its ITN coverage by improving supply channels and specifically addressing the heterogeneity in use amongst women of reproductive age by region and age through tailored strategies and monitoring.

The fertility rate in Ghana among women of reproductive age continues to decline, but is still considered higher than in other comparable countries, with 3.56 children per woman [31]. Our study revealed that women with children were more likely to use ITNs compared to women without children. Specifically, primiparous women were 65% more likely, multiparous women 93% more likely, and grand multiparous women 107% more likely to use ITNs compared to nulliparous women. This finding may be due to the perceived risk of malaria infection to childhood mortality, prompting the greater use of ITNs [32]. Women from high-income households showed a 69% lower likelihood of ITN utilization, while those from middle-income households demonstrated a 41% lower ITN utilization compared to women from low-income households. Several factors may explain this trend. First, women in higher-income households may perceive a lower risk of malaria due to improved housing conditions and reduced mosquito exposure. Second, they may have greater access to alternative malaria prevention methods, including insecticide sprays, repellents, or prophylactic medications. Third, ITN distribution programs often target lower-income populations, making ITNs more accessible to them through free or subsidized distribution efforts[8, 33–35].

The study further revealed a reduced likelihood of ITN use among women living in urban areas; urban women were 44% less likely to use ITNs compared to their rural counterparts. This disparity may be attributed to the higher prevalence of malaria in rural areas, which could increase perceived vulnerability and, consequently, promote greater ITN use in rural communities [26]. Additionally, the relatively low cost of ITNs compared to other malaria prevention methods may make them a more accessible and the preferred option in rural settings [28]. The higher likelihood of ITN utilization by the residents of the Brong-Ahafo (122%) and Upper West (159%) regions of Ghana compared to the Eastern region could be due to these regions being more rural, consistent with other studies that report that type of residence affects ITN use [4, 36]. These regions also have high malaria prevalence, especially in children under five and pregnant women, which increases the perceived threat, and are the target of most malaria eradication campaigns [37]. This increased perceived threat also encourages the consistent use of ITNs in these regions. The Brong-Ahafo and Upper West regions benefited from improved health facility ITN distribution, which is linked to a reliable supply chain and centralized policies [12]. Community norms, such as the perception of ITNs as a protection against mosquito nuisance rather than solely for the protection against malaria, drive up ITN utilization in these regions [38].

Women who owned three or more ITNs were 52% less likely to use them compared to women who owned one or two ITNs. This counterintuitive finding is consistent with previous studies by Maung et al. (2018) and Singh et al. (2011), which reported that an increased number of ITNs in a household does not necessarily translate to higher utilization [39, 40]. Reasons include the possibility of perceived surplus, misallocation within households, or reduced perceived need of ITNs due to availability. Households with more ITNs may not necessarily ensure equitable or proper usage among members, particularly if some nets are kept unused as reserves, are damaged, or are allocated to unused sleeping spaces. Mere availability of ITNs is not sufficient to drive their use, and behavioral, perceptual, or contextual factors such as perceived need, household sleeping arrangements, or complacency may influence utilization.

With the projected increase in urban flooding in Ghana and its role in sustaining mosquito vectors, climate-related events are expected to contribute to a rising malaria burden across Africa. In this context, higher ITN utilization in urban areas is critical to mitigating the anticipated surge in malaria cases linked to flooding. Strengthening ITN use in these settings can enhance population protection and improve malaria control efforts amid changing environmental conditions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The main strength of this study is our use of a large, nationally representative dataset, allowing for generalizability of the findings to the broader population of women of reproductive age in Ghana. Moreover, the GMIS program uses standardized data collection methods with trained professionals devoted to collecting accurate data, which minimizes bias and reduces the likelihood of misclassification commonly associated with recall-based survey data. In addition, the specific focus on women of reproductive age and the examination of many predictor variables constitutes a major strength in contextualizing the extent of ITN use amongst a key at-risk group for malaria control.

However, there are a few limitations to consider in the present study. First, due to the cross-sectional study design, causality cannot be inferred. Second, possible social desirability and inherent recall bias of over-reporting may also have influenced rates of reported net ownership and utilization. Third, the survey did not collect data on the physical condition or age of ITNs, which could affect their use and effectiveness. Fourth, data on sleeping arrangements were not captured, which may influence utilization patterns. Finally, although the study adjusted for several confounders, there may be unmeasured variables such as cultural beliefs, household decision-making dynamics, or previous malaria experiences that could affect ITN use. Despite these limitations, this study adds to the existing literature by providing an updated analysis of the factors associated with ITN utilization in Ghana using the most recent malaria-specific nationally representative data.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of factors influencing ITN utilization among women of reproductive age in Ghana. The findings underscore the significance of demographic variables such as age, education, parity, household wealth, and exposure to media in shaping ITN utilization patterns. Addressing these factors is crucial for promoting equitable access to and utilization of ITNs, ultimately contributing to effective malaria control strategies in Ghana. Future interventions should consider designing and implementing targeted health education programs in local languages that specifically address younger, nulliparous, and less-educated women who were found to have lower ITN usage rates. Again, the Ghana Health Service should utilize antenatal and postnatal care visits to reinforce ITN use messages by integrating them into maternal and child health services. Agencies such as the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP), the President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI), and the Ministry of Health should encourage national malaria programs to monitor the actual use of ITNs, and not just distribution numbers, by including behavioral indications in health surveys and reports.
