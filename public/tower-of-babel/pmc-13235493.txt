A roadmap for developing a program to self-manage post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis risk
Authors: Jackie L. Whittaker, Alison M. Hoens, Candice Archibald, Michael A. Hunt, Christina Y. Le, Justin M. Losciale, Maxi Miciak, Amber D. Mosewich, Trish Silvester-Lee, Linda K. Truong, Dominic Wade, Ewa M. Roos, Linda C. Li, SOAR Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Youth and young adults who experience a traumatic knee injury (i.e., ligament, meniscus, or cartilage injury; intra-articular fracture or dislocation) have a 6-fold elevated risk of early-onset post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis (PTOA), contributing to joint pain and disability for most of their adult lives. This reality represents a daunting personal and societal burden. Currently, there is no proven clinical program that supports individuals at-risk of PTOA self-manage their knee health and reduce their risk, despite evidence that interventions supporting exercise, physical activity, and healthy body composition have promise. This paper describes the development of the Stop OsteoARthritis (SOAR) Program, a multi-component, virtual program that integrates education, behaviour change techniques, tailored progressive exercise and physical activity, self-monitoring, and regular physiotherapist interactions. Guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, we describe and reflect on our iterative process for 1) identifying key elements and strategies to self-managing knee health and PTOA risk, 2) developing a program to deliver these elements and strategies, and 3) exploring barriers, facilitators, and opportunities to implementing the program in clinical settings. This information provides a roadmap for developing other programs aimed at reducing the enormous burden of musculoskeletal conditions.

Introduction

The prevalence of osteoarthritis (OA) in adults in their third and fourth decade is growing, with a 66% surge in cases between 1990 and 2019 [[1], [2], [3]]. The rise of early-onset OA is driven by an increased incidence of risk factors, including joint injury [4], inactivity, and obesity [5,6].

Youth and young adults who experience a traumatic knee injury including an isolated or combined ligament, meniscus or cartilage injury, intra-articular fracture or dislocation, are at a 6-fold-elevated risk of post-traumatic knee OA (PTOA) [7,8], with approximately 50% developing radiographic OA within 10 years [9,10]. Given the early age of onset, this can lead to joint pain and disability for much of their adult lives, contributing to inactivity, disability, sick leave, early retirement, reduced quality of life, and elevated premature mortality [11,12]. This represents an alarming personal and societal burden [11,13].

Despite empirical and experiential knowledge about the elevated risk of OA after injury, there is a critical care gap. People who have a traumatic knee injury are rarely aware of their risk for PTOA, nor do they receive care that supports them to self-manage their knee health and PTOA risk long-term [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. This inaction fuels a need commonly voiced by knee OA patients - ‘I had no idea my injury would impact my whole life; I wish I had known what I could have done differently’. On a positive note, interventions that support exercise, physical activity, and healthy body composition show promise for improving health outcomes after knee injuries [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]].

To address the current care gap for people at risk of knee PTOA, our team developed the Stop OsteoARthritis (SOAR) program. This multi-component, virtual program integrates education, behaviour change techniques, tailored progressive exercise and physical activity, self-monitoring, and regular physiotherapy interactions to promote self-management of knee health and PTOA risk. The development process has involved designing, evaluating and refining SOAR, from initial conception through feasibility [18,20,22] and proof-of-concept studies [21,22,24] [25]. To enhance the transferability and scalability of the program into the real-world, we have prioritized early and authentic engagement of key interest holders (groups or individuals with a legitimate interest in a health issue either because they are affected by it or responsible for decisions that could be informed by research evidence) [26], and understanding contextual factors (i.e., barriers, facilitators, opportunities) that could influence implementation [25].

Using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research 2.0 (CFIR) [27], this paper describes the development of the SOAR program, including 1) identifying key elements and strategies for self-managing knee health and PTOA risk (who, what, where and how), 2) developing a program to deliver these elements and strategies, and 3) exploring implementation barriers, facilitators, and opportunities in clinical settings (Fig. 1). The team

The SOAR team is an interdisciplinary collaboration of clinician scientists (physiotherapists), researchers (musculoskeletal health, rehabilitation, person-centered care, health services research, patient-reported outcomes expertise), and patient (lived experience spanning knee injury, surgery, PTOA and knee arthroplasty) and provider (physiotherapists, primary care physicians, and orthopaedic surgeons) partners. Guided by a pragmatist worldview, the team prioritizes practical solutions to the real-world challenge of preventing early-onset knee OA. This includes methodological flexibility, drawing on both quantitative and qualitative approaches, and valuing knowledge for its usefulness in informing patient care and implementation strategies. Patient and provider partners’ engagement was consistent with best practice [28], and corresponded to the IAPP2 spectrum collaborate category, which emphasizes partnering in all decisions, developing alternatives, identifying solutions [29].

We recognize the team members' clinical and research paradigms, social positionality, and multiple, evolving roles (e.g., researcher, patient and provider) and perspectives shaped the development of the SOAR program [30]. Our clinician-scientists and researchers, trained across physiotherapy, epidemiology, and health services disciplines, value and are committed to evidence-informed medicine, person-centered [31] prevention care models, and equitable health care access. Patient partners span varying stages of adversarial growth (positive changes experienced from struggling with highly challenging life circumstances) with contributions ranging from authentic naïve personal experiences to collective experiences and reflecting different stages of recovery and research readiness [32]. Team members’ social positionality spanned gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic background, race and professional roles. While this diversity enriched decision-making, it also influenced how problems were framed, needs prioritized and solutions designed. By explicitly acknowledging these influences, we highlight the need for reflexivity in collaborative intervention development.

The consolidated framework for implementation research (CIFR)

CFIR is a widely used implementation science framework [27,33] for identifying, organizing, and assessing contextual factors that work for and against implementation of an innovation (e.g., new clinical program) across contexts [33]. We chose CFIR as an organizing framework to describe SOAR program development given that research focused solely on assessing the effectiveness of complex interventions [25,34] (multiple interacting components targeting a range of behaviours, outcomes, target groups, or implementation settings) often fails to deliver acceptable, feasible and scalable solutions [[35], [36], [37]], contributing to the underuse of effective health care solutions [38].

CFIR consists of 5 domains: Innovation, Inner setting, Outer setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process (Supplemental File 1) [33]. Briefly, the Innovation domain refers to the new program that continues after implementation (e.g., SOAR program) including its source, evidence-base, design, adaptability (ability to modify or tailor to fit local contexts), trialability (ability to test or pilot on a small scale), and cost. Inner Setting refers to the setting where the innovation is implemented (e.g., fee for service physiotherapy clinic), while the Outer Setting refers to the setting the inner setting exists within (e.g., provincial health system). The Individuals domain describes the roles and characteristics of individuals linked to the innovation (e.g., patients and physiotherapists), while the Implementation Process domain refers to the process framework [39] and/or activities and strategies [40] used to implement an innovation.

CFIR is a pragmatic framework that allows researchers to select the most relevant domain(s) to describe or evaluate an innovation, implementation context, implementation process, or research findings. Because it is an overarching framework, CFIR can act as a scaffold for other implementation and behavioural science theories and models (e.g., COM-B [41]), reporting guidelines (e.g., Template for Intervention Description and Replication; TiDier [42,43]), or frameworks (e.g., Knowledge-to-Action Cycle [44], RE-AIM [45]). We draw on all 5 CFIR domains to describe how we 1) identified key elements and strategies to incorporate within SOAR, 2) developed SOAR, and 3) explored contextual factors to plan for implementing SOAR in clinical settings.

Identifying key elements and strategies for self-managing knee health and PTOA risk

In developing a program to self-manage knee health and PTOA risk, it was essential to understand who is at risk (target population) and why (treatment targets and parameters). It was also important to consider the context for assessing and refining the program, including who was going to receive (users), deliver (providers), administer, and facilitate the program, and in what setting(s), alongside relevant theory. The source of this information was empirical, theoretical and experiential with patient and provider partner perspectives incorporated through their participation in developing international consensus recommendations [46], conversations reflecting on individuals and settings best suited to deliver and support a program, and co-interpretation of qualitative research study findings [15].

Target population(CFIR innovation–evidence-base domain)

Knee injuries are most prevalent between the ages of 15 and 30 years participating in sport and recreational activities [47]. Collectively, these injuries are associated with a 6-fold (HR 5.7; 95%CI 5.0,6.6) increased risk of OA (∼10 years) [7,8],-fold increased odds of arthroplasty (∼20 years) [48]. PTOA risk varies by injury type, with Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) tears (HR 8.2; 95%CI 5.9,11.4), meniscal tears (HR 7.6; 95%CI 5.5,10.5), and intra-articular fractures (HR 7.0; 95%CI 4.2,11.7) associated with a higher risk than collateral ligament tears (HR 4.9; 95%CI 3.3,7.3) [7,8]. Informed by a systematic review and meta-analysis of modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors for knee PTOA [7], the international OPTIKNEE consensus recommendation for who to target to prevent knee PTOA is ‘people with single and multi-structure injuries, and in particular those with an ACL and/or meniscus tear or an intra-articular fracture or dislocation, with priority given to people with symptoms and/or functional deficits that persist beyond usual recovery times, or who experienced a subsequent injury, including surgery’. [46].

Treatment targets and parameters(CFIR innovation–evidence-base domain)

PTOA is a disease characterized by cartilage degradation caused by maladaptive repair responses initiated with injury [49]. The altered cartilage metabolism and degradation can be further compounded by muscle weakness [7,50], altered movement and loading [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57]], and adiposity [6,58,59]. Based on evidence that several of these mechanisms are modifiable with exercise-based activities [[60], [61], [62]], and clinical guidelines for rehabilitation of knee injuries [60,[63], [64], [65]], the international OPTIKNEE consensus recommendation for what to do and when, to prevent knee PTOA states ‘promote knee health through education and exercise-based activities that target risk factors for re-injury and OA including knowledge gaps about knee health and OA, thigh muscle weakness, inactivity and adiposity, using an approach that is person-centered, builds capacity for self-management, starts a soon as possible after injury, and continues across the lifespan’ [46].

Context for assessing the refining a program to self-manage knee health and PTOA risk

Understanding the context for assessing and refining a program to support self-management of knee health and PTOA risk involved considering the roles and characteristics of individuals influenced or who influence the program, and implementation setting. To identify these, we reflected on the target population, treatment targets and treatment parameters, and identified individuals and settings best suited to deliver and support a program. We also conducted research studies, including evidence syntheses, to identify patient priorities and contextual factors that could influence engagement.

Individuals (CFIR Innovation–source and Individuals domain): During program development, we focused on the roles and characteristics of individuals who would receive, deliver, and support the program through provider training, or as a referral source, and research team members who would support administrative functions, infrastructure and fidelity, and ensure the program is grounded in real-world perspectives (Table 1). Setting (CFIR Inner Setting domain): Depending on the health system and available resources, the care of individuals with traumatic knee injuries takes place within public (primary care, specialist care, outpatient rehabilitation), occupational health, or fee-for-service (specialist care, rehabilitation) settings. ‘Usual care’ is described as episodic, fragmented, reactive and focused solely on the acute injury and related surgeries. This approach to care leaves patients lacking knowledge, skills and confidence to manage their elevated risk for knee PTOA [15,18,66]. A tailored program supporting patients to take a proactive role in self-managing their knee health and PTOA risk would address this care gap, while aligning with shifting societal expectations and evolving health policy priorities for disease prevention, person-centered care, and patient empowerment [67,68].

Considering the target population, treatment targets and parameters (Table 2), healthcare provider scopes, and regional healthcare infrastructure (existing care pathways), the SOAR team prioritized the musculoskeletal physiotherapy practice setting. This could include publicly funded outpatient, occupational health, and/or private fee-for-service clinics. Although these settings differ in size, resources, funding models, and patient demographics, they share a commitment to evidence-based, patient-centered care, and have demonstrated an acceptance of virtual care models after the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted access to in-person care [69]. Research studies (CFIR Innovation–evidence-base domain): To identify patient priorities and contextual factors that could influence program engagement, we conducted qualitative studies to explore patients' priorities for exercise, rehabilitation and physical activity after knee injury [15,17,66,71], and scoping reviews to summarize the psychological, social and contextual factors impacting knee injury recovery [16], and exercise-therapy adherence for musculoskeletal conditions [72]. Collectively, these studies highlight that a program must be accessible (physically and financially), support patients’ desires for transparency, autonomy (e.g., actively cope, set action plans, problem solve, contribute to decision-making), hope, and opportunities to practice in a safe setting, while leveraging motivations to return to activity or sport, social support, and life-long health [15,16,18,22,66,71].

Relevant theory(CFIR innovation-evidence-base domain)

Two main theoretical bodies of evidence that were seen relevant for informing a program to self-manage knee health and PTOA risk - behaviour change theory and complex intervention theory.

Behaviour change: Behaviour change theories can help to identify strategies that support the initiation and maintenance of exercise (structured, planned and purposeful activities to improve or maintain lower-limb strength, neuromuscular control and functional capacity) and physical activity (leisure, sport, work or daily living activities that support knee health, general fitness and healthy body composition) behaviors - critical for improving knee health and mitigating PTOA risk. Of direct relevance are the COM-B model and Theoretical Domains Framework [41,73] and evidence about behaviour change techniques associated with the largest exercise/physical activity treatment effects [74,75]. Collectively this theory stresses the importance of incorporating strategies that promote psychological (knowledge and decision-making skills to engage in a behaviour) and physical (skills and abilities to engage in a behaviour) capability, ensure physical (conducive environment and resources to engage in a behaviour) and social (social influences that ‘normalize’ a behaviour) opportunity, and foster automatic (emotions linked to a behaviour) and reflective (beliefs and thoughts about ability and consequences of a behaviour) motivation, as well as tailoring, goal setting, intentional and reflective self-monitoring, and performance feedback into a program to self-manage knee health and PTOA risk [15,73,[76], [77], [78], [79], [80]].

Complex intervention: Complex intervention theory provides a foundation for designing and refining a multicomponent program that can work in real-world conditions. Drawing on the UK Medical Research Council framework [25,34,81], this theory highlights the need to clearly define and justify intervention components, consider how components interact with each other and with implementation contexts, and the value of adaptable and iterative development. These principles inform how to structure and integrate key elements within a program to ensure it accommodates different users, providers, and settings. By attending to complexity at the outset, a program is more likely to be theoretically sound, feasible, scalable, and responsive to changing evidence and interest users’ needs [25].

Developing a program to self-managing knee health and PTOA risk

The process for packaging the key elements and strategies into a program to self-manage knee health and PTOA risk was underpinned by the UK MRCs Framework for complex interventions [25], and included design, feasibility testing and refinement, and proof-of-concept testing and refinement (Fig. 1). Supplemental File 2 provides a summary of key findings from studies informing the development of the SOAR program.

Design(CFIR innovation–source, design, adaptability, trialability, complexity, costdomains)

The SOAR program was designed through an iterative, collaborative process. To integrate the diverse perspectives and lived experiences of the SOAR team, methods included a naming contest (patients and providers), group workshops, one-to-one consultations, experience mapping, iterative prototyping, role-playing scenarios, and informal discussions. When consensus was not present, the team moved forward with the most feasible option with the strongest overall support. The most significant challenges were finding a balance between intervention complexity and real-world clinical settings, and promoting patient capability while allowing physiotherapists to share their expertise and validate patient decisions.

The team brainstormed methods that could be used for equitable delivery of the identified program elements and strategies. Methods shown to promote capability, opportunity, and motivation for exercise and physical activity behaviours (e.g., action planning), and consistent with clinical reasoning models were prioritized. To support implementation and scalability, we also considered feasibility, acceptability, adaptability, trialability, cost, and resources typically available in musculoskeletal physiotherapy settings.

What resulted was a structured complex intervention that integrates person-centered education, behaviour change techniques, and exercise therapy and physical activity to equip patients to proactively manage their knee symptoms and long-term knee health (Table 3). The program's complexity is reflected in the number of components and degree of coordination required across recipients, providers, and administrators, but is managed through thoughtful design, alignment of providers roles and scope, and supporting materials that facilitate delivery in diverse contexts. Feasibility testing and refinement(CRIF innovation–evidence-base domain)

To understand SOAR feasibility, we conducted a quasi-experimental study involving 30 participants with lived experience of various knee injuries, balanced by gender, age, injury type and time since injury who received a 4-week abridged version of SOAR delivered by 4 physiotherapists [20]. We assessed SOAR implementation (e.g., adherence, activity wear time, physiotherapy fidelity), practicality (e.g., adverse events, goal completion), acceptability (ratings of program components value) and preliminary efficacy (e.g., self-reported knee-related symptoms, function and quality-of-life [82], functional restrictions [83], perceived self-management [84], and physical activity [85]) outcomes [86,87]. Findings suggested the program could be delivered as planned, filled an important care gap, was acceptable to participants and providers, and had the potential to change important outcomes including self-reported self-management, physical activity, sport and recreation function, and knee-related quality-of-life (Supplemental File 2).

To gain insights into participant's experiences and perceptions of SOAR, we nested a qualitative description study with 16 of the 30 participants (varied age, gender, program satisfaction) [18]. Participants described SOAR as empowering and reported a strengthened sense of autonomy in managing their knee health. Personalized goal-setting, self-monitoring, and regular physiotherapist interactions were identified as instrumental in building motivation and accountability, while the participant-physiotherapist therapeutic alliance contributed to acceptability and confidence to self-manage. There was also high acceptance of virtual care delivery. Participants reported low satisfaction with exercise and physical activity tracking methods, and lack of physical interactions and touch.

These studies informed program refinements, including adjustments to physiotherapist training and educational topics and materials, emphasizing the value of tracking for enhancing autonomy and active coping, and efforts to make exercise and physical activity tracking more practical (replace digital application with a simple online form), support virtual engagement, and streamline workflow.

Proof-of-concept testing and refinement(CFIR innovation–evidence-base domain)

To assess program efficacy, we conducted a randomized delayed controlled trial involving 53 participants 12-48-months past a medical attention, time-loss knee injury who received an 8-week SOAR program (delivered by 7 physiotherapists) either immediately after baseline testing, or after an 8-week delay [21,24]. Outcomes included knee extensor (primary) and flexor muscle strength, physical activity [85], kinesiophobia [88], knee-related symptoms, function and quality-of-life [82], knee self-efficacy [89], perceived self-management [84], hop function [90], fat mass index, as well as implementation, practicality and acceptability constructs. Despite minimal effect on knee muscle strength, the program was associated with positive changes in self-management, kinesiophobia, physical activity, achieving a Patient Acceptable Symptoms State, and high participant and provider buy-in. Participants in the immediate group received the program for an added 8-weeks (16-weeks total), with continued improvements seen in self-reported knee self-efficacy, quality-of-life, pain and sport and recreation function, and hop function. Detailed summary statistics for all outcomes at 8- and 16-weeks have been previously reported [21], and are summarized in Supplemental File 2.

To better understand how social support influences exercise participation and adherence, we nested a qualitative interpretive description study involving 15 of the 54 participants (varied age, gender, time since injury, injury type, program satisfaction). We identified that exercise behaviour was influenced by individual factors (e.g., motivation or knowledge), and social and relational contexts. Participants emphasized how supportive physiotherapists, family members, and peers were critical for encouraging and sustaining exercise behaviours, with the participant-physiotherapist therapeutic alliance (characterized by trust, collaboration, and shared goals) particularly influential. Participants described physiotherapists who listened, adapted care to their needs, and celebrated their progress as crucial to adherence and motivation. The findings suggest that intentionally fostering relational elements (e.g., empathy, clear communication, and continuity of care) to support a strong therapeutic alliance and integrating mechanisms for social support are likely to enhance the acceptability and effectiveness of the program.

Program refinements after this study included adjustments to physiotherapist training, and educational topics and materials focused on achieving an adequate exercise dose to stimulate physiological muscle changes, and to enable physiotherapists to further share their expertise and validate participants' decisions. We also identified opportunities to reduce program complexity by further simplifying exercise tracking forms, automating the sharing of activity data, and to add a tapering period at the end of the program to support participants’ transition to self-management.

Summary of SOAR development

SOAR is a complex intervention designed by a collaborative interdisciplinary team. The empirical, theoretical, and experiential evidence supporting the program spans the early to intermediate stages of intervention development. Preliminary studies consistently demonstrate that SOAR addresses a critical care gap and is perceived by participants and providers as credible, acceptable, practical, and relevant. Participants describe SOAR as empowering, highlighting gains in autonomy, accountability, and confidence to self-manage their knee health. Providers confirm SOAR is manageable within existing workflows and adaptable to individual needs. A key reflection is that by using multi-methods, we were able to reveal relationships and patterns that would not have been detected by quantitative designs alone, confirming that the impact of complex interventions requires multiple approaches. Collectively, the evidence positions SOAR as a promising intervention for the scalable prevention of early-onset knee PTOA. We emphasize that SOAR remains under development, and conclusions about its effectiveness require confirmation in a fully powered hybrid (Type I) effectiveness–implementation RCT currently underway [91].

Exploring barriers, facilitators and opportunities for implementing SOAR in clinical settings (CFIR innovation, individuals, inner setting, Outer Setting domains)

Knowledge about barriers, facilitators, and opportunities is foundational to developing a practical, targeted, and context-specific plan to support the implementation of SOAR in real-world clinical settings. A better understanding of these contextual factors can help us tailor plans to prevent or reduce the impact of perceived barriers, leverage existing strengths to accelerate uptake, and highlight where adaptations could increase reach, effectiveness, and sustainability. Looking to the future (CFIR implementation process domain)

Building on the promise of the SOAR program and insights gained during development, we can move forward to build a targeted, context-specific plan for implementing SOAR in real-world clinical settings. Guided by the CFIR Implementation Process domain (teaming, addressing needs, addressing context, planning, tailoring strategies, engaging, doing, reflecting and evaluating, and adapting) [33], and RE-AIM framework (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance) framework [45], the process will draw on strategies for the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change compilation. These include site-specific structured needs and readiness assessments, defining individuals' roles and timelines, tailoring workflow and communication, identifying and preparing clinical and administrative champions, staged roll-outs; audit/feedback mechanisms (Supplemental File 3) [40]. This approach will ensure that implementation activities address patients, provider's, and clinic and system contexts to support sustainable integration of SOAR.

Conclusion

The SOAR program was systematically co-developed with patient and provider partners, building on empirical, theoretical and experiential evidence to address a critical care gap for people at risk of early-onset knee PTOA. The integration of person-centered education, behaviour change strategies, and tailored exercise and physical activity offers a scalable, clinically feasible approach that aligns with best-practice care. By embedding implementation considerations throughout development, SOAR is designed for real-world adoption, sustainability, and impact. Future research will evaluate its effectiveness and implementation in clinical settings to optimize knee health outcomes and reduce the burden of PTOA.
