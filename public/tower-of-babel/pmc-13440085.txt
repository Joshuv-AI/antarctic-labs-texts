Predicting early intervention outcomes in autism via individual participant data mega-analysis
Authors: Veronica Mandelli, Elena Maria Busuoli, Michel Godel, Nada Kojovic, Yana Sinai-Gavrilov, Tali Gev, Annarita Contaldo, Eric Courchesne, Karen Pierce, Ofer Golan, Antonio Narzisi, Filippo Muratori, Costanza Colombi, Sally J Rogers, Giacomo Vivanti, Marie Schaer, Liliana Ruta, Michael V Lombardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutism early intervention meta-analyses have yielded important insights into questions such as ‘what works’ and ‘for what’ outcomes. However, because these studies primarily rely on study-level summary statistics, they may be limited with regard to more individualized insights. Mega-analyses utilizing individual participant data (IPD-MA) may be useful for these more individualized questions.MethodsWe conducted an IPD-MA on the Autism Early Intervention Research (AEIR) consortium dataset, comprising n = 582 autistic children (n = 121 female) across 11 datasets collected in clinical and community settings in the USA, Switzerland, Italy, Israel, and Australia. Children received between 3 and 27 months of early intervention (age at start 13–60 months) of varying intensity. Of these datasets, one originates from a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Another 7 datasets are paired up into 5 separate controlled-group design studies, while the remaining 3 datasets come from uncontrolled pre–post design studies. Early Start Denver Model (ESDM; n = 281, 62 female) was compared against non-ESDM treatment-as-usual/community approaches (n = 301, 59 female) (e.g., speech and occupational therapy, applied behavioral analysis, pivotal response training). Outcome variables were Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL) age-equivalent scores, Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS) standardized scores, and Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) calibrated severity scores.ResultsPredictors such as sex and cumulative intervention intensity were largely not associated with change in outcomes. Age at intervention start and pre-intervention developmental quotient (DQ) were strong moderators across all outcomes. Earlier age at intervention start predicted more positive outcomes, while higher pre-intervention DQ predicted accelerated growth on VABS motor and MSEL outcome measures. Interventions were similar in their effects on MSEL and VABS outcomes. However, for ADOS outcomes, ESDM resulted in significantly declining trajectories over time, with the sharpest decline for high pre-intervention DQ individuals. In contrast, ADOS trajectories in non-ESDM diverge in opposite directionalities (i.e. increases or decreases) depending on individual’s pre-intervention DQ.LimitationsData for the IDP-MA was contributed on a voluntary basis and only 1 study was contributed from an RCT. Therefore, the findings may be prone to self-selection bias that may limit generalizability. Inferences from the intervention type comparison (ESDM versus non-ESDM) may be limited due to the heterogeneous approaches lumped together in the non-ESDM category. Larger sample sizes are required for finer-grained comparisons of different intervention types.ConclusionsAge at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ are key individualized predictors and the latter can interact with intervention type to moderate early intervention response.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13229-026-00736-x.

Autism is one of the most common neurodevelopmental conditions in society today and represents a broad population of individuals that are heterogeneous across multiple scales, from genotype to phenotype [1], across development [2], and across important clinical outcomes (e.g., responses to intervention) [3–5]. A top priority on the path towards precision medicine is the development of early intervention approaches that facilitate developmental gains and positive outcomes aligned with multiple different stakeholder perspectives [6–8]. The push for earlier diagnosis and intervention is also paramount [9, 10] and predicated on the idea that there is higher probability of facilitating more positive outcomes because of the greater neural plasticity offered during the earliest periods of neurodevelopment [11]. The top priorities/questions for early intervention research are quite clear. We seek to better understand 1) what works, 2) for whom, and 3) for what [12]? Since heterogeneity in response to early intervention is a high priority topic, it is important to further unpack the ‘for whom’ question. We need to better understand how and why early intervention is facilitating differential individualized outcomes. For these important questions, we have to go beyond examination of on-average group-differences due to intervention and dissect what are the factors or characteristics present in children before intervention begins that would help us predict the subsequent developmental path during a particular intervention [4, 5, 13].

Prior reviews and meta-analyses of autism early intervention research provide an initial starting point on the ‘what works’ and ‘for what’ questions. Such work helps us get a better sense of how early intervention may or may not have effects at a group-level on children’s functional outcomes. However, despite extensive culls through the literature on early intervention, results can seem mixed (e.g. [12, 14–22]). For example, without filtering or controlling for study quality/bias, there is some evidence that various types of early intervention (e.g., developmental and naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions; NDBI) can be effective on-average in changing a variety of outcomes including language, intellectual and social communication abilities [12, 20, 21, 23]. However, a somewhat different picture emerges when evaluating a smaller handful of high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCT). Using this particular restriction, earlier meta-analyses showed that several types of early behavioral interventions may be limited in effectiveness in changing cognitive, language, or core autism symptom domains [12, 14, 18]. However, a more recent updated meta-analysis suggests that some intervention types like naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions (NDBI) (e.g., Early Start Denver Model, ESDM) can promote positive outcomes in cognitive, language, adaptive functioning, and core diagnostic characteristics of autism [21].

While meta-analytic inferences are very important, there may also be some limitations for some research priorities/questions. First, meta-analytic work enables tests of whether replicable non-zero group-level effects exist in the literature. This goal is tailored to answer the ‘what works’ and ‘for what’ questions quite well. Nevertheless, these goals may be complicated when heterogeneity in response to intervention exists (i.e. answering the ‘for whom’ question). Although evidence from high-quality RCTs suggests that group-level effects vary considerably across the literature [12, 14, 18, 21], this does not preclude the fact that the interventions may still have important effects on specific types of individuals. Thus, a different analytic approach may be complementary for answering the ‘for whom’ question. Approaches such as individual participant data meta-analysis (IPD-MA) or ‘mega-analysis’ [24] may be complementary to meta-analysis by allowing for deeper insight than what may be possible from meta-analysis alone.

Mega-analysis utilizes the raw data from individual participants across many studies and allows for testing not only group-level effects, but also factors that explain individual differences in intervention response. To our knowledge, 3 IPD-MA/mega-analyses exist in the literature [19, 25, 26]. One study compared early intensive interventions, such as variants of early intensive behavioral intervention (EIBI) and NDBI, to ‘treatment-as-usual’ (TAU) or eclectic interventions. The primary findings here suggest that early intensive interventions have a small effect on intellectual and adaptive functioning. Other moderating individual difference factors such as sex, age, baseline cognitive or adaptive functioning level, did not interact with intervention type to explain heterogeneity in intervention response [19]. A second more recent IPD-MA examined applied behavior analysis (ABA) versus eclectic TAU interventions. While regression analyses at the level of individual-participant data did not explicitly compare ABA versus TAU, the investigators did find significant differences in the percentage of individuals with adaptive and intellectual functioning scores in the non-clinical range following intervention. Additionally, reliable change index (RCI) scores were higher in ABA compared to TAU intervention for adaptive, intellectual functioning, and autism severity outcomes. Finally, intervention intensity, operationalized as number of hours per week, had a significant effect on adaptive, intellectual functioning, and autism severity outcomes [25]. A third IDP-MA compared non-speaking children at intervention start whom did (‘advancers’) versus did not (‘non-advancers’) acquire single words or phrase speech [26]. Four broad intervention categories were compared but could not differentiate ‘advancers’ from ‘non-advancers’. Children that did not advance to acquiring phrase speech had later intervention start and shorter intervention duration, and had higher autism severity, lower adaptive functioning, and verbal and non-verbal developmental quotients (DQ) at baseline. In summary, 2 of the 3 previous IPD-MA studies examined ABA-based compared to TAU interventions and found some effects of intervention type. The other study compared initial non-speaking children whom either advanced or not in terms of language milestones by the end of intervention. This study found little evidence of differences across four broad intervention categories. All 3 IDP-MAs found that moderators such as baseline child characteristics (e.g., cognitive and adaptive functioning), and intervention characteristics (e.g., intensity) are important predictors of outcomes.

A second limitation of meta-analytic work is that statistical power to detect on-average group-level effects may be low when restrictions are placed on filtering for only a small subset of high-quality studies. Indeed, in contrast to earlier meta-analyses, the more recent updated meta-analyses with higher statistical power via the inclusion of more studies, tends to reveal somewhat difference inferences [21]. Additionally, the small handful of existing high-quality RCT studies tend to have relatively small sample sizes. In the case of small sample sizes, unadjusted descriptive statistics and standardized effects size computed from them will be inherently less precise and possibly more prone to effect size inflation [1]. Utilizing unadjusted statistics in meta-analysis is a necessary step to homogenize effect size computation across heterogeneous studies. However, this attribute does not allow for correction for covariates within-study that would typically be applied in each individual study. To tackle this issue to some extent, moderator analyses from meta-regression models can be implemented to test associations with study-specific factors and covariates (e.g., mean age at intervention start). These moderator analyses are limited though to study-specific summary factors (e.g., mean age) that themselves are descriptive statistics computed per study and are inherently not the same as measuring and controlling for those covariates on individuals (e.g., age at intervention start per individual). Again, utilization of mega-analysis addresses this limitation by allowing for covariates to be applied within-study at the level of individuals.

To overcome these limitations, we developed the Autism Early Intervention Research (AEIR) consortium to pool together individual participant data from many studies on early intervention in autism for the purpose of conducting mega-analyses. AEIR has been able to pool together 645 autistic individuals with age range between 13 to 60 months. Intervention approaches were reduced to two main types: Early Start Denver Model (ESDM) and non-ESDM high-quality early interventions approaches (e.g., ABA, discrete trial training (DTT), pivotal response training (PRT)) as well as interventions employed in community settings and commonly referred ‘treatment-as-usual’ (e.g., speech, occupational, psychomotor therapy). A contrast of ESDM versus non-ESDM is utilized in this work for sake of convenience, given the roughly half split between these two intervention approaches amongst the final contributed data within the AEIR consortium dataset (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1). Contrasting ESDM to non-ESDM approaches in a mega-analysis can be useful for comparing to other meta-analyses [23] as well as previous individual high-quality ESDM RCT studies [27–30]. This study complements another prior IDP-MA that compared ESDM to other intervention approaches [26]. However, this prior IDP-MA focused on a subset of autistic children that were non-speaking at intervention start. In contrast, the current work focuses on all autistic children enrolled in available intervention datasets for the IDP-MA. Comparing the current work to the 2 other prior IDP-MAs, these prior studies did not focus on ESDM and instead focused more on EIBI approaches [19, 25]. In addition to contrasting the effects of ESDM vs non-ESDM intervention types, we also evaluate main effects and interactions between factors such as the child’s sex, cumulative intervention intensity, age at intervention start, and pre-intervention DQ as possible factors that can predict significant variance in intervention responses.

Methods

All secondary data analysis reported here was approved by the Province of Trento Azienda Provinciale per i Servizi Sanitaria (APSS) ethical committee in accordance to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Datasets

Data utilized in this study come from 11 international datasets on early intervention in autistic toddlers as part of the Autism Early Intervention Research (AEIR) consortium. From 2019 through late 2021/early 2022, searches were conducted on PubMed and Google Scholar for autism early intervention studies, focusing on approaches such as ESDM, joint attention, symbolic play, engagement and regulation (JAPSER), and Paediatric Autism Communication Therapy (PACT). Upon identifying studies, we contacted corresponding authors to ask if they could contribute individual participant data from their studies, with a requirement of contributing Mullen Early Scales of Learning (MSEL), Griffith Mental Development Scales (GMDS), Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS), and/or Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) measures. Further additional contact was made with other known researchers conducting autism early intervention research. A total of 16 individuals were initially emailed. Of those 16, n = 3 never replied to the initial email. Another n = 3 could not contribute for various reasons (e.g., lack of access to historical data, data sharing constraints, or lack of specific types of variables needed for the mega analysis). The remaining individuals replied and were able to contribute data to the consortium. The final set of datasets contributed ended up being primarily those that compared ESDM to some other non-ESDM intervention type. Thus, one reason for the dichotomous distinction between ESDM and non-ESDM was based on the fact that many of the original studies had this same type of comparison. However, due to the limited inclusion of some but not all datasets in the field, there may be a self-selection bias in the AEIR consortium dataset. All AEIR datasets have already been previously published independently [3, 29, 31–39]. The AEIR dataset comprises n = 582 (female n = 121) autistic children, with ages at intervention start ranging from between 13 and 60 months. All datasets have at least 2 timepoints within an individual but some datasets possess more than 2 timepoints per individual. Of the 11 contributed datasets, 4 could be paired up due to prior use in papers where ESDM and non-ESDM were compared (see Supplementary Table 1). After assigning dataset pairs appropriately there were a total of 9 studies. Of the 9 studies, 1 could be classified as a randomized controlled trial (RCTs). Another 5 studies were classified as controlled-group design studies, while the remaining 3 were classified as uncontrolled pre–post design studies. Because the majority of studies in AEIR are not RCTs, it is likely that intervention types may not be closely matched on a range of baseline characteristics. Analysis of the full AEIR dataset (see Results) confirmed certain biases across baseline variables. Therefore, we implemented a procedure to control for baseline differences called propensity score matching (PSM). PSM was used to ensure that intervention types were matched on a number of baseline variables. The resulting PSM final sample was n = 484 (female n = 98), broken down into ESDM (n = 242, female n = 55) versus non-ESDM (n = 242, female n = 43) intervention types. See Supplementary Table 1 for characteristics of individual datasets and Supplementary Table 2 for characteristics of ESDM vs non-ESMD samples (Fig. 1A).

Early intervention programs

Participants received between 3 and 27 months of early intervention from varying approaches and variable levels of intervention intensity (Supplementary Table 1). Early interventions also varied in their format of administration and could be comprised of individual, group, and parent intervention components. Given the considerable number of participants that completed one type of early intervention known as Early Start Denver Model (ESDM) we partitioned the data into two groups: 1) ESDM (n = 241, females = 62) or 2) non-ESDM intervention (non-ESDM; n = 301, females = 59). The ESDM programs were implemented either as individual (1:1) therapy or as group and school-based interventions (please refer to each individual study for detailed information, Supplementary Table 1). The non-ESDM interventions represent a combination of other types of early interventions commonly used in the literature and in community settings (e.g., speech, psychomotor, occupational therapy, ABA, DTT, PRT, Treatment and Education of Autistic and related Communication handicapped CHildren (TEACCH), etc). Notably, non-ESDM is a category that includes data from many high-quality evidence-supported approaches (e.g., PRT, TEACCH, ABA-based programs) and is not just an eclectic mix of TAU. Furthermore, within each dataset in the non-ESDM group, we could not easily split them into more granular discrete categories such as EIBI, NDBI, or other structured educational approaches (e.g., TEACCH). The reason for this is that many interventions in the non-ESDM group are combinations of multiple high-quality evidence-supported approaches (see Supplementary Table 1 for a list of different approaches utilized with the non-ESDM group for each dataset). The primary key ingredient that differentiates non-ESDM is that they do not utilize ESDM techniques specifically. A caveat for interpreting the contrast between ESDM and non-ESDM is that some of the approaches within the non-ESDM category utilize NDBI approaches (e.g., PRT) that are conceptually overlapping with elements of ESDM. However, we could not add interventions that utilize non-ESDM NDBI approaches to ESDM interventions because these other NDBI approaches were always utilized in a mixed fashion alongside other non-NDBI approaches (e.g., EIBI, TEACCH, etc). While the contrast of ESDM versus this broad non-ESDM category may be useful for highlighting specific differences due to ESDM, it may also be suboptimal as an approach due to the lack of specificity within the non-ESDM category.

Outcome measures

All datasets had commonalities with reference to measures utilized to examine cognitive ability, language, motor, adaptive functioning, and autism symptom severity. For cognitive ability, language, and motor skills, most datasets in AEIR, with the exception of those originating in Italy, utilized the Mullen Early Scales of Learning (MSEL) [40]. Scores on the MSEL that were utilized were subscales of expressive and receptive language (EL, RL), visual reception skills (VR) and fine motor (FM). For these subscales, we utilized age-equivalent scores in our developmental trajectory analyses to examine how outcome measures change over age. Adaptive functioning was measured in all datasets with the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS) [41]. For the VABS we utilized standardized scores on domains such as Communication, Socialization, Daily Living Skills, and Motor. Finally, concerning autism symptom severity, here we utilized the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS) [42–44]. ADOS CSS scores are advantageous because they are standardized relative to a child’s age and language ability and ensure comparability across ADOS administration modules. Three ADOS CSS scores are used as outcomes in this work: SA (social affect), RRB (restricted repetitive behaviors), and the Total CSS score.

Predictor measures

In this work we conducted a mega-analysis to clarify the main effects and interactions between intervention and subject characteristics, aiming to disentangle the contribution of each aspect. To achieve this, we needed to extract measures that were common across all datasets to use as predictors in our analysis. Regarding intervention-specific factors potentially able to predict intervention outcome measures, we assessed type of early intervention (ESDM or non-ESDM) and a cumulative intervention intensity index. Cumulative intervention intensity is conceptualized as the number of hours accumulated by a specific point during the intervention and is computed by multiplying the numbers of hours per week by the number of weeks acquired in intervention at a specific timepoint. We then weighted this cumulative intensity to account for the number of adults and children present in the intervention context [45]. Thus, cumulative intensity is computed as follows: cumulative intensity = (cumulative hours of intervention * number of adults)/number of children present. Concerning child characteristics at intervention start, we analyzed cognitive abilities at intervention start reported as developmental quotient scores (DQ). DQ scores were derived from the MSEL Early Learning Composite (ELC) score. The exception here was for Italian datasets whereby MSEL was not collected. In this situation, DQ scores were derived from the Griffith Mental Development Scales (GMDS) [46]. Finally, age at intervention start and biological sex were also selected and analyzed as demographic factors that might be predictors associated with intervention responses.

Statistical analyses

In the first set of analyses, we used the full AEIR dataset and evaluated whether the initial pre-intervention characteristics of children or intervention intensity were comparable between the ESDM and non-ESDM groups. To examine this, we employed a linear mixed effect model with fixed effects of intervention type, sex, and their interaction and with dataset modeled as random effect. Additionally, we utilized a chi-square test to investigate whether the distribution of males and females significantly differed by intervention type. These results showed that intervention types differed slightly in age at start and intensity. Therefore, to mitigate selection bias and baseline imbalances, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) [47]. This approach serves as a non-parametric preprocessing step to reduce model dependence when estimating treatment effects in observational data. PSM analyses were conducted in R using the MatchIt package [48]. Propensity scores were estimated for each participant via a logistic regression model, with treatment assignment predicted by three baseline covariates: age at intervention start, pre-intervention DQ, and intervention intensity index. We employed a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm. To ensure high-quality matches and maintain common support, we applied a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Following the matching procedure, balance was assessed using Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) for each covariate.

In the main set of analyses, we aimed to test the influence of the predictors on intervention outcomes. We utilized a set of linear mixed effect models to account for repeated measures and handle nested within-study factors. Three sets of models were analyzed, organized by whether the outcome measures were from MSEL, VABS, or ADOS. For MSEL models, the dependent variable was the age-equivalent scores of each of the MSEL subscales, with one model run for each subscale. Datasets from Italy were excluded from these models as these datasets did not have MSEL data available. For VABS models, the dependent variable was the standardized domain scores, with a separate model for each VABS domain. For the ADOS we employed three distinct models, with each model analyzing a different CSS score as the dependent variable. In terms of the independent variables, we used the same variables for all models to enable comparisons between dependent variables. Fixed effects in the models were age, intervention type, cumulative intervention intensity index, sex, age at intervention start, and pre-intervention DQ. Interaction effects were also modeled as fixed effects and include 2-way interactions between age and pre-intervention DQ, age at intervention start, and intervention type. We also modeled 3-way interactions of intervention type*age*age at intervention start and intervention type*age*pre-intervention DQ. For analyses using the ADOS, pre-intervention ADOS scores were also used as a covariate to account for baseline differences. Random effects were also utilized in the model to account for subject-specific age intercepts and slopes (i.e. age modeled within subject-ID, dataset). All linear mixed effect modeling was conducted in R using the lmer function within the lmerTest R library. To identify significant effects across multiple analyses, p-values were utilized to compute false discovery rate (FDR) values, and then FDR control for multiple comparisons was achieved with thresholding at q < 0.05. After running the main linear mixed effect model with 3-way interactions, we also implemented follow-up models within each intervention type, using all the same variables as the main model, except dropping terms that had to do with intervention type. These follow-up models were helpful for decomposing specific 2-way interactions and main effects within a specific intervention type. Code implementing all analyses can be found at https://gitlab.iit.it/bmp006-public/LAND_IIT/aeir1.

Results

Pre-intervention differences between ESDM and non-ESDM

In this study we compiled individual participant data from the AEIR consortium across 11 datasets (Fig. 1A) comprising n = 582 autistic individuals. Data were split into two main types of early intervention: 1) ESDM (n = 281, male = 77.9%, age range = 14–57 months) and 2) non-ESDM (n = 301, male = 80.3%, age range = 13–60 months). See Supplementary Table 2 for a breakdown of descriptive statistics of baseline measures between these intervention types. Intervention types did not differ in proportion of males versus females (χ2 = 0.39, p = 0.52). After controlling for dataset, pre-intervention DQ did not differ between intervention types (F(1,577) = 2,9, p = 0.22). Age at intervention start differed between intervention types (F(1,574) = 31.6, p = 8.65 e-08), with ESDM showing on average younger ages than non-ESDM by 3 months (mean age, ESDM = 30.77 months; non-ESDM = 33.74). Intervention intensity index, operationalized as weeks of intervention * hours per week * number of adults/number of children present, also differed between intervention types (F(1,574) = 66.5, p = 6.48e-15) with ESDM being on-average more intense than non-ESDM (Supplementary Table 3). Given these differences between intervention types, we next implemented PSM analyses to ensure tight matching of intervention types across baseline variables such as intervention intensity, age at intervention start, and pre-intervention DQ (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table 4). These analyses left us with a final dataset of n = 242 receiving ESDM and n = 242 receiving non-ESDM intervention. All further results are based on the PSM dataset.

MSEL outcome variables

Across all models using MSEL outcome variables, we found no evidence surviving FDR q < 0.05 for main effects of sex and intervention type (see Supplementary Table 5 for all statistics). Cumulative intensity also had largely no effect on most MSEL outcome variables. The exception was a significant effect of cumulative intensity on MSEL RL. This effect can be explained by generally higher RL skills with more accumulated intervention intensity.

Adding to the list of mostly null effects, there also were no significant 3-way interactions between intervention type, age, and moderator variables (i.e. age at intervention start or pre-intervention DQ) or 2-way interactions between intervention type and age or moderators (i.e. age at intervention start, pre-intervention DQ) (Supplementary Tables 5–6). The notable absence of effects that incorporate intervention type indicate that MSEL outcome variables are somewhat similar between ESDM and non-ESDM.

In contrast to the aforementioned null effects, there was some evidence for moderator variables such as age at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ significantly explaining variation in MSEL outcomes. In particular, for all MSEL outcomes we found strong main effects for these moderators as well as 2-way interactions between these moderators and age (Figs. 2B, 3B). The main exception was a non-significant age*age at start interaction for MSEL EL. The main effect of age at intervention start on MSEL EL can be explained by higher age equivalent scores with earlier age at start. Figure 2B qualitatively shows that this effect for MSEL EL is more pronounced in non-ESDM compared to ESDM. Aside from MSEL EL, the age*age at start interaction effects for VR, FM, and RL subscales can be explained by a slight developmental acceleration of trajectories (Supplementary Tables 5–6) and that can be visually shown in Fig. 2A–B. In this figure we cut age at intervention start into deciles in order to show how trajectories changes as one goes up in deciles of age at intervention start. The developmental acceleration effect describing these age*age at intervention start interactions is visually more pronounced in ESDM compared to non-ESDM and further follow-up analyses within each intervention type confirm that the interaction is significant in ESDM, but is non-significant in non-ESDM (Supplementary Tables 6). In contrast to ESDM, non-ESDM shows a conjunction of main effects of age and age at intervention start, but with little acceleration over development.

Turning to the effect of pre-intervention DQ, we found that all MSEL subscales had significant age*pre-intervention DQ interaction effects, explained as a ‘fanning out’ effect (Fig. 3A–B). Deciles with higher pre-intervention DQ have steeper trajectories than the flatter trajectories characterizing deciles with low pre-intervention DQ individuals (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Tables 5–6). Figure 3B, indicates that these interaction effects are apparent in both ESDM and non-ESDM and further follow-up analyses within each intervention type confirm strong statistically significant interventions in both ESDM and non-ESDM (Supplementary Tables 6).

Finally, in addition to analyses on specific MSEL subscales, we also ran analyses using MSEL Early Learning Composite (ELC) as a dependent variable. Only age at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ were significant predictors as main effects, with earlier age at start and higher pre-intervention DQ associated with higher MSEL ELC scores (Supplementary Table 5).

VABS outcome variables

For models using VABS domain standardized scores as outcome variables, non-significant effects at FDR q < 0.05 were observed for most main and interaction effects (Supplementary Tables 5–6). The exception was a small main effect of cumulative intensity on VABS Socialization scores, described by decreased scores with increased intensity. Other exceptions were the ubiquitous main effects of age at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ across all VABS domains. These effects are explained by higher VABS standardized scores with earlier age at intervention start and higher pre-intervention DQ (Figs. 2C, 3C; Supplementary Tables 5–6). Finally, for the VABS Motor domain, we observed a significant age*pre-intervention DQ interaction. This interaction is explained by flat trajectories for deciles with the highest pre-intervention DQ alongside progressively declining trajectories for deciles with lower pre-intervention DQ scores (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Tables 5–6). Figure 3C qualitatively shows that this effect was more pronounced in ESDM compared to non-ESDM and further follow-up analyses confirm that this interaction is statistically significant in ESDM, but is non-significant in non-ESDM (Supplementary Tables 6).

ADOS outcome variables

Our final set of models utilized ADOS CSS SA, RRB and Total scores as outcome variables. These models utilized the same types of predictors as the MSEL and VABS models, but also included pre-intervention ADOS CSS Total scores as a covariate to control for baseline ADOS severity. ADOS CSS SA and Total, but not RRB, models had a significant 3-way interaction between intervention type*age*pre-intervention DQ (Supplementary Table 5). This 3-way interaction is described in plots in Fig. 4C. These plots and follow-up statistical models within each intervention type (Supplementary Table 6) show that within ESDM there is a conjunction of 2 significant main effects of age (i.e. significantly declining trajectories over time) and pre-intervention DQ (i.e. lower ADOS CSS scores in individuals with higher pre-intervention DQ), but with no 2-way age*pre-intervention DQ interaction effect (Supplementary Table 6). These results in ESDM can be contrasted to a quite different picture in non-ESDM. Here there are no significant main effects of age or pre-intervention DQ, but a significant 2-way age*pre-intervention DQ interaction effect (Supplementary Table 6). This interaction effect is explained by opposite directionality of trajectories as a function of pre-intervention DQ. Deciles with lower pre-intervention DQs had declining trajectories, while individuals with higher pre-intervention DQs had increasing trajectories (Fig. 4C).

Aside from these 3-way interactions we also observed a significant main effect of age at intervention start on ADOS CSS Total scores. However, we should be careful to not over-emphasize the importance of these significant versus non-significant effects in the different intervention types, as there were no 2-way or 3-way interactions incorporating intervention type or age at intervention start (Supplementary Table 5). Discussion

In this work we present insights from an IDP-MA of autism early intervention datasets from the AEIR consortium. This IDP-MA was positioned to facilitate answers to primary questions within research on early intervention in autism - that is, what works, for whom, and for what [12]. To target what works and for what questions, we focused on the contrast of ESDM versus other types of non-ESDM intervention approaches (e.g., EIBI, NDBI, eclectic community-based treatment-as-usual options). In contrast to some past work suggesting differences between ESDM compared to non-ESDM interventions [23, 27, 29], but compatible with other findings of no differences [26, 30], we found little evidence of quantitative differences between intervention types. The only statistically significant differences incorporating intervention type (i.e. 2-way or 3-way interactions with intervention type) were via 3-way interactions between intervention type, age, and pre-intervention DQ on ADOS CSS SA and Total outcomes. These results show significant declining ADOS scores trajectories in ESDM, indicating improvements over time. However, these results are also moderated by a main effect of pre-intervention DQ, such that the individuals with relatively higher pre-intervention DQs had the steeper declining trajectories. These results suggest that ESDM works to help decrease symptom severity over time and that individuals with higher pre-intervention DQ tend to show lowest ADOS scores. In contrast, within non-ESDM ADOS symptom severity trajectories do not show a general trend of significant decline over time. Rather, trajectories in non-ESDM manifest in opposite directionalities in high versus low pre-intervention DQ. Given the presence of the interaction effect in non-ESDM and the absence of one in ESDM, these results support the idea that pre-intervention DQ plays a bigger moderating role for non-ESDM. However, the conjunction of 2 main effects of age and pre-intervention DQ ESDM, alongside the absence of a main effect of age in non-ESDM, indicates that ESDM generally tends to have positive facilitative effects for reducing symptom severity over time, while non-ESDM does not have such a general trend. Going further towards more personalized insights, we can focus on an important contrast shown in Figure 4C for individuals in the highest pre-intervention DQ deciles (i.e. light pink lines). Under non-ESDM intervention, these individuals show increasing symptom severity over time. However, under ESDM this decile shows the steepest decline in severity. Contrast this to individuals in the lower deciles of pre-intervention DQ (i.e. dark red lines) – both ESDM and non-ESDM interventions show subtle declining trajectories and not much differentiation between the two intervention types. The effect of intervention type on ADOS scores depends heavily on where one focuses on in terms of pre-intervention DQ. The differences tend to be biggest at the higher ranks of pre-intervention DQ. These observations on key moderating effects of pre-intervention DQ and how they interact with intervention type may help clarify mixed findings across the literature. Furthermore, when combined with the idea that age at intervention start also matters as a main effect (i.e. starting earlier leads to lower levels of ADOS symptom severity), one strategy for facilitating positive personalized outcomes for high pre-intervention DQ individuals is early enrollment in ESDM compared to non-ESDM. However, future research is needed to further understand how robust this result is, particularly in higher ranges of pre-intervention DQ. Additionally, future research may help dig deeper into more mechanistic explanations that may underlie and explain why these types of effects occur.

One of the key insights enabled by our IDP-MA relative to meta-analysis or prior smaller scale individual studies is the ability to isolate individualize factors and their key role in moderating developmental trajectories throughout the course of early intervention. Several factors tested here in this mega-analysis have been previously theorized as being potentially important individualized moderators of intervention response, particularly age at intervention start, pre-intervention DQ, intervention intensity, and sex [3, 5, 11, 30, 49–51]. A characteristic separating this work from past work is that most past studies were individually of small sample size. Thus, this work benefits from pooling across datasets and increases statistical power and can make for a more definitive statement about the presence or absence of such effects on early intervention response. Although these mega-analyses benefitted from boosts in statistical power, we were unable to find significant effects for sex. This lack of an effect for sex is important given theories about sex differences in the general population and how they may or may not affect autism MSEL, VABS, and ADOS scores in early development [52, 53]. Similar to observations of mostly null effects of sex, there were many null effects of cumulative intervention intensity. A key caveat to our modeling of intensity is that we used a measure of cumulative intensity, in order to quantify the potential compounding effect of intensity over time. The lack of significant effects mirrors other past work highlighting little effect of intensity on outcomes [50]. There were some exceptions, such as significant effects of cumulative intensity on MSEL RL and VABS Socialization outcomes. The directionality of the effect of intensity on MSEL RL was positive – i.e. higher scores with more cumulative intensity. This effect is as predicted from ideas that more intensity leads to more positive outcomes and matches observations showing that higher intensity can have some positive effects [25, 26]. In contrast, the effect on VABS Socialization was in the opposite direction – i.e. lower scores with more cumulative intensity. This opposite effect on VABS Socialization should be investigated in future work, as it runs counter to most predictions about the effect of intervention intensity.

This work also found ubiquitous and strong impact for two moderator variables that significantly explain variation in MSEL and VABS outcomes regardless of intervention type: age at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ. For age at intervention start, the main effect of this variable can be interpreted as higher scores on MSEL and VABS outcomes for individuals that started intervention earlier. Age trajectories were also subtly associated with age at intervention start for MSEL VR, FM, and RL subscales. These effects were explained by developmental acceleration of age trajectories as individuals start intervention later in life. While Figure 2B might hint that this effect is more pronounced in ESDM compared to non-ESDM, it is important to underscore that the 3-way interaction testing for a difference between intervention types was non-significant. This non-significant interaction may be indicative of a true null effect of differences between intervention types, but it could also signal a lack of statistical power for detecting a potentially very subtle difference between intervention types. Nevertheless, these effects of age at intervention start are highly relevant to the discussion centered around the need for earlier diagnosis in order to begin interventions earlier [4, 9, 11, 54]. The theoretical idea behind this push is to capitalize on greater earlier neural plasticity to facilitate developmental gains for children [55, 56]. The main effects of age at intervention start are supportive of these theoretical ideas, as a ubiquitous finding is that MSEL, VABS, and ADOS CSS Total outcome scores are generally more positive with earlier intervention start. The subtle age*age at start interactions showing acceleration of trajectories in older individuals may be suggestive of a possible ‘catch-up’ effect that could be occurring for individuals that start intervention later in life. Overall, our results provide strong and definitive support for advocacy for earlier diagnosis and intervention, as age at intervention start was one of the most important moderating factors playing a largely positively influence an individual’s response to intervention. Taken one step further, healthcare systems could prioritize intervention as a rapid response to early developmental concerns and work to eliminate barriers/time-constraints that prevent children from receiving intervention as early as possible.

For pre-intervention DQ, this moderator was ubiquitously found as a significant main effect, with higher pre-intervention DQ being predictive of generally higher scores on MSEL and VABS, and lower ADOS CSS scores particularly in ESDM. We also found that pre-intervention DQ interacted with age, indicating differential developmental trajectories depending on the child’s general level of ability pre-intervention. Rates of growth on MSEL subscales tend to be steeper for children that have higher pre-intervention DQ (Fig. 3D). Differences in trajectories also appeared for the VABS Motor domain, whereby high pre-intervention DQ individuals tend to maintain age-appropriate skill levels, while individuals low in pre-intervention DQ show declining trajectories that indicate a progressive effect of falling further behind age-appropriate norms as they get older. Overall, these results mirror prior work (e.g. [5, 19, 26, 57]), showing that pre-intervention developmental level plays a role in moderating the effect of intervention on outcomes.

There are several caveats and limitations to underscore. First, the development of the AEIR consortium was not restrictive in dataset inclusion criteria. Consequently, most of the contributing datasets were derived from controlled-group or uncontrolled pre–post studies. One one contributed study originated from a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The current work used a statistical technique called propensity score matching (PSM) to mimic a characteristic of RCTs whereby randomized assignment is used as a design strategy to minimize differences between interventions on baseline scores. Thus, the PSM procedure helps to statistically ensure that there are minimal differences between interventions on baseline characteristics. Second, inclusion of data into the AEIR consortium was based on voluntary contributions from researchers of published studies or other known researchers in the field as of 2021–2022. Because contributions came from only a subset of datasets known at the time, there might be a self-selection bias in AEIR consortium datasets that might limit generalizability to all autism early intervention research. Most datasets contributed to AEIR were predominantly from studies that contrasted ESDM to some other non-ESDM comparison intervention. As such, these datasets may not be representative of the full scope of other intervention approaches used in the autism early intervention literature. Furthermore, within the non-ESDM category, not enough data was present to make a more specific fine-grained categorization of these types of interventions. Future work that digs deeper into a more fine-grained categorization of intervention types in IDP-MAs is needed. Nevertheless, our contrast of ESDM to a diverse range of non-ESDM interventions may limit our ability to adequately test the ‘what works’ question. Third, although we have tested for effects like sex, due to the still limited number of actual females (e.g., around 60 individuals per each intervention type), statistical power to robustly test for sex differences in this study may be limited. Fourth, although we found some small effects for cumulative intensity, most outcome variables had no effect of cumulative intensity. These null effects must be taken with caveat that intensity was not a variable that could be systematically manipulated and evaluated. Future work with more careful manipulations and variability in intervention intensity would be necessary to fully test whether this variable has important impact on outcomes.

In summary, using IDP-MA this work shows that when children receive high-quality evidence-supported intervention approaches, the effect of intervention type is secondary to the larger moderating effects of age at intervention start and pre-intervention DQ. For MSEL and VABS outcomes, our contrast of ESDM versus non-ESDM intervention types yielded limited evidence for differences between interventions. However, there were some important distinctions for how ESDM versus non-ESDM affect ADOS symptom severity trajectories. ESDM exerts effects that decrease ADOS SA and Total severity over time. In contrast, non-ESDM interventions result in autism symptom severity trajectories that are much more heterogeneous and dependent on the individual’s pre-intervention DQ. The sharpest contrast for differences between intervention types in symptom severity trajectories can be seen in deciles of high pre-intervention DQ. ESDM results in sharp declines in symptom severity over time, while non-ESDM results in slight increases over time. These results may suggest that ESDM has most impact on autism symptom severity for individuals within the higher ranks of pre-intervention DQ. Third, we found limited evidence for effects related to a child’s sex or cumulative intervention intensity. These null findings must be taken with the caveat that both variables may not have been ideal (e.g., low power for females, lack of systematically varying intensity levels) in the current dataset. In conjunction with powerful past meta-analytic work (e.g. [12, 14, 15, 19–21, 23, 50]), IDP-MAs [19, 25, 26] provide a useful complimentary approach that may help in identifying factors that explain heterogeneity in response to early intervention in autism.
