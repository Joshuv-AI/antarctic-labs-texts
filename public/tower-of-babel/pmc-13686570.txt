Codocytosis in the Dog: 345 Cases (2020–2022)

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCodocytes, or target cells, are a morphologic variation of erythrocytes characterized by increased membrane surface area relative to volume. Introduction

Target cells, or codocytes, are bell‐shaped erythrocytes with a bullseye appearance, which results from an increased surface area‐to‐volume ratio, often due to membrane excess or decreased intracellular content, and is associated with reduced osmotic fragility [1, 2].

In dogs, codocytes are commonly described on peripheral blood smears, yet their diagnostic and clinical significance remains unclear. Proposed associations with codocytosis are largely extrapolated from human medicine, where codocytes have been linked to regenerative anemia, liver disease, hemoglobinopathies, and postsplenectomy states [3, 4, 5, 6]. In regenerative anemia, immature erythrocytes possess excess membrane, which predisposes them to codocyte morphology during smear preparation. In addition, macrocytic erythrocytes with weaker membranes may be more prone to deformation, enhancing the codocyte appearance [7, 8].

Codocytosis may also occur in non‐anemic states. In hepatic disorders, altered lipid metabolism—particularly increased cholesterol‐to‐phospholipid ratios—can modify erythrocyte membranes and promote codocyte formation [9, 10, 11, 12]. Similar changes may occur in hypothyroidism, which also affects lipid handling. Congestive heart failure has been associated with codocytosis through multifactorial mechanisms, including chronic hypoxia and erythropoietin‐driven ineffective erythropoiesis, hepatic congestion impairing lipid metabolism, and diminished splenic filtration of poikilocytes [13]. Post‐splenectomy codocytosis is attributed to loss of erythrocyte culling and metabolic alterations affecting membrane composition [8].

In a recent study of 312 diabetic dogs, codocytosis was observed more frequently in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis, potentially due to disrupted lipid metabolism and oxidative injury [14]. Despite these observations, the incidence, disease associations, and diagnostic relevance of codocytosis in dogs have not been investigated.

The objective of this retrospective, descriptive study was to characterize clinical conditions associated with codocytosis in dogs by evaluating hematologic profiles, clinical diagnoses, and pharmacologic histories. Improved understanding of codocyte‐associated morbidities may enhance the interpretation of blood smears and inform clinical decision‐making in veterinary patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Case Selection

This retrospective descriptive study was conducted at a single veterinary teaching hospital. The study period spanned from September 2020 through October 2022. Electronic medical records were searched using the hospital's laboratory information system to identify canine patients with CBCs that included a reported RBC morphology of codocytosis graded as ≥ 2+.

Codocytosis grading was derived from microscopic peripheral blood smear evaluation performed by board‐certified clinical pathologists. Dogs were included if codocytosis was graded as 2+ (moderate) or greater; patients with < 2+ codocytosis were excluded from descriptive and statistical analyses.

Data Collection

The following data were extracted for each dog: signalment (age, breed, sex), pre‐existing and current medical conditions, current medications, and results of CBC, serum biochemistry, and peripheral blood smear evaluation. Diagnoses were categorized as either pre‐existing (established before the visit associated with codocytosis) or new (diagnosed at the time of codocytosis detection). Disease categories included cardiac, endocrine, gastrointestinal/pancreatic, hepatobiliary, immune‐mediated, infectious, musculoskeletal, neoplasia, neurological, ophthalmic, renal/urinary, and other. It was also documented if dogs had diagnoses in multiple categories. Infectious diagnoses were categorized independently unless they involved a region covered by another specific category (e.g., cholangiohepatitis classified under hepatobiliary). Diagnoses were made and coded by the attending clinicians and documented for the study through review of the medical records.

Medications administered at the time of codocytosis detection were reviewed and categorized by pharmacologic class. These included: antibiotics, anticonvulsants, cardiac medications (e.g., angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, diuretics, pimobendan, antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants), chemotherapy/immunomodulators (e.g., cyclophosphamide, vincristine, mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclosporine), corticosteroids, gastrointestinal agents (e.g., antiemetics, prokinetics, proton pump inhibitors), non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and opioids.

Each drug category was defined based on the known primary mechanism of action and/or body system for which its use is primarily selected. Combination therapies were recorded in multiple relevant categories.

Hematology, Blood Smear Evaluation, and Serum Chemistry Profiles

CBCs were performed using the ADVIA 2120i Hematology System (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Tarrytown, NY, USA). Manual leukocyte differential counts and RBC morphology evaluations were performed by a medical technologist and reviewed by a clinical pathology resident and a board‐certified clinical pathologist on Modified Wright‐stained blood smears. Blood smears were prepared using fresh ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulated whole blood and stained using an automated slide stainer (Hematek 3000, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics).

RBC morphologic abnormalities were recorded, including codocytes, acanthocytes, dacryocytes, echinocytes, elliptocytes, keratocytes, ghost cells, spherocytes, stomatocytes, nucleated RBCs, reticulocytes, schistocytes, eccentrocytes, and Howell‐Jolly bodies. RBC characteristics such as anisocytosis, macrocytosis, microcytosis, poikilocytosis, polychromasia, and rouleaux were also recorded. Inclusions such as basophilic stippling, Heinz bodies, and Howell‐Jolly bodies were noted when present.

Codocytosis was graded using a semi‐quantitative system adapted from Weiss et al., based on counts performed at 1000× magnification using an oil immersion objective [15]. The average number of codocytes was determined from ten uniform microscopic fields, each containing approximately 250–300 RBCs. The grading scheme used for codocytes is depicted in Table 1.

Only dogs with ≥ 2+ codocytosis (i.e., a mean codocyte count of ≥ 6 cells per field) were included for further analysis. Other RBC abnormalities were graded according to the established guidelines of the American Society for Veterinary Clinical Pathology and as described by Weiss et al. [15, 16].

Total WBC counts and differential cell counts (segmented neutrophils, band neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils) were recorded. Neutrophil toxic changes (Döhle bodies, toxic granulation, vacuolation) and the presence of hypersegmented neutrophils were noted. Platelet counts were recorded, and platelet aggregation was noted when present.

Serum biochemistry profiles were performed using the Cobas c501 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA) with manufacturer‐supplied reagents. Analytes included: glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, phosphorus, calcium, sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, anion gap, total protein, albumin, globulin, ALT, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin, cholesterol, amylase, and lipase.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons of proportions of categorical data were conducted using the chi‐square test. In analyses, values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using commercially available software (Stata v. 16.1, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

During the study period, 14 010 complete blood counts were performed in dogs. Of these, 907 dogs had codocytes noted on blood smear analysis of their initial CBC (6.5%). Five hundred sixty‐two of these dogs had only 1+ codocytosis noted, so they were excluded from continued analysis. This resulted in 345 dogs included in the study. The incidence of substantial codocytosis in dogs was 2.5% over the 2‐year study period. Within the 345 dogs, 281 were noted to have 2+ codocytosis (C2, 81.4%), 57 dogs had 3+ codocytosis (C3, 16.5%), and 7 dogs had 4+ codocytosis (C4, 2.0%). Neoplasia was diagnosed in 102 dogs and of those 28 (24.3%) were diagnosed with lymphoma or leukemia. Other common tumors were hemangiosarcoma (N = 19 dogs, 16.5%) and mast cell tumor (N = 8 dogs, 7.0%). Gastrointestinal/pancreatic diseases were diagnosed in 54 dogs, and of those, 21 (36.2%) were diagnosed with or suspected to have pancreatitis. Chronic enteropathies, including inflammatory bowel disease, lymphangiectasia, and protein‐losing enteropathy, were also common in this group (N = 12 dogs, 20.7%). Infectious diseases were diagnosed in 45 dogs with dermatologic infections, such as pyoderma and otitis, diagnosed in 11 dogs (21.6%). Other common infections in this group included septic processes such as pyothorax and septic peritonitis (N = 10 dogs, 19.6%), as well as canine Parvoviral enteritis (N = 10, 19.6%).

Hepatobiliary diseases were diagnosed in 59 dogs with portosystemic shunts or other congenital abnormalities, being most common in this group in 28 dogs (43.1%). Gallbladder mucocele was another common diagnosis in 8 dogs (12.3%). The median age of dogs diagnosed with hepatobiliary disease (6 years, range 0.25–14 years) was significantly lower when compared to the whole population of dogs (p = 0.005).

While endocrine diseases were not one of the most common body systems represented in dogs with codocytes (N = 32, 9.3%), 28 of the dogs diagnosed with endocrine diseases were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus with or without ketoacidosis. Of the 26 dogs in C2 with endocrine disease, 22 were diabetic dogs (10 with diabetes mellitus and 12 with diabetic ketoacidosis). All 6 dogs in C3 with endocrine disease were diabetics (2 with diabetes mellitus and 4 with diabetic ketoacidosis).

One hundred four dogs were not receiving any medications on the day of blood collection for the CBC, on which codocytes were noted. Two hundred forty‐one dogs were receiving at least one medication. Sixty dogs were receiving one medication (17.4%), and 181 dogs were receiving more than one medication (52.5%). Antibiotic medications were the most common in dogs with codocytes (N = 127, 36.8%). When comparing the frequency of codocytes, the distribution of antibiotic usage was significant, with a higher proportion of dogs receiving antibiotics in C3 and C4 compared to C2 (p < 0.001). Anti‐emetics (N = 114, 33.0%; p = 0.26) and corticosteroids (N = 75, 21.7%; p = 0.28) were other common medications in these dogs, but were not significantly distributed across the severity of codocytosis. The most common antibiotics described in dogs with codocytosis were metronidazole (N = 46/127, 36.2%), penicillins (N = 38/127, 29.9%), fluoroquinolones (N = 14/127, 11.0%), and doxycycline (N = 13/127, 10.2%). Thirteen dogs (10.2%) were receiving multiple antibiotics. Medications are summarized in Table 6.

Discussion

Codocytes were identified in about 6.5% of all canine blood smears from a specialty hospital population, with 2.5% being noted at frequencies of 2+ or higher. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first report of codocytosis prevalence in a canine population. This prevalence is consistent with the general perception of codocytes as a relatively uncommon but recognizable poikilocyte type in dogs. Importantly, this is the first study to characterize the clinical contexts and disease associations of codocytosis in dogs, providing insight into its potential diagnostic and pathophysiologic relevance.

Despite prior suggestions that codocytes are primarily associated with regenerative anemia or erythropoietic disorders, our findings indicate that codocytosis in dogs is not strongly correlated with anemia, macrocytosis, or regenerative response [17, 18]. Similar proportions of anemic and macrocytic dogs were observed across all codocyte grades, and reticulocytosis did not differ significantly among groups. These findings suggest that while codocytosis may accompany regenerative responses, it is not exclusive to anemic states and may be driven by alternative mechanisms such as membrane lipid remodeling. A previous description of dyserythropoiesis, polymyopathy, and cardiac disease in three English springer spaniels reported codocytes as a common finding, but no patients of this breed or this combination of problems were identified in the current study; however, it is possible that cases of dyserythropoiesis were not identified or simply described as non‐regenerative [19].

One of the most compelling associations in this study was between codocytosis severity and hepatobiliary disease. Dogs with higher grades of codocytes (3+ or 4+) had significantly elevated ALT activities and a higher proportion of hepatobiliary diagnoses compared to dogs with 2+ codocytosis. These results support prior literature from both human and veterinary medicine indicating that hepatic dysfunction, particularly involving lipid dysregulation, can alter red blood cell membrane composition and increase membrane surface area, thereby promoting codocyte formation [9, 10, 11, 12]. In humans, codocytes are a recognized feature of liver disease, particularly cholestatic disorders, due to altered lecithin‐cholesterol acyltransferase activity and increased plasma cholesterol levels. In dogs, similar mechanisms have been proposed, and our findings reinforce a hepatocellular contribution to codocyte formation, especially in cases involving congenital hepatic anomalies or gallbladder disease.

Codocytosis was also commonly identified in gastrointestinal or pancreatic disease, particularly in dogs with suspected or confirmed pancreatitis and chronic enteropathies. These diseases may contribute to codocyte development through chronic inflammation, altered lipid absorption, or secondary hepatic effects. Inflammatory bowel disease and protein‐losing enteropathy may impair intestinal lipid handling, leading to downstream effects on erythrocyte membranes, though this mechanism requires further investigation [20]. Interestingly, the strong association between gastrointestinal disease and higher codocyte grades suggests a potential systemic metabolic component that may extend beyond localized pathology.

Diabetes mellitus, especially cases complicated by ketoacidosis, was a notable endocrine comorbidity in this population. This corroborates findings from a recent study by Slead et al., which documented a higher frequency and severity of codocytosis in diabetic dogs, particularly those in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) [14]. Hyperlipidemia, oxidative stress, and changes in membrane integrity during DKA may all contribute to this phenomenon. These findings mirror observations in human diabetics, where altered erythrocyte deformability and membrane lipid content have been documented [3].

Neoplastic disease was the most frequently recorded diagnosis overall but did not show a statistically significant association with codocyte severity. It is possible that codocytosis in these cases represents a paraneoplastic phenomenon, secondary to treatment, or is coincidental, given the high prevalence of cancer in the study population and the frequency with which CBCs are performed in dogs undergoing cancer treatment.

Medication use was highly prevalent in this cohort, with over 70% of dogs receiving at least one drug at the time of blood collection. Antibiotics, particularly metronidazole and penicillins, were the most frequently administered, and their use was significantly associated with higher codocyte grades. Although no direct causative link between these medications and codocytosis has been established in veterinary or human literature, their association may reflect the underlying severity or nature of illness in these patients rather than a direct erythrotoxic effect. Notably, prior studies have not implicated antimicrobial agents in codocyte formation, but further investigation may be warranted given this correlation.

Collectively, these findings support a multifactorial pathogenesis for codocytosis in dogs, with hepatobiliary dysfunction, gastrointestinal pathology, and endocrine disease (especially diabetes) all contributing potential mechanistic roles. The absence of associations with anemia or reticulocytosis in most cases indicates that codocytosis is not a simple marker of erythropoietic activity but may instead reflect membrane‐level alterations driven by lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, or impaired splenic clearance.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, the varied diagnostic evaluations, the fact that the population of dogs was exclusively from a specialty hospital, and the reliance on semi‐quantitative blood smear interpretation, which may be subject to interobserver variability. Additionally, only dogs with ≥ 2+ codocytosis were included, potentially excluding cases with subtle but clinically meaningful findings.

Conclusion

While traditionally associated with anemia, codocytes in this study were more frequently observed in dogs with hepatobiliary, gastrointestinal, and metabolic disorders, particularly those involving lipid metabolism, such as diabetes mellitus and liver disease. These findings suggest that codocytosis may reflect underlying systemic pathology rather than erythropoietic response alone. Recognition of this poikilocyte on blood smear review should prompt clinicians to consider broader metabolic or hepatobiliary evaluation. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate the prognostic relevance and pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying codocytosis in canine patients.
