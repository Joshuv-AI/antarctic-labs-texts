Transient myopia induced by acetazolamide in a pregnant patient with idiopathic intracranial hypertension: A case of successful rechallenge☆
Authors: Luai Eldweik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcetazolamide is the mainstay of treatment for idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH). Transient myopia is a rare ocular adverse effect, usually attributed to ciliary body edema and forward displacement of the lens–iris diaphragm.Case presentationA 44-year-old woman developed IIH during pregnancy following hormonal therapy for in-vitro fertilization. She was started on acetazolamide 1 g/day and, after five doses, experienced sudden bilateral blurred vision that improved with myopic correction and resolved spontaneously within 24 hours after discontinuation of acetazolamide. Examination showed no signs of angle closure or choroidal effusion. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) confirmed preserved ganglion cell layer and no axonal loss. After counselling the patient about the potential for recurrent transient myopia and other acetazolamide-related adverse effects, acetazolamide was reintroduced at a lower dose (500 mg/day). No recurrence of myopia occurred, and papilledema improved markedly over six weeks.ConclusionThis case illustrates a rare idiosyncratic effect of acetazolamide in pregnancy-associated IIH and demonstrates that rechallenge at a lower dose may be tolerated without recurrence.Keywords: Acetazolamide, Adverse drug reaction, Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, IIH, Papilledema, Pregnancy, Pseudotumor cerebri, Rechallenge, Transient myopia

Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH)is a disorder characterized by increased intracranial pressure without an identifiable structural or infectious cause. It typically affects women of childbearing age and is associated with obesity, hormonal changes, and certain medications. During pregnancy, treatment is challenging due to the limited safety data regarding potential effects on the developing fetus for commonly used agents. Acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, is the most widely used medication for lowering intracranial pressure in IIH. Although generally well tolerated, it has been rarely associated with reversible ocular side effects such as transient myopia, acute angle closure, and choroidal effusion. We present a case of acetazolamide-induced transient myopia in a pregnant woman with IIH, with successful rechallenge at a lower dose and complete resolution of symptoms.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old woman who had received hormonal therapy for in-vitro fertilization earlier in the year developed IIH during pregnancy in the setting of increased body weight. She presented with headaches, dizziness, and blurred vision. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated findings consistent with elevated intracranial pressure, including a partially empty sella, posterior globe flattening, and dilation of the peri-optic subarachnoid space with optic nerve sheath distension. Magnetic resonance venography (MRV) showed bilateral transverse sinus stenosis. Lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 60 cm H2O. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was otherwise normal, with normal glucose, protein, and cell count. Cerebrospinal fluid drainage provided transient symptomatic relief.

At presentation, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/20 in the right eye (OD) and 20/25 in the left eye (OS). Pupillary responses were normal with no afferent defect. Fundus examination revealed 3+ bilateral papilledema, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic nerves demonstrated marked thickening of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) with preservation of the ganglion cell layer (Fig. 1A). Humphrey 24-2 visual fields revealed slightly enlarged blind spots in both eyes (OU), more pronounced OS (Fig. 2). A diagnosis of IIH during pregnancy was made, and acetazolamide 500 mg twice daily (BID) was started.

After the fifth dose, the patient experienced sudden bilateral blurred vision without pain, redness, or headache. She visited a local optometrist who documented new-onset myopia of −2.00 diopters (D) OD and −2.50 diopters (D) OS, compared with her baseline spherical equivalent of −0.50 diopters (D) in OU. Her vision improved with myopic correction and returned to normal within 24 hours of discontinuing acetazolamide. She presented to the clinic later in the same week for evaluation. On examination, there were no signs of anterior chamber shallowing, choroidal effusion, or angle closure.

The episode was consistent with acetazolamide-induced transient myopia. After a discussion of medical and procedural alternatives, including furosemide and serial lumbar punctures, the patient elected to restart acetazolamide at 500 mg/day.

At six-week follow-up, she remained asymptomatic with BCVA 20/20 OU and papilledema reduced to 1+ OU. OCT revealed significant reduction in RNFL thickness (Fig. 1B) and no ganglion cell loss OU. Her refractive error returned to baseline (−0.50 D spherical equivalent OU), consistent with her pre-IIH and pre-pregnancy refraction, and remained stable throughout four months of follow-up after acetazolamide rechallenge.

Discussion

Pregnancy complicates IIH management because of the restricted range of safe medications available to lower intracranial pressure.1 Although acetazolamide, a sulfonamide derivative, is usually avoided during early pregnancy, published data suggest that it can be used safely when maternal vision is threatened, particularly after the first trimester.2 In general, alternative options such as serial lumbar punctures, lumbar drainage, or optic nerve sheath fenestration are preferred when drug-related side effects occur during pregnancy.3

Several sulfonamide derivatives including acetazolamide, topiramate, and hydrochlorothiazide have been infrequently associated with ciliochoroidal effusion. Acetazolamide in particular has been linked to several transient ocular effects such as acute myopic shift, narrowing of the iridocorneal angle with or without elevated intraocular pressure, and ciliochoroidal detachment.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, the prevailing theory suggests that acetazolamide induces an idiosyncratic reaction within the uveal tissues that results in forward displacement of the lens-iris diaphragm. In their comprehensive review, Kaisari and colleagues identified 23 published cases of acetazolamide-induced myopic shift reported since 1956. Reported doses ranged from 125 to 1000 mg, with a median dose of 500 mg/day. Acute myopic shift developed within 3 to 24 hours, with a median onset of 24 hours, and resolved within 2 to 14 days after discontinuation of the medication. In the present case, symptoms occurred after the fifth dose. This delayed onset may reflect inter-individual variability in uveal tissue susceptibility, differences in dosing schedule and exposure, and pregnancy related physiologic changes that may affect fluid dynamics and tissue permeability. About one third of cases demonstrated angle closure ocular hypertension, and about half showed evidence of uveal effusion. After the medication was stopped, every patient achieved full recovery within a median of 5 days.12 Published data on acetazolamide re-exposure after an induced myopic shift are limited. Galin and colleagues reported two cases in which acetazolamide was reintroduced after resolution of myopia, with no recurrence in one patient restarted at 500 mg/day and recurrence within a few hours in another.7 Our case adds additional evidence that recurrence is not inevitable and that carefully monitored re-exposure at a lower dose may be tolerated in selected situations.

In this case, acute myopia developed after only five doses of acetazolamide and resolved within 24 hours of stopping treatment. There were no symptoms suggesting coexisting angle-closure mechanism. The close temporal relationship between starting the medication, the onset of myopia, and its rapid recovery, together with the absence of recurrence after reinitiation at a lower dose, strongly supports a causal link. The successful rechallenge offers important insight into the underlying pathophysiology. Because immune mediated reactions typically recur or worsen upon re-exposure, the absence of any recurrent myopic shift or angle changes argues against an immunologic mechanism. Our patient did not recur on re-exposure at a lower daily dose. This may indicate a threshold effect in susceptible individuals, resolution of a transient uveal response over time, or both. We therefore describe this as an observation rather than proof of dose dependence.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare idiosyncratic ocular effect of acetazolamide and provides insight into its underlying pathophysiology. The complete resolution of symptoms after discontinuation, together with the absence of recurrence upon reintroduction, supports a transient, self-limited response of the ciliary body that alters the position of the lens-iris diaphragm. Although the literature generally advises stopping the medication permanently when such reactions occur, this experience suggests that a carefully monitored rechallenge may be considered in selected patients after detailed discussion of risks and benefits. Rechallenge should only be attempted when patient vision requires continued medical therapy and when other treatment options have been reviewed with the patient. Under these controlled circumstances and with appropriate follow up, a trial of reintroduction may be both safe and effective.
