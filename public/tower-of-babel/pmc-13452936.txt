Relapse risk and functional outcomes in anti-LGI1 encephalitis: A multicenter retrospective analysis
Authors: Chun-Hong Shen, Bo Jin, Ye Du, Wen-Jing Shen, Rui Li, Yin-Xi Zhang, Qi-Lun Lai, Hao Song, Shan Wang, Mei-Ping Ding, Shuang Wang, Yi Guo, Meng-Ting Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated protein 1 (LGI1) encephalitis presents relapse risk and potential functional impairment, with no established maintenance treatment guidelines.ObjectivesThe study aimed to uncover prognostic signatures, and identify predictors of relapse and poor outcome using real-world evidence.DesignThis multicenter retrospective study included anti-LGI1 encephalitis patients from five tertiary hospitals in Eastern China between January 2015 and January 2024, with ≥ 24-month follow-up.MethodsRelapse was defined as the presence of new or worsening clinical features after at least 3 months of stability or improvement. Poor outcome was defined as a modified Rankin Scale score > 2 at last follow-up.ResultsA total of 104 patients were included (median age 56.5 years, 60.6% male). Following the acute phase, the majority (66.3%) received prolonged corticosteroid treatment (≥6 months) without steroid-sparing maintenance immunotherapy (SSMI), whereas 16.3% received SSMI. Twelve patients (11.5%) experienced relapse. Hyponatremia at diagnosis was more common in relapsing patients (p = 0.017), and was significantly associated with an increased risk of relapse in Cox regression (hazard ratio = 4.67, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.41–15.53, p = 0.012). Poor outcome was observed in 17 patients (16.3%), with male sex (odds ratio [OR] = 5.12, 95% CI = 1.27–34.48, p = 0.041) and hyponatremia (OR = 4.69, 95% CI = 1.54–15.47, p = 0.008) as potential risk factors.ConclusionThis study suggests that hyponatremia is associated with an increased risk of relapse and poor outcome in anti-LGI1 encephalitis. These findings may facilitate individualized risk stratification, but further validation in larger prospective cohorts is needed.Keywords: anti-LGI1 encephalitis, relapse, risk factor, hyponatremia

Introduction

Anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated protein 1 (LGI1) encephalitis is an autoimmune disorder mediated by antibodies targeting LGI1, a protein that is broadly distributed throughout the brain, including the hippocampus, cerebral cortex and cerebellum. Clinically, the disease is characterized by cognitive impairment, psychiatric symptoms, and seizures often in the form of faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS), along with commonly observed hyponatremia and mesial temporal lobe abnormalities on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).1,2

Immunotherapy is effective in alleviating acute symptoms for most patients; however, residual neurological deficits and relapse risk remain significant concerns. Previous studies have demonstrated that 13.0–33.0% of patients experience poor long-term outcomes.2–4 Relapse occurs in a subset of patients, but reported rates vary widely and predictors remain unclear, largely due to limited cohort size in most studies.2–11 Notably, relapses emerge years after initial onset, accompanied by worsened functional scores and poor outcomes in approximately one-third of cases. 5 It is therefore imperative to employ larger cohorts to characterize relapse and functional outcomes, and determine their associated factors.

Although some studies have demonstrated the efficacy of oral steroid-sparing maintenance immunotherapy (SSMI) in reducing relapse rates,9,11 the overall evidence remains inconclusive.3,5 Given the additional constraints such as accessibility, adverse effects, and cost, its real-world utilization is relatively limited.4,7,10,12 Recently, Alkabie et al. have also suggested that prolonged corticosteroid (CS) without SSMI use may be a viable approach for most patients with anti-LGI1 encephalitis. 6 Overall, post-acute management strategies—including CS tapering, immunosuppressive regimens, and their long-term efficacy and safety profiles—lack standardized protocols, reflecting critical gaps in clinical practice.6,9,13

This multicenter real-world study aims to explore the profiles of relapse and long-term outcome in anti-LGI1 encephalitis, as well as their potential risk factors, thereby providing evidence to stratify high-risk populations and optimize therapeutic and management strategies.

Materials & methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective study of consecutive patients newly diagnosed with anti-LGI1 encephalitis between January 2015 and January 2024 at five tertiary medical centers from four cities in Eastern China (Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Shaoxing People’s Hospital, Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital, and Huzhou Third People’s Hospital). Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) characteristic presentations of anti-LGI1 encephalitis 14 ; (2) LGI1-immunoglobulin G (IgG) positivity in serum and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) by fixed cell-based assay (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany); (3) follow-up ≥ 24 months after initial immunotherapies; and (4) exclusion of alternative diagnoses. Exclusion criteria comprised coexisting neural autoantibodies, central nervous system (CNS) infections, severe neurological or psychiatric disorders, or insufficient follow-up/data. This research was approved by the ethics committee of each participating center: Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No. 2024-LSY-0682, date: 7 Jun 2024), Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No. 2025-0267, date: 24 Apr 2025), Shaoxing People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025-100-01, date: 28 Apr 2025), Huzhou Third People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025-132, date: 11 Apr 2025), and Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital, Ningbo University (Approval No. 2025-144, date: 25 Apr 2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective, anonymized nature of the study.

Data collection

Data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records, covering demographics, clinical presentations, and treatment regimens during the initial episode of encephalitis. All paraclinical characteristics, including results from serum and CSF analysis, antibody tests, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electroencephalography (EEG), and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18FDG-PET) were obtained at the time of diagnosis, before immunotherapy. Serial serum sodium measurements during hospitalization were also extracted from the medical records. Follow-up data were collected from discharge until the last visit through clinical visits or telephone interviews, which were conducted every three months during the first year and every six months thereafter. The collected data included clinical presentations, treatment modifications, drug adverse effects and ancillary tests (e.g., MRI, antibody titers and EEG when available). Status epilepticus (SE) was defined in accordance with the 2015 criteria established by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). 15 Disease severity at diagnosis was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores, with mRS > 2 indicating moderate-to-severe severity. 16 First-line immunotherapy included CS, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), plasma exchange, and second-line agents included rituximab (RTX) and cyclophosphamide (CTX). SSMI referred to immunosuppressants prescribed to facilitate the gradual discontinuation of CS while avoiding relapse, comprising mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), azathioprine (AZA), tacrolimus (TAC) or repeated courses of RTX. For RTX, repeated courses were defined by the administration of one or more infusions at 6-month intervals or upon CD19+ B-cell repopulation after the initial infusion.6,9,17

Evaluation and definition of outcomes

The outcomes were evaluated independently by two experienced neurologists (CHS and MTC). In cases of discrepancy, a consensus was reached through discussion. If agreement could not be achieved, a third expert (BJ) was consulted, and the result was determined through consensus. Relapse was defined as the presence of new or worsening clinical features after at least 3 months of stability or improvement. 9 During the post-acute phase, an isolated seizure or seizures more likely to represent epilepsy rather than symptomatic seizures, was not classified as relapse.5,18,19 Accordingly, the determination of relapse was based on a comprehensive evaluation of seizure semiology, encephalitic features, ancillary evidence, clinical response to re-initiation of immunotherapy, and other relevant findings. Functional outcomes for all patients were categorized based on the mRS score at the last follow-up. The mRS score of ≤ 2 indicated good outcome, while an mRS score > 2 indicated poor outcome. 2

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range (IQR) after testing for normality with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test for categorical variables, and Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Demographic, clinical and paraclinical characteristics at diagnosis were evaluated as candidate variables for analysis of each outcome. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into the corresponding multivariable model. Multicollinearity between covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). For relapse, Kaplan-Meier curves were used to compare relapse-free survival. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to calculate hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI), and the proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. For poor functional outcome, logistic regression was applied to estimate odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI.

Missing data were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Twenty imputations were performed using logistic regression for binary variables and pooled according to Rubin’s rules. To evaluate the robustness of the regression findings, estimates from the MICE-imputed dataset were compared with those from the complete-case subset (patients without missing values for any candidate variable). All statistics were performed in R (version 4.3.2), with two-sided p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Demographic, clinical features and treatment

Among the 112 patients diagnosed with anti-LGI1 encephalitis, 8 were excluded (4 were lost to follow-up, 3 had coexisting contactin-associated protein-like 2-IgG, and 1 declined treatment and was lost to follow-up), leaving 104 eligible patients for analysis. The median onset age was 56.5 years (IQR = 44.5–66.0 years, range = 19–84 years), with 60.6% being male. At the time of diagnosis, the most common clinical manifestations were seizures (94, 90.4%), followed by cognitive impairment (72, 69.2%), psychiatric symptoms (34, 32.7%), sleep disturbances (26, 25.0%), and autonomic dysfunction (e.g., arrhythmia, hyperhidrosis) (15, 14.4%). Hyponatremia was present at diagnosis in 33 patients (31.7%), with a median serum sodium level of 132.0 mmol/L (IQR = 129.8–132.9 mmol/L) among those affected. Overall, the median number of sodium measurements per patient during hospitalization was 2 (IQR = 1–4), with 71 patients (68.3%) undergoing at least two measurements; the median interval between the first and last measurement was 11.5 days (IQR = 7.8–14.3 days). When the nadir sodium value was considered, hyponatremia was observed in 43 patients (41.3%). The median mRS score was 3.0 (IQR = 3.0–4.0), with 86 patients (82.7%) exhibiting moderate-to-severe severity. All patients received first-line immunotherapy, with most (72, 69.2%) undergoing combined treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone plus IVIG or plasma exchange. Nine patients received second-line immunotherapies (RTX = 7, CTX = 2). The median interval from symptom onset to initiation of immunotherapy was 32 days (IQR = 13.8–92.0 days). Following the acute stage, a prolonged, gradually tapered course of daily oral CS was commonly prescribed. The typical regimen involved oral prednisone at ∼1 mg/kg/day for two weeks after intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by a taper of 5 mg every two weeks, according to the Chinese expert consensus on autoimmune encephalitis. 20 The majority of patients (69, 66.3%) received prolonged CS monotherapy (≥6 months). In contrast, another 17 patients (16.3%) transitioned to an SSMI following CS discontinuation: MMF (n = 12), repeated RTX (n = 3), TAC (n = 1), and AZA (n = 1). Fourteen patients (13.5%) received CS treatment without SSMI for less than 6 months. The median CS duration was 10.0 months (IQR = 6.0–13.2 months). Antiseizure medications (ASMs) were administered to 87 patients (83.7%), with 12 (11.5%) receiving three or more ASMs.

Among 100 patients receiving CS, 3 patients (3.0%) discontinued due to severe adverse events (hepatic dysfunction, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and menstrual irregularity). Of 20 patients receiving other immunosuppressants, 4 (20.0%) reported treatment-related adverse events: TAC-induced gastrointestinal reactions (n = 1), MMF-induced hepatic dysfunction (n = 1), arthralgia after RTX therapy (n = 1), and pneumonia following CS combined with MMF (n = 1). Among 32 patients receiving oxcarbazepine (OXC) or carbamazepine (CBZ), 5 (15.6%) developed a rash and subsequently switched to alternative ASMs.

Characteristics and management in relapsing patients

During a median follow-up of 4.6 years (IQR = 3.3–6.5 years, range = 2–11 years), 12 patients (11.5%) experienced at least one relapse (Figure 1, Table S1). The relapse rate was 11.8% in the largest center (n = 68) and 11.1% in the remaining four centers combined (n = 36). Of these, 10 patients (Patient 3–12) had a single relapse, while two patients (Patient 1 and 2) experienced a second relapse at 1.6 years and 0.6 years after the first, respectively. The median interval from the initial disease onset to the first relapse was 1.2 years (IQR = 0.8–2.2 years). At first relapse, the symptom spectrum mirrored the initial onset. All patients experienced seizures: FBTCS (n = 7) were most common, followed by focal seizures (n = 6, including pilomotor seizures in 4 cases), and FBDS was observed in 2 patients. Memory impairment was present in 9 patients. The median mRS score at relapse was 3.0 (IQR = 2.8–3.0). Compared with the initial episode, scores were lower or same in most patients; however, two patients showed worsening as indicated by higher scores.

Regarding immunotherapy, all relapsing patients had received first-line therapy at the initial onset, and one patient also received RTX as second-line therapy at that time. Subsequently, most patients (9/12) were receiving prolonged CS (≥6 months) alone, while 2 received an SSMI with CS discontinuation. Notably, half of the relapses (7/14) occurred while patients were still on immunotherapy: 2 on MMF, 1 on RTX exposure, and 4 on low-dose CS. For relapse management, CS or IVIG were used in 14 relapses, either alone or in combination (including one case of CS combined with efgartigimod). RTX was administered as second-line therapy for 5 relapse events. All 12 patients responded to the immunotherapy administered for their relapses and remained clinically stable thereafter. Finally, 7 patients were receiving an SSMI from their last relapse until the final visit.

Risk factors for relapse

To identify factors associated with relapse, we compared clinical characteristics between patients who relapsed and those who did not. As shown in Table 2, hyponatremia at diagnosis was significantly more frequent among the relapsing patients (66.7% vs. 27.2%, p = 0.017), whereas no significant differences were observed in all other demographic, clinical, paraclinical features or treatment-related variables.

Univariable Cox regression found that hyponatremia was associated with an increased risk of relapse (HR = 4.67, 95% CI = 1.41–15.53, p = 0.012; Table S2), while no other variable showed a significant association. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (Schoenfeld test, p = 0.300). Kaplan–Meier analysis similarly revealed a significant difference in relapse-free survival between patients with and without hyponatremia (p = 0.006, Figure 2). Given missing data for candidate variables (i.e., CSF, MRI, and EEG features), we also performed a complete-case sensitivity analysis (n = 73). In this subset, univariable Cox regression yielded a consistent and statistically significant association between hyponatremia and relapse (HR = 8.81, 95% CI = 1.78–43.68, p = 0.008).

Predictors of poor outcome

At last follow-up, the median mRS score had improved to 1 (IQR = 1–2). Functional outcomes were good in 87 patients (83.7%) and poor in 17 (16.3%). Univariable logistic regression identified male sex and hyponatremia as factors significantly associated with poor outcome (Table S3). These two variables were entered into an exploratory multivariable model, in which both were associated with poor outcome (male sex: OR = 5.12, 95% CI = 1.27–34.48, p = 0.041; hyponatremia: OR = 4.69, 95% CI = 1.54–15.47, p = 0.008), with negligible collinearity (VIF = 1.00). In the complete-case analysis (n = 73), hyponatremia remained significantly associated with poor functional outcome (OR = 4.22, 95% CI = 1.21–15.33, p = 0.024). With respect to treatment, second-line immunotherapy was used in 4/17 (23.5%) patients with poor outcome and 5/87 (5.7%) with good outcome; SSMIs were used in 4/17 (23.5%) and 12/87 (13.8%), respectively.

Discussion

This multicenter study delineated prognostic profiles in a moderately large cohort of patients with anti-LGI1 encephalitis. The relapse rate was relatively low at 11.5%. Hyponatremia at diagnosis was associated with an increased risk of relapse. Poor functional outcome occurred in 16.3% of patients and was potentially linked to male sex and hyponatremia. Furthermore, we systemically described the immunotherapy strategies employed in this cohort, reflecting clinical practices across the participating centers.

Our cohort comprised 104 patients with a median onset age of 56.5 years and a male predominance. This median age is somewhat lower than that typically reported in Western cohorts,2,5,6,8,9 whereas the overall demographic profile is consistent with previous Chinese studies.3,7,10,12 The observed relapse rate was 11.5%. While this falls below the broad range of rates in the existing literature (13.3–51.8%),2–10 it is comparable to the 14% reported in a recent large-scale study involving 210 patients. 5 The heterogeneity in reported relapse risk across studies can be attributed to several factors. First, the required period of symptom improvement or stability before relapse varies, ranging from ≥ 2 months2–4,10,11 to ≥ 3 months5–7,9 or even ≥ 6 months. 8 Second, the different interpretations of relapse may contribute to this disparity. For instance, recent studies have proposed more detailed descriptions: Seery et al. refine the relapse criteria by excluding specific cases with an isolated seizure or probable epilepsy, 18 while Baumgartner et al. require an increase of ≥ 1 point in the mRS score and/or immunotherapy reinitiation following initial improvement. 19 In our cohort, all relapsing patients exhibited manifestations consistent with prior descriptions, and their symptoms resolved following the re-initiation of immunotherapy. In any case, this underscores the necessity for establishing a standardized, antibody-specific consensus on relapse criteria, thereby enhancing consistency in clinical management. Furthermore, discrepancies in sample composition, clinical phenotypes, follow-up duration, and treatment protocols across studies also account for the variation in reported relapse risk.

To guide aggressive and personalized immunotherapy, identifying predictors of relapse remains essential. Previous studies have highlighted several potential risk factors: a cohort of 44 patients identified acute sleep disturbance as predictive of relapse, while a larger, recent study by Campetella et al. found that patients with residual cognitive impairment had a higher relapse risk.3,5 In the present cohort, we observed a trend toward an association between sleep disorders and relapse, although it was not statistically significant—a finding that warrants validation in future studies. Notably, hyponatremia at diagnosis was significantly associated with a higher risk of relapse. Previous studies have shown that hyponatremia was associated with higher serum LGI1-IgG titers, CSF antibody positivity, and worse functional outcomes,19,20 but its relationship with relapse has not been reported. The prevalence of hyponatremia in the literature varies widely (33.7–76.0%),3,4,7,10,11,18 whereas our cohort showed a lower rate (31.7%). Several factors may explain this discrepancy, among which the timing of sodium measurement is paramount. Here, we defined hyponatremia using serum sodium levels measured at diagnosis, thereby avoiding potential confounding from treatment-related fluctuations and ensuring greater homogeneity across patients. In contrast, most published studies have typically reported nadir values without standardizing timing or frequency of sodium measurements. When the nadir criterion was applied to our cohort, the prevalence of hyponatremia rose to 41.3%, and this association with relapse and poor functional outcome remained significant (data not shown). Although the exact mechanism linking hyponatremia to relapse was unknown and not explored in this study, several plausible pathophysiological explanations have been proposed. It has been suggested that LGI1 antibodies may interfere with hypothalamic osmoregulation, leading to hyponatremia, which in turn may disrupt blood-brain barrier integrity via cellular osmolarity and inflammatory mediator release, thereby facilitating persistent inflammation.21,22 Semi-quantitative analysis of 18F-FDG-PET has also linked hyponatremia to metabolic abnormalities in extra-hypothalamus regions, involving the caudate nucleus, frontal and temporal lobes.23,24 It has been speculated that these features may reflect or provoke a more pronounced immune response against the brain, potentially increasing susceptibility to recurrent neuroinflammation and symptom relapse. Taken together, our findings suggest an association between hyponatremia and relapse risk, which may be useful for risk stratification and guiding treatment approaches. However, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized assessment protocols are needed to confirm these exploratory observations.

Regarding immunotherapy for anti-LGI1 encephalitis, CS treatment at disease onset has been reported to reduce the risk of relapse. 5 In our cohort, all but four patients received CS treatment, which precluded a meaningful assessment of this association. Additionally, our multicenter data showed that prolonged CS monotherapy was the most common maintenance regimen, reflecting the prevailing practice across the participating centers. This preference was influenced by concerns regarding the potential adverse effects of SSMI (e.g., infection risk), cost, accessibility, and uncertain efficacy. Despite the relatively low rates of relapse and poor functional outcomes observed in our cohort, these findings could not be interpreted as evidence that CS alone is equivalent or superior to SSMIs or second-line agents. Moreover, the systemic adverse effects of long-term CS therapy may extend beyond infectious complications, encompassing diabetes, weight gain, sleep disturbances, metabolic syndrome, osteoporosis, and others. Hence, further investigation is warranted to establish optimal CS dose tapering and treatment duration. Previous studies have indicated that RTX may be effective in preventing relapses in anti-LGI1 encephalitis, and MMF was associated with a significant reduction of relapse rate.9,11,18 In contrast, other reports have not found such positive associations.5–7,12 In our cohort, second-line agents were used in one relapsing patient and eight non-relapsing patients, and SSMIs in two relapsing and fourteen non-relapsing patients. Given the retrospective design and infrequent use of RTX and SSMIs, all treatment-related data in this study are descriptive, and no definitive treatment recommendations can be established. Therefore, stratifying patients based on relapse risk becomes more crucial for enabling targeted immunotherapy strategies.

At final follow-up, 83.7% of patients achieved favorable outcomes while 16.3% exhibited poor outcomes, consistent with previous reports.2–4,8,11 In addition to hyponatremia, male sex was identified as a factor associated with poor outcome. This elevated risk may be attributable to sex-based immunological differences (e.g., androgen-mediated B-cell activation), delayed healthcare-seeking behavior, or genetic determinants. 25 While human leukocyte antigens (HLA) DRB1*07:01 is a dominant risk allele in Caucasian and Jewish populations, the DRB1*03:01 allele is associated with risk in Chinese cohorts, where it occurs more frequently in females and is linked to better treatment response.26–29 Our prior work adds that male patients often present with later onset, FBTCS, and CSF signs of greater neuroinflammation, whereas females more frequently had focal seizures. 30 However, two studies have indicated that females tend to exhibit worse outcomes, potentially attributable to the variations in sample size, follow-up duration, and immunopathogenic factors across ethnicities.16,26 Additionally, given the limited number of patients receiving second-line agents or SSMIs and the potential for confounding, our treatment-related data are descriptive. Future prospective studies are warranted to assess treatment efficacy on functional outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, although the cohort is relatively large for this rare disease, the sample size remains modest, and the limited number of outcome events may partially constrain statistical power. Second, the retrospective design may introduce bias and limit causal inference. For instance, serum sodium measurements were not standardized in frequency or timing, and hyponatremia at diagnosis may not capture the nadir value, potentially leading to an underestimation of the condition; the recorded prevalence of FBDS was relatively low, likely reflecting under-ascertainment; and treatment decisions were influenced by multiple factors, precluding any definitive conclusions about treatment-outcome relationships. Third, the uneven distribution of cases across the five centers may reflect local referral patterns and center-specific practices, representing a potential source of bias. Finally, the mRS may not provide a comprehensive evaluation of disease outcomes, particularly cognitive deficits. An mRS score of ≤ 2 does not guarantee good recovery, as patients may still experience short-term memory loss or mild executive dysfunction that impair daily living. Therefore, future prospective studies should be conducted with well-designed protocols to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Our study observed a relatively low relapse rate in anti-LGI1 encephalitis and identified an association between hyponatremia and increased relapse risk. Male sex and hyponatremia were additionally linked to poor long-term outcomes. These features may facilitate risk stratification for tailored management and monitoring. However, further prospective studies are warranted.
