Stress redistributing interdigitated architectures inspired by the diabolical ironclad beetle for stretchable systems
Authors: Sieb Chanchamnan, Weiyi Wang, Se Rim Jang, Il Won Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlexible and stretchable structural systems require geometries capable of maintaining mechanical stability under repeated deformation while minimizing localized stress concentration. In this study, a beetle elytra-inspired flexible architecture based on the interdigitated suture geometry of the diabolical ironclad beetle was developed and compared with conventional Peano curve-based structures. The proposed geometry incorporated elongated elliptical motifs designed to enhance tensile deformability and promote stress redistribution during loading. Finite element analysis demonstrated that the beetle-inspired structures exhibited more uniform strain propagation and lower local stress concentration than conventional structures, particularly in the horizontal configuration. Uniaxial tensile testing further confirmed enhanced deformation sustainability and delayed structural failure in the beetle-inspired architectures. In addition, comparative simulations of deformable sensor-interconnect systems revealed reduced peak stress accumulation near rigid island regions. Overall, the present study demonstrates that beetle-inspired interdigitated geometries can effectively enhance flexibility and mechanical stability in stretchable systems, suggesting their strong potential for future flexible and deformable devices requiring high durability under repeated mechanical deformation. Subject terms: Physics, Chemistry

Introduction

The development of flexible and stretchable structural systems has attracted considerable attention owing to their broad applicability in wearable electronics, biomedical devices, flexible sensors, and deformable interconnect systems1–6. Among various structural strategies, serpentine and Peano-inspired geometries have been widely investigated because their curved pathways enable large elastic deformation and improved flexibility under external loading7–9. However, conventional structures can still exhibit mechanical reliability limitations under repeated deformation, as previous studies have reported localized strain accumulation and fatigue crack initiation in highly curved regions of stretchable interconnect systems10.

Biological systems provide valuable design principles for overcoming these limitations through mechanically optimized hierarchical architectures11–13. In particular, the exoskeletal suture structure of the diabolical ironclad beetle has recently attracted significant attention because of its exceptional resistance to fracture and impact loading14. Previous studies reported that the beetle elytra contain interdigitated jigsaw-like interfaces capable of effectively dissipating mechanical stress and suppressing catastrophic fracture propagation15,16. In addition, recent studies demonstrated that bioinspired curved interconnect pathways could reduce stress concentration while maintaining high deformability and structural reliability17.

In this study, we demonstrate a beetle elytra-inspired flexible structural architecture based on the interdigitated suture geometry of the diabolical ironclad beetle for mechanically reliable stretchable systems. The proposed structure incorporates elongated and vertically interconnected elliptical motifs designed to enhance tensile deformability and reduce localized stress concentration during mechanical loading. Finite element analysis and uniaxial tensile testing were performed to evaluate stress distribution, deformation behavior, and mechanical stability, while the applicability of the bioinspired geometry to deformable sensor-interconnect systems was comparatively investigated using conventional and beetle-inspired structural layouts. The results demonstrate that the beetle-inspired interdigitated geometry effectively enhances stress redistribution and mechanical adaptability compared with conventional Peano-based structures, highlighting its strong potential for future flexible and stretchable devices operating under repeated deformation conditions.

Results and discussion

Design concept of beetle elytra-inspired flexible structures

Figure 1 illustrates the design strategy of the beetle elytra-inspired flexible structures developed in this study. The proposed geometry was derived from the interdigitated suture architecture observed in the exoskeleton of the diabolical ironclad beetle, which has been reported to effectively dissipate external mechanical stress through mechanically interlocked interfaces. Based on this biological mechanism, a bioinspired curved geometry composed of elongated elliptical interconnections was designed and compared with a conventional Peano curve-based structure.

Design concept and structural comparison of beetle elytra-inspired flexible architectures.

a Schematic illustration of the interdigitated suture geometry derived from the diabolical ironclad beetle elytra and the corresponding bioinspired structural motif. b Finite element analysis of anti-trapezoidal (AT), double-hook (DH), and beetle-hook (BH) interlocking geometries showing stress distribution under tensile loading conditions. c Quantitative comparison of maximum and average stress values of the investigated interlocking geometries. d Schematic illustrations of conventional Peano curve-based and beetle elytra-inspired flexible structures in vertical and horizontal configurations

As shown in Fig. 1a, the characteristic beetle-inspired geometry was extracted from the suture interface of the beetle elytra and simplified into a periodically repeated structural motif. Unlike the conventional Peano curve composed of relatively broad circular pathways, the beetle-inspired structure exhibited vertically elongated interconnections with narrower neck regions and continuously connected deformation pathways. Such a configuration was expected to promote gradual tensile deformation while suppressing excessive local stress accumulation at highly curved regions.

To further investigate the stress redistribution capability according to interlocking geometry, three representative interfacial configurations including anti-trapezoidal (AT), double-hook (DH), and beetle-hook (BH) structures were comparatively analyzed through finite element simulations, as shown in Fig. 1b. The stress distributions revealed clear differences depending on the interlocking morphology. Among the investigated geometries, the beetle-hook structure exhibited the lowest stress concentration throughout the interlocking region, whereas the anti-trapezoidal structure showed pronounced stress localization near the sharp corner regions. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the beetle-hook geometry exhibited substantially lower maximum and average stress values compared with the other structures (Fig. 1c), indicating that the bioinspired interdigitated geometry effectively redistributed externally applied mechanical stress.

Based on these observations, flexible architectures incorporating repeated beetle-inspired pathways were designed in both vertical and horizontal orientations and compared with conventional Peano curve structures, as illustrated in Fig. 1d. Compared with the control structures, the beetle-inspired pathways exhibited increased interconnection density and elongated deformation regions. Such geometric differences were expected to influence tensile deformation behavior, stress propagation pathways, and overall mechanical adaptability during external loading.

Finite element analysis of structural deformation behavior

Finite element analysis was performed to investigate the deformation behavior and stress distribution characteristics of the control and beetle-inspired flexible structures under tensile loading conditions. Figure 2a presents the equivalent elastic strain distributions of the four structural configurations including control vertical, control horizontal, beetle vertical, and beetle horizontal structures.

Finite element analysis of conventional and beetle-inspired flexible structures under tensile loading.

A 10 mm tensile displacement was applied symmetrically to both ends of each structure. a Equivalent elastic strain distributions of control vertical, control horizontal, beetle vertical, and beetle horizontal structures. b Total deformation distributions of the corresponding structures under tensile loading. c Quantitative comparison of total deformation values, d maximum stress, and e average stress values. f Heatmap analysis summarizing the mechanical behavior of the investigated structures

The strain distributions demonstrated distinct deformation characteristics depending on both structural geometry and orientation. In the conventional control structures, strain concentration was primarily localized near the highly curved turning regions of the Peano pathways. Particularly in the control vertical structure, substantial strain accumulation occurred at the narrow connecting regions between adjacent loops, indicating nonuniform stress propagation during tensile deformation. In contrast, the beetle-inspired structures exhibited more distributed strain propagation throughout the elongated interconnection pathways. The repeated elliptical geometry enabled gradual deformation across multiple interconnected units rather than concentrating strain within limited curvature regions.

The total deformation profiles shown in Fig. 2b further supported these tendencies. The beetle horizontal structure exhibited the most uniform deformation distribution among the investigated groups, whereas the control structures displayed more localized displacement behavior near the terminal curved regions. The elongated interdigitated pathways of the beetle-inspired structures appeared to improve load-sharing capability between neighboring deformation units, thereby promoting mechanically stable deformation.

Quantitative analysis revealed that the beetle horizontal structure exhibited the largest total deformation value among all groups (Fig. 2c), indicating enhanced structural flexibility during tensile loading. Meanwhile, the maximum stress analysis demonstrated that the beetle horizontal structure exhibited the lowest peak stress value (Fig. 2d), suggesting effective suppression of local stress concentration. In contrast, the beetle vertical structure demonstrated higher maximum stress due to increased structural density and reduced spacing between neighboring pathways, which enhanced mechanical coupling via the bonded film and interaction geometry. The finite element model utilized a bonded contact Augmented Lagrange formulation with asymmetric behavior, ensuring that no physical contact or collision occurred between adjacent units. However, the close proximity of pathways facilitated stronger stress-transfer interactions through the continuous substrate, so deformation in one unit influenced neighboring units and led to localized stress accumulation under tensile loading.

Interestingly, the average stress values shown in Fig. 2e and the heatmap analysis in Fig. 2f demonstrated relatively similar overall stress magnitudes among the investigated structures despite substantial differences in local stress concentration behavior. These results indicate that the primary advantage of the beetle-inspired geometry was not simply reducing the overall stress magnitude, but rather redistributing localized stress throughout the deformable pathways. Such behavior is mechanically advantageous because suppression of local peak stress can delay crack initiation and improve structural durability under repeated deformation conditions18,19.

Experimental tensile behavior of flexible structures

To experimentally validate the simulation results, uniaxial tensile testing was performed using a universal testing machine (UTM) at a crosshead speed of 125 mm.min-1, with loading continuously applied until catastrophic fracture of the specimen occurred. The tensile properties were obtained from the complete stress-strain response, as shown in Fig. 3a. Representative images of the fabricated structures for the tensile test are presented in Fig. 3b.

Experimental tensile evaluation of conventional and beetle-inspired flexible structures.

a Experimental setup for uniaxial tensile testing using a universal testing machine. b Optical images of the 3D-printed structures for the tensile testing (scale bar = 1 cm). c Stress-strain curves of horizontal control and beetle-inspired structures. d Stress-strain curves of vertical control and beetle-inspired structures. e Representative fracture morphologies of the control and beetle-inspired structures after uniaxial tensile testing (scale bar = 1 cm)

The experimental observations demonstrated distinct deformation and fracture behaviors depending on structural geometry. In the control horizontal structure, fracture initiation occurred relatively early at the highly curved connecting regions, resulting in abrupt stress reduction during tensile loading. Conversely, the beetle horizontal structure maintained structural continuity over a substantially larger strain range before failure. The elongated elliptical pathways enabled progressive unfolding and gradual deformation throughout the line.

The stress-strain curves shown in Fig. 3c further supported these observations. The beetle horizontal structure exhibited significantly extended strain tolerance compared with the control horizontal structure, indicating improved stretchability and deformation sustainability. Although the peak stress of the beetle-inspired structure was slightly lower than that of the control structure, the structure sustained larger deformation while maintaining structural continuity before catastrophic failure. Even though the peak stress was slightly lower than that of the control structure, the extended deformation range demonstrates improved deformation tolerance and progressive failure behavior, rather than increased fracture strength. In the vertical configurations shown in Fig. 3d, the beetle-inspired structure exhibited higher stress values than the control vertical structure during tensile loading. This behavior was attributed to the increased number of interconnected pathways and reduced free spacing within the vertically aligned geometry, which increased structural stiffness under vertical deformation20–22. Nevertheless, the beetle-inspired vertical structure still exhibited progressive deformation behavior without abrupt structural collapse, indicating a mechanically stable tensile response.

Overall, the experimental tensile results showed strong agreement with the finite element analysis, confirming that the beetle-inspired interdigitated geometry effectively altered stress propagation pathways and improved structural deformability during tensile loading conditions.

Mechanical behavior of single-channel structures

To further isolate the geometric contribution of the beetle-inspired motif, single-channel structures were additionally analyzed, as shown in Fig. 4. The control single-channel structure consisted of a conventional Peano-type pathway, whereas the beetle-inspired single-channel structure incorporated elongated vertically connected elliptical motifs.

Mechanical analysis of single-channel flexible structures both sides by 10 mm.

a Design schematics of conventional and beetle-inspired single-channel structures. b Equivalent elastic strain distributions obtained by finite element analysis. c Displacement distribution profiles of the corresponding structures under tensile loading conditions. d Quantitative comparison of maximum stress values, and (e) average stress values

The equivalent elastic strain distributions shown in Fig. 4b revealed distinct stress propagation behavior depending on structural geometry. In the conventional single-channel pathway, strain concentration was predominantly localized near the outer curvature regions of the repeated loops. In contrast, the beetle-inspired geometry promoted more distributed strain propagation along the entire deformable pathway. The elongated interdigitated curvature enabled gradual stress transfer between neighboring deformation regions, thereby reducing excessive local stress accumulation.

The displacement distribution profiles shown in Fig. 4c further demonstrated that the beetle-inspired structure exhibited more uniform deformation behavior during tensile loading. Although both structures exhibited similar overall deformation trends, the beetle-inspired geometry maintained smoother deformation continuity along the interconnected pathways.

Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the maximum stress values of the two structures were relatively similar (Fig. 4d). However, the beetle-inspired structure exhibited a slightly lower average stress value compared with the conventional control structure (Fig. 4e). These findings suggest that the bioinspired geometry may contribute to more uniform stress distribution without substantially increasing peak mechanical loading.

Furthermore, the beetle-inspired single-channel structure reduces average stress compared to the conventional design. However, the maximum stress remains largely unchanged because only a single, uninterrupted load-transfer pathway in present. Under tensile loading, critical curved regions continue to experience localized stress concentrations, and the lack of interconnected pathways limits the redistribution of peak stresses. In contrast, the multi-pathway beetle-inspired networks shown in Fig. 2 provide multiple interconnected deformation routes, which enable efficient load sharing among adjacent structural units. This networked architecture redistributes localized stresses, suppresses peak stress concentrations, and leads to a more significant reduction in maximum stress. Therefore, while the single-channel configuration improves stress uniformity, it does not substantially reduce peak stresses. The interconnected network design introduces an additional load-sharing mechanism that further enhances overall stress mitigation.

Overall, the single-channel analysis suggests that the elongated interdigitated geometry itself contributes to improved mechanical adaptability and stress redistribution even in simplified structural configurations without complex multi-pathway interactions.

Application to stretchable sensor structures

To investigate the practical applicability of the proposed geometry, beetle-inspired interconnect pathways were incorporated into deformable sensor structures, as illustrated in Fig. 5a. Conventional control sensor structures and beetle-inspired sensor structures were comparatively analyzed through finite element simulations.

Application of beetle-inspired geometry to deformable sensor-interconnect systems.

a Three-dimensional models of conventional and beetle-inspired sensor structures. b Equivalent elastic strain distributions of the sensor-interconnect systems under tensile loading. c Total deformation distributions of the corresponding structures. d Magnified stress distribution images showing deformation behavior near rigid island regions. e Quantitative comparison of maximum stress values and f average stress values between conventional and beetle-inspired sensor structures

As shown in Fig. 5b, c, the beetle-inspired sensor structure exhibited more distributed deformation and strain propagation throughout the interconnect pathways. In contrast, the conventional control structure showed pronounced stress accumulation near the terminal curvature regions adjacent to the rigid sensor islands. Such localized stress concentration may increase the risk of interconnect fracture and mechanical instability during repeated deformation23.

The magnified stress distribution images shown in Fig. 5d further demonstrated that the beetle-inspired pathways effectively dispersed localized stress along the elongated curvature regions surrounding the rigid islands. Quantitative analysis revealed that the beetle-inspired sensor structure exhibited a lower maximum stress value than the conventional control structure (Fig. 5e), indicating improved suppression of local stress concentration near mechanically vulnerable regions.

Although the average stress value of the beetle-inspired structure was slightly higher than that of the control structure (Fig. 5f), the reduction in peak stress is mechanically more important for preventing crack initiation and structural failure in stretchable interconnect systems. These results suggest that the beetle-inspired geometry effectively redistributed externally applied stress throughout the deformable pathways while maintaining mechanical continuity around the rigid sensor components.

Additionally, the beetle-inspired structure exhibited a slightly higher average stress than the control structure, this increase primarily indicates a more uniform redistribution of the applied load across the interconnect pathways rather than excessive stress localization. Because crack initiation in stretchable interconnects is typically governed by localized peak stresses, the observed reduction in maximum stress is anticipated to enhance mechanical reliability. Nevertheless, the effect of the slightly elevated average stress on long-term fatigue behavior and polymer matric durability under cyclic loading was not addressed in the study and warrants further investigation in future experimental and computational research.

Overall, the present findings demonstrate that the proposed beetle-inspired geometry may serve as a promising structural strategy for future flexible sensors, stretchable electronics, and deformable biomedical devices requiring enhanced mechanical reliability under repeated mechanical deformation.

Conclusion

In this study, a beetle elytra-inspired flexible structural architecture was developed based on the interdigitated suture geometry of the diabolical ironclad beetle and systematically compared with conventional Peano curve-based structures through finite element analysis and uniaxial tensile testing. The proposed bioinspired geometry incorporated elongated elliptical interconnection pathways that enabled more uniform strain propagation and improved stress redistribution during tensile deformation. The results demonstrated that the beetle-inspired structures exhibited reduced local stress concentration and enhanced deformation capability compared with conventional structures, particularly in the horizontal configuration. Experimental tensile testing further validated the numerical findings by confirming improved deformation sustainability and delayed structural failure under large strain conditions. Furthermore, the application of the proposed geometry to stretchable sensor-interconnect systems demonstrated reduced peak stress accumulation near rigid island regions, indicating improved mechanical reliability for deformable electronic applications. Overall, the present study suggests that beetle-inspired interdigitated structures provide an effective strategy for enhancing flexibility and mechanical stability in future stretchable and deformable systems.

Materials and methods

Structure design

The sensor structures were categorized into two types: a circular control structure and a structure inspired by the diabolical ironclad beetle. The control structure was modeled with a radius of 10 mm, a tangential angle of 45°, a width of 1 mm, and a thickness of 0.01 mm. The beetle-inspired structure featured an elytra with an elliptical ratio of 1:1.814,17, a minor axis of 6 mm, a major axis of 10.8 mm, a tangential angle of 25°, a width of 1 mm, and a thickness of 0.01 mm. Four stretchable structures were designed: vertical and horizontal control structures, and vertical and horizontal beetle-inspired structures. The vertical and horizontal control structures measured 127.28 × 118.99 mm, the vertical beetle-inspired structure measured 138.87 × 137.41 mm, and the horizontal beetle-inspired structure measured 131.15 × 129.44 mm. To enable stretchability on both sides, a thin film measuring 150 × 200 × 0.1 mm was modeled and mounted between the designed structures, using a bonded contact with the Augmented Lagrange formulation and asymmetric behavior.

Additionally, the flexible sensor structures, comprising both control and beetle-inspired geometries, were connected using a square connector measuring 54 × 54 × 2 mm. The thickness of the connection structure was 2 × 0.2 mm, consistent with the overall design. Interlock structures were developed in three configurations: anti-trapezoidal, double hook, and beetle hook, each with dimensions of 5.4 × 10 × 1 mm24. Structural analysis was conducted using finite element analysis in Ansys Workbench 2023 R1 (ANSYS, Inc., USA).

Material properties

This study selected TPU, gold, and ABS as representative materials commonly used in flexible electronic devices. The mechanical behavior of a thin film was modeled and defined as a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) with a density of 1.178 kg.m-3, Young’s modulus of 1.647 MPa, shear modulus of 584.96 MPa, and Poisson’s ratio of 0.4078 for its material properties. Additionally, the designed structures were defined as gold, the material properties, and a density of 19,300 kg.m-3, Young’s modulus of 75.920 MPa, shear modulus of 26.732 MPa, and Poisson’s ratio of 0.42. Furthermore, AT, DH, and BH were defined as ABS, with a density of 1,050 kg.m-3, a Young’s modulus of 2.408 GPa, and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.4078, each material is assigned according to the Ansys Granta Materials database.

Processing methods of finite element analysis and meshing

The interlocking designs, including anti-trapezoidal, double-hook, and beetle-hook meshes, were configured to allow adaptive sizing, three resolutions, and multizone meshing with a 0.2 mm element size for all components. To assess the effectiveness of the interlocking mechanisms, the bottom part of each structure was fixed, while a 5 N force was applied to the top side of each interlocking structure. Additionally, analyses of both the single-line stretchable sensor and the complete stretchable sensor structure were conducted by applying a 10 mm displacement to each end of the structures, producing symmetric tensile loading to evaluate the flexibility of the control and beetle-inspired designs. The mesh was generated automatically for all components, including the bottom and top thin films and the designed structure. The thin film had a body size of 5 mm, while the structure measured 0.5 mm. Finally, the wearable sensor structure, both the control and the beetle, enabled adaptive sizing, three resolutions, and multizone meshing with 1 mm elements for all parts. To evaluate the structural stress distribution, a 20 mm displacement was applied to the middle of the wearable sensor. Figure S1 summarizes the elements and nodes during the computational analysis.
