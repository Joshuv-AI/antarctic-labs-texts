HCV screening among surgical patients in a tertiary hospital of Northern China
Authors: Chunmiao Pang, Xingjie Niu, Fangjie Zhang, Zhihui Liu, Zhandong Lin, Liuchang Nie, Guomin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe diagnosis and treatment rates for hepatitis C virus (HCV) remain below target levels. This study aims to define and analyze the HCV care cascade for surgical patients in a high-prevalence region, evaluate the impact of a hospital-wide elimination program, and identify barriers and facilitators to completing the diagnostic pathway.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed de-identified electronic health records from 114,968 consecutive preoperative patients undergoing routine Hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody screening at a tertiary academic medical center between January 2021 and December 2024. We evaluated the effectiveness of a multifaceted HCV elimination program initiated in 2022 by assessing patient management through a predefined three-step care cascade: (1) universal anti-HCV antibody screening; (2) HCV RNA testing within 72 h for antibody-positive individuals; and (3) prompt hepatology referral within the patient’s hospitalization for RNA-positive cases. Data on patient demographics, clinical department, HCV antibody results, HCV RNA testing completion status, and key clinical variables (emergency surgery, transfusion history, dialysis, length of stay, physician practice duration) were extracted from standardized electronic health records. Data accuracy was verified via a manual audit of 200 randomly selected records. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. A multivariable binary logistic regression model with backward stepwise selection was employed to identify independent predictors of HCV RNA testing completion, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess model goodness-of-fit. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post-hoc power analysis confirmed the study was sufficiently powered (> 80%) for its primary outcomes.ResultsAmong 114,968 patients, the overall anti-HCV positivity rate was 2.03% (95% CI: 1.95–2.11; n = 2,334), with significant demographic disparities: males had a 1.48-fold higher positive rate than females (2.45% vs. 1.63%, P < 0.001). The prevalence of hepatitis C antibodies exhibited a nonlinear positive correlation with age distribution, with infection rates rising significantly after the age of 30. The 70–79 age group presented an 11.74-fold greater prevalence than patients aged 40–49 years (3.64% vs. 0.31%, P < 0.001). Significant gaps were observed in the care cascade, with only 33.6% of anti-HCV positive patients completing RNA confirmation. There were notable interdepartmental disparities: Hepatobiliary Surgery had the highest anti-HCV positivity rate (7.90%), whereas Pediatric (0.04%) and Obstetric (0.26%) departments had the lowest. Multivariate logistic regression identified key factors associated with testing completion. A hospital stay of ≤ 7 days was the strongest barrier (aOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.39–0.60; P < 0.001). Conversely, receipt of dialysis was the strongest facilitator (aOR = 8.69, 95% CI: 3.83–19.76; P < 0.001), followed by admission to an HCV-related department (aOR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.75–2.71; P < 0.001) and history of blood transfusion (aOR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.20–2.76; P = 0.005). Physicians with < 5 years (aOR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.27–2.01; P < 0.001) or 5–10 years of experience (aOR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.35–2.18; P < 0.001) were more likely to order testing than those with > 10 years of experience. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an overall correct classification rate of 69.6%.ConclusionThis study identifies a critical breakdown in the hepatitis C virus (HCV) care cascade at our institution, where a substantial “diagnostic gap” resulted in only 33.63% of anti-HCV positive patients receiving confirmatory RNA testing. The high seroprevalence (2.03%) in our pre-operative cohort, particularly among males and the elderly (peaking at 3.64% in the 70–79 age group), underscores the need for targeted screening. The primary barrier was identified as a fundamental incompatibility between the two-step diagnostic process and clinical workflows, with short hospital stays (≤ 7 days) being the strongest negative predictor. This systemic failure, observed across various non-hepatology departments, contrasts sharply with high performance in settings like Nephrology (87.80% testing rate) and mirrors challenges reported globally. Conversely, downstream linkage to care proved successful, with hepatology referral rates rising to 66.67% in 2024, demonstrating the effectiveness of structured, technology-driven interventions like electronic referral systems. These findings advocate for fundamental system-level reforms, including the implementation of reflex RNA testing, intelligent EMR alert systems, and performance-linked quality metrics, to bridge the diagnostic gap and advance progress towards HCV elimination goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04456-z.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a significant public health challenge in China, with an estimated national burden of 10 million chronic cases [1]. The asymptomatic nature of chronic HCV infection contributes to low detection rates, as most cases are identified incidentally through routine health screenings rather than symptomatic presentation. Globally, only 19% of HCV-infected individuals were aware of their status in 2015 [2], with many presenting advanced complications such as cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma at initial diagnosis. While direct-acting antiviral (DAA) regimens now achieve cure rates exceeding 95% [3], attaining WHO elimination targets necessitates improved screening strategies. Early treatment initiation not only enhances clinical outcomes [4] but also reduces annual healthcare expenditures [5].

The World Health Assembly’s 2022–2030 Global Health Sector Strategy on Viral Hepatitis established ambitious elimination targets: an 80% reduction in new hepatitis C cases and a 65% decrease in HCV-related mortality by 2030, conditional on achieving 90% diagnosis and 80% treatment coverage rates [6]. Recent surveillance data highlight significant implementation gaps in China, with 2024 national HCV diagnosis and treatment rates reported at 51% and 8%, respectively [7], emphasizing the critical need for optimized interventions.

To accelerate progress toward national elimination goals, our institution launched a focused HCV micro-elimination program in 2022. The program was built around a standardized, time-driven care cascade with the following explicitly defined core components and monitoring procedures.

1.Universal Screening: All eligible patients admitted to the hospital underwent routine anti-HCV antibody testing as part of the standard admission protocol. 2. Physician-Mandated RNA Testing with a 72-Hour Target: For all patients with a positive anti-HCV screening result, the attending physician was required to manually place the order for confirmatory HCV RNA testing. The program enforced a strict target timeframe of ≤ 72 h from the reporting of the positive antibody result to the placement of the RNA test order.3. Mandatory In-Hospital Specialist Referral: For all inpatients with a confirmed positive HCV RNA result, the attending physician was required to personally place a referral order to either the Hepatology or Infectious Diseases department. This referral was required to be completed during the patient’s index hospitalization.4. Compliance Monitoring and Patient Tracking: Adherence to these protocols was monitored in real-time via the hospital’s integrated electronic health record. A centralized registry tracked all viremic patients, and designated staff ensured that physician orders for both RNA testing and specialist referrals were placed within the stipulated timeframes before patient discharge.

Given that our hospital mandates routine infectious disease screening for all surgical patients prior to invasive procedures, this large, accessible cohort of surgical patients (n = 114,968) provided a high-yield population to evaluate the program’s effectiveness and to clarify the completion status of the HCV cascade, particularly among non-specialty departments where systemic failure often occurs. We conducted this retrospective analysis (2021–2024) to: (1) quantify the completion status of the institutional HCV care cascade; (2) assess demographic and departmental variations in antibody positivity; and (3) identify key factors influencing adherence to HCV RNA screening, an initial and critical failure point. Data accuracy was verified via a manual audit of 200 randomly selected electronic health records to ensure methodological rigor.

Methods and materials

Study design and participant enrollment

This retrospective cohort study analyzed de-identified medical records of 114,968 consecutive preoperative patients who underwent routine Hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody screening at a tertiary academic medical center (Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University) from January 2021 to December 2024. Duplicate records were removed based on unique patient identification numbers. The cohort included patients from 15 surgical departments (Hepatobiliary Surgery, Orthopedics, Cardiothoracic Surgery, Pediatric Surgery, Gynecology, Obstetrics, Otolaryngology & Ophthalmology, Neurosurgery, General Surgery) and interdisciplinary medical units conducting image-guided interventions (Cardiology: coronary angiography with stent placement; Gastroenterology: hepatic biopsy; Nephrology: renal biopsy; Pulmonology: bronchoscopy; Neurology: neurointerventional procedures), as well as other medical departments performing surgical procedures. We defined the HCV-related departments as the Hepatobiliary Surgery Department and the Gastroenterology Department.The data we extracted included patient gender, age, admitting department, attending physician, whether they underwent a surgical procedure, dialysis status, history of blood transfusion, HCV antibody result, whether an HCV RNA test was performed within 72 h of a positive antibody result, the HCV RNA result, and whether a referral to the hepatology clinic was made during the hospitalization.

To ensure data quality and methodological rigor, a stringent validation process was implemented. Data accuracy was verified via a manual audit performed by two independent, blinded research assistants on 200 randomly selected electronic health records (EHRs). The audit focused specifically on the primary outcome variables (HCV RNA testing completion and Linkage to Care status) and key demographic data. This process confirmed a high level of fidelity, with the overall concordance rate between the extracted dataset and the source EHRs exceeding 98%. Any initial discrepancies were resolved through consensus or adjudication by a senior investigator.

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the HCV micro-elimination program on a strictly defined, two-step clinical pathway. The primary endpoints were limited to two time-sensitive outcomes: The first was the completion of HCV RNA testing, measured as a physician’s test order placed within 72 h of a positive antibody report. The second was linkage to hepatology care, measured as a specialist referral order placed before discharge from the hospitalization during which the RNA-positive result was confirmed. This design intentionally excludes post-discharge outcomes like treatment initiation.

Detection of anti-HCV and HCV RNA

Plasma was isolated by centrifuging whole blood at 1,500×g for 10 min. HCV antibody testing was conducted in duplicate via 150 µL aliquots on the Sysmex HISCL™−5000/800 automated immunoassay system. Samples demonstrating a signal-to-cut-off (S/CO) ratio ≥ 1.0 (as per the manufacturer’s diagnostic threshold) in either replicate were classified as positive. Quality control (QC) procedures for the anti-HCV assay involved running commercial positive and negative controls with every batch. All assay runs were deemed acceptable only when internal and external QC results fell within 2 standard deviations of the established mean. The overall inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for the anti-HCV assay was maintained below 15% throughout the study period, confirming assay reliability.

For HCV RNA quantification, 200 µL of plasma was analyzed via real-time PCR (Bio-Rad CFX96 system) with genotype-independent primers and probes, achieving a validated lower detection limit of 15 IU/mL for all HCV genotypes (1–6). Rigorous quality assurance for the quantitative HCV RNA assay included the concurrent use of low, medium, and high viral load controls in every run. The inter-assay CV for HCV RNA quantification was consistently maintained at ≤ 5% across all measured genotypes, ensuring high longitudinal precision and reproducibility.

All experimental workflows strictly adhered to the manufacturer-specified protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Intergroup comparisons were conducted via Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Two binary logistic regression models were developed to address distinct research aims. The first model evaluated predictors associated with anti-HCV positivity, incorporating sex (coded as male = 1, female = 0) and age groups (with < 20 years as the reference category), and included a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. The second model identified factors associated with failure to undergo hepatitis C virus RNA testing, with the outcome defined as not tested (0) versus tested (1). Covariates included sex, history of blood transfusion (yes = 1, no = 0), age (continuous), emergency surgery (yes = 1, no = 0), length of hospital stay (≤ 7 days = 1, >7 days = 0), physician’s years of practice (continuous, categorized as 1 = < 5 years, 2 = 5–10 years, 3 = ≥ 10 years), known history of HCV (yes = 1, no = 0), HCV-related department (yes = 1, no = 0), and dialysis treatment (yes = 1, no = 0). A backward stepwise selection approach based on the likelihood ratio test was employed, retaining only variables significant at α < 0.05. Model fit was evaluated via the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The results from both models are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05. A post-hoc power analysis confirmed > 80% statistical power for the primary outcome measures.

Age- and Gender-Specific epidemiology and screening burden

Descriptive analysis confirmed a markedly greater percentage of male participants (2.45%) than female participants (1.63%) (χ2 = 96.77, P < 0.001). Furthermore, a pronounced age-dependent gradient was evident (χ2 = 1125.65, P < 0.001), with the highest raw rates concentrated in the 60–69 years (3.58%) and 70–79 years (3.64%) cohorts.

Male sex was confirmed as an independent risk factor (adjusted odds ratio = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.36–1.61; P < 0.001). A highly significant nonlinear dose–response relationship was observed between age and HCV risk (Wald χ2 = 673.56, P < 0.001). Compared with the reference group (< 20 years), the risk increased exponentially across the lifespan. The association became statistically significant from the 30–39 age group onwards (aOR = 3.57, 95% CI: 1.83–6.98; P < 0.001). The adjusted risk peaked dramatically in the 70–79 age cohort (aOR = 41.53, 95% CI: 22.81–75.63), demonstrating that this demographic carries over forty times the odds of testing positive compared to the reference group. All age groups from 30 years onwards showed statistically significant associations (P < 0.001), except for the 20–29-year group (aOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.43–3.16; P = 0.761).

Overall care cascade performance and longitudinal impact assessment

Among 114,968 surgical patients screened for HCV antibodies, 2,334 tested positive, yielding a positive rate of 2.03% (95% CI: 1.95–2.11) (Table 2). This prevalence significantly surpassed China’s estimated national average of ≈ 0.91% [8], confirming that the tertiary center serves a high-yield, high-risk patient population.

The primary failure point in the HCV care cascade was the diagnostic step. Only 33.63% (785/2,334) of anti-HCV positive patients completed the confirmatory HCV RNA testing (Table 2). Among those who were tested, 41.78% (328/785) demonstrated active viremic infection requiring treatment. The HCV RNA positivity rate (the rate of active infection among those tested) remained stable across all study years (range: 38.30%–46.11%; χ2 = 2.966, P = 0.397).

Longitudinal analysis revealed a critical divergence between the effectiveness of upstream and downstream interventions following the launch of the micro-elimination program in 2022 (Fig. 3).

Confirmatory HCV RNA testing rates showed significant interannual variation (χ 2=18.422, P < 0.001). The rate initially rose from 34.06% in 2021 to a temporary peak of 41.42% in 2022, coinciding with the program’s launch and mandatory physician training. However, this compliance proved unsustainable: the rate subsequently declined sharply to 31.64% in 2023 and 29.52% in 2024 (Table 2; Fig. 3). This long-term decline, despite the program’s defined ≤ 72-hour completion target, suggests that non-structural measures (like training) were insufficient to overcome systemic, workflow-based barriers, leading to poor sustained diagnostic adherence.

Comparative analysis of pre-program/pre-disruption (2021–2022) versus post-training discontinuation/post-pandemic (2023–2024) periods showed a significant decline in HCV RNA screening adherence (59.81% vs. 44.30%; χ2 = 11.637, P < 0.001). This highlights that the discontinuation of annual training sessions after 2022, potentially compounded by healthcare system disruptions during pandemic containment, contributed to the deterioration of diagnostic adherence.

In stark contrast, the downstream linkage to specialist care showed marked and sustained success. The hepatology referral compliance rate for confirmed viremic cases exhibited significant variation over the study period (χ2 = 19.477, P < 0.001). This rate steadily increased from 37.50% in 2021 to a substantial 66.67% in 2024 (Table 2; Fig. 3). This continuous upward trajectory validates the high impact and compliance monitoring success of the program’s downstream components, such as the standardized electronic referral and proactive patient recall systems, which successfully reduced loss-to-follow-up after viremia confirmation.

Cross-Specialty heterogeneity in the hepatitis C care cascade: analysis of screening and diagnostic completion

Analysis of Anti-HCV detection results across clinical departments

Significant heterogeneity in anti-HCV positivity rates was observed across 15 clinical departments (χ2 = 1,249.21, P < 0.001). (Table 3). This disparity is visually represented by the green squares (A) in Fig. 4, which plots the anti-HCV positivity rate against departmental specialty.Hepatobiliary Surgery demonstrated the highest anti-HCV positivity rates (7.90%, 386/4,888), exceeding rates in Orthopedics: 2.07% [428/20,686]; Cardiothoracic Surgery: 2.91% [42/1,443]). Elevated positivity was also noted in neurosurgery (3.72%, 120/3,227) and nephrology (2.95%, 41/1,389), the latter potentially reflecting hemodialysis-associated transmission risks [9]. Non-specialist medical departments (including ENT-ophthalmology (2.12%, 330/15,542) and neurology (1.74%, 3/172)) exhibited moderate incidence rates. In contrast, Obstetrics (0.26%, 11/4,159), Gynecology (0.89%, 58/6,503), and Pediatric Surgery (0.04%, 1/2,418) presented the lowest seroprevalence rates, consistent with their typically younger age and lower-risk demographics. The Pediatric Surgery rate (0.04%) differed markedly from the 19.92% prevalence reported in high-risk pediatric cohorts at infectious disease referral centers [10], underscoring institutional differences in patient population risk profiles. This overall heterogeneity was confirmed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.000476): the positive rate in Hepatobiliary Surgery (7.90%) was significantly greater than those in Pediatric Surgery (0.04%), Obstetrics (0.26%), Gynecology (0.89%), and Other Internal Medicine departments (0.59%). Furthermore, positive rates in Gastroenterology, Orthopedics, General Surgery, and Cardiology Departments were all confirmed to be significantly higher than the rate observed in Pediatric Surgery (all P < 0.05), confirming the concentration of HCV risk within specific, targetable hospital specialties.

HCV RNA testing practices across departments

In the hepatitis C screening process, conducting confirmatory HCV RNA screening for anti-HCV positive patients represents the critical first step of the care cascade. Among 2,334 anti-HCV positive patients, only 33.63% (785/2,334) underwent confirmatory HCV RNA testing (Table 3). This profound diagnostic gap is the central failure point of the institutional cascade. Figure 4 visually represents this adherence challenge: the height of the blue bars (B) in the figure demonstrates the vast disparity in the HCV RNA testing rate across departments. Nephrology had the highest testing rate (87.80%, 36/41), potentially reflecting heightened clinical vigilance among hemodialysis patients, followed by general internal medicine (63.8%, 60/94) and cardiothoracic surgery (54.1%, 24/42). The most significant drop-offs were observed in high-volume, non-specialty units, ENT-Ophthalmology recorded an extremely low testing rate of 4.55% (15/330). Orthopedics (18.46%, 79/428) and Neurosurgery (19.17%, 23/120) also demonstrated critical failure to complete the two-step diagnosis. Even Hepatobiliary Surgery, despite having the highest seroprevalence (7.90%), maintained an unsatisfactory RNA testing rate (46.1%, 178/386).

Comparison of HCV diagnosis and referrals across departments

The 785 patients who completed RNA testing yielded an overall HCV RNA positivity rate of 41.78% (328/785), with the highest rates observed in Respiratory Medicine (66.67%, 10/15), neurosurgery (60.87%, 14/23), and otolaryngology-ophthalmology (60.00%, 9/15). Hepatobiliary surgery yielded the greatest number of HCV RNA-positive cases (78/328), followed by general surgery (60/328) and cardiology (41/328). The overall referral compliance for HCV RNA positive patients was 46.04% (151/328). The pink dots (C) in Fig. 4, representing referral rate, showed conflicting and highly variable performance, demonstrating inconsistent adherence to linkage protocols (χ2 = 53.30, P < 0.001). Cardiothoracic Surgery achieved 100.00% referral compliance (9/9), suggesting highly effective linkage protocols within that specific department’s workflow, despite a low initial RNA testing rate. Conversely, Gynecology (0/6), Orthopedics (25.64%, 10/39), and Cardiology (39.02%, 16/41) exhibited notably low referral rates. This severe drop-off in the downstream linkage to care, particularly in large non-specialty departments, represents the second major gap in the cascade, underscoring the necessity for robust electronic health record (EHR) monitoring systems to ensure patient transfer to specialty care.

Independent predictors of HCV RNA testing Completion​

To identify independent factors associated with the successful completion of the confirmatory HCV RNA test (diagnostic adherence), a multivariable binary logistic regression model with backward stepwise selection was employed. The model demonstrated a good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.812) and revealed several significant independent predictors (Fig. 5). The analysis confirmed that short length of hospital stay constitutes the single strongest structural barrier to diagnostic completion, confirming the fragility of the two-step diagnostic model in a high-turnover surgical setting. Patients with a hospital stay of ≤ 7 days were significantly less likely to undergo HCV RNA testing (aOR: 0.48; 95% CI: 0.398–0.586; P < 0.001). This factor, the strongest negative predictor in the model, highlights the inadequacy of traditional workflows that rely on multiple physician actions and patient availability, as patients are often discharged before follow-up testing can be ordered or results returned.

Conversely, receiving dialysis emerged as the strongest facilitating factor (aOR: 8.69), indicating that patients with clinical complexity and comorbidities significantly increased the likelihood of completing the diagnostic pathway. Other factors that positively influenced diagnostic completion included referral to an HCV-related department (aOR: 2.18) and a history of blood transfusion (aOR: 1.82). Furthermore, longer physician practice duration acted as a protective factor against failure to test; patients seen by physicians with shorter experience (< 5 years and 2 − 5 years) showed increased odds of non-completion compared to those with ≥ 10 years of practice. The absence of significant effects for age and sex confirmed that these demographic factors did not independently influence testing rates when accounting for clinical and provider-related variables.

Sensitivity analysis using the − 2 log-likelihood change method indicated that ‘hospital stay ≤ 7 days’ was the most influential predictor in the model (change value = 47.016,P < 0.001), suggesting that shorter hospital stays are the primary barrier to completing RNA testing. The statistical robustness of the model was confirmed by a post hoc power analysis. For all significant predictors with moderate to large effects (e.g., dialysis treatment [aOR: 8.69, power > 0.999], hospital stay ≤ 7 days [aOR: 0.48, power > 0.92]), the study demonstrated sufficient power (> 0.80). In contrast, the non-significant intergroup comparison of physician practice years (aOR: 0.94) had low power (< 0.20), likely reflecting a trivial association or a true null effect.

Discussion​​

This study, through an analysis of pre-operative HCV screening data from a large hospital in Northern China, reveals both the severe challenges and potential opportunities in the path towards achieving the WHO’s 2030 hepatitis C elimination goals [11]. Our central finding identifies the fundamental obstacle of an “incompatibility between the two-step diagnostic process and the clinical workflow”, leading to critically low rates of subsequent HCV RNA testing. This core problem—a pervasive “diagnostic gap”—aligns with the concerns outlined in the Expert Consensus on Quality Improvement of Hepatitis C Diagnosis and Treatment [in Chinese] [6]and now represents the primary bottleneck in elimination efforts.

High Seroprevalence and low testing rate: A paradox requiring immediate resolution

The anti-HCV positive rate of 2.03% in our pre-operative cohort is significantly higher than the national average and nearly double the 1.08% rate reported in a comparable tertiary hospital study in South China [12]. This underscores the geographical heterogeneity of HCV infection and its concentration within specific clinical populations. The age-related risk gradient, peaking in the 70–79 years age cohort (3.64%), reflects the generational impact of historical exposures, a trend consistent with patterns observed in other developing countries [13]. As a large-scale study focused exclusively on the preoperative surgical population, our high 2.03% prevalence rate confirms that routine screening is highly cost-effective, supporting the findings of Masood et al. [14] and falling far above the suggested 0.59% screening threshold [15]. This finding directly contrasts with previous research that questioned the benefit of routine preoperative screening for viral infectivity prior to elective orthopaedic operations [16], thus highlighting the critical role of local prevalence and population risk in determining screening feasibility.

However, this high yield is starkly contrasted by a critical implementation gap. Only 33.63% of anti-HCV positive individuals received subsequent RNA testing. This diagnostic gap is considerably wider than the ~ 50% rate Chen et al. [17]reported in Southern China and the ~ 86% rate in specialized infectious disease hospitals in Beijing reported by Han Ying et al. [18]. Our findings place the local diagnostic gap within a global and national context. The low HCV RNA testing rate of 33.63% in our center echoes the 42.7% rate reported by Miller et al. in the US [19], confirming this as a universal challenge, not merely a local resource issue. The problem’s nature, however, becomes clearer through comparison with a specialized setting: our rate is dwarfed by the ~ 86% achieved by Han Ying et al. in a Beijing infectious disease hospital [18], highlighting how institutional expertise influences outcomes. Ultimately, the stark contrast between departments—such as the 87.80% rate in Nephrology versus lower-performing units—shifts the blame from patient adherence to a more fundamental cause: system and workflow incompatibility.

Systemic analysis of core barriers and their generalizability

Multivariate regression analysis identified a short hospital stay (≤ 7 days) as the strongest negative predictor for HCV RNA test completion. This finding, aligning with White et al. ([20], pinpoints the core issue: the traditional two-step diagnostic model is inherently incompatible with the fast-paced rhythm of high-turnover surgical workflows. The stability of the HCV RNA positivity rate (38.30–46.11%) across the study period suggests that this variation was driven primarily by systemic compliance issues, not changes in the underlying infected population. Longitudinal analysis confirmed the failure of non-structural interventions, with the testing rate declining sharply from a peak of 41.42% in 2022 to 29.52% in 2024, a deterioration likely exacerbated by COVID-19 pandemic-related healthcare disruptions [21, 22].

The incompatibility we identified is a model of systemic failure that applies well beyond our pre-operative cohort. This model fails in General Internal Medicine and Emergency Departments, where early discharge and competing priorities cause confirmatory testing to be overlooked. The problem amplifies in outpatient clinics, where the requirement for a second visit makes the diagnostic pathway particularly fragile. Consequently, any clinical context defined by brief patient encounters—whether single or short-term—shares this precarious relationship with the two-step HCV diagnosis.

Our findings echo those of Sahakyan et al. [23] by not only confirming the importance of hospital stay duration but also pinpointing “≤7 days” as a precise risk threshold for intervention. Similarly, while Bartlett et al. [24] noted clinical inertia despite DAA access, our study reveals that this bottleneck has shifted downstream, now residing at the diagnostic stage rather than treatment initiation.

Success stories and the value of structured intervention

Yet a different story unfolded downstream. While the diagnostic stage faltered, linkage to hepatology care strengthened markedly, with referral rates rising from 37.50% to 66.67% between 2021 and 2024. This success highlights the efficacy of a structured, technology-driven system. The lesson is clear: once a process like the electronic referral is locked in, adherence becomes the default. This aligns with Abbott et al. [25] and confirms that systematic intervention can effectively secure care continuity.A broader-scale exemplar comes from Egypt, by 2022, the chronic infection rate of HCV had decreased by 93% compared with that in 2015 [26]. Pivotal to this success was a top-level design that systematically eliminated barriers between diagnosis and treatment, offering a nationwide solution to the problem of “diagnostic loss” identified in our study.

Moving towards elimination: Specific, actionable systemic intervention recommendations

Based on the above analysis, we propose the following specific, measurable, and time-bound action plans:

1.Mandatory Implementation of Reflex Testing: Hospital administration should mandate the laboratory department to automatically perform HCV RNA reflex testing on all anti-HCV positive plasma samples within the next 6–12 months. This merges the “two-step diagnosis” into an “automatic one-step process,” fundamentally preventing patient loss due to requiring a second test order.2. Deploy a Compulsory EMR Alert System: The Information Technology department, in collaboration with clinical departments, should deploy a compulsory hard-stop pop-up alert within the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system within three months. For inpatients, this alert will trigger when a physician attempts to discharge a patient who is “anti-HCV positive but has not completed RNA testing,” requiring the physician to either place the RNA test order or provide a valid clinical rationale before proceeding. For outpatient or emergency department patients, upon a positive anti-HCV result, the laboratory will automatically perform the HCV RNA test. Simultaneously, an electronic alert will be sent to a designated infection control physician, who will be responsible for tracking the HCV RNA status of all such outpatients and emergency department patients. Upon confirmation of a positive RNA result, this physician must enroll the patient in an HCV patient monitoring registry, promptly contact the patient or their current attending physician, and advise them to seek consultation or referral to the Hepatology or Infectious Diseases department.3. Set Performance-Linked Quality Metrics: The quality control and performance management departments should immediately incorporate the “HCV RNA test completion rate for anti-HCV positive patients” into the quarterly performance evaluations of relevant departments. Set clear targets for high-turnover departments, e.g., increasing the completion rate from baseline to 60% within 1 year.4. Establish a Closed-Loop Referral Pathway: Develop a one-click hepatology consultation/referral module within the EMR.
