Asymptotic behavior of Carleman weight functions and application to controllability
Ariel A. Pérez

Abstract
. In the development of controllability and inverse problem results for semi-discrete systems, by using Carleman estimates, it is required to estimate of the discrete operators applied to Carleman weight functions. This work aims to establish the asymptotic behavior of Carleman weight functions under these discrete operators. We provide a characterization of the error term in arbitrary order and dimension, extending previously known results. This generalization is of independent interest due to its applications in deriving Carleman estimates for semi-discrete stochastic operators. The aforementioned estimates hold for Carleman weight functions used for parabolic, hyperbolic, and elliptic operators, which are applied to obtain control and inverse problems results for those operators. We apply these results to obtain ϕ \phi -controllability result for a fully discrete parabolic operator, which is based in a Carleman estimate for a fully-discrete parabolic operator.

Introduction

Let
d\geq 1
and
T,L_{1}
,
\ldots
,
L_{d}
be positive real numbers; and
\Omega:=\Pi_{i=1}^{d}(0,L_{i})
, with
\omega\Subset\Omega
. Consider the following parabolic problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}y-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}(\gamma_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}y)=\mathbbm{1}_{\omega}v,&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(x,t)=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(x,0)=g(x),&x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

here
\gamma_{i}(x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
, and
\mathbbm{1}_{\omega}
stands for the indicator function of the set
\omega
. The goal is to find
v\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
such that
y(T)=0
. In this case, it is said that the system (
1.1
) is null-controllable. The aforementioned controllability problem was solved independently by Lebeau and Robbiano
Lebeau and Robbiano (1995)
and Fursikov and Imanuvilov
Fursikov and Imanuvilov (1996)
. The usual methodology is that the null-controllability result for the system (
1.1
) is equivalent, by a duality argument, to the following observability inequality

(1.2)

|q(0)|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\text{obs}}^{2}\left\|q\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\omega\times(0,T))},

where
q
is a solution to the adjoint system

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}q+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}(\gamma_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}q)=0,&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
q(x,t)=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
q(x,T)=q_{T}(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

A popular strategy to prove the inequality (
1.2
) is based on a Carleman estimate for the system (
1.3
). The first application of these estimates was to prove unique continuation properties (see
Carleman (1939)
). Later, for control and inverse problems Carleman estimates have become a fundamental tool. A unified approach to Carleman estimates for second-order partial differential equations is the reference
Fu et al. (2019)
and references therein.

Unfortunately, it is not feasible to achieve the null-controllability for the discretization of system (
1.1
). The next example shows that the null controllability generally does not hold for fully-discrete systems by building a non-observable function for a discretization of the adjoint system (
1.3
). To this end, let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
T>0
, and define
h=1/(M+1)
and
\Delta t=T/N
. Then, the uniform meshes in space and time are given by follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\{x_{i}:=ih\,;\,i\in\{0,\ldots,M+1\}\},\quad\mathcal{N}=\{t_{j}:=j\Delta t\,;\,j\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\}.

Thus, we consider a regular partition of the square
(0,1)^{2}
defined by
(0,1)^{2}\cap\mathcal{M}^{2}
where
\mathcal{M}:=(0,1)\cap\mathcal{K}
. Using the finite-difference scheme, the adjoint discrete system (
1.3
) of the heat equation, for
d=2
,
\gamma_{i}(x)=1
and
L_{i}=1
, can be written as

(1.4)

\begin{split}D_{t}q_{i,j}+\Delta_{h}\mathtt{t}^{+}q_{i,j}&=0\quad\forall(x_{i},x_{j},t_{j})\in\mathcal{M}^{2}\times\mathcal{N},\\
q_{i,0}=q_{i,M+1}&=0\ \ \ \forall(x_{i},t_{j})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N},\\
q_{0,j}=q_{M+1,j}&=0\ \ \ \forall(x_{j},t_{j})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N},\\
\end{split}

where the five-point approximation of the Laplacian operator in uniform meshes is utilized, as given by

(1.5)

\Delta_{h}q_{i,j}:=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(q_{i+1,j}+q_{i,j+1}+q_{i-1,j}+q_{i,j-1}-4q_{i,j}\right),

and
D_{t}q:=\dfrac{\mathtt{t}^{-}q-\mathtt{t}^{-}q}{\Delta t}
in
\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathtt{t}^{\pm}q(t):=q(t\pm\Delta t/2)
and
q_{ij}=q(x_{i},x_{j})
. Combining the ideas of
Zuazua (2005)
and
Ervedoza (2019)
, we can build a non-observable solution of the system (
1.4
).
Indeed, notice that for

(1.6)

\tilde{q}_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}\ \ 1,&i=j\text{ even,}\\
\ \ 0,&i\neq j,\\
-1,&i=j\text{ odd},\\
\end{cases}

it follows that

(1.7)

\Delta_{h}\tilde{q}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-\frac{4}{h^{2}},&i=j\text{ even,}\\
\ \ \ 0,&i\neq j,\\
\ \ \frac{4}{h^{2}},&i=j\text{ odd,}.\\
\end{cases}=-\frac{4}{h^{2}}\tilde{q}_{i,j}.

Then
\tilde{q}_{i,j}
is an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{h}
, with eigenvalue
-4/h^{2}
. In turn, for
\alpha\Delta t<1
and with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}:=\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha\Delta t}\right)^{1/\alpha\Delta t}
, it obtained that
D_{t}\mathfrak{a}^{\alpha t}=\alpha\mathtt{t}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{\alpha t}
. Thus,
considering
\alpha=4/h^{2}
such that
\alpha\Delta t<1
it follows that
\overline{q}_{i,j}:=\mathfrak{a}^{-4(T-t)/h^{2}}\tilde{q}_{i,j}
solves (
1.4
) since
D_{t}\overline{q}=\frac{4}{h^{2}}\mathtt{t}^{+}\overline{q}_{i,j}
. Therefore, if any diagonal nodes do not belong to
\omega
yields
\left\|\overline{q}_{i,j}\right\|_{L^{2}_{h}(\omega\times(0,T))}=0
although the norm of
\overline{q}(x,0)
has size
\mathfrak{a}^{-C/h^{2}}
. This implies that a discrete observability inequality like the continuous setting (
1.2
), cannot be achieved for a fully-discrete system (
1.4
).

Due to the aforementioned counter-example, achieving null controllability in the fully or semi-discrete setting is not possible in general, even though an alternative objective is to build bounded controls such that at a given time the norm of the solution of the system decreases exponentially, as the discrete parameters
\Delta t
and/or
h
tend towards zero. That is, in the case of space semi-discrete system, to build semi-discrete control
u
such that at time
T
the solution of the system
y
(with an appropriate norm on the space of solutions
X
) verifies

(1.8)

\left\|y(x,T)\right\|_{X}\approx C\sqrt{\phi(h)},

with
C>0
,
\left\|u\right\|_{Y}\leq C
uniformly respect to
h
(with the appropriate norm in the space of controls

), and
where

is any given function of the discretization parameter such that

(1.9)

\liminf_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\phi(h)}{e^{-c/h}}>0.

This approach is known as
\phi
-null controllability for the space semi-discrete system, and also can be formulated for time-discrete systems and/or fully-discrete systems.

In several setting, the

-null controllability has been studied and the analysis is based on the penalization Hilbert uniqueness method, we refer to the work

and recently for parabolic system with Kirchhoff boundary condition in

2.
Asymptotic behavior of smooth functions

In this section, we aim to establish the asymptotic behavior of a smooth function which will be applied in particular in the next section to Carleman weight functions. To this end, we consider functions continuously defined in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
. We define the average and difference operators, in the direction
e_{i}
, with
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
being
the usual basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as

(2.1)

\begin{split}A_{i}u(x)&:=\frac{1}{2}\left(u(x+\frac{h}{2}e_{i})+u(x-\frac{h}{2}e_{i})\right),\\
D_{i}u(x)&:=\frac{1}{h}\left(u(x+\frac{h}{2}e_{i})-u(x-\frac{h}{2}e_{i})\right),\end{split}

For these operators, we have the following product rule.

Lemma 2.1

(
(
Boyer et al., 2010b
, Lemma 2.1 and 2.2)
)
.

Let the functions
u
and
v
be continuously defined in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we have the difference and average operators satisfy

(2.2)

\displaystyle D_{i}(uv)=

\displaystyle D_{i}u\,A_{i}v+D_{i}v\,A_{i}u,

(2.3)

\displaystyle A_{i}(uv)=

\displaystyle A_{i}u\,A_{i}v+\frac{h^{2}}{4}D_{i}u\,D_{i}v.

It is possible to generalize the above product rule for the difference and average operators.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
u
and
v
be two smooth functions and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, it holds a Leibniz rule for the difference operator given by

(2.4)

D_{i}^{n}(u\,v)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}D_{i}^{n-k}A_{i}^{k}u\,A^{n-k}_{i}D^{k}_{i}v.

Moreover, for the average operator, we have

(2.5)

A_{i}^{2m}(uv)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{h^{2j}}{2^{2j}}D_{i}^{2j}(uv),\quad\forall m\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

The proof follows by induction, with the base case given by Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Note that (
2.5
) is stated for an even power, although by using (
2.3
) the odd case can also be handled.

Our first result states the asymptotic behavior of a smooth function when several iterations of the discrete operator are applied, which is a higher-dimension extension of
(
Lecaros et al., 2021
, Proposition 4.1)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f
be a smooth function defined in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
. For

i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
we have

(2.6)

\begin{split}A^{n}_{i}f=&f+R_{A_{i}^{n}}(f),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\\
D^{n}_{i}f=&\partial_{i}^{n}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{split}

where
\displaystyle R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f):=h^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{n-2k}{2}\right)^{n+2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-\sigma)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\partial_{i}^{n+2}f(\cdot+\tilde{e}_{i}\sigma)d\sigma
and

\displaystyle R_{A_{i}^{n}}(f):=\frac{h^{2}}{2^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(n-2k)^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)\partial_{i}^{2}f(\cdot+\tilde{e}_{i}\sigma)d\sigma
,
with
\tilde{e}_{i}:=\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}e_{i}
and
\sigma\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

The proof follows a similar strategy developed in the proof of
(
Lecaros et al., 2021
, Proposition 4.1)
with the Taylor expansion

(2.7)

f(x+\eta)=\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\frac{1}{j!}f^{(j)}(x;\eta,\ldots,\eta)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-\sigma)^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}f^{(l)}(x+\sigma\eta;\eta,\ldots,\eta)d\sigma.

Indeed, denoting
\displaystyle\tau_{(n-2k)i}f(x):=f\left(x+\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}e_{i}\right)
, we use (
2.7
) with
l=2
,
\displaystyle\eta=\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}e_{i}
to obtain

\tau_{(n-2k)i}f=f+\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}\partial_{i}f+\frac{(n-2k)^{2}h^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)\partial_{i}^{2}f(\cdot+\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}e_{i}\sigma)d\sigma.

Then, using the above identity for the average operator, we have

\displaystyle A_{i}^{n}f=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\tau_{i}+\tau_{-i}\right)^{n}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\tau_{(n-2k)i}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(f+\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}\partial_{i}f\right)+R_{A_{i}^{n}}(f)

Thus, using that
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}=2^{n}
and
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}k=n2^{n-1}
we conclude that

A_{i}^{n}f=f+R_{A_{i}^{n}}(f),

which is the first identity from (
2.6
).

In turn, for the second identity from (
2.6
), we proceed similarly. Using (
2.7
), with
l=n+2
and
\displaystyle\eta=\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}e_{i}
, it follows that

\displaystyle D^{n}_{i}f=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{n}}\left(\tau_{i}-\tau_{-i}\right)^{n}f

(2.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\tau_{(n-2k)i}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}\right)^{j}\partial_{i}^{j}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f).

Now, using from
(
Egorychev, 1984
, Equation 2.2)
the Tepper identity

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}(x-k)^{r}=\begin{cases}0,&\ 0\leq r<n,\\
n!,&\ r=n,\end{cases}

we notice that the non-zero terms, in the latter expression of (
2.8
), are only for
j=n
and
j=n+1
. Moreover, we have

(2.9)

\mathcal{S}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-k\right)^{n+1}=0.

Indeed, notice that applying the substitution
k\mapsto n-k
, we obtain

\mathcal{S}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{n-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-(n-k)\right)^{n+1}=(-1)^{n}(-1)^{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-k\right)^{n+1}=-\mathcal{S}

Hence, (
2.9
) holds.
Thus, by using (
2.9
) it follows that (
2.8
) becomes

(2.10)

\begin{split}D_{i}^{n}f=&\frac{1}{h^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{(n-2k)h}{2}\right)^{j}\partial_{i}^{j}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f)\\
=&\frac{1}{h^{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\frac{1}{j!}h^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-k\right)^{j}\partial_{i}^{j}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f)\\
=&\frac{1}{h^{n}n!}h^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-k\right)^{n}\partial_{i}^{n}f\\
&+\frac{1}{h^{n}}\frac{1}{(n+1)!}h^{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{n}{2}-k\right)^{n+1}\partial_{i}^{n+1}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f)\\
=&\partial_{i}^{n}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f),\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

A combination of the estimates from (
2.6
) yields estimates when the composition of the discrete operators is made in different directions.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
f
be a smooth function defined in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
. For

i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(2.11)

\begin{split}A_{j}^{m}A_{i}^{n}f=&f+R_{A_{j}^{m}}(f)+R_{A_{i}^{n}}(f)+R_{A_{j}^{m}A_{i}^{n}}(f),\\
D_{j}^{m}D_{i}^{n}f=&\partial_{j}^{m}\partial_{i}^{n}f+R_{D_{j}^{m}}(f)+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f)+R_{D_{j}^{m}D_{i}^{n}}(f),\end{split}

and

(2.12)

D_{i}^{n}A_{j}^{m}f=\partial_{i}^{n}f+R_{D_{i}^{n}}(f)+R_{A_{j}^{m}}(\partial^{n}_{i}f)+R_{A_{j}^{m}D_{i}^{n}}(f);

where

R_{A^{m}_{j}A^{n}_{i}}(f):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{m}a_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)(1-\sigma^{\prime})\partial_{j}^{2}\partial_{i}^{2}f\left(\cdot+\tilde{e}_{ij}\right)d\sigma^{\prime}d\sigma,

R_{D^{m}_{j}D^{n}_{i}}(f):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{m}b_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)^{n+1}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{m+1}\partial_{j}^{m+2}\partial_{i}^{n+2}f\left(\cdot+\tilde{e}_{ij}\right)d\sigma^{\prime}d\sigma,

and

R_{D^{n}_{i}A^{m}_{j}}(f):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{m}c_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)^{2}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{n+2}\partial_{i}^{n+2}\partial_{j}^{2}f\left(\cdot+\tilde{e}_{ij}\right)d\sigma^{\prime}d\sigma;

with
\tilde{e}_{ij}=\tilde{e}_{ij}(n,k,h,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}):=(n-2k)\frac{h}{2}e_{i}\sigma+(m-2k^{\prime})\frac{h}{2}e_{j}\sigma^{\prime}
for
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in[0,1]
and

a_{k,k^{\prime}}:=\frac{h^{4}}{2^{m+n}}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(n-2k)^{2}}{4}\frac{(m-2k^{\prime})^{2}}{4},

b_{k,k^{\prime}}:=\frac{(-1)^{k+k^{\prime}}h^{4}}{(n+1)!(m+1)!}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{n-2k}{2}\right)^{n+2}\left(\frac{m-2k^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{m+2},

and

c_{k,k^{\prime}}:=\frac{(-1)^{k}h^{4}}{(n+1)!2^{m}}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{n-2k}{2}\right)^{n+2}\left(\frac{m-2k^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}.

3.
Asymptotic behavior of Carleman weight functions

This section is devoted to extending the results on discrete operations performed on the Carleman weight functions, presented in
Lecaros et al. (2021)
, to arbitrary dimensions. We set the weight function of the form
r=e^{s\varphi}
and
\rho=r^{-1}
, with
s\geq 1
a parameter; where
\varphi(x)=e^{\lambda\psi(x)}
and the function
\psi
fulfills suitable assumptions concerning the specific issue; that is, controllability or inverse problems. Our main objective is to obtain an asymptotic behavior describing the dependence on
s,h
in the following basic estimates in domain
\Omega
when the discrete operators are applied.
Let us recall that a multi-index has the form
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Then, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write the order of the multi-index as
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
, the compact notation of a higher-order operator as
\partial^{\alpha}:=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}
and we also adopt the notation

\xi^{\alpha}:=\xi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\xi_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.

Now, we consider two fundamental estimates for our weight function.

Lemma 3.1

(
(
Boyer et al., 2010b
, Lemma 3.7)
)
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
multi-indices. We have

\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}(r\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{|\beta|}(-s\varphi)^{|\alpha|}\lambda^{|\alpha+\beta|}(\nabla\psi)^{\alpha+\beta}+|\alpha||\beta|(s\varphi)^{|\alpha|}\lambda^{|\alpha+\beta|-1}\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle+s^{|\alpha|-1}|\alpha|(|\alpha|-1)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|}).

Let
\sigma\in[-1,1]
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. We have

(3.1)

\begin{split}\partial^{\beta}\left(r(x)(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)(x+\sigma he_{i})\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|}(1+(sh)^{|\beta|}))e^{\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(sh)}.\end{split}

Provided
sh\leq 1
we have
\partial^{\beta}\left(r(x)(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)(x+\sigma he_{i})\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|})
. The same expressions hold with
r
and
\rho
interchanged and with
s
changed to
-s
.

Another useful estimate is the following.

Corollary 3.2

(
(
Boyer et al., 2010b
, Corollary 3.8)
)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
and
\delta
multi-indices. We have

(3.2)

\begin{split}\partial^{\delta}\left(r^{2}\,\partial^{\alpha}\rho\,\partial^{\beta}\rho\right)=&|\alpha+\beta|^{|\delta|}(-s\varphi)^{|\alpha+\beta|}\lambda^{|\alpha+\beta+\delta|}(\nabla\psi)^{\alpha+\beta+\delta}\\
&+|\delta||\alpha+\beta|(s\varphi)^{|\alpha+\beta|}\lambda^{|\alpha+\beta+\delta|-1}\mathcal{O}(1)\\
&+s^{|\alpha+\beta|-1}\left(|\alpha|(|\alpha|-1)+|\beta|(|\beta|-1)\right)\mathcal{O}(1)\\
&=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha+\beta|}).\end{split}

The proofs of the above results can be found in
Boyer et al. (2010b)
. We aim to obtain a general estimate for the discrete operators applied to the Carleman weight function.
Mimicking the notation for multi-indices in the continuous setting, we define for the two-dimensional index
k=(k_{i},k_{j})
, with
k_{i},k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, the discrete operator
\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}:=D_{i}^{k_{i}}D_{j}^{k_{j}}
. Analogously, for the average operator, we define
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}:=A_{i}^{l_{i}}A_{j}^{l_{j}}
with
l:=(l_{i},l_{j})
being a two-dimensional multi-index. Considering the previous notation and Proposition
2.4
, we can extend
(
Lecaros et al., 2021
, Proposition 4.5)
to arbitrary dimensions.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a multi-index and
k,l
be two-dimensional multi-indices. Let
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
; provided that
sh\leq 1
, we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle r\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho

\displaystyle=r\partial^{k+\alpha}\rho+s^{|\alpha|+k_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})

\displaystyle+s^{|\alpha|+k_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})+s^{|\alpha+k|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Proof.

Applying Corollary
2.5
to
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho
, we write

(3.4)

\displaystyle r\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho=

\displaystyle r\partial^{k}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)+rR_{D^{k_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)+rR_{D^{k_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)

\displaystyle+rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho).

Then, using the first identity of Corollary
2.5
, it follows for the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.4
) that

\displaystyle r\partial^{k}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle r\partial^{k+\alpha}\rho+rR_{A_{j}^{l_{j}}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)+rR_{A_{i}^{l_{i}}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)+rR_{\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)

Now, our next task is to estimate the last three terms on the right-hand side above. Using Corollary
2.5
and Lemma
3.1
we obtain the estimates

\displaystyle rR_{A_{j}^{l_{j}}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{|\alpha|+|k|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle rR_{A_{i}^{l_{i}}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{|\alpha|+|k|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle rR_{\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}}(\partial^{\alpha+k}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{|\alpha|+|k|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{4}),

which yields

(3.5)

\displaystyle r\partial^{k}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=r\partial^{k+\alpha}\rho+s^{|\alpha|+|k|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Similarly, we have the estimates

(3.6)

\displaystyle rR_{D^{k_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{k_{j}+|\alpha|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle rR_{D^{k_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{k_{i}+|\alpha|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle s^{|k|+|\alpha|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{4}).

Thus, combining the estimates (
3.6
) and (
3.5
) in (
3.4
) we obtain (
3.3
).
∎

Another useful estimate is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\delta
be multi-indices and let
k,l
be two-dimensional multi-indices. Provided
sh\leq 1
, we have

(3.7)

\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\left(\partial^{\beta}\left(r\partial^{\alpha}\rho\right)\right)=\partial_{i}^{k_{i}}\partial_{j}^{k_{j}}\partial^{\beta}\left(r\partial^{\alpha}\rho\right)+h^{2}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|}).

Moreover, for
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
we have

(3.8)

\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\delta}\left(r^{2}\left(\partial^{\alpha}\rho\right)\partial^{\beta}\rho\right)=\partial^{k+\delta}\left(r^{2}(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)\partial^{\beta}\rho\right)+h^{2}\mathcal{O}(s^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}),

and for
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in[0,1]
we get

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{k}_{h}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\beta}\left(r(x)\partial^{\alpha}\rho(x+\sigma he_{i}\right))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|}),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\partial^{\delta}\left(r^{2}(x)\left(\partial^{\alpha}\rho(x+\sigma he_{i})\right)\partial^{\beta}\rho(x+\sigma^{\prime}he_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}).

Proof.

The method of proof is analogous to that used in demonstrating Proposition
2.4
. Specifically, Corollary
2.5
is applied to
\partial^{\beta}(r\partial^{\alpha}\rho)
, followed by the application of Lemma
3.1
to establish result (
3.7
). In a similar fashion, equations (
3.8
) and (
3.10
) are derived using the same approach, with the conclusion utilizing Corollary
3.2
.
∎

Now, we state the following Theorem that provides us an estimate for several computations of the discrete average and derivative operators applied to our Carleman weight function.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\alpha
be a multi-index and
l,k,n,m,p
and
q
be two-dimensional multi-indices. For
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and for
sh\leq 1
, we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho)=

\displaystyle\partial^{k+\alpha}(r\partial^{n}\rho)+s^{|\alpha|+n_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})

\displaystyle+s^{|\alpha|+n_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})+s^{|n|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})

and

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\beta}(r^{2}\mathbf{A}^{p}_{h}\mathbf{D}^{q}_{h}(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho)=

\displaystyle\partial^{k+\beta}(r^{2}(\partial^{q+\alpha}\rho)\partial^{n}\rho)

\displaystyle+s^{|\alpha+n+q|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Proof.

From Corollary
2.5
we write

r\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho=r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{D^{n_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{D^{n_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho).

Then, applying the operator
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(rR_{D^{n_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))

(3.13)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(rR_{D^{n_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)).

Let us focus on the term
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))
. We notice that, for this term, using the first equation from Corollary
2.5
applied to
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho
gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}\rho)+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}R_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}}(\rho))

\displaystyle+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}R_{A_{j}^{m_{j}}}(\rho))+\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}R_{\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}}(\rho)).

Thus, applying Proposition
3.3
to the first term from the right-hand side above, and then (
3.9
) from Lemma
3.4
we can estimate

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{h}^{l}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho))=

\displaystyle\partial^{k+\alpha}(r\partial^{n}\rho)+s^{|\alpha|+n_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})+s^{|\alpha|+n_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})

\displaystyle+s^{|n|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Similarly, we can follow the same steps above to estimate the last three terms of (
3.13
), which are of order
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})
and
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{4})
, and conclude the proof of (
3.11
). To prove (
3.12
), we note that thanks to Corollary
2.5
we write

r\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho=r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{D^{n_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{D^{n_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)+rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\rho)

and

r\mathbf{A}_{h}^{p}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{q}(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=r\partial^{q}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{p}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)+rR_{D^{q_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{p}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)+rR_{D^{q_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{p}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)+rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{q}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{p}\partial^{\alpha}\rho).

Thus, multiplying the above expressions by
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\rho
and arguing as in the proof of (
3.11
) the estimate of (
3.12
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We remark that the estimates in this section remain valid if we interchange
\rho
and
r
. This is because Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
still hold with this change, and we can replace
s
by
-s
in these statements.

It is worth noting that the previous results can be extended to the case where the weight function is time-dependent. For instance, if we consider a weight function of the form
r(x,t)=e^{\tau\theta(t)\varphi(x)}
, the condition
sh\leq 1
needs to be replaced by
\tau h(\max_{[0,T]}\theta(t))\leq 1
, which implies
\tau\theta(t)h\leq 1
. This is typically the case for parabolic operators where the Carleman weight function has the form
e^{\tau\theta(t)\varphi(x)}
and the function
\theta(t)
is given by

(3.14)

\theta(t)=\frac{1}{(t+\delta T)(T+\delta T-t)},\quad t\in[0,T],

for some
0<\delta\leq 1/2
. The parameter
\delta
is chosen to avoid singularities at time
t=0
and
t=T
since
\theta
must be bounded on the interval
[0,T]
to obtain an estimate similar to Theorem
3.5
for the time-dependent case. Moreover, we notice that

(3.15)

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\theta(t)=\theta(0)=\theta(T)=\frac{1}{T^{2}\delta(1+\delta)}\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}\delta},

Thus, the condition
sh\leq 1
for
\theta
as in (
3.14
) is replaced by
sh(T^{2}\delta)^{-1}\leq 1
. This situation is documented in the works
Boyer and Le Rousseau (2014)
;
González Casanova and Hernández-Santamaría (2021)
;
Lecaros et al. (2023a)
. Therefore, Theorem
3.5
is a generalization of the estimates presented in those works.

For the aforementioned Carleman weight function, we obtain, in the case of time derivative, the following asymptotic behavior, which stands for a generalization into an arbitrary dimension of Theorem 2.9 from
Cerpa et al. (2022)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\alpha
be a multi-index and let
m,n,j,k
be two-dimensional multi-indices. Then, provided
\tau h(\delta T^{2})^{-1}\leq 1
, we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(r\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho\right)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(r\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho\right)+Ts^{|\alpha|+n_{i}}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})

\displaystyle+Ts^{|\alpha|+n_{j}}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})+Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

and

(3.17)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}_{h}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{j}\left(r\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho\right)=&T\theta s^{|\alpha|+|n|}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1).\end{split}

Proof.

We begin as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, by using Corollary
2.5
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))+\partial_{t}(rR_{D^{n_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}(rR_{D^{n_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))+\partial_{t}(rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho)).

Note that applying Corollary
2.5
to
\partial_{t}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))
we write

(3.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho)+\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{j}^{m_{j}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))+\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}A_{j}^{m_{j}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho)).

Thus, our next task is to estimate the last three terms from the right-hand side above. First, focusing on
\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))
, thanks to the first estimate from
(
Boyer and Le Rousseau, 2014
, Proposition 2.14)
it follows that

(3.19)

\partial_{t}(r(x,t)(\partial_{i}^{2}\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho)(x+(m_{i}-2k)e_{i}h\sigma/2,t))=Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|+2}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1).

Then, with the above estimate and the asymptotic expansion of
R_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}}
given by Proposition
2.4
we obtain
\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{i}^{m_{i}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))=Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})
. A similar procedure for the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
3.18
) allows to estimate

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{j}^{m_{j}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(rR_{A_{i}^{n_{i}}A_{j}^{m_{j}}}(\partial^{n+\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{4}).

Therefore, the previous last three estimates yield that (
3.18
) can be estimated as

\partial_{t}(r\partial^{n}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))=Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(rR_{D^{n_{j}}_{j}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle Ts^{|\alpha|+n_{j}}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(rR_{D^{n_{i}}_{i}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle Ts^{|\alpha|+n_{i}}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(rR_{\mathbf{D}_{h}^{n}}(\mathbf{A}_{h}^{m}\partial^{\alpha}\rho))=

\displaystyle Ts^{|\alpha|+|n|}\theta(t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2}).

Combining these last four estimates, we establish (
3.16
).

The estimate (
3.17
) can be obtained following a similar strategy.
∎

.

Theorem
3.7
is a generalization of the second and third estimates from
(

.

Remark 3.9
.

In the study of inverse problem for hyperbolic operators
Baudouin et al. (2015)
;
Baudouin and Ervedoza (2013)
and
Baudouin et al. (2021)

is considered the following Carleman weight function

(3.20)

\psi(t,x)=|x-x^{\ast}|^{2}-\beta t^{2}+c_{0},\quad\varphi(t,x)=e^{\mu\psi(t,x)},

where
x^{\ast}\notin[0,1]^{2}
, and
c_{0}>0
is such that
\psi\geq 1
in
[-T,T]\times[0,1]^{2}
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\mu\geq 1
is a suitable parameter. Thus, in this setting, we can write

e^{\lambda\varphi}=e^{\lambda\tilde{\theta}(t)\phi(x)}

with
\tilde{\theta}(t):=e^{-\mu\beta t^{2}}
and
\phi(x):=e^{\mu(|x-x^{\ast}|^{2}+c_{0})}
. Hence, for these weight functions, we can apply Theorem
3.5
to determine its asymptotic behavior since the time variable function is bounded in the interval
[0,T]
. However, this is not the case for Theorem
3.7
since the time-dependent factor does not take the same form as
\theta(t)
in the parabolic case. See also
Zhang and Zhao (2022)
and
Zhao and Zhang (2023)
, where a similar weight function is considered to study the coefficient inverse problems for damped wave and Schrödinger equations, respectively.

Remark 3.10
.

In the same direction as the aforementioned remark, in
Wang and Zhao (2024)
, in the study of an inverse problem for a semi-discrete stochastic parabolic operator is considered, a Carleman weight function like (
3.20
). For this reason, the estimates established above provide a useful tool to extend the results from
Wang and Zhao (2024)
to arbitrary dimensions.

Similarly to the previous results, it is possible to obtain an asymptotic expansion considering the time and spatial discrete operators. The main task is to generalize to arbitrary dimensions the asymptotic behavior obtained in
Lecaros et al. (2023a)
.Now, we will introduce some notations to define the discretization of the time variable. To do this, we recall
\Delta t=T/N
, with
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=

\displaystyle\{j\Delta t\,;\,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\},\quad\overline{\mathcal{N}}=\mathcal{N}\cup\{0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\ast}=

\displaystyle\{(j-1/2)\Delta t\,;\,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\},\quad\overline{\mathcal{N}}^{*}=\mathcal{N}^{\ast}\cup\{T+\Delta t/2\}.

We define the time discrete derivative of a function
y:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
sampled on
\mathcal{N}
as follows

\displaystyle D_{t}y:=\dfrac{\mathtt{t}^{+}y-\mathtt{t}^{-}y}{\Delta t}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{N}^{\ast},

where the translation in time is defined by
\mathtt{t}^{\pm}y(t):=y(t\pm\Delta t/2)
, with
t\in\mathcal{N}
. For the time-discrete operator we have the following asymptotic behavior

Lemma 3.11
.

Provided
\Delta t\tau(T^{3}\delta^{2})^{-1}\leq 1
, we have

(3.21)

\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{p}e^{s\varphi})D_{t}(\theta^{-p}e^{-s\varphi})=\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{p}e^{s\varphi}\partial_{t}(\theta^{-p}e^{-s\varphi}))+\mathcal{E}(\Delta t)\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1),

where
\mathcal{E}(\Delta t):=\mathcal{E}(\Delta t,p,\delta,\tau,T):=p(p+1)\frac{\Delta t}{\delta^{p+4}T^{2}}+p\frac{\Delta t}{\delta^{p+3}T^{2}}+2p\tau\frac{\Delta t}{\delta^{p+4}T^{4}}+\tau^{2}\frac{\Delta t}{\delta^{p+4}T^{6}}+\tau\frac{\Delta t}{\delta^{p+3}T^{4}}
.

Proof.

We use the first-order Taylor formula for
f:=\theta^{-p}e^{-s\varphi}
to write

(3.22)

Assumption:
Let
\overline{\omega_{0}}\subset\omega

\Omega

\psi>0\quad\text{in }\widehat{\Omega},\quad|\nabla\psi|\geq c\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\omega_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{n_{i}}\psi(x)\leq-c<0\quad\text{for }x\in V_{\partial_{i}\Omega},

where

is a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\partial_{i}\Omega
in
\tilde{\Omega}
, where the outward unit normal

to
\Omega
extends from
\partial_{i}\Omega
.
For
\lambda\geq 1
and

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=e^{\lambda\psi(x)}-e^{\lambda K}<0,

5.
Concluding remarks and perspectives

In this work, we established the asymptotic behavior of the Carleman weight functions in arbitrary dimensions for spatial and time discrete operators, which are fundamental tools in developing Carleman estimates for semi-discrete and fully discrete operators. Recent works, such as
González Casanova and Hernández-Santamaría (2021)
and
Lecaros et al. (2023a)
, have used Carleman estimates to analyze the controllability of fully discrete 1-D heat equations with Dirichlet and dynamic boundary conditions respectively. Hence, this work extends the results to arbitrary dimensions presented in
González Casanova and Hernández-Santamaría (2021)
. For this reason it seems promising using the result presented in this paper as Theorems
3.5
and
3.13
to extend the results obtained in
Lecaros et al. (2023a)
. Moreover, as is mentioned in remark
3.9
, the asymptotic behavior also holds for the weight functions utilized in Carleman estimate for semi-discrete hyperbolic operators then a similar approach as the inverse problems could be adapted into arbitrary dimensions for these semi-discrete operators.

In a similar direction, another unexplored research topic where Carleman estimates are fundamental is the discrete or semi-discrete inverse problem for parabolic operators. In recent years, results for semi-discrete hyperbolic operators
Baudouin et al. (2015)
;
Baudouin and Ervedoza (2013)
have provided stability estimates for inverse problems. Nevertheless, there is only one stability estimate results for semi-discrete deterministic parabolic operators
Lecaros et al. (2025)
. Further,
Wu et al. (2024)
considers an inverse problem for a semi-discrete stochastic parabolic equation in the one-dimensional setting. Thus, the results presented in this work are important tools for studying those semi-discrete inverse problems, as Carleman estimates are the main tool to achieving these results, see remark
3.9
. Indeed, in
Lecaros et al. (2026b)
are obtained to three Carleman estimates for semi-discrete stochastic parabolic operator in arbitrary dimension by using Theorems
3.5
and
3.13
. These Carleman estimates are applied to study inverse random source and Cauchy problems, see
(
Lecaros et al., 2026b
, Theorem 1.1)
and
(
Lecaros et al., 2026b
, Theorem 1.3)
.

Recently, several works have been considered the controllability problems for semi-discrete stochastic partial differential equations. In
Zhao (2025)
,
Wang and Zhao (2025)
and
Wang and Zhao (2024)
is established the
\phi
-null controllability of second and fourth-order semi-discrete stochastic parabolic operators, (see
Boyer (2013)
for details of
\phi
-null controllability for semi-discrete systems). Moreover, in
Lu and Liu (2026)
the authors obtain
\phi
-null controllability resutls for a cascade system of backward stochastic semi-discrete fourth- and second-order parabolic. Concerning the controllability issue, the task is to establish a relaxed observability inequality through a Carleman estimate for the respective semi-discrete backward adjoint system, then to obtain
\phi
-null controllability result for its respective forward semi-discrete stochastic system. Using this methodology, it is obtained in
Zhao (2025)
,
Wang and Zhao (2025)
and
Wang and Zhao (2024)
controllability results for a spatial semi-discrete approximation of a 1-D stochastic parabolic operators. It is worth noting that spatial terms are key to obtaining the Carleman estimates present in those works. For this reason, the estimates related to the Carleman weight function proved in the present paper are fundamental in extending the aforementioned results to arbitrary dimensions. Moreover, due to the difficulty of managing the estimation on the Carleman weight function, there are only two works concerning a higher order operator in 1-D, see
Cerpa et al. (2022)
and
Wang and Zhao (2024)
for controllability results of a deterministic and stochastic fourth-order operator, respectively. This difficulties, by using Theorems
3.5
, are overcome in
Lecaros et al. (2026a)
where the authors obtain
\phi
-controllability result for semi-discrete stochastic parabolic operators in arbitrary dimension which generalize the results obtained in
Zhao (2025)
.

In turn, in
Zhao and Yuan (2024)
is obtained a stability estimate of the discrete Calderon’s problem with partial data measured on an arbitrary portion of the boundary where a suitable Carleman estimate is the main tool. This is an improvement of the stability result presented in
Lecaros et al. (2023b)
and also needs to estimate repeated application of the discrete spatial operator on the Carleman weight function to obtain a weaker version, with respect to its continuous framework, of the unique continuation property. In the same direction, in
Fernández-Bertolin et al. (2021)
is obtained a discrete version of the three spheres inequality, and in
Fernández-Bertolin et al. (2024)
is studied the unique continuation properties of the discrete fractional Laplacian operator both of them via discrete Carleman estimate where their approach mimics the methodology from
Ervedoza and de Gournay (2011)
. Hence several computations related with trigonometric identities are needed in the development of the discrete Carleman estimate in the works
Fernández-Bertolin et al. (2021)
;
Ervedoza and de Gournay (2011)
;
Lecaros et al. (2023b)
. Thus, the estimates from Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.5
give an alternative way to develop of Carleman estimates for discrete elliptic operators.

6.1.
Conjugated operator

For simplicity of notation we write
r:=e^{s\varphi}
and
\rho:=r^{-1}
, where our weight function is defined as

, for

for

By regarding this weight function, the first step is to consider the change of variable
q=\rho z
. Our first task is to split the conjugate operator
\mathtt{t}^{-}r\mathcal{P}(\rho z):=-\mathtt{t}^{-}rD_{t}(\rho z)-\mathtt{t}^{-}r\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathtt{t}^{-}D_{i}\left(\gamma_{i}D_{i}(\rho z)\right)
into simple terms that we will estimate separately. By (

), we have

(6.1)

\begin{split}\mathtt{t}^{-}r\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathtt{t}^{-}D_{i}\left(\gamma_{i}D_{i}(\rho z)\right)=&A_{1}z+A_{2}z+B_{1}z+C_{h}z,\end{split}

where
\displaystyle A_{1}z:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathtt{t}^{-}(rA_{i}^{2}\rho\,D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}z))
,
\displaystyle B_{1}z:=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\mathtt{t}^{-}(rD_{i}A_{i}\rho D_{i}A_{i}z)
,

\displaystyle A_{2}z:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\mathtt{t}^{-}(rD_{i}^{2}\rho A_{i}^{2}z),

and

\begin{split}C_{h}z:=&\sum_{i=1}^{d}h_{i}\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{t}^{-}(rD_{i}^{2}\rho A_{i}^{2}z)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}h_{i}\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{t}^{-}(rD_{i}A_{i}\rho D_{i}A_{i}z)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{i}\gamma_{i}\mathtt{t}^{-}(A_{i}D_{i}\rho A_{i}^{2}z)\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{h^{2}_{i}}{4}D_{i}\gamma_{i}\mathtt{t}^{-}(D_{i}^{2}\rho D_{i}A_{i}z)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{h^{2}_{i}}{4}\mathtt{t}^{-}(D_{i}A_{i}\rho D_{i}^{2}z).\end{split}

Now, using that
\mathtt{t}^{+}z=\mathtt{t}^{-}z+\Delta tD_{t}z
and Lemma
3.11
with
p=0
, provided
\Delta t\tau(T^{3}\delta^{2})^{-1}\leq 1
it follows that

(6.2)

\begin{split}\mathtt{t}^{-}(r)\,D_{t}(\rho z)=&D_{t}z-\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime}z)+C_{\Delta t}z,\end{split}

with
C_{\Delta t}z:=\Delta t\left(\frac{\tau}{\delta^{3}T^{4}}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\delta^{4}T^{6}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)\right)\mathtt{t}^{+}z+\tau\Delta t\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime})\varphi D_{t}z
. Now, we set

e^{s\varphi}\partial_{t}(e^{-s\varphi})=-\tau\varphi\partial_{t}\theta
and by Theorem
3.5
we have
rA_{i}D_{i}\rho=s\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
. Thus, for
I_{31}^{i}
we have the following estimate

(7.69)

\begin{split}I_{31}^{(i)}=&\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}Ts^{2}\theta\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)\,|z|^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\ast}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}T\theta\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})\,|D_{i}z|^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore,
I_{31}
can be estimated as

I_{31}=\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}dTs^{2}\theta\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)\,|z|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\ast}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}T\theta\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}((sh)^{2})\,|D_{i}z|^{2}.

7.8.
Estimate of
I_{32}

Let us set
\alpha_{32}:=2s\Delta_{\Gamma}\phi\tau\varphi\theta^{\prime}
. We have shifting the discrete time variable
I_{32}
is given by
\displaystyle I_{32}=\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}\alpha_{32}|z|^{2}
. Then, it follows that

I_{32}\geq-\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}Ts^{2}\theta\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)|z|^{2},

since
|\theta^{\prime}|\leq CT\theta^{2}
,
\left\|\Delta_{\Gamma}\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{h}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast})}\leq\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
and
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{C(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
.

7.9.
Estimate of
I_{33}

We have to estimate

\displaystyle I_{33}:=-\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime}\,z)D_{t}z
. Note that by using the identity
\mathtt{t}^{-}(z)D_{t}z=\frac{1}{2}D_{t}(|z|^{2})-\frac{\Delta t}{2}|D_{t}z|^{2}
,
I_{33}
can be rewritten as

I_{33}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime})\,D_{t}(|z|^{2})+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime})|D_{t}z|^{2}.

Now, using
(
Lecaros et al., 2023a
, Equation (2.9))
on the first integral above we have

\begin{split}I_{33}=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\,D_{t}(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathtt{t}^{+}(|z|^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{+}(|z|^{2})\mathtt{t}^{+}(\theta^{\prime})n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime})|D_{t}z|^{2}.\end{split}

We note that
\mathtt{t}^{+}(\theta^{\prime})\,n>0
on
\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}
since
\theta^{\prime}=(2t-T)\theta^{2}
. Moreover, thanks to
\varphi<0
in
\mathcal{M}
, it follows that

(7.70)

-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{+}(|z|^{2})\mathtt{t}^{+}(\theta^{\prime})\,n>0.

Thus, we can drop the boundary terms and
I_{33}
can be estimated as

I_{33}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\tau\varphi\,D_{t}(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathtt{t}^{+}(|z|^{2})+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{\ast}}\tau\varphi\mathtt{t}^{-}(\theta^{\prime})|D_{t}z|^{2}.

Now, let us focus on the integral with the term
|\mathtt{t}^{+}z|^{2}
. Using that
|\mathtt{t}^{+}z|^{2}\leq C|\mathtt{t}^{-}z|^{2}+C(\Delta t)^{2}|D_{t}z|^{2}
and Theorem
3.13
we obtain

\begin{split}I_{33}\geq&-\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathtt{t}^{-}(\mu_{33})|\mathtt{t}^{-}z|^{2}-\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}(\Delta t)^{2}\mathtt{t}^{-}(\mu_{23})|D_{t}z|^{2}-\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathtt{t}^{-}(\gamma_{23})|D_{t}z|^{2},\end{split}

with
\mu_{33}:=(\tau T^{2}\theta^{3}+\frac{\tau\Delta t}{\delta^{4}T^{5}})\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
and
\gamma_{33}:=\Delta t\tau T\theta^{2}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
. We can shift the integral with the term
|\mathtt{t}^{-}z|^{2}
.
