Genome wide identification, structural characterization, expression analysis, and regulatory network inferring of BAG gene family in barley
Authors: Bahman Panahi, Rasmieh Hamid, Feba Jacob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe BAG (Bcl-2-associated athanogene) gene family plays a crucial role in plant stress responses by regulating programmed cell death, protein homeostasis and molecular chaperone interactions. While they have been extensively studied in model plants such as Arabidopsis and rice, the functional role and evolutionary dynamics of BAG genes in barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) are still largely unexplored. In this study, seven HvBAG genes were identified and characterised and their structural diversity, phylogenetic classification, chromosomal distribution and expression patterns were analysed. Comparative analyses with rice, maize, wheat and Arabidopsis revealed pedigree-specific expansions and conserved motifs underlining the functional importance of BAG proteins in stress adaptation. Our study revealed variations in protein stability, hydrophobicity and subcellular localisation, with HvBAG3 predominantly localised in the nucleus and HvBAG5 in the mitochondria, suggesting specialised cellular functions. Synteny and duplication analyses showed that segmental duplications and purifying selection have contributed to the expansion and evolutionary conservation of HvBAG genes. Expression profiling under heat stress revealed differential regulation in different tissues, with HvBAG3 significantly upregulated in roots and shoots, suggesting its role in heat stress resistance. Furthermore, miRNA-mediated regulation of HvBAG genes provides an additional level of post-transcriptional control that refines the mechanisms of stress response. This study provides a framework for understanding the functional diversity of BAG genes in barley and offers insights into their evolutionary history and regulatory mechanisms. These results have practical implications for the breeding of stress-resistant barley varieties and point to BAG genes as potential targets for genetic engineering strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57105-0.Keywords: BAG gene family, Barley, Stress response, Gene duplication, Heat stress adaptation, Regulatory networkSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Plant genetics

Introduction

Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is one of the earliest domesticated cereal crops and continues to play a vital role in global agriculture, particularly in temperate regions. It is a primary source of food, animal feed, and raw materials for the malting and brewing industries, with global production surpassing 150 million metric tons annually1. Despite its ability to adapt to various climatic conditions, barley productivity is increasingly threatened by abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures2,3. These environmental challenges significantly impair photosynthetic efficiency, disrupt nutrient uptake, and alter metabolic pathways, ultimately resulting in reduced yields4. Heat stress, especially during the anthesis and grain-filling stages, accelerates chlorophyll degradation, hinders carbon assimilation, and compromises kernel development, occasionally causing yield losses exceeding 30%5. Therefore, enhancing stress tolerance through molecular and genetic approaches is essential for ensuring sustainable barley production in a changing climate.

One promising strategy for improving stress resilience is the functional characterization of stress-associated gene families, such as the Bcl-2-associated athanogene (BAG) family. BAG proteins act as molecular co-chaperones, interacting with heat shock proteins (HSP70/HSC70) to help maintain cellular homeostasis under stress conditions6. In plants, BAG genes are involved in various physiological processes, including programmed cell death (PCD), stress signal transduction, and proteostasis7,8. Based on the conserved domain architecture, plant BAG proteins are typically categorized into three structural types: (1) proteins that contain only the conserved BAG domain; (2) proteins that have an N-terminal ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain in addition to the BAG domain; and (3) proteins that have a calmodulin-binding/IQ motif in combination with the BAG domain9,10. This structural diversification is functionally significant as the UBL domain is generally associated with proteasome-mediated degradation, whereas the IQ motif mediates calcium/calmodulin-dependent signaling. Functional studies in Arabidopsis thaliana have shown that AtBAG-4 enhances heat tolerance through regulatory interactions with stress-responsive transcription factors, while AtBAG-7 modulates unfolded protein response (UPR) pathways to alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress11,12. Moreover, AtBAG-6 promotes fungal resistance by facilitating autophagy-mediated defense mechanisms13. Similar functions have been observed in other crop species, such as rice (Oryza sativa)14,15, maize (Zea mays)16, and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum)17, where BAG genes exhibit differential expression patterns in response to drought, salinity, and oxidative stress.

Despite increasing evidence supporting the role of BAG proteins in stress adaptation, their functional significance in barley remains largely unexplored. Given their involvement in stress response pathways, a comprehensive investigation of the BAG gene family in barley is essential. This study aims to characterize the evolutionary history, expression dynamics, and functional interactions of barley BAG proteins, providing new insights into their potential roles in abiotic stress adaptation. These findings will inform targeted breeding and genetic engineering strategies aimed at developing stress-resilient barley cultivars.

Methods and material

Identification and characterization of the BAG gene family in barley

To identify members of the BAG gene family in Hordeum vulgare, we conducted a genome-wide search using BAG protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana as query sequences against the Hordeum vulgare reference genome (IBSC_v3) employing the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) approach. Specifically, the BAG domain HMM profile (Pfam ID: PF02179) was retrieved from the Pfam database and used to search the barley proteome with HMMER v3.3.2 (http://hmmer.org/), applying an E-value cut-off of 1e − 5. Candidate sequences were retained only if they contained the complete BAG domain, as confirmed by SMART v9.0 (http://smart.embl.de/) and NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) v3.20 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/cdd.shtml). We confirmed the presence of conserved BAG domains by referencing the BAG protein family domain (Pfam: PF02179) in the Pfam database v35.0 (https://pfam.xfam.org/). For the identification of additional domain classes, we also screened for the ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain (Pfam: PF00240) and the calmodulin-binding/IQ motif (Pfam: PF00612) using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) and Pfam. This ensured accurate classification of HvBAG proteins into the three recognized structural types—BAG-only, UBL + BAG, and IQ/calmodulin-binding + BAG. For comparative analysis, we retrieved BAG protein sequences from various plant species, including Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Sorghum bicolor using the Ensembl Plants BioMart tool (https://plants.ensembl.org/biomart/martview/). The coding sequences (CDS), protein sequences, and genomic data for H. vulgare were sourced from the Ensembl Plants database release 59 (https://plants.ensembl.org/). To further characterize the identified BAG proteins, we analyzed physicochemical properties, such as molecular weight (kDa), isoelectric point (pI), and protein length (in amino acids), using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Subcellular localization prediction of HvBAG proteins

The subcellular localization of HvBAG proteins was predicted in silico using three complementary bioinformatic tools: Plant-mPLoc v2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/), TargetP v2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TargetP/), and WoLF PSORT v0.2 (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). Each protein sequence was analyzed independently in all three tools, and a consensus prediction was recorded when at least two tools predicted the same primary localization. This majority-rule approach increases prediction robustness while accounting for algorithm-specific variability. The combined predictions provided insights into potential compartment-specific functions of HvBAG proteins and facilitated comparison with experimentally validated BAG localizations in other plant species.

Gene structure, domain architecture and promoter analysis in Hordeum vulgare

The genomic and coding sequences of the identified HvBAG genes were analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) v2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) to determine exon–intron organization. Conserved protein domains were identified using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) v3.20 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/cdd.shtml) and aligned with Pfam (PF02179) for confirmation. For promoter analysis, 2 kb upstream sequences (relative to the annotated transcription start sites) were retrieved from Ensembl Plants (IBSC_v3). Promoter sequences were scanned for transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) and cis-regulatory motifs using a multi-database approach to ensure contemporary coverage and cross-validation. Specifically, (i) JASPAR 2024 CORE Plant database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) profiles were scanned using FIMO from MEME Suite v5.5.5 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/fimo) with a significance threshold of p < 1 × 10⁻⁴ and Benjamini–Hochberg correction; (ii) PlantPAN v4.0 (http://plantpan.itps.ncku.edu.tw/) was interrogated using its web interface; and (iii) PlantRegMap (http://plantregmap.gao-lab.org/) predictions were used to complement the PWM-based calls. The old PlantCARE database18(http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was also consulted to ensure comparability with previous literature. To assess concordance between resources, we assigned motif calls to promoter coordinates and considered a motif to be concordant if the same TF family or PWM was detected in at least two databases within a ± 25 bp window. Redundant motif calls were summarized by TF family (e.g. MYB, bZIP, NAC) in order to subsequently categorize them. Motifs were grouped by functional categories (hormone-, stress-, light- and development-related) according to the PlantPAN/PlantCARE ontologies and frequencies were log normalized. Graphical representations of gene structure, domain composition and promoter element distribution were generated using TBtools v1.108 (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools/releases).

Phylogenetic analysis and sequence logos in barley

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis X (MEGA-X) v10.2.6 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) to compare members of the BAG gene family across five plant species H. vulgare, O. sativa, Z. mays, A. thaliana, and S. bicolor. BAG protein sequences for each species were identified via BLASTp searches against UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) or Ensembl Plants, followed by confirmation of the BAG domain (Pfam: PF02179) using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) and HMMER. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA-X, and the phylogenetic tree was generated using the Maximum Likelihood method with the JTT substitution model. Bootstrap support values were calculated from 1,000 replicates to assess branch reliability. Conserved amino acid motifs were visualized with the WebLogo v3.7.12 online tool (https://weblogo.berkeley.edu/), highlighting key functional regions of BAG proteins in A. thaliana and H. vulgare.

Chromosomal localization, Ka/Ks ratio, and collinearity analysis

To analyze the evolutionary dynamics of the BAG gene family members in barley, non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were calculated for duplicated gene pairs using TBtools v1.108. The divergence time (in millions of years ago) was estimated using the formula T = Ks/(2λ), where λ = (2 × 6.5 × 10⁻⁹) × 10⁶. Collinearity relationships between H. vulgare and other species (O. sativa and A. thaliana) were analyzed and visualized using TBtools to investigate conserved syntenic regions and potential evolutionary events that have shaped the BAG gene family in barley.

Expression pattern of HvBAGs under high temperature stress

For the expression analysis of HvBAGs genes, RNA-Seq data related to heat stress in barley were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). Specifically, the dataset PRJNA324116 was chosen to examine the gene expression profiles of HvBAGs under heat stress conditions in shoot and root tissues. The study of shoot and root tissues enhances our understanding of tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms governing HvBAGs and stress adaptation. For data analysis, raw RNA-Seq datasets were retrieved and the initial quality assessment of the data was performed using FastQC v0.11.5 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) to evaluate sequence quality, GC content, and potential adapter contamination. To ensure the integrity of the data, adapter sequences and reads with a Phred quality score below 30 were removed using Trimmomatic v0.32 (http://www.usadellab.org/cms/?page=trimmomatic). The cleaned reads were subsequently mapped to the barley reference genome using Hierarchical Indexing for Spliced Alignment of Transcripts 2 (HISAT2) v2.2.1 (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/). Following this, transcript abundance was quantified using HTSeq-count v0.13.5 (https://htseq.readthedocs.io/en/master/count.html), enabling downstream expression analysis. Expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) and subsequently visualized using TBtools v1.108 through heat map representations to facilitate comparison of expression profiles under heat stress conditions.

Validation of expression pattern by Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) approach

To evaluate the expression patterns and infer the potential functional relevance of the predicted genes, an in-silico validation was conducted utilizing the Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) framework. This approach was selected for its suitability in small-sample contexts and its ability to rigorously assess the predictive contribution of features at the single-sample level. For each tissue type (shoot and root), expression matrices were organized with samples as observations and genes as predictor variables. The classification task was defined as a binary comparison between the Control and Treated groups. Prior to model fitting, all features were standardized using the StandardScaler function implemented in the scikit-learn machine learning library v1.3.0 (https://scikit-learn.org/) to eliminate scale-related bias and ensure equal weighting across variables. Throughout the LOOCV iterations, predicted class probabilities were aggregated to generate Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, from which Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were computed as a quantitative measure of model discrimination. Overall classification accuracy was assessed by comparing predicted versus actual sample classes across all iterations.

Prediction of putative miRNAs targeting HvBAG genes and functional annotation

To identify potential microRNAs (miRNAs) that target H. vulgare BAG (HvBAG) genes, mature barley miRNA sequences were retrieved from publicly available databases. The coding sequences of the identified HvBAG genes were analyzed using the psRNATarget server (2017 release; https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/) with default parameters. The interaction network between the predicted miRNAs and HvBAG genes were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape v3.9.1 (https://cytoscape.org/). For functional annotation, the protein sequences of all HvBAG genes were submitted to the eggNOG-mapper v2 database (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) to retrieve Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotations. KEGG pathway mapping and annotation were interpreted according to the KEGG resource developed by Kanehisa Laboratories19,20. Further enrichment analysis was carried out, and the results were graphically represented using TBtools.

BAG gene regulatory network inference using the machine learning approach

To construct the gene regulatory network, the Random Forest regression algorithm was employed on gene expression matrix from genes with a p-value > 0.05 in both shoot and root tissues. This method facilitated the identification of potential regulatory relationships under both normal and heat stress conditions. At each decision tree node, a random subset of candidate predictors was selected (K = “sqrt”), corresponding to the square root of the total number of genes. This approach optimized the balance between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. The model was trained with an ensemble of 1000 decision trees (nTrees = 1000), ensuring robust and stable predictions of regulatory interactions. Further, the inferred network was analyzed to explore the rewiring of regulatory networks associated with BAG genes across different conditions and tissues. By comparing interactions under normal and heat stress, we identified key regulatory shifts that provide insights into the dynamic modulation of BAG gene functions. Additionally, functional annotation and enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the biological significance of the identified regulatory modules, highlighting their potential roles in stress adaptation and response mechanisms.

Results

Identification and characterization of the BAG gene family in barley

To systematically identify members of the BAG gene family in the barley genome, a comprehensive analysis was performed using BLASTp searches in combination with HMM-based approaches, using reference BAG protein sequences from the model plants rice and Arabidopsis. As a result, seven BAG genes encoding proteins with different sequence lengths ranging from 194 to 1317 amino acids were identified (Table 1). These genes were designated as HvBAG1–HvBAG7 according to their physical positions on the barley reference genome (Morex v3): numbering followed ascending chromosome number (2 H to 7 H), and for multiple genes on the same chromosome, ascending genomic coordinate from the short (p) to the long (q) arm. This chromosomal-order convention is widely adopted in plant gene-family studies (e.g. CER genes in cotton21 and ensures transparency, reproducibility, and ease of comparative mapping. Among these, HvBAG5 was the shortest, while HvBAG3 was the longest protein. The molecular weights ranged from 20.79 to 144.08 kDa, while the predicted isoelectric points ranged from 5.01 to 10.97. Based on conserved domain architecture, the identified HvBAG proteins were classified into three recognized structural types: (1) BAG-only proteins (HvBAG1, HvBAG4, HvBAG5), (2) UBL + BAG proteins (HvBAG2, HvBAG3), and (3) calmodulin-binding/IQ + BAG proteins (HvBAG6, HvBAG7) (Table S1, Supplementary Fig. 1). The UBL domain is typically associated with proteasome-mediated protein degradation and quality control, whereas the IQ motif participates in calcium/calmodulin-mediated signaling pathways, often linked to abiotic stress responses. BAG-only proteins act primarily as co-chaperones that directly regulate HSP70/HSC70 activity without additional domain-mediated signaling. This classification reflects the evolutionary and functional diversification of the BAG gene family in plants.

Furthermore, in silico predictions of the subcellular localization of HvBAG proteins generated using three complementary bioinformatics tools (Plant-mPLoc, TargetP and WoLF PSORT) indicate their distribution in different cellular compartments, including cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes and plasma membrane (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S2). Predictions were consistent across all tools, with HvBAG3 most frequently localized to the nucleus, a compartment typically associated with transcriptional regulation and integration of stress signals, while HvBAG5 was most likely localized to the chloroplasts, suggesting a possible role in stress responses associated with photosynthesis. These results are consistent with experimentally validated localizations by BAG orthologues in A. thaliana9,10 and Solanum lycopersicum17, which show similar compartmentalization patterns, supporting the plausibility of our predictions. However, the computational predictions fail to capture potential stress- or tissue-specific re-localization events. Although direct experimental validation in barley (e.g. transient GFP fusion expression) is beyond the scope of the present study, we have highlighted this as a limitation and indicated that such experiments are planned in future work to confirm the predicted localizations and refine functional hypotheses.

Gene structure, conserved motifs, domains, and chromosomal localization of the BAG gene family in barley

To investigate the evolutionary relationships between the barley BAG (HvBAG) gene family, a phylogenetic tree was constructed showing the division of the HvBAG genes into three different clades (Fig. 2A). This clustering pattern suggests an evolutionary diversification of HvBAG genes, possibly leading to functional divergence. Genes within the same clade may have conserved functions, while those located in separate groups may have undergone structural and functional differentiation to adapt to specific cellular processes in barley.

(B) Gene structure analysis. (C) Domain analysis, (D) Conserved motif distribution. (E) Corresponding motif sequences of HvBAG genes in barley.

Structural analysis of the HvBAG genes revealed considerable variation in exon-intron organization, illustrating the evolutionary complexity of this gene family (Fig. 2B). The number of exons varied among the HvBAG genes, with some members exhibiting a compact gene structure while others contained extensive intronic regions. This structural diversity suggests that exon-intron shuffling, gain or loss contributed to the functional specialization of HvBAG genes. Interestingly, genes within the same clade tend to have more conserved exon-intron patterns, emphasizing their evolutionary relatedness and potential functional similarity.

Analysis of the conserved domains within the HvBAG proteins (Fig. 2C) revealed the presence of important functional domains associated with molecular chaperone activity. Along with the canonical BAG domain, additional structural features, including ubiquitin-like motifs, were identified in five HvBAG genes (clades I and III), suggesting a potential expansion of their functional roles beyond traditional BAG-mediated protein homeostasis. Conversely, clade II genes exhibited additional coiled-coil regions that may enhance protein-protein interactions, further supporting the hypothesis of functional diversification within the family. This variation in domain architecture suggests that HvBAG proteins may have specialized roles under different stress conditions and developmental processes in barley.

Motif analysis identified several conserved motifs in the HvBAG proteins, highlighting their evolutionary conservation and functional significance (Fig. 2D). Motifs 1, 3 and 7 were present in almost all HvBAG proteins, suggesting their fundamental role in BAG protein function, while certain motifs were only present in specific clades, indicating possible functional specialization within subgroups. The conservation of these motifs emphasizes their possible involvement in cellular stress regulation and protein quality control mechanisms. Further motif sequence analysis (Fig. 2E) provided insights into the amino acid composition of the conserved domains and revealed key residues that are probably crucial for the function of the BAG protein. The sequence logos show a high degree of conservation between essential amino acids within these motifs, suggesting their importance in maintaining protein stability and mediating protein-protein interactions. The observed sequence divergence between different HvBAG proteins may indicate adaptive modifications possibly related to specific stress responses or regulatory pathways in barley. Overall, the integrated phylogenetic, structural and motif analyses provide compelling evidence for the evolutionary diversification of the HvBAG gene family leading to both conserved and specialized functional roles.

Barley BAG protein’s phylogenetic classification

Using the protein sequences of Hordeum vulgare (Hv), Oryza sativa (Os), Arabidopsis thaliana (At), Zea mays (Zm), and Sorghum bicolor (Sb), we constructed a phylogenetic tree that classified the BAG gene family into four distinct clades (Fig. 3). For Arabidopsis and rice, eight BAG proteins were identified through BLASTp searches against UniProt, followed by confirmation of the BAG domain (Pfam: PF02179) using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) and HMMER. This result is consistent with the recent genome-wide analysis by 17, replacing earlier reports of six BAG members in each species. For barley, seven BAG proteins were identified in the present study using the same BLASTp–CDD–HMMER pipeline. For maize (Zea mays), 21 BAG protein sequences were retrieved from the Ensemble Plants database and validated for the BAG domain using CDD and HMMER. This comparatively high number, which exceeds previous monocot BAG family reports, may reflect lineage-specific expansion, alternative isoforms, or differences in genome annotation. For sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), eight BAG proteins were identified using the same approach. Applying an identical identification procedure to all species ensured methodological consistency and accurate cross-species comparison. The complete list of BAG protein sequences for all species used in the phylogenetic analysis is provided in Supplementary file S1 for full transparency.

Chromosomal distribution, collinearity, and Synteny analysis of the HvBAG gene family in barley

The chromosomal distribution of seven HvBAG genes in Hordeum vulgare was identified and showed scattered localization across five chromosomes (Fig. 4A). Specifically, the HvBAG genes were assigned to chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 with one gene each and chromosome 7, with chromosome 7 harboring two genes (HvBAG-6 and HvBAG-7). This revised number of seven genes corrects the earlier underestimation. This scattered arrangement suggests that the expansion of HvBAG genes in barley was primarily caused by segmental duplications rather than tandem duplications, as no physically clustered gene pairs were identified.

To investigate the evolutionary relationships within the HvBAG gene family, a collinearity analysis was performed to detect duplicated gene pairs within the barley genome (Fig. 4B). This analysis identified two pairs of collinear HvBAG genes, suggesting that their expansion occurred mainly through segmental duplications. The collinear pairs detected include HvBAG-3 ↔ HvBAG-5 and HvBAG-4 ↔ HvBAG-7, with an additional scattered duplication event between HvBAG-1 and HvBAG-6. These gene duplication patterns are clearly depicted in Fig. 4B, emphasizing the role of segmental duplications in the evolution of the HvBAG family. The identification of these collinear pairs suggests that whole genome duplication (WGD) or large-scale chromosomal rearrangements have contributed to the evolution of the HvBAG family in barley. In particular, the absence of tandem duplications supports the hypothesis that segmental duplications and not local gene duplications have played a predominant role in the expansion of the HvBAG gene family.

Further insight into the selection pressure acting on these genes was gained by analyzing the Ka/Ks ratio, which revealed that all identified gene pairs have a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1, indicating that they are predominantly subject to purifying selection (Table 2). The HvBAG-3/HvBAG-5 pair had a Ka/Ks ratio of 0.86, indicating a relatively high rate of non-synonymous substitutions that may be indicative of functional divergence. Estimated duplication events range between 8.5 and 11.28 million years ago (mya), with segmental duplications being older (~ 11 mya) than dispersed duplications (~ 8.5–9 mya). This evolutionary timeframe emphasizes the long-term conservation of HvBAG genes and their functional importance for barley adaptation. The presence of scattered duplications suggests that certain HvBAG genes may have been duplicated by transposons or genome rearrangements, contributing to the structural and functional diversity of this gene family.

To investigate the evolutionary relationships between the BAG genes of barley, Arabidopsis and rice, a synteny analysis was performed (Fig. 4C). The results showed a stronger syntenic relationship between barley and rice compared to Arabidopsis, consistent with their closer phylogenetic relationship as monocotyledons. A total of three orthologous BAG gene pairs were identified between barley and rice, while only two orthologous pair was found between barley and Arabidopsis. This can be clearly seen in Fig. 4C, where red lines indicate the conserved BAG gene synteny between the species. The extensive syntenic relationship between barley and rice suggests that the BAG genes in these species have retained homologous genomic regions despite evolutionary divergence, supporting the hypothesis that monocotyledons have a more recent common ancestor compared to dicotyledons. The relatively weaker syntenic relationship between barley and Arabidopsis may suggest that the BAG genes in dicotyledons have undergone independent evolutionary changes leading to functional diversification. Furthermore, the presence of multiple collinear BAG gene pairs between barley and rice suggests functional conservation and evolutionary stability within monocotyledons. The retention of these conserved genomic regions suggests a possible role for BAG genes in fundamental cellular processes, particularly stress response and protein homeostasis.

Examination of the Cis-acting elements in Barley’ BAG genes

The study of cis-acting regulatory elements within gene promoter regions is fundamental to understanding transcriptional regulation and the functional role of gene families (Hamid et al., 2024). To obtain a robust and contemporary map of putative cis-regulatory motifs, we scanned the 2 kb upstream promoter regions of HvBAG genes using a multi-database approach: JASPAR 2024 CORE (plant) via FIMO (p < 1 × 10⁻⁴, Benjamini–Hochberg correction)22, PlantPAN 4.0 [PWM-based and ChIP-based predictions]23, and PlantRegMap [functional TF target predictions and motif conservation]24, while retaining PlantCARE18 for historical comparability. The multi-database strategy confirmed the majority of motifs originally detected by PlantCARE and yielded additional TFBSs absent from the older repository. Quantitatively, 85% of high-confidence motifs were detected in at least two independent databases, while 2% were found in only one platform (Supplementary Table S3). The overlaps between the databases are illustrated in the newly added Venn diagram (Fig. 5A). The identified motifs were categorized into four primary functional groups: hormone-responsive, growth- and development-responsive, stress-responsive, and light-responsive elements (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table S3).

Among the hormone-responsive elements, abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABRE) were the most abundant, suggesting involvement in ABA-mediated stress responses (Fig. 5C). In addition, gibberellin-responsive elements, including the GARE motif, P-box, and TATC-box, were detected, indicating a possible role in growth regulation. The presence of ethylene-responsive elements (ERE) and auxin-responsive elements (AuxRR core) suggests that HvBAG genes may be involved in multiple hormones signaling pathways. The frequent occurrence of CGTCA and TGACG motifs associated with methyl jasmonate (MeJA) signaling was confirmed by all four databases, while PlantPAN 4.0 and JASPAR 2024 additionally identified NAC-, bZIP-, and ARF-family TFBSs, which are absent in PlantCARE, strengthening the link between HvBAG regulation and hormone-mediated stress adaptation.

Stress-responsive elements were prevalent in HvBAG promoters, highlighting their potential role in adaptation to environmental stress. Prominent motifs included anaerobic response elements (ARE), MYB-binding sites (MBS) for drought response, stress response elements (STRE), low-temperature response elements (LTR), and TC-rich repeats associated with defense and stress responses (Fig. 5C). MYB and MYC binding sites known to regulate responses to abiotic and biotic stresses were highly abundant, suggesting possible regulatory interactions with MYB/MYC transcription factors. The presence of W-box elements, which serve as binding sites for WRKY transcription factors, suggests that HvBAG genes may play a role in pathogen defense. Other stress-related motifs such as WRE3 and CCGTCC boxes were also identified, supporting the hypothesis that HvBAG genes contribute to stress tolerance. Although no classical heat shock elements (HSE consensus nGAAn) were detected below the stringent threshold, JASPAR 2024 and PlantRegMap predicted potential HSF-related motifs at lower information content scores, and MYC elements known to interact with heat stress-responsive factors were frequent, suggesting indirect regulation in heat stress. The JASPAR dataset also identified potential binding motifs for DREB/CBF proteins and members of the ERF/AP2 family, important regulators of abiotic stress responses, providing additional evidence for HvBAG involvement in adaptation to heat, cold, and oxidative stress.

Among the growth- and development-related elements, multiple copies of the CAT box (associated with meristem expression), the RY element (involved in seed-type regulation), and the O₂ site (associated with the regulation of zein metabolism) were identified, suggesting a possible role in embryogenesis and seed development (Fig. 5C). In addition, the discovery of circadian regulatory elements suggests that some HvBAG genes may be subject to circadian control, which may be important for temporal gene expression in response to environmental stimuli. Light-dependent elements were also widely distributed across the promoters of HvBAG genes, suggesting a possible role in light-dependent gene regulation. The most frequently identified motifs included Box 4, ACE, and G-box, all of which are involved in light-dependent transcriptional activation (Fig. 5C). Additional light-dependent TFBSs predicted by PlantPAN 4.0 and JASPAR 2024 included HY5- and GBF-binding sites, suggesting that these promoters are involved in photomorphogenesis and light-driven stress signaling. In summary, the combination of PlantCARE with recently curated TFBS resources (PlantPAN 4.0, JASPAR 2024, and PlantRegMap) not only confirmed the identification of core motifs, but also revealed a broader repertoire of cis-elements, providing a more comprehensive and timely understanding of HvBAG transcriptional regulation. The integration of several independent databases, together with concordance statistics and complete motif lists (see Table S3), increases the reliability of the promoter analysis and brings the study in line with current bioinformatics standards in plant regulatory genomics.

The predicted models were generated using homology modeling, with GMQE values ranging from 0.67 to 0.75, indicating high structural reliability (Fig. 6). The tertiary structures showed a conserved alpha-helical BAG domain across all HvBAG members, accompanied by variable beta-sheet and loop regions that could contribute to functional diversification.

Interaction between HvBAG Genes and miRNAs in Barley

The interaction between the HvBAG gene family and microRNAs (miRNAs) was systematically analysed to explore post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms affecting gene expression. The results revealed several miRNA targets within the HvBAG gene family that are predominantly associated with cleavage-mediated inhibition, indicating their role in transcriptional degradation and turnover (Supplementary Table S4).

Among the identified interactions, hvu-miR6192 exhibited significant target specificity toward HvBAG-7, which matched the target site at positions 1337–1358. The alignment suggests a strong cleavage-mediated regulatory mechanism characterized by highly conserved miRNA-target pairing with minimal sequence mismatches. Similarly, hvu-miR6196 was predicted to be the target of HvBAG-6 with an alignment spanning positions 1526–1546, further supporting miRNA-directed cleavage as a key regulatory process. Of note, hvu-miR168-5p was predicted to interact with HvBAG-1, albeit with a slightly higher expected value (4.5), indicating a comparatively weaker binding affinity. The nature of this interaction suggests a predominant role in translational repression rather than direct transcriptional cleavage, indicating a possible functional divergence between miRNA-mediated regulatory pathways. Furthermore, hvu-miR6180 was observed to target multiple HvBAG genes, including HvBAG-6, HvBAG-4 and HvBAG-3, supporting the hypothesis of coordinated miRNA-driven regulation of BAG gene expression. In addition, hvu-miR6190 and hvu-miR6198 were identified as regulators of HvBAG-4 and HvBAG-3, respectively, with sequence complementarities indicative of cleavage-mediated gene silencing. The large number of miRNA interactions indicates a highly redundant and intricate regulatory network in which individual HvBAG genes can be modulated by several miRNAs, ensuring tight post-transcriptional control of cellular responses to environmental and developmental factors.

Discussion

The identification and characterization of the BAG gene family in barley offer important insights into their functional roles, evolutionary dynamics, and potential applications for stress tolerance. Studies in model plants such as A. thaliana and O. sativa have emphasized the role of BAG proteins in regulating programmed cell death, maintaining protein homeostasis, and responding to stress25–27. The discovery of seven HvBAG genes with varying physicochemical properties such as molecular weights ranging from 20.79 to 144.08 kDa and isoelectric points between 5.01 and 10.97 suggests potential functional specialization within this gene family. Importantly, the classification of HvBAG proteins into the three established domain types BAG only, UBL + BAG and calmodulin-binding/IQ + BAG provides additional evidence for functional diversification. The UBL-containing HvBAG2 and HvBAG3 are likely involved in ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation processes, as reported for the UBL-type BAG proteins in rice and maize, which function in the removal of misfolded proteins under heat stress16,28. The IQ motif-containing HvBAG6 and HvBAG7 are likely linked to the calcium/calmodulin-dependent signaling pathway, a pathway known to modulate abiotic stress responses and programmed cell death in Arabidopsis and tomato9,17. BAG-only proteins (HvBAG1, HvBAG4, HvBAG5) may act as central co-chaperones with direct HSP70/HSC70 regulation, which is consistent with reports from wheat where BAG-only members are upregulated during early heat stress29. This domain-based classification therefore not only refines the structural annotation of HvBAG proteins but also provides a predictive framework for hypothesizing their stress-related functional roles in barley. These findings are consistent with previous reports in rice, where OsBAG proteins display a wide range of biochemical properties and subcellular localizations, indicating functional diversification30.

The variability in the instability index and GRAVY values among HvBAG proteins suggests differences in stability and hydrophobicity, which may affect protein interactions and cellular localization. Similar observations have been made in wheat (Triticum aestivum), where TaBAG proteins exhibited distinct structural features that aligned with their functions in responding to abiotic stress, particularly heat and drought29,31. Subcellular localization analysis further reinforces this functional divergence, with HvBAG3 primarily found in the nucleus and HvBAG-5 located in the mitochondria. This pattern is reminiscent of Arabidopsis BAG proteins, where AtBAG6 is localized in the nucleus and interacts with chaperone proteins to facilitate proteostasis, while AtBAG7 is associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and plays a role in the unfolded protein response11.

Phylogenetic analysis divided the HvBAG genes into five clades, highlighting both evolutionary conservation and divergence32. The distribution of domain classes among clades supports the hypothesis of subfunctionalisation by gene duplication33, with BAG-only types clustered separately from UBL- and IQ-containing types. The presence of conserved BAG domains, together with additional structural motifs such as ubiquitin-like motifs and coiled-coil regions, suggests that these genes have specialized regulatory functions. Similar findings have been reported in rice and maize (Z. mays), where BAG genes show lineage-specific expansion and domain adaptations associated with stress responses16,28. Importantly, motif analysis indicated that specific motifs, such as Motifs 1, 3, and 7, are shared among all HvBAG proteins, underscoring their essential roles in regulating cellular stress. These results align with studies in Arabidopsis and wheat, which demonstrate that conserved BAG motifs play a critical role in chaperone activity and cellular defense mechanisms29. The phylogenetic classification of barley BAG proteins, together with those from rice, maize, sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), and A. thaliana, provides deeper insights into their evolutionary history. The clear separation of several clades between monocotyledons and dicotyledons supports the hypothesis that lineage-specific diversification, likely driven by gene duplication and subsequent neofunctionalization, has contributed to the acquisition of specialized functional roles, particularly in stress adaptation and protein homeostasis34,35.

Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis shed light on the evolutionary history of the HvBAG family. The HvBAG genes are scattered across five chromosomes and show evidence of segmental duplications, similar to findings in rice and maize, where BAG gene expansion was largely driven by whole-genome duplication events28. The presence of collinear gene pairs indicates that segmental duplications were significant in the evolution of HvBAG genes, a trend also observed in wheat and Arabidopsis, where the retention of BAG genes is linked to adaptive functions in stress resilience9. Additionally, the Ka/Ks ratio analysis suggests purifying selection, indicating functional conservation and reinforcing the evolutionary pressure to maintain BAG gene functions, akin to observations in rice and Arabidopsis11.

The synteny analysis of barley, rice, and Arabidopsis demonstrated a stronger conservation of BAG genes between barley and rice, highlighting their common monocot lineage. Specifically, six orthologous BAG gene pairs were identified between barley and rice, while only one pair was found between barley and Arabidopsis. This finding supports previous comparative genomics studies that emphasize the closer evolutionary relationship between barley and rice, stemming from their shared ancestry within the Poaceae family17,36.

In plants, miRNAs play a crucial role in fine-tuning responses to various abiotic stresses, including heat stress, by targeting stress-associated genes36,37. The identification of specific miRNAs that target HvBAG genes in barley offers new insights into the post-transcriptional control of BAG-mediated stress responses. For example, hvu-miR6192 is predicted to target HvBAG-7, while hvu-miR6196 appears to regulate HvBAG-6, suggesting a potential cleavage-mediated regulatory mechanism. This type of miRNA-guided gene regulation is a well-established mechanism in plant stress adaptation, with miRNAs serving as key modulators of stress-responsive gene networks37–39. The expression patterns of HvBAG genes under heat stress reveal a complex regulatory landscape that varies across different tissues. Notably, HvBAG-3 is significantly upregulated in both treated roots and shoots, indicating its active role in heat stress adaptation. This finding aligns with previous studies on BAG genes in other plant species, where homologs of BAG3 have been associated with stress mitigation through interactions with heat shock proteins (HSPs) and antioxidant enzymes40,41. Conversely, the downregulation of HvBAG-7, HvBAG-4, and HvBAG-6 in heat-treated roots suggests that these genes may be suppressed under specific stress conditions, potentially to prevent excessive protein turnover or energy expenditure in stressed tissues.

The rewiring of regulatory networks involving BAG genes in response to heat stress highlights their essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. BAG proteins interact with molecular chaperones like HSP70 and HSP90, facilitating protein refolding and degradation processes. This interaction is particularly critical during heat stress, as the accumulation of misfolded proteins can lead to cellular damage26,42,43. The differential expression of HvBAG genes in shoots and roots indicates tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms. Shoots, being directly exposed to heat stress, may require a more robust BAG-mediated response, while roots might focus on other protective strategies, such as osmolyte accumulation and the scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) process.

Conclusion

This study was performed to identify and characterize the BAG gene family in barley, providing crucial insights into their evolutionary conservation, functional specialization, and roles in stress adaptation. By conducting comparative genomic analyses with Arabidopsis, rice, wheat, and maize, we uncovered the structural and biochemical variability of HvBAG proteins, highlighting their specialized roles in programmed cell death, protein homeostasis, and stress response mechanisms. Phylogenetic classification and chromosomal distribution analyses suggest that segmental duplications and purifying selection have significantly influenced the expansion and functional retention of BAG genes in barley, reflecting similar evolutionary patterns observed in other plant species.

A key finding of this study is the miRNA-mediated regulation of HvBAG genes, introducing an additional layer of post-transcriptional control in response to heat stress. The observed differential subcellular localization and tissue-specific expression patterns further emphasize the diverse regulatory roles of HvBAG genes in plant stress resilience. Additionally, the predicted interactions between BAG proteins and molecular chaperones (HSP70 and HSP90) indicate a conserved mechanism for maintaining protein stability and cellular homeostasis, particularly under environmental stress conditions. The findings of this study contribute to the existing knowledge of the BAG gene family in plants and offer new insights into their regulatory mechanisms and stress-associated functions. The results have important theoretical and practical implications, especially for crop improvement programs. Understanding the role of HvBAG genes in stress tolerance pathways can inform biotechnological approaches aimed at enhancing stress resilience in barley and other cereal crops. In conclusion, this study establishes a foundational framework for further research on HvBAG genes, with potential applications in genetic engineering and molecular breeding strategies to enhance barley’s stress resilience. By uncovering the evolutionary dynamics and stress-related functions of the BAG gene family, this work paves the way for future efforts to develop climate-resilient crop varieties, contributing to sustainable agriculture and global food security.
