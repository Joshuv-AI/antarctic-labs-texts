Regional specialization of upper‐layer inhibitory interneuron organization in the human prefrontal cortex
Authors: Ivan Banovac, Marija Baković, Davor Mayer, Dora Sedmak, Zdravko Petanjek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGABAergic interneurons play a central role in shaping cortical microcircuit function, yet the anatomical organization of major interneuron populations across different subdivisions of the human prefrontal cortex remains poorly characterized. The present study examined the laminar distribution and regional organization of major GABAergic interneuron populations in the dorsal (Brodmann area 9) and ventral (Brodmann area 14r) portions of the human prefrontal cortex. Postmortem human prefrontal cortex tissue from five normotypical adult male brains was analysed using the following molecular markers: calretinin (CR), parvalbumin (PV), calbindin (CB), and somatostatin (SOM). Double‐labelling immunofluorescence was used to assess overlaps between different GABAergic interneuron populations, and RNAscope in situ hybridization was used to confirm their neurotransmitter phenotype. All analysed populations demonstrated a GABAergic molecular phenotype. CR+ neurons constituted the largest interneuron population in both analysed cortical regions and showed marked supragranular enrichment. CB+ neurons were also concentrated in upper cortical layers and frequently co‐expressed SOM. By contrast, PV+ neurons were concentrated in layers III–IV, whereas SOM+ neurons were distributed more uniformly across layers II–VI. The principal regional difference was a significantly higher proportion of CR+ neurons in BA14r compared with BA9 (10.24% vs. 8.21% of all neurons; p = 0.0248), while exploratory analyses additionally indicated a higher CR/PV ratio and a lower PV/SOM ratio. These differences were driven predominantly by variation in supragranular interneuron composition, while infragranular organization remained comparatively similar between the two cortical regions. These findings support region‐specific specialization of inhibitory interneuron organization in the human prefrontal cortex and suggest that differences in supragranular inhibitory circuitry may contribute to the distinct functional architecture of dorsal and ventral prefrontal cortico‐cortical networks.

INTRODUCTION

GABAergic neurons of the cerebral cortex constitute a highly heterogeneous population that plays a crucial role in regulating neuronal network activity and mediating interactions among principal neurons (Skaggs et al., 2007; Somogyi & Klausberger, 2005). Accordingly, the coordination and integration of cortical functions depend not only on the overall proportion of GABAergic neurons, but also on their diversity.

For many years, immunohistochemical detection of the calcium‐binding proteins—calbindin (CB), calretinin (CR), and parvalbumin (PV)—has been widely used to define the principal classes of cortical GABAergic neurons in primates. This classification is based on evidence that more than 80% of cortical GABAergic neurons express one of these three markers, with minimal overlap between them (Burkhalter, 2008; Gonchar et al., 2007; Gonchar & Burkhalter, 1997; Kubota et al., 1994; Tamamaki et al., 2003; Uematsu et al., 2008; van Brederode et al., 1990; Xu et al., 2010; Yan et al., 1995). This framework has, therefore, been widely adopted as a practical and robust approach for defining major interneuron classes in the primate cortex.

Most calbindin‐positive (CB+) neurons co‐express the neuropeptide somatostatin (SOM), which itself is present in a broader neuronal population (Gonchar & Burkhalter, 1997; Kawaguchi & Kubota, 1997; Kubota et al., 1994; Rogers, 1992). This partial overlap highlights a limitation of classical marker‐based classification and underscores the need to consider combined marker expression when defining interneuron subtypes. Approximately one third of SOM+ neurons do not express detectable levels of CB, including neurons expressing neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) and neuropeptide Y (Banovac et al., 2022). For this reason, SOM has been proposed as a more comprehensive marker than CB, as it encompasses a larger subset of cortical GABAergic neurons without overlapping with the other two major subpopulations. Together, PV+, SOM+ and CR+ neurons are estimated to account for more than 90% of all cortical GABAergic neurons in primates (Hladnik et al., 2014).

Despite advances in transcriptomic approaches, immunohistochemistry remains an essential tool in human postmortem studies, since it provides direct insight into neuronal morphology, laminar distribution, and connectivity patterns. At the same time, recent genetic and transcriptomic studies have revealed a remarkable diversity among GABAergic neurons in the human cerebral cortex (BRAIN Initiative Cell Census Network (BICCN), 2021; Bakken et al., 2021; Berg et al., 2021; Scala et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Jorstad, Close, et al., 2023). Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing across multiple cortical areas has demonstrated a largely consistent cellular composition at the level of major subclasses, but also identified rostro‐caudal gradients in the proportions of specific interneuron populations (Jorstad, Close, et al., 2023; Krienen et al., 2023). Similarly, data from rodent and primate studies suggest that the relative representation of major GABAergic subclasses varies across cortical areas (Rudy et al., 2011; Torres‐Gomez et al., 2020).

However, quantitative data on the laminar organization of these interneuron populations in different subdivisions of the human prefrontal cortex (PFC) remain scarce. This limitation is particularly relevant, given the functional and connectivity differences between dorsolateral and orbitomedial regions of the PFC. The dorsolateral PFC is primarily associated with higher‐order cognitive processes, whereas the orbitomedial PFC is more closely related to emotional and social behavior (Fuster, 2015; Ray & Zald, 2012; Zald, 2007). These regions differ substantially in their connectivity patterns, raising the possibility that their interneuron composition and organization may also differ. Understanding these potential differences would be essential for interpreting the functional specialization of prefrontal circuits and their vulnerability in neuropsychiatric disorders.

The aim of the present study was, therefore, to determine whether the composition and laminar distribution of major GABAergic interneuron populations differ between dorsolateral and orbitomedial subdivisions of the human PFC. By combining double‐labelling immunofluorescence and RNAscope in situ hybridization, we sought to identify region‐specific organizational principles of interneuron networks.

METHODS

Brain tissue samples

The brain samples analysed in this study originated from five male human subjects. The age of the subjects at time of death ranged from 29 to 51 years and a postmortem interval ranged from 6 to 11 h (Table S1).

No neuropathological deviations were found in the brain on autopsy, and the subjects had no medical history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. All subjects died without preagonal state. The brain tissue is a part of the Zagreb Neuroembryological Collection (Judaš et al., 2011; Kostovic et al., 1991) and was obtained with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Zagreb University School of Medicine (380–59–10,106–14‐55/152). The brain tissue is coded, and the subjects' identity and medical history are stored in secure records.

The brain tissue was cut into blocks following Talairach's coordinates (Talairach & Szikla, 1980). Tissue blocks of the dorsolateral and orbitomedial PFC containing the superior frontal gyrus (Brodmann area 9, BA9) and straight gyrus (Brodmann area 14r, BA14r), respectively, (Figures S1 and S2) were selected for further processing (Banovac et al., 2020; Brodmann, 1909; Ongür et al., 2003; Ongür & Price, 2000).

The tissue was first fixed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then dehydrated in an ethanol cascade (70%, 96%, 100%), cleared in toluene, embedded in paraffin (Sadeghipour & Babaheidarian, 2019), and finally cut on a microtome into 20‐μm‐thick coronal slices (Sy & Ang, 2019) and mounted on VitroGnost Plus Ultra adhesive microscope slides (BioGnost, Zagreb, Croatia).

Cytoarchitectonic boundaries and cortical lamination of BA9 and BA14r were delineated on Nissl‐stained (see Supplementary material) and NeuN‐immunolabeled sections according to relevant literature (Ongür et al., 2003; Petrides et al., 2012; Petrides & Pandya, 1999).

Double‐labelling immunofluorescence

Double‐labelling immunofluorescence was performed according to previously published protocols for paraffin‐embedded tissue (Zaqout et al., 2020). Histological sections were first photobleached for 48 h using a LED light source (Neumann & Gabel, 2002; Sun et al., 2017) to reduce autofluorescence. The sections were deparaffinized, and heat antigen retrieval was performed in citrate‐based (pH 6.0) unmasking solution (Boenisch, 2005). Protein blocking was done by incubating the sections for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in normal donkey serum (NDS; Chemicon, USA) diluted 1:30 in permeabilization solution (0.3% Triton X‐100 in 1X PBS; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Afterwards, the sections were incubated overnight in primary antibodies (Table S2) at 4°C and in secondary antibodies (Table S2) for 2 h at RT. The sections were treated with TrueBlack® Lipofuscin Autofluorescence Quencher (Biotium, USA) to further reduce autofluorescence (Banovac et al., 2019; Banovac et al., 2025; Prkačin et al., 2024; Ratko et al., 2024) and coverslipped with VectaMount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, USA).

RNAscope in situ hybridization

The expression of mRNA in the tissue samples was evaluated using RNAscope in situ hybridization (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, USA). The RNAscope® Fluorescent Multiplex Assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was used to detect multiple target mRNAs. A standard protocol, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was used (Jolly et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2014). Pretreatment included deparaffinization, target retrieval with Target Retrieval Reagent, and incubation in Protease Plus reagent (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). The slides were then incubated in the appropriate Probe Mix reagent (Table S3) followed by successive amplifier (AMP) reagents (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Depending on the probes used, visualization was done using channel‐appropriate dyes (TSA™ Plus Fluorescein, TSA™ Plus Cyanine 3 System and/or TSA™ Plus Cyanine 5 System; Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA).

After RNAscope in situ hybridization was completed, the same slides were further processed for single labelling immunofluorescence, according to the previously described protocol (starting with the protein blocking step) (Banovac et al., 2022).

Histological sections were imaged using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus FLUOVIEW FV3000RS, Japan) at high magnification using z‐stacks spanning the entire section thickness.

Quantification of immunoreactive cells

Quantification of immunoreactive cells was done using Neurolucida 2020 (MBF Bioscience, Williston, Vermont, USA) and Neurolucida Explorer (MBF Bioscience) on confocal images of double‐labelled immunofluorescent histological slides. For each cortical region from each brain (n = 5), three histological sections were selected using systematic interval sampling, with every tenth serial 20‐μm section included (corresponding to a nominal interval of 200 μm). Values obtained from the three sections were averaged to generate a single subject‐level estimate for each cortical region.

The counting contour in Neurolucida was defined as follows: The width of the contour was 1000 μm at the pial surface, the contour followed the orientation of neurons in cortical columns, and the contour encompassed the entire thickness of the cortex (from pial surface to the white matter). Cortical layers were delineated on the NeuN channel of each analysed section. Marker‐positive profiles were assigned to a cortical layer according to the position of the NeuN‐positive soma relative to these boundaries.

The counting of immunoreactive cells was done using the Detect cells function in Neurolucida 2020 on maximum intensity Z‐stack images. The Detect cells function automatically marks immunoreactive cells based on the intensity difference between the background and signal (defined by the threshold). Other input parameters for the Detect cells function were the maximum and minimum cell diameter as well as the region of interest, that is, the counting contour (only cells fully encompassed by the counting contour are marked).

Two trained investigators (IB and DS) evaluated the cells marked by the Detect cells function and applied manual corrections where necessary. Before the analysis, the detection and inclusion criteria were harmonized between the investigators using representative sections. Ambiguous or borderline profiles were reviewed jointly by two investigators and classified by consensus. The material was not independently recounted by multiple raters, and no formal intra‐rater or inter‐rater reliability coefficient was calculated. The investigators were not blinded to cortical region or marker identity because regional cytoarchitecture remained identifiable on the sections and knowledge of the marker channel was required for the application of marker‐specific signal intensity and morphological criteria. The same predefined criteria were applied consistently to BA9 and BA14r.

It should be noted that the anti‐CB antibody (Rabbit, polyclonal) faintly stains pyramidal neurons in layers II and III, which is typical for anti‐CB staining. Nevertheless, only intensely stained non‐pyramidal cells were counted in order to evaluate the CB interneuron population. The vast majority of pyramidal CB+ cells could be eliminated from the quantification by applying an adjusted threshold value for the Detect cells function, while edge cases were manually evaluated by two researchers based on morphological features.

The results of the quantification are presented as the proportion of immunolabeled cells within the total cortical neuron population (defined as the total number of NeuN‐immunolabeled cells). Since marker‐positive and NeuN+ profiles were counted on the same double‐labelled section and within the same layer‐specific counting contour, the reported values represent within‐contour proportions of marker‐positive neuronal profiles among NeuN+ neuronal profiles rather than estimates of absolute cell density or total cell number. Normalization to NeuN+ profiles within the same section reduces shared effects of sampled area, layer thickness, neuronal packing density, tissue shrinkage, and section‐to‐section variation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism, version 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA).

The paired t‐test was used to test the differences in the proportions of interneuron populations between BA9 and BA14r as well as to test the differences in the proportions of interneurons between supragranular and infragranular layers.

A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all statistical tests. Effect size was reported as partial η 2, and the mean difference and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the mean difference were also provided. Based on previous anatomical evidence, the primary hypothesis of the study was that the proportion of CR+ neurons differs between BA9 and BA14r. The paired regional comparison of the CR+ population was therefore defined as the primary analysis. Comparisons involving PV+, CB+, and SOM+ populations were secondary analyses performed to assess whether the regional difference was specific to CR+ neurons or reflected a broader shift across marker‐defined populations. Analyses of marker ratios and cortical layers were considered exploratory and were used to describe the relative and laminar organization of the examined populations. No multiplicity adjustment was applied to the single primary comparison. Secondary and exploratory analyses are reported with exact unadjusted p values and are not interpreted as independently confirmed findings.

The relative proportions of the main interneuron populations (CR, PV, and CB ∪ SOM) were analysed as composite data and shown using ternary diagrams. Each data point represents the relative composition of interneurons, normalized so that the three categories sum up to 100%. The data dispersion for each layer was visualized using 95% ellipses with centroids (where the ellipses represent the enclosed region expected to contain 95% of data points, and the centroids represent the compositional centre of the data corresponding to the geometric mean of all the variables).

RESULTS

GABAergic profile of marker‐defined cell populations

Systematic qualitative analyses combining RNAscope in situ hybridization with immunolabelling for CR, PV, CB, and SOM were performed to assess the neurotransmitter phenotype of these marker‐defined cell populations. The analysis included one section from BA9 and one section from BA14r from each of the five brains, resulting in a total of 10 systematically examined sections. Because this material was not sufficient for unbiased population‐level quantification, the RNAscope findings are presented as systematic qualitative validation rather than estimates of co‐expression prevalence.

Expression of SLC17A7 and SLC32A1 mRNA in cortical cells

In the first set of experiments, immunolabelling for the interneuron‐associated markers CR, PV, CB, and SOM was combined with RNAscope probes for SLC17A7 and SLC32A1, markers of glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmitter phenotypes, respectively. SLC17A7 mRNA, encoding vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (VGLUT1), showed abundant expression throughout the prefrontal cortex, with a laminar pattern that was readily discernible by visual assessment. The signal appeared strongest in layers III and V, weaker in layer IV, and was virtually absent from layer I. SLC32A1 mRNA, encoding the vesicular GABA transporter (VGAT), showed moderate‐to‐high expression, and appeared more prominent in supragranular than in infragranular layers.

Within the systematically examined sections, SLC17A7 and SLC32A1 signals were detected in separate cellular profiles, and no convincing cellular co‐expression of the two transcripts was observed (Figure 1a). Every CR+, PV+, CB+, and SOM+ cell profile encountered during the systematic survey co‐expressed SLC32A1 mRNA, whereas no co‐localization of these interneuron‐associated markers with SLC17A7 mRNA was observed (Figure 1b). Thus, within the surveyed material, the expression pattern of all four marker‐defined populations was uniformly GABAergic.

Expression of GAD1 and GAD2 mRNA in cortical cells

To provide additional validation of the GABAergic phenotype, immunolabelling for CR, PV, CB, and SOM was combined with probes for GAD1 and GAD2 mRNA, encoding the two glutamate decarboxylase isoforms GAD67 and GAD65, respectively. GAD1 mRNA showed moderate‐to‐high expression, whereas GAD2 mRNA generally showed low‐to‐moderate expression.

Within the systematically surveyed sections, the great majority of CR+, PV+, CB+, and SOM+ cell profiles showed expression of both GAD1 and GAD2. Every marker‐positive cell profile encountered expressed at least one of the two transcripts (Figure 2a–d). GAD1 and GAD2 signals appeared somewhat more prominent in supragranular than in infragranular layers, but expression was observed in all four marker‐defined populations, across all cortical layers and in both analysed Brodmann areas. Together with the SLC32A1/SLC17A7 findings, these observations provide convergent validation of the GABAergic phenotype of the CR+, PV+, CB+, and SOM+ populations in the examined material.

Co‐expression of interneuron populations

Double‐labelling immunofluorescence experiments were performed to assess the degree of overlap between the four major interneuron populations.

Qualitative analysis confirmed that the analysed interneuron populations represent largely distinct and non‐overlapping subsets of cortical interneurons. CR+ and PV+ cells showed almost no co‐expression with other markers, confirming that they form separate interneuron populations. By contrast, there was a substantial degree of overlap between the CB+ and SOM+ cell populations (Banovac et al., 2022), particularly in supragranular layers. Quantitative analysis revealed that almost 68% of CB+ cells co‐expressed SOM in both BA9 and BA14r, while around 50% of SOM+ cells co‐expressed CB. Co‐expression was strongly layer‐dependent: 80% and 72% of SOM+ cells co‐expressed CB in layers II and III, respectively, whereas co‐expression decreased to 8% in layer IV and 3% in layer V. Conversely, 71%, 88%, and 60% of CB+ cells co‐expressed SOM in layers II, III, and IV, respectively. No co‐expression was found in layer VI (for details see Banovac et al., 2022). This overlap defined a larger CB ∪ SOM population with three distinct subpopulations: CB+SOM+, CB+SOM−, and CB−SOM+. This revealed that the four interneuron markers used in this study (CR, PV, CB, and SOM) defined three largely non‐overlapping interneuron populations—CR+ cells, PV+ cells, and CB ∪ SOM cells.

Laminar distribution of interneuron populations in the PFC

Quantitative assessment of individual interneuron populations demonstrated distinct laminar patterns in the examined cortical regions (BA9 and BA14r).

CR+ cells constituted on average 8.21% of the total number of neurons (NeuN+ cells) in BA9 and 10.24% in BA14r. Quantitative analysis of laminar distribution showed that CR+ cells were most abundant in layer I, where they constituted on average 23.24% of all neurons in BA9 and 32.02% in BA14r. CR+ cells were also numerous in layers II (13.26% in BA9 and 15.87% in BA14r) and III (8.95% in BA9 and 11.24% in BA14r), but their proportion progressively decreased with cortical depth, being low in layers IV (2.65% in BA9 and 3.49% in BA14r), V (1.87% in BA9 and 1.89% in BA14r), and VI (1.26% in BA9 and 1.70% in BA14r). CR+ fibers were most prominent in layers I–III and V (Figure 3).

PV+ cells were slightly less abundant than CR+ cells and accounted for 7.14% of all neurons in BA9 and 6.33% in BA14r. PV+ cells were most abundant in layer IV, where they comprised 9.74% of all neurons in BA9 and 10.02% in BA14r. This was followed by layers III (8.96% in BA9 and 8.49% in BA14r), V (7.85% in BA9 and 4.53% in BA14r), VI (4.59% in BA9 and 5.60% in BA14r), and II (2.27% in BA9 and 3.86% in BA14r). Unlike CR+ cells, PV+ cells in layer II were predominantly confined to the deeper portions of layer II and were almost never present in the middle of layer II or toward its border with layer I. Rare NeuN+/PV+ cells were observed within the NeuN‐defined layer I contour and fulfilled the same inclusion criteria as PV+ neurons in other layers. These cells were found in four of the five analysed specimens, but even in those specimens they were not consistently present in every histological section. Their sparse and inconsistent occurrence did not permit further interpretation of their cellular identity or functional significance. PV+ fibres in BA9 were most prominent in the lower part of layer III and in layer IV, followed by the superficial part of layer III and layers II and VI. In BA14r, PV+ fibers were additionally strongly expressed in layer VI and in the deep portion of layer V. In both regions, layer I contained almost no PV+ fibres (Figure 4). Overall, PV+ fibres showed a very high degree of immunoreactivity, forming dense perisomatic plexuses around pyramidal cell bodies, which is consistent with the innervation pattern of basket cells.

Distinctive organization of cortical interneurons within individual layers of the prefrontal cortex

Analysis of interneuron distribution across cortical layers revealed that the structure of the cortical neural network differs substantially between layers. These differences were particularly pronounced between supragranular and infragranular compartments, as well as within the supragranular compartment, where each layer exhibited a unique interneuron composition.

In supragranular layers, the most numerous interneuron population consisted of CR+ cells, representing on average 11.92% of all neurons in BA9 and 15.30% in BA14r. CB+ cells were the second most abundant group, accounting for 6.36% in BA9 and 7.03% in BA14r. Slightly less numerous were PV+ cells, with an average proportion of 6.14% in BA9 and 5.64% in BA14r, and SOM+ cells, with a proportion of 5.52% in BA9 and 6.66% in BA14r.

In infragranular layers, SOM+ cells were the most abundant, accounting for 6.58% of all neurons in BA9 and 7.39% in BA14r. PV+ cells followed with 6.13% in BA9 and 4.76% in BA14r. CB+ cells were sparsely represented in infragranular layers, with 2.05% in BA9 and 2.51% in BA14r, while CR+ cells were the least abundant, comprising only 1.57% in BA9 and 1.82% in BA14r.

The differences between supragranular and infragranular layers in the proportions of CR+ and CB+ cells were statistically significant (p < 0.0001 for both CR and CB; paired t‐test), whereas differences in PV+ and SOM+ cell proportions were not significant (p = 0.5819 for PV and p = 0.4423 for SOM) (Figure 7c). These findings indicate that molecular differences in the interneuron network between supragranular and infragranular compartments are primarily driven by the CR+ and CB+ cells located in supragranular layers.

PV+ and SOM+ cells were the most abundant populations in layer IV, while CR+ and CB+ cells were less numerous.

When examining individual cortical layers, each supragranular layer displayed a distinct ratio of interneuron populations. In layer I, interneurons consisted almost exclusively of CR+ cells, with only very small proportions of PV+, CB+, and SOM+ cells. In layer II, CR+ cells remained the most numerous, but CB+ cells increased substantially in proportion, reaching levels close to those of CR+ cells. SOM+ cells were slightly less numerous than CB+ cells in this layer, whereas PV+ cells were the least represented of all populations. In layer III, CR+ cells again represented the largest proportion on average, accompanied by a substantial number of PV+ cells. SOM+ cells were the third most numerous group, followed by CB+ neurons, which were less abundant in this layer.

Unlike the supragranular compartment, the relative proportions of interneuron populations in layers IV–VI had overlapping distributions. In all three layers, PV+ and SOM+ cells were the most numerous, while CR+ and CB+ cells were very sparsely represented.

Regional differences in interneuron distribution

Ventro‐dorsal differences in interneuron composition

Comparison of Brodmann areas 9 and 14r revealed consistent ventro‐dorsal differences in the organization of cortical interneurons. The proportion of CR+ cells was consistently higher in BA14r than in BA9 across all five brains (10.24% vs. 8.21%), corresponding to a mean paired difference of 2.03 percentage points (95% CI, 0.42–3.63 percentage points; paired t‐test, t(4) = 3.50, p = 0.0248, partial η 2 = 0.754, n = 5 paired brains; Figure 9a). In every brain examined, CR+ cells were more abundant in BA14r, with no overlap in the range of proportions between the two regions in the analysed sample. Other populations showed minor, non‐significant shifts, with SOM+ cells tending to be slightly more numerous in BA14r and PV+ cells slightly more numerous in BA9, while the proportion of CB+ cells was comparable across regions. Layer‐specific analysis confirmed that the increase in CR+ cells in BA14r was driven by small, consistent increases across all layers, most pronounced in supragranular layers.

Differences in interneuron ratios

Analysis of interneuron ratios further highlighted the aforementioned regional differences. The CR/PV ratio was significantly higher in BA14r across all brains, reflecting the combination of increased CR+ and decreased PV+ cell proportions. In contrast, the PV/SOM ratio was consistently lower in BA14r (Figure 9b), due to a slightly lower proportion of PV+ cells and slightly higher proportion of SOM+ cells in this region. Other ratios (CR/CB, CR/SOM) did not differ significantly between regions.

Together, these results identify a key difference in network structure: BA14r is characterized by a higher CR/PV ratio, reflecting a higher proportion of CR+ cells and lower proportion of PV+ cells, with SOM+ cells only moderately contributing to the divergence.

Estimated interneuron contribution

Based on data obtained from CB/SOM double‐labelling, it is possible to estimate the overall proportion of CB ∪ SOM neurons. The correction for CB/SOM overlap was based on the previously published double‐labelling analysis performed in the same five brains and cortical regions (BA9 and BA14r; Banovac et al., 2022). The size of the CB ∪ SOM population was calculated as follows: N(CB ∪ SOM) = N(CB+) + N(SOM+) − N(CB+/SOM+), thereby counting double‐labelled cells only once. Taking into account the three major interneuron populations defined in this study (CR, PV, and CB ∪ SOM), the composition of the analysed cortical interneuron populations in BA9 among NeuN+ cells was as follows: 8.21% CR, 7.14% PV, and 7.60% CB ∪ SOM. The interneuron composition in BA14r was as follows: 10.24% CR, 6.33% PV, and 8.79% CB ∪ SOM.

The estimated total percentage of labelled cells (CR+, PV+, and CB+ or SOM+) was 22.95% in BA9 and 25.36% in BA14r. The estimated total percentage of labelled cells was considerably higher in the supragranular layers, where 24.42% of neurons in BA9 and 27.96% of neurons in BA14r were labelled with at least one of the interneuron markers used. In the infragranular layers, only 16.34% of neurons in BA9 and 16.48% of neurons in BA14r were labelled with any of the interneuron markers.

In general, the proportion of neurons labelled by the selected marker panel within the total NeuN+ population gradually decreased with increasing cortical depth (Figure 9c). This pattern was consistent with the qualitative observation that fewer cells in the infragranular layers expressed SLC32A1 mRNA.

DISCUSSION

The principal finding of the present study is that the dorsal (BA9) and ventral (BA14r) parts of the human PFC differ in the composition and laminar organization of major GABAergic interneuron populations. BA14r exhibited a significantly higher proportion of CR+ neurons than BA9. In addition, the relative organization of interneuron populations differed substantially between the two cortical regions, as reflected by significant differences in the CR/PV and PV/SOM ratios.

These regional differences were primarily attributable to differences in the organization of supragranular cortical layers. In both analysed regions, CR+ neurons represented the most abundant interneuron population in the upper cortical layers (I–III), but their predominance was particularly pronounced in BA14r. By contrast, infragranular layers (V‐VI) showed a considerably more uniform distribution of all the analysed interneuron populations.

In this study, we identified major cortical interneuron populations using established molecular markers expressed in neuronal somata and proximal dendrites (CR, PV, CB, and SOM). Previous immunohistochemical and in situ hybridization studies have shown that these markers encompass the vast majority of cortical GABAergic neurons in primates (Burkhalter, 2008; Gonchar & Burkhalter, 1997; Kawaguchi & Kubota, 1997; Kubota et al., 1994; Rogers, 1992; Tamamaki et al., 2003; Uematsu et al., 2008; van Brederode et al., 1990; Xu et al., 2010; Yan et al., 1995).

We confirmed previous findings that, in primates, CR+ and PV+ neurons showed minimal overlap between themselves and with the other two major populations, whereas CB+ neurons exhibited substantial co‐expression with the SOM+ population, particularly in supragranular layers (Banovac et al., 2022; Marín, 2025). A relatively small but distinct population of CB+/SOM− GABAergic neurons was identified, primarily in infragranular layers. These findings support the interpretation that the selected marker panel successfully identified the principal GABAergic interneuron classes relevant for large‐scale comparative analysis of cortical inhibitory microcircuitry.

The proportion of labelled neurons identified in the present study accounted for approximately one quarter of the total NeuN+ population, consistent with previous quantitative immunohistochemical studies in primates (Medalla et al., 2023). Recent transcriptomic studies in the human cortex generally estimate that GABAergic neurons constitute up to one third of all cortical neurons (Bakken et al., 2021; Emani et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2022; Hawrylycz et al., 2012; Johansen et al., 2023; Jorstad, Close, et al., 2023; Jorstad, Song, et al., 2023; Krienen et al., 2020; Loomba et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022; van Velthoven et al., 2025), suggesting that some interneuron populations may not have been accounted for by the marker panel analysed in this study.

The neuronal populations not included in the present analysis most likely comprise smaller molecular subclasses (Hodge et al., 2019; Rudy et al., 2011). Current genomic and transcriptomic classifications suggest that the principal populations potentially underrepresented in the present study could be LAMP5‐expressing neurons (Krienen et al., 2020; Schuman et al., 2019) and CR−/VIP+ neurons (Prönneke et al., 2015). These subclasses are unlikely to substantially alter the principal regional comparisons reported here, which were driven primarily by shifts in the dominant interneuron populations.

Taken together, these considerations suggest that although the present immunohistochemical approach does not fully recapitulate the molecular complexity revealed by transcriptomic classification, it provides a sufficiently robust framework for comparative analysis of the major organizational principles of human cortical inhibitory circuitry.

The observed differences in interneuron composition between BA9 and BA14r likely reflect differences in the functional organization of local cortical microcircuits. Notably, the observed regional specialization was driven predominantly by differences in supragranular interneuron composition, suggesting that associative cortico‐cortical processing rather than deeper‐layer output‐related circuitry may be an important anatomical substrate of regional inhibitory specialization in the human PFC. Given the distinct physiological roles of these interneuron populations, such differences are likely to affect local circuit dynamics and may mirror broader functional specialization of cortico‐cortical circuitry within the PFC (Hunt et al., 2023; Krienen et al., 2016; Matho et al., 2021; Petanjek et al., 2008; Petanjek et al., 2011; Petanjek et al., 2023; Sedmak et al., 2018).

PV+ interneurons provide fast perisomatic inhibition of pyramidal neurons and are essential for synchronizing cortical activity and controlling spike timing (Rudy et al., 2011; Tremblay et al., 2016). We hypothesize that the higher PV/SOM and PV/CR ratios in BA9 could reflect a greater need for precise regulation of pyramidal neuron output required for executive functions mediated by the dorsolateral PFC (Fuster, 2015). This is consistent with the distinctive microarchitecture of BA9, characterized by expanded upper cortical layers, a higher proportion of cortico‐cortical projection neurons (Barbas & García‐Cabezas, 2016; Berg et al., 2021; Groenewegen & Uylings, 2000; Kolk & Rakic, 2022; Teffer & Semendeferi, 2012) and abundant large layer IIIc pyramidal neurons that likely represent major targets of PV innervation (Kim et al., 2023; Ongür & Price, 2000; Prkačin et al., 2024; Rajkowska & Goldman‐Rakic, 1995).

By contrast, CR+ interneurons primarily inhibit other interneurons and promote disinhibitory circuit motifs (Dellal et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2015; Loomba et al., 2022; Pfeffer et al., 2013). Their greater abundance in BA14r suggests that disinhibitory microcircuits may play a more prominent role in ventral PFC functions, including emotional, social, and autonomic processing (Fuster, 2015).

SOM+ interneurons regulate excitatory input by targeting distal dendrites of pyramidal neurons (Urban‐Ciecko & Barth, 2016). Although their proportion did not differ significantly between regions, the altered PV/SOM ratio indicates regional differences in the balance between perisomatic and dendritic inhibition.

Although evolutionary interpretations should remain cautious, these findings suggest that regional specialization of inhibitory circuitry in the primate PFC reflects both conserved mammalian organizational principles and lineage‐specific adaptations in interneuron composition. While PV+ and SOM+ interneurons represent highly conserved cortical classes across many mammalian species (Hodge et al., 2019; Kawaguchi & Kubota, 1998; Lee et al., 2023), primates are characterized by an expanded CR+ interneuron population, particularly in the association cortex (Boroujeni et al., 2026; Hladnik et al., 2014; Medalla et al., 2023). The predominance of CR+ neurons in both regions of the human PFC examined in this study, together with regional differences in the PV/SOM ratio, resembles patterns reported in the associative cortex of rodents and marmosets (Krienen et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2010). This suggests that primate inhibitory microcircuits combine conserved organizational features with evolutionary specializations accompanying the increased complexity of higher‐order cognition and behavioral regulation (Marín, 2025; Rudy et al., 2011).

The developmental basis of these regional differences remains unclear but may involve a greater contribution of late‐generated CR+ interneuron populations in BA14r. While PV+ and SOM+ interneurons are largely derived from the medial ganglionic eminence (Hansen et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2022), CR+ interneurons exhibit greater developmental diversity in primates, arising predominantly from the caudal ganglionic eminence (Alzu'bi et al., 2017; Alzu'bi & Clowry, 2020; Feng et al., 2025; Hladnik et al., 2014; Llorca & Deogracias, 2022; Miyoshi et al., 2010; Reinchisi et al., 2012) with additional progenitor sources also proposed (Al‐Jaberi et al., 2015; Alzu'bi & Clowry, 2019; Berg et al., 2021; Hladnik et al., 2014; Jakovcevski et al., 2011; Letinic et al., 2002; Letinic & Rakic, 2001; Molnár et al., 2019; Petanjek et al., 2008; Petanjek, Berger, & Esclapez, 2009; Petanjek, Kostović, & Esclapez, 2009; Radonjić, Ayoub, et al., 2014; Radonjić, Ortega, et al., 2014; Yu & Zecevic, 2011).

In primates, prolonged neurogenesis and expanded neuronal migration may contribute to the increased abundance of CR+ interneurons, particularly in the associative cortex. One potential developmental mechanism is the medial migratory stream described in the infant human brain, which contains CR+ neuroblasts and targets ventromedial and adjacent frontal cortical regions (Feliciano & Bordey, 2013; Grangeray Vilmint & Lelievre, 2012; Nascimento et al., 2024; Paredes et al., 2016; Sanai et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2011), which may contribute to the higher proportion of CR+ neurons observed in BA14r (Schmitz et al., 2022).

The regional specialization of inhibitory circuitry observed in this study may also influence the selective vulnerability of prefrontal networks in neuropsychiatric disorders. The dysfunction of GABAergic interneurons is a hallmark of disorders such as schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, major depressive disorder, and bipolar disorder (Batiuk et al., 2022; Petanjek et al., 2019), all of which prominently affect prefrontal circuits. The distinct interneuron composition of the dorsal and ventral PFC suggests that these regions may differ not only in physiological function but also in their susceptibility to pathological disruption.

The principal limitations of this study are the sample size, the inclusion of male brains only, and the opportunistic nature of the postmortem material, which was obtained from a geographically restricted population. These factors limit the generalizability of the findings beyond the analysed sample. Nevertheless, the paired within‐brain design and the consistent direction of the BA14r–BA9 difference in CR+ neuronal proportions across all five brains support the internal consistency of the principal observation.

In conclusion, BA9 and BA14r differed in the composition and laminar organization of major marker‐defined GABAergic interneuron populations. The most robust regional difference was the consistently higher proportion of CR+ neurons in BA14r across all five analysed brains, primarily reflecting differences in supragranular layers, whereas the other individual marker‐defined populations showed no significant regional differences. Exploratory analyses of interneuron ratios further indicated differences in the relative organization of inhibitory neuronal populations between the two regions. Although the present study was not designed to investigate pathological tissue, the identification of region‐specific inhibitory circuit architectures may help interpret area‐selective abnormalities reported in neuropsychiatric disorders. Taken together, our findings provide evidence for regional specialization of upper‐layer inhibitory interneuron composition in the human PFC and establish an anatomical basis for future studies of the functional differences between dorsal and ventral prefrontal networks and their differential involvement in disease.
