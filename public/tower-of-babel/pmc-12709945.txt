Preparation and characterization of micellar nanoparticles using crude saponins from five Congolese plant species
Authors: Pathy B. Lokole, Nadège K. Ngombe, Dave I. Motomba, Justin B. Safari, Michel K. Mpuza, Rui W.M. Krause, Paulin K. Mutwale, Christian I. Nkanga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nanoparticles (NPs) have significantly advanced medical applications, including drug delivery, immunotherapy, vaccines, and diagnostics. This versatility is partly due to the potential of tailoring NPs from multiple sources. Notably, saponins, amphiphilic plant metabolites, have shown great promise in NP formulation. This study explored the development of micellar NPs using saponin crude fractions (SCFs) extracted from five Congolese plant species: Millettia laurentii, Penthaclethra eetveldeana, Schwenckia americana, Musa paradisiaca, and Musa sapientum. Plant materials were subjected to histological examination through optical microscopy, while phytochemical analyses by thin-layer chromatography confirmed the presence and predominance of saponins in the SCFs. We used a phthalocyanine-isoniazid hybrid (Pc-INH) as a hydrophobic probe to determine the critical micellar concentrations of SCFs and explore the feasibility of developing cost-effective saponin-based micelles (SBMs). Phytochemical screenings indicated saponins in the extracted SCF and other metabolites like flavonoids, phenolic acids, and anthocyanins. Dynamic light scattering and transmission electron microscopy analyses revealed the formation of nano-sized particles, particularly noting SBMs from P. eetveldeana with notable dimensions (157 ​nm, PDI of 0.27, and ZP of −4.01 ​mV) and spherical shape. The micelles from M. laurentii exhibited superior encapsulation efficiency for Pc-INH (55%) compared to control micelles formulated from pure saponin (33%). In vitro tests showed that M. paradisiaca SBMs have the best safety profile for red blood cells, with a 10% hemolysis rate compared to a 150% rate for bulk SCFs. However, there is a significant difference between SCFs and SBMs (p ​< ​0.0001). The release profiles of M. paradisiaca SBMs show a pH-dependent relationship, suggesting potential for stimuli-responsive drug delivery. This work lays the foundation for leveraging plant-derived crude saponins in nanotechnology, emphazising their encapsulation efficiency, controlled release potential, and biocompatibility, paving the way for the cost-effective production of high-value biomedical NPs.

Introduction

Approximately 40% of approved oral drugs and nearly 90% of the developmental drugs are poorly soluble [1], making the solubility of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) one of the major bottlenecks in drug product development. The poor solubility of drugs directly affects oral bioavailability [2]. Administered drugs distributed throughout the bloodstream exhibit a high affinity for plasma proteins [3], leading to low blood concentrations necessary for effective treatment. This indirectly contributes to therapeutic failure and can result in drug resistance. These parameters are considered when developing new therapeutic agents. However, it is notable that 40% of new chemical entities in development are hydrophobic [4], resulting in very low bioavailability [5].

Consequently, it is increasingly important to regulate the aqueous solubility of bioactive compounds, improve their receptor targeting specificity, and manage the conditions of their biodistribution. Nanotechnology has opened exciting new prospects across biology, chemistry, engineering, and medicine [6], offering innovative avenues for disease diagnosis and treatment, particularly when using nanoparticles (NPs) as drug delivery systems (DDS) [7]. Numerous nano-DDS, such as liposomes, nanostructured lipid carriers, solid lipid NPs, nanoemulsions, and micelles, are under investigation as drug carriers [6]. Micelles have garnered significant attention due to their ease of formulation from various amphiphilic compounds [8]. Amphiphilic compounds undergo facile self-assembly, resulting in aggregates characterized by a hydrophilic shell and a hydrophobic core, with the latter suited to encapsulating hydrophobic molecules [9]. Micelles have demonstrated their efficacy in encapsulating therapeutic, diagnostic, and theranostic compounds [10].

Various amphiphilic compounds have been used to formulate micelles, including polymers or copolymers such as polyethylene oxide and polyethylene glycol-polylactic acid [11]. Recently, saponins, a class of natural products, have emerged as crucial raw materials for the formulation of micellar NPs with significant biomedical potential [12]. Saponins possess an amphiphilic structure comprising a lipophilic moiety, typically either steroid or triterpenoid, to which one or more different osidic clusters are attached, rendering them hydrophilic [13,14].

Saponins have been demonstrated in several applications, from sustainable drug delivery [15] to enhanced aqueous solubility of hydrophobic drugs [16]. The application of saponin-based nanoparticles has been particularly prominent in theranostic applications, where therapy and diagnosis (or sometimes tracking drug delivery) occur simultaneously [17].

Despite the benefits, commercial micelle formulation using pure saponins remains a significant challenge, particularly in low-income countries. To address this challenge, we have embarked on a journey to identify suitable alternative materials, particularly by leveraging natural saponins as a promising solution in drug delivery formulations.

The use of saponins in biomedical NP formulations is commendable, particularly given research indicating that saponin-based micelles (SBM) possess the potential to permeate cellular membranes reversibly, facilitating intracellular delivery [12]. This advancement is significant, considering the persistent challenge of intracellular delivery in drug development, underscoring SBM's importance for diverse biomedical applications [18]. However, using highly purified saponins contributes to the costly formulation status associated with nanotechnologies, including SBM. Producing purified ingredients is expensive and significantly limits the application of SBMs to underserved populations. To address this issue and promote equitable access to nanotechnologies, efforts should focus on developing cost-effective formulations that leverage readily available resources and streamline production processes.

A feasible approach involves the extraction of saponin crude fractions (SCFs) for NP formulation assessment. This strategy holds great promise in exploring the rich biodiversity of saponin-containing plants or valorization of agricultural waste. It offers a viable alternative to pure saponins, thereby fostering advancements in biomedical applications.

It is essential to acknowledge that commercially available pure saponins are well studied and exhibit established toxicity to red blood cells (RBCs) [13]. Therefore, before endorsing SCFs as raw materials for micelle formulation, it is imperative to assess their hemato-toxicity, both as crude fractions and as SBMs. This evaluation helps to determine whether secondary metabolites present in the raw extracts contribute to toxicity or mitigate saponin toxicity and whether saponin toxicity diminishes when self-assembled into NPs. Such assessment aligns with the objectives of this work, which seeks to harness the untapped natural resources of the Democratic Republic of the Congo to address public health challenges.

In this study, we investigated SCFs derived from five Congolese plant species—Millettia laurentii, Musa paradisiaca, Musa sapientum, (also known as M. acuminata) Penthaclethra eetveldeana, and Schwenckia americana—as potential cost-effective raw materials for formulating micellar NPs (denoted saponin-based micelles). The extracted SCFs underwent screening to evaluate their phytochemical and hemocompatibility profiles, as well as self-assembly potential through the determination of the critical micellar concentration, nanoencapsulation efficiency, and capacity to deliver hydrophobic compounds, with phthalocyanine-isoniazid hybrid serving as the model hydrophobic drug that has potential to be used in nanotheranostic applications [19]. Overall, this study introduced SCFs as raw materials for the cost-effective nano-encapsulation of theranostic agents. By comparing SCFs with their saponin-based micelle (SBM) counterparts, this research highlights how nanoparticulate formulation can mitigate the intrinsic hemolytic effects of saponins. This finding establishes the biocompatibility of SBMs, positioning them as an affordable nanotechnology for biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Methanol, ethyl acetate, n-butanol, and diethyl ether were sourced from Merck (Germany), while formic acid, acetic acid, dimethylsulfoxide, acetone, toluene, and dichloromethane were procured from VWR (USA). Deionized water was obtained using a Milli-Q apparatus (Millipore, USA). Pure saponin from Quillaja saponin (Quillaja saponaria) was obtained from Alfa Aesar (USA). TLC plates (Silica Gel 60 F254) were purchased from Merck (Germany). As chemical standards for phytochemical screening, quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin (flavonoids standards), rosmarinic acid, ferulic acid, and gallic acid (phenolic acids standards), thymol, and menthol (terpenes standards) were used, all of them purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA). Isoniazid-conjugated-phthalocyanine (Pc-INH) was synthesized by our team, as previously reported [20].

Plant materials

Plant materials used in this study (Fig. 1) include the trunk bark of Millettia laurentii De Wild, stem bark of Penthaclethra eetveldeana De Wild T., and Schwenckia americana D. Roxen ex L. harvested in an urban grove near the Monastère Arbre de vie (4°27′59.7″S 15°16′06.9″E) and leaves of Musa paradisiaca L. and Musa sapientum L. in a domestic banana plantation (4°27′58.4″S 15°14′23.8″E), Mont-Ngafula township (Kinshasa, DR Congo); the plant materials were collected in December 2022. The taxonomical identity of M. laurentii, P. eetveldeana, and S. americana was confirmed by Mr. Boniface Nlandu of the Institut National d’Etudes et des Recherches en Agronomie (INERA) in Kinshasa (DR Congo) with following voucher numbers: No. PL2, No. PL3 and No. PL4, respectively, and Professor Félicien Lukoki and Dr Blaise Bikandu from the herbarium of the Faculty of Sciences and Technology, University of Kinshasa, Kinshasa (DR Congo) confirmed the taxonomical identity of M. paradisiaca and M. sapientum with voucher number of No. 001/2022 and No. 002/2022.Fig. 1Photographs of plant species investigated in this study: (A) M. paradisiaca, (B) M. sapientum, (C) M. laurentii, (D) P. eetveldeana (young plant) and (E) S. americana.Fig. 1

Plant materials were rinsed with deionized water and air dried for two weeks at room temperature, then in the oven at 40°C with an LBX OVF force air drying oven (LBX instruments, France) for a week. The dried materials were ground with an auto crusher (Cytotec 1093 sample mill poss tecator, Sweden), and the obtained powders were stored under ambient conditions for further studies.

Biological sample

The blood pouch (ORh+) was purchased from the blood bank at the transfusion center of the Hôpital General de Référence de Matete Omeco (Kinshasa, DR Congo). This protocol was approved by the ethical committee of the School of Public Health of the University of Kinshasa (Kinshasa, DR Congo).

Botanical and phytochemical screening

Microscopic analysis of plant material

The observation of tissue characteristics of plant material was done by optical microscopy VisiScope BL124 (VWR) using the European Pharmacopoeia reagent and lactic acid reagent (Steinmetz reagent) as previously described by Elaka et al [21]. Micrographs were generated using an iPhone XS Max (iOS 16.6).

Identification and extraction of saponin crude fractions

Before SCF extraction, the presence of saponins in different plant materials was screened by foam index testing, as previously reported [[22], [23], [24]]. Briefly, 2 ​g plant materials were boiled in 100 ​mL of deionized water for 30 ​min. After cooling to room temperature, the volume was readjusted to 100 ​mL with deionized water, and the mixture was filtered on cottonwool followed by filter paper Whatman grade 1 with a pore size of 11 ​μm ​(GE Healthcare, USA) to produce the stock liquid extract. Increasing volumes (from 2 to 10 ​mL) of the stock extract were placed in test tubes and diluted accordingly to 10 ​mL with deionized water. Each mixture was hand-shaken for 15 ​s to produce a foam and allowed to stand steadily for 15 ​min at room temperature. Subsequently, the height of the foam was measured, and the presence of saponin was confirmed by the persistence of the foam height > 1 ​cm.

To extract saponins, 100 ​g of each powder was macerated in 300 ​mL of methanol 80% (20% water) for 48 ​h. The mixture was then filtered through cottonwool and then by filter paper Whatman grade 1 with a pore size of 11 ​μm, and the methanolic extract was subjected to concentration by boiling for 1 ​h and filtered on a Büchner funnel under vacuum. The resultant filtrate was dried in the oven for 3 days at 45°C. The dried product was recovered with pure methanol and filtered through Whatman filter paper grade 1 (pore size: 11 ​μm). The filtrate (130 ​mL) was combined with 390 ​mL of diethyl ether and filtered using a Büchner funnel to obtain the SCF, stored at room temperature in a glass vial tightly closed, away from humidity, for further studies. The extraction ratio of Saponin Crude Fractions (SCF) by plant materials was calculated as follows: WSCFWPMP×100%, with WSCF representing the weight of SCF and WPMP representing the weight of plant material powder). Phytochemicals were screened by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).

TLC was performed to investigate the presence of various phytochemicals—namely saponins, flavonoids, phenolic acids, anthocyanins, coumarins, and terpenoids—in different samples, including total extracts (obtained from methanol and ethyl acetate infusions), the SCFs and SBMs and performed as described by Elaka et al [21]. To analyse phytochemicals in the plant materials, 1 ​g of the powdered sample was extracted using 5 ​mL of methanol to target polar phytochemicals (A), followed by 5 ​mL of ethyl acetate for non-polar phytochemicals (B).

These mixtures were macerated for 24 ​h at room temperature, followed by 30 ​min of ultrasonication. The resulting solutions were filtered through the Whatman grade 1 filter paper. Subsequently, 10 ​μL of each extract was applied onto TLC plates. The elution process continued until the solvent front reached a predetermined line. For the phytochemical analysis of both SCFs and total extracts (of methanol and ethyl acetate), the mobile phases for the screening of (i) saponins (i) was the mixture of n-butanol-water-acetic acid (21:3.5:1.75; v/v/v); (ii) flavonoids was ethyl acetate-formic acid-acetic acid glacial-water (100:11:11:26; v/v/v/v); (iii) phenolic acids was dichloromethane-formic acid-acetic acid (80:10:20; v/v/v); (iv) anthocyanins was ethyl acetate-formic acid-water (100:10:10); (v) coumarins was toluene-diethyl ether saturated with acetic acid 10% (1:1, v/v); and (vi) terpenoids toluene-ethyl acetate (93:7). The standard reference compounds used included (i) alpha-hederin, (ii) chlorogenic acid, (iii) rutin, (iv) ferulic acid, (v) D-catechin, (vi) thymol and (vii) menthol for the above mentioned secondary metabolites respectively. An appropriate revealing agent was used to detect the phytochemical spots, as detailed by Kabamba et al [25]. Observations were made under a UV lamp at wavelengths of 250 ​nm or 366 ​nm.

Preparation of SBM by self-assembly method

Determination of critical micellar concentration (CMC)

To assess the CMC of each SCF, the dye isoniazid-conjugated-phthalocyanine (Pc-INH) was used as a tag due to its limited solubility in water, aiming to identify the concentration at which micellar NPs form. Increasing amounts of SCF from each plant material, as well as pure saponin (as a control), were blended with 2 ​mg of Pc-INH in 5 ​mL of deionized water, the SCF at concentrations corresponding to 1 ​mg/mL, 2 ​mg/mL, 3 ​mg/mL, 4 ​mg/mL and 5 ​mg/mL was poured into each 25 ​mL beaker. The mixtures were stirred at 500 ​rpm for 30 ​min at 55°C, followed by centrifugation at 4000 ​rpm for 45 min at 4°C. The supernatant was analyzed by UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Jenway 741501, USA) at 681 ​nm, which is the maximum absorption wavelength for Pc-INH, which concentrations were determined using a stock solution in DMSO, dilutions, and spectrophotometry UV–Vis analysis. A standard calibration curve was generated, and the concentration was determined using the equation y ​= ​0.003x-0.001, R2 ​= ​0.993.

Preparation of saponin-based micelles (SBMs)

For each SCF, 100 ​mg was dissolved in 15 ​mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 containing 2 ​mg of Pc-INH. The reaction medium was mixed at 500 ​rpm under heating at 55oC for 30 ​min. The mixture was centrifuged at 4000 ​rpm for 45 ​min at 4°C (Hettich zentrifugen, Germany) to pellet down undissolved particles of Pc-INH. The supernatant potentially containing nano-sized SBM was then carefully collected for further analysis. A blank solution containing 2 ​mg of Pc-INH in 15 ​mL of PBS was treated in the same conditions below. This solution was considered the control formulation.

Characterization of SBM

Nanoparticle size and zeta potential

The particle size and zeta potential of the samples were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano ZEN-3600 MAL1043132 (Malvern Instruments, United Kingdom). Measurements were performed at a scattering angle of 173°, using an ordinary cuvette for particle size determination and a capillary cuvette for zeta potential measurements. The Malvern Zetasizer Software v7.12 facilitated data processing and analysis. Results are presented as mean ​± ​SD based on triplicate measurements.

Nanoparticle morphology

The morphology of the micellar NPs was investigated through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a Zeiss Libra-120 KV TEM (Germany). The NPs suspensions were analyzed using particle size and Zeta potential measurements for TEM analysis. A drop of each suspension was placed onto a copper grid for sample preparation, and excess solvent was removed with filter paper. The samples were allowed to dry on the grids for over 48 ​h at room temperature. Data from TEM imaging was analyzed using the software ImageJ version 1.39a/Java 1.8.0_172 (64-bit) and Origin version 9.0.0 (64-bit) SR2 b87.

Assessment of the hemato-toxicity of saponin crude fractions versus nanoparticulate saponins

The hemato-toxicity was assessed according to the method previously reported by Yakubogullari et al [26]. Briefly, 5 ​mL of the blood was washed four times with 0.9% saline solution by centrifugation at 2000 ​rpm for 5 ​min at room temperature. The plasma supernatant was discarded. The remaining erythrocyte pellet was resuspended in saline to create a 1% erythrocyte suspension. This solution was then mixed in a 1:1 ​vol ratio with saline solutions containing SCF at concentrations of 25 ​μg/mL, 50 ​μg/mL, and 100 ​μg/mL, and SBM at concentrations of 100 ​μg/mL, 200 ​μg/mL, and 300 ​μg/mL for analysis. The experiment included a negative control of 0.9% physiological saline solution (C0%) and a positive control of deionized water (C100%). Each mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 ​min and centrifuged at 1000 ​rpm for 10 ​min at room temperature. The absorbance of haemoglobin (Hb) from released lysed red blood cells (RBCs) was measured at 540 ​nm. The percentage of Hb released (H%) was calculated using the following formula to quantify hemolytic activity: H%=As−Ac0%Ac100%−AC0%×100; where AC100% represents the absorbance of the positive control (100%), As denotes the absorbance of the sample, and AC0% is the absorbance of the negative control (0%).

Evaluation of encapsulation efficiency (%EE) and drug loading capacity (%DLC)

Following the centrifugation step in section 2.5.2, the supernatant was subject to further centrifugation at 13,500 ​rpm for 1 ​h at room temperature to pellet down Pc-INH loaded in SBMs and discard any free Pc-INH molecules dissolved in the aqueous liquid mass; blank SBMs have been prepared under the same conditions, the obtained pellets of free SBMs have been dried in the oven for 24 ​h at 40°C. The pellets of Pc-INH solubilized in 5 ​mL of DMSO were then subjected to absorbance measurement at 681 ​nm. The %EE was calculated using the equation %EE=At−AsAt×100, where At represents the theoretical absorbance, assuming complete encapsulation measured before high-speed centrifugation described below, and As denotes the absorbance of the sample encapsulated and measured after high-speed centrifugation; on the other side, the drug loading capacity (%DLC) was calculated using the following equation %DLC ​= ​Wt−WfWm×100, where wt stands for the total weight of Pc-INH used for the encapsulation study, wf stands for the weight of free (unencapsulated) Pc-INH, and wm represents the weight of the dried blank micelles [19,27].

Study of cumulative release of Pc-INH from SBMs

The release study was performed according to the protocol previously reported [28] and focused on the release behaviors in various pH environments: 0.1 ​M acetate buffer (pH 4.5), PBS (pH 7.4), and PBS (pH 9.2) at 37°C. SBMs from M. paradisiaca's SCFs were tested for release kinetics based on their high %EE. After centrifuging at 13500 ​rpm, the resulting SBM pellet was dissolved in the appropriate release medium and placed within a dialysis bag (Membra-Cel MD 10–14 ​× ​100 CLR, Sigma Aldrich). Each filled dialysis bag was then immersed in 20 ​mL of each respective release medium, each containing 1% v/v Tween 80 to aid in the solubilization of the hydrophobic cargo molecules within the hydrophilic release environment, as recommended [29]. The system was incubated, and aliquots were sampled at various intervals: after 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, 15, and 18 ​h. To maintain the volume constant, the same quantity of the withdrawn medium was replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. The concentrations of the released Pc-INH were determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 681 ​nm, using a standard calibration curve, as described earlier. Drug release data were processed using OriginLab 9.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data were represented as the average ​± ​SD; error bars represent the SD of the mean. Graphical data were processed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism v 10.2.0 (335) (GraphPad Software). Comparative analysis was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Šídák's multiple comparison test. Asterisks in the figures indicate significant differences between groups (saponin crude fractions vs nanoparticulate saponins) (∗p ​< ​0.05; ∗∗p ​< ​0.01; ∗∗∗p ​< ​0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < ​0.0001) and hashtag indicate differences between a study group and the control (#p < ​0.05; ##p ​< ​0.01; ####p ​< ​0.001 and ####p ​< ​0.0001) and ns for no significant difference for each respective analysis.

Results and discussion

Microscopic screening of plant materials

Botanical microscopic features were determined to establish the microscopic identity of plant materials used in this study, and setting the stage for standardizing plant materials supply to ensure protection against adulterated plant material [21]. In this study, optical microscopy was used to investigate the anatomical features of each plant material visually, and the resultant data was used to confirm the plant's identity based on the literature information [30]. As shown in Fig. 2, microscopic analysis revealed the characteristic presence of cork fragments in the trunk bark of Millettia laurentii, while vessels and stomata were observed in the leaves of Musa paradisiaca and Musa sapientum, and the observation of the stem bark of Penthaclethra eetveldeana depicted crystalline fibers. Profiling of micellar nanoparticles from SCF

Exploration of SCF extracted

SCF was extracted using the diethyl ether precipitation method previously reported [31]. The extraction capacities of saponins were determined by comparing the mass of dried SCF versus 100 ​g of dried plant materials. Saponins were found in comparable quantities in Musa spp., with a relatively higher concentration in M. paradisiaca (1.74%) than M. sapientum (1.06%). For M. laurentii, P. eetveldeana, and S. americana, the respective extraction capacities were found to be 0.4%, 4.9%, and 1.86%, respectively. The discrepancies in total saponin extracts provide valuable insights into the saponin abundance in each plant, highlighting the potential for targeted extraction and utilization based on saponin content.

Phytochemical screening of SCF

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was utilized as a qualitative analytical method to assess the phytochemical composition of the SCF examined in this investigation. These fractions were chromatographed both as is (as raw materials) and as micellar NPs. According to the findings illustrated in Fig. 3, the TLC analysis consistently detected a significant presence of saponins across all types of samples, whether they were raw saponin in methanolic extracts for M. paradisiaca and M. sapientum exclusively (MpME and MsME) or micellar saponins considered as aggregated saponins from M. paradisiaca and M. sapientum (ASMp and ASMs). A comparison of the saponins TLC profiles of total methanolic extracts from plant materials (M. paradisiaca and M. sapientum) with those of the SCF revealed a good alignment in the saponin signatures across the extracts and fraction samples (except for the micellar samples, which showed different patterns, likely due to molecular self-assembly leading to slower migration). This indicates that these phytochemical groups may have been incorporated into saponin particles during precipitation. The phytochemical profiles of plant materials’ total extracts (Table 1) observed are consistent with previous research findings, where the identified phytochemical groups were reported among the secondary metabolites found in the leaves of Musa species [32], Millettia laurentii [33,34], and Penthaclethra eetveldeana [31,35], suggesting a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds that could potentially contribute to the therapeutic efficacy of the extracted SCF. Indeed, the hemato-toxicity results (vide infra) are promising in this respect.

Critical micellar concentrations

Saponins can self-assemble into micelles in aqueous environments, provided the saponin monomers reach a specific concentration threshold. This threshold is referred to as the CMC [36,37]. The CMC is a pivotal parameter that indicates the lowest concentration of saponins at which micelles begin to form in solution [38]. To track the self-assembly of SCFs into SBMs, as shown in Scheme 1, the potential of saponin molecules to engage in interactions with Pc-INH through their hydrophobic moieties (aglycone) was assessed by increasing amounts of SCFs combined in the presence of a fixed amount of Pc-INH.Scheme 1Flowchart depicting the potential of saponin crude fractions from banana plant leaves to self-assemble into saponin-based micelles after the critical micellar concentration is reached. Pc-INH was selected as the dye and drug model for this study due to its notable hydrophobicity and complex structure [20], which closely approximates the aglycone moiety of saponins. To ascertain the CMC of SCFs, the aqueous solubility of Pc-INH served as an indicator for micelle formation, with UV–vis spectroscopy at 681 ​nm facilitating the measurement process.

This method involved recording the progressive increase in absorbance of Pc-INH correlating with rising concentrations of SCFs. A significant shift in the absorbance pattern of Pc-INH was observed upon reaching the CMC, marking the transition from dispersed saponin crude fractions to micellar aggregates. These aggregates play a crucial role in enhancing the solubilization and signaling potentials of phthalocyanine molecules as described by Setaro et al [39] who showed the potential of block copolymer-based micelles to encapsulate efficiently silicon phthalocyanine (SiPc), which can be used as a photosensitizer in photodynamic therapy, another study from Motlung et al presented the successful encapsulation of phenyldiazenyl phenoxy substituted phthalocyanines—indium phthalocyanine (InPc), and zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc)) in Pluronic F127-based micelles as biocompatible nanocarriers, they showed that the resultant nanocarriers improve the aqueous solubility of InPc and ZnPc [40]The observed alteration in absorbance effectively signifies the formation of micelles, thereby offering a measurable approach to determine the CMC. As shown in Fig. 4, the findings indicated that SCFs from different plant species were able to successfully increase the aqueous solubility of Pc-INH and form micelles at low saponin concentrations, starting from 1 ​mg/mL, except for SCFs from M. sapientum and S. americana. Nevertheless, these results overall underscore the SCFs' potential to form stable micelles for encapsulating hydrophobic molecules, aligning with previous findings [12] that demonstrated the encapsulation of fluorescent molecules in SBM derived from Quillaja saponaria for bio-imaging applications. Subsequently, Vinarov [16] explored the encapsulation of a hydrophobic anticancer drug, namely paclitaxel, within micelles formed by saponins, further evidencing the versatility and effectiveness of SBM in drug delivery systems.

Particle size and zeta potential of SBMs

Data from dynamic light scattering (DLS) indicated that SBMs from pure saponin, M. laurentii, M. paradisiaca and M. sapientum are in the nano-sized range, while SBMs from S. americana are in the micro-sized range (Table 2). The polydispersity index (PDI) values ranged from 0.25 to 0.60. Additionally, the particle surface charges were very low, suggesting that the larger particle sizes observed may be attributed to aggregation. Remarkably, SBMs derived from pure saponin, M. laurentii, M. paradisiaca, M. sapientum, and P. eetveldeana demonstrated small particle sizes within the nanometre scale, with a range size of 150–900 ​nm and PDI in a range of 0.25–0.60, indicating for most of them heterogeneity of size (PDI < 0.35), this is in agreement with results found by Fattahi et al. [44]. The ZP values were low, suggesting that the net total surface charges of SBMs are likely neutral. This implies the high risk for the aggregation of some particles, which may lead to marked particle size heterogeneity, underlying the observed high value of PDI. Hemolytic profiles

Numerous studies have highlighted the toxicity of pure saponins, particularly their capacity to lyse erythrocyte membranes, leading to hemolysis. However, relatively few investigations [13,43] have explored the hemato-toxicity (%H) of saponin fractions (denoted SCFs). To the best of our knowledge, no studies have examined the role of other secondary metabolites and their impact on the hemato-toxicity of saponins towards human red blood cells (RBCs). In this research, the hemolytic profile of SCFs was assessed against human RBCs at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 ​μg/mL.

Considering these saponins were also employed in the formulation of NPs for biomedical applications, the study further examined the hemato-toxicity of SBMs at higher concentrations than those used for SCFs, specifically at 100, 200, and 300 ​μg/mL (Table 3). A comparative analysis of the behavior of saponin crude fractions (SCF) and their nanoparticulate forms (denoted SBMs) was conducted. The hemolysis rate data for SCFs align with the results of Manaargadoo-Catin et al. [48], who described saponins as surfactants having the potential to cause erythrocyte lysis. Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies documenting the hemolytic profile of SBMs, marking a novel area of investigation in the context of reducing the toxicity of saponins through nanotechnological approaches. However, Lv et al. [27] have investigated the hemolytic profile of curcumin-loaded block copolymer (4-arm poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(anhydride-esters) micelles and showed that in vitro, these polymeric micelles do not lead to hemolysis with a hemolysis rate of 3.18% at 100 ​μg/mL to blood from Sprague-Dawley rat. This suggests that the results found with saponin crude fractions from M. paradisiaca are very close to previous findings [27].

Saponin-based micelles’ drug loading capacity, encapsulation efficiency, and cumulative release profiles for Pc-INH

Upon establishing that SCFs possess the potential to form SBMs and that these SBMs can efficiently encapsulate hydrophobic molecules like Pc-INH while concurrently exhibiting a significant reduction in saponin toxicity, further investigation of the encapsulation efficiency of these SBMs for Pc-INH has been conducted before release studies. The aim was to determine the optimal quantity of saponins required to maximize encapsulation within the micelles.

The %DLC for all the SBMs formulations presents the following profiles: 1.46%—pure saponin, 2.49%—M. laurentii, 0.34%—P. eetveldeana, 2.31%—M. paradisiaca, and 0.87%—M. sapientum. This variability indicates differences in the potential of SBMs to encapsulate Pc-INH, which could be influenced by various formulation-related parameters, such as the phytochemical compounds that remained in the SCFs or the composition of the resultant SMBs. The SBMs of P. eetveldeana had the lowest drug loading, indicating inefficient drug encapsulation (Fig. 8A), most likely due to a low affinity between Pc-INH and the micelles. On the other hand, M. laurentii and M. paradisiaca SBMs have the largest drug-loading capacity and appear to be the most successful in drug trapping, suggesting that they may be more physicochemical compatible with Pc-INH. On the other hand, The SBMs from M. laurentii and M. paradisiaca had the highest drug loading potential and appeared to be more effective in drug entrapment than pure saponin and might have, therefore, better physicochemical compatibility with Pc-INH [49]. The results of encapsulation efficiency and cumulative release are concisely summarized in Fig. 8A, indicating at least SBMs from SCFs of M. laurentii exhibited significant differences (p ​< ​0.0001) of %EE to SBM from pure saponin (with %EE of 56% and 33% respectively), and those from M. paradisiaca (40%) exhibited a different significant %EE (p ​< ​0.01) with the SBM of pure saponin, and M. sapientum and P. eetveldeana presented weak %EE (17% and 7% respectively) compared to pure saponin (p ​< ​0.0001). This data provides insights into the effective use of saponins for encapsulating hydrophobic substances within SBMs as described by Wang et al [50] and Yang et al [51] who encapsulated podophyllotoxin (78%) and paclitaxel (90%) efficiently respectively into SBM.

The study of release dynamics for Pc-INH encapsulated within SBMs was conducted under varying pH conditions to simulate different biological compartments for potential in vivo applications such as the acidic milieu of endosomes (pH 4.5) and the neutral blood medium (pH 7.4), and the alkaline milieu (pH 9.2) to extend applications to in vitro studies. SBMs from SCF of M. paradisiaca were selected for in vitro release testing due to their similar %EE to pure saponin and low hemato-toxicity. As depicted in Fig. 8B, the release profile of Pc-INH at pH 7.4 is steadily efficient, with a complete release of 100% Pc-INH occurring within 6 ​h. In contrast, at the alkaline pH of 9.2, the release pattern is initially slow, with almost no release up to 6 ​h, followed by a sudden surge, culminating in 100% release of Pc-INH by the 12-h mark, indicating the onset of release between 8 and 10 ​h. The release was gradual at acidic pH, reaching 60% after 18 ​h. The observed low percentage Pc-INH released in acidic might also be a result of acid degradation of Pc-INH molecules, as reported by Nkanga and Krause [19] who observed liposomes loaded with Pc-INH exhibited optimal release of isoniazid (INH) at an acidic pH of 4.4. Nonetheless, the UV–vis measurements at 681 ​nm were likely integrative enough to consider both the intact Pc-INH and degraded version of phthalocyanine without INH. Nevertheless, this nuanced understanding of the release behavior under different pH conditions underscores the potential of SBMs for targeted drug delivery, adapting to the varied pH environments encountered within the human body and for in vitro applications necessitating alkaline conditions.

The multifaceted release kinetics observed in this study are compelling and align with previous research findings, highlighting the diverse mechanisms through which SBMs can release encapsulated compounds under varying pH conditions. Such phenomena have been documented in the literature as follows: (i) by Wang et al [50] who studied the cumulative release of the saponin-based glycyrrhizic acid in PBS medium (pH 7.4), supplemented with 0.5% Tween 80, at 37°C and found that the formulated micelle can sustainable release podophyllotoxin (a first-line agent for genital warts) in the first 5 ​h, similar to the release kinetics herein observed at pH 7.4; (ii) Yang et al [51] reported the release of paclitaxel (an antitumor agent), which is equivalent to the SBMs release profile at pH 4.5 in the present study, loaded in saponin-based glycyrrhizic acid at the simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.6) and intestinal (pH 6.5) media supplemented with 1% Tween 80 ​at 37°C and showed that the SBM promote a delayed release compared to conventional drug Taxol® which presented a fast release of paclitaxel after 2 ​h.

These comparisons validate the versatility and adaptability of SBMs in drug delivery across different pH environments and position them as a promising platform for the targeted and controlled release of therapeutic agents, enhancing their application potential in the biomedical field.

Nonetheless, further efforts are needed to optimize the drug delivery potential of SBMs, particularly in terms of drug loading, encapsulation efficiency, and cumulative release. These studies will explore challenges and limitations that might be associated with different types of saponins (triterpenoid or steroidal) and the number of glycone chains (mono-, bi-, or tridesmosidic), which might influence the hemolytic profiles, formulation, and stability of SBMs. Additionally, the phytochemical structures in the SCFs will be examined for their impact on the hemolytic profiles, stability, and drug delivery potentials of SBMs, which will enable the gathering of comprehensive information to enhance the application of saponin crude fractions in drug delivery.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop cost-effective NPs for drug delivery of SCFs from five Congolese plant species. Pharmacognostical evaluations revealed that all SCFs formed SBMs to the same extent as pure saponins. Comparative studies showed superior encapsulation efficiency of some SCFs over commercially available pure saponins, with M. paradisiaca showing the greatest stability. Particle characterization through DLS indicated the presence of nanoparticles. TEM combined with ImageJ analysis validated the nanoscale dimension of particles. The study underscored the feasibility of NP formulations from SCF without stringent environmental constraints despite the reputed hemato-toxicity of saponins. The hemolytic profile analysis revealed that a double/triple dose of most SBMs exhibited significantly lower toxicity on red blood cells than the corresponding SCFs. SBMs from M. paradisiaca showed statistically similar encapsulation efficiency with the SBM from pure saponin. Further investigations must be done to extend this study to other plant-rich saponins; standardization and plantation of those plant-rich saponins will be useful to improve the scalability of the production of saponin-based micelles. These findings suggest that SCFs are promising raw materials for designing functional nanomaterials with the potential to address biopharmaceutical challenges and boost the translational development of affordable and accessible nanomedicines. Further in vitro and in vivo studies are essential to comprehensively understand the functional potentials of SBMs loaded with diverse active pharmaceutical ingredients.
