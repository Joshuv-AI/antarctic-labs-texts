Modeling the interpretable geometric-performance relationship of metamaterials on small datasets using Kolmogorov-Arnold operator informed network
Authors: Shengyu Ni, Xingyi Feng, Yuxin Long, Xiangrong Xu, Yifei Qian, Hong Zhang, Xiufang Gong, Junyan Zhu, Yongjie Liu, Qingyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning has been extensively employed in the prediction of metamaterial properties. However, the multi-layer perceptron-kernelled methods lack interpretability and are highly dependent on large datasets, making the end-to-end mapping opaque and computationally expensive and hindering the exploration and application of physical mechanisms. To address these issues, the Kolmogorov-Arnold Operator Informed Network (KAOIN) method is proposed, achieving the lightest neural structure under small-sample conditions while improving accuracy and convergence speed. On this basis, a coupled metamaterial performance prediction framework is constructed, enabling dataset construction, high-fidelity analysis, and performance visualization. This framework is capable of predicting the specific energy absorption of the gradient triply-periodic minimal surface through a mere 50 sets of data. The interpretability and ability to extract physical laws of KAOIN were comprehensively verified through spatial symmetry and the Gibson-Ashby theoretical model. It has been demonstrated that a geometry-performance relationship improves accuracy by up to 44.6% and the convergence speed by 48–89%. This study introduces a novel neural network paradigm capable of exploring physical mechanisms in small datasets and demonstrates its potential for accurately modeling the geometric-performance relationships of metamaterials.

Introduction

Mechanical metamaterials are artificially engineered materials that exhibit unconventional mechanical properties1,2, including negative Poisson’s ratio3,4, high energy absorption5–7, structural multi-stability8,9, and exceptional fracture resistance10,11. This unconventional behavior stems from sophisticated geometric designs that transcend the limitations imposed by the intrinsic properties of constituent materials12, driving advancements in aerospace13, new energy vehicles14, and biomedical devices15. Extensive research has been conducted on typical metamaterials, establishing their geometric-mechanical property relationships—including truss structures16,17, gradient structures18,19, and Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS20–22—though primarily focused on specific configurations and performance criteria23. As structures diversify, modeling and comprehensive analysis of complex mechanical responses linked to geometric parameters remain under-explored23. Furthermore, relying solely on traditional high-fidelity simulation models for performance prediction under complex operating conditions and digital twin tasks in changing environments often leads to computationally intensive workflows24. Consequently, developing an efficient, accurate, and generalizable method for predicting performance is crucial for advancing metamaterial development and engineering applications.

Against this backdrop, deep learning methods serve as effective surrogate models, enabling end-to-end mapping between geometry and mechanical performance while bypassing complex internal derivations25. Currently, deep learning technologies centered on Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) have significantly expanded their applications for prediction tasks in mechanics. Examples include intrinsic modeling of composite materials26,27, predicting deformation modes in thin-walled structures28,29, and estimating mechanical properties of cellular materials30. These applications demonstrate deep learning’s high computational efficiency and robust nonlinear modeling capabilities, reducing task execution times by several orders of magnitude. However, the MLP-kernelled scheme may hinder further development of basic science as a large number of neurons dissolve the physical meaning of parameters in the model and complex hidden layers stifle interpretability31. In this case, while deep learning boasts considerable applicability, it impedes further exploration of the fundamental principles underlying physical phenomena and blocks the upward spiral of natural science and computer science32,33.

The advent of a novel neural network, known as Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs34, addressed the issues above. KAN’s unique interpretability and novel fitting methods have attracted widespread attention and have been applied in emerging engineering fields such as network security35–37, non-destructive testing38–40, fault analysis41, energy42–44, and battery design45,46. KANs are founded upon Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem47,48 and employ learnable edge activation functions, utilizing spline functions49 instead of each weight parameter. To restore the physical laws behind the parameters of neurons, KANs significantly reduce complexity by Lasso regularization while maintaining model accuracy50. To improve the readability of the fitting results, KANs output the most concise results as mathematical functions, making them applicable across different communication and coding scenarios. In the field of metamaterials, Wang et al.51 have demonstrated that KANs can recognize changes in Poisson’s ratio of hexagonal structural elements at different angles and have preliminarily achieved explicit formula-based modeling of “geometry-performance”. However, due to the difficulty of convergence for independent KANs with small samples, the extracted results do not align with the actual geometric parameters, resulting in a lack of spatial recognition ability and interpretability.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs52, as the models capable of embedding prior knowledge, have garnered significant attention in the fields of mechanics and materials science. Its core lies in embedding physical laws as constraints into neural network backpropagation, enabling the network to learn and solve problems even with limited data. Recently, reports have emerged on the fusion of KAN and PINN models. Shukla et al.53 pioneered the replacement of the MLP in PINN with KAN to form Physics-Informed Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (PIKANs). They demonstrated that PIKANs achieve higher accuracy and better convergence stability. On this basis, Wang et al.50 used KAN to replace MLP in different forms (strong, energetic, and inverse) of PINNs, called Kolmogorov-Arnold-Informed Neural Network, have been employed for the resolution of partial differential equations (PDEs) in computational solid mechanics, exhibiting enhanced accuracy and convergence speed. Shuai et al.54 applied PIKAN to Power System Dynamics, reducing the network size by 50%. Inspired by PINNs, Zhang et al.24 developed AL-PKAN. This model employs a gated recurrent unit (GRU) and KAN as the encoder and decoder, respectively, and treats penalty factors and Lagrange multipliers as learnable parameters to dynamically adjust the constraint balance, thereby improving partial differential equation solutions by two orders of magnitude.

Unfortunately, while the above methods significantly improve convergence accuracy and reduce network size, they neglect the interpretability of KAN. In PIKANs, researchers attempted to reduce neural networks’ reliance on data by incorporating physical information. However, the small dataset led to severe Lasso failure and overfitting, resulting in network bloat and low generalizability55. Meanwhile, since PIKANs are constructed based on the PINNs framework, their reliance on prior knowledge may impose limitations on the algorithm. In domains where prior knowledge is insufficient or lacks fixed patterns—such as the recently emerging multi-stable structures9,56,57, programmable structures58,59, and ultra-high-degree-of-freedom design methods60,61—it is challenging to express these through any fixed formula, posing a significant hurdle for PIKANs. Therefore, this study proposes the Kolmogorov-Arnold Operator Informed Network (KAOIN) and its two-stage training scheme, aiming to achieve the following two main objectives: First, we strive to mitigate the Lasso failure issue that KAN faces with small datasets, thereby maximizing sparsity to ensure interpretability. Second, inspired by PINNs, we embed a KAN as an operator during the main network’s backpropagation to enforce physics-like constraints, thereby enabling high accuracy and rapid convergence. Kolmogorov-Arnold operator informed network

Kolmogorov-Arnold operator

The Kolmogorov-Arnold Operator (KAO) is a core component of KAOIN, embedded as a learnable intermediary operator within the loss function of the main neural network. KAO is a specially trained KAN within KAOIN. First, it does not accept the original high-fidelity dataset but is trained using a derived low-fidelity dataset, thereby ensuring optimal interpretability. Second, due to precision constraints, it does not output final predictions itself but instead feeds its outputs into the main network’s loss function. In this paper, to distinguish the special intermediary operator from the fully functional KAN, we name this operator KAO. Each weight parameter of KAO is expressed as a learnable function (e.g., Spline Function, Fourier Function) and is set on the “edges” of the network, in contrast to the approach taken with MLPs, where the selected activation function is fixed on the “nodes”. Training stage I—maximizing Lasso regularization

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the operation of KAOIN can be divided into two stages. In stage I, the low-fidelity network, trained on the initial small dataset, is employed to generate a generalized dataset through dense grid sampling of the input parameter space (in this work, the dataset will be uniformly encrypted 100 times). Unlike the original small-sample dataset with a limited number of discrete points, the generalized dataset provides continuous coverage of the input domain, enabling effective Lasso regularization for KAO training. It is worth mentioning that, traditional augmentation (e.g., adding Gaussian noise, or Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) creates variations of existing data points within the local neighborhood62. In contrast, the generalized dataset leverages the neural network’s interpolation capability to explore the entire input domain, generating physically consistent data points that may not be proximate to any original sample. This global coverage is essential for KAO to identify the true underlying functional relationship rather than memorizing sparse observations.

Training stage II—convergence through soft constraints

In stage II, the KAO is embedded to the loss function of main network to achieve higher accuracy and faster convergence on small-sample datasets. Dataset generation

To build a comprehensive dataset of geometric-performance data pairs in bulk, a coupled framework encompassing geometric modeling, finite-element calculations, and data cleaning was developed. In terms of geometric modeling, implicit function boundaries and exhaustive algorithms were used to model various geometric shapes. Subsequently, quasi-static compression was conducted on all geometric models using ABAQUS to obtain their mechanical properties. This process involved adding markers to the geometric models, thereby creating a sufficient dataset for supervised learning. This enables the explicit relationship between the geometric structure of the TPMS and its mechanical performance to be obtained, thereby facilitating the rapid performance prediction of the structure. Additionally, the DBSCAN algorithm was used to filter out data with significant bias. The screened errors are caused by grid distortion and do not affect the overall pattern. Finally, the impact of the size effect on SEA was tested and emphasized.

Triply-periodic minimal surface

Triply-periodic minimal surface (TPMS) is a kind of unique metamaterial with boundaries determined by implicit functions, which offers unique mechanical property advantages, including high specific strength and stiffness, excellent energy absorption, near isotropic properties, and highly tunable topological geometry63–66. The mechanical properties of TPMS can be improved by reducing the stress concentration points and pressure drop through continuous curvature while maintaining a large surface area, which contributes to the stability and impact resistance under multi-dimensional loading conditions67,68. These performance advantages have gradually made the TPMS-based metamaterial a popular direction in current materials engineering and design. Training the neural network requires a suitable dataset. In this case, the features are the geometrical parameters of the TPMS, while the output is the value of the relevant metric generated by the finite element analysis.

TPMS with a grid number of 2 × 2 × 2 and a unit cell size of 1 × 1 × 1 (mm) were used as case studies. Unique TPMS were identified using maximum and minimum thickness gradients, and the effects of these factors on the structures’ specific energy absorption (SEA) were investigated. Geometric modeling

To address the need to generate large-scale geometric models with diverse characteristics, this work presents a novel TPMS modeling approach based on implicit function theory. Leveraging mathematical principles and geometric topology, this method enables efficient model construction and mesh processing through a sequence of systematic procedures.

Initially, based on the selected TPMS type, a suitable implicit function is used to compute and generate the model surface. The generation range is determined by the number of unit cells the user defines for the cube. At the same time, the grid resolution is set to produce a grid within the three-dimensional space with a resolution of 2×len×20, as shown in Fig. 5a. Each point in the spatial grid is traversed, the function values are calculated for each grid point, and the points with function values equal to the difference or sum of specific constants, gradient thicknesses, and offset values are extracted as the two surfaces for generating the TPMS model, as shown in Fig. 5b.

To convert two independent iso-surfaces into a complete closed triangular mesh model, the vertices of the two iso-surfaces are merged. The surface vertices are filtered and segmented to assign them to different geometric sets based on their positions. This segmentation strategy ensures the uniform distribution of the model in different spatial directions and lays the foundation for subsequent closure operations. After vertex segmentation, the vertices within each set are sorted to ensure the geometric continuity of the boundary curves. The sorted vertex sequences are precisely matched with the points on the spatial edges of the screening model. The system will check whether the number of boundary vertices meets the basic conditions for forming triangles, ensuring sufficient geometric information for subsequent triangulation. When the geometric conditions are met, the Delaunay triangulation algorithm69 is used to generate triangular facets and construct non-boundary surfaces. The triangulation process first projects the 3D boundary curves onto the 2D plane, then applies a planar triangulation algorithm to generate the triangular mesh, and finally maps the 2D results back into 3D space. This projection-profiling-mapping strategy effectively handles complex 3D geometric problems. To ensure complete closure of the model, generating connection surfaces in each of the six main directions (positive and negative X, Y, and Z axis directions), as shown in Fig. 5c. The closure process includes the precise positioning of boundary points, the systematic segmentation of plane segments, and the triangulation of connection surfaces.

To provide sufficient datasets for training neural networks, batch modeling is further implemented in Python. In line with the principle of parameter design, a script-based loop mechanism automatically adjusts the parameters of each generated TPMS model according to preset increments. The entire construction process is a systematic transformation from mathematical functions to complete 3D geometric models, with each step including rigorous geometric validation and error-checking mechanisms to ensure the quality and reliability of the resulting models.

Finite element analysis

A numerical simulation of the mechanical properties was conducted using Explicit Dynamic solver of ABAQUS. To obtain the SEA of each structure, the behavior of TPMS-G under uniaxial compression conditions was mainly simulated. Following the assembly design, each TPMS-G model was sandwiched between two rigid plates (3D discrete rigid, 3.0 × 3.0 × 0.2 mm). Each rigid plate was assigned a reference point at its geometric center, with a point mass of 1 (isotropic) and rotational inertia values of 1 for all principal axes. As shown in Fig. 6a,b, the bottom plate was fixed to enhance computational efficiency, while the top plate was displaced downward to 10% strain (Uz=0.2 mm, all other degrees of freedom are zero, constant displacement) to simulate the quasi-static compression process. In the explicit dynamic analysis step, the total time duration was set to 20. To improve computational efficiency, semi-automatic mass scaling was used and activated throughout the entire analysis step. The target time increment for mass scaling was set to 1 × 10− 5 s, with scaling performed at every increment. A generalized contact was applied to all surfaces in the model, with a hard contact for normal behavior and a friction coefficient of 0.1 for tangential behavior. The material was defined as 304-steel at room temperature, with the following properties: Density, 8 × 10− 9 t/mm3; Young’s modulus, 1.8 × 105 MPa; Poisson’s ratio, 0.29; and the plasticity setting, as detailed in Fig. 6c. The grid uses C3D4 cells with 0.03 mm sides.

TPMS-G with different gradient thicknesses were generated using Python-ABAQUS scripts. This was achieved automatically by using the implicit function-based modeling and converting the structure information into a finite element model. In addition, this script can run finite element simulations in batch mode, thereby increasing the efficiency of data generation. As shown in Fig. 6d, all structures exhibit typical stress-strain response characteristics during compression. During the elastic stage, stress increases rapidly, followed by the onset of plastic deformation. Compared to structures with uniform thickness, those with gradient thickness exhibit slightly lower overall load-bearing capacity. This is because the thinner regions in gradient structures are more prone to localized bending or deformation in the early stages, thereby reducing overall stiffness and energy absorption. However, as compression progresses, the thicker regions gradually contribute to load-bearing, leading to a steady increase in structural performance.

Data cleaning

Data visualization revealed that some data points deviated significantly from the trend. After a detailed inspection, it was found that the grid connections in the thin areas of the structure were incorrect during the grid division stage. Because this extreme situation occurs in only a very small number of models and grid optimization is outside the scope of this study, we directly filter abnormal data points using the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN70). The previous experiment showed that omitting these points did not eliminate features.

Size effect analysis

For metamaterials with identical geometric configurations, their mechanical properties also undergo significant alterations due to size effects71. To test the validity of the data, finite element simulations were used to analyze the impact of unit cell sizes on SEA under uniaxial compression. Using the structure from Sect. 3.1 as a reference, cubic structures with one-dimensional grid numbers ranging from 1 to 5 were constructed while maintaining the overall size (20 × 20 × 20 mm). As shown in Fig. 8, to maintain a constant relative density and eliminate geometric factors, unit cells of different sizes were obtained through scaling. Due to size effects, the SEA of the 2 × 2 × 2 structure is 95.73% of the 4 × 4 × 4 structure (a size widely recognized as representative of the mechanical properties of periodic structures72,73 and 95.18% of the 5 × 5 × 5 structure. Although the 2 × 2 × 2 structure data exhibit approximately 5% deviation from the actual periodic structure, this deviation is consistent across all data points in the dataset. It thus does not affect the structure of the geometry-performance model. In practical engineering applications, we recommend constructing databases using structures with larger grid numbers. Otherwise, when considering size effects, the corresponding coefficients must be applied.

Results

KAOIN achieves deep coupling between the main network and KAO through a two-stage training paradigm. Based on this architecture, this section evaluates KAOIN’s representation capabilities on the TPMS dataset from both metamaterial and neural network perspectives. Section 4.1 first establishes a SEA prediction model that uses wall thickness as a feature, thereby validating KAOIN’s ability to recognize structural spatial symmetry. Subsequently, using relative density and gradient as features, it is found that the mathematical model emerging in KAOIN aligns with the Gibson-Ashby theory. Section 4.2 quantitatively compares KAOIN with baseline models in terms of interpretability (network sparsity), accuracy, convergence speed, and stability, demonstrating KAOIN’s effectiveness in constructing geometric-mechanical properties under small-sample conditions.

Unless specified, all networks in this chapter operate based on the following parameters. The main KAOIN network employs a 5-layer fully connected MLP, with the number of input-layer neurons matching the feature dimension. The hidden layer dimensions are 128, 256, and 128, respectively. The activation function is ReLU, the optimizer is Adam, the batch size is 16, the learning rate is 0.001, and the number of training epochs is 500. KAO’s initial network width is set to [2, 5, 1], the initial grid size is 10, and the basic functions use cubic spline curves. In stage I, the generalizer configuration mirrors the main network, with a batch size of 32, a learning rate of 0.003, and 100 epochs. The generalization dataset is uniformly encrypted to 100 times the size of the initial dataset. In stage II, the weight of the KAO loss is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/\left( {n+1} \right)$$\end{document}, where n represents the number of training iterations.

To test the performance of KAOIN on datasets of different sizes. Using the data generation framework, we constructed three datasets: TPMS-G (423), TPMS-D (50), and TPMS-S (50), where the numbers in parentheses indicate the dataset sizes. To evaluate neural network performance across varying data volumes, five TPMS-G datasets of sizes 50, 100, 200, 300, and 423 were formed via simple random sampling. To ensure reliability, certain tests were executed multiple times, with distinct sampled datasets used for each iteration.

where m is the mass of the TPMS, ρ is the density of the material, η is the relative density of the TPMS, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V_{TPMS}}$$\end{document} represents the volume of the TPMS, and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V_{BOX}}$$\end{document} represents the bound box.

To test the universality of KAOIN under different metamaterials and data volumes, 423 TPMS-G, 50 TPMS-D, and 50 TPMS-S data were obtained through the coupled data generator. To comprehensively evaluate KAOIN’s ability to explore physical mechanisms, multiple datasets were constructed using the same SEA but different features. Using top thickness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_t}$$\end{document}) and bottom thickness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_b}$$\end{document}) as features, KAOIN’s ability to identify spatial symmetry in TPMS was tested. Using relative density (η) and gradient (μ) as features, the consistency of KAOIN’s results with the Gibson-Ashby model was verified, and a model incorporating gradient value corrections was further obtained.

The implicit function determining TPMS is centrosymmetric, and the compression performance is not affected by direction along the same axis. Hence, the influence of top thickness variation is the same as that of bottom thickness variation. This spatial symmetry is mathematically expressed as the same functional form and similar coefficients. Reviewing KAOIN’s performance on datasets, it differs from conventional regression or physics-informed approaches by leveraging automated discovery of physical laws and minimalist expressions of display functions, offering greater potential for physical insights. For structures that have been extensively studied, the relationship between geometry and performance is well-defined. In such cases, researchers can predefine the functional form of the fit and obtain specific parameters through traditional regression methods74. Peng et al.75 analyzed the normalized modulus of various TPMS-based honeycombs via polynomial fitting based on the Gibson-Ashby scaling law. Similarly, PINNs require prior knowledge of the governing PDEs52. Li et al.76 derived the complex nonlocal strain gradient homogenization model as control equations of TPMS and embedded them into PINNs for rapid computation. Additionally, when applying PINN to TPMS, the standard residual minimization method may exhibit convergence anomalies77. However, before KAOIN discovered analogous physical laws, we did not load any predefined forms, enabling its application to unknown domains.

Training stability

The training stability of KAOIN stems from the synergistic interplay between its two-stage architecture and KAO’s soft-constraint nature. To assess reproducibility, we conducted 10 independent training runs for KAOIN, MLP, and KAN on the TPMS-G dataset (data size = 50) with different random seeds. KAOIN exhibits the lowest coefficient of variation in final test MSE (0.08), compared to MLP (0.23) and KAN (0.31). This stability arises from Stage I’s data homogenization, which generates a consistent generalized dataset that anchors the KAO extraction process, reducing dependency on random initialization. From an optimization perspective, KAOIN’s stability can be attributed to the structured loss landscape induced by the KAO term. The explicit geometric-performance relationship introduces a ridge in the parameter space that channels gradient descent toward flat minima that generalize better. In contrast, MLPs navigate rugged landscapes susceptible to sharp minima and overfitting.

This encompasses the number of network layers, the number of neurons per layer, the grid size, the penalty factor in the Lasso function, the optimizer category, the initial learning rate and its decay method, and the number of training rounds. According to numerous tests, the penalty factor is proportional to the network’s sparsity, and the number of neurons and layers is proportional to accuracy but inversely proportional to sparsity within a certain range34. We quantitatively evaluated the KAO grid size, the Lasso regularization penalty factor, and the weighting of the most important loss functions. Ultimately, hyperparameter configurations were uniformly presented.

Analysis of the necessity for a two-stage training strategy

To rigorously validate the necessity of the two-stage training strategy in KAOIN, we conducted comprehensive ablation studies comparing KAOIN against alternative regularization approaches and simplified variants. These experiments were designed to isolate the contribution of each stage and demonstrate that the performance gains cannot be achieved through simpler regularization strategies.

Comparison with conventional regularization methods

Limitation

KAOIN can extract high-precision geometric-performance relationships from small sample datasets. Still, it exhibits significant bias in areas not covered by the dataset, mathematically analogous to the Taylor expansion of edge cases. Three types of geometric images from explicit formulas are plotted as in Fig. 15, generated by KAOIN on datasets with sample sizes of 50, 200, and 423, respectively. All the functions perform well within the limits of the training data and effectively express the relationship between geometric features and mechanical properties. However, when the function is observed over a more extensive range, it is found to act in a completely different trend than empirically, which would result in KAOIN not being able to be applied to prediction efforts outside the range of the training data, significantly limiting its generalizability. Worst of all, in the case of Fig. 15g, the function tends to be infinite, implying that this formula is meaningless in some intervals. A similar problem exists in KAN, where a small number of activation functions, while providing good readability, also limit their expressive power.

Promotions and applications

The proposed KAOIN framework significantly advances interpretable modeling for small datasets, laying the groundwork for exploring intrinsic logic in metamaterials and beyond. While current datasets are limited to mechanical properties under TPMS single-axis compression, we recommend further extending the application of KAOIN, including geometric imperfection analysis, multi-physics coupling, and dynamic loading conditions, cross-material generalization, and so on.

The current framework assumes ideal geometric configurations based on precise implicit functions. However, as noted in mechanical metamaterial research, geometric imperfections can substantially alter failure modes and mechanical responses81. Future work could integrate linear buckling analysis to impose buckling modes as initial imperfections and quantify their impact on energy absorption predictions. Based on this foundation, it is anticipated that KAOIN will enable the establishment of a quantitative relationship between defects and performance.

The present study focuses on quasi-static compression scenarios. Extending KAOIN to dynamic impact, fatigue cycling, or thermal-mechanical coupling scenarios would broaden its applicability to crashworthiness design and aerospace thermal protection systems. The explicit functional form of KAOIN facilitates incorporation of rate-dependent constitutive terms and thermal softening effects.

While demonstrated for metallic TPMS structures, the methodology is transferable to polymer-based, ceramic, or composite metamaterials. The interpretable functional forms enable material-specific coefficients to be identified from limited experimental data, thereby accelerating the qualification of additively manufactured components across diverse material systems.

Conclusion

As a new paradigm in neural networks, the KAOIN emerges as a transformative solution for modeling interpretable geometric-performance relationships in metamaterials under small-dataset conditions. By embedding KAO into the main network’s loss function and carefully designing a two-stage training paradigm, KAOIN achieves optimal sparsity and interpretability while maintaining predictive accuracy. Ablation studies ultimately demonstrate that the two-stage training strategy is indispensable, as neither traditional regularization techniques nor simplified single-stage variants achieve comparable performance.

In the testing of specific cases, the application of gradient TPMS demonstrates that KAOIN can establish reliable geometric-performance relationships with minimal data. The derived formulas undergo rigorous validation and align with established physical principles, including spatial symmetry and Gibson-Ashby theoretical models. Collectively, these findings establish KAOIN as an advanced paradigm for interpretable deep learning in materials science, offering a robust pathway for transparent, physics-based modeling that bridges the gap between data-driven approaches and fundamental scientific understanding.

It is worth noting that identified limitations regarding extrapolation beyond the training domain and sensitivity to data quality warrant careful consideration in applied settings. Collectively, these insights inform future research directions to extend KAOIN’s applicability to dynamic loading conditions, multi-physics coupling scenarios, and cross-material generalization, thereby broadening its utility in advanced metamaterial design and broader engineering applications.
