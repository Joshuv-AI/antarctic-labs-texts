Optimization of recombinant SgUGT94CG1 glycosyltransferase enables efficient bioconversion of bitter mogrosides into sweet mogrosides from immature Siraitia grosvenorii
Authors: Lingshan Liang, Chuncheng Wang, Rongjun Tang, Xiangyu Meng, Wude Mao, He Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMogrosides, natural sweeteners derived from Siraitia grosvenorii (monk fruit), are low-calorie, high-potency, safe, and non-toxic alternatives to sucrose, making them particularly suitable for obese individuals and diabetic patients. However, the content of sweet mogrosides in naturally ripened monk fruit is limited. The glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 addresses this limitation by catalyzing the in vitro conversion of bitter, low-glycosylated mogrosides into sweet, highly glycosylated mogrosides through regiospecific glycosylation.ResultsThe SgUGT94CG1 gene was expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3). Optimal conditions determined by single-factor and response surface methodology were: reaction (26.04% extract, 7.82 mM MgCl₂, 40.1 °C), induction (17.3 °C, 10.5 h, 0.47 mM IPTG), and fermentation (12.44 g/L sucrose, tryptone/yeast extract powder 6:4, 1.5 g/L CaCl₂, 1.52% inoculum). Enzyme activity increased 1.41- to 1.67-fold after optimization, and HPLC confirmed efficient conversion of bitter mogrosides to sweet mogrosides. This study provides a basis for future scale-up studies on utilizing discarded immature monk fruit.Keywords: Glycosyltransferase, Mogroside, Fermentation optimization, Biosynthesis, Siraitia grosvenorii

Introduction

Mogrosides are low-calorie, high-potency natural sweeteners from Siraitia grosvenorii (monk fruit), a medicinal and edible plant native to southern China (Anming Lu, 1984; Wang, 2023). The major sweet components, mogrosides IV and V, are valued as sucrose alternatives for obese and diabetic consumers (Wang et al., 2019). However, sweet mogroside content in mature fruit is limited, while large amounts of immature or defective fruit, rich in bitter mogroside II, are discarded, causing waste and reducing industrial efficiency (ChangYuan Zhou, 2003; Takemoto, Arihara, Nakajima, & Okuhira, 1983).

Current strategies include chemical synthesis, enzymatic hydrolysis, and microbial biosynthesis (Chen, Chen, Wang, & Wu, 2018). Chemical hydrolysis suffers from environmental hazards and low yields (Chen et al., 2022). Enzymatic hydrolysis is mild and efficient but limited by high-solid loading, enzyme inactivation, product inhibition, and substrate constraints (da Silva et al., 2020; Hoppert & Einfalt, 2021; JeremyS.Luterbacher, 2021; Qayoudi & Al-Zuhair, 2022; Timo van der Zwan, Richard P. Chandra, & Saddler, 2019). Synthetic biology has enabled de novo mogroside V biosynthesis, but pathway complexity and low titers still hinder industrial application (Guanyi Qu, 2025).

Several mogroside glycosyltransferases have been identified, including UGT74AC1, UGT74DD1, SgUGT94–289-3, and UGT94–289-2(S. Cui et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2015; Gong et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2022). Among them, SgUGT94–289-3 converts up to 95% of mogroside III into sweet mogrosides under idealized conditions using purified enzyme and substrate (S. a. Z. Cui, Yimei and Xie, Lei and Mo, Changming and Su, Jiaxian and Jia, Xunli and Luo, Zuliang and Ma, Xiaojun, 2023). However, enzyme purification and substrate preparation are costly, and no study has systematically optimized these enzymes for direct conversion of real immature fruit. SgUGT94CG1 has been identified and shows a broader substrate spectrum and potential adaptability to crude substrates (Ma, Mo, Pan, Tang, & Tang, 2013), but its recombinant expression, fermentation, and catalytic parameters have not been systematically optimized, nor has it been applied to real immature bitter fruit.

We therefore hypothesized that systematic optimization of SgUGT94CG1 expression, fermentation, and reaction conditions would enable efficient conversion of bitter mogrosides in immature monk fruit into sweet mogrosides at laboratory scale. This study aimed to (i) construct and express recombinant SgUGT94CG1, (ii) optimize induction, fermentation, and enzymatic reaction conditions, and (iii) validate bioconversion using crude extracts from immature bitter fruit. This work provides a laboratory-scale proof of concept for valorizing discarded immature fruit and offers a basis for future scale-up studies aimed at reducing production costs in the monk fruit industry.

Methods and materials

Materials and reagents

The bitter monk fruit used in this study were sourced from Guilin Sanling Bio-technology Co., Ltd. Fresh bitter monk fruit were washed clean with distilled water, cut into pieces, and dried. A pulverizer was then used to grind the dried fruits into powder, which was stored for later use.

For enzyme activity assays, a clarified aqueous extract of bitter monk fruit was used as the substrate. The preparation procedure was as follows: 10 g of bitter monk fruit powder was placed in a 250 mL flask, followed by the addition of 100 mL distilled water. The mixture was subjected to water bath extraction at 80 °C for 2 h. After filtration, the filtrate was concentrated to 50 mL using a rotary evaporator. The concentrated extract was then sterilized by passing through a 0.22 μm filter and stored at 4 °C for later use. The bitter monk fruit contain various bitter mogrosides, including mogroside IIE, IIIA, and IIIE. This aqueous extract was used as the substrate for all enzyme activity assays.

For the final bioconversion experiment, raw bitter monk fruit powder was used directly without prior extraction, as described in Section 2.8.

The experimental materials were sourced as follows: the pGEX-4 T-1 vector and E. coli Rosetta (DE3) expression strain were purchased from Guangzhou Aikeshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ampicillin (AMP) was obtained from Guangzhou Shurui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Tryptone and yeast extract powder were purchased from Guangdong Huankai Microbial Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). Uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPG) was obtained from Xi'an Qiuyue Innovation Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China). The Bradford protein concentration assay kit was purchased from Biyun Tian Bio-technology Co., Ltd. The mogrosides standard was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

All key experiments (enzyme activity, conversion efficiency, and optimized condition validation) were performed with three independent biological replicates. Preliminary screening experiments (substrate specificity) were performed with two biological replicates, as specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Vector construction

Heterologous protein expression technology enables the study of gene function and interactions (Liujie Huo, 2019). Therefore, this study cloned the gene fragment encoding the mogroside glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 into the pGEX-4 T-1 plasmid vector and expressed it in E. coli Rosetta (DE3). This method enables rapid, low-cost expression of the enzyme.

Plasmid vector construction was performed using SnapGene software. The BamHI and XhoI restriction sites were added to both ends of the target protein SgUGT94CG1 gene sequence. Subsequently, the sequence was inserted into the multiple cloning site (MCS) of the pGEX-4 T-1 plasmid vector. The constructed recombinant plasmid map and gene sequence were then submitted to Guangzhou Aikeshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for gene synthesis, recombinant plasmid construction, and transformation into the E. coli Rosetta (DE3) expression strain.

The gene encoding SgUGT94CG1 was obtained from Siraitia grosvenorii(Itkin et al., 2016). The complete nucleotide sequence of the gene used in this study is provided as Supplementary Data S1.

Strain preservation and culture medium

The recombinant E. coli Rosetta (DE3) was streaked on LB solid medium containing ampicillin (AMP) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were inoculated into LB liquid medium containing ampicillin, followed by overnight cultivation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The resulting recombinant E. coli seed culture was stored at 4 °C for later use. For long-term preservation, an equal volume of 30% glycerol was added to the recombinant E. coli seed culture, and the mixture was stored at −80 °C.

LB liquid medium: 1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract powder, and 1% (w/v) NaCl were dissolved in distilled water, made up to 100 mL, and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. After cooling, ampicillin was added to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, and the medium was ready for use.

LB solid medium: Based on the LB liquid medium, 2% (w/v) agar was added. The mixture was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. After cooling to around 50 °C, ampicillin was added to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, thoroughly mixed, and poured into plates.

Fermentation medium: 0.6% (w/v) tryptone, 0.4% (w/v) yeast extract powder, 0.15% (w/v) CaCl₂, 0.5% (w/v) NaCl, and 1.24% (w/v) sucrose were dissolved in distilled water, made up to 100 mL, and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. After cooling, ampicillin was added to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, and the medium was ready for use.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequence analysis

The gene sequence of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was translated into an amino-acid sequence using SnapGene 7.0.2 software (https://www.snapgene.com/), and the conserved domain of the enzyme gene sequence was analyzed using the online program CD-Search (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). The amino acid sequence was compared using the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) BLASTp program, and the homology of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 with other glycosyltransferases was determined. The amino acid sequence of the glycosyltransferase with high homology was analyzed using MEGA 11.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/) and the ESPript 3 (http://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/) program. A phylogenetic tree of the plant glycosyltransferase system was constructed using MEGA 11.0 for multiple plant-derived glycosyltransferases.

Secondary structure analysis

The secondary structure of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was analyzed using the SOPMA online tool (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl), and its transmembrane region was predicted using the TMHMM online program.

Tertiary structure analysis

The homology modeling of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was completed utilizing the SWISS-MODEL online program (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/) on the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (SIB) platform. Based on this, the resulting homology modeling structure model was visualized using Rasmol software, and the solvent accessibility of the protein as well as the modeling quality of the protein structure model were analyzed and evaluated using Swiss-Pdb Viewer 4.1.0 software (https://spdbv.unil.ch/).

Prokaryotic expression

The strain was inoculated into LB liquid medium containing ampicillin at a final concentration of 0.2 mM, with an inoculation ratio of 1% (v/v). The culture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm in an orbital incubator until the optical density (OD600) of the bacterial culture reached 0.6–0.8. For induction, IPTG was added to the culture at a final concentration of 0.2 mM, and the culture was incubated at 16 °C for 20 h.

The induced bacterial cells were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed with 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer and resuspended in the same buffer. The resuspended cell suspension was subjected to ultrasonic disruption using a cell disruptor at 150 W, with a duty cycle of 2 s on and 5 s off, for a total of 20 min. After sonication, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was collected as the induced supernatant, and the pellet was collected as the induced pellet.

Similarly, the uninduced culture was processed to prepare the uninduced supernatant and the uninduced pellet. All four fractions (induced supernatant, induced pellet, uninduced supernatant, and uninduced pellet) were analyzed by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis.

Standard curve and determination of enzyme activity

A gradient concentration of 0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard was prepared. 5 μL of different concentrations of BSA protein concentration standards and protein samples to be tested were taken from the respective solutions and placed in 96-well plates. 250 μL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining solution were added to each well. The absorbance values at 595 nm were measured using a microplate reader. Subsequently, the protein concentration standard curves were plotted based on the results.

The standard solution of UDPG with gradient concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 mM was prepared. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and loaded into the injection vial. The concentration of UDPG was determined by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). The UDPG standard curve was plotted based on the peak area and UDPG concentration obtained from the results.

The 400 μL reaction mixture for enzyme activity determination (Table 1) was thoroughly mixed and incubated at 40 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of 1 M hydrochloric acid solution was added to the mixture, which was then allowed to stand for 5 min to inactivate the enzyme. Following this, 100 μL of 1 M sodium hydroxide solution was added, and the mixture was stirred well to neutralize the hydrochloric acid. The reaction mixture was then diluted fivefold with distilled water, centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 3 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator for further high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) detection and analysis.

Single-factor condition optimization

Single-factor condition analysis

The catalytic conditions for each enzyme differ; under optimal conditions, enzyme activity and efficiency are significantly enhanced, while under suboptimal conditions, activity is greatly reduced or lost (Lancaster, Abdallah, Banta, & Wheeldon, 2018). By setting five factors such as bitter fruit extract concentration (%), enzyme addition volume (μL), pH, temperature (°C), and magnesium chloride concentration (mM), the enzyme activity of monk fruit glycosyltransferase was analyzed. These analyses were conducted to determine the optimal enzymatic reaction conditions.

Expression vectors are central to heterologous expression systems. They incorporate expression elements into the basic vector backbone to enable proper expression of the target gene (Santos et al., 2022; van der Zwan et al., 2019). To enhance induction efficiency, by setting three factors such as temperature (°C), time (h), and IPTG concentration (mM), the enzyme activity of monk fruit glycosyltransferase was analyzed. These analyses were conducted to determine the optimal induction expression conditions.

The microbial growth medium used during the fermentation phase contains numerous components, and fermentation conditions vary widely. Carbon sources, nitrogen sources, mineral composition, and other factors in the fermentation medium, as well as inoculum volume and filling volume during fermentation, significantly impact bacterial growth and metabolism, thereby influencing recombinant bacterial enzyme activity (Islam, Sarker, Mohiuddin, & Suzauddula, 2019). By setting eight factors such as carbon source and concentration, nitrogen source and concentration, inorganic salt concentration (mM), strain inoculum amount (%), and liquid volume (mL), the enzyme activity of monk fruit glycosyltransferase was analyzed. These analyses were conducted to determine the optimal fermentation conditions for enzyme production. It should be noted that the optimization was performed in a stepwise manner: the enzymatic reaction conditions were optimized first; the optimal reaction conditions were then fixed while optimizing the induction conditions; finally, the optimized reaction and induction conditions were both applied while optimizing the fermentation conditions.

Plackett-Burman design

According to the results of single-factor experiments on enzymatic reaction conditions, the low level (−) and high level (+) of five factors (bitter fruit extract content, enzyme addition amount, pH, temperature, and magnesium chloride concentration) were used as the range of levels for each factor. Using enzyme activity as the index, the Plackett-Burman (P—B) experimental design was applied. Each group was repeated three times. Box-Behnken design

Based on the Plackett-Burman (P—B) design results for enzymatic reaction conditions and induction expression conditions, the three most significant influencing factors were determined, along with three factors for fermentation enzyme production conditions. Using Design-Expert 12, a Box-Behnken (B—B) experimental design was conducted, with enzyme activity as the response variable. Three parallel experiments were performed for each group, and statistical analysis of the experimental results was conducted to investigate the interaction effects among the three influencing factors and to validate the predicted optimal fermentation conditions. Each group was repeated three times. In vitro transformation verification

In recent years, there has been increasing research on the use of glycosyltransferases to synthesize natural bioactive compounds, such as steviol glycosides, rebaudiosides, and ginsenosides (Philippe, De Mey, Anderson, & Ajikumar, 2014; Qu et al., 2023). However, studies on the use of glycosyltransferases for sweet mogrosides synthesis remain limited. Compared to chemical synthesis, which is complex, has low catalytic efficiency, requires large amounts of chemical reagents, and risks environmental pollution and residual chemicals, enzymatic methods are faster and cleaner but suffer from poor regioselectivity and low efficiency. Biosynthetic approaches, on the other hand, allow the conversion of immature or damaged monk fruit, which have not naturally transformed into sweet mogrosides, into storable sweet mogrosides. This not only reduces waste but also lowers costs and increases yield (Zhao & Li, 2018). For the empty-vector control, E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells harboring the empty pGEX-4 T-1 vector were cultured, induced, and lysed under the same conditions as the recombinant SgUGT94CG1 expression. The crude extract from the empty-vector cells was then used in parallel bioconversion assays with the same mogroside substrate and reaction conditions as described below.

For the final bioconversion experiment, raw bitter monk fruit powder was used directly as the substrate without prior extraction. The procedure was as follows: 100 mg of bitter monk fruit powder was weighed and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. After cooling, the powder was directly added to the reaction mixture. Then, 1.56 mL of crude enzyme solution was added to the experimental group, while an equal volume of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) was added to the blank group. Subsequently, a final concentration of 7.82 mM magnesium chloride was added to both groups, and the reaction system was adjusted to a final volume of 5 mL using Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0). The mixture was incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 40.1 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, 1 mL of 1 M hydrochloric acid was added, and the solution was allowed to stand for 5 min to inactivate the enzyme. Then, 1 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide solution was added and mixed well to neutralize the hydrochloric acid. Finally, the reaction system was diluted three times with distilled water, centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 3 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter for HPLC analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Design-Expert 12 (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) for response surface methodology (RSM) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) for other comparisons. For two-group comparisons (e.g., before and after optimization in Fig. 10), independent-sample t-tests were used. Results

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequence analysis

The gene sequence of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 is 1371 bp in length. By translating the gene sequence using SnapGene 7.0.2, it was determined that the translated protein consists of 452 amino acids (Table 3), with a theoretical molecular weight of 50.6 kDa. By analyzing the conserved domains of the amino acid sequence of the enzyme protein using the online program CD-Search, it was found that glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 belongs to the GT-B type glycosyltransferase within the GT1 superfamily (Fig. 1A). The GT-B type glycosyltransferase is characterized by a protein consisting of two loosely connected Rossman fold domains, while the GT-A type glycosyltransferase contains a tightly packed β-strand-α-helix-β-strand (β/α/β) Rossman fold domain, and the GT-C type glycosyltransferase is a transmembrane protein (Zhang et al., 2021).

The amino acid sequence of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was compared for sequence similarity and homology using the online program BLASTp from NCBI. Mature proteins can only exhibit their correct biological functions in specific subcellular structures, and subcellular localization allows predictions to be made regarding protein function, metabolism, and interactions (D. Chen et al., 2015; Kilgore et al., 2025). Using the online program WoLF PSORT to predict the subcellular localization of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1, the subcellular localization coefficients were determined as follows: cytoplasm (cyto) = 7, cytoskeleton (cysk) = 3, nucleus (nucl) = 2, and chloroplast (chlo) = 1. Based on these coefficients, it can be concluded that glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 is most likely located in the cytoplasm.

Solvent accessibility analysis

The region of amino acid residues on the surface of a protein molecule that may interact with solvent molecules is referred to as the Solvent Accessibility Surface Area (SASA) of the protein. This is a key characteristic that determines the folding and stability of the protein. The different distribution patterns of amino acids in the inner and outer layers of the protein determine its solvent accessibility (Savojardo, Manfredi, Martelli, & Casadio, 2021). The tertiary structure model of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was analyzed for its solvent accessibility using Swiss-Pdb Viewer software, with results shown in Fig. 3C. In the figure, the colors blue, green, yellow, and orange represent increasing solvent accessibility from weak to strong. The distribution pattern of these four colors from the interior to the exterior indicates that the solvent accessibility of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 gradually increases from the inside to the outside.

Ramachandran plot analysis

The Ramachandran plot for the tertiary structure modeling of glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 is shown in Fig. 3D. Regions with rational and stable structures are referred to as the Allowed Region, enclosed by the yellow lines in the plot. Regions with rational structures but less stable conformations are called the Additional Allowed Region, enclosed by the blue lines outside the yellow lines. Regions with irrational structures are termed the Disallowed Region, located outside the blue lines. From the Ramachandran plot, it can be observed that the vast majority of amino acids in this model fall within the Allowed and Additional Allowed Regions, indicating that the three-dimensional structure of the model is reasonable.

Single-factor analysis results

The optimization was conducted in a stepwise manner. First, the enzymatic reaction conditions were optimized. Analysis of the single-factor experiment results for enzyme reaction conditions revealed that enzyme activity gradually increased with the addition of monk fruit extract, reaching a peak at 25% addition, and then decreased (Fig. 4A). Similar trends were observed in experiments with different crude enzyme solution addition volumes, pH levels, enzyme reaction temperatures, and MgCl2 concentrations. The optimal enzyme reaction conditions were determined as follows: monk fruit extract addition at 25%, crude enzyme solution addition at 31.25%, pH 8, reaction temperature at 40 °C, and MgCl2 concentration at 7.5 mM (Fig. 4B-E).

Subsequently, under the optimal enzymatic reaction conditions established above, the induction conditions were optimized. The optimal induction conditions were determined as follows: induction temperature at 20 °C, induction time of 12 h, and IPTG final concentration at 0.4 mM (Fig. 4F-H).

Finally, under the combined optimal enzymatic reaction and induction conditions, the fermentation conditions for enzyme production were optimized. The optimal carbon source was sucrose at a concentration of 12 g/L. The optimal nitrogen source was a complex nitrogen source consisting of a 6:4 mixture of tryptone and yeast extract powder. The optimal inorganic salt was CaCl2 at a concentration of 1.5 g/L. The optimal bacterial inoculum was 1.5%, and the optimal shake flask volume was 50 mL (Fig. 5A-H). (See Fig. 6.)

Conclusions

In this study, a recombinant expression plasmid pGEX-4 T-1-SgUGT94CG1 was successfully constructed and transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) for heterologous expression. The initial enzyme activity was determined to be 34.357 U/g. Single-factor experiments, Plackett–Burman design, and Box–Behnken design were sequentially employed to systematically optimize the enzymatic reaction conditions, induction conditions, and fermentation conditions of the glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1.This stepwise optimization strategy ensured that each stage was optimized under the optimal conditions established in the preceding stage, with the final combined process yielding an enzyme activity of 57.31 U/g. Compared with the pre-optimization levels, the enzyme activity was increased 1.41-fold, 1.44-fold, and 1.67-fold, respectively.

In this study, enzyme activity was determined by measuring total UDPG consumption. UDPG is the direct sugar donor for all glycosyltransferase (UGT) reactions, and its consumption is stoichiometrically coupled to the transfer of glucose to the acceptor substrate (Cook, Culbertson, & Zabotina, 2017). Therefore, UDPG depletion serves as a reliable proxy for glycosyltransferase activity. The UDPG consumption assay was selected over direct HPLC quantification of mogroside products because the crude E. coli extract and the complex monk fruit substrate contain various UV-absorbing compounds that interfere with product quantification. In contrast, the UDPG-based assay is less susceptible to such matrix interference. However, we acknowledge that this method measures total UDPG consumption rather than specifically quantifying the target glycosylation products, as UDPG may be consumed by side reactions such as endogenous glycosidases, nonspecific glycosyltransferases, or UDPG degradation(Cho, Oiwa, Hosomichi, Yabe, & Imai, 2023). To mitigate this limitation, a no-enzyme control was included in every assay to eliminate background UDPG degradation.

Finally, the conversion of mogroside IIE was verified by HPLC analysis. The chromatograms showed a marked decrease in the substrate peak and the appearance of new product peaks with retention times consistent with those of sweet mogrosides, indicating a highly efficient bioconversion. However, we acknowledge that relying solely on HPLC retention times is insufficient to definitively confirm the identity of the conversion products, as co-eluting compounds or structurally similar mogrosides may produce overlapping peaks. To further validate the product profile, LC-MS/MS analysis will be performed in our future work to provide unequivocal structural confirmation of the glycosylated products, thereby complementing the HPLC-based evidence presented in the current study.

To contextualize our results, we compared the performance of SgUGT94CG1 with other previously characterized mogroside glycosyltransferases. UGT74AC1 was reported to achieve up to a 4.17 × 104-fold increase in catalytic efficiency towards mogrol through directed evolution of its sequence, with significantly enhanced efficiency towards UDP-glucose (Li et al., 2020). Xinyi Lu and colleagues constructed a novel multi-enzyme system combining three UGTs with UDP-glucose regeneration, which significantly increased the content of sweet mogrosides in immature monk fruit extract from 57% to 95% (Lu et al., 2024). SgUGT94–289-3 was shown to convert 95% of mogroside III into sweet mogrosides (Cui et al., 2023).

While all these enzymes exhibit high catalytic efficiency and conversion rates, it is important to emphasize that these performance metrics were obtained under idealized conditions using purified enzymes and highly purified substrates, with stringent reaction parameters that are difficult to replicate in large-scale settings. In contrast, our SgUGT94CG1 was used as a crude enzyme extract directly on raw bitter monk fruit, without prior purification of either enzyme or substrate. This approach offers distinct advantages for potential industrial application: simple reaction conditions, low cost, and the valorization of discarded fruits that would otherwise be agricultural waste. Although direct quantitative comparison between our crude system and the purified systems reported in the literature is complicated by differences in assay conditions, our results demonstrate that SgUGT94CG1 is a robust biocatalyst with promising potential for future scale-up bioconversion of immature monk fruit.

Studies have shown that in well-managed orchards, the fruit maturation rate can exceed 85.0% (J. Chen, 2011). Another investigation on the introduction and cultivation of monk fruit reported a maturation rate of over 80% (Tian, 2010). These data indicate that even under optimal growing conditions, approximately 15%–20% of fruits remain immature at the end of the growth cycle. To address this, some researchers have developed ethephon-based ripening techniques, which can ultimately bring up to 70% of immature fruits to full maturity(L. Wang, 2014). However, the application of such ripening strategies or the cultivation of these fruits to full maturity requires substantial labor and material resources. In contrast, our study offers a direct approach by utilizing these 15%–20% immature fruits for the production of sweet mogrosides, thereby reducing land occupation time, eliminating the need for secondary harvesting, and improving overall cultivation efficiency. It should be noted that the present study was conducted at laboratory scale and does not constitute industrial validation. No pilot-scale, scale-up, mass-balance, or process-economics analyses were performed. Therefore, the industrial applicability of this process remains to be established in future studies.

In summary, a recombinant expression strain harboring the glycosyltransferase SgUGT94CG1 was successfully constructed and heterologously expressed in E. coli. Through systematic optimization of induction expression, enzymatic reaction, and fermentation conditions, the enzyme activity was substantially enhanced. Moreover, the in vitro bioconversion of bitter mogrosides catalyzed by SgUGT94CG1 was validated, demonstrating the potential of this enzyme for future industrial-scale production of sweet mogrosides.
