Neural Response to Familiar Names Predicts Outcome of Comatose ICU Patients: A Prospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Min Wu, Yujia Di, Sheng Kuang, Jun Hu, Jie Zhang, Kang Wang, Jingchen Zhang, Chunyou Chen, Jiajia Zhou, Tong Li, Benyan Luo, Nai Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting the outcome of comatose patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) can inform decision making but remains challenging. Recent studies suggest that task-state electroencephalography (EEG) can detect covert cognition and facilitate patient prognosis. This study aimed to predict the outcome of comatose patients, by assessing covert processing of familiar names using a state-of-the-art EEG frequency tagging approach. Eighty-nine comatose patients following acute brain injury were recruited from five ICUs. Patients were presented with a rapid stream of familiar names and acoustically matched but unintelligible control sounds. EEG responses tracking the familiar names and control sounds were extracted in the frequency domain and utilised to predict the outcome of each patient, which was assessed at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injury using the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE). Name-tracking EEG responses positively correlated with GOSE scores. A machine learning model integrating EEG responses and clinical characteristics achieved AUCs of 0.86, 0.88, and 0.86 in the test set, and 0.91, 0.90, and 0.85 in the external validation set, for predicting outcomes at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively. These findings underscore that EEG assessment of residual processing of familiar names relates to patient outcomes and has the potential to predict outcome of comatose ICU patients.Subject terms: Prognostic markers, Disorders of consciousness, Electroencephalography - EEG, Machine learning

Introduction

Outcome prognostication in comatose patients following severe acute brain injury is a great challenge. Accurate prediction in the intensive care unit (ICU) is crucial for decision-making regarding the continuation or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST). Traditional methods rely on detecting obvious behavioural responses and show limited prediction accuracy. By incorporating a range of clinical and imaging data, more comprehensive prognostic models such as the International Mission for Prognosis and Analysis of Clinical Trials (IMPACT) model and the Corticosteroid Randomisation After Significant Head injury (CRASH) model for traumatic brain injury (TBI) have been developed, primarily to predict unfavourable outcomes such as death and severe disability1–3. Outcome prediction for patients who lack clear signs of an unfavourable prognosis, however, remains challenging4.

Non-invasive bedside electroencephalography (EEG) is increasingly being used in prognostic models. EEG assessment based on auditory stimuli has attracted attention since it is unaffected by the status of the patient’s eyes5–12. Among different types of auditory stimuli, familiar names, especially the subject’s own name (SON), are particularly salient due to their high personal relevance13–16. Previous studies demonstrate that the subject’s own name elicits stronger brain responses than other auditory stimuli, especially when spoken by a familiar voice17,18, and can be recorded during sleep, anaesthesia, or disorders of consciousness (DoC)19–21.

EEG responses to familiar names are often analysed using the event-related potential (ERP) approach, which extracts the EEG response time course aligned to the onset of each name. To isolate the name-specific response, most studies present names as oddball stimuli embedded in other sounds and analyse the oddball response, e.g., mismatch negativity (MMN) and P318,20,22. Several studies showed that an individual-level discriminative response to the SON, typically a novelty P3, can be detected in some patients with impaired consciousness and has been associated with subsequent recovery18,20,23. More recently, the frequency-tagging approach has been introduced, which rapidly presents target stimuli at a fixed rate, e.g., frequency f, so that the stimulus-related neural response also repeats at the same rate. In the neural response spectrum, the strength of this response is reflected by the power at f, as well as its harmonically related frequencies, e.g., 2 f, 3 f24. Using synthesised speech with neutral prosody and no self-relevance, speech-based frequency-tagging studies have already shown promise in predicting the outcome of DoC patients8,10. While EEG responses to auditory stimuli (including SON) have been studied in DoC, their prognostic value in acute ICU coma remains uncertain. This study examines whether combining salient stimuli (i.e., familiar names) with frequency tagging can improve outcome prediction.

In this study, we designed a frequency-tagging paradigm by presenting familiar names. This paradigm combines the high perceptual salience of familiar names (to elicit stronger neural responses) and the high signal quality of the frequency-tagging approach (to reliably measure these neural responses). We hypothesised that comatose ICU patients with stronger EEG responses to familiar names would have better outcomes, assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE).

Results

Population characteristics

We screened 461 patients with acute brain injury in five ICUs and 89 patients were enroled. After excluding patients with poor EEG quality (n = 5), withdrew consent after EEG acquisition (n = 2), and without available outcome data (n = 1), 81 patients were included in the final analysis (63 in dataset 1 and 18 in dataset 2; Fig. 1a, Table 1). The 18 patients from the fifth ICU (dataset 2) were used solely for external validation of prediction models. Among all patients, the median age was 58 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 53–69), and 71.6% were male. Overall, 33.3% had TBI and 66.7% had cardiac arrest (CA). The median Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score at ICU admission was 3 (IQR: 3–4). The median delay between brain injury and EEG assessment was 6 days (IQR: 3–10; Table 1). Detailed characteristics of each dataset are shown in Table S1 and no significant differences in clinical characteristics were observed between the two datasets. Additionally, 31 age-matched healthy controls (median age 59 years; IQR: 53-69) were included to first establish the EEG paradigm.

Neural responses to names and acoustic control

Names and the acoustic control were presented at a constant rate (0.75 s per stimulus, corresponding to 1.33 Hz), such that neural tracking of names or the acoustic control could be reflected in EEG power at 1.33 Hz. In both healthy controls and comatose patients, significant EEG responses were observed at 1.33 Hz in response to both names and the acoustic control when compared to the neighbouring frequency bins (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a, b, left). Patients showed significantly larger EEG responses to names than to acoustic control (6.11 dB [IQR: 0.88–11.59] vs. 2.52 dB [IQR: –1.06–6.47], P = 0.0006; Fig. 2b, right), whereas healthy controls showed comparable responses to names (13.96 dB [IQR: 11.34–16.58]) and acoustic control (12.04 dB [IQR: 7.32–15.89], P = 0.052; Fig. 2a, right). Significant EEG responses were also observed at 2.67 Hz and 4 Hz (i.e., two and three times the name frequency, referred to as the harmonics) in both groups (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a, b, left). Furthermore, when we calculated the difference in EEG responses to names and the acoustic control, indicative of name-specific processing, there were significant EEG responses at 1.33 Hz and both harmonics (all P values < 0.01, Fig. 2c, d).

Individually, using a bootstrap-based detection test at the tagging frequency, 34/63 patients in dataset 1 showed a significant response to names (6-month outcome, recovery of consciousness n = 23), and 23 exhibited a significant response to the acoustic control (6-month outcome, recovery of consciousness n = 15). Additionally, 51 patients in dataset 1 completed both the frequency-tagging paradigm and the SON oddball ERP paradigm on the same day (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Fig. 1). A SON-evoked P3 was detected in 15/51 patients;10/15 had recovery of consciousness at 6 months and 5/15 did not. Relative to the frequency-tagging EEG response, the SON-P3 showed lower sensitivity (41.7% vs 74.2%) but higher specificity (81.5% vs 65.6%) for predicting outcome (Table S2).

Correlation between neural responses and outcomes

The EEG name response at 1.33-Hz (i.e., name-frequency) showed a statistically significant, moderate correlation with the GOSE score (range 1–8, with higher scores indicating better outcome) assessed at 1, 3, or 6 months post-injury (1 month: Rho = 0.481, P = 0.0002; 3 months: Rho = 0.434, P = 0.0006; 6 months: Rho = 0.423, P = 0.0006, FDR corrected, Fig. 3a). In contrast, the 1.33-Hz EEG response to the acoustic control was not reliably correlated with the GOSE score (1 month: Rho = 0.258, P = 0.044; 3 months: Rho = 0.254, P = 0.044; 6 months: Rho = 0.295, P = 0.044, FDR corrected, Fig. 3b). The difference between EEG responses to names and the acoustic control also significantly correlated with the GOSE score, with the correlation strength comparable to that of the name response (1 month: Rho = 0.481, P = 0.0002; 3 months: Rho = 0.442, P = 0.0003; 6 months: Rho = 0.453, P = 0.0003, FDR corrected, Fig. 3c). Similar results were observed for the TBI and CA subgroups (Supplementary Figs. 2–3).

Predicting patient outcomes using EEG name responses

Outcomes were assessed using the GOSE with the primary dichotomous endpoint defined as recovery of consciousness (GOSE ≥ 3 [good] vs 1–2 [poor]). We employed a linear classifier to predict this outcome. On the training set, when the classifier had access only to basic clinical variables (age, aetiology, and GCS score), it showed an AUC of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.69-0.71) at 1 month, 0.68 (95% CI, 0.66-0.69) at 3 months and 0.67 (95% CI, 0.65-0.68) at 6 months (Fig. 4a). When the classifier had access only to EEG responses to names, it achieved higher AUCs at all time points: 0.86 (95% CI, 0.85-0.88) at 1 month, 0.75 (95% CI, 0.73-0.76) at 3 months, and 0.73 (95% CI, 0.71-0.75) at 6 months (Fig. 4a). When the classifier combined basic clinical variables and EEG responses, it yielded even better performance: the AUC was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92-0.96), 0.80 (95% CI, 0.79-0.85), and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.74-0.83) at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively. These findings were confirmed on the test set (from dataset1), where the clinical-EEG model consistently outperformed the other two models and maintained an AUC above 0.85: 0.86 (95% CI, 0.81-0.88) at 1 month, 0.88 (95% CI, 0.86–0.89) at 3 months, 0.86 (95% CI, 0.83–0.89) at 6 months (Fig. 4a, b, Table 2). The performance of the clinical-EEG model was further confirmed in an independent validation set (dataset2), showing AUCs of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.76–0.95), 0.90 (95% CI, 0.77–0.93) and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.73–0.97) at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injury (Table 2). Excluding patients with withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment did not substantially change the main results (Table S3).

As a secondary endpoint aligned with conventional functional recovery thresholds, we additionally evaluated functional outcome (GOSE ≥ 4 [good] vs 1–3 [poor]). Outcome prediction remained robust, with a pattern of higher sensitivity but lower specificity (training: sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.64; test: sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.59; validation: sensitivity 0.75, specificity 0.64; Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 4). Similar performance was observed for survival prediction (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Prognosis in CA and TBI subgroups with multimodal prognostic models

The prognostic model reported in the previous section included only basic clinical variables (age, aetiology, and GCS score). In the following, we considered more sophisticated multimodal prognostic models in the two subgroups, i.e., TBI and CA. For the TBI group, we additionally considered the IMPACT core and IMPACT core + CT models. The clinical-EEG model outperformed the IMPACT core model and showed comparable performance to the IMPACT core + CT model (Table S6). Furthermore, including the EEG name response as a feature further boosted the performance of the IMPACT core + CT model, showing an AUC of 0.92 for 6-month outcome prediction. For the CA subgroup, we additionally considered time to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC, Supplementary Fig. 6), EEG background patterns (Supplementary Fig. 7), and neuron-specific enolase (NSE, a blood biomarker of neuronal injury, with higher values indicating more severe injury, Supplementary Fig. 8) as features. A multivariable model including all these predictors (i.e., age, GCS, time to ROSC, EEG background, NSE) achieved a similar AUC to the clinical-EEG model (i.e., age, GCS, EEG response to names) at 1 month, but lower AUC at 3 and 6 months post-injury (Table S7). Including the EEG name response as an additional feature further boosted the performance of the multivariable model, showing AUCs of 0.85, 0.86, and 0.86 at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injury.

Discussion

To reliably measure residual cognitive ability in comatose ICU patients, this study presented highly salient and self-relevant stimuli, i.e., familiar names, and quantified the neural response using a frequency-tagging EEG experimental design. Our results revealed potential preservation of covert cognition in a subset of unresponsive patients, indicated by reliable EEG responses to familiar names. The name response significantly correlated with patient outcomes as assessed by GOSE scores. Based on the EEG name responses and basic clinical characteristics, a linear model achieved promising predictive performance for the outcomes at 1, 3, and 6 months after injury. Since this model requires only bedside EEG measurement and the analysis pipeline is fully automatic, it provides a solution for resource-limited clinical settings. The current experiment took about 40 min but the acoustic control condition could be dropped in future applications, making the test accomplishable within 20 minutes.

Previous studies have shown that DoC patients exhibit preserved cortical responses to salient stimuli9,16,18,20, supporting the concept of covert cortical processing in unresponsive patients25. Compared with synthesized speech and complex auditory commands that were used in previous studies8,10,26, familiar names spoken by a familiar voice are of much higher salience and personal relevance20,23,27,28. In this study, familiar names elicited significantly stronger responses than the acoustic control, i.e., backward speech. Importantly, backward speech preserves low-level acoustic properties while removing intelligibility and personal salience; therefore, the enhanced tracking for familiar names more plausibly indexes higher-order processing linked to name recognition. In previous studies on healthy participants, however, comparable EEG/MEG responses were observed for intelligible speech (not names) and acoustically matched unintelligible speech (further discussion of the EEG responses to continuous speech is provided in Supplementary Information)29–31. Therefore, more salient stimuli such as names may more effectively drive neural responses in unresponsive patients. Future studies could investigate how intelligibility, familiarity, and self-relevance may separately contribute to prognosis.

Here, we used frequency tagging to improve the reliability of the EEG measurement. The approach repeats names at a constant frequency f, causing the neural response to names to also repeat at f. Instead of estimating the entire time course of name-related ERPs, the frequency-tagging approach selectively analyses the response at f, which dramatically reduces the number of parameters to estimate and increases the signal-to-noise ratio24. The frequency-tagging approach can capture classic ERPs to each stimulus of interest, although the periodic presentation of the stimulus creates a strong expectation, which may additionally engage slow neural oscillations24.

Previous studies have demonstrated the value of EEG in predicting outcomes for acute unconscious patients using both resting-state EEG features and stimulus-based paradigms8,10,32–36. For example, Fischer et al. showed that the novelty P3 to SON in auditory oddball paradigms could predict awakening within three months20. Conceptually, oddball paradigms probe the capacity to detect a rare, salient deviant within a stream of standards, and may require residual higher-order cognitive abilities. Comparatively, the frequency-tagging approach probes whether the brain can generate a reliable, sustained response by presenting repeated high-salience information over time in the frequency domain; this design may be less vulnerable to occasional missed detections and may tolerate fluctuating arousal20,37. Notably, the EEG name responses were observed even in patients with malignant EEG backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We therefore interpret these responses as reflecting residual stimulus tracking in a prognostic “grey zone”11,38, rather than intact higher-order cognition. Consistent with this interpretation, our head-to-head comparison indicates a sensitivity-specificity trade-off: frequency tagging was more sensitive, while SON-P3 was more specific (Table S2). These results support a complementary workflow in which frequency tagging serves as an initial bedside screen, followed by SON oddball ERP to increase specificity when feasible.

Notably, while EEG is a standard prognostic tool in cardiac arrest, its utility in TBI has traditionally been viewed as limited due to lesion heterogeneity. However, our stratified analysis reveals that the TBI subgroup exhibited the incremental prognostic value of EEG. This is likely because TBI outcomes are often driven by diffuse axonal injury or network disconnection39,40, which can be difficult to infer from structural imaging or clinical evaluations. A functional EEG marker that captures preserved stimulus tracking may therefore add value in TBI by indexing residual cortical processing that is not readily reflected by structural measures.

Our study also has some limitations. First, although patients were recruited from five sites and an independent validation dataset was included, the number of patients achieving good functional recovery (GOSE ≥ 4) was relatively small; therefore, the findings require validation in larger cohorts, including adequately powered subgroup analyses in both CA and TBI. Second, there was variation in the timing of EEG data collection after brain injury due to challenges obtaining consent and coordinating with clinicians, making it unclear what the optimal time window for assessing neural responses is. Third, while we excluded patients under deep sedation, 52% of the participants were under moderate sedation during the EEG recordings, and sedation may have lingering effects on neurological status for up to 72–96 h after discontinuation in severely ill patients. However, EEG response and model performance showed no significant difference between sedated and un-sedated patients (see Supplementary Information). Fourth, absent EEG name response in individual patients should be interpreted cautiously due to potential hearing loss. Future studies should investigate the influence of hearing loss on the EEG name response in comatose patients, e.g., by measuring the auditory brainstem response, and exclude patients with profound or complete hearing loss (e.g., pure tone threshold > 80 dB, ~1-2% prevalence in elderly listeners and TBI patients41,42). Here, we tested the influence of hearing loss on the EEG name response using an additional group of healthy participants. A weak but positive correlation was found between pure-tone audiometric threshold and the EEG name response (Supplementary Fig. 9), consistent with previous findings that EEG speech-tracking activity is slightly enhanced in hearing-impaired listeners43,44. Fifth, our behavioural assessment relied on the GCS and GOSE rather than the coma recovery scale-revised (CRS-R), which may limit our ability to differentiate DoC subgroups and to refine prognostic categories. Future studies should incorporate rapid yet sensitive scales such as the FOUR score or SECONDs, and, when feasible, the CRS-R, to improve diagnostic and prognostic precision. Finally, the correlation between EEG name response and outcome is not perfect, as some patients with strong responses still had poor outcomes and conversely some patients with weak responses recovered.

To translate this frequency-tagging paradigm into routine clinical practice, several steps are needed in future studies. First, the EEG name-response provides incremental prognostic value when added to established models, supporting its role as an add-on biomarker rather than a replacement for current tools. This could be implemented as an analysis module integrated with existing markers and a complementary auditory oddball response. Second, a practical next step is to translate this paradigm into a standardised bedside ICU workflow and provide an individual-level predicted probability of recovery. Third, future work should further test stimulus specificity by introducing alternative control conditions, such as the patient’s name spoken by an unfamiliar voice. Finally, larger prospective multicentre studies are required to confirm generalizability across aetiologies, define the optimal recording window after injury, and quantify the effects of sedation and peripheral auditory impairment. Given the multifactorial nature of recovery, this EEG marker will likely add most value when integrated with clinical variables, imaging, and other prognostic measures.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the EEG response to familiar names, measured using bedside EEG and automatically analysed using the frequency-tagging method, is related to the outcome of comatose ICU patients. This measure is not intended to replace established multimodal prognostic tools. However, as a convenient bedside measurement, the method could be useful for outcome prediction in comatose ICU patients, especially in resource-limited settings where advanced assessments are unavailable or impractical.

Methods

Participants

This prospective multicentre study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04411719) was initially conducted in four ICUs (dataset 1). A fifth ICU was subsequently included as an external validation cohort (dataset 2) (see details in Supplementary Information). All patients admitted with acute brain injury were regularly screened within 72 h on ICU admission. Inclusion criteria were: (1) in a coma state (defined as a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 8 or less) upon admission and at the time of screening; (2) diagnosis of traumatic brain injury or cardiac arrest (CA); and (3) age of 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pre-existing DoC before the injury; (2) pre-injury deafness based on family reports or medical records (likely indicating profound hearing loss that severely affected daily communication); (3) lack of ethical consent from the patient’s surrogates; and (4) administration of deep sedation within the 24 hours prior to assessment. Deep sedation was defined as meeting any of the following dosage thresholds: propofol ≥ 4 mg/kg/h, midazolam ≥ 0.2 mg/kg/h or fentanyl ≥ 2 μg/kg/h6,45. Patients were required to meet all inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria to be enrolled. Since stopping sedation was deemed unsafe in some cases, 52% of included patients were under sedation prior to assessment (details in Supplementary Information).

Additionally, 31 age-matched healthy control participants were recruited and underwent the same EEG protocol as the patients. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the coordinating centre (the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine) and from the Research Ethics Committees of each participating centre. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients’ surrogates for the patient experiment and from healthy volunteers for the control experiment, respectively. The patients’ surrogates also provided consent for participation in outcome interviews.

Clinical data acquisition

GCS scores were assessed by a trained ICU physician on admission (after initial stabilisation), during screening, and at the time of EEG assessments. For intubated patients, the verbal score was calculated using the Rutledge model based on the eye and motor subscales46. Functional outcomes were evaluated using the GOSE (range from 1 to 8, with higher scores indicating better outcomes) at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after the injury through a structured telephone interview if the patients had been discharged from the hospital47. The interviewer conducting the outcome assessments was blinded to the results of prior examinations, including routine clinical evaluations, and was unaware of the EEG results. Outcomes were dichotomised in two complementary ways. First, for recovery of consciousness, outcomes were classified as poor (GOSE 1–2: death or vegetative state; no recovery of consciousness) versus good (GOSE 3–8: ranging from severe disability to good recovery; recovery of consciousness). Second, for functional outcome, outcomes were classified as poor (GOSE 1–3) versus good (GOSE 4–8), where GOSE ≥ 4 represents favourable functional recovery.

EEG data acquisition and processing

Auditory name paradigm

The EEG experiment was performed during the presentation of the auditory stimuli. The stimuli consisted of five familiar names, i.e., the patient’s own name and four names of close relatives, which were recorded by a close family member of the patient (54% by the children and 27% by the spouse) in their native dialect to represent typical daily pronunciation. All names were adjusted to the same intensity and duration (0.75 s), following the procedure described in Ding et al.48. In each trial, 30 names (five different names, each repeated six times) were presented in a randomised order, without any gap between names. Therefore, the total duration of a trial was 22.5 s (30 × 0.75 s), and the names were presented at a rate of 1.33 Hz, i.e., 1/0.75 s. Different trials were separated by a silence interval uniformly distributed between 1.5 and 2 s. The auditory stimuli were delivered diotically through insert earphones (Bose® SoundSport® in-ear headphones) at a fixed sound level (90 dB SPL, which was measured by a 1 kHz pure tone that had the same root mean square as the names).

The whole experiment was divided into four blocks, with 30 trials in each block. Two blocks presented names, and the other two blocks presented the acoustic control, which were the same name sequences played backward in time (Fig. 1b). The acoustic control was not intelligible but was matched with the name sequences in terms of the acoustic rhythm and the sound spectrum – reversing a signal in time does not alter the power spectrum. The blocks presenting names and acoustic control stimuli alternated, with the order counterbalanced across participants. After each block, a rest period was provided to allow for patient status checks and to give medical staff time to perform necessary clinical duties. The auditory stimuli were delivered using MATLAB Psychtoolbox.

EEG recording and processing

EEG data were collected at the patients’ bedside using a 64-channel active BrainCap (Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany) placed according to a standard 10–20 system. Impedance across all channels was maintained below 10 kΩ. FCz and AFz served as online reference and ground electrodes, respectively. The EEG recording was digitised with a sample rate of 1000 Hz.

Data preprocessing and analysis was performed using EEGLAB 2020.049 and MATLAB (version 2020a, MathWorks). The raw continuous EEG signal was down-sampled to 100 Hz, re-referenced to the average of mastoid, and bandpass filtered between 0.5 and 40 Hz using a Butterworth filter (zero phase shift, filter order: 6). All data were visually inspected, and bad channels contaminated with excessive noise were interpolated using the spherical spline method in EEGLAB (average number of interpolated channels: 3 ± 2, mean ± SD across participants). Five patients with more than 10 bad channels were excluded from further analysis. Independent component analysis was then performed, and artefactual components were identified and discarded using the EEGLAB plugin ICLabel50.

Data were segmented into 22.5-second epochs corresponding to individual trials. To calculate the difference EEG waveform, each epoch of EEG response to the acoustic control epochs was subtracted from each epoch of EEG response to names in the time domain. After subtraction, epochs were averaged across trials for each condition, i.e., names, control, or their difference, and transformed into the frequency domain using the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), resulting in a frequency resolution of 0.044 Hz/bin. Since names were presented at a fixed rate of 0.75 s, we therefore focused on the EEG power at the corresponding frequency i.e. 1.33 Hz (calculated as 1/0.75 s) and its harmonics. The response power at 1.33 Hz (i.e., name frequency), 2.67 Hz (i.e. two times the name frequency) and 4 Hz (i.e., three times the name frequency) was normalised by dividing the mean power averaged over two frequency bins on each side of the frequency. The normalised power was expressed in dB, with 0 dB representing equal response relative to the noise floor. The power was averaged across a frontocentral electrode cluster sensitive to auditory responses (Fz, Cz, F1, F2, FC1, FC2)51.

A one-sided exact test was used to identify significant EEG responses in individual patients. The power at the target frequency was compared to the power over all other frequency bins between 0.5 and 4 Hz, excluding the harmonics of the target frequency (i.e., 2.67 Hz and 4 Hz). The null hypothesis assumed that the power at the target frequency was not significantly higher than the power in neighbouring frequencies. The statistical significance (exact P-value) of the response at the target frequency was determined as the percent of frequency bins that had power stronger than the target frequency8.

Outcome prediction model

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to predict patient outcomes. The clinical-only model included three clinical features—age, aetiology, and the GCS score on admission. These clinical features were selected based on their established relevance to prognosis52–55. The EEG-only model incorporated the 1.33-Hz, 2.67-Hz, and 4-Hz EEG responses to names and the difference response between names and acoustic control at the same three frequencies. The combined clinical-EEG model integrated the EEG responses with the clinical features. The outcome variable was defined as either good or poor based on the GOSE score (see Clinical data acquisition). Each model was evaluated separately for predicting outcomes at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injury. Additionally, the same three models were adapted to predict survival.

Dataset 1 consisting of 63 patients was split into training (N = 37, approximately 60%) and test (N = 26, approximately 40%) sets. The distributions of aetiology and outcomes were balanced across two datasets. Since the number of patients in each class (i.e., good or poor outcomes; survival or death) are not balanced, oversampling was applied to the class with fewer samples to match the sample size of the other class. This oversampling process was repeated 1000 times, each with a different random seed. An LDA model was trained on the training set using Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). The mean accuracy from the LOOCV was used to identify the optimal regularisation parameter. For each patient, the mean probability across the 1000 resampling iterations was used as the final prediction. The trained LDA models from each of the 1000 iterations were applied to the test set and generated a predicted probability for each patient. Dataset 2, including 18 patients, was used as an external validation set.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were calculated to evaluate the predictive performance of the model. The optimal decision threshold was determined on the training set using the Youden Index56, which gets the highest sum of sensitivity and specificity. Patients with predicted probabilities exceeding this threshold were classified as having a good outcome/survival, while those with probabilities below the threshold were classified as having a poor outcome/death. The overall classification performance was evaluated by using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, and negative likelihood ratio. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated using a 1000-time bootstrap for both performance metrics and prediction probabilities for individual patients.

Multimodal data

In addition to the basic clinical variables (age, aetiology, and GCS score), we considered additional predictors commonly used in multimodal prognostic models for patients after cardiac arrest and TBI. For patients after CA, the time from collapse to ROSC, defined as the first return of a palpable pulse was collected57,58. Routine 19-channel video-EEG monitoring was performed within 96 h of injury, with continuous recordings for 24–72 h. The EEG data were visually analysed by two experienced electrophysiological reviewers and classified into highly malignant (generalized suppression, burst suppression), malignant (discontinuous, low voltage) and benign (continuous) EEG background59,60. Neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels were measured within 24 hours, 48 hours and 7 days after injury. All but one patient (39/40, 97.5%) after cardiac arrest had NSE data. Among the 39 patients, 30 (76.9%) had more than one NSE measurement. Based on these predictors, a multimodal prognostic model was constructed for the CA subgroup by including age, GCS score, time to ROSC, EEG background pattern, and NSE levels.

For patients after TBI, predictors for the IMPACT core and core + CT models were collected3. The core predictors included age, GCS motor score, and pupillary reactivity. Additional predictors including the presence of hypoxia, hypotension, Marshall CT classification, epidural haematoma, and traumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage (tSAH) were incorporated into the IMPACT core + CT model.

Statistics

All statistical tests were based on bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap61. In the bootstrap procedure, all participants were resampled with replacement 10000 times. To test the significance of a spectral peak at frequency f, the response power at f was compared with the mean power of the neighbouring four frequency bins using a bootstrap approach. The significance coefficient was calculated as (2 N + 1)/10001, where N equalled the number of times the response power at f was stronger than that at neighbouring frequencies in the resampled data. The same resampling procedure was used to compare the response to names and acoustic control. To test the correlation between the neural responses and outcomes, Spearman’s correlations were conducted. When multiple comparisons were performed, P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All P values were two-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as a P value of less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using MATLAB, version 2020a.
