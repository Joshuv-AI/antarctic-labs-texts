Abstract
. In this note, we construct examples of bounded smooth convex domains with no non-trivial analytic discs on the boundary which possess a holomorphic self-map without fixed points so that the iterates do not converge to a point (that is, the Denjoy-Wolff theorem does not hold). We also show that, in the case of bounded convex domains with C 1 + ε C^{1+\varepsilon} -smooth boundary which have non-trivial analytic discs on the boundary, the cluster set of the orbits of holomorphic self-maps without fixed points can be equal to the principal part of any prime end of any planar bounded simply connected domain.

30C35; 30D05; 30D40; 32F45.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a bounded domain,
N\geq 1
. Let
F\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\Omega)
be a holomorphic self-map of
\Omega
. The
target set of
F
is the set

T(F):=\{z\in\partial{\Omega}:\exists z_{0}\in\Omega,\>\>\text{and a sequence}\>\>\{n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}\>\>\text{such that}\>\>F^{\circ n_{k}}(z_{0})\to z\>\>\text{as}\>\>k\to\infty\}.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 1
, satisfies
the Denjoy-Wolff property
if for any holomorphic self-map
F
of
\Omega
either
T(F)=\emptyset
or there exists a point
p\in\partial\Omega
such that the sequence of iterates
\{F^{\circ n}\}
of
F
converges uniformly on compacta to the constant map
z\mapsto p
, that is
T(F)=\{p\}
.

It is a well known and classical result in complex analysis that the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
in
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the Denjoy-Wolff property. This fact was independently proven by Denjoy and Wolff
[
18
,
27
,
28
]
at the beginning of the last century. Since then, it has been a fundamental tool in the iteration theory of holomorphic maps and it has been extended in many different direction by several mathematicians (see,
e.g.
,
[
2
]
, and
[
23
]
in the context of distance non-expansive maps of Gromov hyperbolic metric spaces).

If
\Omega
is convex, Abate (see,
[
2
, Theorem 2.4.20]
) proved that if
f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\Omega)
then
T(F)=\emptyset
if and only if
f
has fixed points in
\Omega
. Thus, for a bounded convex domain
\Omega
the Denjoy-Wolff property holds if and only if
T(F)
contains only one point for every holomorphic self-map
F
of
\Omega
without fixed points.

The first generalization to higher dimensions of the classical Denjoy-Wolff theorem appeared in the second half of the last century, when Hervé
[
21
]
proved that the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{N}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
verifies the Denjoy-Wolff property. Abate in
[
1
,
2
]
extended the result of Hervé to strongly convex domains with
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth boundaries. Later Budzyńska
[
14
]
(see also
[
4
]
) removed the boundary regularity assumptions and extended this result to strictly convex domains, while in
[
12
]
the same result has been proved for bounded convex domains which are Gromov hyperbolic with respect to the Kobayashi distance—such a class includes, for instance, smooth D’Angelo finite type bounded convex domains (see
[
29
,
30
]
). See also
[
15
,
17
]
and references therein for relevant results about the Denjoy-Wolff theorem in complex Banach spaces and in symmetric domains.

However, the basic feature of Gromov hyperbolicity which allows the Denjoy-Wolff theorem is the “bending inside” property of geodesics. Based on such observation, recently, Bharali and Zimmer
[
8
]
(see also
[
13
]
) introduced the notion of “visibility” and Bharali and Maitra
[
7
, Theorem 1.8]
proved if
\Omega
is a visible bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
then it satisfies the Denjoy-Wolff property.

However, in general, visibility is a stronger condition that the Denjoy-Wolff property: in
[
6
, Corollary 1.6]
it is proved that there exist bounded simply connected domains in
\mathbb{C}
which are not visible but for which the Denjoy-Wolff property holds.

On the other hand, the only known examples in the literature of convex domains not having the Denjoy-Wolff property are product domains: if
D_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N_{1}}
,
D_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N_{2}}
are two bounded convex domains and
F\in\mathcal{O}(D_{1},D_{1})
is any holomorphic self-map without fixed points, then the holomorphic self-map
D_{1}\times D_{2}\ni(z,w)\mapsto(f(z),w)
of the bounded convex domain
\Omega:=D_{1}\times D_{2}
does not have fixed points in
\Omega
and its target set contains
\{p\}\times D_{2}
for some
p\in\partial D_{1}
. In particular,
\Omega
does not have the Denjoy-Wolff property. Even in this case however, it is not clear what the target set could really be. We mention here that in the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, Hervé
[
20
]
proved that the target set of a holomorphic self-map of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
has to be contained in either
\{e^{i\theta}\}\times\overline{\mathbb{D}}
or in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\times\{e^{i\theta}\}
(for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
). So, apparently, the existence of non-trivial analytic discs on the boundary is the primary obstruction of the Denjoy-Wolff property.

The main aim of this paper is to deal with (non-trivial) constructions of holomorphic self-maps of bounded convex domains for which the Denjoy-Wolff property fails and to examine the possible target set in case the domain contains a non-trivial analytic disc in the boundary. Indeed it turns out that there exist bounded convex domains with smooth boundaries containing no non-trivial analytic discs for which the Denjoy-Wolff property does not hold:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a bounded convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
, such that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth,
\partial\Omega
does not contain non-trivial analytic discs and
\Omega
does not have the Denjoy-Wolff property.

Our construction, in fact, shows that there is a complex affine line
L
such that
\partial\Omega\cap L\neq\emptyset
and a holomorphic self-map
F
of
\Omega
such that
T(F)\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap L
.

In case of bounded convex domains with non-trivial analytic discs on the boundary, we can also prove the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
be a bounded convex domain with
C^{1+\varepsilon}
-smooth boundary. Suppose that
\partial\Omega
contains a complex affine disc. Let
K
be the principal part of any prime end of any bounded simply connected domain in
\mathbb{C}
. Then there exist a holomorphic self-map
F
of
\Omega
without fixed points such that, up to homotheties,
T(F)=K\subset\Delta
.

In particular,
T(F)
can be as “bad” as the principal part of any bounded simply connected domain, for instance, it can have non-integer Hausdorff dimension.

Also, it follows from the previous theorem that if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, is a bounded convex domain with
\mathcal{C}^{1+\varepsilon}
-smooth boundary for some
\varepsilon>0
and satisfies the Denjoy-Wolff property, then
\partial\Omega
does not contain non-trivial analytic discs.

Existence of non-trivial analytic discs in the boundary of convex domains prevents visibility. Also, in
[
13
, Section 5]
and
[
25
, Section 5]
there are examples of smooth bounded convex domains with no non-trivial analytic discs on the boundary such that the order of contact of the boundary with a complex affine line is “very high” and do not enjoy visibility. These similitudes between the (little-known) conditions for a bounded convex domain to be visible and for satisfying the Denjoy-Wolff property suggest the following conjecture, which is open even in the smooth case:

Conjecture:

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a bounded convex domain,
N\geq 2
. Then
\Omega
is visible if and only if it satisfies the Denjoy-Wolff property
.

The proofs of our results are based on the following construction. Let
\mathbb{H}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}\zeta>0\}
. Let
V\subset\mathbb{H}
be bounded convex domain such that
0\in\partial V
and
\partial V
is
\mathcal{C}^{1+\varepsilon}
at
0
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\Psi:(0,\delta)\to(0,+\infty)
be a convex strictly increasing function such that
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\Psi(x)=0
. The
wedge

W(V,\Psi,\delta)
is defined as

W(V,\Psi,\delta):=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\|(z_{1},z_{2})\|<\delta,z_{1}\in V,\operatorname{Re}z_{2}>0,\operatorname{Re}z_{1}>\Psi(\operatorname{Re}z_{2})\}.

The set
W(V,\Psi,\delta)
is a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
H:=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:z_{1}=0\}
is a complex supporting hyperplane for
W(V,\Psi,\delta)
such that

I_{V,\Psi,\delta}:=\partial W(V,\Psi,\delta)\cap H=\{(0,it):t\in[-\delta,\delta]\}.

We have:

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
\delta>0
and for every
V\subset\mathbb{H}
bounded convex domain such that
0\in\partial V
and
\partial V
is
\mathcal{C}^{1+\varepsilon}
at
0
for some
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a convex strictly increasing function
\Psi_{0}:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\Psi_{0}(x)=0
with the following property. If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, is any bounded convex domain so that there exists an invertible affine (holomorphic) transformation
L:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
L(W(V,\Psi_{0},\delta))\subset\Omega
and
L(I_{V,\Psi_{0},\delta})\subset\partial\Omega
then
\Omega
does not have the Denjoy-Wolff property.

The proof of the previous theorem is based on the construction of a “one-variable-bidimensional dynamics’ trick, and it is based on the following generalisation of a theorem of Carathéodory, which can be of interest by itself:

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
g:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be univalent. Let
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and let
\{w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
be a sequence converging non-tangentially to
\sigma
such that there exists
C>0
so that
k_{\mathbb{D}}(w_{n},w_{n+1})\leq C
for all
n
. Then

\Pi[\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})]=\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\}),

where
\Pi[\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})]
is the principal part of the prime end of
g(\mathbb{D})
corresponding to
\sigma
and
\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})
denotes the cluster set of
\{g(w_{n})\}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove Proposition
1.5
and some preliminary results. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.4
and, as corollary, Theorem
1.2
. In Section
4
we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
and construct some explicit examples.

2.
Preliminary results

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Recall that the Kobayashi (pseudo)distance
k_{\Omega}
is the inner distance associated to the Kobayashi-Royden pseudometric

\kappa_{\Omega}(z;v):=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}\{|\lambda|:\exists f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},\Omega)\>\>\>\text{such that}\>\>\>f(0)=z,f^{\prime}(0)\lambda=v\}.

The important property of the Kobayashi distance is that it is contracted by holomorphic maps, in particular it is invariant under biholomorphisms. For further details we refer the reader to the standard book
[
22
]
.

Throughout this note, we set
\mathbb{H}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}\zeta>0\}
to be the right half plane, while
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
denotes the Riemann sphere.

The classical Lehtö-Virtanen’s theorem (see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Theorem 3.1.1]
) allows to get information on the non-tangential limit of a univalent map of
\mathbb{D}
by knowing the limit along a curve. Such a result has been generalized for sequences in
[
6
, Lemma 5.4]
(based on an argument from
[
5
, Theorem 1.5]
). In the following we need a more refined version of
[
6
, Lemma 5.4]
. As a matter of notations, if
g:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic map, and
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, let

\begin{split}\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma):=&\{p\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:\hbox{there exists a sequence $\{r_{n}\}\subset(0,1)$ converging to $1$}\\
&\hbox{ so that}\lim_{n\to\infty}g(r_{n}\sigma)=p\}.\end{split}

In other words,
\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma)
is the radial cluster set of
g
at
\sigma
. Also, we let

\begin{split}\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma):=&\{p\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:\hbox{there exists a sequence $\{\zeta_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}$ converging non-tangentially to $\sigma$}\\
&\hbox{ so that}\lim_{n\to\infty}g(z_{n})=p\}.\end{split}

That is,
\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)
is the non-tangential cluster set of
g
at
\sigma
. Moreover, if
\{w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
is a sequence converging to
\sigma
, we let

\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\}):=\{p\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:\hbox{there exists a subsequence $\{w_{n_{k}}\}\subset\mathbb{D}$ so that}\lim_{k\to\infty}g(w_{n_{k}})=p\},

that is,
\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})
is the cluster set of the sequence
\{g(w_{n})\}
.

We also need to recall the concept of principal part of a prime end of a simply connected domain in
\mathbb{C}
(see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Chapter 4]
). Let
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain and let
g:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
be a Riemann map. The map
g
defines a one-to-one correspondence between the points of
\partial\mathbb{D}
and the prime ends of
\Omega
, which are defined in terms of equivalence classes of null-chains
(C_{n})
. If
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, we denote by
\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})
the corresponding prime end in
\Omega
. Hence, the
principal part
of
\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})
is defined as

\begin{split}\Pi[\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})]:=&\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:\hbox{there exist a null-chain $(C_{n})$ representing $\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})$}\\
&\hbox{and a sequence $\{\zeta_{n}\}$ such that }\zeta_{n}\in C_{n}\hbox{ and}\lim_{n\to\infty}\zeta_{n}=\zeta\}.\end{split}

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
g:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be univalent. Let
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and let
\{w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
be a sequence converging non-tangentially to
\sigma
such that there exists
C>0
so that
k_{\mathbb{D}}(w_{n},w_{n+1})\leq C
for all
n
. Then

\Pi[\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})]=\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma)=\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)=\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\}).

Proof.

It is a result due to Carathéodory that
\Pi[\hat{g}(\underline{\sigma})]=\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma)=\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)
(see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Theorem 4.4.9]
). Actually, the equality
\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma)=\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)
can be inferred directly by our proof. Clearly
\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})\subseteq\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)
. Thus we have to prove the converse. Without loss of generality, conjugating with a rotation if necessary, we can assume
\sigma=1
. Let
p\in\Gamma_{N}(g;1)
and let
\{\zeta_{n}\}
be a sequence converging non-tangentially to
1
so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(\zeta_{n})=p
. This means (see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Corollary 6.2.6]
) that there is
C_{0}>0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
r_{n}\in(0,1)
so that

k_{\mathbb{D}}(\zeta_{n},r_{n})\leq C_{0}.

Note that, since
\{\zeta_{n}\}
converges to
1
this implies that
\{r_{n}\}
converges to
1
as well.

Let
\Omega:=g(\mathbb{D})
. Since

k_{\Omega}(g(\zeta_{n}),g(r_{n}))=k_{\mathbb{D}}(\zeta_{n},r_{n})\leq C_{0},

it follows by the “Distance Lemma” (see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Theorem 5.3.1]
) that
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(r_{n})=p
(note that in particular this implies directly
\Gamma_{R}(g;\sigma)=\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)
).

Since
\{w_{n}\}
converges non-tangentially to
1
, by the same token as before, there exists
C_{1}>0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
s_{n}\in(0,1)
so that

k_{\mathbb{D}}(w_{n},s_{n})\leq C_{1}.

We claim that there exist
C_{2}>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
there exists
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
so that

(2.1)

k_{\mathbb{D}}(r_{n},s_{m_{n}})\leq C_{2}.

In particular, since
\{r_{n}\}
converges to
1
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{m_{n}}=1
.
Assuming the claim for the moment, we have for
n\geq n_{0}

\begin{split}k_{\Omega}(g(\zeta_{n}),g(w_{m_{n}}))&=k_{\mathbb{D}}(\zeta_{n},w_{m_{n}})\leq k_{\mathbb{D}}(\zeta_{n},r_{n})+k_{\mathbb{D}}(r_{n},s_{m_{n}})+k_{\mathbb{D}}(s_{m_{n}},w_{m_{n}})\\
&\leq C_{0}+C_{2}+C_{1}.\end{split}

Therefore, by the Distance Lemma,

\lim_{n\to\infty}g(w_{m_{n}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}g(\zeta_{n})=p,

hence
p\in\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})
, that is,
\Gamma_{N}(g;\sigma)\subseteq\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})

In order to prove (
2.1
), note that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

k_{\mathbb{D}}(s_{n},s_{n+1})\leq k_{\mathbb{D}}(s_{n},w_{n})+k_{\mathbb{D}}(s_{n+1},w_{n+1})+k_{\mathbb{D}}(w_{n},w_{n+1})\leq 2C_{1}+C=:C_{2}.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
r_{n}\geq s_{1}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. For every
n
there exists
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{n}\in[s_{m_{n}},s_{m_{n}+1})
. Since
[s_{m_{n}},s_{m_{n}+1}]
is a geodesic for
k_{\mathbb{D}}
it follows that

k_{D}(s_{m_{n}},r_{n})\leq k_{D}(s_{m_{n}},s_{m_{n}+1})\leq C_{2},

and (
2.1
).
∎

In the sequel we will mainly use the previous result for univalent functions on
\mathbb{H}
. Using the Cayley transform from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{H}
one immediately has from Proposition
2.1
:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
g:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
be a univalent function. Let
\{w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{H}
be a sequence converging non-tangentially to
0
such that there exists
C>0
so that
k_{\mathbb{H}}(w_{n},w_{n+1})\leq C
for all
n
. Then

\Gamma_{R}(g;0)=\Gamma_{N}(g;0)=\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\}),

where
\Gamma_{R}(g;0)
is the cluster set of
\{g(r)\}_{r>0}
at
0
,
\Gamma_{N}(g;0)
is the non-tangential cluster set of
g
at
0
and
\Gamma(g;\{w_{n}\})
is the cluster set of
\{g(w_{n})\}
for
n\to\infty
.

In the following we also need the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
h:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be holomorphic. Assume that
h
extends
C^{1}
at
0
and
h(0)=0
. Then
h^{\prime}(0)\in(0,+\infty)
. Moreover, if
h^{\prime}(0)<1
then
\{h^{\circ m}(w)\}
converges non-tangentially to
0
.

Proof.

The first part follows at once by the classical Julia-Wolff-Carathéodory’s theorem for the unit disc (see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Theorem 1.7.3]
) applied to the function
C\circ h\circ C^{-1}
where
C:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
is defined by
C(z)=\frac{1-z}{1+z}
. If
h^{\prime}(0)<1
, since
C
is conformal at
1
and maps
1
to
0
, the result follows from
[
10
, Proposition 1.8.7]
applied to
C\circ h\circ C^{-1}
.
∎

3.
Convex wedges with no Denjoy-Wolff property

Let
V\subset\mathbb{H}
and
g:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
be a bounded holomorphic function. The
graph
of
g
over
V
is

G(V,g):=\{(\zeta,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\zeta\in V,w=g(\zeta)\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}.

As a matter of notation, if
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 3
, we denote by
z^{\prime\prime}:=(z_{3},\ldots,z_{N})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{H}
be a simply connected domain such that
0\in\partial V
and
\partial V
is
\mathcal{C}^{1+\varepsilon}
-smooth at
0
, for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that
g:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
is a bounded univalent function. Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, such that

(i)

\Omega\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{N}:\operatorname{Re}z_{1}>0\}
;

(ii)

G(V,g)\times\{0^{\prime\prime}\}\subset\Omega
.

Then there exists a holomorphic self-map
F
of
\Omega
such that

(1)

F
has no fixed points in
\Omega
,

(2)

T(F)=\{0\}\times\Gamma_{N}(g;0)\times\{0^{\prime\prime}\}
.

In particular, if
g
does not have non-tangential limit at
0
then
\Omega
does not have the Denjoy-Wolff property.

Proof.

Let
P:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
denote the projection
z\mapsto z_{1}
. By hypothesis (i),
P(\Omega)\subset\mathbb{H}
. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{H}\to V
be a biholomorphism. Since
0\in\partial V
and
\partial V
is assumed to be
C^{1+\varepsilon}
at
0
, it follows that
0
is an accessible point of
\partial V
and, in particular, there exists a continous curve
\gamma:[0,1)\to V
such that
\lim_{t\to 1}\gamma(t)=0
. Hence, (see,
e.g.
,
[
10
, Proposition 3.3.3]
), there exists a point
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{H}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
\varphi
has non-tangential limit
0
at
\sigma
. Up to pre-composing
\varphi
with an automorphism of
\mathbb{H}
, we can assume that
\sigma=0
. Also, the hypothesis that
\partial V
is
\mathcal{C}^{1+\varepsilon}
-smooth at the origin, thus, by the local version of the Kellogg-Warschawski theorem
[
26
, Theorem 1]
,
\varphi
extends
C^{1}
at
0
. In particular,
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)
is continuous at
0
. By Lemma
2.3
,
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
.

Let
\lambda>0
be such that
\lambda\varphi^{\prime}(0)<1
, let
h(z_{1}):=\varphi(\lambda z_{1})
,
z_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
. Note that
h\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
,
h
is
C^{1}
at
0
,
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)<1
. Therefore again by Lemma
2.3
, for every
\zeta\in\mathbb{H}
the sequence
\{h^{\circ m}(\zeta)\}
converges non-tangentially to
0
. Moreover, by construction,
h(\mathbb{H})\subseteq V
.

Now, for
z\in\Omega
, let
f(z):=(h\circ P)(z)=h(z_{1})
and let
