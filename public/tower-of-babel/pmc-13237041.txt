Slope displacement forecasting with limited field data using time series model
Authors: Iftakhar Al Mahmud, Yifei Li, A Q M Zohuruzzaman, Shams E Shifat, Sadik Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSlope displacement forecasting is crucial for geotechnical early warning. Conventional deterministic models cannot adapt easily to real-time monitoring, and large deep-learning models demand extensive data and compute. We leverage IBM’s Tiny Time Mixer (TTM), a compact, MLP-Mixer inspired time-series model with specialized temporal and channel mixing that scales linearly with sequence length. TTM was applied to daily aggregated data from six instrumented highway embankment slopes, including soil moisture, matric suction and temperature at 1.5–5 m depths, plus rainfall. These inputs yielded 16-channel time series of roughly 950–998 points per slope, while the displacement target is available only at sparse measurement times, comprising approximately 36–38 inclinometer surveys per slope over ~ 2.75 years. A transfer-learning protocol was used; the model was sequentially fine-tuned on four slopes and evaluated zero-shot on two held-out slopes. On trained slopes, TTM forecasts proved highly accurate. For example, on the active Terry Road slope the fine-tuned model gave MAE ≈ 0.394 mm (R2=0.99), and on the I-220 N Ramp MAE ≈ 0.257 mm (R2=0.97). Even on more stable slopes, absolute errors were sub-millimetric (e.g. MAE ≈ 0.005–0.023 mm, with R2=0.84 and 0.72). Crucially, in zero-shot tests the model maintained high accuracy on unseen sites: the Sowell Road slope forecast had MAE = 0.132 mm (R2=0.99) and McRaven Road MAE = 0.203 mm (R2=0.96). These results indicate that learned precipitation–displacement relationships transfer robustly across slopes. Overall, this study shows that lightweight, efficient time-series models like TTM can deliver accurate, real-time slope forecasts for early warning in resource-constrained geotechnical monitoring applications.Keywords: slope stability, time series forecasting, geotechnical monitoring, transfer learning, TTMSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Slope stability monitoring remains a central task in geotechnical engineering, where continuous assessment of evolving conditions enables early warning systems that can prevent catastrophic failures. Most monitoring campaigns yield only a few hundred to roughly 1,000 readings over multi‑year periods1, with real projects often logging 950–998 points2. In the context of geotechnical slope monitoring, limited field data typically arises not because environmental variables cannot be logged frequently, but because the key target variable, subsurface lateral displacement, is often measured only intermittently (e.g., inclinometer surveys conducted weeks to months apart due to cost, access, and operational constraints).

Classical slope stability analysis has long relied on deterministic, physics-based formulations. The Limit Equilibrium Method evaluates static equilibrium of potential failure masses via slice-based Factor of Safety calculations3, whereas the Finite Element Method resolves stress-strain behavior by discretizing the domain, demanding detailed inputs and substantial computation while remaining sensitive to parameter uncertainty4. Though powerful in design stages, these methods are essentially static and not feasible to adapt for real-time monitoring or evolving field conditions.

As instrumentation expanded and temporal records became available, attention shifted toward data-driven models. Early applications of Artificial Neural Networks captured nonlinear behavior without imposing explicit physical assumptions5, yet their performance deteriorated when training sets dipped below four figures6. Support Vector Machines showed respectable generalization with modest data7, but effectiveness hinged on kernel selection and waned as sparsity increased7,8. Ensemble learners such as XGBoost delivered strong results on structured, tabular inputs and rainfall-induced landslides9, while offering interpretable feature importance, but they did not inherently capture sequential dependence, central to monitoring trajectories.

Precipitation is a well-known trigger for slope instability, as rainwater infiltration elevates pore pressure and weakens soil shear strength. Under the influence of rainfall, many slopes exhibit continuous but episodic deformation, often following a stepwise pattern, accelerating during wet periods and stabilizing in dry periods. These characteristics make real-time monitoring and forecasting of slope displacement critically important for early warning and risk mitigation. An accurate predictive model can provide advance notice of accelerating movements, enabling preventive measures to reduce losses.

Over the past decade, diverse time-series modeling approaches have been applied to forecast landslide or slope displacements. Traditional statistical models like Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) have been used to capture trends and periodic components in slope movement records10. However, purely statistical methods struggle with the highly nonlinear and multi-factor nature of landslide processes. Least-squares SVM model (optimized with wavelet denoising and genetic algorithms) significantly outperformed a baseline neural network in predicting rainfall-triggered landslide displacements11. Deep learning models, particularly recurrent neural networks such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), have achieved success in landslide displacement forecasting by learning temporal dependencies from past displacement series. LSTM-based models can inherently account for memory effects in displacement time series and have demonstrated higher accuracy than traditional models in several cases12–16. Researchers have also begun exploring Transformer-based architectures for slope deformation time series. Transformer models leverage self-attention mechanisms to identify important time lags and long-range dependencies, potentially capturing the varying influence of past events on current displacement17. The emergence in 2024 of foundation-scale time series models-Moment (348 M parameters)18, TimesFM (200 M parameters)19, and Chronos (20-710 M parameters)20-appeared to offer large-scale pre-training on billions of sequences, shows promise for adapting time series models to slope stability analysis.

Despite these advances, several limitations persist with existing forecasting models. Many state-of-the-art time series models are data-hungry and computationally intensive. Deep learning models such as LSTMs typically contain a large number of trainable parameters, which not only require substantial training data but also increase computational cost. Another challenge is the generalization of models beyond the conditions on which they were trained. A model tuned to one landslide may not perform well on another without retraining. Indeed, most data-driven methods in the literature are developed case-by-case; there has been little success in learning a universal predictor that transfers to unseen slope sites.

Motivated by these considerations, this study explores a novel lightweight time-series model for slope displacement prediction: IBM’s Tiny Time Mixer (TTM). While ARIMA variants are computationally lightweight, they are fundamentally univariate and assume linear stationarity, neither condition holds for slope displacement driven by nonlinear hydromechanical coupling across nine concurrent sensor channels, and extending to vector autoregression becomes parameter-intractable below 1,000 observations. MLP-based architecture such as N-BEATS, N-HiTS, and TSMixer support multivariate inputs but lack pre-trained backbones, requiring all temporal representations to be learned from the target dataset al.one; with fewer than 1,000 daily observations per slope, this creates substantial overfitting risk. TTM is the only architecture in this class that combines a pre-trained temporal backbone with a dedicated exogenous channel mixer for structured multi-sensor inputs and frozen-backbone fine-tuning that minimises overfitting under data scarcity. The TTM model offers a compact neural architecture (on the order of only ~ 1 million parameters) based on MLP-Mixer principles21. Multi-level Tiny Time Mixers (TTM), built on the TSMixer paradigm22, separates temporal and feature mixing inside specialized blocks, achieving linear complexity and learning effectively from short sequences21.

Transfer learning emphasizes the portability of this framework. Each monitored slope presents unique geological conditions, so naïve model reuse rarely suffices. By freezing backbone parameters and tuning a lightweight decoder, TTM retains broad temporal knowledge while adapting locally, preventing catastrophic forgetting when fine-tuned on fewer than 1,000 points. Sequential fine-tuning across multiple slopes lets the model accumulate experience, enabling zero-shot forecasts for emergencies and few-shot updates with only days of new data16,21. Efficient inference on battery-powered, CPU-based devices keeps the solution aligned with typical field constraints.

This study addresses the limitations of conventional machine-learning approaches for continuous slope displacement forecasting under data- and resource-constrained conditions by employing Multi-level Tiny Time Mixers (TTM), a compact architecture designed for few-shot and zero-shot forecasting and suitable for CPU/GPU deployment. We validate the approach using six real-world slope monitoring datasets and a sequential fine-tuning protocol that promotes cross-site transfer and enables zero-shot deployment on previously unobserved slopes. Each slope contains only 36–38 inclinometer displacement observations over ~ 2.75 years. To align these sparse displacement measurements with routinely recorded environmental covariates, we aggregate to daily resolution, yielding 950–998 samples per slope. Although fewer than 1,000 points may appear substantial by general time-series standards, this remains small for training deep models from scratch.

Together, these strands of prior work motivate the methodological choices that follow. the specific contributions of this study are: (i) the first application of a pre-trained time series foundation model with a dedicated exogenous channel mixer to multi-sensor slope displacement forecasting under a sparse-data constraint of fewer than 1,000 observations per site; (ii) a sequential cross-slope fine-tuning protocol demonstrating reliable zero-shot displacement forecasting at unseen sites without any local retraining; and (iii) the first application of cross-slope conformal prediction intervals to slope displacement forecasting, providing statistically rigorous uncertainty quantification calibrated entirely on residuals from previously trained slopes. Together, these contributions establish light pre-trained time series foundation models as a viable framework for data-scarce, multi-sensor geotechnical monitoring applications where conventional deep learning architectures are precluded by dataset size. The next section details the model design, pre-training corpus, fine-tuning procedure, and evaluation protocols used to substantiate the proposed approach.

Methods

Monitoring network and site context

All six slopes are highway embankments underlain by Yazoo Clay, a highly expansive, low-permeability marine clay of Eocene age geographically confined to central Mississippi, southern Alabama, and Louisiana23,29. The slopes share a common soil type, a humid subtropical climate with mean annual rainfall of approximately 1,400 mm, and similar embankment geometry and construction history under MDOT State Studies 286 and 31623,24. This geological and hydrological homogeneity ensures that the six slopes constitute an internally consistent dataset for sequential transfer learning, while the variation in displacement activity across sites, from actively creeping slopes such as Terry Road to more stable slopes such as Metro Center and I-20 W, provides a meaningful range of hydromechanical behaviours for the model to learn from.

Instrumentation scheme and data collection protocols

A comprehensive field instrumentation system was deployed at each slope to monitor critical geotechnical parameters influencing slope stability. Industrial-grade sensors were strategically positioned at multiple depths and locations to capture spatial and temporal variations in soil conditions. The monitoring infrastructure employed standardized equipment configurations across all sites to ensure data consistency and enable robust cross-slope transfer learning.

Soil moisture (m³/m³) was measured using GS‑1 sensors at 1.5-, 3-, and 5-meter depths; Meter Teros 21 sensors captured water potential (kPa) at the same depths; RT‑1 sensors recorded soil and air temperatures; and ECRN‑50 tipping-bucket rain gauges with EM50 data loggers recorded precipitation (mm). Inclinometer casings of 9-meter depth were surveyed bi-weekly to monthly. Environmental variables were logged hourly from August 2018 through June 2021, yielding approximately 2.75 years of high-resolution coverage and 36–38 inclinometer readings per slope, with more frequent campaigns in 2019 and monthly intervals thereafter.

Dataset preparation and feature engineering

Initial data collection yielded substantial datasets for each monitoring site, with hourly measurements across 11 primary parameters. The instrumentation network captured soil moisture (m³/m³ VWC), soil temperature (K), and matric suction (kPa) at three depths (1.5 m, 3 m, 5 m), supplemented by air temperature (K) and precipitation (mm) measurements. Comprehensive quality control procedures were implemented to ensure data integrity and reproducibility across the monitoring network. Sensor spikes and physically implausible readings were identified using the interquartile range (IQR) method applied independently to each channel on each slope: values falling below Q1–1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were flagged as outliers and removed. This threshold was applied per-channel rather than globally to account for the differing physical ranges of soil moisture, matric suction, temperature, and precipitation across sites and depths. Duplicate timestamps arising from data logger resets or communication errors were identified and removed, retaining the first valid record at each timestamp. Missing values resulting from both outlier removal and sensor malfunctions were replaced by the channel-wise median computed over the full available record for that slope and sensor depth. Median imputation was selected over mean imputation because the median is robust to residual extreme values and sensor drift not captured by the IQR filter, providing a more stable central tendency estimate for channels such as matric suction and precipitation that exhibit irregular temporal distributions. It is nonetheless important to acknowledge the limitations of this approach: missing data in geotechnical monitoring typically arises from three sources: isolated point dropouts due to power or communication failures, short contiguous outages of several days caused by sensor malfunction, and long-term channel loss from permanent instrument failure. Median imputation is well-suited to the first category but becomes progressively less appropriate as gap duration increases, as substituting a static central tendency for dynamically evolving signal risks suppressing the hydromechanical features most relevant to displacement forecasting. No channels were dropped entirely due to malfunction; all input channels were retained across all six slopes following imputation. Following outlier removal and imputation, processed hourly datasets ranged from 22,823 to 25,169 observations per slope. Temporal aggregation from hourly to daily resolution employed domain-appropriate statistics: soil moisture, temperature, and matric suction were computed as daily means to represent characteristic daily conditions, while precipitation was summed over 24-hour periods to preserve total daily water input. Advanced feature engineering expanded the dataset from 11 to 15 variables through the incorporation of precipitation memory effects. Recognizing the cumulative influence of antecedent rainfall on slope stability, additional precipitation variables were systematically engineered, including previous day precipitation, 7-day, 14-day, and 21-day cumulative precipitation windows. The sparse temporal resolution of inclinometer measurements (36–38 surveys per slope over ~ 2.75 years) introduced a methodological challenge for time-series forecasting. To construct a daily target series aligned with the environmental covariates, we interpolated displacement values between successive surveys piecewise linear interpolation in time, producing continuous daily trajectories from the sparse inclinometer records. This approach preserves long-term trends and enables multi-step forecasting. Environmental parameter characterization

The environmental record reveals coupled hydro-thermal behavior central to slope stability. Volumetric moisture content responds to precipitation with depth-dependent lags, shallow sensors reacting promptly while deeper sensors exhibit delayed increases indicative of low-permeability infiltration. Water potential records show seasonal matric suction cycles, typically dropping during rainfall events and signaling reduced effective stress. Soil temperature profiles (Fig. 3) display seasonal patterns with depth-specific phase shifts, reflecting thermal diffusion effects that can influence pore pressures. Air temperature provides the necessary atmospheric context. These multimodal, temporally entangled signals form the substrate that the forecasting model must learn to interpret.

TTM architecture and channel design

The TTM model processes time series data through a lightweight and scalable architecture designed for efficient forecasting. First, input sequences are channel-wise normalized to zero mean and unit variance. These normalized sequences are then divided into non-overlapping temporal patches, forming a tensor , where c is the number of channels, n is the number of patches, and pl is the patch length. Each patch is embedded into a hidden dimension space using a learnable linear projection, resulting in 21. A learnable resolution prefix token is optionally prepended to encode the data’s sampling frequency. The embedded sequence then passes through multiple levels of stacked Mixer blocks. Each level performs adaptive patch reshaping to capture temporal dependencies at multiple scales, while TSMixer layers model intra- and inter-channel relationships. A lightweight decoder and a final linear forecast head produce the output sequence , where c′ is the number of target channels and fl is the forecast horizon. During fine-tuning, only the decoder and forecast head are updated, enabling efficient adaptation to downstream tasks with minimal computation.

For a geotechnical interpretation, the TTM can be understood as learning two related behaviours simultaneously: how slope conditions develop through time, and how the different monitored variables interact with one another. The temporal mixing component evaluates the sequence of conditions over the previous 52 days, enabling the model to recognise antecedent-response patterns familiar in slope engineering, for example, rainfall accumulation followed by delayed increases in pore pressure and subsequent slope movement. The channel mixing component learns relationships among variables measured at different depths and across different physical processes, relating precipitation and cumulative rainfall indices to moisture increases, suction reductions, and the resulting displacement response within the embankment. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the model receives 52 days of historical inputs, separates them into rainfall-based control variables and soil-state conditional variables, and combines temporal and channel mixing to generate a 16-day cumulative displacement forecast.

Schematic of the TTM-52/16 forecasting framework for slope displacement prediction. Illustration of the three-stream input organisation and dual mixing mechanism used for slope displacement forecasting. Historical sensor records over the preceding 52-day context window are organised into a target stream (cumulative slope displacement), conditional channels (soil moisture, matric suction, and temperature at 1.5 m, 3 m, and 5 m depths), and control channels (daily precipitation and 14-day cumulative rainfall indices). Within the Tiny Time Mixer, a temporal mixing block learns antecedent hydro-climatic patterns and delayed slope response across the 52-day window, while a channel mixing block learns interactions among rainfall, soil state variables, and displacement. The outputs of both blocks are combined in the forecast head to produce predicted cumulative slope movement over the subsequent 16-day horizon.

The number of data points available for each slope section in this study is fewer than 1,000. Given this constraint, employing a lightweight Tiny Time Mixer (TTM) configuration with a context length of 52 days and a prediction length of 16 days (TTM-52/16) is more suitable than larger canonical configurations (such as TTM-512/96, TTM-1024/96, or TTM-1536/96), which are neither computationally efficient nor statistically justified for smaller datasets. The latest TTM release (v2.1) supports daily-frequency inputs, aligning effectively with the sampling frequency of the slope monitoring data.

The 52-day context window provides sufficient history to capture cumulative rainfall, suction, and moisture trends across multiple storm events, while the 16-day horizon corresponds approximately to the medium-range period over which antecedent hydrological loading remains a dominant control on embankment deformation for the monitored slopes. Longer horizons were not pursued here because forecast uncertainty grows substantially with lead time under data-sparse conditions, and the TTM-52/16 configuration would require replacement with a larger variant (e.g., TTM-512/96) demanding substantially more training data than these datasets provide. Conversely, very short horizons of 1–3 days offer limited additional value over simple persistence for the slowly deforming clay embankments studied here. The 16-day horizon therefore represents a practical compromise between lead time and forecast reliability for medium-term displacement assessment.

The TTM architecture incorporates an exogenous mixer block and decoder channel-mixing module that collectively enables the model to capture complex multivariate relationships in data. The exogenous mixer block integrates known future variables-such as precipitation or temperature to guide predictions of target variables, thereby allowing the model to incorporate anticipated external influences into slope stability forecasts. When modeling multivariate time series with strong inter-channel correlations, the decoder channel-mixing module can be activated during fine-tuning to learn cross-variate dependencies across historical time steps.

The target channel configuration designates cumulative slope movement as the primary objective of prediction. Conditional channels encompass the nine multi-depth soil parameters -moisture, suction, and temperature at 1.5 m, 3 m, and 5 m depths-these variables allow the model to learn historical soil-atmosphere interactions critical to slope stability but are not assumed to be forecastable. Control channels incorporate precipitation and its cumulative derivatives (e.g., 14 days), which are assumed to be known or forecastable from meteorological sources.

Transfer learning framework

In this study, “transfer learning” refers to re-using a pre-trained time-series forecasting model (TTM) that has already learned generic temporal patterns from large, heterogeneous public datasets and adapting it to a new geotechnical target domain. Formally, knowledge encoded in the pre-trained backbone parameters is transferred to the highway embankment slope datasets used here. During fine-tuning, the backbone weights are frozen and only a lightweight decoder, exogenous mixer, and linear forecast head are updated on each slope dataset. This differs from standard “generalization,” which considers performance only within a single dataset or domain; in our case, the model first generalizes across generic pre-training datasets and then transfers that knowledge to multiple instrumented slopes via sequential fine-tuning.

A sequential training paradigm was adopted in which the TTM model was exposed to slope monitoring datasets one at a time, in a pre-determined order. Unlike pooled training strategies, which blend data across all sites, this approach preserves site-specific temporal dynamics and allows for the progressive accumulation of geotechnical knowledge, enabling the model to gradually adapt its representations to different behavioral regimes. The ordering constituted a geotechnical curriculum: the model first learned from Slope3 (Terry Road), which exhibited the most active instability and then progressed to increasingly stable or remediated slopes (I‑220 N Ramp, Metro Center) before concluding with I-20 W. The sequential fine-tuning across slopes further implements transfer learning at the project scale: the model adapts to each slope in turn while retaining shared temporal structures learned from previous sites, thereby enabling zero-shot application to unseen slopes (Sowell Road and McRaven Road) without any local retraining. After data processing and feature engineering, model parameters and hyperparameters are specified and a sequential training loop is executed across the training slopes. The final model is saved and then applied for zero-shot forecasting on the held-out slopes, followed by evaluation of zero-shot performance.

Random sampling was explicitly avoided to maintain the integrity of temporal dependencies. Within each slope-specific dataset, data were partitioned into training (60%), validation (20%), and testing (20%) subsets. However, given the limited dataset sizes, during fine-tuning, we further divided the training subset such that 90% of the training samples were used for gradient-based updates. Thus, fine-tuning updates were performed on 54% of the total samples per slope (0.9 × 0.6).

In this study, the 14-day cumulative precipitation was initially employed as a control variable for forecasting slope movement. Subsequently, the same methodological approach was applied using 7-day and 21-day cumulative precipitation variables, allowing comparative evaluation against the initial 14-day precipitation-based forecasts.

Only the decoder and, when applicable, the exogenous mixer was updated during the fine-tuning phase, while the backbone remained frozen. Zero-shot performance was evaluated by directly applying the sequentially trained model (Terry Road → I‑220 N Ramp → Metro Center → I-20 W) to Sowell Road and McRaven Road data without any local fine-tuning. The last 90% of the dataset was used exclusively for prediction. This strict test demonstrates deployability at unseen sites when rapid monitoring is required, and site-specific data is not yet available.

Training was conducted for a maximum of 200 epochs, but an early stopping mechanism was employed to terminate training when further progress became statistically insignificant. Specifically, training would halt if validation loss failed to improve for 10 consecutive epochs, with a minimum delta threshold of 0.000001 required to qualify as a meaningful improvement. This approach ensured that model updates were grounded in stable performance gains, reducing the risk of overfitting to short-term noise in sparse time series while maintaining optimal model performance.

The optimization process used the AdamW algorithm with a learning rate of 0.0004 and a batch size of 32. Throughout training, model checkpoints were recorded at the end of each epoch, but only the model corresponding to the lowest observed validation loss was retained for final evaluation. This best-performing model, rather than the most recent one, was used during inference across all configurations, ensuring that evaluation results reflect the most generalizable parameter state rather than transient improvements.

Performance metrics and evaluation framework

The evaluation of the TTM architecture’s predictive performance was conducted using a set of quantitative metrics aligned with the demands of geotechnical forecasting. The primary metric used was Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which reflects the average magnitude of prediction error without over-penalizing large deviations, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) that reflects the degree of variance between predicted and actual values; and the coefficient of determination (), which quantifies the proportion of variance in the observed slope movement explained by the model predictions relative to a mean-baseline. MAE provides an interpretable magnitude of average error, RMSE emphasizes larger deviations that may be critical for hazard-sensitive applications, and provides a normalized measure of explanatory power. Evaluation benchmarks

To contextualise TTM performance against reference forecasters, two baselines were evaluated using the same test partitions and evaluation metrics. The Linear Drift baseline fits an ordinary least-squares trend line to the 52-day context window and extrapolates it linearly over the 16-step horizon, providing a deterministic trend-only forecast without any nonlinear learning.

A Standard LSTM baseline was additionally implemented to quantify the architectural advantage of TTM’s pre-trained backbone over a randomly initialised recurrent model. A two-layer LSTM (hidden size 128, LayerNorm, dropout 0.1) was trained individually on each slope using a 65/15/20 temporal split and the same 10 input channels as TTM (nine conditional soil parameters and 14-day cumulative precipitation). Because cumulative slope displacement increases monotonically, the training and test partitions occupy different displacement ranges. To address this without altering the absolute displacement prediction target, a linear trend was fitted to the training partition and subtracted from the full series prior to StandardScaler normalisation; the trend was added back after inverse-transform at evaluation, so that MAE and RMSE are reported in original mm units directly comparable to TTM. Training used AdamW (lr = 0.0001, weight decay = 10⁻⁴), CosineAnnealingLR scheduling, HuberLoss (δ = 1.0), batch size 32, up to 300 epochs, and early stopping (patience = 20, threshold = 10⁻⁶). Each slope was trained independently with a fresh model initialisation; all six slopes are therefore evaluated on their own test partitions with no zero-shot condition applied to the LSTM.

Uncertainty quantification: cross-slope conformal prediction intervals

To quantify predictive uncertainty for early warning applications, split conformal prediction intervals (PIs) were constructed for both zero-shot slopes following the framework of Vovk et al. Conformal prediction is a distribution-free, model-agnostic post-processing method that wraps any deterministic forecasting model and produces statistically valid PIs without requiring distributional assumptions or architectural modifications to TTM. Nonconformity scores were assembled as the absolute residual for every forecast window and horizon from the four trained slopes, yielding a pooled calibration set of 714 windows. This cross-slope calibration is the only methodologically valid approach in the zero-shot setting, where no local residuals exist at the deployment site; calibrating on the test slope’s own residuals would constitute data leakage. The per-horizon half-width was computed as the empirical quantile of calibration scores at the finite-sample-corrected level , guaranteeing for any test point exchangeable with the calibration set. Quantiles were computed separately for each of the 16 forecast horizons at both 80% () and 90% () nominal levels, so that interval width naturally reflects the growth of uncertainty with lead time.

Perturbation-based feature sensitivity analysis

To examine which input channels drive model predictions during slope acceleration phases, a perturbation-based feature sensitivity analysis was applied to the two zero-shot slopes. For each input channel in turn, the TTM model was re-run on the test set with that channel replaced by a physically motivated substitute value, and the resulting change in MAE (ΔMAE = MAEperturbed − MAEbaseline, in millimetres) was recorded. Conditional soil state channels (volumetric moisture, matric suction, and temperature at 1.5 m, 3 m, and 5 m depth) were replaced by their training-set means to avoid out-of-distribution inputs, as these variables are never observed at zero under field conditions. The precipitation control channel was set to zero, reflecting the physically valid dry-period condition frequently observed during the monitoring period. Results were stratified into acceleration phases, defined as forecast windows falling within the top 25th percentile of mean absolute daily displacement rate over the 16-day prediction horizon, and stable phases, to isolate channel contributions specific to elevated displacement conditions.

Hardware environment and resource management

All model training was executed on a single NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU with 12 GB of VRAM and 32 GB of system RAM. The TTM‑52/16 configuration takes approximately 15–25 min per slope, with peak GPU memory under 6 GB. These characteristics confirm suitability for resource-constrained deployments typical of geotechnical monitoring, facilitating real-time inference on modest edge hardware and rapid retraining when seasonal shifts or maintenance events necessitate recalibration.

Discussion

Slope stability monitoring has progressively shifted from traditional physics-based models toward data-driven approaches, with modern systems routinely tracking soil moisture, matric suction, temperature, and precipitation at multiple depths25. While transformer-based foundation models capture global temporal dependencies through self-attention mechanisms26, their quadratic complexity and large parameter count hinder geotechnical application under sparse-data conditions. This study addresses these challenges using IBM’s Tiny Time Mixer (TTM), a compact MLP-based architecture that mixes information along temporal and channel dimensions through dedicated exogenous mixers, enabling joint exploitation of rainfall, suction, moisture, and temperature signals. Transfer learning is realised by freezing the pre-trained backbone and fine-tuning only lightweight decoder and exogenous-mixing layers, followed by sequential fine-tuning across slopes to encourage cross-site knowledge transfer.

The results demonstrate that a pre-trained time series foundation model can achieve sub-millimetric displacement forecasting accuracy under the sparse-data conditions characteristic of geotechnical field monitoring, with performance extending reliably to zero-shot transfer at unseen sites. Across the four trained slopes, TTM achieves MAE of 0.005–0.394 mm and R2 of 0.72–0.99. The lower R2 at Metro Center and I20W reflects their very low displacement variance rather than systematic model bias. Importantly, in the zero-shot setting (Sowell Road and McRaven Road) where the model generated forecasts for unseen data without training, TTM retains strong explanatory power () with MAE on the order of 0.1321–0.2032 mm, demonstrating that displacement-precipitation relationships learned from the trained slopes transfer reasonably well to unseen sites without any local fine-tuning. The practical significance of sub-millimetric MAE on these stable slope’s merits direct consideration. Metro Center and I20W accumulate total displacements of only 4.47 mm and 4.57 mm respectively over the entire monitoring period, corresponding to creep rates of approximately 1.6–1.8 mm/year. At this rate, the 16-day forecast horizon encompasses an expected displacement increment of only 0.07–0.08 mm, well below any geotechnical warning threshold. For such slowly deforming slopes, the value of accurate forecasting lies not in predicting the absolute displacement magnitude, which is inherently small, but in detecting acceleration: a departure from the background creep trend that may signal the onset of progressive failure. An MAE of 0.005–0.023 mm preserves the resolution needed to detect such departures; a prediction error of this magnitude would not mask a sudden increase in displacement rate even on these low-activity slopes. In contrast, on actively creeping slopes such as Terry Road, which accumulated 85 mm over the same period at 32 mm/year, the 16-day forecast window spans approximately 1.4 mm of expected displacement, and the MAE of 0.394 mm represents a relative error of 28% of the expected increment, a practically meaningful margin for early warning decisions. This contrast illustrates that the geotechnical utility of the model scales with site activity: on stable slopes it provides a sensitive baseline monitor capable of detecting rate changes, while on active slopes it delivers actionable displacement magnitude forecasts directly relevant to threshold-based alert systems.

The reported MAE values warrant interpretation against standard inclinometer measurement tolerances. Manual inclinometer surveys of the type conducted in this study, sequential portable probe readings taken at fixed depth intervals along 9 m casings at approximately monthly intervals, carry a typical field repeatability of ± 0.5 mm under in-situ conditions, accounting for operator technique, temperature variation, and casing deformation, consistent with standard geotechnical practice. The MAE values of 0.132–0.394 mm on actively deforming slopes therefore fall at or below this conventional tolerance, meaning forecast errors are comparable to or smaller than the inherent measurement uncertainty of the target series. On actively creeping slopes, Terry Road (85 mm cumulative displacement) and I220N (16 mm), where total displacement substantially exceeds the noise floor, the sub-millimetric MAE represents genuine trend-tracking accuracy sufficient for threshold-based early warning decisions. On stable slopes, Metro Center and I20W, with only 4–5 mm total displacement over the monitoring period (as discussed above), MAE values of 0.005–0.023 mm fall well below the noise floor and should be interpreted as confirmation that the model does not generate spurious displacement trends where none are present, a necessary property for any early warning system where false alarms carry operational costs, rather than as independently verifiable sub-millimetric accuracy.

The cross-slope conformal PIs translate point forecasts into calibrated uncertainty bounds directly applicable to early warning. On Sowell Road, the 90% PIs achieved 96.0% mean empirical coverage across the full 16-day horizon, remaining above nominal at every forecast step, with half-widths growing from ± 0.300 mm at day 1 to ± 0.677 mm (Table 3) at day 16. Point forecast MAE increases from 0.069 mm at day 1 to 0.138 mm at day 7 and 0.273 mm at day 16 for Sowell Road, and from 0.092 mm to 0.193 mm at day 7 and 0.405 mm at day 16 for McRaven Road, confirming gradual error accumulation without abrupt degradation across the full horizon (Fig. 9). On McRaven Road, mean coverage fell below nominal (78.6% for the 80% PI; 85.6% for the 90% PI), though it remains near-nominal at day 1 (95.7% and 96.3% for the 80% and 90% PIs respectively), where early warning decisions are most consequential. This undercoverage reflects a fundamental constraint of split conformal theory: marginal validity holds only when test residuals are exchangeable with calibration residuals, a condition weakened when the test slope exhibits systematically larger errors than the calibration set. Within-slope temporal calibration confirmed that McRaven Road’s residuals are non-stationary, such that no fixed calibration split produces simultaneously valid and informative intervals; adaptive recalibration using a rolling residual window is identified as the appropriate remedy and a priority direction for future work. For operational deployment, these intervals support a risk-graded alerting strategy: when the upper PI bound approaches a site-specific displacement-rate threshold, precautionary action is warranted even if the point estimate remains below it, converting a deterministic forecast into a probabilistic risk signal suited to multi-level warning systems.

The 16-day forecast horizon adopted in this study warrants explicit discussion in the context of operational early warning requirements. The 3–7 day lead time identified as the industry benchmark for practical evacuation decisions is natively satisfied within the existing framework: since TTM-52/16 produces all 16 forecast steps simultaneously, forecasts at any lead time from day 1 through day 16 are directly extractable without retraining. This window also corresponds to the most stable portion of the prediction horizon, where 90% PI half-widths remain within ± 0.300 – ±0.329 mm and empirical coverage stays above 94% on Sowell Road and above 86% on McRaven Road, confirming that the 3–7 day operational benchmark is reliably met within the current output. Extending beyond 16 days faces three compounding constraints: displacement is driven by precipitation events that cannot be reliably anticipated beyond 10–14 days, so forecast error accumulates as input uncertainty propagates regardless of model architecture; a longer horizon requires a proportionally longer context window and more training data than the approximately 950-point datasets available here can support; and the TTM-52/16 configuration is architecturally fixed at the 16-day output length, with larger variants requiring substantially more data. A practical path for multi-horizon deployment is a cascaded architecture, with TTM-52/16 serving as the short-range engine for 1–16 day alerts coupled with a coarser seasonal model for longer-range risk assessment.

To quantify the architectural advantage of TTM’s pre-trained backbone, a Standard LSTM baseline was evaluated on all six slopes under conditions optimised for the architecture, individual per-slope training, the same 10 input channels as TTM, and linear detrend normalisation to address the monotonically increasing displacement series. Even under these best-case conditions, the LSTM achieves MAE of 0.172–5.393 mm, between 4x and 127x larger than the corresponding TTM values of 0.005–0.394 mm (Table 2). The largest relative gap occurs at Metro Center (0.647 mm versus 0.005 mm) and I20W (0.172 mm versus 0.023 mm), where TTM’s pre-trained sensitivity to fine hydromechanical fluctuations in low-variance displacement signals cannot be replicated from approximately 600 training samples. On the zero-shot slopes, TTM achieves MAE of 0.132 mm (Sowell Road) and 0.203 mm (McRaven Road) without any local training, outperforming the LSTM trained with full site-specific data (1.870 mm and 0.842 mm respectively). This result directly confirms that a randomly initialised model trained on site-specific data alone cannot substitute for the cross-domain representational richness of a pre-trained foundation model backbone under the sparse-data conditions characteristic of geotechnical monitoring.

The model’s response to the soil sensor inputs can be interpreted within the framework of the hydromechanical behavior documented in this dataset rather than treated as a black-box statistical outcome. As shown in Fig. 3, matric suction at all three depths drops in response to precipitation events while volumetric moisture content rises concurrently, behavior consistent with the low saturated hydraulic conductivity of Yazoo Clay (approximately 10⁻⁸ cm/s27 at fully compacted state), which impedes lateral drainage and causes infiltrating water to accumulate within the embankment profile. The resulting pore pressure increase reduces effective stress and produces the creep acceleration visible in the inclinometer record. Within the 52-day context window, a rainfall-driven suction decrease at the 1.5 m sensor appears to the model as a negative gradient in the shallow water potential channel coinciding with rising moisture content - a consistent multi-channel signature that precedes displacement increase by 5–10 days. This lag reflects the wetting front transit time through low-permeability Yazoo Clay and is physically consistent across all six slopes because it is governed by the same formation-wide hydraulic diffusivity27,29. The model’s exogenous channel mixer processes all nine soil channels jointly and learns to associate this depth-ordered signature with the displacement response that follows. The zero-shot transferability to Sowell Road and McRaven Road is therefore grounded in a repeatable hydromechanical process rather than distributional similarity alone. It should be noted that this interpretation applies to gradual, wetting-front-driven creep; extreme rainfall events that collapse suction across multiple depths within a single inter-survey interval are underrepresented in the interpolated training signal.

The daily displacement series were constructed by linearly interpolating between 36 and 38 inclinometer readings acquired around 1,000 days (mean inter-measurement interval ~ 27 days). Two effects of this sparse sampling warrant acknowledgement. First, any displacement event that initiates and resolves within a single inter-survey window is absent from the interpolated signal; the model therefore learns to reproduce a low-pass filtered representation of slope movement, and the reported MAE values reflect accuracy against this smoothed reference rather than against the true high-frequency deformation field. Second, because interpolated values lie on straight lines between adjacent survey points, the training signal inherently rewards trend-following behaviour, which partly explains the high R2 values (0.96–0.99 on zero-shot slopes) achieved on these slowly creeping slopes. Consequently, the framework is most appropriately applied to monitoring gradual, seasonally modulated displacement, where the ~ 27-day sampling cadence is commensurate with the dominant deformation timescale, rather than to rapid-onset failure scenarios unfolding over hours to days. A partial mitigation is that TTM’s 52-day context window of continuously logged soil moisture and matric suction provides a leading hydromechanical indicator that can anticipate wetting-front driven acceleration before the next inclinometer survey. Future work should prioritise continuously logged in-place sensors to expose episodic displacement pulses and enable a genuine test of early warning capability under higher-frequency deformation regimes. Notably, the baseline comparison provides indirect evidence that TTM learns more than simple trend continuation: at forecast horizon h = 16, TTM reduces MAE by 44–49% relative to a Linear Drift baseline that by construction represents the optimal smooth-trend extrapolation of the context window. Since both TTM and the Linear Drift baseline are evaluated against the same interpolated reference series, this margin cannot be attributed to differences in target smoothness, both models face the same smoothed target, and instead reflect TTM’s ability to exploit the hydromechanical forcing encoded in the exogenous channels beyond what is recoverable from the displacement trajectory alone. This margin, achieved in zero-shot mode on unseen slopes, suggests the model captures hydromechanical forcing dynamics beyond what is recoverable from the interpolated displacement trajectory alone, partially offsetting the smoothing limitation.

These technical limitations carry a direct responsibility in safety-critical deployment. Because the model has no empirical basis for predicting sudden acceleration events absent from its training signal, false negatives, forecasting continued gradual movement immediately before a rapid failure, cannot be ruled out. Practitioners should therefore treat TTM as a decision-support tool that complements, rather than replaces, conventional geotechnical monitoring and engineering judgment. Responsible deployment should pair displacement forecasts with threshold-based alerts from the continuously logged soil moisture and matric suction channels, maintain regular inclinometer survey schedules, and document the interpolation-based training data provenance so that downstream users understand the reliability boundaries of the model. Within these boundaries, the conformal prediction intervals and per-horizon MAE profiles reported in this study characterise the conditions under which TTM forecasts are reliable, specifically, gradual seasonally modulated displacement driven by antecedent hydromechanical loading, and it is within this operational envelope that the framework natively satisfies the 3–7 day early warning benchmark. Claims of operational readiness should be understood as conditional on this envelope: the framework is not validated for, and should not be applied to, rapid-onset failure scenarios where displacement accelerates over hours to days without antecedent hydromechanical precursors detectable in the 52-day context window.

The differences in model performance across precipitation window lengths reflect the infiltration and pore pressure response characteristics of Yazoo Clay. As a low-permeability expansive clay with saturated hydraulic conductivity on the order of approximately 10⁻⁸ cm/s at fully compacted state27, these embankments exhibit slow wetting front propagation and pronounced depth-dependent pore pressure delays. A 7-day window captures immediate near-surface moisture changes but is too short to represent cumulative pore pressure buildup at depth, where pressure pulses attenuate and may lag surface rainfall by days to weeks28. The 14-day window better aligns with the infiltration timescale to the shallow and mid-depth sensor horizons (1.5–3.0 m), consistent with field observations from Yazoo Clay slopes where sensors at 1.5 m respond within days while deeper sensors show progressively delayed increases29; it is therefore unsurprising that this window performs best on trained slopes, where the model learns this lag structure directly from data. The 21-day window encodes a longer antecedent moisture history that also captures slower pore pressure diffusion to the 5.0 m horizon; research on clay slopes has shown that such multi-week antecedent rainfall history is among the most reliable predictors of pore pressure threshold exceedance and slope reactivation30, likely explaining the 21-day preference in the zero-shot setting where no site-specific calibration is available. Windows outside the 7–21 day range were not evaluated: sub-weekly windows would miss multi-day infiltration lags characteristic of low-permeability clay, while windows beyond 21 days risk seasonal drift and redundancy given the 52-day displacement context already available to the model.

The proposed framework is not limited to field monitoring; it may also be applicable to physical model tests on landslides and slope failures. Centrifuge modelling instruments slope models with pore pressure transducers, displacement sensors, and accelerometers simultaneously across multiple channels, generating short but densely sampled multivariate time series, structurally similar to the field datasets analysed here. Fang et al.31 reviewed five decades of centrifuge landslide modelling and identified landslide early warning as a key future direction, noting that monitoring now spans displacement, pore pressure, suction, and seismic response under triggering factors including rainfall, earthquakes, and water level changes. Xu and Fang32 demonstrated through centrifuge tests and finite element analysis that unsaturated slope deformation under post-rainfall earthquakes is governed by the antecedent matric suction state, precisely the kind of multi-variable, path-dependent temporal dependency that TTM’s pre-trained representations and exogenous channel mixer are designed to capture. Applying TTM to such controlled experimental datasets would provide a rigorous testbed for evaluating the model’s ability to learn hydromechanical lag structure under known boundary conditions, free from the confounding variability of field settings. Future work in this direction is encouraged.

The perturbation-based feature sensitivity analysis, results presented in Fig. 10, identifies which input channels drive TTM predictions during acceleration phases on the two zero-shot slopes. On Sowell Road, cumulative precipitation is the dominant informative channel during acceleration phases (ΔMAE = + 0.0024 mm), followed by mid-depth moisture (Moisture-3 m: +0.0016 mm) and deep temperature (Temp-5 m: +0.0008 mm) with all soil state channels contributing negligibly, indicating that the 52-day displacement context supplemented marginally by rainfall foreknowledge is sufficient to characterise forecast behaviour at this site. This reversal is physically consistent with Yazoo Clay hydraulics: by the time acceleration occurs, antecedent pore pressure accumulation is already encoded in the deep moisture record, rendering the surface precipitation signal redundant and marginally confounding. The model has learned, without site-specific fine-tuning, to suppress rainfall and rely on deep soil moisture state during acceleration, consistent with a low-permeability clay slope where infiltration lag governs displacement response.

While the current framework uses soil moisture, matric suction, temperature, and precipitation as inputs, TTM’s exogenous channel mixer is architecturally agnostic to input type, allowing additional geotechnical parameters to be incorporated without structural modification. Groundwater levels would directly encode the pore pressure state governing effective stress reduction, among the most physically meaningful displacement predictors on slopes with a fluctuating water table. Seismic accelerometer data would extend applicability to earthquake-prone regions where combined rainfall-seismic triggering governs slope behaviour, as demonstrated in recent centrifuge studies32, and construction-induced vibrations could similarly be treated as a transient exogenous channel. The practical requirement in each case is simply that the new parameter be logged at daily or sub-daily resolution and time-aligned with existing inputs, a condition readily met by modern IoT monitoring systems. The primary challenge is not architectural but data-driven: sufficient training examples pairing new inputs with measured displacement are needed for the model to learn the corresponding lag and coupling relationships, which further motivates the sequential transfer learning protocol adopted here.

Building on this foundation, the current study establishes TTM as a high-performance data-driven baseline. Future work will explore integrating geotechnical physical constraints directly into the model’s learning framework. A natural extension is a multi-objective fine-tuning loss in which the standard MSE is augmented with physics-penalty terms enforcing geomechanically motivated constraints, such as displacement monotonicity under sustained hydromechanical loading and rate-boundedness derived from site-specific shear strength parameters at the observed matric suction and moisture levels. These constraints are broadly applicable across clay embankment monitoring contexts, as the underlying geomechanical principles governing moisture-driven creep and pore pressure-induced displacement are not site-specific. A complementary direction is residual hybrid modelling, where a simplified physics-based model provides a baseline displacement trajectory and TTM is trained solely on the nonlinear residual, capturing the episodic creep and precipitation-triggered accelerations that deterministic models cannot resolve. This hybrid formulation preserves physical interpretability while leveraging TTM’s capacity for multivariate hydro-climatic representation, and is applicable to any monitoring context where a physics-based first-order estimate can be derived from available sensor data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of the time series-based model like Tiny Time Mixer (TTM) architecture for forecasting slope displacement in geotechnical engineering applications. The analysis was conducted using six instrumented highway embankments slopes in Jackson, Mississippi, established under MDOT State Studies 286 and 31623,24. Following data cleaning and feature engineering, daily aggregated measurements of moisture, suction, temperature, and precipitation were used to predict displacement. Notably, each slope dataset comprised fewer than 1000 data points.

Configured with a context length of 52 and a forecast horizon of 16, TTM achieved robust predictive performance under sparse-data conditions. Sequential fine-tuning across multiple slope sites enabled effective knowledge transfer and supported reliable zero-shot forecasting at previously unseen locations. Comparative evaluation showed that a 14-day cumulative precipitation window optimized training performance, whereas a 21-day window improved generalization to unseen slopes by better capturing cumulative hydrological forcing, highlighting the importance of physically informed input selection. Residual error analysis confirmed that forecast errors are not uniformly distributed: systematic underprediction is concentrated during active displacement phases coinciding with peak environmental stressors, while errors approach zero during stable periods, consistent with the phase-dependent hydromechanical behaviour of Yazoo Clay embankments. Perturbation-based feature sensitivity analysis on the two zero-shot slopes revealed site-dependent channel importance during acceleration phases: on McRaven Road, deep soil moisture emerged as the dominant driver while precipitation became actively degrading, consistent with Yazoo Clay’s infiltration lag dynamics, whereas on Sowell Road the displacement context alone was sufficient, demonstrating that the model adaptively exploits exogenous channels when they provide independent predictive information.

At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, continuous daily displacement series were obtained by interpolating between 36 and 38 sparse inclinometer readings per slope, which simplifies the underlying deformation process and may favor models that track smooth trends. Second, all six slopes are founded on Yazoo Clay within a single humid subtropical region, which constrains direct transferability to fundamentally different geological settings such as fractured rock slopes, loess deposits, or arid-climate embankments. Nonetheless, the TTM framework’s architecture and training methodology are soil-agnostic, and the approach should extend to other high-plasticity clay formations exhibiting similar moisture-driven creep behavior. Future work should validate the methodology across diverse geological and climatic settings to establish broader generalizability. Expanding the monitoring network to additional instrumented slopes spanning diverse displacement activity levels and geological settings would also enable leave-one-slope-out cross-validation, providing a more statistically powerful generalization test than the current two-site zero-shot evaluation permits and establishing the boundary conditions under which the sequential fine-tuning protocol transfers reliably across independent sites.

Third, while TTM produces deterministic point forecasts, this study demonstrates that statistically rigorous uncertainty bounds can be appended post-hoc via split conformal prediction without model retraining. Cross-slope calibration on residuals from the four trained slopes yielded well-calibrated 90% PIs on Slope-Sowell Road (96.0% mean coverage) with half-widths of ± 0.300-±0.329 mm across the operationally critical 3–7 day window and ± 0.677 mm at day 16, confirming that the framework natively satisfies the industry benchmark for evacuation lead time. Coverage on Slope-McRaven Road fell below nominal, consistent with that slope’s non-stationary residual distribution. Adaptive recalibration strategies that update conformal quantiles with accumulating site-specific residuals are identified as a priority direction for extending reliable uncertainty quantification to slopes with evolving displacement regimes.

Overall, the results suggest that compact time-series foundation models such as TTM provide a promising avenue for slope stability monitoring in resource-constrained settings. Their ability to leverage transfer learning, ingest multivariate environmental inputs, and operate with low computational overhead makes them well suited for deployment alongside IoT-based monitoring networks and real-time warning systems. Future research directions fall into three themes. First, validation across diverse geological and climatic settings, including fractured rock slopes, loess deposits, semi-arid embankments, and reservoir bank slopes, to establish the boundary conditions under which cross-site transfer learning remains reliable. Second, physics integration and sensor enrichment: augmenting the fine-tuning loss with geomechanical penalty terms enforcing displacement monotonicity and rate-boundedness, and exploring residual hybrid modelling in which a simplified physics-based model provides the baseline displacement trajectory while TTM is trained on the nonlinear residual to capture episodic precipitation-triggered accelerations; additional sensor modalities including groundwater level, seismic accelerometers, and construction vibration channels can be incorporated through TTM’s exogenous channel mixer without structural modification, provided they are logged at daily or sub-daily resolution. Third, IoT-based operational deployment: automating the sequential fine-tuning pipeline, implementing rolling conformal recalibration as site-specific residuals accumulate, and coupling displacement forecasts with threshold-based alerting logic to support risk-graded early warning and proactive infrastructure asset management.
