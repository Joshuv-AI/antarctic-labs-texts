First-time use of deer antler-derived xenografts for management of severe peri-implantitis with titanium mesh: a case report
Authors: Mahdi Kadkhodazadeh, Aida Kheiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing dental implant use correlates with rising biological complications. For severe peri-implantitis with significant bone loss, choosing the right sustaining treatment is challenging, as nonsurgical methods often fail. This case details a novel management approach for a male patient with serious peri-implantitis, specifically highlighting the initial use of deer antler xenografts in such a procedure.Case presentationA 42-year-old Iranian male presented with significant challenges, including 8 mm of vertical bone loss and a probing depth of 12 mm around a bone-level Interlock implant (4*11 mm). The novel treatment approach incorporated deer antler xenografts, a titanium mesh to maintain space, and a collagen membrane to encourage guided bone regeneration. Follow-up evaluations, both 2D and 3D, four months postsurgery, revealed complete bone regeneration up to the rough neck of the implant. Furthermore, 24 months of follow-up after the delivery of the crown showed stable results.ConclusionThis case illustrates the promising potential of deer antler-derived xenografts in addressing severe peri-implantitis. It emphasizes the necessity for further clinical trials to validate these observations and enhance treatment protocols.

Background

Peri-implant inflammation is the most prevalent biological complication affecting individuals who receive dental implants, with an estimated prevalence rate of 11.5% at the implant level [1]. These peri-implant diseases can be categorized into two main groups: peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis [1, 2]. Periimplantitis, in particular, is a pathological condition that arises from the accumulation of polymicrobial plaque and is often considered a progression of untreated peri-implant mucositis [1]. From both clinical and radiographic perspectives, periimplantitis is characterized by inflammation, bleeding upon probing, increased probing depth (PD), and ongoing bone loss that exceeds normal physiological remodeling [3, 4]. The progression of this condition tends to be nonlinear, which highlights the necessity for early and decisive intervention to halt its advancement. While nonsurgical treatments and improvements in oral hygiene are regarded as the gold standard for managing peri-implant mucositis, their effectiveness in resolving peri-implantitis has been limited [5]. Furthermore, Xenografts are among the most studied biomaterials for repairing peri-implant bone defects. A recent clinical trial compared their effectiveness to that of autologous bone. The only outcome that showed statistically significant advantages for the xenograft was radiographic bone filling [6]. Additionally, a case series evaluating xenografts in the reconstruction of peri-implant bone defects demonstrated reliable outcomes in both probing depths and radiographic bone filling [7]. Various surgical treatments have been put forward for periimplantitis, with reconstructive techniques yielding the most promising outcomes. In particular, reconstructive surgery has demonstrated significant reductions in probing depth, substantial bone regeneration, and a lower rate of disease recurrence [8, 9]. This case report aims to assess the outcomes of a reconstructive approach for treating peri-implantitis, utilizing titanium mesh and xenograft sourced from deer antler, marking a novel application in this field.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old Iranian male presented to our clinic with a primary complaint of persistent discomfort at the implant site, which had been placed eight years prior. He did not report any systemic issues or current medication usage.

Clinical examination

The evaluations conducted encompassed the following: 1. Pocket probing depth (PPD) measured to the nearest millimeter from the gingival margin, 2. Assessment of bleeding on probing (BOP) within 30 s after probing [10], and 3. A full-mouth plaque score, determined with an O’Leary plaque index of 37% during the initial examination [11]. These evaluations were performed at six specific sites around each implant: mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, distopalatal, palatal, and mesiopalatal, using a Williams periodontal probe from Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA. Clinical and radiographic assessments indicated approximately 8 mm of vertical bone loss as well as a probing depth of 12 mm around a bone-level interlock implant in the #30 site (see Fig. 1). Signs of peri-implantitis were noted; however, the implant itself showed no mobility, and its three-dimensional positioning was found to be satisfactory. Surgical treatment and intervention

After gathering the initial data, the periodontist performed a thorough scaling and root planing (SRP) procedure, which included polishing. The procedure included thorough root planing, complemented by supragingival and subgingival scaling across the entire mouth, utilizing both curettes and an ultrasonic titanium scaler, along with manual plastic curettes. Additionally, patients were provided with guidance on oral hygiene practices, including the Bass technique for tooth brushing and the use of dental floss for interdental cleaning. Before starting the treatment, the periodontal parameters for each treatment method were also carefully assessed. On the day of surgery, the patient was instructed to rinse his mouth with 0.2% Chlorhexidine for one minute before the administration of local anesthesia (20 mg/mL lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine). Crestal and intrasulcular incisions were made, along with a single vertical incision at the distobuccal line angle of the medial tooth. After elevating a full-thickness flap, a 5 mm area beyond the osseous defect was revealed, and all granulation tissue was thoroughly removed. The implant surface underwent decontamination through mechanical methods (using titanium curettes and titanium brushes) and chemical methods (applying a Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution for three minutes). The surgical site was then rinsed completely with sterile saline. For this case, deer antler-derived xenografts from Maral Pajoohesh Shams Co. (Iran) were utilized. The graft material (sized 1000–2000 µm) was hydrated for 30 min before application, filling the defect. To maintain space and thwart soft tissue invasion into the defect area, a titanium mesh (Ti-mesh; 28.0 × 10.0 mm, 0.15 mm thickness, 0.8 mm pore diameter, 3.1 mm fixation hole diameter; Jeilmedical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was carefully shaped to fit the defect and secured in place. A resorbable collagen membrane (Tutopatch, 15 × 20 mm, RTI Surgical Co., Germany) was then placed over the Ti-mesh. Flap advancement was performed with minimal periosteal incisions, and closure of the flap was achieved using interrupted simple sutures made of nonresorbable monofilament (4–0 Nylon).

Post‐operative care

Postoperative care involved taking 500 mg of acetaminophen every 6 h to manage pain and 500 mg of amoxicillin every 8 h for infection prevention, both prescribed for a 2-week duration. Additionally, the patient was instructed to use a 0.2% Chlorhexidine mouthwash three times daily during the same period. Sutures were removed at the end of the two weeks. Detailed information regarding surgical procedures is presented in Fig. 2. Two weeks following the surgery, the healing progressed without complications. Radiographic analysis conducted four months later revealed complete bone regeneration around the implant, with bone levels restored up to the rough surface of the implant neck, as depicted in Fig. 3. Postsurgical guidance advised the patient to use cold compresses and to abstain from smoking, using straws, or spitting. Follow-up

A comprehensive clinical evaluation was performed at the two-year mark. This assessment included intraoral photography, periodontal charting, periapical radiography, and a CBCT scan to closely examine the changes in bone thickness surrounding the patient’s implant. Thankfully, there were no signs of persistent inflammation. Following the delivery of the prosthesis, the patient began a maintenance protocol to ensure oral hygiene and implant health. Discussion and conclusion

This case report highlights the successful management of peri-implantitis using a deer antler-derived xenograft in combination with a titanium mesh. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first clinical application of a xenograft sourced from deer antlers. Considering that peri-implantitis stems from a bacterial origin, a stepwise therapeutic strategy is essential. Residual infection can compromise treatment outcomes, underscoring the importance of meticulous surface decontamination. Although no standardized guidelines currently exist for cleaning implant surfaces, previous studies have reported positive results with methods such as titanium brushes and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). A thorough clinical evaluation is scheduled at the two-year follow-up, including intraoral photography, periodontal charting, periapical radiography, and CBCT imaging to closely assess changes in the bone thickness around the patient’s implant [12, 13]. In our study, we utilized mechanical cleaning techniques before applying a chemical treatment with H2O2 and sterile saline. The primary objective of treating periimplantitis is to regenerate the supporting tissues that have been lost around the implants [14]. The focus of reconstructive therapies is to achieve marginal bone gain around previously affected threads, along with the reduction of inflammation and probing depths (PDs) [15, 16]. In this regard, guided bone regeneration (GBR) using bone graft substitutes has shown encouraging results. While no single graft material has been established as the definitive option for reconstructive procedures in periimplantitis, evidence suggests that xenografts demonstrate particularly favorable outcomes, especially concerning the reduction of probing depths [17, 18]. In a recent meta-analysis, it was found that using xenografts resulted in less postoperative soft tissue recession and higher radiographic bone levels [19]. One of the significant advantages of xenogenic grafts is that they eliminate the need for a secondary harvesting area. They also have beneficial features such as biocompatibility and a porous architecture that closely resembles human bones [20]. In this particular study, we utilized a novel xenograft derived from deer antlers, which are unique among mammals for their remarkable ability to fully regenerate [21].Recent studies on this xenograft have highlighted its distinctive morphological characteristics. Research by Mahboubian et al. indicated that the particle size of antlers varies from 150 to 2000 µm, exhibiting rounded edges in comparison to bovine grafts. Additionally, these antlers displayed greater surface roughness (surface area: 15.974 m2/g) and featured a slit-like pore morphology, with a total porosity of 40% [18]. Furthermore, the use of antler xenografts in treating calvarial bone defects in rabbits has yielded promising outcomes [20]. Beyond the selection of bone substitutes, ensuring the stability of bone grafts at the surgical site is vital. Given that the morphology of the current defect was not entirely enclosed [22], the treatment was supplemented with a Ti-mesh. This material, classified as a type of nonresorbable barrier in guided bone regeneration (GBR) procedures, offers the necessary rigidity to maintain space and prevent collapse of the contour. Additionally, its flexibility and ease of handling enable clinicians to effectively shape and adapt it to various bone defects [23, 24]. In this instance, we utilized a Ti-mesh with adequate stiffness to facilitate shaping. Additionally, the Ti-mesh was wrapped with a xenogenic resorbable collagen membrane. Following the principles of PASS, the combination of both hard and soft space maintainers is beneficial in preventing tissue collapse, enhancing adaptability over the defect, and supporting chemotactic/hemostatic functions as well as cell adhesion [25]. Moreover, a recent meta-analysis indicated that resorbable membranes lead to reduced bleeding on probing (BOP) and pocket depth (PD) [5]. In our case, the surgical site healed without incident, showing no signs of Ti-mesh exposure or gingival irritation, which are common postoperative complications associated with Ti-mesh use [26]. We made efforts to achieve a tension-free flap advancement for primary closure, promoting submerged healing in an aseptic environment [16, 19]. The findings of this study should be approached with care. It’s important for future investigations to incorporate larger, prospective clinical trials to thoroughly assess the effectiveness of xenografts derived from deer antlers as well as other materials. Moreover, in-depth histological studies are crucial to grasping the biological responses and long-term outcomes linked to various graft materials. Such research will allow us to fine-tune treatment protocols and ultimately enhance patient outcomes. In summary, the present case demonstrated a successful treatment for peri-implantitis utilizing a xenograft from deer antlers, combined with titanium mesh and a collagen membrane, yielding encouraging results for bone regeneration and implant stability. This case underscores the necessity of a systematic approach to diagnosis and treatment, especially considering the diverse definitions and classifications of peri-implantitis. Maintaining rigorous oral hygiene and attending regular follow-up appointments are vital for preventing recurrence and ensuring favorable long-term results. Ongoing clinical and histological research is essential to validate the efficacy of these materials and improve treatment strategies for peri-implantitis.
