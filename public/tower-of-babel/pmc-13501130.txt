The incidence and perinatal outcomes of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer: an Australian population-based study
Authors: Eleanor Hall, Richard G McGee, Christopher W Rowe, Marc Remond, Paul Benitez-Aguirre, Antoinette Anazodo, Nadom Safi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the incidence and perinatal outcomes of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer (PATC).DesignA population-based, retrospective data linkage cohort study.SettingNew South Wales (NSW), Australia.PopulationWomen giving birth in NSW between 1994 and 2013. Women were stratified into a PATC group (women diagnosed with thyroid cancer during pregnancy (gestational) or within 1 year postpartum) or a non-cancer pregnant control group.Main Outcome MeasuresPregnancy, maternal and perinatal outcomes, time to next birth, and thyroid cancer incidence and management.ResultsThree hundred and seventy of 1 788 451 women giving birth during the study period were diagnosed with PATC (incidence rate = 20.7/100 000 women giving birth; annual increase adjusted for maternal age = 5.0% (95% CI: 3.1%-6.9%)). Sixty-two women had gestational thyroid cancer and 308 had postpartum thyroid cancers. Twenty-seven (43.5%) women with gestational thyroid cancer had a subsequent birth. Treatment data were available for 41 women with gestational thyroid cancer. Thirty-two (78.0%) underwent a thyroidectomy during pregnancy, while 6 deferred thyroid surgery until postpartum. Radioactive iodine was administered to 24 (58.5%) of these women postpartum. Women with postpartum thyroid cancer had increased odds of gestational diabetes (adjusted odds ratio: 1.59, 95% CI 1.09-2.32; adjusted for the covariates available within the dataset).ConclusionThe rising incidence of PATC over the study period mirrored an increase in the general population. Most women with gestational thyroid cancer had antepartum surgical management followed by postpartum radioactive iodine with favorable outcomes for the women and their babies.

Thyroid cancer is the fifth most diagnosed cancer in adult women worldwide (1) and has been increasing faster than any other cancer, with an age-standardized incidence estimated to be to 13.6 per 100 000 women (2, 3). For reasons that are not well understood, thyroid cancer is much more common in women than in men, a difference that begins at menarche and progressively rises through the reproductive years (4). Exposure to reproductive hormones, namely estrogen, progesterone, and human chorionic gonadotrophin, is stimulatory to the thyroid follicular epithelium, and both benign and malignant thyroid nodules grow in the peripartum period (4, 5). Although the incidence of thyroid cancer in the general population is well reported, there are limited robust data describing recent incidence trends in pregnant women in diverse populations.

Contemporary management of thyroid cancer has undergone a paradigm shift in the last decade, moving away from universal treatment with total thyroidectomy followed by ablative radioactive iodine, to a personalized, risk-adapted treatment strategy, which could include active surveillance or thyroid lobectomy alone for low-risk cancers (6). Knowledge of the impacts of cancer and its treatment in the setting of pregnancy is important to inform treatment personalization.

Pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer (PATC) encompasses diagnoses during pregnancy and within one year postpartum (7). Large retrospective studies have demonstrated equivalent survival outcomes regardless of diagnosis setting (before, during, or after pregnancy) or the timing of surgery (8-10). However, there are conflicting data regarding whether rates of recurrence are higher in women diagnosed during pregnancy than in women diagnosed while not pregnant (8, 11, 12). The diagnosis of thyroid cancer can impact the mental health and financial security of affected individuals and their families (13-15). Such stressors may be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes (16).

There are limited robust data on neonatal outcomes following maternal thyroid cancer in pregnancy. Recent retrospective reviews from China have reported an association between thyroid cancer in pregnancy and preterm delivery and small-for-gestational-age outcomes (17), and an increased risk of fetal growth restriction compared to other cancers (18). However, these findings are limited by small sample sizes, and heterogeneity in maternal treatment exposures.

To address these issues, we conducted a large population-based cohort study across New South Wales (NSW), Australia to describe the incidence of PATC, current management practices, and time to next birth of women diagnosed with PATC. We further aimed to estimate associations between PATC and perinatal and survival outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study in NSW, Australia. NSW is the largest state in Australia by population, with more than 8 million residents, representing approximately one-third of the Australian population. The NSW health datasets used in this study therefore capture a large, diverse, and population-level cohort of pregnant women, including all births recorded in the state during the study period. As such, the sample is highly representative of the wider Australian population and provides findings that are generalizable within the Australian context and broadly comparable to other high-income settings with similar healthcare systems.

We used linked data from 7 large Australian datasets comprising: the NSW Perinatal Data Collection (PDC), the NSW Central Cancer Registry, the NSW Admitted Patient Data Collection (APDC), the Register of Congenital Conditions, the Registrar of Births, Deaths and Marriages, the Cause of Death Unit Record File, and the Perinatal Death Review. To link these datasets, we used probabilistic data linkage through the NSW Centre for Health Record Linkage (19). Note that the APDC dataset, from which we accessed cancer treatment information, only contains data from July 1, 2001 onward. NSW routinely collected datasets cover all public and private hospital births, allowing for comprehensive population-based ascertainment of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer across the state.

We included all women who gave birth between January 1, 1994 and December 31, 2013 and their children, using strict criteria for what was considered a birth. Specifically, we included all live births, as well as stillbirths of at least 20 weeks’ gestation or at least 400 g birth weight (20). For women with multiple births during the study period, we included each pregnancy that met the inclusion criteria as a separate data point.

We stratified each eligible pregnancy into 1 of 3 groups. The first group consisted of women who were diagnosed with any type of thyroid cancer during pregnancy, as well as their children (the “gestational thyroid cancer group”). The second group consisted of women who were diagnosed with any type of thyroid cancer within one year of giving birth, as well as their children (the “postpartum thyroid cancer group”). Finally, the comparison group comprised all women who were not diagnosed with cancer during pregnancy or within one year of giving birth, as well as their children (the “non-cancer pregnant control (control) group”). We excluded women diagnosed with cancers other than thyroid cancer during pregnancy or within one year of giving birth, as their management and outcomes may have differed from those with no pregnancy-associated cancer.

The cohort for this study did not include individuals diagnosed with thyroid cancer outside the context of pregnancy or postpartum. This is because our primary linkage framework was built around the NSW PDC, into which NSW Central Cancer Registry data and other health datasets were merged, resulting in a cohort limited to women with pregnancies.

Main outcome measures

Maternal outcomes were assessed in relation to pregnancy, birth and cancer management. Key outcomes included maternal death, maternal survival to 5 years, type, stage and timing of cancer, treatment, and time to next birth. Data on the extent of cancer spread were obtained from the NSW Cancer Registry, which categorizes cancers into 3 stages: Stage 1 refers to cancer confined to the tissue of origin; Stage 2 indicates spread to nearby lymph nodes and/or adjacent organs; and Stage 3 denotes distant metastasis (21). For cancer treatment, a validated list of ICD-10-AM and Australian Classification of Health Interventions codes was used (total thyroidectomy (30296-00 and 30296-01); total thyroidectomy, following previous thyroid surgery (0297-00); subtotal thyroidectomy, following previous thyroid surgery (30297-01); thyroidectomy following previous thyroid surgery (30297-02); sentinel lymph node biopsy of axilla (30300-00); sentinel lymph node biopsy, not elsewhere classified (30300-01); total thyroid lobectomy, unilateral (30306-00 and 30306-01); subtotal thyroidectomy, bilateral (30308-00); subtotal thyroidectomy for thyrotoxicosis (30309-00); subtotal thyroidectomy, unilateral (30310-00); excision of lesion of thyroid (30310-01); subtotal thyroidectomy, substernal (90046-00); total thyroidectomy, substernal (90046-01); thyroidectomy, with removal substernal thyroid (90046-02); whole body study using iodine (61426-00); and administration of a therapeutic dose of Iodine (16009-00)). To assess maternal morbidity, a composite validated population-based measure of maternal morbidity (the Maternal Morbidity Outcome Indicator [MMOI]) was used (22). The MMOI includes 14 major maternal morbidities (eg, acute abdomen, acute renal failure, acute psychosis) and 11 procedures (eg, hysterectomy, procedures to reduce blood flow to uterus) (22). Any women admitted to hospital during pregnancy, or within 42 days postpartum, with any of these diagnoses or procedures was deemed to have had a severe maternal morbidity outcome.

Neonatal outcomes that were assessed comprised perinatal death, preterm birth, low birthweight, small for gestational age (23, 24), large for gestational age, low 5-minute Apgar score, admission to special care nursery or neonatal intensive care unit, prolonged hospital stay, congenital malformation, and a composite neonatal adverse outcome indicator (NAOI). The NAOI is a validated population-based measure of severe neonatal morbidity that is designed to identify infants with serious morbidity at birth using routinely collected population data (25). It includes 12 diagnoses (eg, respiratory distress syndrome, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy) and 7 procedures (eg, resuscitation, central venous or arterial catheter) (25).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics for nominal data are reported as counts and percentages, while continuous measures are presented as means and standard deviations. To compare study groups, differences in continuous variables were assessed using the t-test or 1-way ANOVA, and for categorical variables, the chi-squared test or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test were used.

To estimate the yearly change in the incidence of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer (stratified by gestational or postpartum) over the study period, we used a Poisson regression model that was both unadjusted and adjusted for women's ages. The direct age-standardized incidence rate was estimated using 1994 Australian population data for women giving birth as the standard population.

Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test were used to compare all-cause mortality between the gestational and postpartum thyroid cancer groups. The time to death (from any cause) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death. Surviving participants were censored at the end of the follow-up date (September 30, 2018).

We used separate generalized estimating equation (GEE) models with an exchangeable correlation structure with robust standard errors to compare the likelihood of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes between study groups, fitting one model per outcome. For these models, we used clustering at the level of the individual to account for women contributing more than one pregnancy to the dataset. That is, the correlation we dealt with is repeated measures within the same individual rather than clustering at the hospital level. Available covariates included in the models were: maternal age, country of birth, remoteness, parity, timing of antenatal care, smoking during pregnancy, plurality, pre-existing conditions, a previous cesarean section, and place of birth. These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and established evidence within the obstetric oncology literature and were prioritized for inclusion based on their identified role as potential confounders. Consistent with a theory-driven approach aimed at estimating adjusted associations rather than causal effects, covariate selection was guided primarily by clinical knowledge and confounding structure rather than automated or data-driven algorithms. As a secondary measure to ensure model parsimony and numerical stability, univariable screening with a P-value of <.25 was used to inform variable entry into multivariable models, without overriding the inclusion of clinically important confounders. Final models were determined by considering collinearity, statistical significance, and goodness of fit. However, known clinical confounders were retained regardless of statistical significance to ensure robustly adjusted associations. To avoid misinterpretation consistent with concerns raised by the “Table 2 fallacy,” we report only the adjusted association for the primary exposure in each outcome-specific model, and do not present or interpret coefficients for adjustment covariates. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR), adjusted odds ratios (AOR), and 95% CI. As multiple births have a different clinical course and baseline risk profile compared with singletons, we included plurality as a covariate in the multivariable GEE models to account for its potential confounding effect on the association between study groups and adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. In order to address the possibility that adjustment alone may not have fully addressed potential differences between singleton and multiple births, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to singleton pregnancies (ie, excluding all multiple births) in which we repeated the multivariable analyses. Findings with a P-value <.05 or a CI not including 1 were considered statistically significant. We analyzed the data using R Core Team software (2013) (26) and IBM SPSS Version 30 (IBM Corporation).

Ethics approval

This project received ethics approval from the NSW Population & Health Services Research Ethics Committee (reference HREC/17/CIPHS/11).

Results

There were 1 788 451 women who gave birth in NSW during the twenty-year study period. Of these women, 370 were diagnosed with PATC, comprising 62 women (who gave birth to 64 babies) diagnosed with gestational thyroid cancer and 308 women (312 babies) diagnosed with postpartum thyroid cancer. An additional 2003 women were diagnosed with other types of pregnancy-associated cancer during the study period; these women were excluded from the control group but remained included in the total study population and in incidence calculations.

Incidence

The incidence of PATC from 1994- to 2013 was 20.7 per 100 000 women giving birth (while the incidence of other types of pregnancy-associated cancer was 112.0 per 100 000). The incidence of PATC significantly increased during the study period (unadjusted annual increase, 5.4% [95% CI: 3.5%-7.4%]; annual increase adjusted for maternal age, 5.0% [95% CI: 3.1%-6.9%]; see Fig. 1).

Treatment data for women with PATC

Treatment information relating to thyroid surgery and radioactive iodine was only available for women diagnosed on or after July 1, 2001 when the APDC commenced. All other follow-up data (including hospitalizations and mortality) were complete for all women regardless of date of diagnosis.

Treatment of gestational thyroid cancer

Of the 62 women diagnosed with gestational thyroid cancer, treatment data were available for 41 (66%). Among these 41 women, 32 (78.0%) underwent thyroidectomy during pregnancy, with 6 of these 32 women having additional thyroid surgery after childbirth. All 17 women diagnosed with localized disease underwent thyroidectomy during pregnancy, while 76.5% (13/17) of regional-stage cases underwent thyroidectomy during pregnancy. For the 32 women who underwent thyroidectomy during pregnancy, most surgeries took place during the second trimester (25/32, 78.1%), with a mean gestational age at the time of the procedure of 22 weeks (95% CI: 20-25 weeks). Of the 9 women diagnosed during pregnancy who did not have thyroid surgery during pregnancy, 6 underwent surgery within 1 year of giving birth. The mean interval from childbirth to thyroidectomy for these women was 53 days (95% CI: 32-75 days). Three women had no record of thyroid surgery during pregnancy or within 1 year of childbirth.

As expected, no women received iodine-131 treatment during pregnancy. Twenty-four (58.5%) women had iodine-131 treatment within 1 year postpartum. The mean interval from childbirth to iodine-131 treatment was 110 days (95% CI: 90-131 days). Women with regional thyroid cancer were more likely to receive iodine-131 within 1 year of childbirth than women with localized thyroid cancer (14/17 (82.4%) vs 7/17 (41.2%), P = .013).

Two women did not receive any interventional thyroid cancer management during pregnancy or within 1 year postpartum. Both women were diagnosed with papillary thyroid cancer of unknown staging. One of these women was diagnosed at 40 weeks gestation and the other in the second trimester. Both women received treatment within 2 years post-partum, with one undergoing surgery and iodine-131 treatment and the second receiving iodine-131 at 26 months post-diagnosis date (19 months postpartum).

Treatment of postpartum thyroid cancer

Of the 308 women diagnosed with thyroid cancer in the postpartum period, treatment data were available for 243 (78.9%). Of these 243 women, 219 (90.1%) underwent thyroidectomy within 1 year of diagnosis and 94 (38.7%) received iodine-131 treatment within 1 year of diagnosis. Women with distant thyroid cancer or regional thyroid cancer were more likely to receive iodine-131 than women with localized thyroid cancer (4/6 (66.7%) vs 43/76 (56.6%) vs 46/150 (30.7%), P < .001).

Survival outcomes

Six of the 370 women with PATC died by the end of the follow-up period. The mortality incidence rate for women with PATC was 13.3 deaths per 10 000 person-years follow-up time (95% CI: 5.4-27.6). Of the 6 reported deaths, 3 occurred among the 62 women with gestational thyroid cancer (mortality incidence rate =37.4 deaths per 10 000 person-years follow-up time, 95% CI: 7.5-109.2) and 3 among the 308 women with postpartum thyroid cancer (mortality incidence rate = 8.1 deaths per 10 000 person-years follow-up time, 95% CI: 1.6-23.6).

Cause of death information was available for 5 of the 6 women. Of these, 2 had thyroid cancer recorded as principal cause of death (metastatic medullary carcinoma and; papillary thyroid carcinoma with unknown staging). The other 3 women had a second primary cancer diagnosis after their PATC diagnosis, with 2 dying from the secondary cancer and 1 from an intraventricular hemorrhage.

Pregnancy outcomes

Tables 3 and 4 present the obstetric morbidities, labor, and birth outcomes for the cohort. While women with gestational thyroid cancer had a greater risk of experiencing a composite severe maternal morbidity outcome than women in the control group (4.9% vs 2.4%), GEE modeling revealed that this difference was not significant (AOR = 1.99, 95% CI 0.49-8.1). Women with postpartum thyroid cancer were more likely to have a history of gestational diabetes compared to women in the control group (AOR =1.59, 95% CI 1.09-2.32).

Tables 3 and 5 present perinatal outcomes for babies born to women in the study cohort. Sensitivity analysis—singleton births only

When we repeated the multivariable analyses for pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, and restricted the cohort to singleton pregnancies (ie, excluding all multiple births), we observed that the associations between study group and adverse maternal outcomes were materially unchanged (data are presented in Table S1 (27)). Effect estimates were similar in direction and magnitude to those observed in the primary analysis, indicating that the inclusion of multiple gestations did not meaningfully influence the study findings.

Pregnancy and birth after a previous diagnosis of gestational thyroid cancer

Follow-up data relating to women's births after the index birth was available for a minimum of 4 years, extending up to 2018. Among the 62 women diagnosed with gestational thyroid cancer, 27 (43.5%) experienced at least one subsequent birth following their index birth, while 9 (14.5%) women had at least 2 subsequent births, and 3 (4.8%) had at least 3 subsequent births. The mean interval to the first birth post-index birth was 1108 days (95% CI: 948-1269 days). Among the women with subsequent births, 16 (59.3%) were nulliparous at diagnosis during their index pregnancy, and 11 (40.7%) had a parity of ≥1 at the time of diagnosis.

Subsequent births were recorded for 11 of 25 (44.0%) women with a diagnosis of localized gestational thyroid cancer, 13 of 26 (50.0%) women with a diagnosis of regional gestational thyroid cancer, and 3 of 10 (30.0%) women with a diagnosis of gestational thyroid cancer of unknown stage. There was only one woman diagnosed with distant gestational thyroid cancer, and she did not have a subsequent birth.

Discussion

This large data linkage cohort study presents important information regarding incidence and outcomes of PATC from a dataset representative of the Australian population. In interpreting findings of the analyses conducted for this study, it should be noted that we aimed to estimate associations, rather than demonstrate causation, between PATC and perinatal outcomes. Further, it is important to recognize that while the overall dataset is very large, the number of PATC cases in our cohort was relatively small, especially when these cases were stratified into gestational and postpartum thyroid cancer subgroups. This likely resulted in wider CIs, reduced precision, and limited statistical power for our calculations. It is possible that this limitation may explain why we did not observe statistically significant differences for some outcomes, including overall survival and several maternal and neonatal outcomes. It is therefore important to interpret findings of our analyses cautiously and to regard them as most appropriate as a base from which to generate hypotheses that may be useful for informing future research.

These data demonstrate that the increasing incidence of thyroid cancer that is seen in global registries (1, 28) and in the general Australian population (29) is mirrored in pregnancy-specific cohorts. Our study aligned with this trend, with an overall incidence of PATC of 20.7 per 100 000 births in NSW and an annual increase of 5.0% over the study period, even after adjusting for maternal age.

Women with postpartum thyroid cancer were older than women in the control group. This aligns with Australian epidemiological data showing that thyroid cancer is most diagnosed in women in their 30 and 40 s (29), representing a well-recognized peak in thyroid cancer incidence during the later reproductive years as well as a shift toward older maternal age. In part, the observed increase in incidence is attributable to overdiagnosis (28, 30, 31). However, it is likely that epidemiological changes, including increasing rates of obesity, may also be contributing, particularly in young adult cohorts (32-34).

Management practices demonstrated by these data reflect important factors in PATC-care. In this study, a greater proportion of women in the gestational thyroid cancer group were diagnosed at a regional stage (52%) compared with women in the postpartum thyroid cancer group (32.8%) with the latter group being more likely to be diagnosed with localized disease. This may reflect appropriate clinical judgement (4), where sonographically “low risk’ thyroid nodules can have investigation (eg, biopsy) and management (thyroidectomy) deferred to the postpartum period, whereas higher risk presentations (such as with lateral neck lymphadenopathy) necessitate management during pregnancy. Such management approaches are supported by guidelines (35) and are likely to become more common given global trends toward de-escalation of low-risk thyroid cancer management. Despite this, a relatively high proportion of gestational thyroid cancer cases (78%) underwent surgical management during pregnancy at an appropriate gestational age during the second trimester (mean gestational age at surgery of 22 weeks), concordant with guideline recommendations to minimize fetal risk (6, 36).

Where radioactive iodine was administered, this occurred universally postpartum. Radioactive iodine is recommended following thyroidectomy for a subset of DTC with a higher risk of recurrence (37). Radioactive iodine is contraindicated in pregnancy and poses challenges in the postpartum period, with no close contact allowed between mother and baby for 7 days post-treatment (38). Furthermore, breast tissue may be at risk of exposure to ionizing radiation as it expresses the sodium-iodide symporter, which is upregulated during lactation. Breastfeeding should therefore cease 8 weeks before radioactive iodine and should not be recommenced post treatment (39).

In our study, 58.5% of women received radioactive iodine postpartum, with the average interval from delivery to RAI treatment being 110 days (95% CI: 90-131). There is conflicting evidence regarding the impact of timing of radioactive iodine post-pregnancy on treatment outcomes, and treatment decisions must weigh patient circumstances, disease risk, and family dynamics (4). It is likely that future PATC cohorts will have lower rates of surgical management and lower rates of radioactive iodine reflective of trends toward active surveillance and less-than-total thyroidectomy for lower-risk thyroid cancers.

Of note, we report that women diagnosed with postpartum thyroid cancer had significantly higher odds of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) compared with controls. This association was specific to the postpartum group and did not extend to the gestational thyroid cancer group. Emerging data from large cohort studies increasingly support a link between diabetes mellitus, including GDM, and thyroid cancer. For example, a recent meta-analysis of 4 cohort studies comparing women with GDM to their non-GDM counterparts found the relative risk of developing thyroid cancer was 1.30 (95% CI 1.17, 1.43) over a 3-20-year follow-up period (40). Whether this association is driven by underlying drivers of metabolic syndrome, or physiologic changes specific to GDM in pregnancy, or other factors, requires further study.

With regards to maternal outcomes, while the mortality rate for our cohort was higher among women diagnosed with thyroid cancer during pregnancy compared with those diagnosed postpartum, this difference was not significant. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that pregnancy does not worsen thyroid cancer prognosis, although persistent or recurrent disease has been reported in some pregnancy-associated cases (11).

Regarding obstetric and neonatal outcomes, this study shows that babies born to women with gestational thyroid cancer were at increased odds of experiencing severe neonatal complications compared to those born to women in the control group. This result is difficult to interpret and may represent be a Type 1 error given that the NAOI is a composite outcome and no single neonatal outcome was assessed to be statistically different between the study groups. Nonetheless, NICU admissions were more frequent (26.6%) in the gestational thyroid cancer group compared with the postpartum thyroid cancer group (14.7%) and controls (16.4%). Although these differences did not reach statistical significance in multivariable models, the trend may reflect heightened clinical vigilance, marginally increased rates of iatrogenic early delivery, or multidisciplinary management practices in tertiary settings. Other large cohort studies are reassuring (41) in respect to a lack of impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes, although some studies report increased rates of maternal venous thromboembolism and higher rates of cesarean section (42-44).

The available literature on neonatal outcomes in maternal thyroid cancer is mixed. While the aforementioned studies suggest no meaningful impact on neonatal outcomes, others have raised concerns about subtle thyroid dysfunction or growth restriction in offspring (18, 41, 45, 46). Our findings support the suggestion that neonatal outcomes for babies of mothers with PATC are generally favorable.

Women with pregnancy-associated cancer desire information about future fertility and, for cancers where treatment may affect future fertility, options for fertility preservation (47). Our study findings add reassurance for those women with pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer who have not completed family formation (48). Among the women diagnosed during pregnancy, 43.5% had at least one subsequent birth. The average interval was approximately 3 years, in keeping with standard clinical guidance to delay conception for at least 6 months after radioactive iodine treatment.

Interestingly, nulliparous women were more likely to have a subsequent birth, perhaps suggesting that the lower rates of subsequent births observed among women who had previously had a birth may have been associated with completion of family formation rather than any deleterious impact of the cancer diagnosis or treatment on fertility. However, while fertility was generally preserved following cancer treatment, rates of subsequent pregnancy varied by disease stage at diagnosis, with no births observed among women with distant disease. This may indicate that the diagnosis and treatment of more advanced disease may have future impacts on fertility.

Strengths and limitations

Pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer is uncommon. A major strength of this population study is its design, encompassing all births and thyroid cancer diagnoses of women giving birth in NSW over a 20-year period with a minimum 5-year follow-up. Additionally, in accordance with the contemporary literature, we included women with a diagnosis of postpartum thyroid cancer as a proxy for undiagnosed cases during pregnancy, allowing identification of all cases of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer that resulted in a birth. One main limitation of the study is the lack of data on early pregnancy loss including termination of pregnancy. In addition, the baseline maternal characteristics available for analysis were obtained from the NSW PDC. While the PDC captures a wide range of pregnancy-related demographic and clinical variables, it does not include data relating to maternal height, weight, or BMI measurements, nor does it include information on individual-level thyroid cancer risk factors such as prior radiation exposure, family history, or rare genetic syndromes relevant to thyroid cancer risk. As a result, we were unable to adjust for such indicators in our multivariable models. Residual confounding due to unavailable variables, such as BMI, is possible and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Despite the large underlying pregnancy population in our cohort, pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer is rare. This resulted in small case counts for some outcomes and wider confidence intervals, indicating reduced precision. These estimates should be interpreted with caution. Key strengths of this study are the inclusion of treatment data capturing real-world management practices such as high rates of antepartum thyroidectomy, and the extended period of follow-up, enabling calculation of time to next birth and mortality. This addresses a need identified by women for tailored information on future fertility and long-term outcomes, and for clinicians to create educational and counseling resources (47).

Conclusions

In this large population-based cohort study linking perinatal, cancer and hospital data over 2 decades, the incidence of pregnancy-associated thyroid cancer in NSW increased significantly, mirroring global trends. Women with postpartum thyroid cancer had significantly higher odds of GDM, which invites further study. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were reassuring despite intervention being common during and following pregnancy. Fertility was largely preserved in women with gestational or postpartum thyroid cancer except where distant disease was present.
