Intelligent health management strategies, adaptation, and validation of the SAT-SF questionnaire into Spanish in cancer patients
Authors: Lidia V. Cerron Tito, Juleysi Delgado Baca, Marcos Espinola-Sánchez, Lindsey W. Vilca, Salomon Huancahuire-Vega
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionA sudden cancer diagnosis creates a psycho-emotional and social crisis, making it crucial to manage emotional coping strategies and assess comprehensive health care, addressing both behavioral and practical aspects of patients’ daily lives. Although there are instruments that measure strategies for emotional coping, emotional adjustment or post-traumatic growth, we still do not have a questionnaire in Spanish that comprehensively evaluates health self-management.AimThis study aims to translate into Spanish and determine the validity and reliability of the Spanish version of the Smart Management Strategy for Health Assessment Tool (SAT-SF) evaluation questionnaire in Peruvian patients with cancer.MethodologyCross-sectional study. Translation and back-translation of the SAT-SF were carried out. Next, 271 Peruvian adult patients of both sexes diagnosed with different types of cancer participated in the validation process. The factor structure was studied through Confirmatory Factor Analysis, the scale’s reliability through Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients, and the factorial invariance of the scale according to sex. For concurrent validity, the association with the Medical Outcomes Study (MOS) social support scale was analyzed.ResultThe original model of three related dimensions presents acceptable fit indices to the data (χ2 = 1202.23; df = 402; p < .001; RMSEA = .086 [90% CI .080–.091]; SRMR = .065; CFI = .940; TLI = .930). The scale’s factor structure has shown evidence of being strictly invariant for the groups of men and women. The reliability indices according to dimensions were Personal competencies (α = .91; ω = .92), Preparation strategies (α = .87; ω = .86) and Implementation strategies (α = .89; ω = .91). Additionally, it was evidenced that the dimensions of the SAT-SF are significantly related to the dimensions of the Social Support scale.ConclusionThe SAT-SF questionnaire was adapted to Spanish, demonstrating adequate construct validity, internal consistency, reliability, and concurrent validity.

Introduction

One of the leading causes of mortality in the world, regardless of the income levels of any country, is cancer (Torre et al., 2016). In 2020, the ‘Global Cancer Statistics’ reported 19.3 million new cases and 10 million deaths due to cancer in the world. Additionally, they placed America in third place being the continent with a 20.9% cancer incidence and 14.2% of global cancer mortality for both men and women (Sung et al., 2021).

According to data from the National Institute of Neoplastic Diseases of Peru, it is estimated that the annual incidence of cancer in Peru between 2017 and 2021 has been of 12,000 new cases per year. These cases are more frequent in the adult and older adult population, with a higher incidence in women accounting for 63.2% of all cases. Regarding the affected anatomical site, cervical and breast cancers are the most frequent in women, and in men, the most frequent sites of cancer are the stomach and prostate (Perú, 2021).

The sudden diagnosis of cancer is a traumatic event that generates various types of crises, among which we highlight the psycho-emotional and social crisis (Riba et al., 2019). This imbalance manifests itself in various ways, such as anxiety, fear, depression, and other emotional and social problems such as guilt, anger, frustration, and social isolation, which directly influence the treatment process, recovery from the disease, management of long-term effects, and decision-making (Brandão et al., 2017). The oncologic patient experiences a stage of ‘adaptation’, considered as a constant process where he or she tries to cope with emotional suffering and seeks to solve problems, until he or she manages to gain control over the unexpected events of life (Rivera-Cruzatt et al., 2022; Villoria et al., 2021). Therefore, support through emotional coping strategies, such us social and family support, religion, counseling, and conversation therapy has been shown to have a positive impact on patients’ health outcomes (Mungase et al., 2021; Roszkowska & Białczyk, 2023). Therefore, it is essential that patients manage their health in a holistic manner, encompassing behavioral and practical aspects of their daily lives. This includes self-management of health, interaction with the healthcare system, self-care and informed decision-making. These factors not only improve clinical outcomes, but also contribute to greater adherence to treatment and a better quality of life (Dědová et al., 2022).

There are various specialized instruments that evaluate emotional coping, emotional adjustment and post-traumatic growth in cancer patients or those with other types of health conditions (Dieter & Sigrun-Heide, 2021). Among the most commonly used are the Brief COPE and the Mental Adjustment to Cancer Scale (Mini-MAC). These are also used: the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI), and the Cancer Coping Questionnaire (CCQ) (Castro et al., 2015; Román Calderón et al., 2015; Toledano-Toledano et al., 2020).

Over the past 10 years, the Smart Management Strategy for Health Assessment Tool – short form (SAT-SF) was developed, a questionnaire that allows patients and health personnel to evaluate comprehensive health self-management strategies in the face of a psycho-emotional and social crisis precipitated by the sudden diagnosis of chronic diseases, such as cancer. It has proven to be practical and useful, as it provides feedback on the behaviors and strategies that need to be changed to achieve better health outcomes and helping patients strengthen their skills and compensate for their weaknesses (Yun et al., 2018). This instrument was developed and validated, and its sensitivity was determined in adult Korean cancer patients. The SAT-SF, uses three strategies to assess comprehensive health management: core competencies (SF-C), preparation strategies (SF-P), and implementation strategies (SF-I), and each strategy consists of 10 items, making a total of 30 items (Yun et al., 2018). The core strategies evaluate 4 strategies: proactive problem solving, positive rethinking, creating empowered relationships, and sharing experiences. The preparation strategies, which correspond to the phase prior to action, evaluate 5 strategies: setting goals and actions (preparation), rational decision making, creating a healthy environment (construction), planning based on priorities, and searching for vital values. The implementation strategies evaluate 6 strategies: self-sustainability, self-motivation, self-implementation (maintenance), reflection, energy conservation, coping with activities (Yun et al., 2018).

This instrument has been used in research that evaluates the association of the self-care strategy with health and various variables (Yun & Sim, 2021). Its adaptation into Spanish could be very useful to promote comprehensive self-management of health in patients with cancer and/or those suffering from other health crises, allowing them to identify their strengths and weaknesses and overcome the crisis.

Therefore, this work aimed to translate, adapt, and determine the validation and reliability of its structure in the Spanish short version of the Smart Management Strategy for Health Assessment Tool (SAT-SF) evaluation questionnaire in Peruvian patients with cancer. The results obtained in the Spanish-speaking population will consistently replicate the findings observed in the original version, suggesting that the variables studied are similarly applicable in both contexts.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional design was chosen for the present study. The questionnaire was validated in 271 Peruvian patients diagnosed with different types of cancer from the Peruvian Institute of Oncology and Radiotherapy (IPOR). Non-probabilistic convenience sampling was carried out. After authorizing the study, the health institution provided the contact information of the patients, who were contacted by the researchers by telephone and were explained the procedure for completing the online survey, after giving an informed consent. The inclusion criteria were: patients diagnosed with cancer, of legal age, of both sexes, treated at the Peruvian Institute of Oncology & Radiotherapy, who were in any stage of cancer and/or treatment and who gave their consent to participate in the study. Those under the age of majority, who had not been diagnosed with cancer, who did not understand Spanish and those who did not give informed consent were excluded. The sample size was estimated using the electronic calculator by Daniel Soper, which considers the number of observed and latent variables, the anticipated effect size (λ = .15), the desired statistical significance (α = .80), and the level of statistical power (1−β = .05), resulting in a minimum required sample of 270 participants (Jobst et al., 2023).

Original instrument

The evaluation instrument of the Smart Management Strategy for Health (SAT-SF) was built and validated in English and has 3 strategies (factors): core competencies (SF-C), preparation strategies (SF-P), and implementation strategies (SF-I). Each contains 10 questions, rated according to a 4-point Likert scale (never = 0, sometimes = 1, very often = 2, and always = 3). The final score is from 0 to 90, the higher the score, the higher the level of strategy the patient uses. The three factors or subscales of the SAT-SF showed high reliability with good internal consistency in the original published version (Cronbach’s α: .906 for SF-C, .874 for SF-P, and .868 for SF-I) (Yun et al., 2018).

Procedure

The present study had 2 phases: translation and cultural adaptation and validation of the instrument (Figure 1). Before beginning, permission was requested from the author of the original instrument via email, which was granted, and afterward an editable document of the instrument was received. The translation process was carried out according to the recommendations of Guillemin et al., with an initial translation by a group of translators, back-translation by other translators without knowledge of the original instrument, a review committee, and a focus group as pre-testing before validation (Guillemin et al., 1993). Phase two includes the stages of content validation and pilot testing with 270 people, culminating in the stage of reliability and internal and concurrent validation.

Translation, adaptation, and validation process.

Initial translation

Four doctors were selected to collaborate as interpreters in this study. They are fluent in English and Spanish; however, they are from South American countries. Each one prepared a separate translation, and then the four interpreters met with a Peruvian medicine professor and an American specialist in the translation of tools, thus forming the review committee. In a face-to-face meeting, the six evaluated the translations of each instrument and created a unified version of them, including observations on the necessary modifications.

Back translation

The unified version was sent to two translators certified by the College of Translators of Peru, who did not have prior information about the study. The American instrument translation specialist coordinated the consolidation of the translations. Both the unified version and the two translations by official translators were sent to the main author of the original instrument, who approved both versions.

Focus group

Subsequently, the focus group was carried out with 7 oncology patients to evaluate the adequate understanding of each item. This way, minimal modifications were made such as eliminating redundant terms, and the change of two words (item 5 the word ‘dark’ was changed to ‘negative’; item 6 ‘opened’ was changed to ‘considered’). Appendix 1 shows the Spanish version which was used in the validation study, which includes the changes made due to comments from the focus group, compared with the original English version.

Instrument validation

For the second phase, a group of experts made up of seven health professionals (4 doctors and 3 psychologists) were in charge of validating the questionnaire’s content (Appendix 2). Their review analyzed content validity through relevance, representativeness, and clarity of the items, quantified through the Aiken V coefficient proposed by Ventura-Leon (Ventura-León, 2022). The items received a favorable evaluation by the experts (V > 0.70).

Next, adult Peruvian patients diagnosed with different types of cancer treated at the Peruvian Oncology & Radiotherapy Institute were invited to participate voluntarily. Informed consent was obtained, highlighting that both the consent process and the application of the respective questionnaire were carried out online using a specific web application (Google Forms). With the data obtained, the factorial structure of the scale, reliability, factorial invariance according to sex, and concurrent validity with other instruments, such as the MOS perceived social support scale (Medical Outcomes Study), were evaluated. The MOS scale used was the Spanish version, validated in patients with cancer. The authors indicated that this questionnaire showed a high level of reliability (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient = .94) and a three-factor model that accounted for 54.2% of the total variance: informational, affective, and instrumental (Baca, 2016).

The SAT-SF scale was presented with the following statement: The following items are the core competencies/preparation strategies/implementation strategies that you could use to proactively overcome a crisis and grow positively in your life. Please read each question carefully and circle the number that best describes your activities during the last month.

The questionnaire included three additional preliminary questions that helped the participant to understand more clearly the focus of the instrument. These questions were: identify the current major health crisis, define the main objective, and determine the stage of change in which the participant is in order to achieve this objective. In addition, some sociodemographic data (sex, age, marital status, educational level, income, religion) and clinical information (type of cancer, stage of treatment and stage of cancer) were collected. All of these data were self-reported.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the items was performed to show the distribution of item responses. This analysis is important because it helps to identify problems such as skewed distributions (>90%), ceiling or floor effects, and insufficient variance (Byrne, 2013). The Weighted Least Squares Means and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimator was used to study the scale’s factor structure since the items were at the ordinal level (Brown, 2015). To evaluate the functioning of the original model of three related dimensions developed in the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), the following adjustment criteria were used: RMSEA (<.08), SRMR (<.08), CFI (>.90), and TLI (>.90) (Byrne, 2010; Kyriazos, 2018). Regarding the factor loadings, values lower than 0.30 indicate the presence of problematic items (Brown, 2015). To evaluate the reliability of the scale, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and omega coefficient were used, where a value ω > .80 is appropriate (Raykov & Hancock, 2005).

Multi-group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) was used to evaluate the factorial invariance of the scale according to sex, where a sequence of four hierarchical variance models was proposed: (1) configural invariance, (2) metric invariance, (3) scalar invariance and (4) strict invariance. To compare the sequence of models, a modeling strategy was used, for which the differences in the RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) were used, where differences less than < .015 evidence the invariance of the model between the groups (Chen, 2007). The differences in the CFI (ΔCFI) were also used, where values less than < .010 show the invariance of the model between the groups (Chen, 2007).

Regarding validity based on the relationship with other variables, a SEM analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between the SAT-SF scale and a criterion variable, the MOS scale. The same estimator and adjustment indicators used in the CFA were used. For the statistical analysis, the RStudio environment for R was used. Specifically, the ‘lavaan’ package performed for the CFA, and the ‘semTools’ package performed for the factorial invariance (Rosseel, 2012).

Ethical aspects

The conduct of this study was approved and authorized by the members of the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Peruana Unión (011-CE-FCS-UPeU-2023) and by the Ethics Committee of the Peruvian Oncology & Radiotherapy Institute (IPOR-PA23001).

All patients were informed about the research objectives and their rights according to the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. The patients voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and gave their online informed consent before the questionnaire was applied. No type of compensation was given for conducting the online survey. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee.

Validity based on internal structure

In the present study, it was found that the model of three related dimensions presents acceptable fit indices to the data (χ2 = 1202.23; df = 402; p ≤ .001; RMSEA = .086 [90% CI .080–.091]; SRMR = .065; CFI = .94; TLI = .93). Also, it is observed in Figure 2 that the dimensions present significant relationships, and the majority of the items present high factorial weights with the dimension to which they belong. Considering this evidence, this model was used for the following analyses. The factors are connected to each other with correlations of 0.74, 0.76 and 0.80, indicating relationships between core, preparation and implementation strategies.

Validity based on the relationship with other variables

Considering the literature review, an SEM model was proposed to evaluate the relationship between the SAT-SF and MOS scales. It was evident that the structural model presents adequate fit indices (χ2 = 2263.18; df = 1112; p ≤ .001; RMSEA = .062[90% CI .058‒.066]; CFI = .99; TLI = .99) and the measurement models are adequately represented by its items. Figure 3 shows that the three dimensions of the SAT-SF Spanish version are significantly related to the three dimensions of the Social Support scale. Scale reliability

In the total study sample, the three dimensions of the SAT-SF scale present adequate reliability indices: Personal competencies (α = .91; ω = .92), Preparation strategies (α = .87; ω = .86), and Implementation strategies (α = .89; ω = .91).

Discussion

This study carried out thorough processes to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the SAT-SF questionnaire in Spanish. The results provide solid evidence on the validity and reliability of the SAT-SF in a sample of Spanish-speaking patients. Sociodemographic analyses reveal that the questionnaire was administered to a diverse sample, with representation from various regions of the country and a majority of participants from the capital. In addition, it was observed that older adults constitute a relevant group (40.59%), which highlights the importance of designing interventions focused on this population to improve their capacity for self-management of health, especially in those with frequent diagnoses such as breast and prostate cancer.

The translation process was based on the guidelines established by Guillemin et al., and fortunately, the original author was willing to help with the project (Beaton et al., 2000). Including bilingual medical professionals, a bilingual native English speaker, and a non-fluent English teacher helped form a solid team to translate the instrument and incorporate suggestions from the original author, the focus group, and the expert judges. This helped ensure a more accurate and understandable translation.

Regarding content validity, the experts gave their opinion, which made it possible to demonstrate the representativeness, clarity, and relevance of the items of the SAT-SF questionnaire in its Spanish version. This finding is similar to what was reported in the original instrument, where the Delphi method was used, and the experts mentioned that the items were relevant and valuable (Yun et al., 2015).

Regarding the internal structure, the three-dimensional model showed an adequate fit, supporting the construct validity of the instrument, similar to the original model (Yun et al., 2018). The high factor loadings and the consistency in the relationships between dimensions reinforce the validity of the SAT-SF Spanish version, allowing us to affirm that this instrument is capable of accurately capturing health self-management in this population. Furthermore, the factorial invariance between men and women suggests that the SAT-SF can be used reliably in both groups without gender bias, which expands its applicability in different clinical contexts.

Research on the SAT-SF Spanish version indicates that this instrument has appropriate psychometric properties, evidencing internal coherence, the ability to distinguish between different variables, and simultaneous validity in cancer patients and that its application could be verified in patients facing other types health illnesses and even in the general population (Yun & Sim, 2020). For example, for older people who face the impact of economic crises on their well-being, SAT scores are positively related to a better physical and mental quality of life, effectively differentiating between different population groups. Additionally, SAT-SF scores correlate with social support, spiritual well-being, and life satisfaction (Yun et al., 2018).

The validity of the three factors of the original SAT-SF was significantly correlated with the scores of previously validated questionnaires such as the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory, a 10-item scale that measures domains of personal growth after a traumatic event, the Meaning in Life Questionnaire-MV3, a 3-item scale that measures meaning in life and psychological well-being (Quezada-Berumen & González-Ramírez, 2020; Steger & Samman, 2012). In our study, the analysis of the relationship between the SAT-SF and social support (assessed using the MOS scale) shows that the dimensions of health self-management are significantly correlated with perceived social support. This finding not only supports the convergent validity of the SAT-SF, but also underlines the relevance of having support networks as a key factor for effective self-management, especially in patients with chronic diseases. These support networks may include professional interventions such as coaching or health counselling, which have been shown to be effective and cost-effective management models.

Finally, the results showed that the three dimensions of the SAT-SF present high reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients greater than .85, which ensures that the questionnaire is internally consistent and suitable for future applications in research and clinical practice.

Those individuals with high scores in the three sections of the SAT-SF Spanish version exhibit achievements in achieving fundamental goals, maintaining good health, experiencing a high quality of life, experiencing satisfaction, showing self-leadership, resolving conflicts, and accepting social support from caregivers, friends, and health personnel (Ito & Tadaka, 2017). Some patients deal with crises proactively; however, others need the support of a professional, such as a coach or health advisor, which has proven to be an effective and profitable management model (Krishnamurthi et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2020). Cancer patients need to strengthen their ability to cope with the crisis, such as diagnosis, recurrence, or terminal period of cancer (Yun et al., 2020).

Therefore, our findings have significant theoretical and practical implications. In theory, they contribute to the field of psychometrics and health assessment, reinforcing models on the importance of health self-management. In practice, they impact clinical intervention and medical research and can be a useful tool in occupational health. In the clinical setting, this instrument allows to assess the ability of Spanish-speaking patients to manage chronic diseases, such as cancer, through periodic follow-up every 3–6 months, personalizing self-care interventions and monitoring progress. In research, it helps to identify patterns of health self-management in different populations, providing valuable data to design specific interventions that improve health outcomes at the community level.

Although the adapted instrument has adequate psychometric properties, this study has some limitations. The participants were not randomly selected, so the results cannot be extrapolated to the entire population of cancer patients. Our sample focused on cancer patients receiving medical care at a single Institute in an urban area, the results may not be representative and generalized to other rural populations. Although a rigorous linguistic adaptation process was carried out, some expressions may not adequately reflect the cultural reality of certain Spanish-speaking groups. There is also information and recall bias since the information was collected through a self-reported survey, and participants may not be precise in their answers or forget details of the information requested. On the other hand, the validation results, which are exclusively in oncology patients, cannot be extended to patients with other chronic diseases, or the application of the instrument should be previously verified. Additionally, sensitivity to change, such as improvement or progression of cancer or the occurrence of new events, was not assessed. The test-retest was also not evaluated, which may not be feasible due to the frequent changes in the cancer patient’s health status and quality of life.

Despite these limitations, this study is a first step towards the validation of the SAT-SF Spanish version. Future studies in different clinical or sociocultural contexts are needed to confirm its general validity.

In conclusion, adapting to Spanish and validating the SAT-SF Spanish version questionnaire has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, suggesting that it is a valid and reliable tool. Therefore, this Spanish-language assessment tool can facilitate comprehensive self-management of health and is feasible for use in the Peruvian or Spanish-speaking population with cancer.
