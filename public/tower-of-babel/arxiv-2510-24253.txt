Equivalence of Discrete and Continuous Otto-Like Engines assisted by Catalysts: Mapping Catalytic Advantages from the Discrete to the Continuous Framework
Marcin Łobejko, Tanmoy Biswas, Michał Horodecki

Abstract
The catalytic extension of a discrete two-stroke engine employs a cyclic auxiliary system—the catalyst—that remains decoupled from the baths and performs no work, yet enhances power and efficiency beyond the corresponding non-catalytic counterpart. Theoretical models of discrete engines are relatively easy to analyze but remain challenging for experimental implementation due to the required control over individual strokes. In contrast, externally driven engines that are simultaneously coupled to both heat baths — the so-called continuous engines — are more experimentally feasible. Here, we establish an equivalence between discrete and continuous machines, both with and without a catalyst, by mapping the discrete unitary processes and thermalization steps onto an interaction Hamiltonian and a Markovian model of dissipation. As a result, by replacing probability flows with probability currents, we construct an analogous continuous machine corresponding to previously demonstrated catalytic schemes that generalize Otto engines. We illustrate this mapping for the simplest catalytic extension of the Otto engine, demonstrating catalytic enhancement in the continuous regime.

In the context of thermal machines, a catalyst must satisfy two key conditions: (i) it does not introduce any new energy scale into the machine and can, in principle, have a degenerate spectrum; (ii) it is restored to its original state after the completion of the thermodynamic cycle without thermal intervention, i.e., it remains thermally uncoupled.

Within this generalized

This raises an important question: To what extent do the results obtained for discrete models of Otto-like engines remain valid for continuous engines that are more realistic? In particular, can such models accurately predict the same efficiency, and how do the two approaches compare in terms of power/work output?
Addressing this question naturally leads to an important follow-up: to what extent do the catalytic advantages observed in the discrete Otto-like scenario translate to the continuous case?

These questions point to a broader issue in the field. Much of the theoretical work has concentrated on recovering the dynamics of a continuous engine as the limiting case of a discrete engine, achieved by taking the cycle duration to be infinitesimally small. Most of these studies have focused on Otto engines (without a catalyst)

;

. This approach inherently relies on discrete engines where the work extracted per cycle vanishes in the limit of infinitesimally small cycle time . Although theoretically motivated, such discrete engines again have limited practical relevance because the work extracted per cycle vanishes in the limit of infinitesimal cycle duration.

In this Letter, rather than taking the continuous limit of a discrete engine, we introduce a continuous analog by implementing weak coupling to external baths along with an appropriate interaction Hamiltonian. This allows us to establish a direct correspondence between key quantities in the discrete and continuous frameworks, where the mapping is simply realized by replacing changes in probability flow with the dynamical probability current. Within this unified picture, we analyze efficiency and power in both paradigms. Notably, for catalytic Otto-like engines, the efficiency remains invariant under this mapping, showing that the catalytic enhancement in the discrete engine carries over to its continuous counterpart. Furthermore, the connection between discrete and continuous Otto-like engines can be captured by a single characteristic time constant—interpreted as the cycle duration—which links the power of a continuous engine (work per unit time) to that of a discrete engine (work per cycle). Overall, this mapping highlights how discrete models provide valuable insights for understanding and optimizing efficiency and power in continuous thermal machines.

Discrete engine.
We start with a mathematical formulation of the discrete engine. We consider a quantum system defined by a density matrix:

\rho=\sum_{k}p_{k}\outerproduct{k}{k}

(3)

and Hamiltonian,

H_{0}=H_{0,h}+H_{0,c},

(4)

where we split the degrees of freedom into so-called
hot
and
cold
ones. We consider then a swap process (permutation), such that some of the states
\ket{k}
are transposed with each other. In accordance, we swap states
\ket{u_i}
with
\ket{d_i}
(i.e.,
\ket{u_i}\leftrightarrow\ket{d_i}
), while remaining states
\ket{r_i}
remain unchanged. The process is realized via a permutation
S
, such that

S\ket{u_i}=\ket{d_i},\quad S\ket{d_i}=\ket{u_i},\quad S\ket{r_i}=\ket{r_i}.

(5)

Then, we define the heat as follows (for
k=h,c
):

Q_{k}=\Tr[H_{0,k}(\rho-S\rho S^{\dagger})],

(6)

together with work as a total change in the average energy of the state
\rho
, i.e.

W=\Tr[H_{0}(\rho-S\rho S^{\dagger})]=\Tr[\left(H_{0}-S^{\dagger}H_{0}S\right)\rho]=Q_{h}+Q_{c},

(7)

which defines the first law of thermodynamics. One can easily show that

Q_{k}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\Delta p_{i},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\Delta p_{i}=p_{u_{i}}-p_{d_{i}},\quad\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}=\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}-\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}

(9)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}=\bra{u_i}H_{0,k}\ket{u_i},\quad\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}=\bra{d_i}H_{0,k}\ket{d_i}.

(10)

Efficiency of the process is defined as:

\eta_{\text{disc.}}=\frac{W}{Q_{h}}=1+\frac{Q_{c}}{Q_{h}}=1+\frac{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\Delta p_{i}}{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\Delta p_{i}}.

(11)

We refer
\Delta p_{i}
as
probability flow
. The following model is purely mathematical, where the
heat
and
work
are not yet physically justified. Nevertheless, the model can function as a discrete thermal machine, provided a dissipative process is executed to restore the system to its initial state and thereby close the thermodynamic cycle.

In particular, the notion of work and heat naturally emerges, where the engine’s state consists of two thermal states at different temperatures, i.e. when

\displaystyle\varrho_{h}\otimes\varrho_{c}=\frac{e^{-\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}]}\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}]}.

(12)

In this case, by thermalizing hot (cold) degrees of freedom, we provide from the corresponding heat bath an energy
Q_{h}
(
Q_{c}
), interpreted commonly as heat. For the non-equilibrium thermal state given by Eq. (
12
), one can prove the following Clausius inequality:

\displaystyle\beta_{h}Q_{h}+\beta_{c}Q_{c}\leq 0,

(13)

which ensures the second law and upper bounds efficiency by the Carnot limit.

Continuous engine.
Next, we move to the mathematical framework of the continuous engine. Here, we consider a general model with a total Hamiltonian
H_{0}+V_{t}
given by

H_{0}=H_{0,h}+H_{0,c}\quad;\quad V_{t}=\sum_{i}g_{i}\left(\outerproduct{u_{i}}{d_{i}}e^{-i\Omega_{i}t}+h.c.\right)

(14)

where the states
\ket{u_i}
and
\ket{d_i}
are eigenstates of
H_{0}
, i.e.,

H_{0,k}\ket{u_i}=\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}\ket{u_i},\quad H_{0,k}\ket{d_i}=\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}\ket{d_i}.

(15)

for
k=h,c
. We consider a resonant driving such that

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}=\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}+\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}=\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}-\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}.

(16)

In the interaction picture, system evolves under the local master equation:

\dot{\rho}_{I}(t)=-i[V_{0},\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{h}[\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{c}[\rho_{I}(t)]:=\mathcal{L}[\rho_{I}(t)],

(17)

where the “local” assumption translates to condition
Levy and Kosloff 2014
;
Chiara et al. 2018
;
Hewgill et al. 2021
:

\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{h}[H_{0,c}]=\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{c}[H_{0,h}]=0.

(18)

Generally, the local model of the dissipator is justified when the strength of the free Hamiltonian significantly exceeds the coupling strength, i.e.,
\|H_{0}\|\gg g_{i}
. The first law is expressed by the equation:

\displaystyle\dot{E}(t)=P(t)+J_{h}(t)+J_{c}(t)

(19)

where the internal energy
E(t)
, power
P(t)
, and heat currents
J_{k}(t)
are given by:

\displaystyle E(t)=\Tr[(H_{0}+e^{iH_{0}t}V_{t}e^{-iH_{0}t})\rho_{I}(t)],

(20)

\displaystyle P(t)=-\Tr[\dot{V}_{0}\rho_{I}(t)],\quad J_{k}(t)=\Tr[\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[H_{0,k}+V_{0}]\rho_{I}(t)],

(21)

where we used the condition given in Eq. (
18
) and
\dot{V}_{0}=-i\sum_{k}g_{k}\Omega_{k}(\outerproduct{u_k}{d_k}-\text{H.C.})
. Here
H.C.
denotes the Hermitian conjugate. Furthermore, for the Gibbs states, we assume their stationarity with respect to the corresponding dissipators, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{h}[\varrho_{h}]=\mathcal{D}_{c}[\varrho_{c}]=0,

(22)

one can derive, based on Spohn’s inequality
Spohn 1978
;
Landi and Paternostro 2021
, the following “second law inequality”:

\displaystyle\frac{dS(t)}{dt}\geq\sum_{k=h,c}\beta_{k}\left(J_{k}(t)-\Tr[\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\rho_{I}(t)]\right),

(23)

where
S(t)=-\Tr[\rho_{I}(t)\ln\rho_{I}(t)]
is the von Neumann entropy. It is seen that entropy production is not generally non-negative here, which is a well-known issue with a local master equation
Levy and Kosloff 2014
;
Chiara et al. 2018
;
Hewgill et al. 2021
;
Hofer et al. 2017
. However, we will impose a non-negative entropy production in the non-equilibrium steady state (NESS).

For a time-independent Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
, we assume a
stationary state
in the long time limit, i.e.,
\rho_{I}(t)\to\rho_{I}^{s}
, which implies
\mathcal{L}[\rho_{I}^{s}]=0
. Then, the stationary values of the power and heat currents are given by:

\displaystyle P=-\langle\dot{V}_{0}\rangle,\quad J_{k}=\langle\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[H_{0,k}]\rangle+\langle\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\rangle,

(24)

where
\langle A\rangle=\Tr[A\rho_{I}^{s}]
. Then, the non-negative entropy production in the NESS is provided whenever

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}_{h}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\rangle=\langle\mathcal{D}_{c}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\rangle=0,

(25)

from which we get the Clausius inequality:

\displaystyle\beta_{h}J_{h}+\beta_{c}J_{c}\leq 0.

(26)

In principle, the condition (
25
) is satisfied for the examples we demonstrate later. Furthermore, with a definition of a stationary
probability current
:

\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle=ig_{i}\Big\langle\outerproduct{u_{i}}{d_{i}}-\outerproduct{d_{i}}{u_{i}}\Big\rangle

(27)

we get expressions for the power and heat currents in the stationary state:

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i}\Omega_{i}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle,\quad J_{k}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle.

(28)

Together with a resonant condition (
16
), this reveals the energy conservation:

P=J_{h}+J_{c}.

(29)

Finally, the efficiency of the introduced continuous engine is given by:

\eta_{\text{cont.}}=1+\frac{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle}{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle}.

(30)

In a nutshell, the general model of the continuous heat engine, that brings the expression for heat currents (
28
) together with the first law (
29
) and the second law (
26
), is solely based on the resonant interaction (Eqs. (
14
), (
16
)), the local master equation model (Eq. (
18
)) and the condition given in Eq. (
25
).

Catalysis in discrete and continuous engine
.
To complete the thermodynamic cycle of a conventional heat engine, the working medium must be restored to its initial state through a dissipative process. As an alternative, we introduce an ancillary system—the
catalyst
—that satisfies the
catalysis condition
, namely, its marginal state returns to the initial one after the work stroke (see Fig.
1
). In particular, a discrete engine assisted by a catalyst prepared in the state
\rho_{s}
may undergo the following two-stroke process:

\displaystyle\rho_{s}\otimes\rho_{h}\otimes\rho_{c}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{work stroke}}S\!\left(\rho_{s}\otimes\rho_{h}\otimes\rho_{c}\right)\!S^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{heat stroke}}\Tr_{h,c}\!\left[S\!\left(\rho_{s}\otimes\rho_{h}\otimes\rho_{c}\right)\!S^{\dagger}\right]\otimes\rho_{h}\otimes\rho_{c},

(31)

where
S
denotes the global unitary operation implementing the work stroke.
Under the
catalytic condition

\rho_{s}=\Tr_{h,c}\!\left[S\!\left(\rho_{s}\otimes\rho_{h}\otimes\rho_{c}\right)\!S^{\dagger}\right],

(32)

the working system returns to its initial state, thereby completing the thermodynamic cycle.

Inclusion of a catalyst results in an effective enlargement of the Hilbert space without introducing any additional energy (i.e.,
\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}
or
\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}
). Thus, without loss of generality, the catalyst may be introduced as a system with a trivial Hamiltonian. More generally, the catalysis condition (
32
) is defined as a preservation of the probabilities defined via projectors
\Pi_{m}
, namely

\forall m,\quad\Tr[\Pi_{m}(\rho-S\rho S^{\dagger})]=\Tr[\left(\Pi_{m}-S^{\dagger}\Pi_{m}S\right)\rho]=0,

(33)

where
\Pi_{m}
is a projector onto the subspace of the catalytic degrees of freedom. Then, the catalysis condition can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\forall m,\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}\Delta p_{i}=0\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}=\lambda_{u_{i}}^{m}-\lambda_{d_{i}}^{m},

(34)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\lambda_{u_{i}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\bra{u_i}\Pi_{m}\ket{u_i},\quad\lambda_{d_{i}}^{m}=\bra{d_i}\Pi_{m}\ket{d_i},

(35)

with
\Delta p_{i}
introduced in Eq. (
9
).

The analogous condition as Eq. (
33
) for the continuous engine is given by:

\forall m,\quad\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{m}]\rangle=i\langle[V_{0},\Pi_{m}]\rangle=0,

(36)

where we assume that
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{k}[\Pi_{m}]=0
, which follows from the fact that the catalyst’s degrees of freedom are not coupled to the heat baths. Similarly to the previous considerations, for the continuous case, we assume that states
\ket{u_i}
and
\ket{d_i}
are eigenstates of the projectors
\Pi_{m}
, such that

\Pi_{m}\ket{u_i}=\lambda_{u_{i}}^{m}\ket{u_i},\quad\Pi_{m}\ket{d_i}=\lambda_{d_{i}}^{m}\ket{d_i},

(37)

where for all
m
, we have eigenvalues
\lambda_{b}^{m}\in\{0,1\}
. Using the formulation
\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle
from Eq. (
27
), one can rewrite Eq. (
36
) as

\forall m,\quad\sum_{i}\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle=0.

(38)

That results in a
continuous catalysis condition
.

We emphasize that for the discrete engine, the catalysis condition is imposed to complete the cycle for degrees of freedom that do not thermalize. In contrast, for the continuous engine the cyclicity condition in Eq. (
38
) arises naturally from relaxation, provided the system is ergodic. Since the catalyst is not directly coupled to the heat baths, ergodicity must instead be ensured through the interaction
V_{0}
.

Catalytic Otto-like engines.
Within this general framework, we find a one-to-one correspondence between the performance of discrete and continuous engines. Namely, by comparison side by side the expressions for exchanged amounts of heat and heat currents from Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
28
), efficiency from Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
30
), catalysis conditions in Eq. (
34
) and from Eq. (
38
), work and power from Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
29
):

\displaystyle Q_{k}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\Delta p_{i},\quad\quad\quad\;

\displaystyle J_{k}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle,

(39)

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{disc.}}=1+\frac{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\Delta p_{i}}{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\Delta p_{i}},\quad

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{cont.}}=1+\frac{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle}{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle},

(40)

\displaystyle\forall m,\quad\sum_{i}\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}\Delta p_{i}=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall m,\quad\sum_{i}\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle=0,

(41)

\displaystyle W=Q_{h}+Q_{c},\quad\quad\quad\;

\displaystyle P=J_{h}+J_{c},

(42)

and finally with Clausius’ inequality:

\displaystyle\beta_{h}Q_{h}+\beta_{c}Q_{c}\leq 0,\quad\beta_{h}J_{h}+\beta_{c}J_{c}\leq 0,

(43)

it is seen that both models are equivalent by replacing

\Delta p_{i}\leftrightarrow\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle.

(44)

The mapping is then achieved by introducing a set of characteristic times, such that

\displaystyle\tau_{i}=\Delta p_{i}/\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle.

(45)

In the following, we focus on a special class of catalysis where the work stroke is governed by a particular set of permutations, referred to as
simple permutations
, introduced for discrete Otto-like engines (with a catalyst) in
Łobejko et al. 2024
;
Biswas et al. 2024
. It encompasses only those permutations that transfer an equal non-zero amount of probability from one eigenstate of the catalyst to another. Simple permutation is characterized by the unique non-trivial probability flow given by the solution of Eq. (
34
):

\displaystyle\forall\;i,j\quad\Delta p_{i}=\Delta p_{j}\equiv\Delta p.

(46)

The primary reason for focusing on such permutations is that, with a suitable catalyst, one can achieve any catalytic enhancement of efficiency, realizing values between the Otto and Carnot limits
Biswas et al. 2024
. This makes them natural candidates for transferring catalytic advantages to continuous Otto-like engines via the mapping in Eq. (
44
).

Analogous to the catalysis condition derived for discrete Otto-like engines in Eq. (
46
),
an equivalent condition for catalysis under simple permutations arises in the continuous setting.
Specifically, starting from Eq. (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle\forall\,i,j\quad\langle\dot{p}_{i}\rangle=\langle\dot{p}_{j}\rangle\equiv\langle\dot{p}\rangle,

(47)

as the equation describing the catalysis condition in the continuous scenario has an identical form to that in the discrete case (see Eq. (
41
)).

For the class of Otto-like engines defined by Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
), there exists a single characteristic time
\tau
such that
\langle\dot{p}\rangle=\Delta p/\tau
. In the discrete case, quantities such as work and heat are evaluated per cycle, while in the continuous case they are expressed as time rates. Hence,
\tau
represents the cycle duration, linking work per cycle in discrete scenario to power in the continuous scenario via
P=W/\tau
. The corresponding power–efficiency relation for catalytic Otto-like engines is

\displaystyle P=\frac{W}{\tau},\quad\eta=\frac{W}{Q_{h}}=\frac{P}{J_{h}}=1+\frac{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}}{\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}}.

(48)

We find that the proposed class of heat engines generalizes the Otto formula—expressed as a ratio of energy differences—and therefore refer to them as
catalytic Otto-like engines
.

Demonstration of the mapping (from discrete to continuous model).

To illustrate the applicability of our results, we show how the discrete engine can be mapped onto its continuous counterpart. In Refs.
Łobejko et al. 2024
;
Biswas et al. 2024
, we introduced a class of discrete catalytic Otto-like engines that outperform their non-catalytic analogs. In the discrete setting, these engines are defined by the states
\ket{u_i}
and
\ket{d_i}
forming the permutation matrix
S
in Eq. (
5
). The corresponding continuous model is then obtained by constructing the interaction Hamiltonian
V_{t}
in Eq. (
14
) from these states.

We now map the discrete Otto engine and its simplest catalytic extension with a two-dimensional catalyst
Łobejko et al. 2024
;
Biswas et al. 2024
onto the continuous regime (see Appendix A and B for details). In the discrete setting, the efficiencies are

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm Otto}=1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}<\eta_{\rm Catalytic}=1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{2\omega_{h}}.

(49)

Furthermore, Ref.
Łobejko et al. 2024
identified a regime where the catalytic engine outperforms the Otto engine in work output for all efficiencies,

\displaystyle W_{\rm Otto}(\eta)<W_{\rm Catalytic}(\eta),\quad\eta\in[0,1-\beta_{h}/\beta_{c}],

(50)

demonstrating an ultimate catalytic enhancement in both work and efficiency. Based on the general result for catalytic Otto-like engines given by Eq. (
48
), the continuous engine reproduces the same efficiencies (
49
). Furthermore, we proved that in this regime the characteristic times satisfy

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm Otto}\geq\tau_{\rm Catalytic}.

(51)

(See Sec. IV C of the Supplementary Material for detailed proof). Consequently, within the regime where inequality in Eq. (
50
) holds, the correspondence between the discrete and continuous engines extends from work to power, yielding

\displaystyle P_{\rm Otto}(\eta)<P_{\rm Catalytic}(\eta).

(52)

This demonstrates how the relatively simple design of the discrete engine can be mapped onto its more experimentally accessible continuous version. The resulting power–efficiency trade-off is presented in Fig.
2
.

Discussion:

We establish a mapping between discrete Otto and catalytic Otto-like engines with their continuous counterparts, illustrated for the Otto engine and an Otto-like engine with a two-dimensional catalyst. The key element is the probability flow
\Delta p
in the discrete engine, corresponding to the probability current
\langle\dot{p}\rangle
in the continuous case. Notably, for a certain class, the efficiency remains unchanged under this mapping, so any catalytic enhancement in the discrete engine carries over directly. Moreover, all thermodynamically relevant quantities in the discrete setting have analogs in the continuous case, preserving the overall thermodynamic structure.

Our results open several promising directions for future research. An important one is the
experimental demonstration
of the catalytic advantage on platforms such as trapped ions
Roßnagel et al. 2016
and superconducting qubits
Aamir et al. 2025
. Another is extending the mapping to a
general discrete Otto engine
with a
d
-dimensional catalyst operating at efficiency
1-(n\omega_{c}/d\omega_{h})
,
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, and its continuous analog
Biswas et al. 2024
. It would also be valuable to quantify the cost of catalytic enhancement in terms of cycle duration and control complexity, following the thermodynamic cost analysis in
Taranto et al. 2023
. Finally, exploring how quantum resources such as entanglement and coherence transform under this mapping could clarify their role in enhancing the performance of thermal machines
Levy and Gelbwaser-Klimovsky 2018
;
Latune et al. 2021
;
Manzano et al. 2019
;
Biswas et al. 2025
.

Appendix A: Example 1- Otto engines (without a catalyst)

Discrete Otto engine: The initial density matrix describing the engine is taken as a product of the Gibbs states of the hot and cold systems,

\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta_{h}H_{0,h}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta_{h}H_{0,h}}]}\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta_{c}H_{0,c}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta_{c}H_{0,c}}]},

(53)

where each subsystem is modeled as a two-level system with Hamiltonian

H_{0,k}=\omega_{k}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{k},\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(54)

We consider a single swap operation
S
defined by

\ket{u}:=\ket{10}_{h,c}\xleftrightarrow{S}\ket{01}_{h,c}=:\ket{d},

(55)

(56)

Q_{k}
from the definition given in Eq. (
39

\displaystyle\Delta p

\displaystyle=p_{u}-p_{d}=\bra{u}\rho\ket{u}-\bra{d}\rho\ket{d},=\frac{\left(e^{-\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}-e^{-\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}\right)}{(1+e^{-\beta_{h}\omega_{h}})(1+e^{-\beta_{c}\omega_{c}})},

(57)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{h}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{h}_{u}-\varepsilon^{h}_{d}=\left(\langle u|H_{0,h}|u\rangle-\langle d|H_{0,h}|d\rangle\right)=\omega_{h},

(58)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{c}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{c}_{u}-\varepsilon^{c}_{d}=\left(\langle u|H_{0,c}|u\rangle-\langle d|H_{0,c}|d\rangle\right)=-\omega_{c}.

(59)

By substituting Eqs. (
57
), (
58
), and (
59

Q_{k}
given in Eq. (

(60)

Finally, we can calculate the efficiency from Eq. (
40
) as

\eta=\frac{W}{Q_{h}}=1+\frac{Q_{c}}{Q_{h}}=1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}.

(61)

The detailed calculation is given in the Sec. III A of the Supplementary material.

Continuous Otto engine: The dynamical model of the continuous Otto engine is described by the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=H_{0,h}+H_{0,c}=\omega_{h}\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{h}+\omega_{c}\sigma_{-}^{c}\sigma_{+}^{c}

\displaystyle V_{t}

\displaystyle=g(\outerproduct{u}{d}e^{-i\Omega t}+\text{H.C.})=g\left(\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{c}e^{-i\Omega t}+\text{H.C.}\right)

\ket{u}=\ket{10}
and
\ket{d}=\ket{01}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\rho]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[V_{0},\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{h}[\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{c}[\rho_{I}(t)],\quad

(64)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad V_{0}=g\Big(\outerproduct{u}{d}+\text{H.C.}\Big).

\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i,j}]\rangle=0

is the projector on the eigenstate of

given in Eq. (
53

heat bath that obeys the detailed balance condition
\gamma^{k}_{+}/\gamma^{k}_{-}=e^{-\beta_{k}\omega_{k}}.
The detailed calculation of
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
has been presented in Sec. III B of the supplementary material.

Substituting the value of

from Eq. (

\displaystyle J_{h}

\displaystyle=\omega_{h}\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}\Delta p}{(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right),

(67)

\displaystyle J_{c}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}\Delta p}{(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right),

(68)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=J_{h}+J_{c}=\left(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c}\right)\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}\Delta p}{(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right).

\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{h}[H_{0,c}]=\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{c}[H_{0,h}]=0.

(112)

Generally, the local model of the dissipator is justified whenever
\|H_{0}\|\gg g_{i}
.

The energy conservation with power
P(t)
and heat current
J_{k}(t)
is generally given by:

\frac{d}{dt}\Tr[(H_{0}+V_{t})\rho_{S}(t)]=-P(t)+J_{h}(t)+J_{c}(t).

(113)

where

\displaystyle P(t)=-\Tr[\dot{V}_{t}\rho_{S}(t)],\quad J_{k}(t)=\Tr[\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[H_{0}+V_{t}]\rho_{S}(t)],

(114)

with
\dot{V}_{t}=-i\sum_{i}g_{i}\Omega_{i}\left(\outerproduct{u_{i}}{d_{i}}e^{-i\Omega_{i}t}-h.c.\right)
. By introducing an interaction picture with a unitary transformation
\rho_{I}(t)=e^{-iH_{0}t}\rho_{S}(t)e^{iH_{0}t}
, we get

\frac{d}{dt}{\rho_{I}(t)}=-i[V_{0},\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{h}[\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{c}[\rho_{I}(t)]:=\mathcal{L}[\rho_{I}(t)].

(115)

and

\displaystyle P(t)=-\Tr[\dot{V}_{0}\rho_{I}(t)],\quad J_{k}(t)=\Tr[\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[H_{0,k}+V_{0}]\rho_{I}(t)].

(116)

Appendix C
Discrete and continuous Otto-like engine (without catalyst)

C.1
Discrete Otto-like engine

The initial density matrix is a product of hot and cold system’s Gibbs states, i.e.,

\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{0,h}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{0,h}}]}\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta H_{0,c}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{0,c}}]},

(117)

with a simplest two-level system Hamiltonian:

H_{0,k}=\omega_{k}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{k},\quad\text{with}\quad k\in\{h,c\},

(118)

such that

\rho=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\Big(\outerproduct{00}{00}+a_{h}\outerproduct{10}{10}+a_{c}\outerproduct{01}{01}+a_{h}a_{c}\outerproduct{11}{11}\Big),

(119)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{k}=1+a_{k}
and
a_{k}=e^{-\beta_{k}\omega_{k}}
as introduced in Eq. (
92
)). Then, we consider a single swap
S
such that

\ket{u}:=\ket{10}\xleftrightarrow{S}\ket{01}=:\ket{d},

(120)

namely

\displaystyle S\ket{01}=\ket{10},\quad S\ket{10}=\ket{01},

(121)

\displaystyle S\ket{11}=\ket{11},\quad S\ket{00}=\ket{00}.

(122)

Recall that from Table
1
, the transferred amount of heat
Q_{h}
and
Q_{c}
given as:

Q_{k}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\Delta p_{i},

(123)

where

\displaystyle\Delta p_{i}=p_{u_{i}}-p_{d_{i}},\quad\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{k}=\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}-\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k},

(124)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{u_{i}}^{k}=\bra{u_i}H_{0,k}\ket{u_i},\quad\varepsilon_{d_{i}}^{k}=\bra{d_i}H_{0,k}\ket{d_i}.

(125)

Therefore, for the state
\rho
given in Eq. (
119
) we can identify

p_{u}=\langle u|\rho|u\rangle=\frac{a_{h}}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}\quad;\quad p_{d}=\langle d|\rho|d\rangle=\frac{a_{c}}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})},

(126)

that allows us to calculate

\Delta p=p_{u}-p_{d}=\frac{(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}.

(127)

Next, we calculate

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{h}_{u}-\varepsilon^{h}_{d}=\left(\langle u|H_{0,h}|u\rangle-\langle d|H_{0,h}|d\rangle\right)=\left(\langle 10|H_{0,h}|10\rangle-\langle 01|H_{0,h}|01\rangle\right)=\omega_{h},

(128)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{c}_{u}-\varepsilon^{c}_{d}=\left(\langle u|H_{0,c}|u\rangle-\langle d|H_{0,c}|d\rangle\right)=\left(\langle 10|H_{0,c}|10\rangle-\langle 01|H_{0,c}|01\rangle\right)=-\omega_{c}.

(129)

Substituting the obtained expression of
\Delta p
,
\Delta\varepsilon^{h}
and
\Delta\varepsilon^{c}
from Eq. (
127
) and Eq. (
179
), we can calculate
Q_{h}
and
Q_{c}
from Eq. (
123
) as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{h}\Delta p=\frac{\omega_{h}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}

\displaystyle Q_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon^{c}\Delta p=\frac{-\omega_{c}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}.

(130)

Finally, the work and efficiency of the engine are given by the standard Otto formula. Substituting
Q_{c}
and
Q_{h}
from Eq. (
C.1
) into the definitions of work and efficiency, we obtain

W=Q_{h}+Q_{c}=\left(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c}\right)\Delta p=\frac{(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})},\qquad\eta=1+\frac{Q_{c}}{Q_{h}}=1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}.

(131)

C.2
Continuous Otto engine

The dynamical model of the continuous Otto engine is described by the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=H_{0,h}+H_{0,c}=\omega_{h}\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{h}+\omega_{c}\sigma_{-}^{c}\sigma_{+}^{c}

(132)

\displaystyle V_{t}

\displaystyle=g(\outerproduct{u}{d}e^{-i\Omega t}+\text{Herm. Conj.})=g\left(\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{c}e^{-i\Omega t}+\text{Herm. Conj.}\right)

(133)

such that
\ket{u}=\ket{10}
and
\ket{d}=\ket{01}
as stated in Eq. (
120
).
From the main text, we recall that the time evolution of the engine in the interaction picture is governed by the local master equation:

\mathcal{L}[\rho]:=\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[V_{0},\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{h}[\rho_{I}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{c}[\rho_{I}(t)]\quad\text{where}\quad V_{0}=g\Big(\outerproduct{u}{d}+\outerproduct{d}{u}\Big).

(134)

The dissipator of the master equation is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}[\rho]=\gamma^{k}_{+}\left(\sigma^{k}_{-}\rho\sigma^{k}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma^{k}_{+}\sigma^{k}_{-},\rho\right\}\right)+\gamma^{k}_{-}\left(\sigma^{k}_{+}\rho\sigma^{k}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma^{k}_{-}\sigma^{k}_{+},\rho\right\}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(135)

Equivalently, the local master equation governing the evolution of an observable
\mathcal{O}_{I}
in the interaction picture follows from the Heisenberg equation of motion:

\frac{d\mathcal{O}_{I}}{dt}=i[V_{0},\mathcal{O}_{I}]+\mathcal{D}_{h}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O}_{I})+\mathcal{D}_{c}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O}_{I}):=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{O}_{I}],

(136)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{k}(\mathcal{O}_{I})=\gamma_{+}^{k}\left(\sigma_{+}^{k}\mathcal{O}_{I}\sigma_{-}^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{+}^{k}\sigma_{-}^{k},\mathcal{O}_{I}\right\}\right)+\gamma^{k}_{-}\left(\sigma_{-}^{k}\mathcal{O}\sigma^{k}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{-}^{k}\sigma_{+}^{k},\mathcal{O}_{I}\right\}\right),

(137)

with
k\in\{h,c\}
. Within this model, we have

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]=-\Gamma_{k}V_{0}\quad\text{where}\quad\Gamma_{k}=(\gamma^{k}_{+}+\gamma^{k}_{-})/2\quad\text{with}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(138)

The expectation value of

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]
with respect to the stationary state

\rho^{s}_{I}
(where stationarity means
\mathcal{L}[\rho^{s}_{I}]=0
) can be expressed as

\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\rangle=\Tr\left(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[V_{0}]\,\rho^{s}_{I}\right)=\Tr\left(V_{0}\,\mathcal{L}[\rho^{s}_{I}]\right)=-(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\langle V_{0}\rangle=0,

(139)

where in the last step we used Eq. (
138
) and the fact that
[H_{0},V_{0}]=0
. In this particular scenario, the definitions of the stationary heat currents
J_{h}
and
J_{c}
, given in Table
1
, reduce to

\displaystyle J_{h}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}\langle\dot{N}\rangle=\Delta\varepsilon^{h}\langle\dot{N}\rangle=\omega_{h}\langle\dot{N}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad J_{c}=\sum_{i}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\langle\dot{N}\rangle=\Delta\varepsilon^{c}\langle\dot{N}\rangle=-\omega_{c}\langle\dot{N}\rangle,

(140)

where we substitute the value
\Delta\varepsilon^{h}
and
\Delta\varepsilon^{c}
from Eq. (
128
) and Eq. (
129
). From
J_{h}
and
J_{c}
, we can calculate power as

P=J_{h}+J_{c}=\left(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c}\right)\langle\dot{N}\rangle,

(141)

as given in Table
1
. It is straightforward to see that the efficiency is given by

\eta=\frac{P}{J_{h}}=1+\frac{J_{c}}{J_{h}}=1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}.

(142)

Next, we calculate the stationary values of the heat currents and the power of the engine by evaluating
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
. Let us introduce the operator

\dot{N}:=ig\left(\outerproduct{u}{d}-\outerproduct{d}{u}\right)=ig\left(\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{c}-\sigma_{+}^{h}\sigma_{-}^{c}\right),

(143)

whose expectation value in the stationary state
\rho^{s}_{I}
yields
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
i.e.,

\langle\dot{N}\rangle=ig\Tr[(\sigma_{-}^{h}\sigma_{+}^{c}-\text{Herm. Conj.})\rho_{I}^{s}].

(144)

The value of
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
can be obtained by evaluating the expectation value of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]
with respect to the stationary state
\rho^{s}_{I}
, starting from Eq. (
136
):

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]\rangle

\displaystyle=i\langle[V_{0},\dot{N}]\rangle+\langle\mathcal{D}_{h}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]\rangle+\langle\mathcal{D}_{c}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]\rangle

\displaystyle=2g^{2}(p_{10}-p_{01})-(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\langle\dot{N}\rangle=0,

(145)

where we have used the fact that the expectation value of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]
with respect to the stationary state
\rho^{s}_{I}
vanishes, i.e.,
\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\dot{N}]\rangle=0
. Here, the stationary value of occupation probabilities are defined as

\displaystyle p_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Tr[\outerproduct{ij}{ij}\,\rho_{I}^{s}]\equiv\langle\Pi_{i,j}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad\Pi_{i,j}=\outerproduct{ij}{ij}.

(146)

Then, a closed set of equations can be obtained by imposing the stationary conditions
\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i,j}]\rangle=0
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{0,0}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-(\gamma^{h}_{+}+\gamma^{c}_{+})p_{00}+\gamma^{h}_{-}p_{10}+\gamma^{c}_{-}p_{01}=0,

(147)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{1,1}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-(\gamma^{h}_{-}+\gamma^{c}_{-})p_{11}+\gamma^{h}_{+}p_{01}+\gamma^{c}_{+}p_{10}=0,

(148)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{0,1}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-(\gamma^{h}_{+}+\gamma^{c}_{-})p_{01}+\gamma^{h}_{-}p_{11}+\gamma^{c}_{+}p_{00}+\langle\dot{N}\rangle=0,

(149)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\Pi_{1,0}]\rangle

\displaystyle=-(\gamma^{h}_{-}+\gamma^{c}_{+})p_{10}+\gamma^{c}_{-}p_{11}+\gamma^{h}_{+}p_{00}-\langle\dot{N}\rangle=0,

(150)

which, upon solving, yields

\langle\dot{N}\rangle=\frac{\gamma^{c}_{-}\Gamma_{h}-\gamma^{h}_{-}\Gamma_{c}}{(\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}}{g^{2}}\right)}.

(151)

For a thermal bath satisfying the detailed balance condition
\gamma_{+}^{k}/\gamma_{-}^{k}=e^{-\beta_{k}\omega_{k}}
with
k\in\{h,c\}
, this can be rewritten as:

\langle\dot{N}\rangle=\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})}\frac{\Delta p}{\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}=\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)},

(152)

where
\Delta p
is the change in probability for the discrete version of this engine as given in Eq. (
127
). Substituting the value of
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
from Eq. (
152
) to Eq. (
140
) and Eq. (
141
) we have

\displaystyle J_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{h}\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right),

(153)

\displaystyle J_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\omega_{c}\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right),

(154)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{h}+J_{c}=\left(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c}\right)\left(\frac{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}(a_{h}-a_{c})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})(\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c})\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)}\right).

(155)

C.3
Calculation of characteristics time

We compute the characteristic time of the engine by taking the ratio of the work per cycle for the discrete Otto-like engine [Eq. (
131
)] to the power of the continuous Otto-like engine [Eq. (
155
)]. Specifically,

\displaystyle P=\frac{W}{\tau_{\rm Otto}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\tau_{\rm Otto}=\frac{W}{P}=\frac{\Delta p}{\langle\dot{N}\rangle},

(156)

where
\Delta p
denotes the change in probability for the discrete engine [Eq. (
127
)] and
\langle\dot{N}\rangle
represents the probability current [Eq. (
152
)]. Substituting these expressions yields

\tau_{\rm Otto}=\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}{g^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma_{h}+\Gamma_{c}}{2\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{c}}\right).

(157)

We can express the characteristic time
\tau_{\rm Otto}
using the relaxation time:

\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}=\frac{2}{\gamma^{k}_{+}+\gamma^{k}_{-}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{k}},\quad\text{where}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(158)

That gives:

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm Otto}=\frac{\tau_{h}^{\rm eq}+\tau_{c}^{\rm eq}}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{g^{2}\tau_{h}^{\rm eq}\tau_{c}^{\rm eq}}\right).

(159)

Appendix D
Discrete and continuous Otto-like engine with qubit catalyst

D.1
Discrete Otto-like engine with qubit catalyst

Let us now analyze the discrete Otto like engine with a qubit catalyst. The initial density matrix is given by the product of state of the catalyst, the hot and the cold system’s Gibbs states, i.e.,

\rho=\rho_{s}\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta H_{0,h}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{0,h}}]}\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta H_{0,c}}}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{0,c}}]},

(160)

with a two-level system Hamiltonian:

H_{0,k}=\omega_{k}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{k},\quad\text{with}\quad k\in\{h,c\},

(161)

and the state of the catalyst

\rho_{s}=p\outerproduct{1}{1}+(1-p)\outerproduct{2}{2}.

(162)

Therefore, the combined state
\rho
can be written as

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle\frac{p}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\Big(\outerproduct{100}{100}+a_{h}\outerproduct{110}{110}+a_{c}\outerproduct{101}{101}+a_{h}a_{c}\outerproduct{111}{111}\Big)

(163)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{(1-p)}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\Big(\outerproduct{200}{200}+a_{h}\outerproduct{210}{210}+a_{c}\outerproduct{201}{201}+a_{h}a_{c}\outerproduct{211}{211}\Big).

where
\mathcal{Z}_{k}=1+a_{k}
and
a_{k}=e^{-\beta_{k}\omega_{k}}
as introduced in Eq. (
92
)). As argued in the main text, since the Hamiltonian of the catalyst does not affect the relevant thermodynamic quantities such as heat and work, we take the catalyst Hamiltonian to be trivial.

Now, consider a permutation operator
S
, composed of two swap operations acting on disjoint subspaces of the vector space on which
\rho
is supported. It is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{u_1}=\ket{200}\xleftrightarrow{S}\ket{110}=\ket{d_1}

(164)

\displaystyle\ket{u_2}=\ket{101}\xleftrightarrow{S}\ket{210}=\ket{d_2},

(165)

and acts as an identity on the rest of the basis vectors. The transferred probabilities
\Delta p_{1}
and
\Delta p_{2}
can be evaluated from their definitions, introduced in the main text, as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta p_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{u_{1}}-p_{d_{1}}=\langle u_{1}|\rho|u_{1}\rangle-\langle d_{1}|\rho|d_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[(1-p)-pa_{h}\right],

(166)

\displaystyle\Delta p_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{u_{2}}-p_{d_{2}}=\langle u_{2}|\rho|u_{2}\rangle-\langle d_{2}|\rho|d_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[pa_{c}-(1-p)a_{h}\right].

(167)

Next, we would like to obtain the value of
p
from Eq. (
166
) and Eq. (
167
) employing the catalysis condition. Recall that the general form of the catalysis condition, as described in the main text, is given by (See also table
1
):

\forall m,\quad\sum_{i}\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}\Delta p_{i}=0,

(168)

where
\Delta\lambda_{i}^{m}=\lambda_{u_{i}}^{m}-\lambda_{d_{i}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{u_{i}}^{m}=\bra{u_i}\Pi_{m}\ket{u_i},\quad\lambda_{d_{i}}^{m}=\bra{d_i}\Pi_{m}\ket{d_i},

(169)

and
\Pi_{m}
denotes the
m^{\text{th}}
eigenprojector of the catalyst.

In this example, the catalyst is a qubit, and therefore
m=1,2
. Inspecting the eigendecomposition of
\rho
in Eq. (
163
), the projectors onto the first and second eigenspaces of the catalyst can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\outerproduct{100}{100}+\outerproduct{101}{101}+\outerproduct{110}{110}+\outerproduct{111}{111}

(170)

\displaystyle\Pi_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\outerproduct{200}{200}+\outerproduct{201}{201}+\outerproduct{210}{210}+\outerproduct{211}{211}.

(171)

This will allow us to calculate

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda^{1}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{1}_{u_{1}}-\lambda^{1}_{d_{1}}=\langle u_{1}|\Pi_{1}|u_{1}\rangle-\langle d_{1}|\Pi_{1}|d_{1}\rangle=-1\quad;\quad\Delta\lambda^{1}_{2}=\lambda^{1}_{u_{2}}-\lambda^{1}_{d_{2}}=\langle u_{2}|\Pi_{1}|u_{2}\rangle-\langle d_{2}|\Pi_{1}|d_{2}\rangle=1

(172)

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{u_{1}}-\lambda^{2}_{d_{1}}=\langle u_{1}|\Pi_{2}|u_{1}\rangle-\langle d_{1}|\Pi_{2}|d_{1}\rangle=1\quad;\quad\Delta\lambda^{2}_{2}=\lambda^{2}_{u_{2}}-\lambda^{2}_{d_{2}}=\langle u_{2}|\Pi_{2}|u_{2}\rangle-\langle d_{2}|\Pi_{2}|d_{2}\rangle=-1.

(173)

Using Eq. (
166
), Eq. (
167
), Eq. (
173
), and Eq. (
172
), the catalysis condition in Eq. (
168
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda^{1}_{1}\Delta p_{1}+\Delta\lambda^{1}_{2}\Delta p_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta p_{1}+\Delta p_{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{2},

(174)

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda^{2}_{1}\Delta p_{1}+\Delta\lambda^{2}_{2}\Delta p_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta p_{1}-\Delta p_{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{2}.

(175)

Note that the above Eq. (
174
) and Eq. (
175
) are equivalent. Thus, either equation can be used to determine the value of
p
. Using the explicit form of
\Delta p_{1}
and
\Delta p_{2}
from Eq. (
166
) and Eq. (
167
), we can solve Eq. (
174
) and obtain
p
as follows:

\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[(1-p)-pa_{h}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[pa_{c}-(1-p)a_{h}\right]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac{1+a_{h}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}.

(176)

Substituting the obtained expression of
p
from Eq. (
176
) into Eq. (
166
), gives

\Delta p_{1}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[\frac{(a_{h}+a_{c})}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}-\frac{(1+a_{h})a_{h}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[\frac{a_{c}-a_{h}^{2}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right]=\frac{1}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}\left[\frac{a_{c}-a_{h}^{2}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right]=\Delta p_{2},

(177)

where to write the final equality, we use the fact
\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{2}
from Eq. (
174
).
Let us denote

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{h}\mathcal{Z}_{c}}\left[\frac{a_{c}-a_{h}^{2}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right]=\frac{1}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}\left[\frac{a_{c}-a_{h}^{2}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right]:=\Delta p.

(178)

Employing the expression for
\Delta p
from Eq. (
178
), we calculate the heats
Q_{h}
and
Q_{c}
using their definition given in Table
1
. In order to do so we first compute

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{1}^{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{u_{1}}^{h}-\varepsilon_{d_{1}}^{h}\right)=\Big(\langle u_{1}|H_{0,h}|u_{1}\rangle-\langle d_{1}|H_{0,h}|d_{1}\rangle\Big)=\Big(\langle 200|H_{0,h}|200\rangle-\langle 110|H_{0,h}|210\rangle\Big)=-\omega_{h},

(179)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{2}^{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{u_{2}}^{h}-\varepsilon_{d_{2}}^{h}\right)=\Big(\langle u_{2}|H_{0,h}|u_{2}\rangle-\langle d_{2}|H_{0,h}|d_{2}\rangle\Big)=\Big(\langle 101|H_{0,h}|101\rangle-\langle 210|H_{0,h}|210\rangle\Big)=-\omega_{h},

(180)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{1}^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{u_{1}}^{c}-\varepsilon_{d_{1}}^{c}\right)=\Big(\langle u_{1}|H_{0,c}|u_{1}\rangle-\langle d_{1}|H_{0,c}|d_{1}\rangle\Big)=\Big(\langle 200|H_{0,c}|200\rangle-\langle 110|H_{0,c}|210\rangle\Big)=0,

(181)

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{2}^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{u_{2}}^{c}-\varepsilon_{d_{2}}^{c}\right)=\Big(\langle u_{2}|H_{0,c}|u_{2}\rangle-\langle d_{2}|H_{0,c}|d_{2}\rangle\Big)=\Big(\langle 101|H_{0,c}|101\rangle-\langle 210|H_{0,c}|210\rangle\Big)=\omega_{c}.

(182)

Using the fact
\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{2}=\Delta p
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{h}\Delta p_{i}=-\omega_{h}\Delta p_{1}-\omega_{h}\Delta p_{2}=-\omega_{h}\Delta p-\omega_{h}\Delta p=-2\omega_{h}\Delta p=\frac{2\omega_{h}}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}\left[\frac{a_{h}^{2}-a_{c}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right],

(183)

\displaystyle Q_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Delta\varepsilon_{i}^{c}\Delta p_{i}=\omega_{c}\Delta p_{2}=\omega_{c}\Delta p=\frac{-\omega_{c}}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c})}\left[\frac{a_{h}^{2}-a_{c}}{1+2a_{h}+a_{c}}\right].

(184)

Finally, the work and efficiency of the engine are obtained by substituting
Q_{h}
and
Q_{c}
from Eqs. (
183
) and (

D.3
Calculation of characteristics time

In this section, we determine the characteristic time of the engine by taking the ratio between the work per cycle of the discrete Otto-like engine with a qubit catalyst (see Eq. (
185
)) and the power output of the continuous Otto-like engine with a qubit catalyst (See Eq. (
258
)) i.e.,

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm Catalytic}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W/P=\Delta p/\langle\dot{N}\rangle

(259)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{a_{c}+a_{h}}{1+a_{c}+2a_{h}}\right)\left(\alpha_{2}+\frac{A}{4g^{2}}\right)+\left(\frac{1+a_{h}}{1+a_{c}+2a_{h}}\right)\left(\phi_{1}+\frac{B}{4g^{2}}\right)+\left(\frac{\left(a_{h}^{2}-a_{c}\right)(\alpha_{1}+\phi_{1}+\xi_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\phi_{2}+\xi_{2})}{(1+a_{c})(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c}+2a_{h})}\right)\right].

Now we will write the characteristic time in terms of relaxation time with respect to hot and cold bath denoted as
\tau_{h}^{\rm eq}
and
\tau_{c}^{\rm eq}
where

\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}=\frac{2}{\gamma^{k}_{+}+\gamma^{k}_{-}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{k}},\quad\text{where}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(260)

Using the above Eq. (
260
) we can obtain

\gamma^{k}_{+}+\gamma^{k}_{-}=\frac{2}{\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}}\Longleftrightarrow\gamma^{k}_{-}\left(1+\underbrace{\frac{\gamma^{k}_{+}}{\gamma^{k}_{-}}}_{=a_{k}}\right)=\gamma^{k}_{-}(1+a_{k})=\frac{2}{\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}}\Longleftrightarrow\gamma^{k}_{-}=\frac{2}{\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}(1+a_{k})}\quad\text{where}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(261)

Employing the fact
\gamma^{k}_{+}/\gamma^{k}_{-}=e^{-\beta_{k}\omega_{k}}=a_{k}
, we can obtain from Eq. (
261
)

\gamma^{k}_{+}=\frac{2a_{k}}{\tau_{k}^{\rm eq}(1+a_{k})}\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\{h,c\}.

(262)

Substituting the expression of
\gamma^{k}_{-}
and
\gamma^{k}_{+}
from Eq. (
261
) and Eq. (
262
), we can simplify
\tau_{\rm Catalytic}
as

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm Catalytic}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{1+ah(1+a_{c}+a_{h})}{(1+a_{h})(1+a_{c}+2a_{h})}\right)\left(\frac{(\tau^{\rm eq}_{c})^{2}}{\tau^{\rm eq}_{c}+\tau^{\rm eq}_{h}}\right)+\left(\frac{1+3a_{h}+a_{c}(3+a_{h})}{(1+a_{c})(1+a_{c}+2a_{h})}\right)\left(\frac{(\tau^{\rm eq}_{h})^{2}}{\tau^{\rm eq}_{c}+\tau^{\rm eq}_{h}}\right)+\left(\frac{2\tau^{\rm eq}_{h}\tau^{\rm eq}_{c}}{\tau^{\rm eq}_{c}+\tau^{\rm eq}_{h}}\right)\right]
