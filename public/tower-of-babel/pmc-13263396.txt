Homozygous TFR2 (c.2093_2096del) Mutation in an Asymptomatic Patient With Type 3 Hereditary Hemochromatosis, First Report From Saudi Arabia
Authors: Omar M. Raslan, Dana S. Alamoudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHereditary hemochromatosis (HH) is an inherited disorder of iron metabolism characterized by progressive iron accumulation in multiple organs. While most cases are associated with HFE mutations, non‐HFE variants such as mutations in the transferrin receptor‐2 (TFR2) gene represent rare causes of iron overload. We report a 37‐year‐old Saudi male who was incidentally found to have markedly elevated ferritin levels during routine laboratory testing. The patient was asymptomatic and was incidentally found to have significant hyperferretinemia through routine lab evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated diffuse hepatic iron deposition. Furthermore, Whole exome sequencing identified a homozygous TFR2 c.2093_2096del frameshift mutation. Using these results to reach a diagnosis, the patient was treated with frequent therapeutic phlebotomies, leading to a reduction of ferritin levels. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of homozygous TFR2 c.2093_2096del mutation‐associated hereditary hemochromatosis in Saudi Arabia. This case sheds light on the importance of considering non‐HFE HH in patients with unexplained hyperferritinemia as early diagnosis ensures the prevention of long‐term complications.

Introduction

Hereditary hemochromatosis (HH) is an inherited disorder of iron metabolism characterized by abnormal iron overload leading to iron deposition into various organ tissues [1]. Excess iron promotes oxidative stress and cellular injury through mechanisms such as the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ferroptosis, which contribute to progressive tissue damage [2]. Due to the slow progression of iron accumulation, early stages are asymptomatic and are only manifested with abnormally elevated iron studies [3, 4]. The hallmark “triad” of HH consists of liver cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, and copper skin pigmentation [3, 4].

There are currently four recognized types of HH depending on the mutated gene [5]. Type 1, which involves the Hemostatic Iron Regulator (HFE) gene, is considered to be the classic form of HH as it accounts for up to 95% of its cases [1, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Type 2, also known as juvenile hemochromatosis, is related to mutations in either the Hemojuvelin (HJV) gene or the Hepcidin Antimicrobial Peptide (HAMP) gene [9]. Type 3 is related to the Transferrin‐Receptor (TFR2) gene [1, 6]. Type 4, involving the SCL40A1, is the only type to have an autosomal dominant pattern. It has a variable age onset ranging from 10 to 80 [1]. Hepcidin acts as the principal negative regulator of systemic iron homeostasis by inhibiting intestinal iron absorption and iron release from macrophages through degradation of the ferroportin transporter [3]. HH types 1, 2, and 3 increase intestinal iron absorption by lowering hepcidin levels, a negative regulator of iron homeostasis. In contrast, type 4 HH (also known as ferroportin disease), results in iron accumulation either from hepcidin resistance (hepatic type) or loss of ferroportin export function (macrophage type) [3, 10, 11].

While TFR2‐related HH mainly affects Caucasian populations, it is very rare with less than 50 cases reported in the literature and prevalence of one in 1,000,000 [12]. Although cases of type 3 HH have been reported in Asia, there is a significant lack of epidemiological data regarding this disorder in Saudi Arabia [12].

We report the first documented case of homozygous TFR2 c.2093_2096del mutation‐related hereditary hemochromatosis in Saudi Arabia, augmenting the geographic and biologic spectrum of this rare disease entity.

Case History/Examination

A 37‐year‐old Saudi male patient presented to the outpatient clinic with elevated ferritin levels after undergoing routine community‐based laboratory testing. The patient was asymptomatic; he did not have any inflammatory or gastrointestinal symptoms. He works as a commercial airline pilot. He smokes occasionally but does not consume alcohol. His physical exam was unremarkable. He has no known family history of hematological diseases or other hereditary conditions; his parents are maternal cousins, and all his first‐degree relatives' ferritin levels were tested, all of which were within reference ranges. In terms of his medical background and comorbidities, he has chronic hepatitis B infection that is well controlled with Entecavir, as well as dyslipidemia controlled on statin therapy.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The patient's investigations revealed markedly elevated ferritin levels at 2346.87 ng/mL, his transferrin saturation was > 50%. Liver enzymes were within normal range except for aspartate aminotransferase enzyme (AST), which was shown to be elevated (Table 1). Secondary causes such as inflammation, hemoglobinopathy, and liver cirrhosis were ruled out (normal CRP, normal hemoglobin electrophoresis, normal liver echotexture on abdominal US, and normal liver function tests). To assess for iron overload, a multiplanar enhanced MRI of the upper abdomen with pre‐ and postcontrast dynamic imaging of the liver was done, which demonstrated homogeneous diffuse T2 signal hypointensity, suggesting iron deposition and overload. Echocardiography was unremarkable. Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) was then ordered and sent out. Targeted next‐generation sequencing with enrichment of exonic regions and exon‐intron boundaries (±15 nucleotides) was performed. The obtained nucleotide sequences were aligned to the hg19 reference genome, and variants were analyzed and classified following ACMG guidelines. The results showed a homozygous mutation, c.2093_2096del, in the TFR2 gene. This variant involves the deletion of 4 nucleotides, leading to a change in the amino acid sequence beginning at codon 698 through a frameshift (p.Arg698fs). This is expected to result in the absence or decreased expression of the corresponding protein. Hormonal profile was also sent to assess for any secondary iron‐related endocrinopathies and was all normal. Based on these results, the patient was started on a weekly phlebotomy (500 cc) without chelating therapy.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Throughout the course of his treatment, ferritin levels were gradually decreasing as shown in Figure 1, indicating improvement and responsiveness to therapy. His ferritin levels have steadily declined, reaching near target levels (< 50 ng/mL) after 15 phlebotomies (over 1 year). His liver enzymes have normalized, and he remains clinically stable.

Discussion

Type 3 HH is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic mutations in the TFR2 gene (TFR‐related HH). Clinically, it often resembles type 1 HH, though it may present at an earlier age, similar to juvenile forms [6, 13]. Our patient displayed biochemical iron overload that was detected incidentally despite the absence of symptoms. MRI and genetic testing confirmed TFR2‐related HH. The identified c.2093_2096del frameshift mutation has been previously implicated as a causative mutation in hemochromatosis [13]. Although this frameshift mutation results in the typical pathogenic mechanism associated with type 3 HH, this genotype remains extremely rare, with only two prior Clinivar submissions, both classified as pathogenic [14]. Moreover, this variant was predicted to be deleterious with a CADD score of 33, well above the established pathogenicity threshold of 20 [15].

Historically, the first well‐characterized TFR2 pathogenic variant was the homozygous Y250X, seen in two Sicilian families [16]. Since then, the mutational and geographic landscape has been broadened considerably, with cases being reported in Africa, Asia, and the Middle East [17, 18, 19, 20]. Additionally, to our knowledge, this is the first case of a homozygous c.2093_2096del mutation documented in Saudi Arabia which further broadens the mutational and geographic spectrum of TFR2‐related HH.

The clinical manifestations of TFR2‐related HH are widely variable. A study in 2015 concluded that type 3 HH patients present with a severe disease phenotype at a younger age [21]. This could help contextualize the lack of symptoms in our patient if not for the phenotypic variability presented in the literature. Clinical expression could range from asymptomatic iron overload to severe disease presentation. For instance, a recent case reported a late onset of TFR2 HH in a Japanese female patient, with a habit of consuming 10 g of alcohol and 5 cigarettes per day, who presented with progressive arthropathy and liver dysfunction [19], and hepatomegaly with cutaneous pigmentation in a Taiwanese patient with chronic hepatitis C and uncontrolled diabetes [20]. Conversely, other cases, such as an asymptomatic Spanish patient with no toxic habits, presented with elevated ferritin (> 1000 ng/mL) and γGT levels, closely resembling the presentation seen in our patient [16]. Another study presented two asymptomatic Italian siblings aged 12 and 13, both homozygous for IVS17+5636G>A, who presented with similar elevations in iron levels but variable transferrin saturations [22]. The heterogeneity between the cases reinforces the impact of environmental factors and genotypic variability on penetrance [4].

This case underscores the importance of considering non‐HFE hemochromatosis in Middle Eastern populations presenting with unexplained hyperferritinemia, even when asymptomatic. Nonetheless, while our patient was asymptomatic, he had exhibited biochemical evidence of overload that was responsive to appropriate treatment; indicating that without intervention, progressive tissue iron deposition would be expected to result in long‐term end‐organ complications.

This case has several limitations. Family cascade screening was not performed as family members were not conveniently accessible and informed consent was not provided to proceed. In addition, comprehensive genetic testing is not routinely covered financially in our setting, which further limited the feasibility of the screening. Therefore, inheritance and penetrance within the family pedigree were not clarified. Nonetheless, cascade genetic testing of first‐degree relatives remains clinically important in the timely diagnosis and management of HH within the family [23]. Also, liver biopsy was not done as the patient refused to go through the procedure due to its invasive nature. Although liver biopsy remains the gold standard in assessing hepatic iron deposition, less invasive MRI techniques have been validated as a noninvasive quantification method in assessing liver iron overload [24]. In this case, the combination of markedly elevated iron studies, characteristic MRI findings of hepatic iron deposition, and confirmatory genetic testing was considered sufficient to establish the diagnosis and proceed with therapeutic phlebotomy. Finally, the long‐term outcomes and the trajectory of this specific homozygous variant remain unknown, as no prior reports with the same exact homozygous mutation have been longitudinally characterized. Future regional genetic studies may clarify penetrance and genotype–phenotype correlations in Arab populations.

We report a rare homozygous c.2093_2096del mutation in TFR‐related HH, not previously documented in Saudi Arabia. This report helps expand the already growing geographical and mutational spectrum of HH and highlights the variability in its clinical expression. Our findings also emphasize the importance of considering non‐HFE causes of hemochromatosis in patients with unexplained hyperferritenemia and reinforce the significance of timely diagnosis through biochemical and genetic screening followed by appropriate therapeutic phlebotomy to prevent complications such as cirrhosis, diabetes, skin pigmentation, or arthropathy.
