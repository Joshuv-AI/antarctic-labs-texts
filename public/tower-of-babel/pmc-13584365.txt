Letter to the Editor regarding “Functions of dendrobine in a zebrafish model of diabetic retinopathy”
Authors: Jing Chen
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read with interest the study by Zhu et al. evaluating dendrobine (DEN) in a larval zebrafish model of high-glucose-induced diabetic retinopathy (DR) [1]. By combining retinal vascular imaging, histology, behavioral testing, oxidative-stress assays, and transcriptomic analysis, the authors provide useful evidence that DEN attenuates several changes induced by short-term high-glucose exposure. I believe that three issues should be considered when interpreting the therapeutic and mechanistic implications of these findings.

The main issue is the timing of DEN administration. DEN and glucose were given concurrently from 3 to 6 days post-fertilization, and DEN was not introduced after a retinal abnormality had been established [1]. The experiment therefore addresses whether DEN can limit injury while high-glucose stress is being induced, rather than whether it can reverse an existing diabetic retinal lesion. This distinction is clinically relevant. A compound that reduces retinal injury when present from the onset of metabolic stress may not necessarily retain the same efficacy once vascular and neural retinal changes are established. The 130 mmol/L glucose larval model was originally developed as a short-term model of early hyperglycemia-related retinal vascular change [2], and subsequent work has continued to distinguish short-term larval immersion models from longer-duration diabetic models used to study more established retinal complications [3]. The present results therefore support a protective effect of DEN during acute high-glucose exposure, but they do not yet demonstrate therapeutic efficacy against established DR.

A DEN-only group under normoglycemic conditions was also not included. This is relevant because several outcomes interpreted as rescue, including developmental and transcriptomic changes, could also be influenced by direct effects of DEN on normal larval development. A delayed-treatment design would address both concerns more directly. For example, vascular or structural retinal abnormalities could first be documented after glucose exposure, followed by initiation of DEN while hyperglycemic conditions are maintained. Such a design would more closely test the question faced in clinical treatment: whether DEN can improve retinal injury that is already present rather than prevent injury from developing.

A second issue is whether the retinal benefit can be separated from systemic glucose lowering. At 40 mg/L, DEN reduced both retinal vessel enlargement and whole-body glucose levels, and most major outcomes were assessed at the same 6-day time point [1]. The authors suggest that retinal improvement may occur before major systemic metabolic change and may therefore reflect a local retinal effect. However, no serial measurements or glucose-matched comparison were performed to establish this temporal sequence. The authors appropriately acknowledge that systemic metabolic effects could not be separated from direct retinal actions [1]. Retina-specific assays, measurements of retinal DEN exposure, or comparisons between groups with similar systemic glucose levels would help determine whether DEN has a retinal effect independent of glucose lowering.

The transcriptomic findings also warrant a more cautious mechanistic interpretation. RNA sequencing was performed on whole larvae rather than isolated retina, although the enriched pathways were subsequently linked to retinal protection [1]. Whole-organism bulk transcriptomic data cannot identify the tissue or cell type responsible for differential gene expression without additional spatial or cell-resolved information [4]. In addition, neither the main Methods section nor Supplementary Text S1 of the study by Zhu et al. [1] specifies whether the reported transcriptome-wide P values were adjusted for multiple testing. This is important because false-discovery control is a standard consideration when thousands of genes are tested simultaneously [5]. The qRT-PCR experiments support the direction of expression changes in selected genes, but they do not establish that the implicated pathways causally mediate the retinal phenotype. At this stage, these findings appear better suited to identifying candidate pathways than “precise molecular targets.” Retina-specific transcriptomic profiling and functional perturbation of the proposed pathways would provide firmer mechanistic support.

In summary, Zhu et al. provide useful evidence that DEN modifies retinal and systemic responses during acute high-glucose exposure in zebrafish larvae. Determining whether DEN can treat established retinal injury, separating local retinal effects from systemic glucose lowering, and validating the proposed pathways within retinal tissue would better define the translational relevance of these findings.
