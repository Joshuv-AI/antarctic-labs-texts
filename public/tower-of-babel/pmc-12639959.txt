USP13 promotes colorectal cancer progression by deubiquitinating and stabilizing HIF-1α
Authors: Bo Gu, Si-Yu Chang, Ke-Ke Li, Bai-Qi Wang, Shao-Bo Ke, Xiao-Dong Zhang, Run-Lei Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) is a key transcriptional regulator in tumor progression, tightly controlled by ubiquitination-mediated proteasomal degradation. While multiple deubiquitinases (DUBs) stabilize HIF-1α, the role of ubiquitin specific peptidase 13 (USP13) in colorectal cancer (CRC) remained undefined. This study aimed to elucidate whether USP13 regulates HIF-1α stability and contributes to CRC pathogenesis.MethodsUSP13-HIF-1α interaction was assessed via co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), immunofluorescence, and domain mapping. Ubiquitination assays evaluated USP13-mediated HIF-1α deubiquitination using K-specific ubiquitin mutants. Functional impacts were tested in CRC cell lines using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated USP13 knockout and overexpression. In vitro assays included proliferation (CCK-8), colony formation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis (HUVECs tube formation). In vivo relevance was validated in xenograft models, USP13-knockout mice, and an AOM/DSS-induced CRC model. Clinical correlation used IHC staining of CRC patient tissues and TCGA data analysis.ResultsUSP13 directly bound HIF-1α via its C-terminal UBA domain, cleaving K48-linked polyubiquitin chains to stabilize HIF-1α and enhance its transcriptional activity. USP13 knockout reduced HIF-1α levels, suppressed CRC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis in vitro, and attenuated tumor growth in xenografts. Conversely, USP13 overexpression exacerbated these effects. Clinically, USP13 and HIF-1α levels correlated positively in CRC tissues and with Ki-67 expression. USP13 deficiency in mice reduced tumor burden in AOM/DSS models, decreased vascular density (CD31+), and downregulated VEGFA/Ki-67. Rescue experiments confirmed HIF-1α dependency for USP13-mediated oncogenic effects.ConclusionsUSP13 promotes CRC progression by deubiquitinating and stabilizing HIF-1α, driving tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis. This identifies USP13 as a pro-tumorigenic factor and potential therapeutic target in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02514-3.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common cancers and is a leading cause of cancer-associated mortality [1]. Hypoxia is a well-established feature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and substantial evidence confirms its prevalence in CRC [2]. In the adaptation to hypoxia, the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) plays a central regulatory role in driving metabolic reprogramming, a key innate advantage that enables cancer cells to refuel malignant behaviors such as migration and invasion [3–5].

Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) is a crucial transcriptional factor that responds to hypoxia. HIF-1 is a heterodimer composed of HIF-1α with HIF-1β (ARNT) [6]. Under normoxia, HIF-1α undergoes hydroxylation by Prolyl hydroxylase domain-containing proteins (PHDs) [7]. Once hydroxylated, HIF-1α is recognized by the E3 ligase von Hippel-Lindau disease tumor suppressor (VHL) and subsequently degraded through proteosome [8, 9]. Under hypoxic conditions, the activity of PHDs is suppressed, leading to the stabilization of HIF-1α [9, 10]. The HIF-1 heterodimer subsequently translocates into the nucleus and, with the assistance of the cofactor p300, binds to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in target genes, promoting the expression of numerous genes involved in cancer cell metabolism, proliferation, angiogenesis and immune suppression, thereby facilitating hypoxic adaptation [11].

As a pivotal transcriptional factor, HIF-1α protein stability, localization, and transcription activity was fine-tuned sophisticatedly through a variety of reversible covalent modifications, termed post-transcriptional modifications (PTMs). These PTMs encompass phosphorylation [12, 13], acetylation [14, 15], methylation [16, 17], ubiquitination [18–21], SUMOylation [22], lactylation [23], and UFMylation [24]. The downregulation of the HIF-1α protein occurs via the ubiquitin-dependent proteasome pathway, however, this can be reversed by a specific category of enzymes called deubiquitinases (or deubiquitylation enzymes, DUBs).

DUBs are enzymes that reverse protein ubiquitination by detaching ubiquitin chains from their substrates. They play critical roles in regulating protein stability, subcellular localization, and enzymatic activity [25]. There are approximately 100 deubiquitinases present in mammalian cells, which are typically grouped into seven categories: ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolases (UCHs), ovarian tumor domain DUBs (OTUs), Machado-Joseph domain-containing proteases (MJDs), JAB1/MPN/MOV34 metalloenzymes (JAMMs), MINDYs, and ZUFSP [26]. DUBs have been connected to multiple activities, like cell cycle regulation, DNA damage repair, cell differentiation, and tumorigenesis [25]. As for HIF-1α, it has been reported to be deubiquitinated and stabilized by multiple DUBs, including but not limited to USP8 [27], USP22 [28], USP25 [29], BAP1 [30] and UCHL1 [31], etc.

As a member of the DUB family, USP13 has been reported to be involved in various processes, including cancer progression, inflammation, autophagy, and cell cycle progression. USP13 functions as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer (BRCA) by stabilizing PTEN [32]. In the process of melanocytes proliferation and differentiation, USP13 is responsible for stabilizing MITF [33]. Another study revealed that USP13 functions to suppress antiviral responses by deubiquitinating STING [34]. In the initiation of autophagy, USP13 is recruited by auto-ubiquitinated NEDD4-1 to stabilize VPS34, thereby promoting autophagy [35]. In G1/S phase transition, USP13 plays a role in deubiquitinating and stabilizing cyclin D1, ultimately promoting gastric cancer (GC) cell cycle progression [36]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), USP13 was shown to enhance metastasis by cleaving the K63-linked polyubiquitination of β-catenin [37]. In macrophages, USP13 positively regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation by enhancing NLRP3 protein stability independently of its deubiquitinating enzyme activity [38]. In cardiomyocyte, USP13 protects hearts from hypertrophy via deubiquitinating and stabilizing STAT1 [39]. Nevertheless, the role of USP13 in CRC remains poorly understood.

In this study, it was found that CRC exhibits an upregulation of USP13, which functions as a deubiquitinase that influences HIF-1α transcriptional activity by enhancing its stability. We reported that USP13 interacts with and enhances the stability of HIF-1α by removing K48-linked ubiquitination. Furthermore, USP13 is essential for facilitating CRC cell growth, colony formation, migration, invasion and angiogenesis. In other words, USP13 may serve as a tumor promoter in the development of CRC and could be a potential target for cancer therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Cells were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin. Specifically, HEK293T HCT15, RKO and MC38 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM; HCT116 in McCoy’s 5 A; and HUVECs in RPMI 1640 medium. Cells were cultivated under hypoxic conditions (1% O2) by using an incubator with O2 control filled with 5% CO2 and balanced with N2. Polyethylenimine (#24765, Polysciences) was used for transfection in this study following the manufacturer’s standard instructions.

Antibodies and reagents

Anti-mouse β-actin monoclonal antibody (#AC004, 1:5,000) and anti-rabbit polyclonal antibody against Lamin A/C (#A0249, 1:1,000) were purchased from Abclonal. Anti-mouse monoclonal antibody against HIF-1α (#66730-1-Ig, 1:5,000) and anti-rabbit polyclonal antibody against USP13 (#16840-1-AP, 1:2,000), LDHA (#19987-1-AP, 1:2,000), GLUT1 (#21829-1-AP, 1:1,000) were purchase from Proteintech. Anti-rabbit monoclonal antibody against E-Cadherin (#3195, 1:2,000), Snail (#3879, 1:,000) and PDK1(#5662, 1:1,000) were purchase from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti-HA tag (#SLAB0202, 1:5,000), anti-MYC tag (#SLAB2903, 1:5,000) and anti-FLAG tag (#SLAB0102, 1:5,000) antibodies were purchased from Smart-Lifesciences (Changzhou Smart-Lifesciences Biotechnology, China). MG132 (#HY-13259) and CHX (#HY-12320) were purchased from MCE (MedChemExpress, USA).

Plasmids

Plasmids expressing FLAG-USP13, FLAG-VHL and MYC-HIF-1α were generated by PCR and cloned into pHAGE-puro-6 tag, and HA-HIF-1α was cloned into pcDNA5. pGL3-TK-Luc, pGL3-HIF-1α-Luc were purchased from Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA). MYC-Ub, HA-Ub (WT and mutants), FLAG-USP13-AE and USP13-targeting shRNA were kindly provided by Dr Bo Zhong (Wuhan University, Wuhan, China). sgRNAs used to target USP13 were synthesized, annealed and inserted into the pLentiCRISPRv2 (#52961, Addgene, USA). shRNAs used to target HIF-1α were synthesized, and inserted into the pLKO.1 (#10877, Addgene, USA).

Luciferase assay

293 T cells were co-transfected with pGL3-HIF-1α-Luc, pGL3-TK-Luc and the indicated expressing plasmids according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase assay was conducted by using chemiluminescent reagents (Promega, USA) and SpectraMax Mini (Molecular Devices, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction kit (#ET101-01-V2, TransGen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from the purified RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (#RR047, Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative Real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green qPCR Mix (#MQ10101S, Monad Biotech) with the Real-Time PCR System (QuantReady K9600, Life Real). Melting curve analysis confirmed amplicon specificity, and the resulting expression data were normalized to ACTB.

Stable cell lines and knockout cell lines construction

The indicated overexpressing or knockout plasmids were co-transfected into 293 T cells with lentiviral packaging vectors pMD2.G (#12259, Addgene, USA) and psPAX2 (#12260, Addgene, USA). The supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection to infect CRC cells. The infected cells were selected by puromycin for at least 48 h. For stable cell lines construction, cells were cultured and identified by corresponding antibodies. For knockout cell lines, cells were counted and seeded into 10 cm dishes, and single clones were picked, cultured, and identified by corresponding endogenous antibodies and sequencing.

Immunoblot (western blot)

The immunoblot experiment was performed as described previously. Cells were collected, washed, and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1% NP-40) containing inhibitors for proteases and phosphotases. After quantification, proteins were resolved in 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (#P0216 Beyotime) in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) at room temperature for 1 h, and probed with corresponding primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight, then incubated with respective horseradish perioxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (#111-035-003 and 115-035-003, Jacksin, 1:10000) at room temperature for 1 h. Detection was carried out by Clarity Western ECL Substrate (#1705061, Bio-Rad, USA) and ChemiDoc XRS + chemiluminescence gel imaging system (#1708265, Bio-Rad, USA).

Immunoprecipitation

The immunoblot experiment was performed as described previously. Cells were collected, washed, and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1% NP-40) containing inhibitors for protease and phosphatase on ice. The lysates were centrifuged (12,000 g at 4℃ for 15 min) and the supernatants were incubated with the indicated antibodies and rProtein A/G Beads (#SA032025, Smart-Lifesciences) at 4℃ overnight. Beads were washed three times in lysis buffer and proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblot using the indicated antibodies.

Ubiquitination assay

Wild-type Ub or its mutans (e.g., the ‘K48’ mutant denotes a ubiquitin molecule wherein lysine 48 is retained while all other lysine residues are mutated to arginine. Conversely, the ‘K48R’ mutant refers to a ubiquitin molecule wherein lysine 48 is specifically mutated to arginine, with all other lysine residues remaining intact) were transfected into the cells as required. Cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM) for 12 h and then lysed with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 0.1% SDS) containing inhibitors for protease and phosphatase. Cell lysates were boiled at 95℃ for 5 min, then centrifuged (12,000 g at 4℃ for 15 min) and the supernatants were incubated with the indicated antibodies and rProtein A/G Beads at 4℃ overnight. Beads were washed three times in lysis buffer and proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblot using the indicated antibodies.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were grown on 15-mm glass coverslips (#801007, NEST, Wuxi, China). Cells were washed in PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (#P804537, Macklin, Shanghai, China) in PBS at room temperature for 10 min. After washes with PBS for three times, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (#30188928, Sinopharm, Shanghai, China) in PBS at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were then washed in PBS for three times and blocked in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (#BD118650, Bidepharmatech, Shanghai, China) in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. Fixed cells were then incubated with anti-mouse HIF-1α (1:200) and anti-rabbit USP13 (1:200) at 4℃ overnight in PBS containing 3% BSA. After washing in PBS for three times, cells were incubated with second antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor-488 (#A11001, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:1,000) and Alexa Fluor-594 (#21207, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:1,000) at room temperature for 1 h. DAPI was used to stain the nucleus. Imaging was carried out using an Olympus Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope.

Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (#P0027, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were washed with cold PBS and suspended with Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent A, followed by incubation on ice for 10 min. Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent B was added to the suspension, followed by centrifugation (12,000 g at 4℃ for 5 min). The supernatant was collected as cytoplasmic extract. The remaining pellet was resuspended with Nuclear Extraction Reagent, incubation on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation (12,000 g at 4℃ for 5 min). The supernatant was collected as nuclear extract. Westen blot was performed to assess the expression levels of proteins in both subcellular fractions.

Cell proliferation

Cells were seeded on 96-well plates (1,000 cells per well, 3 replicants for each cell). After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CCK-8 reagent (CCK-8: DMED = 1:10). The plate was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to assess cell proliferation. Cell proliferation was measured every 24 h.

Colony formation

Cells were seeded on 6-well plates (500 cells per well, 3 replicants for each cell). The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until visible colonies form. The medium was carefully aspirated and washed gently with PBS. Cells were fixed by adding 4% paraformaldehyde or methanol for 15 min at room temperature. Colonies were stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 10–30 min and rinsed with distilled water.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded on 6-well plate. When the cells were nearly confluent, draw a straight scratch using a pipette tip. Rinse the cells with PBS and replace the medium with serum-free culture medium. Capture images of the scratch at the starting time point and at 48 h and the width of the scratch at both time points was measured by image analysis software. The percentage of the wound closure was calculated to reflect the migration rate.

Cell migration and invasion

For migration assays, uncoated Transwell inserts were used. For invasion assays, the upper side of the Transwell membrane was uncoated with Matrigel (E1270, Merck, USA, 50–100 µL per insert) and incubated at 37 °C for 30–60 min to allow polymerization. Cells were counted and resuspended in serum-free medium (typically 100,000 cells in 200–300 µL medium per insert), then carefully pipetted into the upper chamber of the inserts. Complete medium was added to the lower chamber. Chambers were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 12–24 h (for migration) or 24–48 h (for invasion), Non-migrated cells were removed from the upper chamber using a cotton swab and migrated cells were washed gently with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde or methanol for 15 min. Cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 15–30 min, then rinsed with water. Images were captured using a light microscope and penetrated cells were counted in at least 5 randomly selected fields per insert.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (E1270, Merck, USA) was pipetted into each well of the 96-well plate (50–100 µL per well) and incubated at 37 °C for 30–60 min to allow polymerization. HUVECs (Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells) were resuspended in serum-free or reduced-serum medium at a density of 20,000–50,000 cells per well. Cells were carefully seed onto the solidified Matrigel and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 4–12 h. Tube formation was observed at 4-hour intervals under an inverted microscope. Bright-field images were taken and number of meshes, branches and branch length were measured by ImageJ (Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin) as previously described [40]. Meshes refer to closed, loop-like structures formed by connected segments, and branches refer to all vascular segments that are connected to a junction at one end and terminate at a free extremity at the other end.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The protocols for patients were approved by the Institute Research Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Second Hospital, University of South China (2024085). Surgical tumor tissues from CRC patients and xenograft tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. The tissue sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the slides were stained with primary antibodies against USP13 (Proteintech, 1:200), HIF-1α (Proteintech, 1:200), Ki-67 (CST, 1:200), VEGFA (CST, 1:200), c-Caspase3 (CST, 1:200), CD31 (CST, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight after blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were then incubated with HRP-conjugated or fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody for1 hour at room temperature. For chromogenic detection, DAB substrate was added and incubated until brown staining developed (1–5 min). For fluorescence detection, nuclei were stained with DAPI. Next, the slides were mounted and imaged using a light microscope (for DAB) or fluorescence microscope (for fluorescent IHC). Density measurements of blood vessels (CD31+), apoptotic (c-Caspase3+) and necrotic areas and Ki-67+ cells were performed with ImageJ. The CD31-positive area was measured using ImageJ and is expressed as a percentage of the total tissue area analyzed.

Tumor xenograft experiments

Cells were cultured in vitro until they reached the logarithmic growth phase, then digested with trypsin and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve a final density of 1 × 10⁶–1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. Mice were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control groups (n ≥ 5 per group) and received a subcutaneous injection of 100–200 µL of the cell suspension into the flank or dorsal region under sterile conditions. Starting on day 3 post-inoculation, tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every 2–3 days using a digital caliper, and tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula V = 0.5×L×W². Euthanasia was performed when the average tumor volume in the control group reached 1000–1500 mm³, or if mice exhibited significant weight loss or restricted mobility.

AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated CRC mouse model

The mouse model of colitis-associated colon cancer (CAC) was established in accordance with a published protocol [41]. Briefly, male C57 mice aged 8 weeks and weighing over 20 g were selected and grouped with five mice per cage. On the first day of the experiment, AOM (azoxymethane, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was intraperitoneal injected at a dose of 10 mg/kg. One week later, the drinking water was replaced with 2.5% DSS (dextran sulfate sodium, MP Biomedicals, USA) for five consecutive days. The AOM injection and DSS administration were repeated as per the aforementioned protocol after an additional week, followed by the provision of normal drinking water to the mice. On approximately day 80, the experiment was terminated through euthanasia procedures. Colorectal tissues were excised and longitudinally incised, then thoroughly rinsed with PBS before photographic recording. All animal experiments received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University (15060 A).

Results

Identifying USP13 as a candidate DUB for HIF-1α

We screened a DUBs library to identify deubiquitinases that stabilize HIF-1α by increasing its protein levels, as shown by Western blot. As depicted in Fig. 1A and S1A, several DUBs, including USP2, USP8, USP13, USP22, USP25, and USP28, modestly or markedly increased endogenous HIF-1α levels. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay tested candidate DUBs for interaction with HA-HIF-1α, revealing that three DUBs in Fig. 1A may directly bind HIF-1α, with USP13 and USP22 showing notable potential (Fig. 1B). The regulation of HIF-1α by USP22 has been previously reported [28], leading us to focus on USP13. We measured HIF-1α activity using a luciferase assay and found that the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α was notably increased by USP13 (Fig. 1C). USP13 was able potentiated the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α indeed, as evidenced by upregulated expression of its target genes without altering HIF-1α mRNA levels (Fig. 1D). We further observed a strikingly consistent correlation between HIF-1α/USP13 and the expression of hypoxia-related genes (Fig. S1B, S1C). Despite the absence of an effect on HIF-1α mRNA levels, we found that USP13 and HIF-1α expression levels were positively correlated, indicating a functional interplay between them (Fig. S1D). To further investigate the relationship between USP13 and HIF-1α in CRC, we performed IHC staining to assess the expression levels of USP13, HIF-1α, and the proliferation marker Ki-67 in both cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues from CRC patients. The results revealed that the expression levels of USP13 and HIF-1α were significantly higher in cancerous tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues, particularly in regions with high tumor proliferation. Furthermore, both USP13 and HIF-1α exhibited strong expression correlated with Ki-67, suggesting that they may play a synergistic role in tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 1E and J).

USP13 regulates HIF-1α protein stability

FLAG-USP13 was transfected into 293 T cells in a gradient manner, and the protein level of HIF-1α was assessed using Western blot analysis. The results depicted in Fig. 2A demonstrated a positive correlation between the expression of USP13 and the level of HIF-1α protein in a dose-dependent manner. E3 ligase VHL mediated HIF-1α degradation, and this reduction can be dramatically inhibited by USP13 overexpression (Fig. 2B). Endogenous HIF-1α was nearly absent under normoxic conditions, but USP13 slightly increased its expression, while the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively prevented its degradation (Fig. 2C). To investigate USP13’s role in regulating HIF-1α stability, we treated cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to block protein synthesis and accelerate HIF-1α degradation. CHX treatment significantly reduced HIF-1α levels, while MG132 effectively prevented its degradation (Fig. 2D). Under the same conditions, replacing MG132 with USP13 overexpression similarly restored HIF-1α levels (Fig. 2E). As hypoxia persisted, the level of HIF-1α protein increased within 3–6 h before gradually declining. Additionally, E-Cadherin negatively correlated, while Snail positively correlated with HIF-1α expression. Notably, USP13 protein levels showed no significant changes during hypoxia (Fig. 2F). Subsequently, we aimed to investigate whether USP13 enhances HIF-1α protein stability following its exposure to normoxic conditions. The HIF-1α protein levels increased notably in hypoxia, but rapidly decreased to normal within an hour of normoxia (Fig. 2G). Compared to the control, USP13 overexpression increased HIF-1α levels, while the enzymatically inactive mutant USP13-AE (C345A/M664/739 [34]) failed to maintain them, indicating that USP13 stabilizes HIF-1α via its deubiquitinase activity. As anticipated, CHX chase assay demonstrated that the half-life of HIF-1α was notably prolonged with the overexpression of USP13 (Fig. 2H).

We evaluated the protein levels of USP13 and HIF-1α in different CRC cell lines and observed a significant positive correlation between them (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, depletion of USP13 markedly reduced HIF-1α protein levels (Fig. 2J). USP13 upregulated the protein expression of HIF-1α target genes (Fig. S2A). Conversely, genetic knockout of USP13 downregulated their expression both in protein and mRNA level, suggesting a decrease in the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α (Fig. S2B, S2C). We assessed the protein expression levels of HIF-1α under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In USP13 knockout cells, HIF-1α levels were nearly undetectable under normoxia and markedly reduced under hypoxia compared to controls (Fig. 2K). The decrease in HIF-1α protein level caused by USP13 knockout was reversed upon reintroduction of FLAG-USP13 (Fig. 3L). Furthermore, USP13 deficiency accelerated the degradation of endogenous HIF-1α in the presence of CHX (Fig. 2M). Consequently, it can be inferred that USP13 plays a role in maintaining HIF-1α protein level.

USP13 interacts with HIF-1α

Subsequently, we carried out co-IP experiments to verify the interaction between HIF-1α and USP13. Consistent with the findings from the initial screening (Fig. 1B), these two proteins were found to be co-immunoprecipitated with each other (Fig. 3A and B). Additionally, FLAG-USP13-AE bound MYC-HIF-1α less robustly than WT (Fig. 3B), indicating that the deubiquitinating enzyme activity of USP13 plays a critical role in binding to HIF-1α. Reciprocal endogenous co-immunoprecipitation further confirmed the interaction between USP13 and HIF-1α in CRC cells (Fig. 3C and D). The interaction between USP13 and HIF-1α was also validated in tumor tissues formed by MC38 cells and in CRC model (Fig. S5).

Furthermore, confocal microscopy demonstrated the co-localization of HIF-1α and USP13, particularly in the cytosol, following exposure to hypoxia (Fig. 3E). Intriguingly, USP13 localized predominantly to the cytosol regardless of oxygen levels, while HIF-1α displayed hypoxia-dependent nuclear translocation and accumulation relative to normoxic conditions. USP13 potentiates the transcriptional function of HIF-1α by not only increasing its protein levels but specifically enhancing its nuclear accumulation, which is essential for its activity as a transcription factor (Fig. S3A, S3B).

To map the interacting domains of HIF-1α and USP13, truncated MYC-HIF-1α and FLAG-USP13 variants were transfected into 293 T cells. Co-IP says demonstrated that the N-terminal domain of HIF-1α (amino acids residues 1–85) mediated binding to USP13 (Fig. 3F), while the C-terminal UBA domain of USP13 was required for this interaction (Fig. 3G).

We found that USP13 overexpression decreased the HIF-1α-VHL interaction and correspondingly inhibits VHL-mediated ubiquitination of HIF-1α (Fig. 4C and D). To further elucidate the role of USP13 in the deubiquitination of HIF-1α, we assessed the ubiquitination status of HIF-1α in USP13-knockout cell lines using ubiquitination assay. The results demonstrated a significant increase in HIF-1α ubiquitination in USP13-deficient cells compared to the control group (Fig. 4E). Conversely, HIF-1α ubiquitination was reduced by USP13 overexpression (Fig. 4F). Building upon these observations, we introduced the FLAG-USP13-AE mutant to investigate the enzymatic activity of USP13 in regulating HIF-1α ubiquitination. As expected, substitution of FLAG-USP13 with FLAG-USP13-AE resulted in a pronounced increase in HIF-1α ubiquitination, closely resembling the levels observed in the absence of FLAG-USP13 (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results indicate that USP13 stabilizes HIF-1α by deubiquitination, thereby preventing its degradation. Moreover, this function is strictly dependent on the deubiquitinating enzymatic activity of USP13.

K48- and K63-linked ubiquitin chains are the most common ubiquitin conjugation types, with K48 linkage being the only well-established signal for proteasomal degradation. Results showed that when HA-Ub-K48 and FLAG-USP13 were co-transfected, the ubiquitination level of MYC-HIF-1α was markedly reduced. However, when HA-Ub-K48 was replaced with HA-Ub-K63, the ubiquitination level of MYC-HIF-1α remained unchanged (Fig. 4H). These findings indicate that USP13 regulates the stability of HIF-1α by specifically removing K48-linked ubiquitin chains. However, when K48 was mutated to arginine (K48R), the ubiquitination level of HIF-1α remained unchanged despite the presence of FLAG-USP13. In contrast, when K63 was mutated (K63R), FLAG-USP13 expression significantly reduced HIF-1α ubiquitination, as K48-linked ubiquitination remained intact (Fig. 4I). Additionally, we examined other types of ubiquitin linkages to further confirm the specificity of USP13. As shown in the Fig. 4J, when ubiquitin mutants targeting alternative lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33) were transfected, the ubiquitination level of HIF-1α remained unchanged, in the presence of FLAG-USP13. In conclusion, USP13 stabilizes HIF-1α by selectively removing K48-linked ubiquitin chains, thereby preventing its recognition and degradation via the proteasomal pathway.

USP13 promotes tumor angiogenesis

HIF-1α is a pivotal regulatory factor in cellular responses to hypoxic conditions, playing a crucial role in both physiological angiogenesis and pathological neovascularization [42]. It facilitates the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells by directly activating the transcription of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [43, 44]. In the hypoxic TME, the sustained activation of HIF-1α induces tumor angiogenesis, providing essential nutrients and oxygen necessary for tumor growth and metastasis. Our previous results have demonstrated that USP13 stabilizes HIF-1α protein levels through deubiquitination, thereby promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells. However, whether USP13 regulates tumor angiogenesis by maintaining the stability of HIF-1α, thereby indirectly contributing to tumor progression, remains to be further validated.

To validate this hypothesis, we first assessed the impact of USP13 on the tube formation capacity of endothelial cells through a HUVECs tube formation assay [40]. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that USP13 knockdown in HUVECs resulted in significant reductions in tubulogenesis parameters, including the number of tubular structures, the number of branches, and the total length of the branches (Fig. 7A and C). In contrast, the USP13 overexpression obviously enhanced the tube formation ability of HUVECs (Fig. 7D and F). While HIF-1α silencing likewise impaired angiogenesis in HUVECs, reconstitution of USP13 failed to rescue this phenotype (Fig. 7G and I). To further investigate the in vivo role of USP13 in tumor angiogenesis, we subcutaneously injected murine colorectal carcinoma cells MC38 in wild-type (Usp13+/+) and Usp13 knockout (Usp13−/−) C57 mice. Usp13+/+ mice exhibited enhanced tumor growth relative to Usp13−/− mice, as evidenced by significantly increased tumor volume and growth rate. (Figure 7J and K). To further analyze the structural differences within the tumors, we performed H&E staining on tumors of similar size from both groups, and found that the necrotic areas (marked by black lines) in the Usp13+/+ tumors were considerably smaller than those in the Usp13−/− tumors (Fig. 7L and M).

We also performed CD31 IHC staining to quantitatively analyze the density of newly formed blood vessels within the tumors. The results showed that the CD31-positive vessel density in the Usp13+/+ tumors was significantly higher than in the Usp13−/− tumors (Fig. 7N). The increased vascular density in the Usp13+/+ group provided a direct explanation for the smaller necrotic area: Usp13-driven angiogenesis improves oxygen and nutrient delivery within the tumor microenvironment, thereby reducing hypoxia-induced cell death. In contrast, the Usp13−/− tumors, with limited angiogenesis, experienced heightened hypoxic conditions and metabolic stress, resulting in a larger necrotic area (Fig. 7N).

To comprehensively elucidate the mechanistic role of USP13 in tumor growth, we performed IHC staining for c-Caspase-3, VEGFA, and Ki-67 in tumor tissues. IHC analysis demonstrated differential expression profiles in Usp13+/+ versus Usp13−/− tumors: (1) reduced c-Caspase-3+ cells (apoptosis suppression); (2) increased VEGFA+ cells correlating with enhanced vascularization; (3) elevated Ki-67+ proliferation index. These findings collectively indicate USP13 promotes tumor growth through apoptosis inhibition, angiogenesis potentiation, and proliferation enhancement. (Figure 7O and P).

The results demonstrated that, compared to Usp13−/− mice, Usp13+/+ mice developed a greater number of tumors with larger sizes, suggesting that USP13 plays a promotive role in colorectal tumorigenesis and progression (Fig. 8D-F).

H&E staining of colorectal tissue samples revealed that, compared to Usp13−/− mice, Usp13+/+ mice exhibited more severe mucosal damage and larger tumor volumes, further supporting the role of USP13 in promoting colorectal tumorigenesis (Fig. 8G). Moreover, tumors in Usp13+/+ mice showed elevated Ki-67 expression and vascular density (CD31 positive) compared to Usp13−/−, indicating greater proliferative potential and angiogenesis capacity (Fig. 8G and H).

Discussion

Emerging evidence has demonstrated that deubiquitinases have versatile functions in tumorigenesis and development. In the present study, we found that USP13 plays a promotive role in CRC. Mechanically, USP13 stabilizes HIF-1α by deubiquitination to promote cancer cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, invasion and angiogenesis, leading to CRC progression.

As a pivotal transcription factor, HIF-1α is sophisticated regulated by many interacting proteins in either an O2-dependent or O2-independent manner [46]. HIF-1α regulates critical cellular processes in response to low oxygen levels, including glycolysis and angiogenesis. In hypoxic tumor microenvironment, HIF-1α supports the adaptation of cancer cells by activating genes involved in energy production and the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) [47]. Previous studies have shown that several ubiquitination enzymes [20, 21] and deubiquitination enzymes [27–31] contribute to the regulation of the stability of HIF-1α. Our studies proved that USP13 cleaves degradative K48-linked poly-ubiquitination of HIF-1α, thus preventing its proteasomal degradation and activating downstream target genes involved in tumor growth, survival, angiogenesis and metastasis.

USP13 might also protect HIF-1α from degradation by competing with VHL, which is the most important E3 ligase of HIF-1α and mediates K48-linked poly-ubiquitination and degradation of HIF-1α. In another study, Liu et al. showed that NEDD4L (neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally down-regulated 4-like) targets HIF-1α for degradation, thereby suppressing HIF-1α activity in glycolysis, angiogenesis and metastasis, particularly in CRC [21]. The existence of a VHL-independent mechanism for HIF-1α degradation highlights the diversity of HIF-1α regulation, and whether NEDD4L-mediated ubiquitination of HIF-1α depends on molecular oxygen and PHD activity remains to be investigated. Accordingly, deubiquitinases might compete with these E3 ligases to protect HIF-1α from degradation. Our study provides insight into the mechanism of HIF-1α poly-ubiquitination and degradation. However, it is unclear whether there is a competitive relationship between USP13 and NEDD4L or other E3 ligases.

While our study highlights the role of USP13 in stabilizing HIF-1α and promoting CRC, there are a few limitations. The long-term effects of targeting USP13 in clinical settings remain to be fully explored. Additionally, while the study demonstrates USP13’s role in CRC cell lines and animal models, the broader applicability to human CRC treatment and its interactions with other signaling pathways require further investigation. By inhibiting USP13, it may be possible to destabilize HIF-1α, reducing tumor growth and metastasis. This could complement existing therapies that target angiogenesis or metabolic reprogramming. Spautin-1, an effective USP13 inhibitor, has shown great potential in inhibiting autophagy [48], suppressing melanoma [49], NSCLC [50], ovarian cancer [51]and gastrointestinal stromal tumor [52], especially when used in combination with other antitumor compounds [53]. A natural metabolite of estrogen, 2-methoxyestradiol, inhibits tumor cell metastasis by specifically blocking USP13’s deubiquitinase activity towards the K63-linked ubiquitination of β-catenin in KRASG12D-driven NSCLC [37]. Future research should focus on developing specific USP13 inhibitors, exploring their combination with other therapies, and evaluating their efficacy in clinical trials. Surprisingly, USP13 was found to be phosphorylated by CDC-like kinase 3 (CLK3), and this phosphorylation promotes the binding of USP13 to c-Myc, thus stabilizing c-Myc protein levels and enhancing its transcriptional activity in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) [54]. Therefore, understanding the interactions of USP13 with other proteins and its own post-translational modifications is crucial for optimizing targeted therapies. As for HIF-1α, emerging post-translational modifications of HIF-1α are also being discover. Citrullination of HIF-1α by peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PADI4) [55], lactylation [23] and UFMylation [24] of HIF-1α were validated to antagonize its ubiquitination and proteasome degradation, suggesting that HIF-1α may undergo other unidentified post-translational modifications, which play an increasingly important role in tumor metabolism.
