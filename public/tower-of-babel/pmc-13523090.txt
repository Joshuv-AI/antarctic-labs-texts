A Guide for Exploring Pleiotropic Associations in Genome‐Wide Association Studies Using Summary Statistics
Authors: Christina Y Feng, Pierre‐Emmanuel Sugier, Nan Zou, Benoit Liquet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have shown that pleiotropy, whereby a single genetic variant or gene influences multiple traits, is common in complex human diseases. Detecting cross‐phenotype associations from GWAS summary statistics remains challenging because of small effect sizes, extensive multiple testing, heterogeneous effects, and possible differences in effect direction across traits. Methods that jointly analyze multiple traits can improve the ability to detect pleiotropic signals while retaining the practical advantages of summary statistic‐based analyses. Although a range of statistical approaches has been developed for this purpose, practical guidance on their application, assumptions, and interpretation remains limited. This tutorial reviews several widely used methods for pleiotropy detection from GWAS summary statistics, including ASSET, PLACO, GPA, CPBayes, and GCPBayes, and demonstrates their application using breast and thyroid cancer datasets. We also highlight the importance of accounting for effect heterogeneity, correlation, and biological group structure at the gene and pathway levels in the detection and interpretation of pleiotropic association signals.Keywords: cross‐phenotype association, GWAS, meta‐analysis, pleiotropy, summary statistics

Introduction

Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have become a central tool in human genetics, systematically testing hundreds of thousands of genetic variants across large populations to identify associations with complex traits and diseases. Over the past decade, GWAS have generated a rapidly growing body of robust findings that have substantially advanced biological understanding, risk prediction, and translational research [1].

An important insight emerging from these large‐scale studies is the widespread presence of pleiotropy across complex traits. Numerous empirical studies [2, 3, 4] have demonstrated that pleiotropy, whereby a single genetic variant is associated with multiple phenotypic traits, is a common feature of the genetic architecture of complex diseases. From a biological perspective, pleiotropy encompasses a range of underlying mechanisms, including horizontal, vertical, and spurious pleiotropy [3, 5, 6]. However, from a statistical standpoint, GWAS‐based analyses relying on observational association data generally cannot distinguish these causal mechanisms. Consequently, this tutorial focuses on the identification of cross‐phenotype associations without attempting to infer causality. Throughout this work, the terms pleiotropic effects and cross‐phenotypic effects are used interchangeably to describe observational genetic associations between variants and multiple traits, without implying underlying causal mechanisms.

A substantial proportion of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have been reported to be associated with more than one phenotype [3, 7, 8]. For example, large‐scale studies have identified extensive genetic overlap across psychiatric disorders [9, 10, 11], while others have reported shared genetic risk factors among type 2 diabetes, prostate cancer, and obesity [12, 13]. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for cross‐phenotype effects in genetic association studies. More broadly, incorporating pleiotropic effects into genetic analyses can provide important insights into the biological mechanisms underlying complex diseases. It may also improve disease classification, strengthen risk prediction across related outcomes, and support the development of more targeted and personalized therapeutic strategies. In this way, the study of pleiotropy contributes to a more integrated understanding of complex disease biology and offers opportunities to improve the prediction, prevention, and treatment of multiple diseases simultaneously. As GWAS sample sizes continue to grow, cross‐phenotype associations are being detected with increasing frequency, creating a strong need for statistical methods that can identify potential pleiotropic signals from genome‐wide data [5].

Despite this potential, the identification of pleiotropic effects remains methodologically challenging. A substantial proportion of the genetic basis of complex diseases is thought to involve variants with small individual effects, which are difficult to detect using standard GWAS approaches [14, 15, 16]. In addition, GWAS typically rely on univariate regression models that estimate SNP effects marginally, one variant at a time. Because these estimates are not adjusted for other variants and are influenced by linkage disequilibrium (LD), they may capture correlated rather than independent genetic signals, making interpretation more difficult. When individual‐level data are available, multivariate regression approaches that jointly model multiple variants and traits can yield more accurate effect estimates and more informative summary measures. In practice, however, access to individual‐level data is often limited by privacy, ethical, and data sharing constraints. Consequently, many pleiotropy‐focused methods rely on GWAS summary statistics, which are more widely available and typically include effect estimates, standard errors, p‐values, allele frequencies, and genomic positions. The use of summary statistics enables large‐scale integration across studies while preserving participant confidentiality, and has therefore become a central strategy for improving the detection of cross‐phenotype associations.

Several statistical methods have therefore been developed to detect potential pleiotropic signals using GWAS summary statistics [12, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. While these approaches offer increased power through data integration across traits, pleiotropy detection remains challenging because many of the difficulties inherent to single‐trait GWAS persist, including small effect sizes, extensive multiple testing, and limited statistical power. The problem is further complicated when genetic effects vary in magnitude or act in opposite directions across traits, as some methods assume concordant effects and may therefore lose power in the presence of bidirectional or antagonistic associations. In addition, correlation among SNPs due to LD can distort inference when not adequately modelled, while limited use of gene‐level or pathway‐level information may hinder detection of broader pleiotropic structure. Failure to appropriately account for pleiotropic effects can also affect downstream analyses. In particular, unaccounted pleiotropy may bias causal inference methods such as Mendelian randomization, leading to misleading conclusions about cross‐phenotypic relationships. Although substantial methodological progress has been made in recent years, many available pleiotropy detection methods remain underutilized, and systematic guidance on their assumptions, strengths, and limitations is still lacking.

The aim of this tutorial is to address this gap by providing a structured overview of widely used statistical methods for detecting cross‐phenotypic effects in GWAS, covering both frequentist and Bayesian approaches. Using real data, we illustrate the practical application of these methods and discuss their underlying assumptions, advantages, and limitations. Section 2.1 introduces the hierarchical organization of genetic data, spanning SNP, gene, and pathway levels, and discusses the main biological, statistical, and computational factors that guide method selection. Section 2.2 describes the structure of individual‐level genetic data and the summary statistics derived from them, emphasizing the distinctions between these data forms and providing practical guidance on generating GWAS summary statistics, with extensions to gene and pathway analyses. Sections 3 and 4 review the statistical methods considered in this tutorial, with particular emphasis on their implementation in R, and demonstrate their application to a real dataset for investigating cross‐phenotype effects. The tutorial concludes with a discussion of the findings, practical challenges, and future directions.

Background

Genetic Structure: DNA, SNPs, Genes and Pathways

Cross‐phenotypic association studies can be conducted at different scales of analysis, ranging from single SNP tests to hierarchical frameworks that incorporate gene and pathway‐level biological structure. Single SNP analysis focuses on identifying associations between individual SNPs and phenotypic traits, typically through GWAS. In contrast, multilevel modeling extends beyond individual SNPs by incorporating gene and pathway analyses, allowing genetic associations to be interpreted within broader biological contexts. Since gene and pathway‐based analyses are constructed from SNP‐level variation, understanding the biological and statistical representation of SNPs is a necessary first step.

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) consists of a series of nucleotides that are made of nitrogenous bases, namely adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G), as well as a deoxyribose sugar and a phosphate group. SNPs represent variation at a single base in the DNA sequence, occurring at a specific genetic locus and differing across individuals (Figure 1). In genetic association studies, SNP genotypes are commonly encoded under an additive model as 0, 1, or 2, representing the number of minor alleles carried by an individual [22]. For example, if G is the minor allele and A is the major allele, the additive coding assigns 0 to the homozygous major genotype (AA), 1 to the heterozygous genotype (AG), and 2 to the homozygous minor genotype (GG).

Schematic representation of pathway structure, genes, and SNP variation. Chromosomes are organized DNA containing genes located at specific loci. Genes encode proteins and contribute to the regulation of biological traits, while groups of related genes may be classified into pathways. A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a variation at a single base position in the DNA sequence. Such variants differ across individuals, such as between Person 1 and Person 2, and contribute to genetic diversity.

SNPs are the most common genetic sequence variations in the human genome and serve as key genetic markers for studying the genetic basis of complex traits and diseases [23, 24], making them a natural starting point for investigating pleiotropic effects. GWAS typically perform single SNP association analyses that assess the relationship between a trait and each SNP individually across the genome, usually within a regression framework that adjusts for relevant covariates. These analyses generate SNP‐specific summary statistics, such as estimated effect sizes, standard errors, and p‐values. Although this approach has successfully identified many variant trait associations, the variants detected through GWAS generally explain only a modest proportion of the estimated heritability of complex human diseases. This has been attributed to several factors, including limited power to detect variants with small individual effects, the substantial multiple testing burden, the contribution of effects operating at the gene or pathway level, gene‐by‐environment interactions, and other downstream biological mechanisms. In addition, SNPs are often correlated through LD, creating complex dependence structures across the genome. As a result, focusing only on the most significant SNPs that surpass genome‐wide significance thresholds may provide an incomplete view of the underlying biology, as such an approach can overlook correlated variants and the broader functional relationships among them. At a higher level of biological organization, genes are sequences of nucleotides that encode functional RNA or protein products, and SNPs may occur within or near these regions. Functionally related genes are often organized into biological pathways that jointly contribute to complex biological processes, such as metabolism, cell division, and inflammatory response [25, 26]. For example, variants in the ASTN2 gene have been associated with both osteoarthritis and migraine [27, 28], suggesting that apparently distinct diseases may share common biological mechanisms, including pathways related to pain perception.

The hierarchical organization of genetic data, from SNPs to genes and biological pathways, introduces additional complexity for statistical modelling and requires methods that can account for dependence among variants. Despite these challenges, multivariate approaches that incorporate prior biological knowledge to define groups or subgroups, such as gene‐level and pathway‐level units, may improve statistical power and enhance the biological interpretability of genetic associations, thereby helping to uncover mechanisms underlying complex traits and diseases.

Data Structure

The choice of statistical methods for identifying cross‐phenotype associations depends strongly on the structure of the available data. A primary distinction is whether analyses are based on individual‐level data or summary statistics, as this determines both the models that can be fitted and the information available for inference. Individual‐level data provide direct access to genotypes, phenotypes, and covariates, whereas summary statistics typically consist of SNP‐specific effect estimates, standard errors, test statistics, or p‐values derived from single‐trait association analyses.

The type of phenotype is also important, since outcomes may be continuous, binary, survival, or a combination thereof. The statistical model should therefore reflect the distribution and dependence structure of the outcome. Linear regression is commonly used for continuous traits, logistic regression for binary traits, and Cox proportional hazards models for time‐to‐event outcomes. When observations are correlated or clustered, mixed effects models may be used to account for within‐group dependence.

Further complexity arises when analyses extend beyond single SNP associations to higher order genomic units, such as genes or pathways, or incorporate external biological information such as functional annotation. A clear understanding of both phenotype structure and data representation is therefore essential for selecting appropriate methods and obtaining reliable inference in cross‐phenotype association studies.

Summary Statistics and Individual‐Level Data

High‐quality summary statistics from large‐scale genomic studies are now widely accessible, reflecting substantial reductions in the cost of genotyping and the expansion of large biobank and consortium‐based resources. Association results are increasingly disseminated through public databases and study‐specific repositories, including resources linked to the UK Biobank [29] and the NHGRI GWAS Catalog [30]. These resources have facilitated large‐scale genetic studies by providing access to association results for numerous traits, diseases, and genomic regions. Typically, GWAS summary statistics include estimated SNP effects (β^), standard errors (σ^β^), test statistics, p‐values, chromosomal position, and often additional information such as minor allele frequency (MAF), allele labels, imputation quality measures, and functional annotations.

Because summary statistics form the basis of many downstream analyses, their derivation requires appropriate quality control and statistical adjustment to ensure their accuracy and interpretability. Common steps include adjustment for population structure through inclusion of principal components, covariate adjustment for factors such as age and sex, and filtering based on allele frequency and imputation quality. Appropriate significance thresholds or multiple testing procedures are then applied during inference. When data from multiple studies are available, meta‐analysis is commonly used to combine association results across independent cohorts, therefore increasing effective sample size and improving power to detect genetic associations.

Access to individual‐level data is often restricted by privacy regulations, confidentiality agreements, and data sharing constraints. In addition, data availability may differ across phenotypes, with individual‐level data accessible for some traits but only summary statistics available for others. When individual‐level data are available, they offer important advantages for cross‐phenotype association analysis. In particular, they permit direct adjustment for relevant covariates, more flexible joint modelling of multiple variants and phenotypes, and estimation of dependence structures that are not directly available from standard univariate GWAS summaries. By contrast, standard summary statistics are aggregated outputs and therefore do not usually provide the full covariance structure among estimated SNP effects. Consequently, analyses based solely on marginal summary statistics often rely on simplified covariance assumptions unless additional information, such as an external LD reference panel, is incorporated. This loss of dependence information may reduce statistical efficiency and limit power in multi‐trait analyses.

When multiple SNPs are modeled jointly using individual‐level data, the covariance matrix of the estimated regression coefficients can be obtained directly. By contrast, standard marginal summary statistics generally provide only SNP‐specific variances, yielding a diagonal approximation unless off‐diagonal covariance terms are reconstructed separately. Deriving Summary Statistics

In many practical settings, individual‐level data are available for only a subset of phenotypes, whereas other traits are represented solely through published GWAS summary statistics. In such cases, an initial step in cross‐phenotype analysis is to derive summary statistics from the available individual‐level data so that results can be combined across studies. Accurate summary statistics are therefore essential, as they underpin cross‐trait meta‐analysis and pleiotropy detection methods that operate without direct access to participant‐level data. Methods for deriving and analyzing genetic association results may be broadly divided into single‐SNP and multi‐SNP approaches.

Single‐SNP Association Analysis

In GWAS, the standard strategy is to test each SNP individually for association with the trait of interest. Each variant is analyzed separately within a regression framework after appropriate genotype quality control, phenotype definition, and covariate adjustment. The most commonly used models include linear regression for continuous traits and logistic regression for binary traits. In case‐control studies, the SNP effect is estimated as a regression coefficient on the log odds scale such that each additional copy of the minor allele corresponds to an additive change in the log odds of disease. For continuous phenotypes, such as height or weight, linear regression under a Gaussian assumption is commonly adopted. When this assumption is unsuitable, alternative response distributions within the generalized linear modeling framework, such as the Gamma or inverse Gaussian, may be considered with an appropriate link function.

Multi‐SNP Association Analysis

Although single SNP analyses provide a practical foundation for genome‐wide screening, the estimated coefficient in such models reflects only the marginal association between each variant and the phenotype, without accounting for correlations among nearby SNPs induced by LD.

To address this limitation, multi‐SNP models jointly include several variants within a defined genomic unit, such as a gene, within a single regression framework. The resulting coefficients represent conditional effects, in the sense that the association of each SNP is estimated after accounting for the other variants included in the model. When individual‐level data are available, this approach also permits direct estimation of the covariance structure among SNP effect estimates within the genomic region, information not directly contained in marginal summary statistics alone.

A common challenge in multi‐SNP analyses is multicollinearity [31], which arises when predictors are highly correlated. In genetic data, such correlation is largely driven by LD among nearby variants [32]. Regularization methods may help address this issue by stabilizing coefficient estimates in the presence of strong correlation. In the frequentist setting, ridge regression imposes an L2 penalty on the regression coefficients to reduce variance inflation and improve numerical stability. Bayesian regularization provides an alternative by placing shrinkage priors on the regression parameters, thereby yielding posterior distributions for regression coefficients and, where relevant, their associated variance and covariance structures within a unified inferential framework [33, 34].

In this example, the covariance matrix calculated from the Bayesian ridge output represents covariance across posterior draws and therefore summarizes posterior uncertainty in the regression coefficients conditional on the observed data, likelihood, and prior distribution. This posterior covariance is distinct from the estimated sampling covariance matrix of β^, which estimates the variability of the regression coefficient estimator under repeated sampling. Under suitable regularity conditions, the posterior covariance may approximate the frequentist asymptotic sampling covariance as the sample size increases. However, this correspondence is not guaranteed in finite samples and may not hold in high‐dimensional or strongly regularized settings. Therefore, posterior covariance summaries from Bayesian regularized models should not automatically be interpreted as estimates of the sampling covariance matrix required by downstream summary statistic methods.

Methods

Once summary statistics have been obtained, either from individual‐level data or from publicly available resources, the next step is to evaluate cross‐phenotype associations. In this setting, the primary aim is to identify genetic signals that show evidence of association across multiple traits. Different methods address this aim at different inferential levels and therefore provide evidence for different types of cross‐phenotype association patterns. For example, some methods test a global null hypothesis of no association with any trait, where rejection indicates association with at least one trait. Others are designed for two‐trait joint association testing, subset‐based association testing across two or more traits, or latent association class modelling across multiple traits. Therefore, the number of traits supported, the null hypothesis being tested, and the post‐inference summaries used for interpretation differ across methods. These associations can provide further statistical insight into possible pleiotropy [3, 6], but their interpretation depends on the null hypothesis, alternative hypothesis, and post‐inference summaries of each method.

A common strategy for this purpose is cross‐trait meta‐analysis, which combines association evidence across traits to detect shared genetic effects. In practice, however, the large number of tests performed across variants and traits increases the risk of false positive findings, making appropriate control of multiplicity essential. Simple threshold‐based approaches, such as Bonferroni correction, may be used to control the family‐wise error rate, but they are often conservative in high‐dimensional genetic settings and may reduce power to detect true associations, particularly for variants with modest effects [35]. Alternative procedures based on false discovery rate (FDR) control [36] can offer a more favorable balance between error control and power, especially in large‐scale association studies. In addition to multiple testing, cross‐phenotype analyses must account for heterogeneous effects and dependence arising from linkage disequilibrium or sample overlap. Methods for this purpose are broadly divided into frequentist and Bayesian approaches.

Frequentist methods typically assess evidence against a null hypothesis of no shared association using test statistics derived from the observed summary data. Many such approaches can be implemented using GWAS summary statistics alone, making them particularly useful when individual‐level data are unavailable. In addition, they are often computationally efficient and readily scalable to genome‐wide analyses. In this tutorial, we focus on representative frequentist methods including ASSET and PLACO, both of which are available in R.

Bayesian methods provide an alternative framework by modeling uncertainty in the underlying association structure through posterior probabilities. By combining prior assumptions with the observed data, these methods allow flexible inference on association patterns that may be consistent with pleiotropy and are particularly useful in settings involving heterogeneous association patterns or hierarchical genetic structure. They can also incorporate prior biological information at multiple levels, ranging from SNPs to genes and pathways, thereby supporting richer modelling of complex genetic architectures. In this tutorial, we consider Bayesian methods including GPA, CPBayes, and GCPBayes, all of which can be implemented using available R software.

Importantly, the methods considered in this tutorial address related but distinct inferential questions and should not be interpreted as testing a single statistical definition of pleiotropy. Some methods test for evidence of association with at least one trait or one selected subset of traits, whereas others test joint association across traits, model latent association patterns, or distinguish group‐level association from group‐level pleiotropic structure. Accordingly, interpretation must be guided by the null and alternative hypotheses of each method, together with the relevant post‐inference summaries, such as selected phenotype subsets, direction‐specific effect estimates, trait‐specific posterior probabilities, or group‐level posterior measures. ASSET Model

Association analysis based on SubSETs (ASSET) [17] is a widely used frequentist method for detecting subset‐based association signals across multiple studies or traits, thereby providing insight into pleiotropic association patterns [37, 38, 39]. ASSET is designed to detect overall evidence of association for a given variant while allowing the subset of associated traits to differ across variants. In contrast to conventional meta‐analysis, which assumes a common effect across all studies, ASSET searches over subsets of studies or traits and is therefore well suited to settings in which genetic effects are present only for a subset of phenotypes. For example, one SNP may be associated with both phenotypes A and B, whereas another may be associated only with phenotype A, and a third may be associated with phenotypes A, B, and C. A notable feature of ASSET is that it accommodates heterogeneous effect directions across traits. Thus, a given variant may increase risk for one phenotype while decreasing risk for another. This feature is particularly relevant in pleiotropy studies, where shared loci may have opposing effects across related traits or disease subtypes.

ASSET operates on GWAS summary statistics and builds on the fixed‐effect meta‐analysis framework using study‐specific Z statistics. For each SNP, the method evaluates all possible non‐empty subsets of the K studies and computes a subset‐specific meta‐analytic test statistic. It then identifies the maximum evidence of association across subsets and provides both a global p‐value for association and information on the subset of traits contributing to the signal. Importantly, the method also accounts for correlation among study‐specific test statistics, for example when studies share controls or include overlapping samples.

Let (β^k,ŝk) denote the estimated genetic effect and its standard error for a given SNP from study k, where k=1,…,K. Define the study‐specific test statistic as Zk=β^k/ŝk. In standard fixed‐effect meta‐analysis, a pooled test statistic may be written as Zmeta=∑k=1KwkZk, where the weights wk are chosen such that Var(Zmeta)=1. Under inverse variance weighting, one may take wk≈1/ŝk∑k=1K1/ŝk2. Under the simplifying assumption that the standard errors are approximately proportional to 1/nk, where nk is the sample size of study k, the weights reduce to wk=nk∑k=1Knk.

An R implementation of ASSET [41] is available for subset‐based association analysis of heterogeneous traits and disease subtypes. Relative to conventional meta‐analysis, ASSET offers several advantages. It allows for heterogeneity in both the presence and direction of association across traits, and it identifies the subset of traits driving the overall signal, which may aid biological interpretation and replication. However, the number of candidate subsets grows exponentially with the number of traits, which increases computational burden and can reduce power after adjustment for the subset search. In addition, like many SNP‐level methods, ASSET may have limited power to detect weak and diffuse signals spread across multiple variants, and it does not explicitly model higher‐order biological structure such as genes or pathways.

A recent extension, fastASSET [42], improves computational scalability for settings involving large numbers of traits. fastASSET uses a two‐step strategy in which traits showing suggestive evidence of association are first retained, after which the ASSET search is applied to the reduced trait set. Although this screening step may lead to a modest loss of power relative to the full ASSET procedure, it substantially improves scalability and makes the method practical for analyses involving hundreds of traits.

PLACO Model

PLeiotropic Analysis under COmposite null hypothesis (PLACO) is a frequentist method for testing statistical evidence consistent with pleiotropic association at the single‐variant level using GWAS summary statistics, and an R package is available for its implementation [12, 43]. Unlike methods that test a global null hypothesis of no association with any trait, PLACO is designed to identify variants that are simultaneously associated with two traits by testing a composite null hypothesis. Specifically, let β1 and β2 denote the genetic effects of a given SNP on traits 1 and 2, respectively. PLACO tests the null hypothesis H0:β1β2=0, against the alternative Ha:β1β2≠0. Thus, under the null, at least one of the two traits is not associated with the SNP, whereas under the alternative the SNP is associated with both traits. Under the relevant null component, Zk is asymptotically standard normal when βk=0. PLACO is based on the product statistic Z1Z2, which is sensitive to simultaneous association with both traits.

Assuming that the two trait‐specific test statistics are independent, the global null H0 is a mixture of the three component nulls H00, H01, and H02, with probabilities π00, π01, and π02, respectively. Under H00, both Z1 and Z2 follow asymptotic N(0,1) distributions. Under H01, Z1∼N(0,1), whereas Z2|μ2∼N(μ2,1) with μ2∼N(0,τ22), implying that marginally Z2∼N(0,1+τ22). Similarly, under H02, Z2∼N(0,1) and Z1∼N(0,1+τ12) marginally. A key step in the PLACO framework is estimation of the nuisance parameters governing these mixture components.

PLACO has several attractive features. It directly targets statistical evidence for association with both traits under a composite null hypothesis, is computationally efficient, and can be implemented using GWAS summary statistics alone. However, the method is primarily designed for SNP‐level analysis and does not explicitly incorporate higher‐order biological structure, such as genes or pathways. In addition, PLACO is primarily formulated for two traits. Extensions to larger numbers of traits require higher‐dimensional test statistics and may involve increased computational complexity and reduced power.

More recent extensions, including PLACO+, have been developed to better accommodate correlation between traits by incorporating the covariance structure of the test statistics [44]. These extensions are particularly useful in settings involving overlapping samples or closely related phenotypes, where the independence assumption is unlikely to hold.

GPA Model

Genetic analysis incorporating Pleiotropy and Annotation (GPA) is a Bayesian framework for jointly modelling pleiotropic association signals and functional annotation information across multiple GWAS datasets [18]. The method is implemented in R package [45], which supports joint analysis of GWAS summary statistics and annotation enrichment. Functional annotations provide biologically informed labels for genomic elements, often in binary form, that indicate potential roles in protein coding, gene regulation, or other biological processes. Previous studies have shown that SNPs with relevant functional annotations are more likely to be associated with complex traits and diseases [46, 47]. GPA exploits this enrichment structure to improve the prioritization of potentially associated variants.

CPBayes Model

Cross‐Phenotype Bayes (CPBayes) is a Bayesian method for investigating cross‐phenotype associations at the SNP level using GWAS summary statistics [19]. The method performs a Bayesian meta‐analysis across multiple traits and is implemented in a publicly available R package [48]. CPBayes is designed to quantify the overall evidence against the global null hypothesis of no association with any trait and to identify the subset of traits most likely to be associated with a given SNP.

CPBayes is based on a continuous spike and slab prior, originally proposed by Mitchell and Beauchamp [49], to distinguish null from non‐null trait‐specific effects. In this formulation, the spike component represents a near‐null effect through a normal distribution with small variance centered at zero, whereas the slab component represents a non‐null effect through a more diffuse normal distribution. Although both continuous spike and Dirac spike formulations were considered, the authors focused primarily on the continuous spike specification in the final implementation [19].

The CPBayes model is applied on a SNP‐by‐SNP basis. For notational simplicity, the SNP index is omitted. Let (β^k,ŝk) denote the estimated genetic effect and its standard error for trait k, where k=1,…,K. For large sample sizes, CPBayes assumes β^k|βk∼N(βk,ŝk2), where ŝk is treated as a consistent estimator of the true standard deviation of β^k. Let β^=(β^1,…,β^K) and β=(β1,…,βK). More generally, the model assumes β^|β∼NK(β,∑^), where ∑^ is a consistent estimate of the covariance matrix of the summary statistics. When the trait‐specific estimates are uncorrelated, ∑^ reduces to a diagonal matrix.

CPBayes offers several advantages for cross‐phenotype analysis. Simulation studies and real data applications suggest that it performs well in selecting the subset of traits underlying a pleiotropic signal, with higher specificity than some existing subset‐based methods such as ASSET [19]. Here, specificity refers to the proportion of null traits correctly excluded from the selected subset, whereas sensitivity refers to the proportion of truly associated traits correctly retained. In contrast to methods based solely on significance testing, CPBayes also accommodates heterogeneity in both the magnitude and direction of genetic effects across traits. In addition, the method can account for overlapping subjects, such as shared cases or controls across studies.

Despite these advantages, CPBayes also presents practical and interpretational challenges. Inference relies on Bayesian quantities such as the locFDR and BF, whose interpretation and threshold selection are generally less familiar than conventional p‐values. Because these measures depend on prior specification and posterior modelling assumptions, the choice of decision thresholds may vary across applications. This can complicate interpretation and reduce comparability with frequentist procedures.

A further limitation is that CPBayes is applied at the SNP level and does not explicitly model local genomic structure. In regions containing multiple correlated variants, association signals may reflect linkage disequilibrium rather than a shared causal variant. Such colocalization patterns [50] can complicate interpretation and limit the ability of SNP‐level methods to distinguish true shared causal effects from correlated signals.

GCPBayes Model

Although many methods have been developed for pleiotropy analysis using GWAS summary statistics, most operate at the single SNP level and do not explicitly incorporate higher‐order genomic structure, such as genes or biological pathways. As discussed in subsection 2.2, modeling such hierarchical organization requires multi‐SNP approaches that extend beyond traditional single variant analyses. To address this limitation, Group‐level Bayesian Meta analysis for Cross‐Phenotype Genetic Association (GCPBayes) was proposed as a Bayesian framework for evaluating group‐level cross‐phenotype association patterns while incorporating biologically defined group structures; the original method paper describes these patterns in terms of pleiotropic associations and effects [20].

GCPBayes is implemented in a publicly available R package [51]. Building on the CPBayes framework, it introduces sparse group selection through hierarchical spike and slab priors, thereby enabling simultaneous selection of associated groups and variants within groups. By incorporating biologically informed grouping structures, such as genes or pathways, the method supports inference at multiple levels, ranging from individual SNPs to broader functional units. GCPBayes provides a Bayesian meta‐analysis framework for investigating pleiotropic association signals at both the group and variant levels while incorporating biologically defined group structures, such as genes or pathways. Simulation studies reported by the authors suggest favorable performance relative to benchmark methods such as ASSET and CPBayes [20]. More recently, Sugier et al. [21] proposed a new approach called Meta‐analysis model adapted for Pleiotropy Selection with Group structure (MPSG), a penalized multivariate meta‐analysis method with group structure, motivated in part by the limitation that existing methods analyze pleiotropic associations one by one at the variant or gene level rather than jointly across all genetic information. Thus, while GCPBayes remains an important benchmark for structured Bayesian pleiotropy analysis, further advances in scalability and multivariate joint modelling remain active areas of development. In addition, because standard GWAS summary statistics often provide only marginal standard errors without within‐group covariance information, improved covariance reconstruction from external LD reference panels may further strengthen inference in summary statistic‐based applications.

Application to Breast and Thyroid Cancer Datasets

To illustrate the practical application of the methods reviewed above, we examined cross‐phenotype associations between breast cancer and thyroid cancer. Both cancers are common in women and share several hormonal and reproductive risk factors [54]. Epidemiological studies have reported a bidirectional increase in risk that cannot be fully explained by treatment or surveillance, suggesting the presence of shared susceptibility [55, 56]. This hypothesis is further supported by genetic studies that have identified overlapping risk loci associated with both cancers [20, 57].

In this tutorial, we used previously published datasets to demonstrate how different statistical approaches can be applied to real data to investigate cross‐phenotype genetic associations between breast and thyroid cancer [20, 57]. Because the methods differ in their inferential targets, the application focuses not only on implementation but also on interpreting the resulting evidence in relation to each method's null hypothesis, alternative hypothesis, and post‐inference summaries. The analysis focused on genes belonging to candidate biological pathways selected from the KEGG database and prior literature [58]. The data comprised two harmonized case‐control datasets. The breast cancer dataset was derived from the French population‐based CECILE study and included 1125 cases and 1172 controls [59]. The thyroid cancer dataset comprised individual‐level genetic data from three French studies, CATHY, YOUNG‐THYR, and E3N, conducted within the EPITHYR consortium, and included 1129 cases and 1174 controls [60, 61, 62]. Both datasets included only women of European ancestry to reduce potential confounding due to population stratification. Genomic information included SNP identifiers, chromosomal position, major and minor alleles, nearby genes, and pathway annotations. The procedures for genotyping, imputation, and genetic quality control have been described previously [57, 58].

For the breast and thyroid cancer application, we used the same pre‐processed dataset used in the SGMT, GCPBayes, and MPSG studies [20, 21, 57]. The two cancer datasets were generated within the same genotyping and preprocessing framework. The preprocessing described in these studies included construction of a common SNP set across the two cancer datasets, quality control, exclusion of rare and palindromic SNPs, removal of perfectly or highly correlated SNPs, LD/correlation pruning, and restriction to nonoverlapping gene and pathway groups. Thus, allele alignment and potentially ambiguous variants were addressed as part of the original preprocessing and harmonization steps. We therefore used the preprocessed common SNP panel without further modification. The preprocessed dataset comprised a common set of 3707 SNPs across the 22 autosomes, mapped to 331 genes and grouped into 10 non‐overlapping biological pathways (Table 1). This same SNP panel was used for all downstream analyses, including ASSET, PLACO, GPA, CPBayes, and GCPBayes.

CPBayes

CPBayes is a Bayesian meta‐analysis framework for detecting pleiotropic genetic association using summary statistics. The method quantifies global evidence of association and simultaneously identifies the subset of traits most likely to be associated with a given SNP through a spike and slab prior within a Markov chain Monte Carlo framework. Separate implementations are available for correlated and uncorrelated summary statistics. Because the breast and thyroid cancer datasets were derived from independent studies, we used the cpbayes_uncor and analytic_locFDR_BF_uncor functions.

Although CPBayes is designed for genome‐wide analysis, inference is performed on a SNP‐by‐SNP basis. For illustration, we first applied the method to rs12914272, an SNP showing evidence against the CPBayes global null of no association across the two traits. In this application, locFDR <0.05 and log10(BF)>1 were used as working Bayesian evidence criteria for descriptive interpretation rather than as standardized discovery thresholds [19, 20]. Here, locFDR represents the posterior probability that the CPBayes global null is true, where the global null assumes that the SNP is associated with neither breast cancer nor thyroid cancer. Thus, locFDR (<0.05) indicates that the posterior probability assigned to this global null configuration is less than 0.05. A smaller locFDR criterion requires a lower posterior probability of the global null and therefore selects fewer signals, whereas a larger criterion is more permissive. Similarly, log10(BF)>1 is equivalent to BF>10, indicating that the marginal likelihood of the observed data is more than ten times greater under the CPBayes alternative, which allows association with breast cancer, thyroid cancer, or both, than under the CPBayes global null. Bayes factors above 10 are commonly interpreted as strong evidence in favor of the alternative, although such evidence categories should be regarded as descriptive guidelines rather than strict decision thresholds [66]. A larger Bayes factor criterion requires stronger relative evidence in favor of the alternative and is therefore more stringent. Neither criterion controls a frequentist error rate, and both should be interpreted together with the trait‐specific posterior probabilities, posterior effect estimates, and the inferential target of the method.

Specifically, the SNP had a locFDR of 0.009 and a log10(BF) of 1.553. For rs10812800, the PPAs were 0.446 for breast cancer and 0.984 for thyroid cancer; for rs1713411, 0.247 and 0.907, respectively; and for rs878156, 0.217 and 0.971, respectively. These posterior summaries provided stronger support for association with thyroid cancer. Although the breast cancer PPA for rs10812800 was close to 0.5, it remained below the reference value used here in accordance with the median‐model rule for interpreting the marginal PPAj values [20]. Marginal PPAs below this reference value may still be useful for descriptive inspection, particularly in lower power settings, but they were not interpreted here as sufficient evidence of trait involvement.

In contrast, the PPAs for rs1482057 were 1.000 for breast cancer and 0.277 for thyroid cancer, indicating stronger posterior support for association with breast cancer, consistent with the pattern observed for rs12914272. None of the five SNPs had trait‐specific marginal PPAs exceeding 0.5 for both cancers. These findings indicate evidence of association with one or more traits under the CPBayes model, but do not by themselves establish association with both cancers. The trait‐specific PPAs and detailed results are presented in Table S7.

GCPBayes

We next applied GCPBayes to evaluate potential pleiotropic association patterns at both the group and SNP levels. Evidence against the global null hypothesis of no association was assessed using the local false discovery rate, the Bayes factor, and the posterior pleiotropy probability θ, with groups classified as showing group‐level pleiotropic association evidence when θ>0.5 [20]. Within the pleiotropic groups, SNP‐specific effects were then evaluated using posterior medians or credible intervals, depending on the prior specification. GCPBayes provides three modeling strategies, namely the continuous spike (CS) (3), Dirac spike (DS) (4), and hierarchical spike (HS) priors (5).

As an illustration, we analyzed the gene BCAT1, which contained 50 SNPs in the present dataset. GCPBayes requires summary statistics as input, which may be derived either from single SNP models or from joint multi‐SNP models fitted within genes or pathways, as described in subsubsection 2.2.2. We first analysed BCAT1 using single SNP summary statistics under the DS, CS, and HS priors, but no pleiotropic signal was detected. We then considered a joint multi‐SNP framework based on the Bayesian hierarchical generalized linear model BhGLM [67], which jointly models correlated SNP effects within a gene. Under this multivariate specification, 5 of the 50 SNPs in BCAT1 were identified as associated, suggesting greater sensitivity for the joint model in this gene.

In the absence of individual‐level covariance information, diagonal variance estimates were used. The global null hypothesis of no association was assessed using the working criteria locFDR <0.05 and log10(BF)>1. These criteria were used for descriptive interpretation in the same manner as in the CPBayes analysis, with locFDR and Bayes factor evaluated relative to the corresponding GCPBayes group‐level null hypothesis.

Under the DS prior, BCAT1 showed strong evidence against the group‐level global null, with a log10(BF) of 9.193, a local FDR below 0.001, and a posterior pleiotropy probability (θ) of 1.000. These results indicate strong posterior support for potential pleiotropic association at the gene level. By contrast, the CS prior did not detect pleiotropic association. This pattern is consistent with simulation results reported by the authors, in which the Dirac spike prior generally showed greater power than the more conservative continuous spike prior in comparable settings [20].

As in the previous analyses, the model was fitted using estimated SNP effects Betahand the corresponding variance–covariance matrices Sigmah. Discussion

Identifying cross‐phenotype associations is an important step toward understanding how genetic variation contributes to complex traits and disease. Improved detection of pleiotropic associations can strengthen downstream analyses by revealing previously unrecognized genotype phenotype relationships and by clarifying how shared variants contribute to distinct patterns of association across phenotypic domains [68, 69].

Despite substantial methodological progress, detection of pleiotropy from GWAS summary statistics remains challenging. A central difficulty is that pleiotropy is not defined by a single statistical construct, and statistical evidence for association across phenotypes does not by itself establish a biological pleiotropic mechanism. Existing methods differ in the level at which shared genetic effects are modelled, for example at the variant, gene, or group level, and in the null hypotheses they test. These modelling choices determine the types of pleiotropic signal that can be detected and strongly influence interpretation. Additional challenges arise from limitations inherited from single‐trait GWAS, including small effect sizes, extensive multiplicity, and limited power. Genetic effects may also differ in magnitude or direction across traits, further complicating inference. In addition, dependence among SNPs induced by linkage disequilibrium can destabilize estimation, particularly when local correlation is not adequately accounted for. Limited integration of gene‐level or pathway‐level biological information may further obscure pleiotropic structure.

Practical considerations are equally important. Computational scalability remains a major challenge in cross‐phenotype analysis. Although Bayesian methods are often feasible for a moderate number of traits, models that incorporate group structure or hierarchical dependence are typically far more computationally demanding. In particular, hierarchical spike and slab models are usually most practical for relatively small biological groups, reflecting a trade‐off between computational efficiency and richer biological representation.

Taken together, these issues highlight the need for statistical methods that more effectively represent genetic correlation, biological structure, and heterogeneity across phenotypes when analyzing cross‐phenotype associations. This tutorial has reviewed several widely used methods in cross‐phenotype association for pleiotropy research and has illustrated how successive methodological developments have addressed different aspects of this problem. Table 2 summarizes the methods discussed, including their required inputs, reported outputs, ability to accommodate effect direction, and key modelling assumptions.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Methods used to investigate cross‐phenotype association differ fundamentally in how they define the null and alternative hypotheses, and these choices directly determine what constitutes evidence for cross‐phenotype association. In particular, methods differ in whether evidence for potential pleiotropy is defined as any association with at least one trait or as joint association across multiple traits.

For ASSET, the null hypothesis states that a SNP is not associated with any of the traits under study. The alternative is that the SNP is associated with at least one subset of traits, with effects that may act in the same or opposite directions. ASSET implements this framework by searching across all possible trait subsets and identifying the subset providing the strongest evidence against the null. Statistical significance is then assessed using a p‐value adjusted for this subset search. Therefore, ASSET provides evidence for subset‐based association; interpretation as potential pleiotropic evidence requires examination of the selected subset, effect directions, and study context.

PLACO adopts a more restrictive composite null at the SNP level that is specifically designed to test potential pleiotropic effects between two traits. Under this null, a variant is associated with neither trait nor with only one trait, and the null is rejected only when the SNP is associated with both traits simultaneously. By basing inference on the product of the trait‐specific Z statistics, PLACO provides a direct and computationally efficient test of joint association, corresponding to a stricter statistical definition of pleiotropic association.

CPBayes uses a Bayesian global null that is conceptually closer to ASSET. The null corresponds to no association across all traits, whereas the alternative allows association with one or more traits. Because the alternative includes configurations in which only a single trait is associated, rejection of the global null does not by itself imply pleiotropy. Rather, pleiotropic interpretation depends on the posterior subset of associated traits inferred from the model.

GCPBayes extends CPBayes by introducing biologically defined group structure and allowing inference at both the group and variant levels. Although it shares the same global null formulation, its hierarchical spike‐based structure provides posterior summaries that distinguish single trait association from multi‐trait pleiotropic signal within a group. In this sense, GCPBayes permits a more explicit separation of group‐level association and group‐level pleiotropic evidence while retaining the flexibility of a Bayesian hierarchical model.

GPA provides a complementary perspective by modelling latent association states across traits, with optional incorporation of functional annotations to assess annotation enrichment. This framework is particularly useful for characterizing genome‐wide association patterns and prioritizing variants using posterior probabilities or local false discovery rates. Because GPA is based on marginal p‐values and latent classes, its output is best interpreted as evidence for latent cross‐trait association and annotation enrichment patterns, rather than direct evidence of directional pleiotropic effects.

Power and Error Control

Improving statistical power is a central motivation for methods investigating cross‐phenotype associations. In contrast to classical meta‐analysis, which combines studies of the same trait, these methods aim to exploit shared genetic information across multiple traits. Any gains in power therefore depend on assumptions about shared architecture, effect heterogeneity, and cross‐trait correlation. In addition, group‐based methods defined through genomic structure or functional annotation can improve efficiency by reducing the effective multiplicity and concentrating signal within biologically meaningful units.

Error control differs substantially across methods and closely reflects their underlying statistical framework. Frequentist approaches such as ASSET and PLACO rely on p‐value based testing and control error through the null distribution of their respective test statistics. By contrast, Bayesian methods such as CPBayes and GCPBayes report posterior quantities, including local false discovery rates and Bayes factors, that quantify evidence in probabilistic terms. GPA provides a related but distinct mixture modeling framework in which false discovery rates are controlled through posterior classification of SNPs and enrichment states.

Compared with strict family‐wise error control, false discovery rate‐based or posterior probability‐based criteria are generally less conservative and may improve sensitivity to modest effects. However, because these quantities have different interpretations, outputs from different cross‐phenotype association methods are not directly comparable. In particular, p‐values, local false discovery rates, Bayes factors, posterior probabilities, and global false discovery rates measure uncertainty in different ways. Although this limits formal comparisons of power across methods, comparing the sets of identified variants, traits, or groups remains informative for understanding their practical behavior.

Direction of Pleiotropic Effects

The direction of genetic effects adds a further layer of complexity to cross‐phenotype association analysis. Early GWAS approaches relied primarily on univariate SNP‐specific models, often combined through fixed‐effect meta‐analysis. Although effective for detecting marginal association, these approaches do not explicitly model effect direction across multiple traits and may therefore lose power when effects act in opposite directions.

Pleiotropic effects need not be directionally concordant across traits. A well‐known example is the rs334variant in the HBB gene, which causes sickle cell disease in homozygous carriers while conferring protection against severe malaria in heterozygous carriers [70]. More generally, when a variant has opposite effects on related phenotypes, methods that favor concordant effects may experience signal cancellation and reduced power.

Several pleiotropy methods explicitly address this issue. ASSET allows effects to act in the same or opposite directions through its subset‐based framework, while Bayesian approaches such as CPBayes and GCPBayes estimate trait‐specific posterior summaries within a joint model. By allowing positive and negative effects to be represented separately, these methods can preserve signals that may be masked under homogeneous effect assumptions.

PLACO occupies an intermediate position. It does not explicitly estimate trait‐specific directions in the same way as Bayesian models, but the sign of the product of the two Z statistics reflects whether the observed effects are concordant or discordant across traits. In practice, however, PLACO is primarily a test of joint association rather than a full directional modelling framework.

Correlation in Pleiotropy Models

Correlation in GWAS summary statistics is a major challenge for cross‐phenotype association analysis. Correlation across traits may arise from overlapping samples, shared controls, or intrinsically correlated phenotypes, and it can affect both test statistics and their interpretation. Methods that ignore such dependence may exhibit inflated type I error or reduced sensitivity.

Several methods explicitly account for cross‐trait dependence. ASSET and PLACO incorporate covariance information in their test statistics, and PLACO additionally uses a decorrelation step when correlation is present. Bayesian methods such as CPBayes can incorporate non‐diagonal covariance structures for the summary statistics, thereby allowing dependence among trait‐specific effect estimates to be modeled directly.

A distinct but related source of dependence arises from linkage disequilibrium, which induces correlation among SNPs within genomic regions. This feature is especially relevant for gene‐based or group‐based analyses, where multiple correlated variants may contribute to the same signal. GCPBayes partially addresses this issue by modelling grouped SNP effects within genes or pathways, but when only standard summary statistics are available, within‐group covariance must still be approximated or reconstructed rather than directly observed. Developing summary statistic‐based methods that more accurately incorporate local LD structure therefore remains an important area for future work.

Subgroup and Hierarchical Structures

In addition to modeling dependence, cross‐phenotype association analyses can benefit from incorporating subgroup and hierarchical structure within GWAS data. Grouping SNPs into genes, pathways, or annotated functional categories provides a principled way to represent biologically meaningful structure and may improve both detection and interpretation of pleiotropic signals.

Such subgroup structure may be defined in several ways. One approach is genomic, where SNPs are grouped according to physical location or gene membership. Another is functional, where pathways, gene sets, or regulatory annotations are used to represent shared biological processes. Although these definitions differ, they often overlap in practice, and both can enhance inference by concentrating signal within biologically relevant units.

GPA illustrates one way to incorporate subgroup information through functional annotation. Rather than testing variant‐level joint association directly, GPA models enrichment of associated SNPs within annotated categories, thereby providing insight into genome‐wide pleiotropic architecture. GCPBayes offers a more explicit hierarchical framework by combining group‐level and SNP‐level inference within a unified Bayesian model. This distinguishes it from single SNP methods such as ASSET, CPBayes, and PLACO, while retaining variant‐level localization within biologically defined groups.

Beyond Bayesian hierarchical models, frequentist penalized methods have also been developed to incorporate group structure. In particular, the recently proposed MPSG approach [21] uses penalized multivariate meta‐analysis with sparse group selection to jointly identify relevant variants and genes. By relying on an alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm rather than MCMC, MPSG provides a computationally efficient alternative to GCPBayes while retaining structured modelling of genetic information. A full tutorial treatment of MPSG is beyond the scope of this review, but the method illustrates an important recent development in structured pleiotropy analysis.

Computational Efficiency

Computational efficiency is an important practical consideration when selecting a pleiotropy detection method, particularly in large‐scale analyses involving many traits or variants. Marked differences in computational cost arise from the underlying inferential framework and from the extent to which biological structure is modelled explicitly.

ASSET and PLACO are generally among the most computationally efficient methods, as they rely on analytic test statistics and closed‐form approximations. GPA is also relatively efficient, since it operates on genome‐wide p‐values and uses expectation maximization for parameter estimation. These methods are therefore well suited to large‐scale analyses. Bayesian methods such as CPBayes typically require more computation because they rely on MCMC sampling, although they remain feasible for a moderate number of traits [19]. Computational cost increases substantially when group level or hierarchical structure is introduced. GCPBayes illustrates this trade off clearly: the continuous spike and Dirac spike versions are relatively efficient, whereas the hierarchical spike model is considerably more demanding because it jointly estimates group level and SNP level effects within a richer prior structure [20]. As a result, hierarchical spike models are most practical for analyses based on relatively small biological groups.

Overall, methods that explicitly model subgroup or hierarchical structure incur greater computational cost but offer improved biological interpretability and modelling flexibility. Method selection therefore depends on the balance between scalability and the degree of biological detail required for the application.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite substantial progress, several challenges remain in the detection and interpretation of cross‐phenotype association signals and potential pleiotropic effects from GWAS data. One major issue is local genomic correlation induced by LD, whereby correlated variants within a region can generate apparent cross‐phenotype associations that do not correspond to a shared causal effect. Many methods still rely primarily on marginal summary statistics and therefore cannot fully distinguish true shared causality from correlated proxy signals.

This limitation is compounded by simplifying assumptions about covariance structure. Diagonal covariance approximations, which use only marginal variance estimates such as squared standard errors, ignore dependence among SNPs arising from LD and may affect efficiency and downstream inference [20, 21]. More accurate reconstruction of covariance matrices from summary statistics may use ancestry‐matched external LD reference panels, such as 1000 Genomes or the Exome Sequencing Project [71, 72, 73]. However, the performance of these approaches depends on factors including reference panel size, ancestry matching, variant overlap, allele harmonization, and the stability of the estimated LD matrix. Developing and evaluating practical covariance reconstruction strategies therefore remains important, particularly for assessing the sensitivity of downstream inference to diagonal and LD‐informed covariance inputs.

The methods illustrated in this tutorial were applied primarily in a common variant setting. With the growing availability of low‐frequency and rare variants from densely imputed GWAS, whole exome sequencing, and whole genome sequencing, extending cross‐phenotype association analyses to these variants is increasingly important. Because single variant tests are often underpowered for rare variants, rare variant association studies commonly rely on gene‐ or region‐based aggregation tests, including burden tests and variance component approaches such as SKAT and SKAT‐O [74, 75]. This aggregation framework is conceptually related to the use of biologically defined groups in approaches such as GCPBayes, although GCPBayes was not specifically developed for rare variant analysis. Several methods have been developed for cross‐phenotype rare variant analysis, including GAMuT [76], MSKAT [77], Multi‐SKAT [78], and the summary statistic based MTAR method [79]. These methods differ in their statistical frameworks, phenotype assumptions, and data requirements. Implementing them would require a dedicated rare variant workflow, including appropriate variant coverage, variant grouping, frequency‐based filtering and weighting, and sequencing or densely imputed data. Extending the present comparison to rare and low‐frequency variant methods is therefore a valuable direction for future work.

The application is intended as an illustrative example rather than a discovery study or benchmark success case. For this purpose, we used previously published breast and thyroid cancer data that have also been used in related methodological applications [20, 57, 58]. The results should therefore be interpreted as examples of how different cross‐phenotype association methods can be implemented and compared in practice, rather than as evidence of novel breast or thyroid cancer susceptibility loci. Although no variant reached the Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold, the application remains useful for demonstrating each method's output structure and the importance of interpreting results in relation to its inferential target. The absence of multiple‐testing‐significant SNP‐level findings may reflect factors including modest shared effects, the available sample sizes, the restricted SNP panel, and the stringent correction threshold. It does not preclude the presence of distributed gene‐level signals, because multivariate gene‐level analyses can combine information across multiple variants, and previous applications have reported statistically significant group‐level cross‐phenotype associations [20, 21].

Although the present illustration considers two independent binary traits, it provides a focused and accessible setting for demonstrating method implementation and output interpretation. ASSET is particularly advantageous in larger multi‐trait settings, where a broader range of phenotype subsets can be evaluated, and correlation arising from related traits, shared controls, or sample overlap can be incorporated. More broadly, except for PLACO, the methods considered in this tutorial can accommodate more than two traits, although these wider multi‐trait settings are not illustrated in the present application.

Hierarchical Bayesian approaches such as GCPBayes offer flexible modelling of pleiotropic effects across genes and variants, but their computational burden has historically limited large‐scale application. Recent developments have addressed this in part through dedicated pipelines that automate preprocessing, support parallel computation, and enable genome‐wide implementation in practice [21, 53]. Even so, scalability remains an important consideration for methods that model biological hierarchy explicitly.

A further challenge is the lack of harmonized decision criteria across Bayesian methods. Posterior means, medians, credible intervals, local false discovery rates, and Bayes factors may all be used to identify potential pleiotropic signals, but these summaries reflect different inferential goals and are not directly comparable. Greater standardization in reporting and interpretation would improve comparability across studies.

Finally, empirical applications remain uneven across phenotypic domains. Many studies have focused on a relatively narrow range of traits, particularly neurological and psychiatric disorders [10, 11, 69]. Although more recent large‐scale analyses have begun to examine pleiotropy across thousands of traits [80], many still rely on overlap of published associations rather than methods that explicitly model shared genetic effects. As biobank‐scale resources and cross‐consortium collaborations continue to expand, cross‐phenotype association methods and pleiotropy‐focused approaches have substantial potential to improve discovery and reveal biological relationships beyond those detectable by single‐trait analyses. We hope that this tutorial will contribute to their broader and more informed use in cross‐phenotype research.
