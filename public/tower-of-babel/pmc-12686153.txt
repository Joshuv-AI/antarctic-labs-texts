Racial disparities in ovarian cancer survival transcend the somatic mutational landscape
Authors: Robert D Morgan, George J Burghel
Published: 2025

Ovarian cancer is the leading cause of gynaecological cancer-related death in high-income countries (1). The commonest and most lethal subtype of ovarian cancer is high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), which accounts for around 70% of all malignant tumours of the ovary and has a 5-year survival rate of between 35% and 40% (2). Population-level data have shown that Black women diagnosed with ovarian cancer have worse overall survival than White women (3). Black women are also underrepresented in published cohorts of molecularly tested HGSC cases (4). Identifying molecular characteristics specific to HGSC diagnosed in Black women may improve prognostication and facilitate precision medicine.

To understand differences in molecular characteristics between HGSCs diagnosed in Black women and White women, Lawson-Michod et al. performed a comprehensive study reported in Cancer Research (5). The group tested 334 patients diagnosed with HGSC, of whom 211 had self-reported as Black women and 123 as White women. Patients were aged between 20 and 79 years old and had been diagnosed in North America between 1999 and 2015. Over 80% of participants had been diagnosed with advanced-stage HGSC and over 80% had received upfront primary surgery. The researchers performed molecular analyses to determine differences in significantly mutated genes, Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) mutational signatures and gene expression subtypes between racial groups. No survival data were presented. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset was used an additional, separate control group (6).

There were several interesting findings from the study. The prevalence of oncogenic KRAS variants in Black women was higher than in White women. However, the prevalence of KRAS variants in both racial groups was low (≤2%) and none of the identified variants included the targetable p.Gly12Cys missense mutation (7). Secondly, HGSCs in Black women were more likely to harbour a variant in the OBSCN compared to White women (P<0.01). OBSCN encodes for a giant protein called obscurin that may have a role as a tumour suppressor gene in breast and pancreatic carcinogenesis (8). It is notable that many of the OBSCN variants reported by Lawson-Michod et al. were ‘silent’ mutations and therefore may not have impacted protein function. Further research is required to understand the role of obscurin in HGSC, particularly in the immunoreactive subtype. Finally, Lawson-Michod et al. showed that HGSCs in Black women were more likely to have a higher median aneuploidy score (P<0.05) and higher occurrence of chromosome 22q deletion (P<0.05) versus White women, suggesting subtle differences in chromosomal instability between racial groups.

However, what stands out in this study is how molecularly similar HGSCs are in Black women and White women. Indeed, the frequency of key genomic hallmarks such as somatic TP53 variants, low tumour mutational burden and genome-wide copy-number alternation was similar in each group. Moreover, the prevalence of PARP-1/2 inhibitor-actionable genomic features such as germline and somatic BRCA1/2 variants and homologous recombination deficiency was also similar in each group. The authors should be commended on their attempts to understand the driving forces behind differences in ovarian cancer survival in racial groups at the molecular level, however overall, the study highlights that these differences are probably more likely due to other factors that transcend the somatic mutational landscape of this disease, such as social determinants of health. Further research is required to understand how these factors are interconnected so we can develop healthcare systems that provide equity in access, provision and quality, whilst striving for parity of outcomes.
