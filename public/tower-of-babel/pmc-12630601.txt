Assessment of cat exposure to phthalates through phthalate metabolites analysis in the hair samples
Authors: Slawomir Gonkowski, Manolis Tzatzarakis, Elena Vakonaki, Thomas Lamprakis, Krystyna Makowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phthalates are substances used in industry which are harmful to humans and animals. Cats and dogs, living in close proximity to humans, are particularly exposed to the effects of phthalates. Although hair samples seem to be the best matrix for studies on long-term exposure to phthalates, no such studies have been conducted in cats to date. This study aimed to establish concentration levels of selected phthalate metabolites (i.e. monomethyl phthalate-MMP, monoethyl phthalate-MEP, mono-isobutyl phthalate-MiBP, monobutyl phthalate-MBP, monobenzyl phthalate-MBzP, mono-cyclohexyl phthalate-MCHP and mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate-MEHP) in cat hair samples using liquid chromatography with mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. MiBP and MEHP were found in 100% of samples with median 66.6 pg/mg and 35.9 pg/mg, respectively, MBP in 97.1% with median 55.7 pg/mg, MEP in 94.3% with median 152.0 pg/mg, MMP in 85.7% with median < limit of quantification (LOQ) and MBzP in 32.9% with median < limit of detection (LOD). MCHP has not been found in cat hair samples during this study. The study has shown that cats are exposed to phthalates. This exposure is higher in indoor cats and depends on the body condition. On the other hand, statistically significant gender- and age-dependent differences in phthalate metabolites levels have not been found during this study. The results have also shown that hair samples may be used as a matrix during studies on levels of phthalate metabolites in cats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24678-1.

Introduction

Phthalates are organic synthetic substances that are esters of 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid. Phthalates are characterized by elasticity, durability and temperature stability and due to these properties, they are commonly used in the industry1. Phthalates are mainly used as plasticizers in the production of synthetic materials. The use of phthalates depends on their chemical structure. High molecular phthalates, which contain 7–13 carbon atoms in the carbon chain are used among others in the production of toys, cables, electronic elements, office supplies and household items2. In turn, low molecular weight phthalates that contain 3–6 carbon atoms in the carbon chain are often included in paints, varnishes cosmetics and PCV products3. It is estimated that above five million tons of phthalates are produced annually in the world1,2.

Such widespread use of phthalates in industry and the fact that they can be released from plastics make these substances one of the most important compounds polluting the natural environment4. The presence of phthalates has been described in natural environment almost everywhere, in all continents (even in Antarctica)2,5. Their presence has been confirmed in the surface water, both in the seas and inland waters, soil, plants and air6,7. Phthalates are also frequently noted in various types of food and drinking water, into which they penetrate from plastic packaging and bottles1,2.

It is also known that phthalates can get into the bodies of people and animals mainly through the digestive tract, but also through respiratory system, transdermal penetration and placenta during prenatal period2,8,9. Phthalates have multidirectional harmful effects. They may act as hormonal disruptors and affect among others reproductive, digestive and neuronal systems2,10. Previous studies have also described genotoxic and carcinogenic properties of phthalates11,12. Due to the mentioned above toxic qualities of phthalates, monitoring of human and animal exposure to these substances appears to be an important issue in environmental toxicology. It is known that phthalates in human and animal organisms quickly undergo transformation consisting of hydrolysis, oxidation, and glucuronidation2. Therefore, the monitoring of human and animal exposure to these substances includes not only determining the level of phthalates, but also their metabolites.

The main matrix for studies on the levels of phthalate metabolites in living organisms is urine, which results from the fact that the main route of elimination of these substances from the body is the excretory system13. Moreover, phthalate metabolites have also been found in the blood serum, saliva, semen, breast milk and nails in humans1,14–16. It should be pointed out that hair is also an important matrix in studies on the levels of phthalate metabolites17,18. This is because the levels of phthalate metabolites in the hair are not subject to short-term fluctuations and therefore reflect long-term environmental exposure of organism. Moreover, hair sampling is easy, non-invasive, painless and stress free which is particularly important in timid animals. The hair is also material, which can be easily stored and transported. It is also known that in living organism’s phthalate metabolites may undergo metabolic processes consisting in conjugation with sulfate group or glucuronic acid. The knowledge about the occurrence of conjugated phthalate metabolites in the hair is limited, but it has been proven that glucuronide-conjugated phthalate metabolites do not occur in the hair18. Therefore, during the preparation of hair samples for the determination of phthalate metabolite levels, deconjugation is not necessary, unlike urine samples, where the deconjugation of the glucuronide metabolites to free phthalate metabolites by β-glucuronidase addition is the part of the standard protocol for phthalate metabolite analysis18.

The majority of previous studies on the levels of phthalate metabolites in the living organism concern humans and wild animals1,16,19,20. On the other hand, it is known that companion animals, which live in the immediate vicinity of humans and therefore are highly exposed to anthropogenic adverse substances polluting the environment. The main sources of such exposure are food, toys and training devices, especially since in the production of items for animals, unlike products for humans, there are usually no restrictions on the use of plasticizers that have harmful effects21,22. Moreover, companion animals, especially cats, spend a major part of their life at home. This fact together with their rather small size, causes pets to be particularly vulnerable to indoor dust, which main contain anthropogenic pollutants including, among others, phthalates21. Some authors claim that cat exposure to adverse substances may be the mirror of human exposure connected with the influence of indoor dust and therefore cats are considered as a sentinel species for the evaluation of human exposure to indoor contamination21,23. However, until recently issues related to companion animals’ exposure to phthalates and the role of phthalates as a potential reason for pathological states in pets were marginalized in veterinary toxicology. Therefore, the knowledge about the degree of exposure of pets to these substances is extremely limited and previous studies on this issue are not numerous. Till now phthalate metabolites have been found in the urine and feces of cats and dogs living in the USA24,25. In turn phthalates has been described in the serum of cats living in Paris21 and testes of dogs from various regions of United Kingdom, Denmark and Finland26.

Taking the above into account, the aim of this study is to determine for the first time the levels of selected phthalate metabolites in cat hair. The study included six primary metabolites of short alkyl chain phthalates and one metabolite of long alkyl chain phthalate, and included monomethyl phthalate (MMP), monoethyl phthalate (MEP), mono-isobutyl phthalate (MiBP), monobutyl phthalate (MBP), monobenzyl phthalate (MBzP), mono-cyclohexyl phthalate (MCHP) and mono (2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP) (Table 1). The choice of cats for research is not accidental. In the light of previous studies, it is known that cats, living in close proximity to humans, accumulate anthropogenic pollutants connected with indoor environment. Moreover, cats (unlike for example dogs) cannot metabolize the majority of these pollutants and therefore, they are more sensitive to them than other species27,28. Cats are considered as a sentinel species for the evaluation of human exposure to indoor contamination. For this reason, cats are a good sentinel species of human environment pollution with harmful organic substances21,23,27,28. Another important issue is the fact that some symptoms of hormonal disorders in cats that may result from the action of the endocrine disrupting chemicals (e.g. symptoms of thyroid diseases) are very similar to the symptoms occurring in humans27. It should also be emphasized that the levels of phthalate metabolites in cat hair have not been determined so far, even though the hair is the best matrix for assessing long-term exposure and can be easily and non-invasively sampled. Moreover, till now the exposure of cats to phthalates in Poland has not been studied at all. Therefore, results obtained during the present study will contribute to the development of knowledge about the exposure of pets to phthalates and roles of these compounds in veterinary toxicology.

Results

A majority of phthalate metabolites included into this study were observed in cat hair samples (Table 2). Concentration levels and frequency of detection clearly depended on the type of substance. Moreover, clear differences in concentration of phthalate metabolites were noted between particular animals included into research (Table 2, Table S1). Phthalate metabolites were found in the majority of the hair samples studied. Only one sample (no 56) had all analytes studied below the LOD/LOQ (Table S1).

Among compounds included into this study the highest frequency of occurrence was found in the case of MiBP and MEHP. Concentration levels of both these substances were higher than LOD in all samples studied. Moreover, the levels of MiBP were significantly higher than MEHP, and mean value amounted to 130.0 ± 168.4 pg/mg (median 70.4 pg/mg) and 59.3 ± 71.4 pg/mg (median 39.8 pg/mg) in the case of MiBP and MEHP, respectively (Table 2). A slightly lower frequency of occurrence was found in the case of MBP and MEP. Their concentration levels higher than LOD were observed in 97.1% and 94.3% of samples, respectively. MEP concentration level with mean 258.0 ± 418.2 pg/mg (median 152.0 pg/mg) and maximum concentration at the level of 2523.4 pg/mg was the highest among all substances studied (Table 2). Other substances were less common in the studied hair samples and were observed in lower concentration levels. MMP concentration levels above LOD were observed in 85.7% of samples and its mean amounted to 19.5 ± 27.0 pg/mg (median < LOQ). In the case of MBzP levels above LOD were observed in 32.9% samples with mean value 11.9 ± 44.2 pg/mg and median below LOD. In turn, MCHP was the only substance included into the study, whose level did not exceed LOD value in any of the analyzed samples (Table 2, Table S1).

In the case of phthalate metabolites whose levels above LOQ were observed in above 50% of samples (i.e. MEP, MiBP, MBP and MEHP), additional studies were performed. Namely, the levels of these substances were compared between animals of various genders, of various ages, with different body conditions and between outdoor and indoor cats.

In males the mean levels (± SD) amounted to 281.6 ± 444.3pg/mg for MEP, 135.1 ± 160.8 pg/mg for MiBP, 85.1 ± 85.1 pg/mg for MBP and 56.2 ± 56.4 pg/mg for MEHP. In the case of MEP and MiBP mean concentration levels noted in females were slightly lower than in males and achieved 231.0 ± 392.0 pg/mg and 124.1 ± 179.0 pg/mg, respectively. In turn, mean concentration levels of MBP and MEHP in females were slightly higher than in males and amounted to 119.1 ± 186.6 pg/mg for MBP and 62.73 ± 85.91 pg/mg for MEHP. However, intragender differences noted in this study were not statistically significant in any case (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The results of above-mentioned studies clearly indicate that cats are exposed to phthalates, and phthalate metabolites are present in cat hair. It should be pointed out that despite undeniable advantages of hair as a matrix to studies on human and animal exposure to environmental matrices (such as easy and non-invasive collection and most accurate reflection of long-term exposure)29,30, it is rarely used for phthalate metabolites biomonitoring. The majority of previous studies on phthalate metabolites in hair concern humans (Table 3).

By comparing present results with previous observations on humans (Table 3) it should be concluded that cat hair contains the same phthalate metabolites as human hair, and levels of these substances are generally similar. This observation confirms that companion animals are exposed to the same pollutants as humans, which is mainly due to living in the same environment34,35. Of course, some differences in phthalate metabolites levels between cat and human hair are visible. Higher phthalate metabolites levels in the human hair noted in some cases (Table 3) may result from the fact that phthalates are anthropogenic pollutants and many sources of human exposure to these substances are irrelevant when it comes to animals. Examples of such sources known to be associated with phthalates exposure may include lifestyle, use of electronic devices or cars, clothes, various types of food or professional work resulting in occupational exposure1,2,36.

The next reason of observed differences may relate to the fact that pollution of the natural environment with phthalates clearly depends on the region, where studies have been performed and first of all it is connected with the degree of urbanization and industrialization and/or human population density1,2. Extreme differences between regions of the world are also visible in phthalate metabolites levels in human hair (Table 3). Therefore, it can be supposed that such interregional differences also concern cat exposure, and differences between the present results and previous studies on phthalate metabolites in human hair mainly result not from differences between the exposure of humans and companion animals to phthalates but from various degrees of environmental pollution with phthalates in various regions. This is evidenced by the fact that the values ​​observed in this study are much higher or lower than the values ​​observed in people in various parts of the world (Table 3). For example, MEHP levels noted in the present study are extremely higher than those noted in human hair Hongkong31, but clearly lower than levels noted in Taiwan18.

According to the best knowledge of the authors, the only study on phthalate metabolites in animal hair has been performed on wild boars33. By comparing present results with phthalate metabolites levels in wild boars (Table 3) it should be concluded that generally these levels are higher in cats. Probably it is connected with the fact that companion animals live in the immediate vicinity of humans and therefore are more vulnerable to anthropogenic pollutants. However, levels of some phthalate metabolites (MMP or MEHP) were higher in wild boars (Table 3). This could be due to various sources of exposure to phthalates in companion and wild animals. In domestic animals this exposure is connected mainly with packaged commercial food, animal cosmetics, care utensils, contact with humans, indoor dust and objects found in the house21,22,37. In turn, wild animals’ exposure results from pollution of natural environment with the industrial and municipal wastewater, landfills and smoke from industrial plants and households as well as agricultural activity33.

Previous studies on phthalates and phthalate metabolites in companion animals are extremely limited (Table 4).

In addition to the studies ​​presented in the Table 4, the presence of DEHP in testicles of dogs in UK, Denmark and Finland have also been described, but the authors did not provide specific levels of this substance in their work26.

The data presented above (Table 4) together with the present study indicate that companion animals are exposed to phthalates and this exposure may be evaluated through analysis of phthalates and their metabolites in various matrices. However, the comparison of present results with previous observations in dogs or cats is impossible. Firstly, previous studies have been performed in other regions that the current research, and interregional differences in companion animals’ exposure to phthalates are clearly visible (Table 4). Secondly, previous studies were performed on a completely different matrix, and it is relatively well known that concentration of substances in blood, urine or feces reflect short-term exposure, while levels in hair reflects long-term exposure of the organism, that has taken place over a long period of time18,30. Moreover, it is known that levels of substances in blood or urine do not exactly corelate with levels in the hair38. This situation results from the fact that phthalates and their metabolites are quickly eliminated from serum and urine, while in the hair these substances accumulate. This phenomenon is best explained with the following example. Let’s assume that the animal has not been exposed to phthalates in its environment for a long time (several weeks). Then, there was contact with phthalates in high concentrations, e.g. through consumption of heavily contaminated water or food. A few hours after exposure, serum, urine and hair were collected for testing. The levels of phthalates and/or their metabolites in serum and urine will be high, but in the hair much lower, because the substances have not had time to accumulate in the hair. Levels of phthalates or their metabolites in hair will reflect the long-term environmental exposure to phthalates, while levels in the serum or urine – short-term exposure connected with feeding. The opposite situation can be observed, when the animal has been exposed to phthalates (e.g. in food) for a long time. The factor contributing to this exposure was temporarily eliminated, and after a few hours or a day, the analysis was performed. Then the levels of phthalates or phthalate metabolites in the urine and serum will be low and may mislead analysts into thinking that animal is not exposed to phthalates. However, analysis of hair samples will show high levels of these substances and prove that exposure to these substances exists. Therefore, the analysis of the hair reflects long-term environmental exposure to substances polluting the environment.

In spite of the fact that some previous observations on humans have described differences in phthalate metabolites levels depending on gender or age39–41, during the present study such statistically differences have not been found. It is in accordance with observations on cat urine samples, where intragender differences in phthalate metabolites were also not statistically significant24. In the light of previous studies, it can be assumed that intragender differences in levels of phthalate metabolites and other endocrine disruptors in humans are connected with various lifestyles, habits (for example with more frequent use of cosmetics by women), i.e. from factors that are irrelevant in animals1,40,41. In turn, differences between people of different ages are explained by various hormonal activity, metabolic rate and/or diversities in the type of diet1,42. It is possible that the lack of differences in the level of phthalate metabolites between cats of various genders and ages observed in this study is due to the fact that all animals included in the research were castrated or sterilized, which influenced their levels of sex hormones.

During the present study some correlations between phthalate metabolites levels and age of animals have been noted, but these differences were not statistically significant. Previous studies on humans are inconclusive. The levels of majority of phthalate metabolites are higher in children compared to adults and elderly people, but some studies have reported lower levels of certain substances in children in comparison to older persons39,42–44. Moreover, previous studies on cat urine samples have shown that total phthalate metabolites levels increased with increasing age24. The reasons for these age-dependent differences are not entirely clear. It can be assumed that they are caused by differences in metabolism, hormonal activity, body weight and intestinal absorption as well as the amount and type of food42,43. Moreover, in humans, the amount and type of cosmetics and toys used by children are also important39,44.

During the present study differences in phthalate metabolites and cat body conditions have been visible. The average age (± SD) of thinner cats, cats with normal weight and cats with obesity amounted to 4.2 ± 3.8 years, 4.8 ± 3.7 years and 6.8 ± 2.5 years, but it should be noted that there were no statistically significant differences in the age of cats between these three groups. Therefore, observed differences between groups were not due to differences in the age of the animals. Generally lower levels of substances studied have been found in thinner animals, but differences were statistically significant only in the case of MEP. On the one hand, thinner cats consume less food, which, as previous research indicate, may be an important source of animal exposure not only to phthalates but also to other endocrine active substances polluting the environment21,22. On the other hand, exposure to phthalates may cause obesity, what results from alterations in the endocrine system induced by these substances, and what has been described both in humans and laboratory animals45–47. However, the lack of clear statistically significant differences in phthalate metabolites levels between cats with physiological body weight and obese cats observed in this study did not confirm correlations between phthalate exposure and obesity in cats.

The present results have also indicated that the lifestyle of cats may affect the level of exposure to phthalates. Levels of phthalate metabolites were generally higher in indoor cats, although statistical significance was found only in the case of MEP. These observations confirm previous suggestions that indoor dust and contact with elements of home environment (carpets, furniture, blankets, human clothes etc.) may be (besides commercial food) the important source of cat exposure to phthalates48. It should be pointed out that cats may be exposed to pollutants in the indoor dust not only through the respiratory system or skin but also through the ingestion, for example during their licking habits and/or biting various objects21.

An important question remains whether the levels of phthalate metabolites observed in this study may negatively impact the health status of cats. Unfortunately, the answer to this question is not possible in the current state of knowledge. So far, the effects of phthalates on the health of cats have not been studied. Moreover, the metabolism of phthalates in these animals is not fully studied, and therefore it is not known how the levels of phthalate metabolites in the hair translate into the levels of these compounds in the internal organs. On the other hand, previous experimental studies have indicated that even low doses of phthalates may negatively affect the living organisms49,50. In the light of previous studies conducted mainly on laboratory animals or cell cultures and epidemiological observations on humans it is relatively well known that phthalates have multi-directional harmful effects51–53. In the reproductive system phthalates cause morphological changes and functioning disturbances of Sertoli and Leydig cells, contributing to the reduction of the sperm count and motility52. In females phthalates induce defects in folliculogenesis and may cause reproductive cycle disorders, infertility and ovarian failure54. Moreover, phthalates increase the risk of developing pregnancy complications, fetal developmental disorders and miscarriage55,56. Phthalates also have strong neurotoxic effects. They affect both brain and peripheral nervous system, contributing to neuroinflammation, neuropathies, neurodegenerative diseases, developmental disorders of the nervous system and behavioral disorders57,58. It is also known that phthalates, among others, have an adverse effect on the cardiovascular system59, liver60, kidneys61 and immune system62. Previous studies have also shown cytotoxic, genotoxic and carcinogenic activity of phthalates, as well as relationships between exposure to these compounds and obesity and diabetes11,12,63,64.

Taking the above into account, it cannot be excluded that phthalate metabolites levels noted in the present study may be connected with the negative influence on cat status health. However, further comprehensive research clarifying the exact impact of phthalates on the health status of cats is necessary.

Materials and methods

Reagents

During the present study, the following reagents have been used: phthalate metabolites, MMP, MBP, MCHP and MEHP purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis MO, USA), MEP, MiBP, MBZP from Toronto Research Chemicals (TRC Inc, Canada), methanol and acetonitrile (LC–MS grade) purchased from Fisher Chemical, phenobarbital d5 (internal standard—IS) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, ultrapure water produced by a Direct-Q 3UV water purification system (Merck, Germany).

Sample collection

Hair samples were collected from 70 not purebred private owners’ cats of both gender (37 males and 33 females) and various ages (from 1 year to 14 years) living in Olsztyn – the city placed in north-eastern Poland with about 170 000 inhabitants. All animals included in the study were clinically healthy and castrated/sterilized. Animals were divided, according to the recommendation of the International Cat Care organization into junior cats (< 2 years), adult cats (from 2 to 6 years) and mature and senior cats (> 6)65. This division is related to the changes that take place in the cat’s body. Namely, to the second year of life, the cat’s body matures, grows and develops. After the age of 2 (in adult cats) habits and metabolic processes change. Another clear change in metabolism and habits occurs after the age of 6 years, when aging processes start65. Moreover, body condition assessment of each animal was performed according to international cat Body Condition Score system66 and animals were divided into too thin (BCS 1–4), cats with correct weight (BCS 5), and overweight (BCS 6–9). Additionally, information was obtained from the owners whether the cat has outdoor access or not (outdoor and indoor cats). According to information obtained from the owners, all cats included in the study were fed commercial cat food, both dry and wet, and used various types of plastic toys. Characterization of animals included into the study is presented in supplementary materials - Table S2.

Hair samples were collected in October and November 2023. In all animals, samples weighing about 2 g were taken from the abdomen and hair was cut right close to the skin. Immediately after sampling hair was put into small glass containers and stored in the room temperature in dark until further analysis. Due to the fact that hair samples collection was completely noninvasive and painless and was performed during care or veterinary treatments present investigations did not require consent of the Ethical Committee, what it is in agreement with the Act for the Protection of Animals for Scientific or Educational Purposes of 15 January 2015 (Official Journal of Laws of the Republic of Poland 2015, item 266). Simultaneously, the owners of animals were informed about studies and gave verbal consent to sample collection.

Extraction of compounds from the hair samples

Preparation of the hair samples were performed using the methodology developed previously by Tzatzarakis et al. (2015)67 and Gonkowski et al. (2024)33. Initially the hair was cut into small pieces measuring a few millimeters. Then external contaminations were removed from the hair surface through double rising in ultrapure water and double rising in methanol. Then samples were dried at 50 °C. The extraction of phthalate metabolites was performed as follows: 100 mg of each sample, 2 × 2 ml of methanol and 25 ng of IS were put into glass screw tubes (which previously were washed with methanol and dried at 80 °C to avoid external contaminations) and extracted in an ultrasonic water bath for 2 × 2 h. The temperature of the ultrasonic bath was monitoring in order to not exceed 50 °C. Then the extracts were separated, mixed and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen steam at 35 °C and subjected to reconstitution through adding 300 µl of methanol. Next, the resulting solution were put into 2 mL vials with inserts for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis and 3 µL of it was injected into the system.

Instrumentation

A liquid chromatography -triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) 8060 system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used during analysis. A Shimadzu SP-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 μm) was used to analyte separation at a temperature of 30 °C. The analysis was performed with a flow rate of 0.28 mL/min using a 5 mM ammonium acetate gradient as solvent A and acetonitrile 0.1% formic acid as solvent B, starting at 15% solvent B stable for 2.5 min, raised to 95% solvent B (2.5–19 min), and finally at 15% solvent B (19–23). The quantitative and qualitative analysis of phthalate metabolites was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to track precursor-product ion transitions (ESI-). The nebulizing gas flow, drying gas flow and heating gas flow were step up 2.5 L/min, 10 L/min and 10 L/min, respectively. Interface, desolvation, desolvation line (DL) and heat block temperatures were set up at 300 °C, 526 °C, 300 °C, 200 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Method validation

The performance of the analytical method was examined. Standard solutions of compounds studied (at levels 0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 100, 250 ng/ml) were made and their linearity was found to be from 0.9906 for MMP to 0.9992 for MiBP (n = 3). Spiked sample analysis was made for concentrations of 0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 250 and 500 pg/mg with linearity from 0.9901 for MCHP to 0.9995 for MMP (n = 4).

The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were evaluated using the standard error and the slope of the calibration curves. Four repeats of spiked samples (n = 4) at all tested concentration levels were used for the evaluation of the recovery and the accuracy of the method and three repeats for evaluation of the inter-day precision (%RSD). Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics, non-parametric Mann–Whitney test for a comparison of two groups of animals and a Kruskal–Wallis test for the comparison of three groups were used during the present study. In the statistical analysis values below LOQ were considered as LOQ/2 and values below LOD as LOD/2. Differences were considered statistically significant at P-value < 0.05.

Conclusion

The present study has proven that various phthalate metabolites are present in cat hair samples. Their presence indicated that cats are exposed to phthalates polluting the environment to a relatively high extent. It relates to the fact that cats live in the immediate vicinity of humans and are strongly exposed to anthropogenic pollutants. This is the first study on long- term cat exposure to phthalates through hair samples analysis. Hair is a matrix, the collection of which is painless and stress-free, which is important in timid or aggressive animals. This study has shown that hair analysis may be used in evaluation of phthalate metabolites levels in cats.

During the present study some correlations between body conditions and lifestyle of cats and phthalate metabolites have been found. Higher levels of phthalate metabolites in indoor cats indicate that the indoor dust and home items may be an important source of cat exposure to phthalates. On the other hand, contrary to some previous observations on humans, gender- and age-dependent differences in phthalate metabolite levels in cat hair noted in the present study were not statistically significant. Final confirmation of these results requires further biomonitoring studies on a larger group of animals and/or or other (perhaps more accurate) division of animals into different age groups in future studies.

Due to the fact that the exact metabolism of phthalates in cats, as well as influence of phthalates on cat’s organism are unknown, the precise evaluation of correlation between phthalate metabolites levels noted in this study and cats’ health status is impossible. A clarification of these issues require further comprehensive research including analysis of correlations between the levels of phthalate metabolites in various samples collected from the same animal (such as serum, urine, feces and hair) and the levels of selected hormones, hematological parameters, liver enzymes, as well as the occurrence of diseases and metabolic disorders. Only such comprehensive research conducted on a very large animal population will fully explain the real impact of phthalates on cat health status.
