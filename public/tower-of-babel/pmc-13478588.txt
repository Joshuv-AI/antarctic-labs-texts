Validation of the modified transplant symptom occurrence and symptom distress scale and exploring symptom burden in Brazilian kidney transplant recipients
Authors: Niels L. Riemersma, Tim J. Knobbe, Renata M. C. Guedes, Juliana G. Ferreira, Luciana A. Cabanelas, Kris Denhaerynck, Sabina de Geest, Helady Sanders-Pinheiro, José M. Pestana, Renato D. Foresto, Stephan J. L. Bakker, Lúcio R. Requião-Moura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kidney transplant recipients (KTR) may experience burdensome symptoms, including tremor, nausea, and bruises, sometimes caused by immunosuppressive medication, which can negatively influence health-related quality of life. The 59-item Revised Modified Transplant Symptom Occurrence and Symptom Distress scale (MTSOSD-59R) captures patients’ symptom experience but has not been validated in Brazil. We evaluated the Brazilian Portuguese MTSOSD-59R regarding content validity (expert panel), internal structure (factor analysis), and associations with other variables (known-groups approach). Symptom occurrence and distress by sex and age were assessed using ridit analyses. 485 KTR (42.2% women; 50.2 ± 12.8 years) were included at median 7.4 [3.2–13.0] years post-transplantation. The expert-rated scale-content validity index was 0.49. Construct validity and known-group associations were confirmed. The most occurring symptoms were anxiety [ridit score: 0.697], tiredness [0.653], and difficulty seeing well [0.652]. The most distressing symptoms were menstrual problems [0.635], difficulty seeing well [0.606], and genital warts [0.595]. Female/younger KTR tended to report more (distress from) psychological/aesthetic symptoms; male/older KTR tended to report more (distress from) physical symptoms. The MTSOSD-59R showed validity for internal structure and relationships to other variables, but insufficient expert-rated content validity for immunosuppression-specific side effects, suggesting it may better reflect general symptom burden in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56130-3.

Introduction

Subjective patient-reported outcomes (PRO’s), e.g. symptom experience, symptoms of depression, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), are increasingly recognized as key study endpoints because they more accurately reflect the patient’s perspective, capture the broader impact of treatment on daily functioning, and may facilitate shared decision-making between patients and clinicians. The incorporation of PRO’s into clinical research has increased following the 2017 21st Century Cures Acts,1 which provided official guidance for including patient experience in drug development decisions, as well as initiatives by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to organize patient-focused drug development meetings.2

This growing emphasis on PRO’s is particularly relevant for kidney transplant recipients (KTR), for whom symptom burden of immunosuppressive medication use is a major concern3, and may contribute to their lower long-term HRQoL compared to the general population4,5. Maintenance immunosuppressive therapy in KTR typically consists of a calcineurin inhibitor, an antiproliferative agent, and a corticosteroid. Common adverse effects include tremor, headache, and hirsutism or baldness (calcineurin inhibitors); nausea, diarrhea, and abdominal pain (antiproliferative agents); and mood changes, insomnia, and increased appetite (corticosteroids). Symptom burden can be evaluated objectively by healthcare workers, but the subjective perspective of patients is essential to avoid underestimation of true burden of immunosuppressive treatment6. Current questionnaires/scales often focus on symptoms of one specific drug type7,8, assess only certain symptoms as part of a more generical HRQoL assessment9–11, or include conditions/outcomes (e.g. high blood pressure or infections) that are not symptoms,10 which may reduce the conceptual clarity and validity of the scale.

The 59-item Revised Modified Transplant Symptom Occurrence and Symptom Distress Scale (MTSOSD-59R) is the only instrument that adopted an extensive list of relevant symptoms related to immunosuppressive medications used in clinical practice in solid organ transplantation recipients.12 The scale is based on the self-regulation theory, which states that symptom experience is a proximal outcome influencing coping behaviors, treatment adherence, and HRQoL. Instruments measuring symptom experience must therefore demonstrate that their items capture relevant symptoms, reflect coherent constructs—symptom occurrence (i.e., frequency or severity) and symptom distress (i.e., the emotional suffering caused by a symptom)—and relate to theoretically linked clinical and psychosocial variables (e.g., sex, symptoms of depression, and HRQoL). The MTSOSD-59R is clearly defined, standardized, and validated in Dutch, Turkish and South-Korean populations.12–14 A Brazilian Portuguese translation of the MTSOSD-59R already exists,12 but its psychometric properties have never been assessed.

Questionnaire validation is a continuous process: re-evaluation of validity has to take place when applied to new populations, languages, or cultural settings. The American Educational Research Association (AERA), the American Psychological Association (APA), and the National Council on Measurement in Education (NCME) provide guidance for studying psychometric properties of self-report instruments by assessing their reliability (i.e., the extent to which random errors of measurement determine the consistency/precision of the scoring) and validity (i.e., the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretation of test scores for their proposed uses).15 The guidelines state that evidence for validity can be provided in terms of content, response processes, internal structure, relation to other variables, and consequences.15.

Cross-cultural validation is important and aims to evaluate whether the questionnaire behaves similarly in different populations. Still, the symptom experience can vary across populations, being influenced by socioeconomic, demographic and cultural factors. For example, applying the MTSOSD-59R, Dutch recipients reported impotence or menstrual problems most distressing3,16, while Korean recipients reported lack of energy as most distressing symptom13. Also, age and sex differences have been reported, with females generally experiencing more symptom burden, and younger individuals experiencing more psychological/aesthetic symptoms3. Thus, results on symptom experience cannot be readily generalized across countries. Brazil is ranked fourth globally in the absolute number of kidney transplants performed in 202317, but symptom occurrence and distress have not yet been assessed in this population.

Therefore, in this study we aimed to evaluate the validity of the Brazilian Portuguese MTSOSD-59R regarding its content, internal structure, and relationship to other variables. The second aim of the study was to explore symptom experience of Brazilian KTR and to examine potential differences by sex and age group.

Methods

Design and study population

For this single-center cross-sectional study, participants were recruited between November 2024 and July 2025 using a convenience sampling method through two approaches: (1) immediately after routine blood collection, or (2) while waiting in the outpatient clinic for their appointment with the transplant nephrologist. All eligible patients were invited to participate based on their availability and the presence of a researcher during the recruitment periods. Written informed consent was obtained. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Federal University of São Paulo (approval number: 0608/2024), and conducted in accordance with the guidelines laid down in the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.18,19 The manuscript complies with the STROBE guidelines, following all relevant items from the STROBE checklist (Table S1).

Inclusion criteria were an age ≥ 18 years, having a functioning kidney graft ≥ 1 year post-transplantation, and attending a routine outpatient clinic visit with accompanying blood tests. Exclusion criteria were a combined or sequential kidney transplantation with another type of organ, or being unable to comprehend the questionnaires. All questionnaires were administered through face-to-face patient interviews conducted by trained interviewers to ensure data completeness and comprehension, given the variability in health literacy in Brazil and anticipated low response rates via email.20 The small group of interviewers consisted of nurses, researchers and medical students that worked at Hospital do Rim and had affinity to the research. All interviewers received the same training from the coordinating researcher (NLR), consisting of instructions on how to administer the questionnaires, after which after which the coordinating researcher attended the first patient interview with each interviewer, and remained available throughout the study to answer questions and ensure standardized administration. Interviewers were permitted to clarify symptoms unfamiliar to the patient but were instructed not to influence the patient’s responses.

All patients were treated in the context of The Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde; SUS), which provides free access to health care, including complex procedures such as organ transplantation, for all Brazilian citizens. Immunosuppressive drugs are provided free of charge to all transplant recipient through SUS. The KTR were followed by a multidisciplinary transplant team, including nephrologists, pharmacists, nurses, psychologists, and dietitians. Education on correct dosing and timing of immunosuppressive medication was emphasized at discharge and reinforced during outpatient clinic visits.

Immunosuppression

Maintenance immunosuppression therapy generally consisted of a combination of tacrolimus (target trough levels: 5–10 ng/mL), azathioprine or mycophenolic acid, and prednisone. Other immunosuppressive medication (cyclosporine, sirolimus, or everolimus) were less often used.

Population characteristics

Clinical, biochemical and pharmacotherapeutic data were extracted from the electronic patient file. Data included age, sex assigned at birth, ethnicity (self-declared), educational level, having a partner, weight, height, comorbidities, primary kidney disease, dialysis vintage, medication use, tacrolimus/cyclosporine trough levels, serum creatinine, and donor characteristics. Glomerular filtration rate was estimated using the CKD-EPI 2021 equation21, based on the mean creatinine value from the four most recent post-transplant measurements. Educational level was categorized into three categories: ‘low’ (non-illiterate, illiterate, primary education, incomplete lower secondary education), ‘middle’ (completed lower secondary, incomplete upper secondary education, and completed upper secondary education), and ‘high’ (incomplete higher education, completed higher education, and postgraduate education).

The MTSOSD-59R

Symptoms were evaluated with the MTSOSD-59R, which assesses the occurrence and distress of 60 symptoms associated with immunosuppressive medication use, specifically tacrolimus, cyclosporine, corticosteroids, azathioprine, mycophenolic acid, mTOR inhibitors and belatacept.12 One item is sex-specific, i.e. menstrual problems for females and impotence for males, resulting in a total of 59 symptoms assessed per patient. The questionnaire evaluates the occurrence and distress of symptoms over the past four weeks using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not occurring/not at all distressing) to 4 (always present/extremely distressing). The questionnaire had previously been translated into Brazilian Portuguese using a culturally sensitive approach, by the MAPI Research institute (France) in line with the principles of good practice for the translation and cultural adaptation of patient reported outcome measures.22 At the start of the study, six minor revisions were made based on feedback from a small workgroup meeting comprising two kidney transplant nurses and three transplant nephrologists (Table S2).

Validation of the Brazilian Portuguese MTSOSD-59R

The validity of the Brazilian Portuguese version of the MTSOSD-59R was evaluated according to the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing.15 In this study, we focused on evidence based on content, evidence based on internal structure, and evidence based on relationships to other variables. Evidence based on content

The original MTSOSD-59R items were generated by an international expert group.12 Therefore, using convenience sampling, we invited ten transplant nephrologists (7 females) to participate in an expert panel and to rate each item on relevance as a side effect of the immunosuppressive medication used by KTR. Because Brazil is a continental country with marked regional cultural differences, specialists were recruited from across all major regions: 1 from the North/Central-West, 3 from the Northeast, 3 from the Southeast, and 3 from the South. These experts reflect the diversity of patients treated in our hospital.23 The experts independently graded each item on a scale from 1 (not relevant at all) to 4 (very relevant), and items with a score ≥ 3 were considered as relevant, according to the method of Lynn et all.24 An item content validity index (I-CVI) was defined for each item, as the proportion of experts that rated an item as either 3 or 4. Subsequently, a scale content validity index (S-CVI) was defined as the mean of all I-CVIs.

Following the interview, the patients were invited to provide any comments or feedback on the questionnaire. The MTSOSD-59R allows participants to address additional symptoms which are not already listed in the MTSOSD-59R. These were assessed for relevance.

Evidence based on internal structure

A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to examine the two-factor model representing symptom occurrence and symptom distress. Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimation was used because of the ordinal nature of the items. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Factor loadings were examined to assess the strength of relationships between each item and its respective latent construct. An exploratory confirmatory factor analysis was performed with item reduction based on item-level factor loadings < 0.30. Lastly, correlation between the two factors was assessed.

Evidence based on associations with other variables

Associations of the MTSOSD-59R with other variables were assessed using the known groups approach based on the a priori hypotheses that symptom occurrence and symptom distress are higher in (1) females compared to males, in (2) KTR with more depressive symptomatology, in (3) KTR with lower physical HRQoL, and in (4) KTR with lower mental HRQoL. Based on these eight hypotheses, outcome analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method, yielding a significance threshold of 0.00625.

Depressive symptomatology was assessed with the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).25 The questionnaire evaluates 9 DSM-IV criteria for depression with a score ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) for each item. Higher scores reflect more severe depressive symptomatology26,27.

HRQoL was assessed using the Brazilian Portuguese 12-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12).9,26 This questionnaire measures physical and mental health across eight domains and creates a physical- and mental component summary score (PCS, MCS, respectively). The PCS was derived by averaging the physical functioning, role-physical, pain, and general health domains. The MCS was derived by averaging the role-emotional, vitality, emotional well-being, and social functioning domains. Higher scores reflect better HRQoL.

Symptom occurrence and symptom distress sum scores were calculated. Differences between males and females were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test, with rank-biserial correlation used to assess effect size. The relationship of the PHQ-9, PCS, and MCS with symptom occurrence and symptom distress were evaluated with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Cronbach’s Alphas were calculated to assess internal consistency of the PHQ-9 , the PCS and the MCS.

Analysis of symptom experience

Symptom occurrence and symptom distress were assessed using ridit (relative to an identified distribution integral transformation) analyses: a nonparametric method to compare and rank ordinal data from multiple groups.27,28 In short, the ridit is a score between 0 and 1 in which a score > 0.5 indicates a higher occurrence or distress of the symptom, whereas a ridit score < 0.5 indicates a lower occurrence or distress of the symptom, compared to the reference group (which equals 0.5). The reference groups were occurrence or distress of all symptoms among all KTR, as well as occurrence or distress of all symptoms within each subgroup by gender or age. Age was categorized based on the median age of 51 years, to ensure balanced groups. Comparisons between groups were based on the ten most occurring/distressing symptoms based on ridit scores, thereby preserving the ordinal nature of the data. These comparisons were hypothesis-generating and were therefore not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Sample size estimation

This study is part of a larger research project which aimed to evaluate associations of symptom burden with medication adherence, depressive symptomatology and HRQoL. An a priori sample size estimation was performed, using G*power (version 3.1). A sample size of 485 participants was needed to detect a small effect size (f = 0.02) in a linear regression model of symptom burden with HRQoL, with an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 0.80. Patient inclusion continued until the prespecified number of 485 participants was reached. For the present study, all available patients were included.

Results

Population characteristics

Of the 490 KTR enrolled, five were excluded for reasons listed in Fig. 1, resulting in a final sample of 485 KTR included in the analyses. Among these 485 KTR, 205 (42.2%) were women, mean ± SD age was 50.2 ± 12.8 years, and mean estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 49.9 ± 20.8 mL/min/1.73m2. The expert panel consisted of 10 kidney transplant nephrologists (7 women) from across Brazil. The experts rated 14 (23.3%) symptoms as relevant side effects of immunosuppressive medication. After the interview, patients had no comments about the MTSOSD-59R, except for specifications on why some symptoms were occurring (e.g. larger breasts because of pregnancy or anxiety for changes in medical results). A total of 48 (9.9%) patients reported additional symptoms, many of which closely resembled those already included in the MTSOSD-59R (e.g. warts located in areas other than the hands or feet, pain in the knee, or sensation of heat in the body), or unrelated to side effects of immunosuppressive medication (e.g. pain in the transplant region, retinal detachment or glaucoma).

Evidence based on internal structure

In confirmatory factor analysis, the two-factor model, consisting of occurrence and distress, demonstrated good model fit for the CFI (0.961), the TLI (0.961), and the RMSEA (0.031), although the SRMR of 0.126 exceeded the commonly recommended threshold of < 0.08, indicating some residual covariance. 90% of the occurrence items had factor loadings > 0.300 to the factor symptom occurrence (Table S3). The three symptoms that loaded most strongly to the occurrence factor were lack of energy, tiredness, and muscle weakness. 93% of the distress items had factor loadings > 0.300 to the factor symptom distress (Table S4). The three symptoms that loaded most strongly to the distress factor were tiredness, lack of energy, and swollen gums. The exploratory confirmatory factor analysis in which ten items were removed based on factor loadings < 0.30 (Table S3 and S4) resulted in a slightly better CFI (0.970) and TLI (0.970), an identical RMSEA (0.031), and a slightly improved but still elevated SRMR (0.113). The latent factors were moderately correlated (r = 0.56, p < 0.001), suggesting related but distinct constructs.

Evidence based on relation to other variables

The known groups approach was used to assess the performance of the MTSOSD-59R in association with other variables. The median PHQ-9 (depressive symptomatology) score was 6.0 [3.0–11.0]. Median PCS and MCS scores were 48.5 [39.5–54.8] and 54.0 [41.6–58.3], respectively. Symptom experience in males versus females

Among males, most occurring symptoms were anxiety [0.680], difficulty seeing well [0.649], and wind [0.635], while most distressing symptoms were impotence [0.649], sleep difficulties [0.618], and hearing loss [0.607] (Table 4). Among females, most occurring symptoms were anxiety [0.726], tiredness [0.684] and bruises [0.667], while most distressing symptoms were difficulty seeing well [0.647], joint pain [0.606], and genital warts [0.597]. Male KTR tended to report more general physical symptoms (e.g. trembling hands, sleep difficulties, excessive appetite), and more distress from hearing loss, back pain, and muscle cramps/weakness, compared to females (Table 4). Female KTR tended to experience more frequently psychological symptoms (e.g. restlessness/nervousness, concentration or memory problems, and mood swings), and more distress from psychological symptoms (e.g. anxiety and depression) and aesthetic symptoms (e.g. thinning of hair/hair loss and brittle skin), compared to males.

Symptom experience in younger versus older KTR

Among KTR below the median age of 51 years, most occurring symptoms were anxiety [0.721], wind [0.647] and tiredness [0.642], while the most distressing symptoms were menstrual problems [0.643], difficulty seeing well [0.603], and sleep difficulties [0.592] (Table 4). Among KTR above the median age of 51 years, most occurring symptoms were difficulty seeing well [0.682], anxiety [0.674], and tiredness [0.665], while most distressing symptoms were genital warts [0.642], impotence [0.619], and difficulty seeing well [0.609]. Younger KTR tended to report more psychological symptoms (e.g. mood swings and restlessness/nervousness), and more distress from psychological problems (e.g. anxiety and depression), compared to older KTR (Table 4). Older KTR tended to report more general symptoms of becoming older (e.g. dry skin, bruises, and muscle weakness), and more distress from this type of symptoms (e.g. impotence, back pain, brittle skin), compared to younger KTR.

Discussion

This large cross-sectional study provides preliminary evidence of validity for the interviewer-administered use of the Brazilian Portuguese MTSOSD-59R in outpatient Brazilian KTR. The factor structure and the known relations of the MTSOSD-59R to other variables were confirmed. However, the expert panel rated the overall relevance of the items as low. The most occurring symptoms were anxiety, tiredness, and difficulty seeing well, while the most distressing symptoms were menstrual problems, difficulty seeing well, and genital warts. Female/younger KTR tended to report more (distress of) psychological and aesthetic symptoms, whereas male/older KTR tended to report more (distress of) physical symptoms.

Questionnaire validation is an ongoing process, and the use of questionnaires in new populations requires renewed evidence of validity.29 Our expert panel rated many symptoms as irrelevant, resulting in a S-CVI of 0.49, which indicates insufficient content validity according to conventional psychometric benchmarks,30 while previous validation studies of the scale in Turkish and South-Korean populations reported excellent content validity scores of 1.00 and 0.98, respectively.13,14 However, these previous studies focused on whether indeed symptoms represented side effects of immunosuppressive therapy, whereas we asked the experts to rate the relevance of the symptoms as side effects of immunosuppressive therapy, which perhaps is more aligned with the intended purpose of the MTSOSD-59R: to support patients and physicians in making decisions about immunosuppressive therapy. Other hypotheses are that the low S-CVI may reflect cross-cultural differences in symptom interpretation or differences in clinical priorities among physicians. The low expert-rated content validity indicates that the MTSOSD-59R may be better conceptualized as a measure of general symptom burden in KTR rather than immunosuppression-specific side effects in the Brazilian context.

Multiple symptoms that were highly distressing or frequently occurring for our patients were considered irrelevant by the expert panel. This discrepancy suggests that Brazilian transplant nephrologists may benefit from routine use of standardized PRO measures such as the MTSOSD-59R to better capture patient-prioritized symptoms. Notably, visual disturbances, anxiety, tiredness, and lack of energy were rated as of low relevance by experts yet reported as highly burdensome by patients, indicating that these domains warrant more systematic assessment during clinical encounters. It is possible that Brazilian nephrologists are less inclined to attribute certain nonspecific or multifactorial symptoms directly to immunosuppressive therapy, particularly when such symptoms may also reflect general illness burden, comorbidities, or aging-related processes. Moreover, the original item selection was based on literature review and an expert panel from different clinical and geographic contexts, which may have been influenced by the composition and priorities of that panel. Taken together, these finding highlight a potential gap between patient-experienced burden and clinician-attributed immunosuppressive toxicity, and provide evidence supporting the need for stakeholder-informed scale refinement. Systemically including patient perspectives in future content validation, including patient panel involvement, cognitive interviewing, and potential item revision or reduction, may strengthen the questionnaire’s relevance and content validity by better reflecting the concepts of interest. This is also a known limitation of the MTSOSD-59R, which was created based on literature and an expert panel.12 Despite its current limitations, the scale provides a most needed assessment of patient-reported symptom experience in Brazilian KTR.

The two-factor structure of the MTSOSD-59R, comprising symptom occurrence and symptom distress, was supported by factor analytic results. Although symptom distress is inherently conditional on symptom occurrence, the moderate correlation observed suggests that it captures additional information on the subjective impact of symptoms, supporting the value of assessing both dimensions. While the CFI, TLI, and RMSEA suggested good fit of the confirmatory factor analysis model, the SRMR of 0.126 exceeded the commonly recommended threshold of < 0.08, indicating that some residual covariance is not captured by the model. This may reflect the multidimensional nature of symptom experience or the influence of items with low factor loadings. Exploratory analysis following the removal of low factor loading items showed some improvement in model fit. The MTSOSD-59R may also include redundant symptoms, such as “tiredness” and “lack of energy”, which are likely near-synonymous in practice and display similarly high factor loadings. These findings identify potential targets for item reduction in future studies to improve the structural validity of the MTSOSD-59R.

It was deemed necessary to administer the MTSOSD-59R with an interviewer, because of the high variability in health literacy in Brazil20, and previous experiences that resulted in low response rates. Socially desirable responding on sensitive topics (e.g. impotence and menstrual problems, which have been identified as among the most distressing symptoms in prior studies3,16,31) may have influenced our findings. This bias likely resulted in underreporting of frequency and severity. Notably, despite probable underestimation, impotence was the most prevalent symptom among male KTR [ridit score: 0.649], suggesting that its true burden may be substantially higher. Computer-assisted self-interviewing could reduce this social desirability bias, by offering more privacy while being available for respondents with limited literacy32,33, and should be implemented in future research to obtain more accurate data on sensitive topics.

In agreement with previous studies, we found that patients with more symptoms of depression and patients with a lower HRQoL reported a worse symptom status12,16,31. Also consistent with literature is that women reported higher symptom occurrence/distress compared to men3,12,13. These results may add to the growing recognition of sex-based differences in disease presentation, treatment response, and health outcomes, and underline the importance of analyzing women and men separately in medical research.

Identifying which symptoms are most occurring and distressing is essential for guiding patient-centered care, optimizing symptom management, and improving HRQoL. Wilson and Cleary already described that symptom status may play an important role in HRQoL.34 Individual characteristics (e.g. symptom amplification and value preferences) and environmental characteristics (e.g. social and psychological support) may influence this process,34 and may therefore differ strongly per region. Indeed, anxiety was in our population the most occurring symptom, while this was notably less reported in Dutch populations.3,16 Anxiety may arise from various sources, including corticosteroid use,35 uncertainty about transplant longevity,36 and the broader systemic or socioeconomic challenges faced by Brazilian KTR. Differences in coping styles across populations may also influence symptom perception, as problem-focused coping was previously associated with lower depression in KTR, whereas avoidant coping corresponded with higher depression.37 Another important symptom in our population was difficulty seeing well. We hypothesize that limited and unequal access to healthcare in rural Brazil remains a major challenge and may contribute to preventable disease progression,38 such as diabetic retinopathy.39 The high prevalence of diabetes in our cohort (28.9%) may partly explain the burden of visual complaints. These differences highlight the need for regional-specific studies that assess symptom experience.

Symptom experience also differed between groups, based on sex and age, within our population. Overall, we noticed that older/male KTR tended to report more general (distress of) physical symptoms while younger/female KTR tended to report more (distress of) psychological and aesthetic symptoms. We may hypothesize that older individuals may prioritize physical functioning and maintaining independence, while males may be influenced by societal expectations around physical strength and resilience.40 On the other hand, younger recipients may experience more emotional symptoms associated with long-term illness and the impact on their active lifestyles. In the end, while patterns were observed across sex and age groups, the most important consideration is the individual patient, ensuring that care is tailored to their specific needs. Patient stakeholder involvement may improve the representation of relevant concepts and strengthen the evaluation process. Their absence is a limitation and may have contributed to lower content validity estimates. Future studies may include patient stakeholders, assess test–retest reliability in stable transplant recipients, evaluate measurement invariance across sex and age, and examine whether meaningful subscales of the MTSOSD-59R can be derived.

This study was strengthened by its large cohort of 485 KTR, which allowed thorough evaluation of the symptom experience of Brazilian KTR. The questionnaires were administered through interviews, which resulted in complete data but may have introduced interviewer and social desirability bias. We acknowledge that the reported prevalence of sensitive symptoms, such as impotence, menstrual problems or genital warts, may be substantially underestimated due to social desirability bias inherent in the face-to-face interview format. We tried to minimalize the interviewer bias by having regular startup meetings and by ensuring that every researcher received the same training. Convenience sampling of the study population, immediately after blood draw or while waiting for appointments, may have introduced selection bias by excluding patients with more severe symptoms who may have cancelled appointments or been hospitalized. Also, the study population was drawn from a single high-volume center within the SUS system, which may limit generalizability to kidney transplant recipients in other regions of Brazil or in private healthcare settings with different access to follow-up care. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference: it remains unclear whether the symptoms were side effects of the medication or attributable to another (pre)existing condition.

In conclusion, the interviewer-administered Brazilian Portuguese MTSOSD-59R demonstrated evidence of validity in terms internal structure and associations with other variables. However, low expert-rated content validity suggests that it may be better conceptualized as a measure of general symptom burden in KTR rather than immunosuppression-specific side effects, and underscores the need for further psychometric work before routine clinical implementation. Our findings highlight the importance of population-specific, tailored healthcare.
