Inadvertent short external rotator tendon release during anterior-approach total hip arthroplasty: a prospective magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Rajrishi Sharma, Manjot S. Birk, Ahmed El-Bakoury, Kelly Johnston, Andrea Veljkovic, Richard E.A. Walker, Peter Salat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The direct anterior approach for total hip arthroplasty (DAA THA) is believed to be a tissue-sparing approach that aims to spare the short external rotator (SER) tendons and posterior capsule, but the rate of SER release is unknown. We sought to prospectively determine the early appreciable percentage of SER tendon release among patients undergoing DAA THA for osteoarthritis using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).Methods:We recruited participants undergoing DAA THA for osteoarthritis for this single-centre, prospective study. All participants underwent a 1.5 T MRI preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively. The surgeon recorded the status of SER tendons intraoperatively. Two fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologists independently reviewed MRIs for integrity of SER tendons at each time point.Results:We recruited 25 participants with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 3 or 4 osteoarthritis. The average age was 65 (range 51 to 80) years, and the average body mass index was 28.2 (range 21.2 to 40.8). Of the 25 participants, 13 (52%) were male; 14 (56%) participants were undergoing THA for their left hip. On preoperative MRI, all SER tendons were intact. Intraoperatively, the surgeon reported 2 (8%) purposeful releases of the obturator internus for assistance on exposure and no purposeful piriformis or obturator externus releases. On postoperative MRI, consensus reading concluded complete release of the obturator internus in all participants (100%), piriformis release in 72% of participants, and obturator externus release in 4% of participants. We observed significant differences between SER tendon integrity on postoperative MRI and the surgeon’s reported releases for obturator internus and piriformis (p < 0.001).Conclusion:Given the complexity of attachment of conjoint and piriformis tendons to the lateral capsule and appropriate stem positioning, release of these tendons viewed on MRI is inevitable. This may be owing to their inadvertent release during capsular exposure, as these tendons merge with the capsule, or during lateralization and posterization rasping of the femoral broach, especially for the obturator internus.

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is one of the most successful and commonly performed orthopedic procedures, providing durable pain relief and functional restoration for patients with end-stage hip pathology.1–3 Several surgical approaches to the hip have been described, each with distinct anatomical considerations, advantages, and limitations. In recent years, the direct anterior approach (DAA) has gained increasing popularity because of its potential for early postoperative recovery, decreased pain, and reduced soft-tissue trauma.4,5 It is often described as a muscle-sparing approach, as it utilizes an intermuscular and internervous plane between the sartorius and tensor fascia lata superficially, and between the gluteus medius and rectus femoris in the deeper layer, theoretically preserving the short external rotators (SERs) and surrounding musculature.3,6,7

Historically, the anterior approach to the hip was first described by Carl Hueter in 1817 and popularized by Smith-Petersen in 1917. Initially, it was used to treat developmental dysplasia and, later, femoral neck fractures. As the focus of arthroplasty evolved toward minimally invasive techniques and tissue preservation, a trend toward an anterior approach occurred.4 The SERs — comprising the piriformis, superior and inferior gemelli, obturator internus, and quadratus femoris — originate from the posterior pelvis and insert on the posterior aspect of the greater trochanter. These muscles function as dynamic stabilizers and contribute to posterior joint stability. Disruption of the SERs can alter hip biomechanics and gait mechanics and increase the risk of hip instability.

The current trend of DAA has not been without criticism. The approach is associated with a steep learning curve, a higher risk of intraoperative complications during the early phase of surgeon experience, and limited extensibility compared with posterior approaches.3 Moreover, adequate femoral exposure often necessitates capsular and soft-tissue releases that may compromise the SERs. Hip capsular release can greatly assist femoral elevation and broaching during DAA THA.1,8 This release — along with femoral rasping — has been noted to cause inadvertent damage to the SERs.9,10 We hypothesized that the true incidence of SER release is higher than commonly recognized among surgeons performing the DAA. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to determine the proportion of SER tendon damage observed on postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) among patients who underwent DAA THA with lateral capsular release for hip osteoarthritis, and to compare these findings with surgeons’ reported purposeful intraoperative releases.

Methods

We conducted a prospective pre–post comparative study in a single-centre, academic, university hospital. We included patients undergoing elective primary DAA THA for primary osteoarthritis of the hip who were willing to participate in the study after providing informed consent. We excluded patients with posttraumatic or inflammatory osteoarthritis, avascular necrosis, previous infection, or neuromuscular disorder, patients undergoing revision THA, and patients who had contraindications to MRI.

We calculated the minimum required sample using G-power software. To achieve a power of 80% at an α error probability of 0.05, we required 22 patients to participate. To allow for a potential 10% loss of follow-up, we aimed to recruit 25 patients to this study.

All MRI scans were performed on a 1.5 T scanner. All patients had preoperative baseline MRI scans within 4 weeks of the surgery and postoperative MRI with metal artifact reduction of the operative hip at 3 months post-THA. Two attending musculoskeletal radiologists reviewed MRI on a dedicated AGFA Picture Archiving and Communication System to establish integrity of the SER tendons (piriformis, obturator internus, and obturator externus). Both radiologists were blinded to the intraoperative data regarding SER tendon releases. We established intraobserver reliability by repeating measurements 2 weeks later. We assessed interrater agreement by comparing data obtained by the 2 radiologists. Any differences in integrity of SERs were resolved by consensus by the 2 musculoskeletal radiologists.

Prospective gathering of data on whether the surgeon thought they had or had not released the piriformis, obturator internus, and obturator externus tendons was documented immediately after the surgery. Baseline patient demographic characteristics — including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and presurgical risk factors — were recorded. We also obtained data on the implants.

All patients were operated on by the senior author (R.S.), a fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon who had completed the DAA learning curve before the start of this study. Patients were positioned on a Hana orthopedic table. The surgeon performed a DAA with a 6- to 10-cm skin incision starting from a point 3 cm lateral and distal to the anterior superior iliac spine and directed toward the head of the fibula. He used a combination of sharp and blunt dissection to the subcutaneous tissue to expose the tensor fascia lata fascia, which he subsequently incised. A T-shaped anterior capsulotomy was performed with the longitudinal limb parallel to the femoral neck and the transverse limb along the intertrochanteric ridge. The femoral neck was cut in situ, and the femoral head was removed by a corkscrew. The surgeon performed an anterior and inferior capsular release to facilitate the femoral retraction required for adequate exposure of the acetabulum. No traction was used during the preparation of the acetabular component. The acetabulum was reamed, and the acetabular component was inserted in standard fashion. The acetabular component position was verified intraoperatively by C-arm fluoroscopy. A femoral retractor was inserted between the superolateral capsule and the gluteal minimus to protect the abductors during the subsequent capsular releases required for femoral elevation. No posterior capsular release was done. The surgeon released the superolateral capsule using electrocautery with protection of the SER tendons (piriformis, obturator internus, and obturator externus) and looking for the lateral capsular flip. If the femur was not amenable for elevation after capsular release, the surgeon released only the obturator internus tendon under direct visualization using electrocautery. The operative leg was then adducted, extended, and externally rotated to allow for femoral canal preparation. The surgeon then performed femoral preparation in standard fashion using femoral broaches. If more lateralization of the femoral component was required, this was done using the femoral broaches. The final component was inserted, and final intraoperative radiographs were obtained. The anterior capsule was routinely repaired in all cases and no drains were used.

All patients had routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis for venous thromboembolism in the form of enoxaparin. All patients were reviewed by a physiotherapist and were mobilized with full weight bearing. All patients were discharged from the hospital on the same or the following day. All patients were seen for postoperative follow-up at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 12 months, and 24 months.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed data using SPSS version 24. The surgeon was not involved in the statistical analysis. The results were analyzed using the Student t test and Spearman correlation analysis. We defined statistical significance as a p value less than 0.05.

Ethics approval

The study received approval from the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary.

Results

Thirty patients were approached at the outpatient clinic, and 25 patients with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 3 or 4 osteoarthritis were successfully recruited to the study. The mean age of this cohort was 65 (range 51 to 80) years. Thirteen (52%) patients were male. The mean BMI was 28.8 (21.2 to 40.9). All 25 participants completed pre- and post-procedural MRI, and no patients were lost to follow-up. The Pinnacle (Depuy Synthes), the Trilogy (Zimmer Biomet), and the G7 (Zimmer Biomet) acetabular systems were used in 18, 5, and 2 surgeries, respectively. The Trilock femoral stem (Depuy Synthes), the Corail stem (Depuy Synthes), the M/L taper (Zimmer Biomet), and the Taperloc (Zimmer Biomet) were used in 11, 7, 5, and 2 surgeries, respectively.

On preoperative MRI, all SER tendons were intact. Intraoperatively, the surgeon reported 2 purposeful releases of the obturator internus (8%) for assistance on exposure and no purposeful releases of the piriformis or obturator externus. On postoperative MRI, consensus reading concluded complete release of the obturator internus in all participants (100%), release of the piriformis in 72% of patients, and release of the obturator externus in 4% of patients.

There was a significant difference between preoperative and postoperative SER tendon integrity on MRI (Table 1 and Figure 1). There was also a significant difference between the postoperative MRI assessment of SER tendon integrity and the surgeon’s reported releases for the obturator internus and piriformis (p < 0.001) (Table 2 and Figure 1). There was complete integrity of the iliotibial band in all cases. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred in this cohort of patients at 2-year follow-up.

Discussion

In this prospective study, we found that the rate of inadvertent surgical release of the SER tendons was high during DAA THA. Our data showed a significantly higher percentage of SER tendon injuries observed on postoperative MRI than the intraoperatively reported purposeful surgical releases.

During DAA THA, capsular releases assist femoral elevation, allowing for easier access to the femoral canal.1,8,11 This inherently places the piriformis and conjoined tendon at risk of injury. A previous cadaveric study has shown that, in some cases, the SERs are released either voluntarily to gain more exposure or involuntarily when releasing the lateral capsule to gain access to the femur.10 The intricate and variable insertion of these tendons increases the vulnerability of injury, particularly of the obturator internus, given the anatomical relationship of its footprint and capsule. Releases may occur at an increased rate during the steep learning curve and in the early phase of surgeon experience. Additionally, femoral version, soft-tissue tension, and anatomical variability increase susceptibility to inadvertent tendon release. In this study, despite the care taken during release of the lateral capsule and protection of the posterior capsule to obtain the capsular flip, SER tendons were inadvertently released. A previous cadaveric study described the detailed insertion of the SER tendons and showed that these tendons have a complex insertion to the proximal femur.9 This complex insertion, in our opinion, can pose an increased risk of SER tendon injury during femoral broaching. The cadaveric study also showed that the piriformis insertion is located more superiorly and posteriorly to that of the conjoined tendon (obturator internus, gemellus superior, and gemellus inferior). This could explain the higher percentage of injury to the obturator internus (100%) than the piriformis (72%) in our study. Moreover, it seems that the footprint of the obturator externus is relatively far from the zone of injury during broaching of the proximal femur in DAA THA.

We purposefully used several uncemented implants to see if there were any differences in the status of the SER tendons during femoral broaching. These implants included both short metaphyseal and standard uncemented metaphyseal femoral implants. Although this study was not appropriately powered and used a limited sample of specific stems, we found no difference in the integrity of the SER on postoperative MRI by the type of implant used.

The high percentage of SER tendon release might suggest a high incidence of instability in affected patients. However, no patients had a postoperative dislocation within 2 years of follow-up. In our opinion, the stability of the DAA is more likely the consequence of an intact posterior capsule rather than the integrity of the obturator internus or piriformis. Takao and colleagues12 found that the posterior capsule is a more important stabilizer than SERs when cadaveric hips were tested in distraction. As we did not release the posterior capsule intraoperatively, we hypothesize that the frequent release of the piriformis and obturator internus did not significantly compromise posterior hip stability in our cohort. However, our study was not powered to assess the precise incidence of postoperative instability, which is reported to occur in less than 1% of patients who undergo DAA THA.2

Several studies have compared outcomes of DAA THA when SER tendons were or were not released.13,14 Those studies did not find inferior clinical outcomes when the SER tendons were surgically sacrificed.13,14 However, the status of the SER tendons was determined to be either preserved or injured on the basis of the surgeon’s intraoperative data.14,11 Our findings suggest that relying solely on surgeons’ intraoperative assessment may be insufficient to accurately determine preservation of the SER tendons; postoperative MRI evaluation should be used for confirmation.

Limitations

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, sample size calculation with adequate power, and lack of losses to follow-up. Moreover, we obtained preoperative baseline MRI to establish the integrity of the SER tendons before DAA THA. This allowed us to ensure that the SER tendon damage observed on the postoperative MRI scans occurred intraoperatively. Furthermore, we used a range of implants to understand whether the inadvertent release was specific to the implant, which it was not. In addition, having both musculoskeletal radiologists blinded to the intraoperative data of the SER tendon surgical releases minimized reporting bias. However, despite using metal-subtraction techniques on postoperative MRI, artifacts may have affected visualization of the SER tendon, and assessment of tendon integrity may have been difficult to determine, especially if some fibres remained intact posteriorly.

Conclusion

Given the complexity of the attachment of the obturator internus and piriformis to the lateral capsule, release of these tendons to some degree seems inevitable, especially with a lateral capsular release for femoral elevation. We confirmed that tears of the obturator internus and piriformis tendons were common in DAA THA and not readily apparent to the surgeon during the procedure, while the obturator externus was rarely released. Lateralization and posterization during rasping of the femoral broach may cause unintentional release of the SER, especially the obturator internus. These findings suggest that the perceived benefits of the DAA are not related to the integrity of the obturator internus or piriformis tendons but may be owing to other factors such as the integrity of the iliotibial band, gluteal musculature, and the posterior capsule.
