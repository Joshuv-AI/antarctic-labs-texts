Finite element analysis of engine pistons: comparative assessment of aluminium and titanium alloys under combined thermal-structural loading
Authors: Elias Wakshume, Gadisa Sufe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFinite Element Analysis (ANSYS 19.2) was used to compare four piston materials — Aluminium 7075, Aluminium 2618, Aluminium 4032 and Titanium Ti-6Al-4 V — under combined mechanical and thermal loading. The study reports steady-state thermal and static structural snapshots for comparison. Under the present steady-state setup (uniform crown temperature and static crown pressure), Aluminium 2618 exhibited the lowest total deformation (0.476 mm) compared with Aluminium 7075 (0.951 mm) and Ti-6Al-4 V (0.833 mm), representing ~ 50.0% and ~ 42.9% reductions in deformation, respectively. Al-2618 also showed the lowest maximum heat flux in these steady runs (≈ 2.059 × 10⁻¹² W·mm⁻²) compared to Ti-6Al-4 V (≈ 5.148 × 10⁻¹² W·mm⁻²), and the highest minimum safety factor (6.732), which is ~ 123.7% greater than that of Al-4032 (3.010). These steady-state results suggest Al-2618 offers a favorable combination of dimensional stability and mechanical resilience for the modeled conditions; however, we highlight limitations of steady-state, static loading and recommend transient, cycle-based analyses prior to final material selection.Keywords: Engine piston, Deformation, Thermal stresses, Static-stress, Solid work, ANSYS 19.2, Titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V), Aluminium 7075, Aluminium 2618, Aluminium 4032Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The internal combustion engine is a component of a vehicle that provides propulsion. Its most critical component is the piston, which is a cylindrical component that moves back and forth within the cylinder, transferring force to the crankshaft. The piston must withstand high pressure and temperature due to combustion1,2. Fatigue failure, as a result of high stress and thermal loads, is a common phenomenon that reduces the power of an engine, causes overheating, and increases repair or replacement expenses for maintenance. Pistons must be designed in such a way as to reduce stress and temperature during operation for better durability and overall engine performance. Innovations in materials and cooling techniques are crucial in enhancing the resilience of pistons, allowing them to operate more efficiently under extreme conditions while prolonging the lifespan of the engine components3–5.

Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis can often be applied to simulate pistons under operating conditions, simulating stress, temperature, and deformation without the physical prototype and conserving time and cost. ANSYS static structural and steady-state thermal analyses can observe the stress distribution, temperature, and deformation distribution under boundary conditions imposed. Literature indicates that the highest stress, temperature, and deformation occur at the top surface of the crown due to combustion6–8. Maximizing piston life involves minimizing stress and deformation. Optimizing the design by eliminating unnecessary material is one way to do this, which can reduce costs while enhancing performance. Reducing unnecessary piston material reduces stress and deformation, according to previous research. Optimizing a piston by minimizing mass sometimes raises stress and deformation. Therefore, more analysis is needed to ascertain if material removal actually improves piston performance9,10.

Engine piston

A piston is a vital component in all reciprocating internal combustion engines. It operates as a two-way, reciprocating member housed within the engine cylinder, forming a gas-tight seal with the help of piston rings. The primary function of the piston is to convert the energy generated by the expanding gases during combustion into mechanical energy by transmitting force through a connecting rod to the crankshaft11–13. This conversion process drives the rotary motion required to power the vehicle or machine. The piston design shown in the Fig. 1 is a common configuration used in diesel engines, known for its high strength and thermal resistance. Diesel engines operate under significantly higher pressures and temperatures compared to gasoline engines. During the combustion process, the maximum pressure inside the combustion chamber can reach up to 7 MPa (70 bar), while the temperature on the piston crown (top surface) can exceed 900 °C. These extreme conditions impose severe thermal and mechanical loads on the piston14,15.

Solid work model of piston.

Due to these high thermal loads, it is crucial to implement effective piston cooling strategies. Without adequate cooling, the piston may overheat, leading to thermal deformation, seizure, or even failure due to loss of material strength at elevated temperatures. In modern engines, cooling is often achieved using oil jets directed onto the underside of the piston or through internal oil galleries inside the piston body to circulate lubricant and remove excess heat. The sealing of combustion gases is another critical role of the piston16. This is accomplished using piston rings, which are precision-fitted around grooves on the piston’s outer surface. Pistons in internal combustion (IC) engines are equipped with a series of narrow iron rings, which are installed in precision-machined grooves located just below the piston crown (the top surface of the piston). These components, known as piston rings, play a crucial role in maintaining engine performance, efficiency, and durability. Each piston ring is manufactured with a small split or gap along its circumference. This split allows the ring to expand outward and maintain contact with the inner cylinder wall using its inherent spring-like tension. The pressure applied by the ring ensures a tight seal between the piston and the cylinder wall, even under varying thermal and mechanical loads during engine operation17–19.

There are generally two types of piston rings, each serving a specific function:

I.Compression Rings (Upper Rings): These rings have a solid face and are located closer to the piston crown. Their primary purpose is to seal the combustion chamber, preventing the high-pressure combustion gases from leaking into the crankcase. A good seal is essential to maintain compression, improve power output, and reduce emissions.

Their primary purpose is to seal the combustion chamber, preventing the high-pressure combustion gases from leaking into the crankcase. A good seal is essential to maintain compression, improve power output, and reduce emissions.

II.Oil Control Rings (Lower Rings): These rings typically have a narrow edge and a U-shaped or slotted profile. Their function is to scrape excess oil off the cylinder wall and return it to the crankcase. This prevents oil from entering the combustion chamber, reducing oil consumption and minimizing smoke or deposits.

Study materials

For the purpose of this study, four engineering materials have been selected based on their suitability for piston applications in internal combustion engines, where high thermal and mechanical stresses are present. These materials include, Aluminum Alloy 7075, Aluminum Alloy 2618, Aluminum Alloy 4032 and Titanium Alloy Ti-6Al-4 V. The selection of these materials is guided by their favorable combinations of mechanical strength, thermal conductivity, low density, and resistance to high-temperature deformation. These properties are critical in optimizing piston performance, especially in terms of minimizing weight, resisting thermal expansion, and withstanding combustion pressures without failure23. Hybrid piston design strategies

Given the high strength but low thermal conductivity of Ti-6Al-4 V (k ≈ 6.7 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹) and the comparatively high thermal conductivity of the aluminium alloys (k ≈ 130–150 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ for Al-7075/2618/4032), hybrid concepts can combine the mechanical benefits of titanium with the superior heat-removal capability of aluminium. Two practical strategies are proposed:

Strategy A — Localised titanium reinforcement (thin cap) on an Al-2618 body with enhanced oil cooling. Use Al-2618 as the main piston body for its favorable heat conduction and dimensional stability, and place a thin Ti-6Al-4 V cap (only where peak mechanical stresses occur) bonded to the crown or ring lands. Minimize Ti cap thickness to reduce thermal resistance and integrate an oil gallery or intensified underside oil-jet to remove heat beneath the cap. This reduces mass increase and preserves the Al thermal path to the oil. Using the steady results reported here (Ti max heat flux ≈ 5.15 × 10⁻¹² W·mm⁻² vs. Al-2618 ≈ 2.06 × 10⁻¹² W·mm⁻²), the design must ensure the Ti region is actively cooled or thin enough to avoid excessive substrate temperature rise.

Strategy B — Al-2618 piston with a ceramic thermal-barrier coating (TBC) on the crown + oil cooling. Apply a thin ceramic TBC to the combustion-facing surface to reduce heat transmission into the piston metal while relying on Al-2618’s high conductivity below the coating to carry residual heat to the oil-cooled underside. TBC protects material properties during temperature spikes and is widely used in high-temperature engine components. For either strategy, a complete feasibility assessment requires transient thermal–structural FEA and fatigue life estimation (S-N or strain-life). The assessment metrics should include peak crown temperature, maximum thermal gradient, maximum principal stress, minimum safety factor, and estimated fatigue life.

Pre-processing

The setup of the study, including the application of boundary conditions, was carefully designed to replicate the extreme operating conditions experienced by the piston during the combustion cycle of an internal combustion engine. Specifically, the model accounts for the high-pressure loads generated during the power stroke which can reach up to 20 MPa or more in diesel engines as well as the intense thermal loads, with surface temperatures on the piston crown exceeding 1000 °C.

To ensure the accuracy and relevance of the finite element analysis (FEA), both thermal and mechanical boundary conditions were applied simultaneously in the simulation environment. These include:

Pressure loading on the top surface of the piston to simulate the force generated by the expanding combustion gases.

Temperature distribution over the piston surface based on experimental or empirical data to simulate heat transfer during combustion.

Fixed constraints at the piston pin bosses to represent the connection with the connecting rod, limiting translation while allowing realistic deformation behavior.

FEA setup and boundary conditions

Steady-state thermal and static structural analyses were performed in ANSYS 19.2 to obtain baseline comparative results across four materials. For the steady runs reported here a conservative uniform crown temperature and a static crown pressure were applied to create a consistent comparison case across materials. Specifically, the originally reported runs applied a static pressure of 20 MPa to the crown and a uniform crown temperature of 1000 °C (these values were used as a conservative, comparative loading case and are reported here for transparency).

Important note and planned re-runs: the pressure and thermal loading used in steady snapshots do not represent the full transient engine cycle. Because realistic operation involves time-dependent pressure and temperature cycles (peak in-cylinder pressure ≈ 20 MPa in many diesel engines), the manuscript has been revised to (a) explain the steady-state baseline choice and (b) describe the improved simulation plan that will be executed to validate these findings: a coupled transient thermal–structural analysis using (i) time-dependent crown heat flux or gas temperature trace, (ii) cyclic in-cylinder pressure with peak ≈ 20 MPa, (iii) convective heat transfer to the oil-cooled underside (oil-jet convection coefficient to be estimated from engine oil jet geometry or literature). The revised Methods list the ANSYS checks to be performed before re-runs (material property units, element types, contact definitions, face selection for boundary conditions and mesh sensitivity).

Conclusion

Under the steady-state comparative simulations presented here, Aluminum 2618 showed the lowest total deformation (0.476 mm) and the highest minimum safety factor (6.732). Compared with Al-7075 (0.951 mm) this represents a ~ 50.0% reduction in deformation, and compared with Ti-6Al-4 V (0.833 mm) a ~ 42.9% reduction. Al-2618 also had the lowest reported maximum heat flux in the steady runs (≈ 2.059 × 10⁻¹² W·mm⁻²). These steady-state results indicate that Al-2618 performs favourably for the modeled loading case. However, because the present work used steady-state and static snapshots, the results should be validated by coupled transient thermal–structural analyses and fatigue modeling under realistic cyclic loading before making final material selection recommendations. We outline a clear plan for these re-runs and for hybrid piston designs which may combine the strengths of titanium and aluminium for high-performance applications.
