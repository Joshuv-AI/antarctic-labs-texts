Reassessing Parity‐Related Variation in Female Body Morphology: A Matched Analysis in a Contemporary Population
Authors: Jooyeon Lee, Jinhee Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMorphological differences between parous and nulliparous women have long been interpreted as reproductive signatures. However, such patterns may instead reflect demographic composition. This study aimed to reassess parity‐related morphological variation by reducing confounding from age and BMI using propensity score matching (PSM).MethodsAnthropometric data from 1339 Korean women aged 20–39 years were analyzed. Forty‐five direct and derived body dimensions, collected following ISO 7250‐1:2017, were compared between parous and nulliparous women before and after 1:1 nearest‐neighbor PSM on age and BMI. Group differences were examined for torso breadths, depths, circumferences, and proportional indices.ResultsUnadjusted analyses reproduced well‐known findings: parous women showed greater abdominal breadths and depths, larger circumferences, and adiposity patterns commonly attributed to childbirth. After PSM, however, most differences—including widely cited markers of lower‐body enlargement and postpartum contour change—were substantially attenuated or statistically nonsignificant. Only a small number of proportional indices (e.g., underbust/weight, waist/weight, elbow/weight) retained modest differences, indicating that parity leaves limited, anatomically localized morphological signatures rather than broad structural changes.ConclusionsDemographic composition, rather than reproductive biology alone, accounts for much of the observed variation in cross‐sectional datasets. Matching‐based approaches clarify the independent contribution of parity, strengthen causal inference in anthropometric research, and underscore the need to consider population structure when interpreting life‐history‐related variation in adult female morphology.Keywords: anthropometry, life‐history biology, morphological variation, parity, population structure

Introduction

Human body morphology reflects the cumulative interaction of growth, aging, metabolic processes, physical activity, nutritional exposures, and reproductive life‐history events (Ellison 2003; Wells 2007). Female morphological variation across adulthood is particularly shaped by changes in adiposity distribution, soft‐tissue composition, and pelvic or lower‐torso proportions—traits that have been interpreted within evolutionary, physiological, and biomedical frameworks (Amiri et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2024; Soltani and Fraser 2000; Wells et al. 2010). Among these factors, reproductive history, especially pregnancy and childbirth, has long been assumed to contribute meaningfully to adult female body shape. Parous women are often described as having larger abdominal circumferences, flatter lower‐body contours, or altered distributions of adiposity compared to nulliparous women (Amiri et al. 2023; Wells et al. 2010). Yet the degree to which these differences reflect reproductive biology itself remains unclear.

A central challenge in interpreting parity‐related morphology is the difficulty of disentangling biological effects from demographic structure. Age‐related increases in body mass and changes in adipose distribution can independently influence waist, hip, and limb dimensions, creating patterns that mimic presumed reproductive effects (Kuk et al. 2009; Wells et al. 2008; Zamboni et al. 1997). Consequently, when parous women differ systematically from nulliparous women in age or body mass—as is common—observed group differences may arise from confounding rather than parity itself. Large‐scale anthropometric studies have acknowledged this limitation, but analytic solutions remain scarce. For example, Wells et al. (2010) reported parity‐related differences in abdominal and thigh dimensions but noted that age and parity are interrelated factors that earlier analyses did not fully separate, making causal interpretation inherently difficult in cross‐sectional datasets. In addition, reproductive outcomes may not be entirely independent of preexisting morphological characteristics, as certain body traits may be associated with reproductive opportunities through social or biological selection processes, potentially contributing to observed group differences.

More broadly, observational anthropometric datasets are inherently vulnerable to selection bias. Morphological traits are shaped by multiple interdependent factors, and the cross‐sectional nature of most surveys means that researchers cannot manipulate or control these conditions experimentally. Traditional analytic approaches—including regression‐based methods such as ANCOVA or mixed‐effects models—attempt to adjust for covariates by modeling outcome–predictor relationships. However, when covariate distributions differ substantially between groups, as is often the case for parity, these approaches may rely on extrapolation beyond regions of common support. As King and Zeng (2006) demonstrate, such inferences in regions where one group has little or no empirical representation lead to extreme model dependence, where estimated effects are driven not only by observed data but also by assumptions imposed by the model specification. This, in turn, increases the risk of biased or unstable inference (Etminan et al. 2021; Greenland et al. 1999).

Analytic methods capable of reducing confounding are essential for accurately assessing reproductive influences on body morphology. Propensity score matching (PSM) provides one such framework by balancing comparison groups on key demographic covariates (Austin 2009; Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). Through matching, parous and nulliparous individuals can be made comparable in age and BMI, allowing any remaining morphological differences to be interpreted more confidently as parity‐related rather than demographically driven. Integrating matching into anthropometric research therefore fills an important methodological gap and refines the interpretation of morphological diversity.

The present study revisits parity‐related morphological variation using anthropometric data from 1339 Korean women aged 20–39 years. East Asian populations remain underrepresented in global morphology research, and Korean women offer a valuable case for examining body shape variation within a non‐Western demographic context (Cho et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2017). This age range is particularly informative: body shape variation increases markedly during the early 30s, coinciding with both reproductive transitions and age‐related shifts in adiposity. By comparing conventional unadjusted analyses with PSM‐adjusted comparisons in which age and BMI are balanced across parous and nulliparous women, we evaluate whether morphological differences commonly attributed to childbirth persist after addressing demographic confounding.

By applying a matching‐based framework to a contemporary East Asian population, this study refines the estimation of parity‐related morphological variation and contributes to ongoing efforts to distinguish biological effects from population structure in anthropometric research. More broadly, the findings highlight the importance of addressing confounding when interpreting morphological diversity in modern human populations.

Methods

Sample and Anthropometric Variables

Data for this study were drawn from the seventh edition of the SizeKorea national anthropometric survey, conducted in 2014. The analysis focused on women aged 20–39 years who had complete information on age, childbirth history, BMI, and a total of 32 anthropometric variables. These variables comprised 22 direct body measurements, nine proportional indices derived by normalizing circumferences to body weight, and one body‐composition variable (body fat mass). Only participants with full records across all variables were included in the final dataset. After excluding cases with missing data, the final analytic sample consisted of 1339 women (663 aged 20–29; 676 aged 30–39). Childbirth experience was self‐reported and coded as a binary variable (0 = nulliparous, 1 = parous). Age and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) were retained as descriptive variables and later included as covariates for propensity score estimation. Anthropometric variables included direct measurements and ratio indices derived from those measurements. Vertical dimensions were standardized by cervical height to express proportional vertical relationships while minimizing head‐size effects, whereas horizontal dimensions were standardized by body weight to reduce overall size differences and enhance comparability across the sample. In total, 45 variables were used. All measurements followed ISO 7250‐1:2017 standards. Anthropometric data were collected using a Martin anthropometer. To ensure data quality, the protocol strictly controlled for multiple sources of error, including intra‐ and inter‐measurer variability, as well as intra‐ and inter‐subject variability associated with physical conditions or postural habits. Measurement reliability was maintained through standardized training and repeated measurements within the survey framework.

This study used publicly available, de‐identified data from the SizeKorea dataset. Informed consent was obtained by the original data collection team, and the present study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Incheon National University and determined to be exempt from IRB approval (Approval No. 7007971‐202 012‐001).

Propensity Score Estimation and Matching Procedure

To reduce demographic confounding when comparing parous and nulliparous women, we applied PSM (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). Age and BMI were selected as covariates because both are strongly associated with parity and independently affect adult female body shape. A logistic regression model was used to estimate the propensity score, defined as the probability of belonging to the parous group given observed covariates. A 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm was implemented to ensure direct comparability between groups while maintaining interpretability of the matched sample. A caliper of 0.07 (0.2 × SD of the propensity score; Austin 2011a, 2011b) was applied to improve match quality by restricting matches to individuals with similar covariate profiles. This approach prioritizes covariate balance and reduces the risk of poor‐quality matches, even at the cost of reduced sample size, representing a trade‐off aimed at improving covariate balance and internal validity (Austin 2011a, 2011b; Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008).

Balance diagnostics were conducted following matching. Independent t‐tests compared the means of propensity scores and covariates (age, BMI) between matched groups; no significant differences remained, indicating successful covariate balance (p > 0.05). In addition, standardized mean differences were examined to further assess covariate balance. Visual diagnostics using histograms further confirmed improved group comparability. Figure 1 summarizes the overall analytical workflow, including sample selection, propensity score estimation, matching, and comparative analysis. Age and BMI were selected as key covariates due to their well‐established associations with both parity and adult body morphology. All statistical analyses, including propensity score estimation, matching, and subsequent comparisons, were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29).

Comparative Analysis of Conventional and PSM‐Adjusted Results

We examined childbirth‐related morphological variation using two analytic approaches. (1) Conventional analysis: Group differences were assessed using unadjusted data in which parous and nulliparous women were compared directly without controlling for demographic confounders. (2) PSM‐adjusted analysis: Morphological comparisons were reevaluated using matched data in which age and BMI were balanced across groups, enabling a more accurate estimation of parity‐related variation. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent t‐tests were performed for normally distributed variables, and Mann–Whitney U tests were applied otherwise. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.01 to reduce Type I error risk given multiple comparisons. Results from the two approaches were compared to assess the extent to which demographic confounding influenced interpretations of childbirth‐related morphological differences.

Supplementary Analysis by Number of Children

To explore whether morphological variation differed by parity level among women with childbirth experience, an additional subgroup analysis was conducted within the parous sample. Participants were categorized into women with one child and those with two or more children based on self‐reported number of children. Group differences in anthropometric variables were first assessed using the same statistical procedures described above (independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on normality).

Because age and BMI may also differ across parity levels within the parous group, supplementary adjusted analyses were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with age and BMI entered as covariates. This analysis was conducted to explore whether observed differences by number of children remained after adjustment for these demographic factors. As this analysis was exploratory and secondary to the main PSM‐based analysis, results were interpreted cautiously.

Results

Initial Sample Structure and Demographic Imbalance Prior to Matching

Before matching, the analytic sample consisted of 1339 women: 556 parous and 783 nulliparous (Table 1). Clear demographic imbalance was evident between groups. Most women in their 20s (78.3%) were nulliparous, whereas women in their 30s were overwhelmingly parous (91.0%). A chi‐square test confirmed a strong age–parity association (χ 2 = 624.56, p < 0.001), indicating that childbirth experience in this dataset was highly confounded with age. Consistent with these patterns, initial differences in propensity scores (PS), age, and BMI further demonstrated noncomparability between groups. Prior to matching, the parous group showed significantly higher PS values (U test, p < 0.001), older age (U test, p < 0.001), and higher BMI (U test, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

PSM Performance and Covariate Balance

Before matching, substantial demographic imbalance was observed between parous and nulliparous women, particularly in age (SMD = 2.01, variance ratio = 2.28) and BMI (SMD = 0.43, variance ratio = 0.76), indicating that the two groups were not directly comparable. A chi‐square test further confirmed a strong association between age group and parity (χ 2 = 624.56, p < 0.001; Table 1), highlighting the extent of demographic confounding. Consistent with these patterns, significant differences were also observed in continuous covariates prior to matching. The parous group showed higher propensity scores, older age, and higher BMI compared to the nulliparous group (Table 2), further demonstrating the lack of comparability between groups.

Following 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.07, a final matched sample of 462 individuals (231 pairs) was obtained. This reduction reflects the application of matching criteria designed to ensure adequate common support and to minimize reliance on model‐based extrapolation (Austin 2011a, 2011b). Post‐matching diagnostics indicated substantial improvement in covariate balance. The SMD for age decreased from 2.01 to 0.16 (approximately 92% reduction), while the SMD for BMI decreased from 0.43 to 0.23. Although these values did not fall below the conventional 0.1 threshold, they represent a substantial reduction in imbalance. In addition, variance ratios for all covariates (age: 1.54, BMI: 1.24) were within a commonly used range for assessing distributional similarity between groups (Austin 2011a, 2011b; Stuart 2010). Consistent with these findings, a chi‐square test indicated that the association between age group and parity was no longer statistically significant after matching (Table 1). Furthermore, no significant differences remained in propensity scores or age, and BMI differences were small and did not meet the study's significance threshold of p < 0.01 (Table 2).

Visual diagnostics using overlapping histograms (Figure 2) and covariate balance plots (Figure 3) further demonstrated convergence in propensity score distributions and improved covariate balance. Collectively, these results indicate that PSM substantially reduced demographic confounding, allowing for a more reliable comparison of childbirth‐related morphological variation.

Childbirth‐Related Morphological Differences Before and After Matching

All comparisons between parous and nulliparous women were conducted using independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on variable normality. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.01.

Before matching, substantial morphological differences were observed between parous and nulliparous women across multiple anthropometric variables. These differences were not uniformly distributed across all measurements but were primarily concentrated in central and lower torso regions. Significant group differences emerged in 24 of the 32 measurements examined. In particular, parous women exhibited larger values in abdominal and trunk‐related dimensions, including waist, hip, and bust breadths and depths (all p < 0.01), and most circumference measurements—except knee circumference—were also higher, indicating broader torso and limb girths. Vertical dimensions showed fewer contrasts, though hip height was significantly higher in the nulliparous group. Proportional indices further reflected region‐specific variation: parous women exhibited higher bust‐ and hip‐circumference‐to‐weight ratios, whereas nulliparous women showed higher waist‐circumference‐to‐weight ratios. Side neck‐to‐bust point length was significantly longer among parous women, indicating postpartum sagging of soft tissue in the breast area. Complete pre‐matching results from t‐tests and Mann–Whitney U tests are provided in Tables S1 (t‐tests) and S2 (U tests). These patterns align with conventional expectations about postpartum torso widening and lowered tissue tautness but remain difficult to interpret causally due to strong demographic confounding.

After PSM, childbirth‐related differences were markedly attenuated. Remaining differences were limited and localized rather than widespread across body dimensions. No significant differences remained in height, breadth, or depth measures under the study's significance threshold (p < 0.01). Circumference measurements also converged, with the exception of calf circumference, which was unexpectedly higher in the nulliparous group. Body fat mass was slightly higher among nulliparous women, whereas body weight no longer differed significantly. Among proportional indices, only three weight‐standardized torso measures—underbust circumference/weight, waist circumference/weight, and elbow circumference/weight—remained significantly higher among parous women. This represents a substantial reduction in the number and extent of significant differences compared to the pre‐matching results. Complete post‐matching results are provided in Tables S3 (t‐tests) and S4 (U tests), and variables showing statistically significant residual differences after matching are summarized in Tables 3 and 4.

A direct comparison of pre‐ and post‐matching results highlights how strongly demographic factors shaped the initial appearance of childbirth‐related differences. Whereas pre‐matching differences were broadly distributed across multiple body regions, post‐matching differences became highly localized and substantially reduced in magnitude. Before matching, large between‐group gaps were observed across numerous breadth, depth, and circumference measures, suggesting substantial postpartum widening of the torso and limbs. However, once age and BMI distributions were balanced through PSM, the majority of these differences disappeared. The transition from widespread differences (24 of 32 variables) to a small number of residual differences underscores the extent to which demographic structure influenced the initial findings. This marked reduction indicates that many of the pre‐matching disparities were not direct effects of childbirth, but rather reflections of underlying demographic structure—particularly age‐ and weight‐related patterns that differed between parous and nulliparous women. Taken together, these findings underscore that parity‐related morphological variation is far more modest and anatomically localized than raw group comparisons imply, and they emphasize the importance of rigorous confounder control when interpreting reproductive effects in anthropometric research.

Supplementary Analysis: Morphological Variation by Parity Level Within the Parous Group

To examine whether morphological variation differed by parity level, comparisons were conducted between women with one child (P1) and those with two or more children (P2+) across all 32 anthropometric variables. Depending on data normality, independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were applied. Initial analyses identified several variables showing significant differences between the two groups. In the parametric analysis, significant differences were observed for bust circumference/weight (t = 2.756, p = 0.006) and waist circumference/weight (t = 3.257, p = 0.001). In the nonparametric analysis, significant differences were found for bust circumference (U = 5151.5, Z = −2.893, p = 0.004), calf circumference (U = 5228.5, Z = −2.740, p = 0.006), upper arm circumference (U = 5123.0, Z = −2.949, p = 0.003), elbow circumference (U = 4964.0, Z = −3.265, p = 0.001), and hip circumference/weight (U = 5220.5, Z = −2.755, p = 0.006).

To determine whether these differences were attributable to parity level rather than underlying differences in age and BMI, ANCOVA was conducted for all 32 variables, with age and BMI included as covariates. After adjustment, only a subset of variables remained statistically significant. Specifically, waist circumference/weight (F = 9.133, p = 0.003) was significantly higher in women with one child (P1), whereas calf circumference (F = 8.261, p = 0.004), upper arm circumference (F = 9.171, p = 0.003), and elbow circumference (F = 8.027, p = 0.005) were significantly higher in women with two or more children (P2+).

All remaining variables, including those initially identified as significant in unadjusted analyses, were no longer statistically significant after adjustment. In total, 28 out of the 32 variables showed no significant differences between parity groups in the ANCOVA results.

Discussion

This study provides three key contributions to the interpretation of parity‐related morphological variation in human populations. First, by explicitly separating demographic structure from reproductive biology, the matched analytical framework suggests that many presumed postpartum differences may largely reflect age‐ and BMI‐associated processes rather than childbirth itself. Second, the findings refine life‐history perspectives by showing that any morphological signatures of reproduction are small, anatomically localized, and embedded within broader age‐graded trajectories of adipose and somatic redistribution. Third, by reducing confounding in observational anthropometric data, the study enhances the causal interpretability of biocultural analyses and provides a more robust empirical basis for understanding reproductive influences on adult female body shape.

This study revisited parity‐related morphological variation with the aim of evaluating which morphological differences, if any, persist once demographic confounding is addressed. Adult female morphology is shaped by interactions among adipose distribution, soft‐tissue properties, metabolic processes, and reproductive investment (Ellison 2003; Wells 2007; Wells et al. 2010). Although previous research has often attributed wider abdominal contours, flatter lower‐body profiles, or altered fat distribution to childbirth (Amiri et al. 2023; Wells et al. 2010), parity is strongly associated with age, BMI, and lifestyle transitions (Kuk et al. 2009; Wells et al. 2008; Zamboni et al. 1997), which may complicate causal interpretation.

Reevaluating Parity‐Related Morphological Variation

Unadjusted comparisons in this study reproduced well‐established patterns: parous women exhibited larger torso breadths and depths, greater circumferences, and longer soft‐tissue lengths in the breast area. These patterns appear to align with interpretations of widespread postpartum widening. However, once age and BMI were balanced through PSM, nearly all of these differences disappeared. This result suggests that many widely cited parity effects may reflect age‐related metabolic changes, increases in central adiposity, and lifestyle shifts associated with family formation rather than childbirth per se.

Nevertheless, three proportional indices—underbust circumference/weight, waist circumference/weight, and elbow circumference/weight—remained significantly higher among parous women after matching. These localized deviations indicate that subtle postpartum adjustments to soft or connective tissues may persist even when demographic structure is controlled. Conversely, the larger calf circumference among nulliparous women likely reflects behavioral or activity‐level variation rather than reproductive biology.

Such inconsistencies parallel previous findings that parity‐related redistribution in well‐nourished populations is variable and not consistently expressed across cultural contexts (Lassek and Gaulin 2006; Nenko and Jasienska 2009). Overall, the results suggest that parity‐related morphological variation tends to be modest, region‐specific, and anatomically constrained rather than global or extensive.

To further examine heterogeneity within the parous group, supplementary analyses were conducted comparing women with one child (P1) and those with two or more children (P2+). While initial unadjusted analyses identified several differences across anthropometric variables, most of these differences were attenuated after adjustment for age and BMI using ANCOVA. Only a small subset of variables remained significant at the p < 0.01 level, including waist circumference/weight, calf circumference, upper arm circumference, and elbow circumference. Notably, waist circumference/weight was higher in P1, whereas limb‐related girths were higher in P2+, indicating that parity‐level effects do not follow a consistent directional pattern. These findings suggest that parity‐related variation is not only limited in magnitude but also heterogeneous and localized, primarily affecting soft‐tissue distribution rather than structural body dimensions.

Interpreting Parity Within Broader Life‐History Trajectories

By separating demographic structure from reproductive biology, this study provides a clearer framework for interpreting the relationship between parity and body morphology. Whereas unadjusted analyses imply substantial postpartum expansion, PSM‐adjusted findings suggest that most apparent differences may reflect age‐ and BMI‐linked metabolic processes. This clarification challenges earlier interpretations that viewed parity as an independent driver of abdominal enlargement or shifts away from gluteo‐femoral fat stores.

In particular, previous life‐history models proposed that reproduction independently accelerates abdominal deposition and reorients energetic allocation toward offspring at the expense of somatic reserves. Yet because age and parity are strongly intertwined in cross‐sectional datasets, these earlier analyses could not fully disentangle reproductive effects from broader age‐related changes. The present results suggest that many patterns historically attributed to parity may instead reflect age‐graded energetic reallocation rather than reproduction alone.

Still, the persistence of small, localized differences after matching indicates that reproduction may leave subtle morphological signatures. However, these signatures are far more modest and anatomically limited than previously assumed. Parity‐related morphology is thus more appropriately conceptualized as a minor refinement embedded within the broader, age‐related redistribution trajectory that characterizes adult female life history. It is also possible that some observed differences may reflect selection processes, such as mate choice, rather than purely physiological effects of reproduction. However, as relevant variables were not available in the present dataset, this interpretation remains speculative.

Importantly, the additional parity‐level analysis further supports this interpretation by demonstrating that even within the parous group, differences are confined to a small number of circumference‐based variables and are not consistently expressed across anatomical regions. This reinforces the distinction between structural morphology and body composition, suggesting that parity‐related effects are more closely associated with localized soft‐tissue variation than with fundamental changes in body shape.

Overall, this matched analytical approach suggests that the morphological imprint of reproduction in contemporary human populations may be more limited than previously assumed. While unadjusted analyses suggest pronounced postpartum widening, controlling for demographic structure reveals that most apparent differences arise from age‐graded metabolic change rather than childbirth itself. The few remaining disparities—modest, anatomically localized, and inconsistent across traits—suggest that parity may contribute only a subtle refinement to the broader somatic trajectory characteristic of adult female life history. By reducing confounding in observational datasets, this framework advances anthropometric inference, providing a more causally grounded foundation for evaluating the interplay between reproduction, aging, and morphological variation in human populations.

Limitations and Future Study

Several limitations should be noted. First, parity was operationalized as a binary variable, limiting the ability to examine differences related to parity order, delivery mode, breastfeeding duration, or postpartum activity. In particular, detailed reproductive‐history variables were not available in the dataset, restricting more nuanced interpretation of parity‐related effects. Second, PSM controls only for observed covariates, meaning that unmeasured confounding may persist. Thus, residual confounding may remain despite efforts to balance key demographic variables. Third, the cross‐sectional dataset limits causal inference and precludes tracking temporal changes across the postpartum period. The sample consists entirely of Korean women aged 20–39 years. Although this addresses a population underrepresented in global morphology research (Cho et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2017), generalizability to other ecological or cultural contexts remains limited. In addition, 1:1 matching reduces sample size and may obscure subtle effects.

Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly when considering causal relationships between parity and morphological variation. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs that follow women across reproductive transitions, alternative matching strategies (such as kernel or full matching), and sampling frameworks that integrate matching into anthropometric survey design. Such approaches will allow more precise evaluation of the temporal and causal dynamics linking.
