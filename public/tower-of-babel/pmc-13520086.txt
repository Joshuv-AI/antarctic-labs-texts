A review on the application of biodegradable alginate-based films in post-harvest quality preservation of fruits and vegetables
Authors: Liang-jie Ba, Yu-ling Zhu, Cheng-lin Luo, Dong-lan Luo, Sen Cao, Zhong-qi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postharvest fruits and vegetables (FVs) are highly susceptible to quality deterioration caused by microbial contamination and endogenous metabolic processes, thereby increasing the demand for green and efficient preservation materials. Sodium alginate (SA), a naturally derived polysaccharide renowned for its outstanding film-forming capacity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, represents a promising bio-based candidate for fruit and vegetable preservation. However, pure SA films have inherent drawbacks, including poor mechanical strength, excessive hydrophilicity and insufficient biological activity. This review provides a systematic synthesis of the structural characteristics, fabrication methodologies, and preservation mechanisms of SA-based films. Furthermore, the advantages and limitations of four principal fabrication techniques—direct coating, solution casting, electrospinning, and layer-by-layer self-assembly—are critically evaluated. Five core mechanisms are clarified: physical barrier; antibacterial activity; antioxidant capacity; nano-reinforcement and intelligent responsiveness. To overcome the limitations of pure SA, this paper systematically analyses the effects of adding bioactive substances—such as plant extracts, antioxidants and nanofillers—on the functional properties of the films. The performance of the functional composite films produced through these modifications was significantly enhanced: for example, cross-linking with tannic acid increased the tensile strength of the film by 22.54%, reduced water vapour permeability by 25.36%, and simultaneously achieved near-total UV blocking. These performance improvements can lead to significant freshness-preserving effects in practical applications. Research has shown that a SA/sodium carboxymethyl cellulose/ε-polylysine composite coating can extend the shelf life of mangoes from 4 days to 10 days, reduce the incidence of black spot disease by 46.04%, and achieve an inhibition rate of 58.10% against Colletotrichum glabrum. Furthermore, after 12 days of storage, the total phenolic content of the coated mangoes was 27.44% higher than that of the control group, whilst the total flavonoid content was 52.17% higher, confirming that this system can delay fruit decay and maintain nutritional quality. In summary, this review confirms that functionalised SA composite films can achieve targeted preservation and real-time freshness monitoring of fruit and vegetables. Future research directions are proposed, encompassing scalable manufacturing processes, rational design of customized formulations, integrated safety and techno-economic assessments, and in-depth exploration of underlying molecular mechanisms—collectively offering a robust scientific foundation for the innovation of SA-based green preservation materials.

Introduction

Fruits and vegetables (FVs) are indispensable components of daily diets, with to their rich nutritional content and diverse physiological effects playing a crucial role in maintaining human health (Arivalagan et al., 2021). However, during harvesting, storage and transport, fruit and vegetables are susceptible to post-harvest quality deterioration caused by the combined effects of microbial contamination and endogenous metabolic processes, resulting in a significant reduction in their shelf life (Akın et al., 2025). This leads to significant nutrient loss and degradation of sensory attributes—such as appearance, texture, flavor, and aroma—thereby exacerbating postharvest food waste (Yao et al., 2023). According to statistics, 1.3 billion tonnes of edible food are wasted globally each year, with fruit and vegetables—which have a low shelf life and are prone to spoilage—accounting for approximately 40.00% of this total; such losses not only result in significant economic damage but also pose food safety risks, highlighting the urgent need to develop effective post-harvest preservation strategies (Wang, Qin, et al., 2025). To address this challenge, conventional preservation strategies such as low-temperature storage, modified atmosphere packaging, synthetic chemical preservatives, and natural biological preservatives have been widely adopted (Riseh et al., 2024). Nevertheless, traditional approaches remain associated with several limitations, such as high energy demand, substantial capital investment requirements, and potential risks of chemical residue accumulation. The petroleum-based materials, owing to their non-biodegradability, have exacerbated the environmental burden. Driven by the dual factors of food safety and ecological protection (Mostafavi & Zaeim, 2020), consumer demand for packaging solutions that ensure food quality stability and extend shelf life has been steadily increasing (Dehghani et al., 2018). In this context, novel functional packaging materials—such as controllable degradable films and active barrier composites—have emerged as pivotal solutions for maintaining food quality stability and achieving extending shelf life (Pillai et al., 2024). Currently, functional membrane systems engineered from natural biological macromolecules (such as proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids) have been applied in the field of food preservation engineering (Liu et al., 2024). Among biopolymers, sodium alginate (SA) has attracted considerable attention due to its unique “egg-crate” ionic cross-linking mechanism—a mechanism that confers upon it the ability to gel at room temperature and a tunable cross-linking density (Wang, Yang, & Yang, 2025), characteristics that are difficult to achieve under equally mild conditions when using chitosan, starch or cellulose derivatives. Furthermore, SA possesses excellent film-forming properties, biodegradability and biocompatibility, making it particularly suitable for preserving the structural integrity and bioactive characteristics of fresh FVs. Consequently, SA shows great potential for application in the field of fruit and vegetable preservation.

SA is a linear polysaccharide of marine origin (Brovko et al., 2023), due to its excellent biocompatibility (Wang et al., 2022), biodegradability (Brus et al., 2017), unique gelation properties (Fu et al., 2011), and as a carrier advantage, SA is widely used in the fields of food, medicine, and cosmetics. Moreover, its excellent safety profile and robust film-forming capability (Zhang, Wang et al., 2025), this material can be coated on the surface of FVs to form a protective layer, and can also be processed into packaging films, presenting broad application prospects in fruit and vegetable preservation and green packaging (Zhu, 2021). However, when SA is used as a sole film-forming agent, its mechanical properties are inadequate—exhibiting relatively low tensile and compressive strength, as well as limited elasticity (Ramdhan et al., 2020). Moreover, SA's inherent high hydrophilicity compromises the water resistance of pure SA films (Luan et al., 2024). Furthermore, the antioxidant and antibacterial properties of SA are insufficient, making it unable to effectively inhibit the growth of microorganisms on the surface of FVs and extend their shelf life, thus limiting its application in fruit and vegetable packaging (Qiao et al., 2024). Such drawbacks are not unique to SA; bibliometric research on global food packaging research trends has verified that unmodified bio-based edible films generally suffer from poor mechanical performance, excessive hydrophilicity and weak biological activity, which restricts their practical industrial utilization, and composite modification is regarded as an essential technical route to address the above deficiencies (Akin et al., 2023). Therefore, it is necessary to add different functional active substances to improve its performance. Enhancing the antibacterial, antioxidant, and mechanical properties of SA through incorporation of plant-derived extracts (e.g., essential oils and polyphenols), inorganic nanoparticles, and other natural polymers (e.g., proteins and cellulose) has been widely explored. To date, numerous studies have reported the use of SA as a functional matrix—combined with various bioactive agents—for fruit and vegetable preservation. For instance, Chen et al. (2021) incorporated thymol—a phenolic compound—into a SA matrix to fabricate a composite film exhibiting pronounced free radical scavenging activity and broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy. This formulation not only improved the mechanical integrity of the SA film but also demonstrated promising potential for application in active packaging systems for fresh-cut fruits. Similarly, Yu et al. (2025) integrated nanocellulose into the SA matrix, markedly enhancing both the mechanical strength and barrier performance of the resulting composite film. Looking ahead, future research will prioritise further optimization of the overall performance of these biobased composites—through rational formulation design and advancement of processing technologies.

Existing reviews on SA have largely focused on molecular structure and the properties of ion-crosslinked gels, covering diverse application areas such as biomedicine, environmental remediation and general food applications (Guo et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2021; Wang, Huang, et al., 2025). They have only briefly addressed the preservation of fruit and vegetables as a secondary topic, lacking a systematic integration of post-harvest horticultural products, edible SA film systems, preservation mechanisms and quantitative performance data. In contrast to the aforementioned broad-ranging review literature, this paper focuses specifically on functional SA films designed for post-harvest fruit and vegetables. It systematically compares the advantages and disadvantages of four mainstream film-forming processes and constructs a comprehensive theoretical framework for preservation comprising five synergistic pathways; It collates quantitative experimental data from composite modification and storage trials on fresh FVs such as mangoes, strawberries and spinach, quantitatively elucidating the effectiveness of modification techniques in addressing the inherent shortcomings of pure alginate films; it categorises and analyses various bioactive modification additives, and matches suitable formulations to the specific post-harvest deterioration challenges of different FVs; Based on the practical needs of the freshness preservation industry, this review proposes future development prospects, overcoming the limitations of existing reviews that focus solely on the research and development of general-purpose materials. This review fills a research gap in the field of post-harvest freshness preservation of horticultural products by providing a specialised and systematic evaluation of edible SA films.

Structure of SA and egg-box model.

As a natural polymer, SA is highly soluble in water, forming a viscous colloidal solution. After absorbing water, its volume can significantly expand, reaching 10 times its original volume (Guo et al., 2020). It dissolves slowly in cold water and can be accelerated by heating; it has better solubility in moderately alkaline environments, but overly strong alkaline conditions may lead to degradation of the molecular chains. Additionally, SA does not dissolve in common organic solvents such as ethanol and ether. In terms of gel properties, SA can undergo a transformation from a solution to a gel when present with divalent cations (such as Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, etc.) (Dodero et al., 2019). The mechanism lies in the combination of the cations with the carboxyl groups (-COO−) of SA, forming ionic cross-linking points, which promote the interconnection of molecular chains and thereby construct a stable three-dimensional network structure (Wang, Yang, ＆ Yang, 2025). Among them, the cross-linking induced by calcium ions (Ca2+) is one of the most core functional characteristics of alginate, and its mechanism is generally referred to as the “egg box” model (Fig. 1B). This model confirms that there are significant differences in the sensitivity of G, M and GM structural units to cationic cross-linking, with the G unit exhibiting far greater binding capacity than the M and MG units (Nakauma et al., 2016); consequently, the G/M ratio becomes the key molecular parameter determining the degree of SA cross-linking and the performance of the film. Although an excessively high G/M ratio can rapidly induce cross-linking, surface densification hinders the inward diffusion of Ca2+, leading to the formation of microcracks and an uneven network within the membrane; conversely, an excessively low G/M ratio results in an insufficient number of cross-linking sites, causing a loose network structure, reduced mechanical strength and increased water vapour permeability (Luan et al., 2024).

In the preservation of fruit and vegetables, the G/M ratio of SA-based films can be adjusted to regulate preservation efficacy by altering the film's water vapour and gas barrier properties, as well as its structural integrity. Dense, continuous SA films formed by Ca2+ cross-linking can effectively inhibit transpiration-induced water loss in fruit; the resulting microenvironment, characterized by low oxygen and high CO₂ levels, reduces aerobic respiration, thereby suppressing ethylene synthesis and minimizing the loss of nutrients such as organic acids and ascorbic acid, thus delaying ripening and senescence (Manzano et al., 2019). However, the inherent high hydrophilicity of SA imposes an upper limit on its water vapour barrier performance; simply adjusting the G/M ratio is insufficient to fully overcome this limitation. It is therefore necessary to strategically incorporate hydrophobic additives (such as essential oils) or nanostructured materials to construct a composite barrier with synergistic gas–moisture regulation capabilities. Ultimately, in practical applications, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the physiological characteristics of the fruit, its surface microstructure, and storage temperature and humidity conditions, and to precisely optimize the G/M ratio and composite modification strategies in order to achieve multi-objective, precise regulation of post-harvest metabolic processes.

Preparation methods of SA-based films

As a protective barrier for the surfaces of FVs, polysaccharide-based films form uniform, structurally intact, solid, and dense layers on produce surfaces. Such films effectively impede the ingress of external contaminants and retard the deterioration of intrinsic food quality, thereby extending the shelf life of FVs (Ding et al., 2025). Concurrently, to preserve the original sensory and functional attributes of the produce, the film material must exhibit adequate optical clarity and be essentially odorless (Suhag et al., 2020). Consequently, the fabrication methodology critically governs the final performance and practical efficacy of polysaccharide-based films. At present, the main methods for producing fruit and vegetable packaging films include direct coating, solution casting, electrospinning and layer-by-layer self-assembly; the preparation processes are shown in Fig. 2. Comprehensive comparison of four film preparation technologies.

Direct coating

The preservation coating represents a safe and straightforward technology for extending the shelf life of FVs (Oyom et al., 2022). By forming a protective film on the surface of produce, this approach effectively retards spoilage and helps maintain sensory quality—including appearance, texture, and flavor—throughout storage (Aziz & Salama, 2022). Direct coating technology, an effective surface treatment method for FVs, primarily employs immersion, spraying, and brushing techniques (Zhang et al., 2021). It mixes natural biopolymer materials (such as starch, SA, etc.) or combines with active substances to construct a composite system, and uses controlled coating technology to uniformly apply it onto the surface of FVs to form a biodegradable film layer. After drying, a dense barrier is formed, and the FVs are preserved (Liu et al., 2023). Notably, because the coating directly contacts food and becomes an integral component of the final product, it must satisfy stringent sensory requirements—including color, texture, and flavor compatibility—to ensure no adverse impact on the inherent sensory attributes of the treated FVs (Abedi et al., 2021). Coating-based preservation has been widely adopted for extending the shelf life of FVs. For example, SA coatings—either alone or in combination with varying concentrations of montmorillonite (MMT)—have been successfully applied to strawberries, significantly preserving their freshness. These findings demonstrate that alginate-based composite coatings effectively prolong strawberry shelf life and hold considerable promise for broader applications in food preservation (Yan et al., 2024). However, several practical challenges hinder the scalability and efficacy of coating-based preservation. Repeated use of coating solutions—particularly in immersion coating—can lead to microbial contamination from the surface microflora of the initial fruit batch, thereby creating cross-contamination risks. Additional limitations include inconsistent coating uniformity, reliance on operator-dependent techniques (e.g., brushing or spraying), and material waste due to solution dripping or overspray. Collectively, these factors constrain the industrial implementation of coating technologies. Addressing these challenges thus necessitates targeted technological innovations (Wang, Zhai, et al. 2023).

Solution casting method

The solvent casting method—a simple, cost-effective film fabrication technique—has been widely adopted in food packaging applications, particularly for preparing polysaccharide-based films (Wei et al., 2025). Its typical processing workflow is as follows: First, the film-forming polymer is dissolved in an appropriate solvent at a controlled temperature to yield a homogeneous solution. Subsequently, plasticizers—such as glycerol—are incorporated in optimised amounts to enhance the film's flexibility and mechanical robustness (Wang, Xue, et al., 2024); additionally, bioactive agents—including essential oils and phenolic compounds—may be introduced in precise proportions to confer antibacterial and antioxidant functionalities (Oliveira et al., 2025). Finally, the resulting film-forming solution undergoes ultrasonic degassing to eliminate entrapped air bubbles, is cast uniformly onto a flat polyethylene mold under controlled environmental conditions, dried to remove the solvent, and subjected to post-drying equilibration to yield a structurally stable film with consistent and desirable functional properties (Wu, Wang, et al., 2025). In numerous studies, this method has been widely employed to fabricate SA-based preservative films. For instance, Ning et al. (2025) developed a composite film composed of SA and konjac glucomannan for strawberry preservation; their findings demonstrated that the film significantly extended the shelf life of strawberries while effectively maintaining their quality throughout storage. This film preparation approach is characterized by its operational simplicity and compositional flexibility, rendering it highly promising for the research, development, and practical application of active packaging materials in fruit and vegetable preservation.

Electrospinning method

Electrospinning technology—characterized by its simplicity, operational ease, and high processing efficiency—offers significant application advantages across diverse industries, particularly in food packaging (Jiang et al., 2025). Compared with conventional membrane fabrication techniques, electrospinning represents an innovative and highly effective approach for nanofiber production, demonstrating distinct superiority in the encapsulation of thermosensitive bioactive compounds (Zhang et al., 2021). Electrospun films exhibit several advantageous structural and functional properties, including an exceptionally high specific surface area, a highly aligned molecular architecture, a precisely tunable porous morphology, and outstanding adhesion to underlying substrates—attributes that render them especially promising for fruit and vegetable packaging applications (Amjadi et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). The typical film fabrication process via electrospinning proceeds as follows: A polymer-based spinning solution is loaded into a syringe and extruded through a metallic needle. Under the influence of a high-voltage electrostatic field, the charged solution at the needle tip forms a characteristic Taylor cone, followed by jet ejection driven by electrostatic forces (Fig. 2). The ejected jet undergoes extensive stretching and spiral elongation during flight, ultimately depositing onto a grounded collector to yield a uniform, dense, and continuous nanofibrous membrane (Topuz & Uyar, 2020). Due to its polyelectrolyte properties, SA exhibits limited molecular chain entanglement in conventional aqueous solutions, thereby affecting its electrospinning performance. To overcome this limitation, highly polar cosolvents (such as glycerol) are typically added to enhance the solution's spinnability, or SA is blended with polymers that are readily electrospun, utilising synergistic chain entanglement to facilitate the formation of stable, continuous SA-based nanofibres (Zhou et al., 2025). However, for food contact applications, the selection of blend components depends not only on the feasibility of the electrospinning process but, more crucially, on the food safety implications. Existing research shows marked divergence on this issue. Wang, Tang, et al. (2023) used a blend of the synthetic polymer PVA and SA to prepare active packaging materials for grape preservation; although the film properties were improved by utilising PVA's chemical stability and high mechanical strength, the potential migration risks associated with PVA as a synthetic polymer in food contact were not assessed—given that the high porosity and large specific surface area of electrospun nanofibres significantly increase the probability of migration of both active components and matrix materials (Amjadi et al., 2020), this omission is particularly significant in applications involving direct food contact. In contrast, Amjadi et al. (2020) selected the natural protein Zein as the blending component for SA. They verified the biocompatibility of the nanofibres through in vitro cytotoxicity assays (HUVEC), with cell survival rates remaining above 80% after 72 h of culture, and explicitly recommended that such porous electrospun materials should be used as the active inner layer of a laminated film rather than as an outer layer in direct contact with food, in order to mitigate migration risks. This difference in strategy highlights a key issue: the food safety of SA-based electrospun nanofibres is highly dependent on the intrinsic safety of the blend components and their mode of contact with food. Compared with synthetic polymers (such as PVA), natural biopolymers (such as Zein, proteins or polysaccharides) not only form effective entanglements with SA via intermolecular hydrogen bonds but also reduce the risk of migration from non-food-grade synthetic materials; however, even when natural components are used, the high porosity of the nanofibres may still lead to the uncontrolled release of active components (such as nanoparticles or plant extracts); therefore, a systematic safety assessment of the final material must be conducted, combining migration testing and cytotoxicity evaluation.

Layer-by-layer self-assembly (LBL)

LBL assembly is a highly efficient and versatile technique for fabricating multicomponent composite thin films. This approach leverages molecular-level interactions—such as electrostatic forces, covalent bonding, and hydrogen bonding—among two or more constituent materials to enable the sequential, alternating deposition of polymers onto a template substrate, thereby enabling precise construction of the target composite film (Jiang et al., 2025). At its core, LbL assembly operates on the principle of complementary electrostatic attraction: polyelectrolytes are deposited in a stepwise manner onto surfaces bearing opposite surface charges, allowing for effective integration of diverse functional components and nanoscale-precise control over film thickness. Furthermore, the process is operationally simple, requires no specialised instrumentation, and is compatible with substrates of arbitrary geometry (Liu et al., 2023). During the assembly process, polyelectrolytes—including both natural biopolymers and synthetic polymers—serve as the fundamental structural units. Their ionization state is pH-responsive, enabling precise modulation of polymer deposition behavior and the resulting film's physicochemical properties. Excessive surface charge arising from charge reversal can be effectively mitigated by optimizing solution parameters (e.g., pH, ionic strength) and fine-tuning the stoichiometric ratio of constituent polyelectrolytes, thereby enhancing the structural stability of the assembled system (Zhang et al., 2022).

LBL assembly technology shows great potential in the field of food preservation. Multilayer composite films prepared using this method exhibit excellent barrier properties and intrinsic bioactivity. Li, Wu, and Li (2024) developed a bilayer packaging film that combines CO₂ colourimetric sensing with antimicrobial activity; the inner layer utilises a agarose matrix containing methyl red/bromothymol blue indicators, whilst the outer layer is based on SA/carboxymethyl cellulose with tannic acid added as an antimicrobial agent. A 14-day freshness preservation trial involving strawberries refrigerated at 4 °C demonstrated that the optimised film significantly delayed fruit quality deterioration, reducing weight loss from 14.54% in the control group to 10.57%, whilst maintaining good levels of firmness and soluble solids. Furthermore, the inner indicator layer undergoes a marked color change (ΔE > 10) due to the accumulation of CO₂ produced by strawberry respiration and microbial metabolism, thereby enabling real-time, non-destructive freshness monitoring. This demonstrates that LBL assembly technology can efficiently integrate multiple functions—including sensing, antimicrobial activity and water vapour barrier properties—to meet the composite preservation requirements of fresh fruit and vegetables. However, LBL technology has inherent limitations in its application: Firstly, the multi-layer alternating deposition process is time-consuming and has relatively low production efficiency, limiting its economic viability for industrialization; for example, the electrospinning-based LbL method employed by Jiang et al. (2025) requires precise control of multiple parameters for each layer (voltage, flow rate, spinning time, and collector speed), making continuous mass production challenging; furthermore, electrospinning-based LbL preparation often relies on organic solvents such as acetic acid and ethanol, and solvent residues may pose biosafety risks in food-contact applications. Secondly, existing research has largely focused on verifying short-term preservation effects (Jiang et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025), whilst there remains a lack of systematic characterization of the interlayer interface stability and functional degradation mechanisms of LbL films under long-term storage conditions with fluctuating temperature and humidity. Consequently, future research should prioritise the development of green, solvent-free assembly methods and establish a comprehensive performance evaluation system covering long-term storage cycles, in order to effectively enhance the commercial viability of LbL-based packaging systems.

Preservation mechanism of SA-based fresh-keeping film

Intrinsic property

During storage and transportation, FVs are typically exposed directly to ambient environmental conditions, rendering them highly susceptible to microbial contamination, water evaporation, and respiratory activity—factors that collectively contribute to quality deterioration and adversely affect both visual appearance and functional usability. Active packaging offers a promising solution by forming a conformal, surface-adherent layer around produce, thereby establishing a dense physical barrier that effectively isolates the commodities from external environmental stressors and provides fundamental preservation functionality (Zhang et al., 2026). This barrier property enables partial retention of internal moisture by suppressing water migration and inhibiting transpiration, thus helping to maintain freshness. Moreover, active packaging can modulate surface hydrophilicity, promote equilibrium of surrounding humidity, and minimize the transmembrane vapour pressure gradient—thereby reducing oxygen diffusion and contact with the produce surface, ultimately decelerating oxidative reactions and enhancing preservation efficacy (Dai et al., 2024). Alginate-based packaging films regulate gas and water vapour transmission by adjusting matrix structural parameters (including film thickness, ionic cross-linking density and the intrinsic G/M monomer ratio), thereby exhibiting outstanding physical barrier properties. These three core factors control oxygen transmission rate (OTR) and water vapour transmission rate (WVP). Research by Luan et al. confirmed that a uniform cross-linked network is formed when the G/M ratio of SA is 0.85, achieving the lowest WVP value (4.0 × 10−12 g·mm·m−2·h−1·Pa−1) among SA samples of different specifications. Provided that the polymer composition and calcium ion cross-linking conditions remain consistent, film thickness exhibits a linear negative correlation with both oxygen transmission rate and water vapour transmission rate. The addition of maize alcohol-soluble protein to SA with a moderate G-segment content can increase the thickness to 0.074 mm, reducing the WVP value by 30.00%. The enhanced ability of the film to barrier against water vapour and gases stems from the lengthening of the tortuous diffusion paths for water vapour and oxygen molecules within the thickened matrix. Meanwhile, Salama et al. (2019) prepared a ternary edible coating system composed of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), SA and chitosan biguanide hydrochloride (CBg). As a polycationic multifunctional cross-linking agent, chitosan biguanide hydrochloride interacts electrostatically with the carboxylate groups on the molecular chains of carboxymethyl cellulose and SA, thereby increasing the overall cross-linking density of the matrix: following the addition of 15.00 wt% chitosan biguanide hydrochloride to a CMC/SA binary matrix, the WVP value decreased to 54.00% of that of the control film; furthermore, during the tomato shelf-life study, this high-crosslinking-density ternary coating effectively inhibited fruit transpiration, significantly reducing weight loss during the storage of fruit and vegetables.

Antibacterial effect

Although SA offers inherent advantages—including excellent film-forming capability and high biocompatibility—its standalone use as a packaging film is limited by insufficient antibacterial and antioxidant activity, relatively low mechanical strength, and excessive hydrophilicity. These shortcomings constrain its efficacy in postharvest preservation and its ability to protect FVs. To broaden its applicability, numerous studies have functionalized SA through the incorporation of antibacterial and antioxidant agents, as well as nanostructured modifiers (Fig. 3), thereby enhancing both the physical properties and functional performance of the resulting films to meet the diverse requirements of fruit and vegetable preservation.Fig. 3Preservation mechanism of SA-based composite film.Fig. 3

Preservation mechanism of SA-based composite film.

To enhance the antibacterial ability of the preservation substrate materials, natural antibacterial active components can be introduced into them to achieve the inhibitory effect on microbial spoilage. Biological macromolecules (such as chitosan) (Li, Liu, et al., 2024), plant extracts (such as essential oils) (Chen et al., 2025), and antibiotic additives (Wang, Yu, et al., 2025) are all effective components that give the preservation materials their antibacterial properties. They can be added to the film-forming substrates to help them achieve certain antibacterial effects. Taking chitosan as an example, chitosan not only serves as a film-forming agent in preservation applications, but can also be added as an antibacterial agent to alginate to enhance its antibacterial performance. Chitosan, as an antibacterial agent, can disrupt the microbial cell membrane structure through electrostatic interaction and chelate the essential nutrients for their growth. Low-molecular-weight chitosan can also penetrate the cells and interfere with the synthesis of nucleic acids and mitochondrial energy metabolism, thereby achieving the purpose of inhibiting microbial growth and reproduction, and effectively delaying the spoilage of food caused by microbial proliferation (Ke et al., 2021). Quantitative antimicrobial assays have shown that chitosan exhibits broad-spectrum inhibitory activity; in particular, low-molecular-weight chitosan is effective against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as foodborne pathogenic fungi such as Candida albicans. Its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) range for Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus is 3.20 to 7.50 mg/mL (Li et al., 2026). In order to enhance the antimicrobial properties of alginate films, Zeng et al. (2022) employed chitosan (CS) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) as wall materials to fabricate microcapsules (LMs), which were subsequently incorporated into the films. Antibacterial assays demonstrated that incorporating chitosan markedly enhanced the antimicrobial activity of the composite films, with higher LM loading levels yielding stronger antibacterial effects. Specifically, at an LM concentration of 2.00% (w/w), the composite film exhibited antibacterial efficacies of 92.09% against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and 93.47% against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). Although chitosan possesses excellent broad-spectrum antibacterial properties, it has significant limitations in its application: its poor water solubility under neutral and alkaline conditions means that its antibacterial activity is only effective at pH < 6.5, which restricts its practical application in fresh fruit and vegetables stored in most near-neutral environments.

As a complementary natural antimicrobial alternative, plant essential oils overcome the pH-dependence associated with chitosan and maintain stable antimicrobial and antifungal properties under acidic, neutral and alkaline storage conditions (Chen et al., 2025). Due to their sustainability as plant-derived substances, their biosafety and their multifunctional antioxidant and antibacterial properties, essential oils are highly favoured in the food packaging sector. Clove oil, for instance, exerts potent antibacterial effects through multiple mechanisms: it suppresses bacterial growth by inhibiting the activity of critical metabolic enzymes; directly interacts with bacterial DNA, thereby disrupting its structural integrity and functional capacity; and attenuates virulence factor production via dual interference with protein binding and gene expression (Li, Li, Shi, et al., 2022). In related research, a composite active packaging film was successfully developed using clove essential oil (CEO) as the primary functional agent. Results demonstrated that the composite film incorporating a 4.00% (v/v) CEO emulsion exhibited antibacterial inhibition rates of 79.51% ± 0.63% against S. aureus and 82.83% ± 0.52% against E. coli. Moreover, the composite film containing a 3.00% (v/v) CEO emulsion reduced the spoilage rate of tomatoes by over 80.00% after 16 days of storage, confirming its pronounced antibacterial efficacy (Wu, Yue, et al., 2025). Similarly, in the study by Rashid et al. (2024), incorporation of CEO emulsion into an alginate-based composite film conferred robust antibacterial properties to the matrix. Notably, increasing the CEO concentration from 4.00% to 10.00% (v/v) led to a significant enhancement in inhibitory activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Overall, compared with low-molecular-weight chitosan at equivalent doses, various plant essential oils exhibit weaker intrinsic antibacterial efficacy against bacteria; however, they possess excellent antifungal activity, which compensates for chitosan's limited ability to inhibit mold growth under neutral pH conditions. Whilst synthetic antibiotics exhibit extremely low MIC values at the microgram level, their use in food packaging systems may result in the contamination of fresh agricultural produce with toxic residues; they have a narrow antimicrobial spectrum; and they are virtually incapable of inhibiting food-spoiling fungi (Wang, Yu, et al., 2025). Consequently, by combining the structural advantages of alginate film substrates, the incorporation of chitosan and plant essential oils into a single alginate matrix has become the mainstream optimisation strategy.

Antioxidant activity

Adding antioxidants to the film-forming matrix can effectively endow the film with antioxidant properties, thereby delaying the oxidation and spoilage process of harvested FVs, and significantly extending their shelf life. (Liu et al., 2025). In such applications, natural polyphenols, plant extracts, etc. are commonly used active ingredients, such as tea polyphenols (TP) (Zhong et al., 2024), curcumin (Zhan et al., 2020), rosemary extract (Dumitriu et al., 2024), etc. Due to their excellent antioxidant properties, they have become common choices for improving the preservation effect of FVs. These active substances can not only directly block or delay the oxidative browning and nutrient loss of FVs through mechanisms such as eliminating free radicals in the storage environment and inhibiting the activity of oxidation enzymes; but also combine with the adhesion of the preservation solution and the slow-release property of the preservation film to form an immediate protective layer for long-term antioxidant protection, providing multi-dimensional assistance in maintaining the freshness and quality of harvested FVs (Li, Yang, et al., 2025). Nartea et al. (2023) demonstrated in their study on tea polyphenols that the phenolic hydroxyl groups within the molecular structure confer a dual antioxidant mechanism: first, by donating hydrogen atoms to scavenge free radicals; and second, by interrupting oxidative chain propagation—thereby significantly enhancing overall antioxidant capacity. In research on composite membranes, an increase in the concentration of tea polyphenol–iron (TP–Fe) nanoparticles was found to correspond linearly with enhanced antioxidant activity of the films, indicating a dose-dependent relationship between antioxidant efficacy and the concentration of bioactive antioxidant components (Wu, Wang, et al., 2025). Orhotohwo et al. (2025) discovered by adding the antioxidant by-product of fennel, which is derived from the secondary plant products of seaweed, to the SA composite film-forming agent that its woody stems and flowers are rich in phenolic substances and can remove free radicals through the hydrogen donor property, resulting in a 51.68% increase in the antioxidant activity of DPPH and a 76.24% increase in the antioxidant activity of ABTS in the mixture. The inhibition rates of the composite film for DPPH and ABTS reached 80.94% and 82.40% respectively, effectively inhibiting oxidative deterioration.

It is worth noting that there is not always a direct correlation between the in vitro antioxidant activity of a film (such as its DPPH/ABTS radical scavenging capacity) and its actual in situ antioxidant protective effect on agricultural produce. In vitro assessments are typically conducted in homogeneous, idealised systems and primarily reflect the ability of active substances to scavenge specific free radicals; however, the actual preservation of fruit and vegetables involves a dynamic process in which multiple factors—such as oxygen permeation, water migration, enzymatic reactions, microbial contamination, and the release and diffusion of active substances within the film—are coupled. Consequently, a high in vitro scavenging rate does not necessarily translate into an equivalent level of storage preservation efficacy; this discrepancy represents a key scientific issue in evaluating the practical performance of films. Nevertheless, in vitro data can still serve as a preliminary indicator of a film's antioxidant potential; however, its actual preservation efficacy in practical applications must be comprehensively evaluated in conjunction with quality indicators such as color, firmness, total phenolic content and weight loss rate of agricultural produce during storage. Consequently, in the development of active packaging films, research into the correlation between in vitro antioxidant capacity and actual preservation performance should be prioritised to provide a more comprehensive basis for assessing their application value.

Nano-filling reinforcement effect

Based on the optimization and regulation of the performance of the composite preservation film through the antibacterial and antioxidant functional components, the nanofillers can also significantly enhance the modification effect of the alginate film. When uniformly dispersed within the film matrix, these nanofillers facilitate the formation of new ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions between polymer molecular chains. On one hand, they serve as effective stress-transfer agents, thereby improving the mechanical properties of the composite film; on the other hand, they establish a nanoscale physical barrier structure that elongates the diffusion pathways for gases and small molecules, thereby markedly enhancing the film's barrier performance (Dai et al., 2024; Deol et al., 2026). In addition, some nanofillers, due to their inherent properties, can cause oxidative stress damage to microorganisms through pathways such as oxidation damage mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS), direct contact with microorganisms, etc., destroying the integrity of their cell membranes, interfering with their normal metabolic processes, and ultimately leading to cell apoptosis (Gao et al., 2025). At present, many studies have introduced nano-materials such as nano-silica, montmorillonite MMT (Yan et al., 2024), and nano‑zinc oxide (Arroyo et al., 2020) into the alginate matrix to enhance its performance. It is worth noting that although metallic nanomaterials such as zinc oxide nanoparticles and silver nanoparticles possess a range of functional advantages, they can migrate into the food matrix, posing safety risks that cannot be ignored; furthermore, excessive addition of nanofillers can lead to particle agglomeration and the formation of microcracks within the alginate film, creating pathways for the diffusion of precipitates. Current regulations governing food contact materials only impose limits on total metal residues, without distinguishing between nanoparticle and ionic forms, resulting in deficiencies in the safety assessment framework. Therefore, appropriately controlling the addition of such nano-fillers in alginate-based substrates and ensuring their uniform dispersion is key to balancing the functional performance of the material with migration safety. In the study by Li, Li, Zhang, et al. (2022), silver nanoparticles were added to the alginate/gelatin composite matrix and their effects on the film performance were investigated. The research results showed that the appropriate introduction of silver nanoparticles could form coordination bonds with the alginate molecular chains, promoting a denser internal bonding of the film; not only did this optimize the water-blocking performance, mechanical strength, and thermal stability of the film, but also due to the inherent properties of silver nanoparticles, the film was endowed with excellent anti-microbial effects. More importantly, the determination of silver residues in citrus fruits after 15 days of storage confirmed that the silver migration level remained within the safe limit stipulated by food contact material standards.

Intelligent response effect

Currently, although conventional SA films can extend the shelf life of FVs to a certain extent, the continuous advancement of consumer expectations regarding food quality has driven growing interest in intelligent responsive packaging. Such packaging systems are capable of promptly detecting external environmental stimuli—including temperature, humidity, pH, and gaseous composition—and dynamically modulating the internal microenvironment of the package to meet the evolving preservation requirements of fresh produce. Consequently, intelligent responsive packaging has emerged as a key developmental direction in next-generation food packaging technologies. (Li, Liu, et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). In current research, incorporating diverse functional components into SA films to confer environmental responsiveness has emerged as a pivotal strategy for developing intelligent active packaging and has been widely adopted. Among these, pH-responsive intelligent films—capable of sensitively detecting microenvironmental changes such as metabolic acid generation and the release of volatile alkaline gases during fruit and vegetable storage—are the most extensively investigated and most widely implemented in postharvest preservation applications. Concurrently, visually responsive intelligent preservation films—enabling real-time, non-invasive monitoring of produce freshness—have drawn substantial attention from food preservation researchers, owing to their distinct advantages of intuitive colorimetric feedback and dynamic, continuous monitoring capability.

It has been progressively adopted in practical applications. The underlying principle of this responsive approach is to incorporate pH-sensitive indicators—such as anthocyanins, syringin (Wu, Su, et al., 2024), betaine extract (Guo et al., 2023), methyl red, and bromothymol blue—into the film-forming matrix, thereby endowing the resulting packaging film with pH-responsive functionality. These indicators undergo visible color changes in response to shifts in the pH of the surrounding environment adjacent to the preservation film. Critically, such pH variations correlate directly with the freshness status of FVs across different storage durations; consequently, the film's colorimetric response provides an intuitive, real-time visual indicator of product quality, enabling effective on-site monitoring of postharvest freshness (Abedi-Firoozjah et al., 2022). Among these, anthocyanins have attracted considerable attention from both industry and the public owing to their broad applicability and robust functional performance. Recently, numerous studies have incorporated anthocyanins extracted from diverse plant sources into film-forming matrices, endowing the resulting films with pH-responsive colorimetric properties. In food preservation applications, a distinct color transition—from purple to dark blue—has been clearly observed under varying pH conditions (Park et al., 2022).

However, unmodified natural anthocyanins, when used as smart pH indicators in food, are prone to degradation by ultraviolet light and high temperatures, leading to photothermal degradation; their high water solubility means that water vapour released from fruit and vegetables can cause anthocyanins to precipitate; and the low cross-linking density and high oxygen permeability of the film can easily lead to the oxidation of anthocyanins. These shortcomings severely limit their industrial application. To address these shortcomings, existing research has proposed the use of green, biocompatible cross-linking modifiers (such as dialdehyde starch (DAS) and chitosan-carrageenan microcapsule carriers) as effective solutions. In the study by Zhu et al., dialdehyde starch (DAS) was used to cross-link the rice protein / SA (RP/SA) film-forming matrix. Through a large number of Schiff base bonds and intermolecular hydrogen bonds, a dense, pore-free film network structure was constructed, thereby physically locking the anthocyanin molecules within the matrix, inhibiting pigment leaching and photothermal decomposition, whilst simultaneously optimizing the composite film's water/ oxygen barrier properties and mechanical properties. When applied to the storage monitoring of fresh-cut Hami melons, this smart film exhibits a visually discernible, stepwise color change from yellow-green to reddish-brown as the fruit decays, providing an intuitive, real-time visual signal of freshness decline in fruit and vegetables (Zhu et al., 2024). Meanwhile, Park et al. co-encapsulated functional natural bioactive compounds with pH-sensitive anthocyanins within chitosan-carrageenan capsules; by utilising a polyelectrolyte composite capsule wall to isolate the anthocyanin molecules from direct contact with water vapour, they slowed down the structural degradation of the pigments and endowed the film with dual functions of freshness indication and antimicrobial activity (Park et al., 2022). These matrix modification and microencapsulation strategies can specifically address core deficiencies in anthocyanins, such as photothermal decomposition, water-soluble leaching and oxidative inactivation, thereby providing reliable technical support for the industrial and commercial application of anthocyanin-based smart indicator films.

Physiological regulation mechanisms for induced wound healing and disease defense

Mechanical damage is one of the key factors contributing to the deterioration of the quality of harvested fruit and vegetables. In addition to providing a physical barrier, SA composite films can also promote wound healing and disease resistance through various physiological regulatory mechanisms. The activation of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway is a well-established mechanism. Zhang et al. (2023) applied a combined coating of chlorogenic acid and SA to pear fruits and found that this treatment significantly activated the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway at the wound site. Specifically, this was manifested by a significant upregulation in the activity of key enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H), 4-coumaroyl-CoA lyase (4CL), cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), thereby promoting the accumulation of monomeric phenolic acids such as trans-cinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid and ferulic acid. This, in turn, facilitates the deposition of lignin and suberin polyphenols in the cell walls of the wound, accelerates callus formation and enhances the mechanical strength of the tissue. Complementing this, enzyme-mediated oxidative defense and signal-induced responses represent another emerging pathway. Sun et al. (2025) encapsulated glucose oxidase (GOx) within a carboxymethyl chitosan/SA composite membrane to construct a glucose/wound-responsive active packaging system. This membrane functions via a dual mechanism: on the one hand, GOx catalyses the oxidation of glucose exuded from the wound, consuming the carbon source and producing H₂O₂, thereby creating a “starvation-like” antibacterial microenvironment that directly inhibits the gray mold fungus; on the other hand, the H₂O₂ produced acts as a second messenger to activate the fruit's own defense system, manifested as increased β-1,3-glucanase activity and reduced levels of malondialdehyde, a product of membrane lipid peroxidation.The above studies indicate that SA composite films can provide effective protection and promote the healing of post-harvest mechanical wounds through various mechanisms, such as activating the plants' own secondary metabolism or introducing exogenous enzyme-catalysed oxidation systems, demonstrating the potential for applications in the preservation of FVs.

Application of SA-based films in food preservation

FVs possess a high water content, rendering them particularly vulnerable to spoilage and quality deterioration caused by microbial proliferation during transportation and storage. Consequently, the development and implementation of effective preservation techniques are critically important for extending their shelf life. In recent years, post-harvest spoilage control strategies based on biopolymers have emerged as a prominent research focus in fruit and vegetable preservation. Among these biopolymers, SA—owing to its natural origin, favorable functional properties, and regulatory acceptance—has attracted considerable attention in food packaging applications. The efficacy of alginate-based films primarily arises from their inherent barrier properties, excellent compatibility with other biopolymers and additives (Yu et al., 2024), and capacity to efficiently encapsulate and deliver bioactive agents (Faidi et al., 2019). Leveraging these attributes, SA has been formulated into diverse functional formats for fruit and vegetable preservation, including direct edible coatings, freestanding preservation films, and intelligent films capable of wound healing and responsive behavior to external stimuli such as light, pH, and temperature. To date, numerous studies have experimentally validated the preservative performance of SA across these application modalities. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while the extent of preservation efficacy may vary depending on factors—including produce type, SA concentration, formulation composition, and storage conditions—the consistent ability of SA to delay spoilage onset and maintain sensory, nutritional, and textural quality is well established (Fig. 4). These advances collectively underscore SA's value as a versatile, multifunctional biopolymer with significant potential for sustainable post-harvest preservation.Fig. 4Application of SA-based composite films in fruit and vegetable preservation.Fig. 4

Among various postharvest preservation strategies, coating application has emerged as one of the most promising approaches—owing to its operational simplicity, uniform surface coverage, and direct adhesion to the surfaces of FVs. When formulated as a coating, SA forms a semi-permeable barrier on produce surfaces, effectively modulating internal–external gas exchange, minimizing moisture loss, and simultaneously serving as a functional carrier for bioactive compounds to enable extended shelf life. Consequently, investigating the efficacy of SA–based coatings in fruit and vegetable preservation holds significant practical value for broadening their implementation across real-world production systems. However, the coating technique still suffers from several limitations, including non-uniform film formation, high residual solvent content, poor substrate adhesion, susceptibility to delamination, and limited capability for fabricating structures with precise microstructural control. Consequently, a dedicated membrane fabrication process is required to produce an integrated active packaging film—enabling accurate tailoring of both the film's microstructure and its functional properties. This film-based approach complements the conventional coating strategy. In the film application, bioactive agents are first incorporated into SA to formulate a composite membrane material endowed with tailored functionalities; the resulting film is then applied to fruit preservation via methods such as encapsulation or surface covering. To further enhance performance in fruit preservation, current research predominantly focuses on modifying SA through the strategic incorporation of functionally specific active substances.

Existing evidence confirms the effectiveness of SA composite membranes in extending the shelf life of fresh produce. Notably, these composites demonstrate significant inhibitory activity against key pathogenic microorganisms responsible for postharvest decay in FVs. Natural plant extracts and essential oils—owing to their inherent non-toxicity, biocompatibility, and environmental sustainability—have emerged as a preferred strategy for modifying alginate-based membranes. These bioactive agents exhibit considerable promise in extending the shelf life of diverse FVs. Their primary antibacterial mechanism involves disrupting the structural integrity of pathogenic bacterial cell membranes on produce surfaces, thereby inhibiting bacterial metabolic activity and reducing pathogen colonization and subsequent tissue infection. Consequently, they enable effective suppression of surface-associated pathogens. To date, numerous studies have confirmed that incorporating such natural antimicrobial agents significantly enhances the antibacterial efficacy of alginate membranes. For example, the research by Liu et al. (2021) demonstrated that the protective coating made from alginate and asparagus waste extract effectively inhibited the growth of Penicillium italicum on strawberries on the 8th day of storage, with only a few insignificant rotting occurring. Similarly, incorporating green tea extract into the alginate–gelatin composite coating effectively enhances its functional properties. Research demonstrates that this modified coating significantly reduces the incidence of star fruit spoilage, effectively suppresses pH changes in the fruit, and well preserves both fruit hardness and color—thereby markedly delaying the deterioration of star fruit quality (Ulfa et al., 2024). In experimental trials conducted by Wardak et al. (2024), alginate coatings blended with kiwi seed essential oil powerfully restrained the growth of Penicillium italicum. Fruits coated with the composite material recorded just 5.10% weight loss after the whole storage cycle. Similarly, a separate study applied an alginate composite coating enriched with ginger essential oil to fresh-cut pears and reported a 58.40% reduction in microbial colony counts during storage relative to the uncoated control group (Lamani & Ramaswamy, 2023). In the context of vegetable preservation, Rashid et al. (2024) formulated a composite edible coating by incorporating a casein emulsion loaded with clove essential oil into a SA–pullulan polysaccharide matrix. When applied as a surface coating on mushrooms, this system demonstrated significant antimicrobial activity: clove essential oil—serving as the primary bioactive agent—exhibited potent inhibitory effects against E. coli and S. aureus. By suppressing microbial proliferation on the mushroom surface during storage and leveraging the coating's high barrier properties against moisture loss, the treatment effectively minimized weight loss and substantially extended the shelf life of the mushrooms.

In addition to naturally derived antibacterial compounds from plants, antibiotic-type additives represent another important strategy for modifying SA membrane room temperature, a SA–natamycin coating film significantly suppressed the expansion of disease lesions on sweet cherries: the lesion diameter was reduced by 64.40% relative to the control group, and the in vitro inhibition rate against hyphal growth of Alternaria reached 81.90% (Cao et al., 2024). In the field of mushroom preservation, Zhu et al. (2019) incorporated nisin into a SA matrix to develop a composite coating for the preservation of button mushrooms. Their results demonstrated that nisin and thyme essential oil exhibited synergistic antibacterial activity, significantly suppressing the growth of aerobic mesophilic bacteria during storage. Furthermore, nisin acted synergistically with L-cysteine to markedly reduce the browning incidence, malondialdehyde content, and cellulase activity in button mushrooms. Collectively, these effects delayed tissue softening and quality deterioration, thereby better preserving the overall storage quality of the mushrooms.

In addition to natural plant extracts and antibiotic-type additives, the incorporation of various novel antibacterial agents and functionalized modifiers has further expanded the modification strategies for alginate-based membranes. The resulting composite modification system enhances membrane stability—primarily through hydrogen-bonding interactions—minimizes leaching of antibacterial components, and enables synergistic antimicrobial effects by leveraging complementary mechanisms of action across different bioactive substances. In recent years, the direct incorporation of active microorganisms with probiotic functions into coating systems has emerged as one of the new avenues for the functional modification of SA-based freshness-preserving coatings. Unlike static chemical additives, active probiotic coatings are able to form an antibacterial barrier on the surface of fruit and vegetables through competitive exclusion and the secretion of organic acids and bacteriocins (Pop et al., 2020). For instance, a recent study fabricated a composite coating by blending SA with fermented Lactobacillus fermentum and applied it to preserve dehydrated papaya. Throughout the entire storage period, no contamination by molds, yeasts, or coliform bacteria was detected, thereby significantly improving the microbiological safety and overall quality retention of the dehydrated fruit (Martínez-Molina et al., 2024). It is worth noting that, among the studies on probiotic-coated preservation methods reported to date, there are still relatively few instances where live L. fermentum bacteria have been incorporated directly into SA film-forming systems as an antimicrobial component; its mechanism of action and broad-spectrum applicability remain to be further explored. As regards other modification strategies, in their preservation trials focusing on passion fruit, Yang et al. (2023) introduced tannic acid to alter the properties of SA coatings. This functionalized film alleviated postharvest senescence symptoms, blocked fruit spoilage progression, and brought prominent improvements to fruit storability. Qiang et al. (2025) reported that a composite coating comprising Pyropia polysaccharides blended with SA exhibited dual functionality—antibacterial activity and biodegradability—thereby effectively suppressing mold growth and maintaining strawberry freshness. In a study investigating the preservation of both climacteric and non-climacteric fruits, the synergistic application of beeswax and Chitosan nanowhisker markedly extended the shelf life of bananas and strawberries; compared with uncoated controls, the composite-coated fruits exhibited significantly reduced weight loss and effectively suppressed respiratory metabolic activity (Kim et al., 2025). Furthermore, He et al. (2024) incorporated yellow peel essential oil (YPEO) into corn protein–pectin nanoparticles and integrated the resulting nanocarriers into a SA–based coating system. The fabricated composite film demonstrated sustained antibacterial activity. Zone-of-inhibition assays revealed that the film exerted potent inhibitory effects against common foodborne pathogens, including E. coli and S. aureus. When applied to postharvest strawberry preservation, the film significantly reduced fruit rot incidence by 43.00%, yielding a rot index of only 31.67. The underlying antibacterial mechanism involves both the bioactive constituents of YPEO and its controlled-release profile: the sustained release ensures prolonged delivery of antimicrobial agents, while oxidative damage induced by these components disrupts the DNA integrity of pathogenic bacteria, thereby suppressing their proliferation.

Similarly, extensive research has been conducted on the development of antibacterial films for postharvest fruit preservation. Whether applied as a coating or as a freestanding film, the underlying antibacterial mechanism remains consistent with the core principle described above; only minor variations arise from differences in application modality. In coating applications, antibacterial agents are delivered directly onto the fruit surface, enabling immediate contact with and rapid inhibition of surface-associated pathogens. In contrast, film-based applications leverage the physical barrier function and controlled-release properties of the film matrix to provide sustained, prolonged delivery of antibacterial agents to the fruit surface—thereby extending the duration of antimicrobial protection and achieving an optimal alignment with the distinct preservation requirements of each application format. For instance, Ning et al. (2025) demonstrated that incorporating an emulsion loaded with tea tree essential oil (TTO) into a SA–konjac glucomannan matrix significantly enhanced the overall performance of the resulting composite film. This improvement was attributed to synergistic hydrogen-bonding interactions between the biopolymers and the particle-filling effect exerted by the dispersed emulsion droplets. Notably, propolis ethanol extract (PEE) and TTO exhibited a pronounced synergistic antibacterial effect, yielding inhibition zones exceeding 18 mm in diameter against both E. coli and S. aureus. Consequently, the film conferred triple-functional protection—antibacterial activity, antioxidant capacity, and regulated gas/water vapour transmission—which collectively suppressed fruit decay, minimized weight loss and tissue softening, and extended the shelf life of fresh strawberries by 3–4 days. In the sweet cherry preservation study conducted by Zeng et al. (2022), lycopene-loaded chitosan (CS)–carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) microcapsules (LMs) were incorporated into a SA–mannan gum polysaccharide film. The cationic nature of chitosan and carboxymethyl chitosan enabled electrostatic interaction with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, thereby disrupting membrane integrity. This formulation exhibited potent antibacterial activity, achieving inhibition rates of 92.09% against E. coli and 93.47% against S. aureus. In sweet cherry preservation, this antimicrobial action effectively suppressed microbial proliferation on the fruit surface, delayed mold growth, reduced spoilage incidence, and slowed overall quality deterioration. Moreover, synergistic contributions from the film's inherent barrier properties and lycopene's antioxidant capacity significantly extended the shelf life of sweet cherries.

It is worth noting that, apart from the validation of antimicrobial efficacy, the practical application potential of SA composite films depends to a large extent on their dose-response balance and the associated trade-offs in sensory quality. An in-depth analysis of the existing literature indicates that the efficacy of antimicrobial agents does not increase linearly with concentration, but typically follows a saturation kinetics pattern: once the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) threshold is exceeded, the marginal inhibitory benefit of further increasing the antimicrobial dose gradually diminishes, whilst the risk of sensory deterioration rises exponentially. Specifically, the available literature suggests that essential oil-based antimicrobials exhibit optimal efficacy within 0.50%∼2.00% (v/v), with concentrations exceeding 2.00% producing diminishing antibacterial gains while introducing pungent off-odors and phytotoxic effects on fruit surfaces (Lamani & Ramaswamy, 2023; Rashid et al., 2024). Antibiotic-type additives (nisin, ε-polylysine, natamycin) show a narrower optimal window of 0.01%∼0.05 % (w/v), beyond which bitter or salty aftertastes compromise sensory acceptability (Wang, Huang, et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2019). Nanocarrier-encapsulated systems, by virtue of their controlled-release kinetics, can operate effectively at lower loadings (0.10% ∼ 1.00%w/v) while mitigating the sensory drawbacks associated with high-dose additive incorporation (He et al., 2024). For example, with regard to essential oil-based additives, whilst ginger oil used by Lamani and Ramaswamy (2023) in fresh-cut pears and clove oil used by Rashid et al. (2024) in mushrooms exhibited stronger antibacterial zones at high loading rates, they simultaneously imparted a strong, pungent odour and off-flavours to the products, thereby masking the natural aromas of the fruit and vegetables themselves. Similarly, whilst antibiotic-type additives such as nisin and ε-polylysine (Wang, Yu, et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2019) possess highly effective antibacterial properties, the literature indicates that when used in excess of the optimal threshold, they introduce a noticeable bitter or salty aftertaste, thereby severely compromising the eating experience. Furthermore, high concentrations of solid additives (such as tannic acid or nanoparticles) increase the surface roughness and opacity of the film, negatively affecting the gloss and color acceptability of coated fruit (He et al., 2024). It is worth noting that in most of the cited studies, systematic dose-sensory parallel evaluations were rarely conducted; the majority relied on a single fixed concentration based on preliminary antimicrobial screening, overlooking the differences in additive tolerance thresholds among different fruit and vegetable hosts. Furthermore, A cross-sectional comparison of the literature reveals that the preservation efficacy of SA-based antibacterial films varies markedly across produce categories. Berry fruits (strawberries, blueberries, raspberries) consistently exhibit the most pronounced responses, with shelf-life extensions and rot suppression rates substantially exceeding those reported for climacteric fruits such as bananas, mangoes, and tomatoes (Kim et al., 2025). This differential responsiveness is likely attributable to the thinner cuticle and higher microbial susceptibility of berry surfaces, combined with the absence of ethylene-driven senescence interference that partially counteracts the protective effects in climacteric hosts. Intermediate responses are observed in stone fruits (sweet cherries), while mushroom and other high-moisture vegetables show the most limited efficacy, often requiring co-application with antioxidant or anti-browning agents to achieve sustained preservation (Zhu et al., 2019). These differences further demonstrate that simply pursuing high concentrations of antimicrobial activity is not advisable; formulation design must strike the necessary balance between maximising antimicrobial activity and maintaining the product's inherent quality. There is an urgent need to establish a comprehensive optimisation framework that integrates antimicrobial efficacy, physicochemical barrier properties and consumer-oriented sensory criteria, in order to bridge the gap between laboratory research and industrial application.

Effects of antioxidant substances introduction on fruit preservation

The introduction of antioxidant substances is the core method to enhance the fruit preservation performance of alginate sodium membranes (Table 4). It alleviates the damage to fruit tissues caused by oxidative stress, delays browning and flavor loss, and directly determines the quality retention effect and storage period of fruits covered with alginate sodium membranes. Its antioxidant activity primarily operates through two distinct mechanisms: direct radical scavenging—mediated by endogenous functional groups such as phenolic hydroxyl, aliphatic hydroxyl, and amino groups—which donate hydrogen atoms or transfer electrons to neutralize reactive free radicals including DPPH and ABTS (Wu, Ding, Nie & Wei, 2024); indirect protection—achieved via synergistic interactions among multiple components, which enhance membrane integrity and hydrophobicity, thereby restricting the permeation of oxygen, light, and water vapour and consequently suppressing oxidative metabolic processes (Yang et al., 2023). Ultimately, this approach mitigates oxidative loss of fruit nutrients, delays enzymatic browning and senescence, and thereby achieves effective fruit preservation. Application of antioxidant substances in fruit and vegetable preservation.

In coating applications, the direct deposition of active SA-based formulations onto fruit surfaces allows for immediate interfacial protection. The incorporated antioxidant agents vary widely in their modes of action. For polyphenolic antioxidants, Yang et al. (2023) demonstrated that cross-linking SA with tannic acid and calcium ions yields a robust double-network hydrogel film. This architecture not only enhances the film's ultraviolet-shielding capacity but also leverages the radical-scavenging activity of phenolic hydroxyl groups—conferring superior antioxidant functionality. Consequently, the film effectively attenuates oxidative damage induced by external light and oxygen exposure, minimizes oxidative degradation of essential nutrients and flavor volatiles in both the exocarp and pulp of passion fruit, and significantly retards postharvest senescence. For essential oil-based antioxidants, the team led by He et al. (2024) introduced dual encapsulated yellow peel essential oil into the film matrix, achieving an 85.00% scavenging rate against DPPH and ABTS radicals. The tight covering formed by this composite film blocks gas and moisture exchange between strawberries and ambient surroundings. The lowered metabolic and oxidative levels further limit ROS overaccumulation, delay quality loss, and inhibit senescence and rot, offering a remarkable extension to the postharvest storage time of strawberries. For nanoparticle- and nanostructure-mediated antioxidants, Zhang, Zhang, et al. (2025) integrated lemon-derived carbon quantum dots (CQDs) into a guar gum/SA composite membrane. The abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on CQDs confer outstanding antioxidant activity (DPPH and ABTS+ scavenging capacities of 42.21% and 43.45%, respectively), effectively suppressing enzymatic browning by inhibiting polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD) activities in asparagus. In a complementary nanostructural approach, Kim et al. (2025) incorporated chitosan nanofibers and beeswax into the SA matrix. This modification improves surface hydrophobicity, establishes a low-oxygen microenvironment that inhibits oxidative metabolism, and preserves stable DPPH radical scavenging capacity, ultimately delaying senescence in bananas and strawberries.

Beyond these primary categories, complementary studies on other bioactive polymers and enzymatic regulatory mechanisms are also noteworthy. Targeting the storage problem of table grapes, Wu, Ding, Nie and Wei (2024) compounded dendrobium polysaccharide with SA to prepare bioactive edible packaging materials. Rich antioxidant substances contained in the composite system can eliminate excess free radicals in fruit tissues, which limits the catalytic effect of PPO, mitigates browning and ultimately achieves a 14-day extension of grape storage time. Similarly, Yu et al. (2025) utilized chitosan-reinforced alginate composites to greatly slow oxidative breakdown and ascorbic acid discoloration in strawberries and bananas, limiting vitamin C loss to 4.78 mg/100 g and 1.54 mg/100 g, respectively. Beyond regulating oxidation inside fruit tissues, the dual-component coating also lessened cellular oxidative injury, postponed fruit ripening and aging, and delivered combined bacteriostatic and barrier effects to noticeably prolong postharvest storage duration. In addition to coatings loaded with exogenous antioxidants, pure polysaccharide matrices can also exert antioxidant effects by modulating the host's endogenous defense systems. Although the SA coating used in the following study contained no exogenous additives, Li, Zhu, et al. (2025) investigated its application on chilled sweet corn, revealing that it down-regulates key genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, thereby enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities (POD, SOD, CAT, APX) and reducing H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation, which effectively suppresses kernel browning and extends shelf life by six days at 4 °C. Moreover, the membrane exhibits excellent water solubility and biodegradability, and can be readily removed from food surfaces via simple water rinsing—fully satisfying safety and green environmental requirements for food-contact materials.

For free-standing films, the antioxidant functionality is often coupled with enhanced physicochemical barrier properties, providing both physical isolation and chemical protection for the packaged produce. Polyphenolic compounds have been widely incorporated into SA film matrices. Chen et al. (2021) incorporated thymol into alginate films; the thymol forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds with alginate chains, markedly enhancing DPPH scavenging in a concentration-dependent manner while exerting synergistic UV-blocking effects, which effectively suppressed enzymatic browning and maintained surface brightness in fresh-cut apples. In a valorization approach, Thivya et al. (2021) utilized waste shallot onion fractions (rich in polyphenols) as active fillers in SA/carboxymethyl cellulose films. The extracted polyphenols concurrently improved the film's gas barrier properties and conferred robust DPPH/ABTS radical elimination, restraining oxidation-triggered browning on fresh-cut potato surfaces with sustained sensory quality. Storage tests further validated its practical preservation capacity, including a reduced browning index, slower texture softening, merely 6.02% moisture loss, and sustained sensory acceptability and quality stability throughout the shelf life of cut potato samples. Nanostructures and nanoparticles offer additional advantages through high surface-area radical adsorption and controlled release. Wang, Qin, et al. (2025) incorporated resveratrol-loaded nanoparticles into an alginate matrix; encapsulation markedly enhanced both the antioxidant capacity and structural stability of the film, with DPPH/ABTS scavenging increasing with nanoparticle loading and retaining high activity even after 20 days. Blueberry preservation experiments confirmed that this film effectively scavenged free radicals and extended shelf life. Coupled with its superior barrier properties, the film successfully suppressed oxidative deterioration and quality degradation of the fruit, thereby extending the shelf life of blueberries. Wang, Xu, et al. (2024) developed a functional film for spinach preservation by incorporating melatonin (MT) into the UiO-66–NH₂ metal-organic framework used as the SA film-forming matrix. The MOF served as a stable carrier for sustained melatonin release, while SA provided structural integrity. This synergistic design significantly suppressed chlorophyll degradation (retained at 1.20 mg/g), reduced MDA accumulation (to 81.20 nmol/mg), and upregulated endogenous SOD, POD, and CAT activities, thereby mitigating ROS accumulation and delaying leaf yellowing.

A comparative review of the existing literature on antioxidant research reveals that berries (strawberries, blueberries) and fresh-cut produce (fresh-cut apples, pears and potatoes) are the categories of fruit and vegetables that respond most significantly to SA-based antioxidant coatings. In the case of berries, their thin skin structure and high anthocyanin content render them highly susceptible to oxidative degradation; however, the free radical scavenging capacity of SA films loaded with polyphenols or plant essential oils can directly block this deterioration pathway (He et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025). In the case of fresh-cut produce, the cutting stress incurred during processing triggers rapid enzymatic browning reactions; consequently, these products are particularly reliant on contact-type antioxidants that take effect immediately at the cut surface (Chen et al., 2021; Thivya et al., 2021). In contrast, fruits with a respiratory jump characterized by a thicker cuticle (such as bananas and passion fruit) exhibit a more moderate antioxidant response; on the one hand, the physical barrier of the peel limits the effective penetration of antioxidants, whilst on the other hand, the endogenous ethylene-driven ripening process weakens the protective effect of exogenous antioxidants to some extent (Yang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025). These differences indicate that the formulation design of antioxidant coatings cannot be a one-size-fits-all approach, but should instead be optimised on a case-by-case basis according to the specific oxidative sensitivity and structural characteristics of the fruit or vegetable in question.

Effects of nanomaterials introduction on fruit preservation

During the post-harvest storage and transportation of FVs, microbial contamination, oxygen permeation, and moisture loss constitute the primary factors driving quality deterioration. Although incorporating antibacterial and antioxidant agents can partially mitigate quality decline, conventional alginate-based matrices suffer from inherent limitations—including inadequate mechanical strength and suboptimal barrier properties—thereby failing to satisfy the requirements for long-term preservation of fresh produce. By incorporating nanomaterials into SA-based composites, the aforementioned limitations can be effectively addressed, thereby enabling efficient preservation of FVs (Table 5). On one hand, nanoparticles—leveraging their intrinsic structural features—are uniformly dispersed within the SA matrix, effectively filling membrane pores and refining the film's microstructure toward greater density. This structural optimization markedly enhances the film's barrier performance against small-molecule permeants such as oxygen and water vapour, thereby mitigating postharvest water loss and suppressing respiratory metabolism in FVs (Arroyo et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2024). On the other hand, nanoparticles engage with SA chains via multiple intermolecular interactions—including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and covalent or ionic cross-linking—thereby reinforcing the polymer network. This yields a denser, more robust three-dimensional architecture, significantly improving key mechanical properties such as tensile strength and elongation at break (Yu et al., 2025), and providing reliable structural integrity essential for effective produce preservation. Currently employed nanofillers for SA films include montmorillonite (MMT) (Yan et al., 2024), zinc oxide nanoparticles (Arroyo et al., 2020), nanocellulose (Jung et al., 2015), nanochitosan (Kim et al., 2025), and silver nanoparticles (Li, Li, Zhang, et al., 2022), among others. The incorporation of these nanomaterials has consistently led to substantial improvements in the functional performance of SA-based films. In the study by Yan et al. (2024), integrating MMT into the SA matrix not only increased the composite film's tensile strength (TS) to 63.09 MPa and elongation at break to 6.47%, but also significantly enhanced its barrier functionality. Consequently, the MMT–alginate composite film effectively suppressed postharvest respiration, minimized water loss, and inhibited membrane lipid oxidation in strawberries—collectively delaying fruit quality deterioration during storage. To improve the storage performance of fresh guava, Arroyo et al., 2020compounded nano‑zinc oxide with alginate and chitosan to prepare dual polysaccharide-based coatings. Interactions between nanoparticles and polymer chains brought complementary advantages for film structure and barrier capacity. This surface covering slowed fruit weight loss, retarded peel chlorophyll fading and postponed pulp softening over the storage cycle. Additionally, the natural antibacterial traits of nano‑zinc oxide endowed the coating with strong pathogen-suppressing ability, greatly preserving the comprehensive quality of harvested guava. Jung et al. (2015) employed cellulose nanofibers and SA cross-linked with Ca2+ to fabricate a dense hydrogel coating, which effectively inhibited pigment leaching from blueberries and significantly enhanced the retention of anthocyanins and total phenolics. Seeking to clarify how nano-cellulose affects the protective performance of alginate coatings, Yu et al. (2025) performed complete preservation trials on banana and strawberry fruits. Experiments demonstrated that nano-cellulose fibers (NCFs) reinforce molecular cross-linking via hydrogen bonding, esterification, and electrostatic interactions, thereby forming a dense, pore-free network structure. This structural enhancement yielded a fracture elongation of 101.00% and a tensile strength of 88.92 MPa, significantly improving hydrophobicity and gas barrier performance. Concurrently, when integrated with chitosan—whose inherent antibacterial activity synergistically complements the NCF matrix—the composite effectively mitigates fruit moisture loss, retards the decline in firmness, and suppresses the degradation of key nutrients, including soluble solids and ascorbic acid. As a result, the shelf life of bananas and strawberries was extended to over 12 days under ambient-temperature storage conditions. A study investigating the preservation of oranges revealed that incorporating silver nanoparticles into an alginate/gelatin composite matrix significantly enhances its functional performance. When applied at an optimal concentration, this nanocomposite membrane effectively mitigates water loss, suppresses microbial colonization, and retards both fruit respiration and the metabolic conversion of organic acids to sugars. Consequently, it delays key indicators of postharvest deterioration—including weight loss, loss of peel gloss, and overall quality decline—thereby substantially extending the shelf life of oranges. Furthermore, the study explicitly demonstrated that silver migration into the fruit remained well below established safety thresholds—specifically, the US primary drinking water standard for silver (Ag < 50 μg/L)—indicating that the silver content in the fruit remained within a safe range for consumption (Li, Li, Zhang, et al., 2022).Table 5Application of nanomaterials and other functional substances in fruit and vegetable preservation.FVs varietiesBlended film-forming agentsActive substancesModification methodsApplication methodsKey indicatorsFVs preservation effectReferencesStrawberry–Montmorillonite (MMT); 10.00% and 20.00%form physical cross-linking via hydrogen bondscoatingTS 63.09 MPa, EAB 6.47%↑.weight loss rate, MDA, respiration intensity (decreased by 43.30%, 25.80% and 57.10%, respectively), TA, soluble sugar, TPC, TSS (decreased by 25.80%, 37.70%, 25.90% and 14.50%, respectively)↓Yan et al. Application of nanomaterials and other functional substances in fruit and vegetable preservation.

Beyond the above-mentioned improvements in barrier and mechanical properties, the optimal loading of nanofillers follows a clear dose-dependent pattern. From a dose-response perspective, the optimal ranges vary by nanomaterial type: 1.00%–5.00% (w/w) for inorganic nanoparticles (MMT, ZnO, AgNPs) and 2.00%–10.00% (w/w) for bio-based nanofibers (nanocellulose, nanochitosan). Exceeding these thresholds induces nanoparticle aggregation, which paradoxically compromises both barrier performance and film flexibility (Yan et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025).

Beyond these physicochemical considerations, the practical feasibility of nanomaterial-reinforced SA films for fruit preservation hinges on three additional pillars: safety, regulatory status and environmental sustainability. In terms of safety, inorganic nanoparticles entail a concentration-dependent risk of ion migration; although Li, Li, Zhang, et al. (2022) confirmed that silver release remained below the US drinking water standard (Ag < 50 μg/L), the potential chronic exposure risk in high-moisture produce warrants caution. By contrast, bio-based nanomaterials—derived from natural polysaccharides—degrade into non-toxic oligosaccharides and monosaccharides, conferring an inherent safety advantage for direct food contact (Kim et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Regulatory wise, inorganic materials require stringent case-by-case safety evaluations due to their ionic toxicity and environmental persistence, which substantially slows commercial translation; bio-based alternatives, conversely, benefit from GRAS or low-risk classifications, offering a more rapid pathway to market. Environmentally, the accumulation of inorganic nanoparticles in soil and aquatic ecosystems, along with their potential ecotoxicological effects, raises growing concerns; bio-based nanomaterials, being biodegradable and biocompatible, align more closely with the principles of green and sustainable packaging. In summary, although inorganic nanofillers currently exhibit superior antimicrobial activity, bio-based nanomaterials present overriding advantages in safety, regulatory compliance and environmental compatibility, positioning them as the more promising candidates for future development of alginate-based fruit preservation coatings. Collectively, the judicious selection of nanofiller type and dosage—guided by both performance enhancement and practical feasibility—constitutes the central tenet for translating laboratory advances into commercially viable preservation technologies.

Emerging functional applications: Intelligent responsiveness and induced wound healing

In contrast to the incorporation of conventional active substances (antibacterial, antioxidant, or nanoscale fillers) discussed in 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, recent advances have expanded the functionality of SA-based edible films toward intelligent real-time monitoring and physiological regulation of fruit defense responses (Table 5). This section presents representative application cases that validate the two advanced mechanisms elaborated in Sections 2.3.5 (smart-responsive effects) and 2.3.6 (induced wound healing).

For intelligent responsiveness, Zhu et al. (2024) incorporated Lycium ruthenicum anthocyanins into a rice protein/SA composite matrix and applied the resulting pH-responsive film to fresh-cut honeydew melon storage. During storage, fruit metabolism generated CO₂ and organic acids, progressively lowering the headspace pH and triggering a visible color transition of the film from yellow-green to red-brown. This color evolution showed a strong quantitative correspondence with the quality deterioration profile of the melon cubes, including the reduction in firmness, increase in weight loss, decline in total soluble solids, and intensification of browning. Based on these correlations, the film color enabled the accurate discrimination of three storage stages—fresh (days 0–1), sub-fresh (days 2–3), and unacceptable (days 5–7). In addition, the intrinsic antioxidant activity of anthocyanins contributed to retarding oxidative deterioration, thereby simultaneously achieving both real-time freshness visualization and quality extension. Li, Wu, and Li (2024) developed a CO₂-sensitive bilayer film based on gellan gum as the sensing inner layer (containing methyl red and bromothymol blue) and SA/sodium carboxymethyl cellulose incorporating tannic acid as the antimicrobial outer layer. When applied to strawberry preservation at 4 °C, the bilayer film not only provided visible color feedback reflecting the ripeness and spoilage progression of the strawberries, but also significantly delayed the decline in hardness and titratable acidity, maintained higher soluble solids content, and reduced weight loss. The GG-MB@SC-6%TA formulation extended the refrigerated shelf life of strawberries by approximately 2 days compared with the unwrapped control, demonstrating the effectiveness of integrating CO₂-responsive indication with antimicrobial protection in a single bilayer architecture. In summary, these application cases demonstrate that the intelligent responsiveness and active barrier functionalities are of both scientific significance and practical efficacy, as they effectively preserve fresh-cut produce while offering consumers real-time, intuitive freshness indicators.

For induced wound healing, Sun et al. (2025) developed a glucose-responsive active packaging film by encapsulating glucose oxidase (GOx) into a carboxymethyl chitosan/SA composite matrix (CSFG) and applied it to mechanically wounded blueberries. In practice, the CSFG treatment reduced the decay rate of gray mold in wounded blueberries by 66.70% compared to the control at 6 days of storage, while maintaining higher firmness (5.85 N vs. 3.60 N), total soluble solids, and anthocyanin/flavonoid contents. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. (2023) investigated the effect of chlorogenic acid combined with SA coating on pear fruit. As mechanistically detailed in Section 2.3.6, this treatment activates the phenylpropanoid pathway, promoting suberin polyphenolic and lignin deposition at wound sites. In application, the combined treatment significantly reduced weight loss (from 4.84% to 3.52% at 10 days) and decay rate (from 44.44% to 5.41%), while maintaining higher tissue firmness and lower membrane permeability compared with the untreated control. Notably, the wound-healing efficacy of chlorogenic acid is optimal at 1.00% ∼ 2.00% (w/v), with higher concentrations causing phenolic oxidation and localized tissue browning (Zhang et al., 2023), while the glucose oxidase system achieves maximal protection at an encapsulated enzyme activity of 10–20 U/mL (Sun et al., 2025). These results demonstrate that SA-based coatings functionalized with plant activators can effectively accelerate wound healing and preserve postharvest quality of mechanically damaged fruits.

Conclusions and prospects

In summary, research into SA-based composite films has progressed from the early proof-of-concept stage into a phase of in-depth development characterized by functional integration and intelligent responsiveness. Current research frontiers are no longer confined to simply utilising the film-forming and barrier properties of SA, but rather focus on strategies such as ionic cross-linking, layer-by-layer self-assembly and the blending of nanomaterials to construct multifunctional composite film systems that combine strong antibacterial and antioxidant activity with environment-responsive release and controllable degradation, with the aim of achieving precise intervention in gas and water vapour transport and microbial niches (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Summary of research framework.Fig. 5

Summary of research framework.

However, although SA-based composite films have demonstrated significant potential for preserving freshness on a laboratory scale, their transition to industrial applications remains constrained by four key bottlenecks: Firstly, the trade-off between performance and cost—high-performance composite films often rely on expensive functional additives or complex preparation processes, making it difficult to balance the economic viability of large-scale production with continuous stability; secondly, the conflict between versatility and specificity—existing research largely employs ‘one-size-fits-all’ formulations, overlooking the lack of adaptability resulting from differences in the respiratory rates, ethylene release rates and epidermal structures of various FVs; Moreover, customized formulations for cross-border commercialisation must directly address the significant differences in food contact material regulations across major markets such as the EU, the US and China—covering multiple dimensions including the scope of authorised substances, migration testing protocols and GMP requirements—whilst global regulatory harmonisation remains unlikely in the foreseeable future. This necessitates that multi-jurisdictional compliance considerations be embedded into formulation design at an early stage. Thirdly, the fragmentation of evaluation systems—current assessments focus predominantly on short-term freshness preservation indicators, lacking a systematic evaluation of ecological safety and economic viability across the entire “production–use–degradation” life cycle. Fourthly, gaps in our understanding of the underlying mechanisms—as systematically outlined in 2.3, 3 of this paper, existing research is largely confined to the detection of macroscopic physicochemical indicators and isolated biochemical markers. There remains a lack of holistic, systematic understanding of how such films influence the preservation phenotype by regulating key molecular events such as cell wall metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging pathways, and surface microbial quorum sensing; Furthermore, there is very little research addressing how SA-based composite films reprogramme hormone signalling, energy metabolism and antioxidant defense pathways in fruit and vegetables at the transcriptional and metabolic network levels.

With regard to the four key research areas mentioned earlier (process optimisation, precision formulation, full life-cycle assessment, and elucidation of molecular mechanisms), and based on the unravel logic of the current bottlenecks, we believe that “elucidation of molecular mechanisms” is the most pressing priority. This is because, unless we elucidate how SA-based active films influence the shelf-life phenotype through molecular networks that regulate cell wall metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging pathways and surface microbial quorum sensing, formulation optimisation and process improvement will remain reliant on a “trial-and-error” approach, making rational design difficult to achieve. Consequently, future research must prioritise the integration of transcriptomics, metabolomics and microbiomics to unravelling the causal chain between “membrane structure – microenvironmental signalling – physiological responses in fruit and vegetables” at the molecular level, thereby laying a solid theoretical foundation for SA-based green packaging materials to progress from the laboratory to the shelf.
