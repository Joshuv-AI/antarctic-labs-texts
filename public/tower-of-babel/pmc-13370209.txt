Cinchona alkaloid scaffold decoded
Authors: Laure Martinelli, Chao Sun, Nicolas Papon, Vincent Courdavault
Published: 2026

Main text

According to the latest World Malaria Report from the World Health Organization (WHO, 2025), the number of malaria cases increased by 3% between 2023 and 2024, reaching an estimated 282 million cases. Despite decades of research and control efforts, malaria remains one of the most life-threatening parasitic diseases worldwide, causing approximately 600,000 deaths in 2024. The disease is caused by unicellular parasites of the genus Plasmodium, which are transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes in intertropical regions. Clinically, malaria manifests as fever, fatigue, and headache, and can be fatal in severe cases, particularly in young children and pregnant women.

For centuries, malaria treatment relied on Cinchona bark, initially used in traditional medicine. The bark of Cinchona species—sometimes referred to as “fever trees” because of their antipyretic properties—is rich in nitrogen-containing specialized metabolites collectively known as cinchona alkaloids, or quinine alkaloids, which account for its medicinal benefits. Following the pioneering isolation of quinine as the principal active compound in the early 19th century, quinine was rapidly recognized as a potent antimalarial drug and remained the first-line treatment for severe Plasmodium infections for decades. Since then, cinchona alkaloids have been regarded as a “privileged class of compounds” owing to their exceptional pharmacological and chemical properties. However, the emergence of quinine resistance in the early 20th century prompted a shift in therapeutic strategies, and quinine and other quinoline-based antimalarials, such as chloroquine and mefloquine, are now used primarily in combination therapies or as follow-up treatments after artemisinin-based regimens. Nevertheless, quinine remains clinically relevant in specific contexts, including treatment during pregnancy and uncomplicated malaria (Achan et al., 2011).

Even at the height of its use, quinine production relied almost exclusively on extraction from the bark of cultivated Cinchona trees. Total chemical synthesis remained economically unviable, although semi-synthetic derivatives helped ensure a more reliable supply. Similarly, bioproduction of quinine in heterologous systems had been proposed but remained unrealized. Indeed, the biosynthetic pathway of quinine—and, more broadly, cinchona alkaloids—remained largely unresolved. Like other prized natural products, such as the anticancer drugs paclitaxel and vinblastine, quinine combines remarkable bioactivity with biosynthetic complexity that challenges both chemists and biologists. Until recently, knowledge of cinchona alkaloid biosynthesis was essentially limited to their classification as monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs), meaning that they derive from strictosidine, which is formed through the enzymatic condensation of tryptamine (providing the indole moiety) and secologanin (providing the monoterpene moiety). However, beyond this general framework, only a handful of intermediates—notably corynantheal, cinchoni(di)none, and quini(di)none—and a few upstream biosynthetic steps had been characterized (Battersby and Parry, 1971; Trenti et al., 2021).

In a recent report, Lombe et al. (2026) addressed this long-standing gap by elucidating the biosynthetic pathway underlying the formation of the quinoline–quinuclidine scaffold. Through an elegant combination of classical and modern approaches, the authors identified 3 previously unreported biosynthetic intermediates, characterized 13 new enzymes, and reconstructed cinchona alkaloid biosynthesis in a heterologous plant system. Thus, after more than a century of research, they provided a near-complete framework for the biosynthesis of quinine and related alkaloids, including quinidine, cinchonidine, cinchonine, and cinchonamine (Figure 1).

(A) Cinchona alkaloids, such as quinine, produced by the tree (Cinchona spp.), have been extensively studied for their pharmaceutical properties, particularly as antimalarial agents.

(B) Overview of the iterative strategy used to characterize intermediates and enzymes involved in cinchona alkaloid biosynthesis. Pathway intermediates were identified and validated using deuterium labeling and feeding experiments in C. pubescens tissues, including roots, stems, and leaves, and crude protein extracts. Enzymes were functionally characterized through heterologous expression in N. benthamiana, E. coli, or S. cerevisiae.

(C) Overview of the elucidated pathway leading to cinchona alkaloids. Both non-methoxylated and methoxylated cinchona alkaloids are shown. Non-methoxylated compounds are represented in black, with names indicated in black. Methoxylated analogs are shown with an additional methoxy group highlighted in green, and their names are indicated in green or prefixed with “10-OMe-”. From the corynantheal-type intermediate, biosynthesis proceeds via reduction catalyzed by multiple aldo-keto reductases (CpKR1–8), followed by O-malonylation mediated by CpMAT (a BAHD malonyltransferase). A subsequent cyclization step, catalyzed by CpMCC (malonyl-corynantheol cyclase), yields a cinchonium-type intermediate, which is then converted by CpCiS (cinchonaminal synthase) to form an indole–quinuclidine intermediate (cinchonaminal or 10-OMe-cinchonaminal). Downstream, this intermediate can either be reduced by CpCiR (cinchonaminal reductase) or oxidized by CpCiO (cinchonaminal oxidase) to form cinchoninone or cinchonidinone, or quinidinone or quininone, intermediates. These intermediates are subsequently reduced by CpKR4 to yield the final alkaloids cinchonine, cinchonidine, quinine, and quinidine. The pathway has been validated for both the non-methoxylated and methoxylated series. Dihydro analogs of cinchona alkaloids (methoxylated or non-methoxylated) are not shown for clarity but follow the same biosynthetic pathway, except for the early steps leading to corynantheal-type intermediates, which have been resolved for the dihydro series but not for their non-dihydro counterparts. Newly identified intermediates are highlighted in a light green box. The dashed arrow indicates multi-step transformations. All enzymes shown were newly characterized by Lombe et al. (2026).

To achieve this breakthrough, the O’Connor and Buell groups used an iterative strategy combining metabolite discovery with enzyme characterization. Structural elucidation of biosynthetic intermediates provided hypotheses about enzymatic transformations, while the products of newly identified enzymes guided the discovery of additional intermediates. These pathway intermediates were identified and verified through deuterium labeling and feeding assays using cell cultures or crude protein extracts and were later characterized by NMR and tandem mass spectrometry. Candidate genes were mined from transcriptomic datasets—both publicly available and newly generated—and functionally validated via heterologous expression in Nicotiana benthamiana, Escherichia coli, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. In addition, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) was used in Cinchona pubescens to validate both the physiological role of candidate enzymes and the on-pathway nature of intermediates.

Using this strategy, Lombe et al. showed that the conversion of corynantheal to cinchonaminal, two previously known intermediates, begins with reduction catalyzed by multiple aldo-keto reductases (CpKR1–8), followed by O-malonylation mediated by a BAHD malonyltransferase (CpMAT). Interestingly, this is followed by an unexpected cyclization reaction catalyzed by a distinct BAHD-family malonyltransferase, CpMCC (malonyl-corynantheol cyclase), and a dioxygenase-assisted ring-opening reaction catalyzed by CpCiS (cinchonaminal synthase), ultimately yielding the indole–quinuclidine structure of cinchonaminal. Three additional downstream biosynthetic steps were further elucidated: (i) reduction of cinchonaminal to cinchonamine by CpCiR (cinchonaminal reductase), (ii) oxidation of cinchonaminal to the stereoisomeric cinchonidinone and cinchoninone by CpCiO (cinchonaminal oxidase), and (iii) reduction of these two stereoisomers to cinchonidine and cinchonine, respectively, catalyzed by CpKR4. Notably, CpKR4 can also reduce corynantheal, highlighting a degree of enzymatic promiscuity within the pathway.

One of the hallmarks of this work lies in the thoroughness with which the O’Connor and Buell groups validated each step of their discovery. This effort relied on VIGS experiments in C. pubescens, combined with systematic heterologous expression and feeding assays to rigorously confirm each enzymatic transformation. Beyond the diversity of techniques employed, the study required testing dozens (if not hundreds) of candidate genes selected from carefully curated transcriptomic data. In addition to the available transcriptome of C. pubescens, they integrated the transcriptomes of Arabidopsis thaliana, Ophiorrhiza major, and Mitragyna speciosa, complemented by a single-nucleus RNA sequencing dataset from C. pubescens generated specifically for this study.

However, despite this comprehensive strategy, two key limitations constrained the validation of the full pathway. First, the mechanism underlying the formation of corynantheal-type intermediates, although resolved for the dihydro cinchona alkaloids, cannot be extrapolated to their non-dihydro counterparts (Lombe et al., 2025, 2026). Second, quinine and its stereoisomer quinidine differ from other cinchona alkaloids—such as cinchonidine, cinchonine, and cinchonamine—by the presence of an additional methoxy group on their aromatic ring. Accordingly, they are derived not from strictosidine but from its methoxylated analog, 10-OMe-strictosidine (Lombe et al., 2026). As such, the biosynthesis of quinine and quinidine was not fully reconstituted in N. benthamiana. Validation of this branch of biosynthesis would therefore have required either access to 10-OMe-corynantheal (in addition to the corynantheal synthesized in the study) or elucidation of the steps leading to corynantheal and 10-OMe-corynantheal. In this respect, with access to the appropriate methoxylated substrate or completion of the remaining biosynthetic steps, heterologous production of quinine and other methoxylated cinchona alkaloids should be achievable.

Through this work, Lombe et al. elucidated the core biosynthetic pathway of cinchona alkaloids, marking a major milestone in MIA metabolism research. With the exception of camptothecin, the biosynthetic routes of most historically and pharmaceutically relevant MIAs are now largely resolved. As previously developed for drugs such as vinblastine and paclitaxel and for the vaccine adjuvant QS-21 (Zhang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), “tree-free” production of cinchona alkaloids in engineered cell factories is now within reach through the integration of the newly identified enzymes into existing bioproduction platforms. Importantly, quinine remains an exciting pharmacological scaffold for new antimalarial drug development, and structural diversification—as illustrated by chloroquine and mefloquine, two synthetic quinine derivatives also broadly used in antimalarial therapy—still offers promising opportunities. In this context, the production of non-natural halogenated cinchona alkaloids reported by the authors may further enrich their pharmacological properties, as recently demonstrated for other MIAs (Runguphan et al., 2010; Bradley et al., 2020, 2023).

Overall, the enzymes characterized in this landmark report provide key insights into other complex pathways. Notably, CpMCC, which is involved in cinchonium formation, was not anticipated to catalyze the observed cyclization. Its apparently reduced malonyltransferase activity, together with its phylogenetic divergence from canonical BAHD enzymes, may point to a previously unknown enzyme clade. More broadly, the iterative strategy combining intermediate identification with enzyme characterization offers a transferable framework for MIA research. Given the conservation of genes and enzymatic functions across MIA-producing species, discoveries in Cinchona may facilitate the elucidation of related metabolic pathways. On the other hand, improvements in other MIA systems could help to resolve remaining gaps—notably the conversion of strictosidine to corynantheal and of 10-OMe-strictosidine into 10-OMe-corynantheal. In conclusion, from bark to bioreactor, quinine may soon complete its transition into the era of engineered natural-product biosynthesis.
