Nutritional dynamics and untargeted metabolomics reveal breed and lactation-dependent shifts in goat milk composition: from colostrum to mature milk
Authors: Binglin Yue, Jie Yang, Donghua Su, Xi Wang, Hui Wang, Yaqiu Lin, Youli Wang, Haitao Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study profiled the nutritional, safety, and metabolic evolution of milk from Chuanzhong Black (CBG) and Jianzhou Big-Ear (JBG) goats during early lactation (days 1–35). Results revealed that colostrum in both breeds contained superior levels of total solids, fat, and protein compared to mature milk. Notably, CBG colostrum exhibited significantly higher IgG concentrations (81.29 mg/mL) than JBG, indicating stronger passive immunity potential. However, safety analysis showed that Aflatoxin M1 (AFM1) concentrations also peaked in colostrum. Untargeted metabolomics identified 804 (in CBGs) and 790 (in JBGs) differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), revealing a distinct functional shift from immunity-supporting compounds in colostrum to energy-provision metabolites in mature milk. Additionally, CBG generally displayed elevated metabolite levels compared to JBG. These findings confirm that milk quality is strictly governed by both breed and lactation stage, providing valuable metabolic insights for evaluating milk quality and establishing baseline grading standards for meat goats.

Introduction

Goat milk is rich in essential nutrients, including proteins, fats, lactose, minerals, vitamins, and various bioactive substances. Owing to this unique milk composition, consumer interest has grown steadily over the last few decades (Prosser, 2021). Milk quality changes naturally over time. The first milk, known as colostrum, is packed with IgG that are vital for the kids' immunity. However, the exact quality of this milk depends on factors such as breed and parity (Fahey et al., 2020; Honan et al., 2020; Kessler et al., 2019; Wilms et al., 2022). During the transitional stage, the milk's primary role shifts from immune protection to nutrition supply, marked by a decrease in immunoglobulins and casein, while an increase in whey proteins, fat, and lactose (Senda et al., 2011). Finally, the milk matures and stabilizes, providing a steady and balanced nutrient source for the growing kids (Ballard & Morrow, 2013).

External factors, such as contaminated feed, also impact milk composition and even safety. When goats consume feed containing aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), it transforms into aflatoxin M1 (AFM1) and passes into the milk. While slightly less toxic, AFM1 is still dangerous—it could damage DNA and impair the immune system (Galvano et al., 1996; Yang et al., 2020). Roughly 1–6% of dietary AFB1 is biotransformed into AFM1 and converted into milk, though this varies by the goat's health and diet (Becker-Algeri et al., 2016; Zentai et al., 2023). Therefore, monitoring AFM1 levels is essential to protect the health of young kids. Recently, metabolomics has become a popular method for studying milk. Using advanced tools like NMR, GC–MS, and LC-MS, researchers could systematically track nutrient changes and identify what factors determine milk quality. The differential metabolites identified across various species, breeds, or physiological conditions could serve as potential biomarkers for subsequent molecular breeding in animals or provide theoretical references for optimizing animal diets (Li et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2020; Scano et al., 2014; Suh, 2022). More recent untargeted metabolomics studies have further characterized the complex metabolic networks in goat milk, revealing how factors such as maternal diet (Wang et al., 2025), regional environments and specific breeds (Brosse et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2025), and specific processing methods (Sánchez-Rodríguez et al., 2024) influence its fine-scale milk composition. Despite these recent advances, comprehensive temporal studies tracking the metabolome of specific meat goat breeds—particularly the dynamic transition from colostrum to mature milk—remain relatively scarce.

Goats are generally raised for either milk or meat. For meat breeds, the nutritional quality of milk is vital for offspring survival, pre-weaning growth, and overall development. Milk composition is predominantly determined by factors such as breed, individual variability, lactation stage, age, parity, breeding season, feeding management, and health status (Castro et al., 2023). Suboptimal milk quality severely diminishes offspring growth and development. To address this and boost survival rates, it is essential to establish breed-specific milk composition and implement science-based feeding methods. The CBG and JBG are two of the most economically significant indigenous meat goat breeds native to Sichuan, China. While both are highly valued, they possess distinct phenotypic advantages: CBG is renowned for its high prolificacy and exceptional environmental adaptability, whereas JBG is characterized by its large frame and rapid early growth (detailed breed characteristics and representative photos are provided in Fig. S1). Despite continuous breeding efforts improving their meat yield, the pre-weaning survival rate of their offspring remains a critical bottleneck, which may be closely associated with insufficient knowledge of maternal milk quality and inadequate colostrum intake. Comparative analysis of the milk composition in these two breeds helps establish clear standards for colostrum, transitional, and mature milk in meat goats. To achieve this, we systematically profiled milk composition from two goat breeds over the first 35 days postpartum. A key goal was to utilize untargeted metabolomics to reveal the distinct metabolic profiles between colostrum and mature milk. Ultimately, this research provides a scientific foundation for evaluating the nutrition and safety of goat milk.

Materials and methods

All animal procedures were performed in strict accordance with the EU Directive 2010/63 for the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Care Committee of Southwest Minzu University (protocol number: SMU-202401162).

Materials

Milk samples were collected at two commercial goat farms in Lezhi County (Ziyang) and Jianyang City (Chengdu), Sichuan Province. The two experimental goat breeding farms, are located in close proximity (only 41.5 km apart). Both are situated on the Chengdu Plain with similar climatic conditions and feeding environments, which maximizes the consistency of environmental conditions for the experimental goats. A total of 13 multiparous CBGs (with an average age of 3.2 ± 0.7 years), and 19 multiparous JBGs (with an average age of 3.5 ± 0.9 years) with similar body condition were selected as experimental animals. To minimize potential confounding effects from both diet and the external environment, both farms implemented an identical standardized feeding and management regimen throughout the trial period. All goats were fed the same total mixed ration (TMR) diet, formulated to meet or exceed their nutritional requirements for lactation, and were managed under the same housing and milking hygiene protocols. All goats were individually housed in separate pens with free access to fresh water. These goats showed no signs of illness during the trial and did not receive antibiotics. Colostrum samples were collected manually during the first milking (within 2 h after delivery), along with milk samples at 12 h postpartum. Subsequently, a small goat milking machine was used to collect milk samples at 8:00 am and 8:00 pm daily on postpartum days 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 14, and 35. A proportional mixture of 50 mL was collected based on milk yield, labeled with identification numbers, and stored in a − 20 °C/−80 °C freezer for milk composition analysis. The dense longitudinal design, with 10 sampling points per animal, substantially improved statistical power. In the repeated-measures linear mixed model (GLIMMIX procedure), each animal served as its own control by being tracked sequentially over time. This approach effectively separated the large baseline biological variation between individuals from the residual error term, thereby reducing residual variance and markedly increasing the model's ability to detect significant effects of breed, lactation stage, and their interactions. Analysis of milk composition

The milk samples were stored at −20 °C for chemical composition analysis and preserved at −80 °C for IgG quantification by single radial immunodiffusion (Fleenor & Stott, 1981). Briefly, the fat content was measured according to the Gerber method (Kleyn et al., 2001), the protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method (Lynch & Barbano, 1999), and the lactose content was analyzed using the Lane-Eynon method (Peres et al., 2005). The total solids content was determined by the direct drying method (Clark et al., 1989), while the ash content was assessed via muffle furnace incineration (Perez & Andujar, 1981). AFM1 levels were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), and the Brix percentage was measured using a Brix refractometer (MISCO PA 202, MISCO, Solon, OH)(Martin et al., 2021).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics analyses were performed on the colostrum and early mature milk (day 7 postpartum) samples (n = 9 per group, 36 samples in total) from two multiparous goat breeds by Biomarker Tech using Waters Xevo G2-XS Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Waters) coupled to an I-class Waters Acquity ultra-HPLC system. The mass spectrometer utilized was outfitted with an electrospray ionization source, and both positive and negative ion modes were performed.

In brief, the milk samples were thawed at 4 °C, and a 100-μL aliquot was taken from each sample and transferred into 500 μL of a pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile solution (1:1, vol/vol). After vortex mixing and centrifuging (12,000 ×g, 4 °C) for 15 min, the supernatant was gathered and dried in a vacuum concentrator. Aqueous acetonitrile (acetonitrile: water = 1:1, vol/vol) was used to dissolve the dried supernatant, and vortex mixing and centrifuging (12,000 ×g, 4 °C) was performed to collect the resulted supernatant for further analyses. Chromatographic separation was conducted on an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters Corp.). The MassLynx 4.2 software (Waters) was used for controlling the acquisition of the mass data. In each data acquisition cycle, data were taken at both low and high collision energies. The quality control sample was prepared by mixing equal amounts of all samples and acetonitrile was used as a blank. The blank and quality control sample was first analyzed to monitor data quality and system stability.

Milk metabolomic raw data were collected by MassLynx 4.2 workstation and pretreated by the Progenesis QI software package for the extraction, alignment, deconvolution, and normalization of peaks. The software was configured to automatically search the chosen databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (http: //www.hmdb.ca/) and METLIN Metabolite Database (https://metlin.scripps.edu/) to identify the metabolites based on accurate (m/z), retention time, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns. According to the metabolomics standards initiative (MSI) guidelines, the identified metabolites in this study primarily achieved MSI Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds), validated by matching the experimental MS/MS spectra against the reference spectra in the aforementioned databases. After being recognized and aligned, all data were normalized to the sum of total ion intensity per chromatogram. Then, the data sets, including peak number (retention time-m/z pair), sample name, and normalized ion intensities, were imported into MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca) to complete data preprocessing, including missing data estimation, median centering, and unit variance scaling.

Metabolomics data analysis

The statistical analysis was performed on the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). Prior to supervised modeling, the sample correlation heatmap and unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) were initially performed to visualize the global metabolic profiles, assess sample clustering, and evaluate the overall variance driven by breed and lactation stage. Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was generated to visualize alterations in metabolite profiles among samples. To control for the false discovery rate (FDR) during multiple testing, the raw P-values from the single-dimensional statistical analysis were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. Differential metabolites were strictly selected according to the following 3 conditions: |log2 FC| > 1, VIP > 1 from OPLS-DA, and an FDR-adjusted P-value (q-value) < 0.05 (which in our dataset corresponded to a raw P-value <0.01). Moreover, a permutation test was conducted to evaluate the quality of the OPLS-DA models. The goodness-of-fit was represented by R2Y, and the predictive ability was indicated by Q2. The relative average normalized levels of the differential metabolites were plotted in a heatmap created using the pheatmap R package. The metabolites were clustered according to their Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. The differential metabolites were further annotated to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways by the KEGG PATHWAY database (http: //www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Pathways with an impact value greater than 0.1 or P < 0.05 were considered as significantly altered pathways.

Statistical analysis

All data were preliminarily organized using Microsoft Excel 2021. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Normality of data and residuals was checked by using the UNIVARIATE procedure of SAS. Milk composition data were analyzed as repeated measurements with the GLIMMIX procedure of SAS. The statistical models included the fixed effects of breed, lactation stage, and their interaction (breed × lactation stage), whereas individual goats were included as random effects. The compound symmetry (CS) covariance structure was selected for the repeated measures based on the lowest AIC and BIC values among candidate structures (variance components (VC), CS, ante-dependence (ANTE (1)), unstructured (UN)). The model equation was as follows:Yijk = μ + Bi + Sj + (B × S)ij + ck + εijk,where Yijk = dependent continuous variable (milk composition), μ = overall mean, Bi = fixed effect of breed i, Sj = fixed effect of lactation stage j, (B × S)ij = interaction between breed and lactation stage, ck = random effect of the kth animal (goat), and εijk = residual error. Comparisons with P ≤ 0.05 were considered significant, and P ≤ 0.01 were considered highly significant. Least-squares means (LSMeans) were computed and presented as LSMeans ± SEM. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey-Kramer adjustment. Effect sizes were evaluated by examining the magnitude of LSMean differences relative to their SEM.

Results and discussion

Analysis of milk composition, immunoglobulin transfer, and Aflatoxin M1 contamination in the early lactation of CBGs and JBGs

Although slight variations existed, the nutritional content did not differ significantly between the two groups (P > 0.05). Specifically, JBG colostrum was richer in most nutrients, showing higher total solids (35.30% vs 31.77%), fat (11.89% vs 11.42%), and protein (18.85% vs 17.28%), while CBG had higher ash levels (2.70% vs 1.88%). However, lactose levels were nearly identical. These variations may arise from a mix of influences, ranging from genetics and environmental conditions to nutritional factors like rumen metabolism. Interestingly, the lactation stage significantly altered milk composition (Table 1), which emphasizes that timing is a critical factor for quality control in the dairy industry. Specifically, most nutrients—including total solids, fat, protein, and ash—followed a downward trend. They peaked in colostrum and dropped to their lowest levels by day 35. Lactose was the only exception: it started at its lowest point in colostrum and increased steadily over time. This pattern aligns with colostrum's specialized role in providing early immunity and concentrated nutrition (Hernández-Castellano et al., 2014). From a quality control standpoint, colostrum is too distinct to be mixed with mature milk. It should be processed separately to meet industry standards. Furthermore, while the statistical difference between breeds was small, the actual variation in nutrient levels (like total solids, protein, and ash) is still noteworthy. These subtle differences, driven by genetics or the environment, could affect the consistency and quality of the final dairy product. Because ruminants could not pass antibodies through the placenta, newborn goats are born without immune protection. They rely entirely on drinking colostrum to acquire IgG—a process known as TPI. Getting enough TPI is a matter of life and death. If a kid fails to absorb enough IgG (passive immune failure), it suffers severe consequences: higher disease risk, stunted growth, lower future milk production, and a shorter lifespan (Castro et al., 2009; Zamuner et al., 2024). Our study found that CBG colostrum contains significantly more antibodies than JBG (81.29 mg/mL vs. 72.46 mg/mL). Physiologically, this breed-specific divergence in immunoglobulins could be attributed to two interconnected mechanisms governing colostrogenesis rather than a simple independent genetic trait. Firstly, the accumulation of IgG in ruminant colostrum relies heavily on active, receptor-mediated transcellular transport, where maternal IgG is captured from the bloodstream by neonatal Fc receptors (FcRn) on mammary epithelial cells during late pregnancy (Baumrucker et al., 2021; Li et al., 2026). The elevated IgG levels in CBG may reflect a superior expression density or binding affinity of these specialized FcRn, leading to higher blood-to-milk transfer efficiency. Secondly, a physiological ‘dilution effect’ should be considered based on distinct breed phenotypes. JBG is characterized by a substantially larger frame and rapid early growth, which are often associated with greater mammary tissue volume and higher initial colostrum yield. This expanded fluid volume could inherently dilute the concentration of immunoglobulins. Nevertheless, this genetic and physiological advantage likely helps CBG kids achieve better TPI and intestinal health (Kessler et al., 2019). This difference underscores the importance of genetic selection and breed-specific management practices in enhancing herd health and productivity. However, for both breeds, timing is everything. IgG levels drop rapidly within the first 12 h after birth (Table 2). This indicates a narrow window for effective feeding, highlighting the need for breed-specific management and prompt colostrum delivery. Previous studies recommend feeding high-quality colostrum with an IgG concentration ≥ 50 mg/mL within the first 2 h of birth. This timely feeding allows the small intestine to absorb the essential immunoglobulins before its permeability gradually decreases (Lopez & Heinrichs, 2022). Besides timing, the main factors influencing immunoglobulin absorption are the IgG concentration in the colostrum and the total volume of colostrum provided during the first feeding (Stott & Fellah, 1983). The absence of significant differences in IgG levels between the two breeds beyond 12 h further underscores the critical importance of early postnatal colostrum management (Table 2). Moreover, our results demonstrated a positive correlation between Brix level and IgG content in colostrum, which is consistent with previous studies indicating a strong relationship between Brix values and IgG concentration in goat colostrum (Kessler et al., 2021). We found severe AFM1 contamination in every sample, regardless of the breed or the time of collection. This widespread presence poses a serious threat to milk safety. Consequently, we need to take urgent action to control these risks and ensure the products are safe for consumption. We tested 94 milk samples from two breeds over the first two weeks. Shockingly, every single sample (100%) tested positive for AFM1 and exceeded the strict EU safety limit (0.05 μg/kg). Furthermore, nearly 14% even surpassed China's higher national threshold (0.5 μg/kg). Biologically, the highest toxin levels were found in the first milk (colostrum)—especially in CBG goats—but fortunately, these levels gradually dropped as lactation continued (Table 3). This confirms that both the goat's breed and the lactation stage influence how toxins accumulate. The high contamination in colostrum is especially alarming because it is the newborn's only source of nutrition. Consuming high levels of AFM1 at this fragile stage could damage the liver and gastrointestinal tract, while also weakening the immune system (Becker-Algeri et al., 2016). Ultimately, this reduces the kids' chance of survival and future productivity. Furthermore, the fact that the toxin remained present throughout the study indicates that the animals are likely consuming contaminated feed continuously (Yang et al., 2020), which points to poor storage or sourcing of TMR ingredients like silage and concentrates. These findings highlight the urgent need for better feed safety practices on these commercial farms. A notable limitation of our study is the absence of direct AFB1 analysis in the TMR, which warrants further investigation. However, the environmental realities of the Sichuan region could not be ignored. The local combination of high humidity and moderate heat consistently drives Aspergillus proliferation in stored dairy feed. When viewed alongside historical data, our findings of elevated AFM1 are entirely consistent with the high mycotoxin background typical of agricultural and dairy products in southern China (Abi Rizk et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2021). To effectively reduce mycotoxin levels in colostrum, it is recommended that meat goat farmers in high-humidity regions such as the Sichuan Basin adopt a two-pronged approach. Firstly, routinely add mycotoxin binders such as bentonite, hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicates (HSCAS), or esterified glucomannans to the TMR to minimize toxin absorption in the gastrointestinal tract and reduce their transfer into milk (Kihal et al., 2023). Based on our longitudinal data, we propose a preliminary three-tier grading standard for meat goat mammary secretions as follows: Grade A (high-quality colostrum): first milking within 2 h postpartum, with IgG ≥ 50 mg/mL (observed range: 72.46–81.29 mg/mL), Brix ≥30%, total solids ≥31%, and protein ≥17%. This grade ensures effective passive immunity transfer. Grade B (transitional milk): 12-48 h postpartum, characterized by IgG 28.72–35.59 mg/mL, Brix 18–22%, total solids 21–25%, and protein 7–13%. It offers good nutritional value but limited immune protection. Grade C (mature milk): From day 7 onward, with IgG < 1.2 mg/mL, Brix <13%, total solids 14–18%, and protein 3–5%. Across all grades, AFM1 levels must remain below 0.5 μg/kg to meet food safety requirements. These thresholds are anchored in our observed temporal changes and provide a practical framework for colostrum management in meat goats.

Distinct milk metabolite profiles between CBGs and JBGs revealed by LC-MS based metabolomics

Milk metabolites generally reflect the metabolic activity of the mammary gland or the entire organism. Here, we employed untargeted metabolomics coupled with LC-MS analysis to investigate differences in milk metabolites between CBGs and JBGs. Colostrum and early mature milk (day 7 postpartum) samples from two multiparous goat breeds were randomly collected for metabolomic analysis (n = 9 per group), including CBG colostrum (CC), CBG mature milk (CM), JBG colostrum (JC), and JBG mature milk (JM). OPLS-DA models (R2Y > 0.9, Q2 > 0.5) demonstrated clear separation among all groups, and validation results support the use of VIP scores to identify significant metabolite differences.

To evaluate the reproducibility of biological replicates and the overall structure of the metabolomic data, a sample correlation analysis was conducted. The correlation heatmap (Fig. 2A) revealed high intra-group correlation and distinct clustering of samples into four groups (CC, CM, JC, and JM). This clustering pattern visually confirms that the metabolome profiles are strongly driven by both the lactation stage and breed genetics, providing a reliable basis for subsequent differential metabolite screening. Before identifying specific differential metabolites, a global PCA was conducted to visualize the overall metabolic variance among all samples (Fig. 2B). The PCA score plot revealed a distinct separation between colostrum and mature milk samples, indicating that the lactation stage is the dominant driver of metabolic variance. Interestingly, within the same lactation stage, samples from the two distinct breeds (CBG and JBG) exhibited considerable overlap with no clear sub-clustering. From both a statistical and biological perspective, this pattern shows that the overall milk metabolome is primarily shaped by the major physiological changes occurring over time, rather than by inherent breed differences. Mathematically, unsupervised PCA works by identifying the directions of greatest variance across thousands of metabolic features. The transition from colostrum to mature milk involves a massive, coordinated reprogramming of the mammary gland, triggering large-scale shifts in hundreds of lipids, carbohydrates, nucleotides, and other compounds all at once. These dramatic temporal changes naturally dominate the principal components. Biologically, CBG and JBG share a highly conserved lactation program typical of the species. Their core nutritional composition therefore remains similar. Breed-specific differences (such as variations in IgG transfer efficiency or certain dipeptide levels) are more like fine adjustments within this common framework, rather than broad rewiring of entire pathways. As a result, these subtler differences are easily overshadowed in a global unsupervised PCA plot. This contrast clearly demonstrates why supervised modeling is so useful. While PCA is excellent for highlighting the dominant drivers (lactation stage), OPLS-DA allows us to remove that overwhelming temporal variation and uncover the more subtle but meaningful breed-specific metabolic signatures. Based on the established screening criteria (|log2 FC|>1, p < 0.01，VIP > 1), a total of 804 (CC vs CM), 790 (JC vs JM), 230 (CC vs JC), and 285 (CM vs JM) DEMs were identified in total ion mode (Fig. 3). Metabolic profile difference of CC vs CM and JC vs JM

Metabolomic analysis revealed a predominant upregulation of metabolites in colostrum compared to mature milk, with 537 (CBG) and 589 (JBG) metabolites elevated, respectively, whereas mature milk showed fewer upregulated metabolites (267 in CBG and 201 in JBG). (Fig. 3A, B). The large number of DEMs identified between colostrum and mature milk within each breed highlights the dynamic nature of milk composition during early lactation. For example, choline derivatives such as the lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) Heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine and the polyunsaturated LysoPC (20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)) were highly expressed in colostrum samples. Consistent with the findings of this study, previous research has also reported high concentrations of choline metabolites in bovine colostrum (Amarjeet et al., 2025). Choline is an essential component for normal cellular function, involved in membrane phospholipid synthesis, neurotransmitter production, and the regulation of cognitive function and brain development (Zeisel, 2004). During the critical transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life, colostrum does much more than provide basic nutrition. Its rich composition plays key structural and signaling roles. It supplies essential precursors for rapid membrane biogenesis, which supports the explosive growth of the intestinal epithelium and other developing tissues. At the same time, it helps support acetylcholine synthesis, which is important for early brain development. Additionally, the elevated levels of glycerophosphorylcholine during early lactation may help prevent metabolic stress in dairy animals by releasing fatty acids to meet elevated energy demands (Klein et al., 2012).

Suberoyl-l-carnitine was identified as a major carnitine derivative in colostrum, functioning as an intermediate in fatty acid metabolism. Carnitine plays an essential role in transporting long-chain fatty acids from the cytosol into the mitochondria of mammary cells for β-oxidation, thereby supplying energy to the organism. Studies have shown that l-carnitine is a critical factor in mitochondrial fatty acid transport and subsequent β-oxidation and energy release. It also helps regulate mitochondrial biosynthesis, influencing both metabolic and immune functions (Kononov et al., 2022). This immune modulation becomes particularly crucial during the shift from passive to active immunity. At this stage, mitochondrial function plays a key role in driving the activation and proliferation of the newborn's immune cells. Furthermore, dietary supplementation of l-carnitine in transition dairy cows has been found to increase milk yield in early lactation and improve milk fat percentage in the first week of lactation, indicating enhanced fat oxidation efficiency (Meyer et al., 2020). Recent research demonstrates that l-carnitine present in milk plays a vital role in promoting the oxidation of long-chain fatty acids in the developing intestine of newborns, which is crucial for improving energy utilization and driving the rapid growth, structural maturation, and barrier formation in the neonatal gastrointestinal tract (Pike et al., 2023).

18-Hydroxycortisol is a steroid hormone belonging to the hydroxylated metabolite of cortisol, secreted by the adrenal cortex. Studies have shown that cortisol ingested through milk could be absorbed by the infant's intestine (Dettmer et al., 2018). Cortisol promotes the release of amino acids, glucose, and fatty acids to supply energy to the body and plays a key role in the development of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, influencing the infant's stress response (Beery et al., 2023). As a precursor to cortisol, 18-Hydroxycortisol is crucial for cortisol synthesis. Our results showed that 18-Hydroxycortisol was highly expressed in the colostrum of both goat breeds, suggesting potential benefits for infant growth and development. Furthermore, consistent with our findings, colostrum contains high levels of inositol derivatives. This polyol exists in milk in both free and bound forms and has been widely added to infant formula to prevent potential nutritional deficiencies during early growth stages (Cavalli et al., 2006; Moles et al., 2015).

We identified a large number of fatty acid derivatives in colostrum. PS (16:0/LTE4) and LysoPE (0:0/14:0) were the predominant saturated fatty acid derivatives. The major unsaturated fatty acid derivatives included PGP (18:0/18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)), glycerophosphoinositol (PI) (20:1(11Z)/18:3(10,12,15)-OH(9)), glycerophosphoethanolamine (PE) (20:5(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z,17Z)/18:1(12Z)-2OH(9,10)), and PGP (i-12:0/20:5(7Z,9Z,11E,13E,17Z)-3OH(5,6,15)). A similar lipid composition has been observed in human colostrum: it is rich in lipid molecules essential for newborns, including elevated concentrations of n-3 PUFAs, palmitic acid (C16:0), myristic acid, and phospholipids (Contarini et al., 2014; Wilms et al., 2022). These specific characteristics of colostrum may reflect the particular needs of newborn kids. For example, the high PUFA content in colostrum may be partly explained by the maximal elongation and desaturation capacity of linoleic and linolenic acids during the first few weeks after birth. Palmitic acid may play a role in establishing the gut microbiome and mucosal homeostasis, while the increased proportion of myristic acid could enhance appetite responses in newborns and improve colostrum yield. Previous studies have highlighted glycerophosphocholine (PC), PE, PI, and glycerophosphoserine (PS) as the most abundant classes of polar lipids (Liu et al., 2020). Polar lipids play a crucial role in infant brain development and cognitive function, and help prevent disorders of lipid metabolism, imbalances in gut microbiota, and inflammation (Zheng et al., 2019).

Disialyllactose, 3′-Sialyllactose, and 3′-N-Acetylneuraminyl-N-acetyllactosamine—all highly expressed in colostrum—belong to sialylated oligosaccharides. These oligosaccharides help prevent intestinal infections by promoting the colonization of beneficial gut microbiota, while also enhancing immune function and supporting neonatal brain development (Ten Bruggencate et al., 2014). Among them, 3′-Sialyllactose has been positively associated with cognitive development in newborns, particularly in language acquisition (Pisa et al., 2021). Studies have also shown that 3′-Sialyllactose supports a healthy intestinal environment in neonatal calves (Short et al., 2016; Fischer et al., 2018). Similarly, 3′-N-Acetylneuraminyl-N-acetyllactosamine contributes to neural development, helps maintain cognitive function, and enhances immune responses (Zhang et al., 2019).

Among the amino acids enriched in colostrum, L-Leucine, N-(1-Deoxy-1-fructosyl)isoleucine, Lysylvaline, and Threonylvaline belong to the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs). BCAAs serve as crucial substrates for protein synthesis in the mammary gland and play important roles in lipid and glucose metabolism, intestinal development, immune response, and mammary health. They are thus considered significant factors influencing fetal development (Zhang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2018). Among them, leucine and isoleucine enhance protein synthesis in bovine mammary cells by activating the mTOR signaling pathway (Appuhamy et al., 2012). As key substrates for the synthesis of glutamine and glutamate, valine supports the growth of mammary tissue and milk production during lactation (Zhang et al., 2019). Moreover, several dipeptides were highly abundant in colostrum, including γ-glutamyltyrosine, tryptophyl-proline, valylhydroxyproline, N-eicosapentaenoyl methionine, γ-glutamylcysteine, glycyl-prolyl-glutamic acid, N-carbamoylglutamic acid, and tyrosyl-glutamine. These compounds play significant roles in physiological metabolism and the antioxidant defense system.

By day 7, the observed metabolic changes reflect a rapid shift to mature milk synthesis. Although full stabilization of goat milk composition requires 14 to 21 days, the primary physical, chemical, and immunological transition from colostrum to milk largely occurs within 2 to 5 days (Raimondo et al., 2024; Romero et al., 2013; Sánchez-Macías et al., 2014). Consequently, day 7 could be classified as an ‘early mature’ stage, distinct from the transitional period. This sampling point effectively contrasts with colostrum and aligns with recent experimental designs treating day 7 secretions as mature milk (Kanmaz et al., 2025). Nucleotide derivatives such as Guanosine 5-diphosphate (GDP) and Uridine 5′-diphosphate (UDP) were highly expressed in mature milk. UDP, an important nucleotide derivative composed of uridine and two phosphate groups, belongs to the nucleoside diphosphate (NDP) family. It plays key roles in various biochemical processes including carbohydrate metabolism, signal transduction, and protein glycosylation. Furthermore, mature milk exhibited high levels of sugar nucleotides, primarily UDP-D-galactose, UDP-glucose, ADP-glucose, and GDP-glucose. These sugar nucleotides are essential for the synthesis of UDP-sugars, which help regulate neonatal growth and immune functions (Characterisation of sugar nucleotides in colostrum of dairy domestic farm animals). Among them, UDP-D-galactose—a critical nucleotide sugar formed by linking UDP and D-galactose via an α-1 phosphate ester bond—serves as a key carrier molecule in galactose metabolism and glycosylation modifications. It is extensively involved in polysaccharide synthesis, cellular signaling, and protein modification processes. Glucose serves as a primary source for lactose and glycerol synthesis in mammary gland secretory alveolar epithelial cells during lactation, while also supplying energy to the body. As reported in previous studies, glucose concentration in milk shows a positive correlation with the relative body weight of breastfed infants (Cheema et al., 2021). Mannose, a functional monosaccharide, possesses notable physiological properties and bioactivities. In milk, mannose is an important natural sugar whose specific roles require further investigation. Additionally, D-mannose has been shown to inhibit tumor growth and exert anti-inflammatory effects through immunomodulatory mechanisms (Zhou, Zheng, & Liu, 2021).

Among the differential metabolites, 434 out of 804 metabolites (from CC vs CM) were enriched in 163 metabolic pathways, while 347 out of 790 metabolites (from JC vs JM) were enriched in 123 pathways (Table S2), and the 10 most significantly enriched metabolic terms in the dataset are displayed (Fig. 4A, B). The most abundant pathways in the current metabolomics dataset of colostrum and mature milk from both goat breeds were: pyrimidine metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, fatty acid elongation, glycerolipid metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, galactose metabolism, among others. In summary, the upregulation of choline derivatives, carnitine derivatives, polyols, fatty acid derivatives, sialylated oligosaccharides, and amino acids—predominantly branched-chain amino acids—in colostrum reflects a dynamic metabolic state during early lactation. This profile is characterized by the capacity to promote neural and intestinal development and enhance immune responses. In contrast, mature milk showed increased levels of nucleotide derivatives and sugar nucleotides, associated with maintaining energy homeostasis and supporting sustained growth. Metabolic profile difference of CC vs JC and CM vs JM

Comparative metabolomics identified 230 and 285 differential metabolites between breeds in colostrum (CC vs JC) and mature milk (CM vs JM), respectively. A greater number of metabolites were upregulated in CBGs compared to JBGs in both colostrum (144 vs 86) and mature milk (230 vs 55) (Fig. 3C, D). Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites revealed distinct profiles between the comparisons. In the CC vs JC group, 115 metabolites were enriched in 74 pathways, with the most significant including alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and morphine addiction. In contrast, the CM vs JM group had 142 metabolites enriched in 71 pathways, prominently featuring insulin resistance and metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 (Fig. 4C, D; Table S2).

Notably, the DEMs identified between breeds at the same lactation stage indicate that genetic background significantly influences milk metabolite profiles. In both colostrum and mature milk, the most enriched pathways common to the metabolomic datasets of the two goat breeds involved cortisol synthesis and secretion, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and tyrosine metabolism. Metabolites such as nicotinamide, NAAG, dehydroascorbic acid, and urinary cortisol were highly expressed in JBG colostrum. In their mature milk, compounds including N2-succinyl-L-arginine, NAAG, and arginylthreonine were upregulated. Studies have shown that arginine, a conditionally essential amino acid, is often limited in colostrum, yet the demands for rapid postnatal growth in newborns are high (Wu & Knabe, 1995). NAAG is a highly important and unique molecule in the brain, serving a dual role as both a neuropeptide and a neurotransmitter. It is the most abundant dipeptide in the mammalian brain, with concentrations exceeding those of many classical neurotransmitters. The abundance of NAAG in both colostrum and mature milk of JBGs reflect the distinctive metabolic characteristics of this breed's milk.

CBGs exhibited a significantly greater number of upregulated metabolites in both colostrum and mature milk compared to JBGs. Specific metabolites highly expressed in CBG colostrum included Gly-Lys-Met, Methionyl-Proline, Glutamylvaline, Pro-Ala-Trp, and Valyl-Tryptophan. Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate—non-essential amino acids synthetized through animal metabolic processes—play an active role in mammary metabolism and exert an important influence on early infant nutrition intake and growth (Rezaei et al., 2016). In CBG mature milk, metabolites such as Alanylisoleucine, N-Acetyl-L-phenylalanine, N-Lactoyl-Tyrosine, Glutamylvaline, and Galactosyl-4-hydroxyproline were notably upregulated. Glycine serves as an essential component in several biologically critical compounds, including heme, glutathione, creatine, nucleic acids, and collagen (Rasmussen et al., 2021).

Sun et al. (2017) reported that glutamate metabolism, glycine biosynthesis, and glycine degradation pathways may represent key metabolic pathways in lactating dairy cows. The adequate availability of glycine, glutamate, and cysteine is essential for glutathione synthesis. More recently, in growing pigs, supplementation of serine and glycine to a low-protein diet was shown to promote growth and skin collagen synthesis (Silva et al., 2020). Therefore, the higher levels of these metabolites in both colostrum and mature milk of CBGs—compared to JBGs—may contribute to a more robust response in milk protein synthesis. Consistent with this, our findings indicate that CBGs exhibit higher milk protein content than JBGs, which may be partly attributed to the elevated abundance of milk metabolites associated with these metabolic pathways. These breed-specific metabolic signatures could serve as potential biomarkers for authenticity verification and traceability of milk origin—an important aspect in preventing food fraud.

Furthermore, certain metabolites may be associated with sensory properties, nutritional value, or even undesirable residues, all of which are critical factors in food safety and quality assurance. For example, Aflatoxin M₁-8,9-epoxide—a highly reactive and carcinogenic metabolite derived from the hepatic activation of AFM1 (Bujons et al., 1995)—was detected in both the colostrum and mature milk of CBGs. This observation strongly aligns with our finding that AFM₁ concentrations were substantially higher in CBG colostrum. While the ability of metabolomics to differentiate milk based on breed and lactation stage provides a powerful tool for ensuring product consistency and safety, a recognized limitation of our untargeted approach is that the current biological interpretations remain largely correlative. Although this study provides a valuable baseline, the exact mechanisms governing these metabolic changes require experimental confirmation. Future work should prioritize targeted functional assays, such as in vitro testing or isotopic tracing. These functional assays are necessary to confirm how specific DEMs actually influence bioactivity, allergenicity, and contaminant metabolism, which ultimately impact dairy quality and consumer safety.

Conclusion

In summary, this study characterizes the dynamic evolution of milk composition and metabolic profiles in CBG and JBG goats during early lactation. Our data demonstrate that nutritional variability is driven by the interplay between breed genetics and lactation stage. Notably, while high colostral antibody levels are critical for passive immunity, the prevalence of toxin contamination highlights an urgent need for rigorous feed management and safety monitoring. Subsequently, synthesizing these findings allowed us to propose a preliminary three-tier grading standard for meat goat milk, anchored by specific IgG, Brix, and nutritional thresholds, alongside a strict AFM1 safety cap. Metabolomic analysis revealed distinct biological strategies between the breeds: CBG colostrum is enriched in metabolites related to energy and amino acid metabolism, whereas JBG mature milk features pathways associated with insulin regulation and nerve signaling. Collectively, these findings elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying milk quality, offering a scientific framework for establishing precise grading standards and safety protocols to support the sustainable development of the goat dairy industry.
