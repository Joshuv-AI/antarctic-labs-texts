Interpretable ensemble meta-learning for estimated ultimate recovery prediction in shale gas wells
Authors: Runshi Huo, Benjieming Liu, Han Dong, Pengfei Li, Wei Xiong, Yuping Sun, Bo Zhang, Shitao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of the Estimated Ultimate Recovery (EUR) of shale gas wells is essential for development planning and economic evaluation. However, complex geological and engineering conditions make reliable EUR prediction challenging, especially in the absence of standardized methods. Although deep learning models provide strong predictive performance, their “black-box” nature limits trust among decision-makers, and current models often neglect interpretability. To address these issues, we propose the LMTR framework, a meta-learner ensemble model for shale gas well EUR prediction. LMTR has a scaffold that integrates Lasso and Ridge regularization to enhance stability and mitigate multicollinearity, while combining a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and a Transformer to capture nonlinear relationships and long-range feature interactions. The model is evaluated using Mean Squared Error (MSE) and R², with leave-one-out cross-validation. Comparative results against 11 baseline models demonstrate that LMTR achieves superior predictive performance. In addition, SHAP analysis and Net Present Value (NPV) evaluation are incorporated to improve interpretability and support engineering optimization. The results indicate that LMTR provides accurate and reliable EUR prediction and offers a practical tool for optimizing shale gas development strategies.

Introduction

Amid rising global energy demand, the advent of horizontal drilling combined with multistage hydraulic fracturing has ushered oil and gas development into the unconventional era. Following the United States’ breakthrough in large-scale shale gas extraction, many countries have incorporated shale gas as a critical component of their energy portfolios1. This technology enables large-scale reservoir stimulation, establishing flow pathways for shale gas—an energy source cleaner than coal or oil. Accurate estimation of Estimated Ultimate Recovery (EUR) is essential for optimizing well productivity and guiding efficient resource development2,3. As a key indicator of economic viability, EUR also informs national planning strategies for natural gas exploitation4,5. Scientifically grounded EUR assessments not only enhance resource utilization but also support the global transition toward carbon neutrality.

Hydraulic fracturing in the development of shale gas creates complex flow pathways within the reservoir, making fracturing effectiveness a direct determinant of gas production. At the same time, fracturing costs remain a dominant factor affecting the economic viability of shale wells6. As such, the accurate estimation of the EUR is crucial for guiding development strategies and assessing commercial value.

Traditional EUR evaluation methods can be broadly categorized into empirical decline curve analysis (DCA), modern DCA, and numerical simulation. Classical empirical approaches—including the Arps model7, the modified Arps model8, and exponential decline models9—predict future production by fitting historical output data with relatively few parameters. However, these methods struggle to accommodate the transient flow characteristics common in shale reservoirs.

In response to the geological and engineering complexities of shale gas production, modern DCA models have emerged. These approaches incorporate reservoir heterogeneity and fracturing parameters, with numerous refinements proposed. For instance, Duong8 introduced a model applied to 37 wells, later extended to over 5,600 Marcellus wells10. Middleton et al.11 proposed a dual-exponential model for analyzing over 20,000 Barnett shale wells. Despite these advancements, increasing model complexity often leads to reduced generalizability and persistent uncertainty in EUR12,13.

Numerical simulation remains another mainstream technique in conventional hydrocarbon reservoirs. However, the highly heterogeneous nature of shale—characterized by matrix flow, natural fractures, and induced fracture networks14,15—renders its flow mechanisms poorly understood16,17. This complexity presents substantial challenges to constructing accurate reservoir models18–20. Furthermore, the processing of complex seismic data from intermediate to deep strata imposes additional constraints on simulation accuracy. These limitations highlight the need for continued advancement in numerical modeling specific to shale systems. Given the inherent constraints of empirical, modern DCA and numerical approaches, data-driven machine learning methods are increasingly gaining traction as promising alternatives for EUR prediction.

The application of machine learning (ML) techniques in estimating the Estimated Ultimate Recovery (EUR) of shale gas wells has garnered increasing attention due to their ability to uncover complex, nonlinear relationships and latent patterns within high-dimensional data. Shale gas development is governed by a myriad of interacting geological and engineering parameters, compounded by intricate multiphase flow processes21. Within this context, ML methods offer a powerful framework to capture the multifactorial dependencies influencing EUR and provide real-time insights to guide well design and operational strategies22.

With the growing complexity of shale reservoir flow mechanisms, driven by advances in extraction technologies, traditional EUR prediction models have struggled to provide accurate assessments, particularly during the early production phase. Classical decline curve analysis and its modern variants require sufficient production history, and numerical simulation demands detailed geological characterization and well-understood flow dynamics23. These constraints limit their effectiveness under uncertain or data-scarce conditions. In contrast, machine learning—empowered by its robust data-mining capabilities—has emerged as a critical tool for EUR forecasting under multifactorial reservoir conditions.

Numerous studies have explored ML-based EUR prediction using diverse algorithms. Models such as linear regression, artificial neural networks (ANN)24, gradient boosting decision trees (GBDT)25, Extra Trees, k-nearest neighbors (KNN)26,27, support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), and multivariate regressions have all demonstrated varying degrees of success. With the rapid evolution of ML, deep learning approaches have gained traction in modeling production potential. Deep neural networks (DNNs)28,29, which extend classical ML with multiple hidden layers, enable hierarchical abstraction of complex input features. Recent studies have implemented deep learning for shale EUR forecasting with notable success30. Hui (2021)31 and Niu (2022)32 applied ANN models to improve EUR prediction accuracy31. Liu et al.5 developed various Deep-ANN architectures incorporating well and completion design parameters32. Park et al.33 integrated neural networks with genetic algorithms (GA) to achieve rapid history matching and optimization. Zhang and Fang employed long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to project long-term production from short-term flow data, highlighting the growing role of temporal sequence modeling in EUR estimation34–36.

The complex, high-dimensional data inherent in efficient shale gas development impose stringent demands on the predictive capabilities of deep learning models37,38. To address this, many researchers have leveraged the complementary strengths of diverse ML algorithms to construct hybrid models for EUR prediction39,40. For instance, Qian et al.41 combined Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR) with SHAP analysis to identify key geological and engineering drivers of EUR. Tamimi et al.42 applied Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) to forecast decline curve parameters. Ensemble approaches based on Random Forests (RF) were explored by Al-Mudhafar and Vyas for improved accuracy43,44. Mohaghegh et al.19 incorporated fuzzy set theory into feedforward neural networks (FFNN) and further integrated deep learning with Monte Carlo simulations to quantify prediction uncertainty. These data-driven approaches not only enhance the mechanistic understanding of shale reservoirs but also provide actionable insights for engineering optimization .

While ML has emerged as a powerful tool for EUR estimation, existing studies still suffer from three critical gaps45: (1) lack of comprehensive model comparison, (2) limited attention to model interpretability, and (3) insufficient theoretical convergence validation.

This study aims to bridge these gaps by enabling accurate EUR prediction during the early design phase of a single-well development plan, immediately after geological and engineering parameters are established, rather than relying on extended production history, which is vital for economically scaling high-cost shale gas operations. The present work introduces several key innovations to achieve high-precision EUR prediction and dominant factor analysis, as outlined below.

Methodology

This study proposes an ensemble learning framework (LMTR) for EUR prediction, which integrates feature selection, nonlinear representation learning, feature interaction modeling, and regression into a unified pipeline. The framework combines Lasso for feature selection, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for nonlinear feature extraction, a Transformer encoder for modeling feature dependencies, and Ridge regression for final prediction.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the LMTR framework consists of four main components: (1) Lasso for feature selection, (2) MLP for nonlinear feature learning, (3) Transformer for capturing feature interactions, and (4) Ridge regression for final EUR prediction. This ensemble pipeline enables effective integration of linear and nonlinear modeling while maintaining model robustness and interpretability.

Fig. 1Overall architecture of the proposed LMTR meta-learner ensemble framework for EUR prediction in shale gas wells. The pipeline consists of five stages: (I) Data Preparation, where primary and secondary features from multiple wells are batched for training; (II) Feature Selection, using a meta regularizer to generate sparse and robust feature representations; (III) Non-Linear Feature Learning, where an MLP learns complex nonlinear patterns; (IV) Feature Dependency Modeling, where a Transformer-based module captures long-range dependencies, and (V) Meta-Learner for EUR Prediction, which aggregates the processed features into the LMTR ensemble to produce accurate EUR forecasts.

Due to the limited size of well-based data, the Transformer encoder is not pre-trained and its parameters are kept fixed during backpropagation to prevent overfitting, as experimental results show that fully training it leads to unstable convergence, while freezing improves stability within the ensemble architecture.

Application

Introduction of the region

The CN area is a deep-water shelf facies dominated by siliceous shale, with the organic-rich Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation serving as the primary reservoir. The reservoir is vertically divided into lower and upper sequences. The lower sequence exhibits superior petrophysical properties: total organic carbon (TOC) of 2.6–4.0%, porosity of 4.0–7.1%, and brittle mineral content of 62–78%. These attributes directly enhance gas storage capacity and fractureability, leading to higher EUR. In contrast, the upper sequence has lower TOC (2.0–3.0%) and porosity (3.0–6.1%), reducing its contribution to EUR.

Critically, the net pay thickness of Layers 1 + 2 (the primary productive interval) ranges from 12 to 24 m in the lower sequence and 0–30 m in the upper sequence. This thickness is a first-order control on EUR because it directly scales the available reservoir volume. Similarly, permeability (though low in shale) influences long-term gas deliverability, while vertical depth affects in-situ stress and fracturing pressure, thereby impacting stimulation effectiveness and EUR. These geological features are incorporated as key input variables in our machine learning models to quantify their relative importance.

Data preparation and feature selection

Shale gas EUR is governed by an interplay of geological, engineering, and operational factors20. Based on the geological controls discussed above (Sect. “Introduction of the region”) and field practice, we compiled 24 features spanning three categories: geological (e.g., permeability, net pay thickness, TOC, vertical depth), engineering (e.g., fracturing fluid volume, proppant mass, stage spacing, fracture length), and operational (test production rate). A novel feature, Acm (effective fracture seepage area), is introduced to better characterize fracture-reservoir contact. Table 2 lists all features with their physical units and ranges.

Liu’s study developed a feedforward neural network model to examine the influence of geological, engineering, and production factors on EUR. Among these, production factors such as Test Production Rate and First-year daily gas flow rate demonstrated strong correlations with EUR, with values of 0.94 and 0.96, respectively. This high correlation is unsurprising, as both Test Production Rate and First-year daily gas flow rate are essentially manifestations of EUR, all reflecting the productivity of shale gas wells. To validate model accuracy, this study also assesses the correlation between the Test Production Rate and the first-year daily gas flow rate, both of which exhibit a high correlation with EUR. However, while these parameters reflect EUR and can be considered alternative representations of the same underlying concept, their presence may influence the correlation and prediction outcomes for other factors. Therefore, this paper retains only the Test Production Rate as a key production factor, excluding the First-year daily gas flow rate, to focus on the impact of geological and engineering parameters on EUR.

A novel aspect of this work is the introduction of the Ac value, representing the effective seepage area of fractures. This metric, derived from Acm (the contact area with the reservoir), replaces the fracture half-length and offers a more accurate representation of fracturing effects and reservoir flow capacity. Initially proposed by Al-Ahmadi et al., Acm characterizes linear flow in shale gas wells and the flow capacity of complex fractures. Al-Ahmadi et al. also provided a calculation methodology for Acm based on linear flow models. This paper extends their work by using microseismic data to calculate Ac, which serves the same purpose as Acm but is derived through a different methodology. The innovative application of Ac enables more precise EUR predictions during the initial design phase, immediately following the completion of the fracturing process. In total, this paper processes microseismic data from 234 shale gas wells and calculates the Ac for each well, thereby contributing to the effective prediction of EUR in the development stage.

The primary objective of this paper is to accurately predict EUR during the design phase of a single-well development plan, immediately following the determination of geological conditions and completion of engineering design. This allows for the optimization of shale gas wells to achieve maximum EUR, rather than predicting and evaluating EUR after production has commenced. Furthermore, machine learning models are employed to assess the relationships between multiple factors influencing EUR, providing actionable insights for engineering optimization.

This study evaluates EUR by selecting 234 wells with complete data that have been in stable production for over two years. Given that longer production times lead to more accurate EUR assessments, the modern production decline model is employed. This approach considers not only geological and engineering factors but also the comprehensive impact of daily production dynamics and management operations on production over time.

Result and discussion

Model results

This study selects twelve machine learning algorithms, consisting of eleven fundamental models and one innovative approach. The fundamental models are grouped based on their underlying principles, including linear regression models, tree-based models, support vector machine models, and proximity models. The linear regression models include Linear Regression, Polynomial Regression, Ridge, and Lasso. The tree-based models include Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and CatBoost. The support vector machine model is represented by SVR, and the proximity model by KNN47,48. The innovative model proposed in this study is the LMTR model42. Hyperparameters for all models were optimized using grid search with 5-fold cross-validation, where the optimal settings were selected based on validation MSE. To ensure fairness, all models shared the same cross-validation splits.

This study utilizes a dataset comprising 5,850 data points from 234 wells, with each well characterized by 24 features and its Estimated Ultimate Recovery (EUR). A total of twelve models are employed, including eleven fundamental algorithms and one innovative approach: Linear Regression, Polynomial Regression, Ridge, Lasso, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, CatBoost, SVR, KNN, and the innovative LMTR model. The dataset is divided into an 80% training set and a 20% test set for model training. Hyperparameter optimization is performed for each model, and K-Fold cross-validation is applied to all models, where the data is partitioned into five folds, enabling repeated training and evaluation across different subsets. This method ensures that the models comprehensively reflect their training effectiveness across various datasets, enhancing their generalization ability and accuracy. Additionally, it mitigates the risks of overfitting and model instability that can arise from data partitioning, thus improving the reliability of model evaluation. Given the limited number of sample points, the K-Fold approach maximizes data usage by allowing each data point to be used multiple times, improving model accuracy and enhancing the overall efficiency of data utilization.

SHAP analysis and NPV

To enable the large-scale and economically viable development of shale gas resources2,50, it is essential to identify and prioritize the engineering parameters that most significantly impact production outcomes. Leveraging SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values for global sensitivity analysis, we systematically disentangle the contributions of individual input variables to the predictive model of Estimated Ultimate Recovery (EUR). The SHAP analysis is shown in Fig. 8. This analysis offers a data-driven framework to optimize development strategies, ensuring that EUR targets are achieved while maximizing net present value (NPV)51.

Fig. 8SHAP.

SHAP.

Our results show that engineering and geological factors affect EUR in highly different ways. To clarify: SHAP simply tells us whether each factor increases or decreases EUR—positive values mean beneficial, negative values mean detrimental. Among all variables, the effective seepage area (Ac) is the most important, consistently and strongly improving EUR. Next is the combined thickness of Layers 1 and 2, confirming that reservoir volume governs production potential.

The SHAP distribution for layer thickness reveals a clear saturation effect: beyond a critical thickness, additional gains are marginal. When thickness is low, SHAP values are narrowly negative, indicating a strong suppressive effect on EUR. Interestingly, the SHAP pattern for Layer 1 + 2 thickness defines three distinct influence zones. Test production rate also ranks high—higher initial rates generally predict better EUR. Stage length shows classic diminishing returns: lengths near the average often suffice, offering a cost-effective trade-off. In thin reservoirs, moderate stage lengths balance EUR and cost. Vertical depth exhibits a bipolar SHAP pattern with a negative link to EUR, suggesting challenges such as higher stress or lower permeability.

Fracture length, proppant concentration, and horizontal length consistently yield positive SHAP values, underscoring their role in enhancing recovery. Taken together, stage length, fracture length, sand volume, and horizontal section length are the four key engineering levers for production optimization. They not only predict EUR well but also drive most fracturing costs, making them prime targets for cost-benefit optimization. Secondary features have weaker isolated effects due to complex interactions. Thus, a multivariate, site-specific optimization approach is needed—one that accounts for parameter interdependencies, geological variation, and project-specific economic goals.

From the overall relationship between EUR and fracturing design, it is evident that fracturing design is closely tied to EUR. However, a larger fracturing scale does not necessarily translate into higher EUR benefits. Therefore, for cost-effective development of a single well, the fracturing scale can easily become the primary factor constraining economic returns. Future research should focus on clarifying the specific impact of fracturing design on EUR. Moreover, fracturing cost is highly dependent on local wellsite conditions; thus, fracturing design should be tailored to local circumstances to maximize economic outcomes, rather than blindly increasing the fracturing scale.

Collectively, our results establish a robust, machine learning-enabled foundation for engineering decision-making in shale gas development. The SHAP-based interpretability framework not only enhances confidence in EUR predictions but also guides efficient hydraulic fracturing design and economic planning. This study underscores the practical utility of explainable AI techniques in resource development, offering actionable insights to bridge predictive modeling with field-scale operational strategies.

Conclusions

This study developed an interpretable ensemble meta-learning framework, termed LMTR, for predicting the estimated ultimate recovery (EUR) of shale gas wells prior to production—immediately after geological and engineering parameters are defined. The framework integrates Lasso for feature selection, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for nonlinear mapping, a Transformer encoder for capturing long-range feature dependencies, and Ridge regression for robust final prediction. Using 234 wells from the ChangNing region with 24 geological, engineering, and operational features, we benchmarked LMTR against 11 conventional machine learning models under a consistent five-fold cross-validation and leave-one-out validation protocol.

LMTR consistently outperformed all baselines, achieving an R² of 0.86 and an MSE of 0.0145—improvements of approximately 5–10% in R² over the next best models (Ridge and Lasso). Leave-one-out validation showed that > 82% of wells had prediction errors below 20%, demonstrating strong generalization. Feature importance and SHAP analyses identified the effective fracture seepage area (Acm ​), test production rate, net pay thickness of Layers 1 + 2, and fracturing stage spacing as the four most influential predictors. Notably, Acm ranked first in seven out of the nine high-performance models, underscoring its critical role in EUR determination. Model interpretability, achieved through SHAP and convergence validation, bridges the gap between black-box predictions and engineering trust.

By enabling accurate EUR prediction at the design stage rather than after months of production, the LMTR framework allows operators to optimize fracturing parameters and well placement in real time, reducing capital risk and improving economic returns. The built-in interpretability (SHAP) bridges the gap between black-box machine learning and engineering trust, providing actionable insights for cost-effective hydraulic fracturing design. Furthermore, the integration with net present value (NPV) analysis directly links geological and engineering drivers to economic viability, supporting large-scale, data-driven shale gas development. This work offers a transparent, generalizable, and field-ready tool for EUR estimation that can be extended to other unconventional reservoirs.
