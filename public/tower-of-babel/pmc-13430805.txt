AWhen screams go unheard: covert violence, women victims, and social denial
Authors: Zahra Saedi, Fatemeh Norouzi Aliabad, Elham Taheri, Elham Azmoude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCovert violence—defined as a pattern of subtle, intentionally hidden, and socially camouflaged abusive behaviors that systematically erode a victim’s reality, autonomy, and self-worth—remains a critically understudied dimension of gender-based violence. Distinct from overt psychological aggression, covert violence operates through tactics such as gaslighting, micro-invalidations, coercive control, and economic manipulation, often evading detection while inflicting profound harm. This scoping review systematically maps the evidence on its manifestations, psychosocial impacts, and the mechanisms of institutional and cultural denial that compound women’s victimization.MethodsFollowing Arksey & O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we conducted systematic searches across five databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, CINAHL) for English-language studies published between 1992 and 2025. The search strategy prioritized terms specific to covert violence (e.g., “covert abuse,” “invisible violence,” “subtle psychological aggression”) alongside related concepts. From 1,325 identified records, 43 resources met inclusion criteria after rigorous screening. Data were extracted into a structured table cataloging study characteristics (design, population, abuse type, key findings) and synthesized thematically.ResultCovert violence emerges as a complex and multifaceted phenomenon characterized by systemic psychological, emotional, and economic abuse that deeply undermines women’s autonomy, mental health, and social functioning. Key manifestations include gaslighting, coercive control, financial restriction, and relational manipulation, which lead to severe mental health outcomes—such as anxiety, depression, and PTSD—as well as physical health deterioration and social isolation. Despite its profound impact, covert violence remains largely invisible and underrecognized due to social denial, cultural minimization, and institutional shortcomings. Barriers to disclosure and support include the absence of physical evidence, fear of retaliation, stigma, and fragmented service systems. Importantly, covert violence frequently intersects with and precedes overt physical abuse, underscoring its role in sustaining cycles of control. Addressing covert violence requires urgent legal reforms to recognize non-physical abuse, trauma-informed training for professionals, integrated support services, and public health initiatives aimed at shifting societal attitudes.ConclusionsOur analysis reveals covert violence as a widespread and deeply embedded issue sustained by structural indifference and epistemic injustice. To effectively address this problem, multi-level interventions are needed, including the legal recognition of non-physical abuse, specialized training for frontline responders, and public campaigns that challenge the normalization of covert harm. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs, cross-cultural comparisons, and the evaluation of integrated intervention models, with particular attention to marginalized populations.Keywords: Covert violence, Psychological abuse, Coercive control, Gaslighting, Institutional betrayal, Social denial, Scoping review, Gender-based violence

Introduction

Violence against women is a complex and multifaceted global issue with severe individual and societal repercussions, recognized by international bodies as a persistent public health and human rights challenge [1, 2]. While visible forms of violence—such as physical and sexual abuse—have received substantial attention in research, policy, and public discourse, covert violence remains largely hidden, conceptually underdeveloped, and critically under-explored [3, 4]. This review conceptualizes covert violence as a distinct pattern of subtle, intentionally hidden, and socially camouflaged abusive behaviors that systematically erode a victim’s reality, autonomy, and social standing. It is characterized by its invisibility, cumulative nature, and reliance on tactics that distort perception and entrap victims within seemingly ordinary relational dynamics. While overlapping with broader constructs such as psychological abuse and coercive control, covert violence is distinguished by its emphasis on behaviors that are deliberately concealed, lack visible markers, and are often normalized within cultural and relational contexts, making them exceptionally difficult for victims to identify and for systems to validate [5, 6].

The insidious nature of covert violence presents significant challenges for both recognition and response. Many women struggle to identify the abuse due to the gradual, normalized, and often culturally embedded nature of these behaviors. Emotional dependency, financial control, fear of social stigma, and internalized blame further delay recognition and disclosure [7, 8]. As a result, survivors frequently suffer in silence, experiencing long-term psychological consequences such as chronic anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, and deep erosion of self-worth—often without being able to pinpoint the source of their distress [7, 9].

When women do recognize the abuse and seek help, their experiences are frequently met with denial, minimization, or outright disbelief—a phenomenon known as secondary victimization. This is particularly common in contexts where the perpetrator maintains a socially respectable image, where physical evidence is lacking, and where patriarchal norms assign women the responsibility for maintaining relationship harmony [10–13]. These dynamics are not confined to a single region; they appear across diverse cultural and geographic contexts, suggesting a global pattern of silencing and invalidation that deepens victims’ isolation and obstructs access to support services [14].

Thus, women experiencing covert violence face a dual burden: difficulty in recognizing the abuse, and social and institutional failure to acknowledge their suffering. This interplay traps victims in cycles of emotional harm and disempowerment, while also obscuring the true scale of covert violence in society. Without adequate recognition, such forms of abuse become normalized, reinforcing patriarchal power dynamics and limiting efforts at prevention and intervention [13, 15, 16].

Despite growing awareness, and recent efforts to scope related concepts [5], a comprehensive interdisciplinary synthesis that specifically examines the full spectrum of covert violence, its distinguishing features, the mechanisms of social denial that sustain it, and its intersection with more overt forms of harm, is notably absent. The novelty and contribution of this review lie in its explicit focus on the covert dimension of non-physical violence, integrating insights from psychology, sociology, law, and public health to disentangle this specific form of harm from the broader umbrella of psychological abuse. By bringing together and critically appraising global evidence, this study aims to sharpen the conceptual understanding of covert violence, illuminate the systemic mechanisms that render it invisible, and contribute to the development of more sensitive diagnostic tools, culturally informed interventions, and robust legal and policy frameworks that reflect the lived realities of diverse women worldwide.

Ultimately, this review seeks to advance scholarly and practical understanding, promote survivor-centered responses, and inform the work of researchers, practitioners, and policymakers committed to naming, recognizing, and tackling the invisible yet devastating impact of covert violence.

Methods

This scoping review explored and mapped the existing literature on the distinct phenomenon of covert violence, its multifaceted impacts on women victims, and the role of social denial in perpetuating these harms. Our methodology was guided by the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005) [17], with enhancements from Levac et al. (2010) [18]. We reported this review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [19].

Identifying the research question

Aligned with the defined focus on covert violence as a hidden and socially denied form of abuse, the primary research question guiding this review was refined to:

“How is covert violence conceptualized and manifested in the existing literature, what are its impacts on women, and how do mechanisms of social denial exacerbate victimization and hinder intervention?”

Identifying relevant studies: search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of electronic databases for peer-reviewed literature published between 1992 and 2025. The start date (1992) was selected to capture literature following the publication of Judith Herman’s seminal work on psychological trauma, which provided a foundational understanding of invisible, cumulative harm relevant to covert violence [20]. Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and CINAHL. Our search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms), refining our focus based on the core construct. The strategy centered on terms directly describing covert or hidden abuse, while also encompassing related but broader constructs to ensure sensitivity:

Although no language restrictions were initially applied to maximize retrieval, the final selection was limited to English-language publications due to resource constraints.

Study selection

All citations retrieved (n = 1325) were imported into EndNote for reference management. After removing 375 duplicates, 950 unique records underwent screening. Two reviewers (Z.S. and F.N.) independently screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria, excluding 860 records. Ninety full texts were assessed independently by the same reviewers for inclusion. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or, if necessary, by a third reviewer (E.A.) to reach consensus. Ultimately, 43 resources met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results (synthesis)

Following scoping review methodology [17, 18], no formal quality appraisal or risk of bias assessment was conducted, as the aim was to map the extent, range, and nature of the evidence rather than synthesize effect sizes. The extracted data were first organized narratively to describe the characteristics of the included resources. A thematic synthesis was then conducted to identify, analyze, and report key patterns (themes) across the dataset related to: (1) the conceptualization and manifestations of covert violence, (2) its impacts, (3) the mechanisms and consequences of social denial, and (4) identified gaps. This synthesis aimed to distinguish findings particularly salient to the covert nature of the violence where possible. Results, research gaps, and recommendations are presented in the subsequent sections.

Among the 43 included resources, 10 were empirical qualitative studies, 7 were empirical quantitative studies, 4 employed mixed-methods approaches, and 9 were conceptual or theoretical analyses (including books and book chapters). Additionally, 8 resources were systematic or evidence reviews, and 10 focused on legal, advocacy, or policy frameworks. The inclusion of non-empirical resources (theoretical works, books, systematic reviews) aligns with scoping review methodology, which seeks to map the full spectrum of evidence and key conceptual contributions in a field.

The qualitative studies primarily used in-depth interviews and focus groups to explore women’s lived experiences of covert violence and social denial. Quantitative studies often utilized surveys and standardized instruments to assess the prevalence and psychological impacts. Mixed-methods studies combined both approaches for a more holistic understanding.

Theoretical and conceptual works helped define covert violence, develop sociocultural frameworks, and identify research gaps. Defining the covert: conceptual distinctions and core manifestations

The literature reveals an evolving but fragmented conceptual landscape. Covert violence is most consistently characterized not by a single behavior, but by a pattern of subtle, hidden, and often normalized actions aimed at destabilizing the victim’s reality and autonomy [21, 22]. A key distinction emerging from the synthesis is the emphasis on invisibility—both in terms of the abuser’s intent to conceal and the social camouflage these behaviors employ [5]. Unlike overt physical aggression, it unfolds through emotional manipulation, psychological degradation, and social or financial control. Victims often characterize this abuse as cumulative and erosive, akin to “a slow death of the self” [20].

While psychological abuse is a core component, the covert dimension is specifically highlighted through tactics like gaslighting, which systematically distorts a victim’s perception of reality [22, 23], and micro-invalidations—subtle verbal or nonverbal communications that negate a person’s thoughts or feelings [8]. The concept of coercive control is central, shifting focus from isolated incidents to ongoing patterns of domination, surveillance, and rule-setting that create a climate of entrapment [24, 25]. This control extends to economic abuse, which strategically limits financial autonomy to foster dependency [26].

Crucially, several resources [27, 28] underscore that the impact of sustained covert psychological abuse can be as traumatic as physical violence, challenging hierarchies of harm that privilege physical evidence. However, legal frameworks often struggle to recognize these non-physical, patterned behaviors as distinct criminal acts [29].

Prevalence and the dynamics of under-reporting: a hidden epidemic

Research highlights the widespread nature of covert violence against women, though its precise prevalence is obscured by its very definition—its hidden nature [1]. Evidence suggests it is potentially more pervasive than overt physical or sexual abuse, functioning as a “hidden iceberg” beneath official statistics. Traditional surveys often fail to capture the nuanced, patterned behaviors of coercive control, leading to significant underestimation [2, 30].

A primary theme across studies is the complex interplay of factors that maintain this invisibility. Internal barriers include the victim’s own difficulty in labeling subtle, normalized behaviors as abuse, often attributing them to relationship problems or personal failings [31]. External barriers are formidable: fear of retaliation, financial/emotional dependency, and the profound fear of not being believed—a fear often validated by experiences of secondary victimization. Cultural norms framing violence as a private matter and fragmented support systems further silence victims and hinder disclosure [59].

The interplay of covert and overt violence

An important finding synthesized from the literature is that covert violence is rarely an isolated phenomenon. Multiple studies indicate it frequently coexists with, precedes, or follows overt physical violence [27, 28]. Covert tactics like gaslighting and isolation can establish control and erode a victim’s support network, making subsequent physical abuse possible and escape more difficult. Conversely, the threat of past or potential physical violence can give coercive control its power. This interplay suggests that covert violence is a core component of a broader strategy of domination, rather than merely a “less severe” form of abuse.

Invisible wounds: compound impacts on health and agency

Covert violence exerts severe, compounding effects. Psychologically, it is strongly linked to depression, anxiety, PTSD, and a profound erosion of self-esteem and self-trust [28, 32]. The chronic, reality-distorting nature of this abuse can lead to complex psychological presentations that are often misdiagnosed [33].

This sustained psychological stress manifests somatically, contributing to sleep disturbances, chronic pain, gastrointestinal issues, and dysregulated immune and stress-response systems, increasing long-term risk for chronic diseases [34–36].

Socially and functionally, the impact is crippling. Abuser-imposed isolation or self-imposed withdrawal due to shame sever critical support networks [37]. The psychological toll and an abuser’s active interference often impair occupational and educational functioning, leading to job loss, economic dependence, and financial entrapment—effects that persist long after leaving the abusive relationship [38, 39].

Systemic amplification: barriers rooted in social and institutional denial

Victims face a multilayered system of denial that amplifies their harm. At the institutional level, a consistent finding is the lack of training and appropriate tools for professionals (healthcare, legal, social work) to identify non-physical, patterned abuse [39]. Legal systems, built on evidence of discrete incidents, struggle with the “proof” of covert control, frequently dismissing cases and perpetuating a gap between lived experience and legal remedy [29, 39].

This institutional inadequacy is underpinned by deeper societal denial. Patriarchal norms that prioritize family privacy, distrust women’s accounts, and trivialize emotional harm create a culture where covert violence is minimized or excused [23, 41]. Victim-blaming narratives shift responsibility from perpetrator to survivor, further silencing disclosure. This systemic failure—from frontline services to cultural discourse—constitutes a form of epistemic injustice, where women’s knowledge of their own abuse is systematically invalidated [6].

Horizons ahead: critical gaps and differentiated directions

The synthesis reveals persistent gaps, particularly concerning the mechanisms of social denial and their variation across cultural and social contexts [42]. There is a pronounced need for longitudinal research to track the long-term trajectory of recovery and the lasting socioeconomic impacts of covert violence [43]. Comparative studies across diverse cultural, legal, and socio-economic settings are essential to move beyond Western-centric models and understand context-specific manifestations and barriers [44].

Methodologically, the field is hampered by a lack of validated, culturally sensitive measurement tools designed specifically to detect covert, patterned behaviors rather than general psychological aggression.

Finally, there is a stark paucity of research on evaluated interventions. Evidence is urgently needed on the effectiveness of trauma-informed, multi-sectoral support models, legal advocacy strategies for non-physical abuse, and prevention programs that target the normalization of covert controlling behaviors [45].

Discussion

This scoping review systematically mapped the literature to clarify the distinct nature, impacts, and social context of covert violence against women. In doing so, it addresses a critical gap: while psychological abuse and coercive control have been reviewed, no prior synthesis has explicitly focused on the covert dimension—the hidden, reality-distorting, and socially camouflaged tactics that define this form of harm—and its intricate relationship with mechanisms of social denial [5, 20]. Our findings reveal that covert violence is not merely a subset of psychological abuse but a patterned strategy of domination characterized by its invisibility and the systemic invalidation that surrounds it.

A central contribution of this review is the clarification of covert violence as a concept distinguished by perpetrator intent (concealment) and social camouflage, which differentiates it from overt psychological aggression. This conceptual sharpening, building upon frameworks like coercive control [25] and recent definitions of covert abuse [5], helps explain why it evades detection by victims and systems alike. The behaviors are repetitive and cumulative, designed to erode autonomy and self-trust gradually. Gaslighting and micro-invalidations exemplify this, directly attacking a victim’s epistemic confidence [8, 21, 23].

The evidence underscores that the covert nature of this violence creates a unique epidemiology. Its prevalence is inherently obscured, existing as a “hidden iceberg” [2, 31]. Barriers to disclosure are compounded by the abuse itself: victims may not recognize normalized, non-physical behaviors as abusive, and when they do, they confront a high likelihood of not being believed [31]. This cycle of invisibility has direct implications, leading to a significant underestimation in public health data and a misallocation of resources.

Critically, our synthesis confirms that covert violence is deeply interwoven with overt physical harm [27, 58]. It often establishes the groundwork for physical abuse through isolation and destabilization, or persists afterward to maintain control. This interplay challenges binary classifications of violence and underscores that risk assessment must account for non-physical, controlling patterns. The impacts are severe and compound across domains: profound psychological trauma (anxiety, depression, complex PTSD) [28, 32], somatic health consequences [34, 35], social isolation, and economic entrapment that hinders escape and recovery [38, 39].

Perhaps the most salient finding is the systemic nature of the barriers victims face, which can be framed as a continuum of epistemic injustice. At the institutional level, professionals lack the training and legal tools to “see” and validate evidence of patterned, non-physical control [29, 40]. This institutional failure is rooted in and reinforced by societal denial—patriarchal norms that privatize abuse, victim-blaming narratives, and the cultural trivialization of emotional harm [30, 41]. Together, they form a powerful mechanism that protects perpetrators and silences survivors, transforming personal suffering into a publicly negated experience [6, 59].

Implications for policy, practice, and future research

The findings demand concrete, multi-level responses:

For Policy & Law: Urgent legal reform is needed to recognize patterned coercive control as a standalone offense, moving beyond an incident-based model. Laws must accommodate evidence of psychological manipulation, digital surveillance, and economic control. Policies should mandate the integration of covert violence risk assessment in protection order proceedings and child custody evaluations.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should inform the interpretation of its findings. Firstly, the restriction to English-language publications may have excluded valuable perspectives and evidence from non-English speaking contexts, potentially introducing a geographic and cultural bias. Secondly, while the search strategy aimed for sensitivity, the conceptual fragmentation in the field means some relevant studies may not have been captured by our selected terms. Thirdly, by including diverse resource types (theoretical, legal) alongside empirical studies to map the conceptual field, the synthesis integrates findings of varying epistemological standing; this is a strength of the scoping methodology but means the conclusions are not based solely on hierarchical empirical evidence. Finally, as per scoping review guidelines, we did not conduct a formal quality appraisal of included studies. The variations in methodological rigor across the included literature should be considered when weighing the evidence presented.

Conclusion

Covert violence constitutes a pervasive and insidious form of gender-based abuse that systematically undermines women’s autonomy, mental health, and social integrity. Characterized by hidden patterns of psychological manipulation, coercive control, and reality distortion, it thrives in the space created by social and institutional denial.

This review contributes to the field by offering the first integrated synthesis that explicitly ties the defining features of covert violence to the mechanisms of epistemic injustice that sustain it. The findings unequivocally demonstrate that covert violence is not a lesser form of abuse, but a core component of patriarchal control with severe public health and human rights implications.

Addressing this issue requires concerted systemic change. This includes reforming legal frameworks to recognize non-physical coercion, equipping all frontline professionals with trauma-informed, gender-sensitive tools, funding culturally attuned support services, and launching public campaigns to dismantle the norms that trivialize psychological harm. Research must continue to illuminate the pathways of abuse and recovery, with a committed focus on marginalized voices.

Ultimately, breaking the silence around covert violence means transforming the social and institutional conditions that currently allow it to remain unheard. It demands a collective shift from denial to recognition, from invalidation to belief, and from isolation to empowered support.
