Evaluating clinical associations of immediate post-traumatic headache: a prospective multicenter study
Authors: Mansoureh Togha, Maryam Bahreini, Sepideh Aarabi, Elham Jafari, Soroor Advani, Nahid Beladi-Moghadam, Narges Yazdi, Muhammad Hussain Ahmadvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-traumatic headache (PTH) is a frequent early consequence of head traumatic injury, yet emergency departments (EDs) lack practical tools to identify high-risk patients. Whether routinely documented trauma characteristics are associated with immediate PTH beyond established clinical factors remains uncertain.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter observational study evaluated patients aged ≥ 18 years presenting with head and/or neck trauma to the ED between 2022 and 2025. To adhere strictly to ICHD-3 criteria and prevent severity measurement bias, the primary analysis was restricted exclusively to a homogeneous cohort of 495 patients with mild traumatic head injury (GCS 13–15, no traumatic brain CT abnormalities). The primary outcome was immediate PTH occurring directly following the traumatic event. To prevent overfitting, candidate variables were restricted a priori to 9 core features. We utilized multivariable logistic regression and evaluated internal validity via 500 bootstrap resamples.ResultsImmediate PTH occurred in 62.8% of patients with mild head trauma. Prior headache history showed the strongest association with PTH. Several trauma-related characteristics, including posterior, frontal, and temporal impact locations, scalp hematoma, and traffic accident mechanism, were independently associated with higher odds of PTH. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) confirmed no significant multicollinearity (< 1.85). The parsimonious 9-variable model demonstrated robust discrimination and stability, with an apparent AUC of 0.855 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.843.ConclusionsAn association model based on 9 routine ED variables identified several clinical and specific trauma features strongly associated with immediate PTH in mild head trauma patients. Introduction

Post-traumatic headache (PTH) is defined as a headache developing within 7 days after head trauma or after regaining consciousness, with acute PTHs lasting less than 3 months and persistent (chronic) PTHs continuing for more than 3 months after the injury. PTH affects approximately 49.3% (95% CI: 44.7–53.9%) of patients following traumatic head injury, with prevalence reaching 60.4% in acute mild head trauma cohorts at two weeks post-injury [1, 2]. Among patients with acute PTH, 52.4% report persistent symptoms at three months, representing a critical transition to chronic disability [2]. This high prevalence and chronification rate necessitate accurate risk stratification at initial emergency department (ED) presentation to identify patients requiring intensive early intervention. Current ED management prioritizes identification of structural pathology requiring neurosurgical intervention, with limited capacity to anticipate subsequent functional outcomes such as PTH [3].

Prior research has identified several demographic and clinical risk factors for PTH, including female sex, prior headache history, and presence of acute symptoms such as photophobia and difficulty concentrating [4, 5]. However, association models integrating these factors to enable bedside risk stratification remain limited. Existing models have focused primarily on demographic variables, acute symptom burden, and psychological factors, achieving modest discrimination [4–6]. These models lack external validation and have not systematically incorporated trauma-specific anatomical characteristics routinely documented during ED evaluation. During ED assessment, detailed information about trauma characteristics is routinely documented, including mechanism of injury, site of impact, and presence of visible tissue injury. Whether these injury-specific anatomical variables contribute independent prognostic information for PTH beyond established demographic and associated factors remains unclear [7, 8].

We conducted a prospective, multicenter study to identify associated factors of immediate PTH among patients presenting to the ED following head trauma. We developed a multivariable model incorporating patient demographics, injury characteristics, and acute clinical findings to assess their relative contribution to immediate PTH risk.

Methods

This prospective, multicenter study enrolled 574 adult patients aged ≥ 18 years presenting to emergency departments with mild-to-moderate head and neck trauma between 2022 and 2025.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (Specialized Committee for Research Ethics in Biomedical Sciences) of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Neuroscience Research Institute “Neural Rehabilitation” (Reference: IR.TUMS.NI.REC.1401.065). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participants were informed of the study objectives, procedures, data collection methods, and their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their clinical care.

Trained personnel systematically collected demographic data (age, sex), trauma characteristics (mechanism, anatomical location, laterality), clinical findings (Glasgow Coma Scale, skull base fracture signs), associated injuries (limb, facial, trunk, scalp hematoma, visible soft tissue injury, multiple injuries), imaging results (brain and neck computed tomography abnormalities), and prior headache history. Specific prior headache phenotypes (e.g., migraine vs. tension-type) were not routinely documented in the acute ED setting. Headache characteristics, prior headache history, and acute symptom burden were assessed using a structured clinical questionnaire custom-designed by the study investigators to align with the diagnostic criteria for Post-Traumatic Headache outlined in the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3). To prevent subjective reporting bias and align with the general ICHD-3 criteria for acute PTH (Code 5.1), the primary analysis was restricted exclusively to an ambulatory cohort presenting with blunt head trauma, a GCS of 13–15, and normal brain CT scans, entirely excluding moderate/severe cases. To adhere strictly to ICHD-3 terminology, we refer to this cohort inclusively as having ‘mild head trauma’, encompassing both mild HEAD TRAUMA and isolated extracranial head trauma. Consistent with ICHD-3 general rules for attributing secondary headaches, the primary outcome was immediate PTH, strictly defined as either a newly occurring headache following trauma or a significantly worsened pre-existing primary headache (≥ 2-fold increase in severity or frequency) occurring directly at emergency department evaluation.

Statistical Analysis Baseline characteristics stratified by PTH status were compared using Mann-Whitney U and chi-square tests. Missing data (age 1.7%, sex 0.9%, laterality 8.5%) were handled via multiple imputation by chained equations (20 datasets). To prevent overfitting, multivariable logistic regression was restricted a priori to exactly 9 core variables, all retained in the final model. We evaluated interactions and ruled out multicollinearity via the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF < 2). Discrimination was assessed via Area Under the Curve (AUC). To definitively evaluate overfitting and ensure methodological consistency, all internal validation and calibration analyses were performed uniformly utilizing 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Discrimination was assessed via Area Under the Curve (AUC), yielding an optimism-corrected AUC. Furthermore, model calibration was assessed visually using a calibration plot generated from these same 1,000 bootstrap resamples to ensure agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies. A secondary negative binomial regression assessed the correlation between immediate PTH and Month 1 symptom burden (headache days), adjusting for age and sex. Analyses were performed in R v4.x (p < 0.05).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

The study evaluated 495 adult patients presenting to the ED following traumatic head injury between 2022 and 2025. Immediate post-traumatic headache (PTH) was present in 311 patients (62.8%). The median age was 39 years (interquartile range [IQR] 29–57), and 353 patients (71.3%) were male.

Baseline characteristics stratified by PTH status are presented in Table 1. Compared to patients without PTH, those with immediate PTH were more likely to have sustained traffic accidents (63% vs. 47%, p < 0.001), experienced frontal trauma (38% vs. 24%, p = 0.003), presented with scalp hematoma (21% vs. 3%, p < 0.001), had visible soft tissue injury (47% vs. 17%, p < 0.001), sustained multiple concurrent injuries (43% vs. 18%, p < 0.001), and reported a history of prior headache (58% vs. 5%, p < 0.001).

Univariate analysis

Univariate logistic regression identified several variables significantly associated with immediate PTH (p < 0.05; complete results in Supplementary Table S1). The strongest univariate association was any prior headache history (odds ratio [OR] 26.4, 95% confidence interval [CI] 13.7–57.3, p < 0.001). Other strong univariate associations included scalp hematoma (OR 9.3, 95% CI 4.0-26.9, p < 0.001), visible soft tissue injury (OR 4.1, 95% CI 2.7–6.5, p < 0.001), facial laceration (OR 3.7, 95% CI 2.2–6.3, p < 0.001), multiple trauma (OR 3.4, 95% CI 2.2–5.3, p < 0.001), and facial trauma (OR 3.4, 95% CI 2.2–5.4, p < 0.001).

Multivariable analysis

In the multivariable analysis (Fig. 1), all VIF values were < 1.85, confirming no significant multicollinearity among the 9 candidate variables. Prior headache history emerged as the strongest independent factor of immediate PTH (adjusted OR 25.10, 95% CI 12.54–56.45, p < 0.001).

Multivariable analysis forest plot

Trauma location and injury characteristics demonstrated significant independent associations. Scalp hematoma was strongly associated with PTH (adjusted OR 4.04, 95% CI 1.46–13.38, p = 0.012). Regarding trauma location, posterior region trauma conferred 2.3-fold increased odds (adjusted OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.32–4.12, p = 0.004), temporal region 2.2-fold (adjusted OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.09–4.52, p = 0.031), and frontal region 2.1-fold (adjusted OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.19–3.78, p = 0.012). Traffic accident mechanism was associated with 2.3-fold increased odds (adjusted OR 2.31, 95% CI 1.28–4.25, p = 0.006). Increasing age demonstrated a minor protective effect (adjusted OR 0.98 per year, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p = 0.006).

The multivariable model demonstrated good discrimination (apparent AUC 0.855, 95% CI: 0.82–0.89). Internal validation via 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.843, confirming robust stability with virtually no overfitting. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate calibration (chi-square 5.61, degrees of freedom 8, p = 0.69), which was further corroborated by bootstrap-based calibration evaluation showing agreement between predicted and observed PTH frequencies (mean absolute error = 0.011; Figure S1).

Sub-acute symptom burden (month 1 Follow-up)

Of the mild HEAD TRAUMA cohort, 265 patients completed the Month 1 follow-up assessment. The presence of immediate PTH in the ED was correlated with a higher acute symptom burden over the subsequent 30 days. Patients without immediate PTH reported an average of 0.34 headache days in the first month, whereas patients with immediate PTH averaged 1.57 headache days. Negative binomial regression adjusting for age and sex indicates that immediate PTH at ED triage is a significant prognostic associate of sub-acute burden, increasing the incidence rate of headache days during the first month by over 5-fold (Incidence Rate Ratio 5.76, 95% CI 1.84–17.22, p = 0.001).

Discussion

Post-traumatic headache affects nearly half of HEAD trauma patients, with over 50% progressing to persistent symptoms and chronic disability [1, 2]. Despite this burden, emergency physicians lack validated tools to identify high-risk patients at initial presentation. Existing models require lengthy psychological questionnaires, impractical for acute settings or advanced imaging unavailable during ED evaluation [4, 5]. We developed a multivariable model using only routine ED variables to address this gap.

In 495 prospectively enrolled adult HEAD trauma patients, a history of prior headache appeared to be the dominant clinical correlate, demonstrating a 25-fold increased odds of immediate PTH. Beyond this established factor, we identified trauma-specific variables that might increase risk: posterior region trauma was associated with 2.3-fold increased odds, temporal 2.2-fold, and frontal 2.1-fold. Scalp hematoma also demonstrated a strong and significant association with PTH. The model achieved an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.84, exceeding prior ED-applicable tools (AUC 0.79) and matching psychosocial questionnaire models (AUC 0.77) without specialized assessments [4, 5, 7, 9].

The strong association between prior headache and immediate PTH confirms existing literature demonstrating a history of migraine and headache as robust risk factors [7, 9]. Our findings align closely with the recent TRACK-TBI cohort study, which reported a similarly high prevalence of acute PTH (60%) following mild head trauma. Consistent with their results, we observed that prior headache history is a major risk factor for immediate PTH, whereas increasing age exerts a protective effect [2]. The independent associated value of specific trauma location patterns represents findings not previously examined in multivariable models [7, 8]. Posterior trauma demonstrated the strongest spatial association, which may reflect cervicogenic mechanisms where occipital impacts strain cervical structures, triggering referred pain via C1-C3 convergence with the trigeminal nucleus [7, 8]. Frontal and temporal trauma may involve periosteal nociceptor activation in richly innervated regions, thereby supporting peripheral neurogenic inflammation mechanisms in which tissue damage initiates nociceptor sensitization [7, 9].

Since our cohort exclusively comprised patients with normal brain CT, the independent effect of scalp hematoma in the absence of intracranial pathology warrants further exploration. While extensively validated as an intracranial hemorrhage triage indicator, this variable has not been previously evaluated as an isolated PTH-associated factor in multivariable models [10, 11]. The observed association (adjusted OR 4.04) might suggest that hematoma could signal substantial local tissue trauma, potentially activating peripheral pain pathways. Alternatively, it may serve as a surrogate marker of impact force severity that is not fully captured by standard structural imaging.

A deliberate methodological design of our study was restricting the multivariable analysis exclusively to mild HEAD TRAUMA, thereby intentionally excluding moderate and severe cases. The defining characteristic of severe traumatic head injury is altered mental status, preventing accurate self-reporting [11]. Trauma triage protocols prioritize life-saving interventions over subjective symptom assessment, and necessary sedation/intubation pharmacologically suppresses nociceptive awareness [12]. This creates systematic under-recognition where severity markers paradoxically associate with reduced symptom reporting, a phenomenon documented in trauma literature showing inverse associations between injury severity and patient-reported outcomes due to selection bias and competing clinical priorities [13, 14].

Clinical management of mild HEAD trauma might benefit from routine evaluation of prior headache history and anatomical trauma characteristics. Recognizing patients with these associated factors in the ED could facilitate timely decisions, such as the administration of mechanism-appropriate multimodal analgesia, which might help address the acute symptom burden we observed [7, 9]. A three-tier approach for acute symptom management could be considered for mild HEAD TRAUMA: patients with fewer associated factors (e.g., no prior headache, diffuse trauma) might be suitable for standard 7–14 day follow-up; those with moderate associations (e.g., one relevant factor) may benefit from enhanced counseling regarding symptom expectations and a closer 48–72 h follow-up; whereas those with multiple associated factors (e.g., prior headache combined with specific focal trauma) might warrant prompt primary care or ED follow-up within 48 h to manage acute pain pathways and potentially mitigate sub-acute exacerbations. Importantly, this framework requires external validation prior to clinical application (Fig. 2).

Observed incidence of immediate post-traumatic headache

Future research would benefit from externally validating this 9-variable association model in independent community cohorts. Longitudinal studies should explore whether these acute associated factors also correlate with persistent symptoms, as the transition from immediate to sub-acute PTH may involve distinct mechanisms [7, 9]. Additionally, since we deliberately excluded moderate-to-severe cases to prevent reporting bias, future investigations are needed to separately evaluate the impact of structural intracranial abnormalities. Finally, prospective studies could establish the real-world clinical utility of these risk profiles.

Limitations

This prospective multicenter study utilized 9 routine ED variables to achieve strong discrimination and internal stability (optimism-corrected AUC 0.84) for identifying immediate PTH associations. Study limitations include its regional scope, tertiary center recruitment, cross-sectional primary outcome assessment, and the requirement for external validation before widespread clinical implementation. Additionally, without systematic documentation of acute amnesia or loss of consciousness, we could not confirm that all patients strictly met ICHD-3 criteria for mild head trauma (Code 5.1). Therefore, our cohort likely includes isolated extracranial head trauma (Code 5.1). Furthermore, because the study was strictly limited to this mild head trauma cohort to ensure reliable symptom reporting, these findings cannot be generalized to moderate or severe head trauma populations with structural brain damage. At the one-month follow-up, 46.5% of the mild head trauma cohort was lost to attrition; however, missingness analyses revealed no significant differences in baseline demographics between retained and lost participants (Age: p = 0.983; Sex: p = 0.680), suggesting data were missing completely at random (MCAR). Despite these constraints, this parsimonious model successfully identified independent associated factors, providing a practical risk stratification framework applicable at the initial presentation of mild head trauma.

Conclusion

This study suggests that among patients with mild traumatic head injury, several routine ED findings, especially prior headache history, focal trauma locations, and scalp hematoma, might provide clinically meaningful information for assessing the likelihood of immediate post-traumatic headache at presentation. Recognizing these associated factors may help clinicians identify patients who might benefit from closer follow-up or targeted early counseling, without requiring additional tests or prolonged assessments. Further external validation is necessary before this associative risk stratification approach can be incorporated into standard ED care.
