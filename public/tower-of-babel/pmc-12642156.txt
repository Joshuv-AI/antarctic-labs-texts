The relationship between social support, psychological distress, and patient activation in peritoneal dialysis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yulin Xu, Yu Chen, Qin Xu, Xiaoqin Liu, Haitong Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo understand the current status and influencing factors of patient activation among peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, and to explore whether social support indirectly influences patient activation through psychological distress.MethodThis study adopted a cross-sectional research design and enrolled 232 PD patients in a peritoneal dialysis center of hospital between March and June 2025. The research tools included a demographic characteristics questionnaire, the perceived social support scale, the Kessler psychological distress scale and the patient activation measure-13 item.ResultsPatient activation was positively correlated with social support (r = 0.68, P < 0.01) and negatively correlated with psychological distress (r = -0.55, P < 0.01). Psychological distress mediated the relationship between social support and patient activation in PD patients, accounting for 16.28% of the total effect. Monthly household income, complications, social support and psychological distress were important factors influencing patient activation in PD patients. Age, gender, monthly household income, complications, psychological distress and social support explained 46.6% of patient activation.ConclusionsThis study identified the factors influencing activation in PD patients and revealed that social support has a reinforcing effect on patient activation by alleviating psychological distress. Based on these, an evidence-based intervention programs integrating social support and psychological distress should be developed to provide a theoretical basis and practical approach for optimizing the comprehensive management of PD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Patient activation, Self-management, Peritoneal dialysis, Influencing factors, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) refers to the pathological state where all types of chronic kidney disease (CKD) have progressed to the end stage, resulting in irreversible renal failure [1]. The global incidence of ESRD continues to rise [1]. Hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD) are the most common treatment for ESRD [2]. Among them, PD is widely recommended as the treatment of choice for ESRD due to the advantages of hemodynamic stability, protection of residual renal function, convenience and lower treatment cost [3]. There are significant differences between HD and PD, such as the principle of dialysis and the mode of operation [4]. HD treatment is mainly carried out by nurses in hospitals. PD patients are required to complete daily operations (e.g., setting up a sterile environment, regulating the temperature of dialysate, connecting catheters) at home by themselves or family members, and any failure in any part of the process may lead to serious complications (e.g., peritonitis) [5]. Although assisted peritoneal dialysis programs administered by nurses or healthcare workers are feasible in other countries [6]. In China, PD is still primarily performed by patients or their family members [7, 8]. Only in a very small number of regions do healthcare workers provide home-based care services. PD patients need to continuously monitor fluid balance and accurately calculate the daily ultrafiltration volume. Continuous PD treatment requires patients to strictly control their diet, take medication regularly, and perform catheter care [4]. In the absence of supervision by healthcare workers, self-management skills are particularly crucial for PD patients.

Self-management refers to the ability to control one’s thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in order to achieve personal and professional goals [9]. Poor self-management by PD patients may lead to technical failure of PD, forcing patients to choose HD, thereby increasing their hospitalization time and financial burden [3–5]. Empowering CKD patients to self-manage their health may offer potential benefits in reducing modifiable risk factors and delaying disease progression [9, 10]. Patient activation is an important prerequisite and key driver of self-management [10, 11]. Patient activation refers to the skills, knowledge, and confidence associated with an individual’s willingness and ability to manage their health [12, 13]. Patients with high activation showed more active self-management engagement [10, 14]. Patients with low activation had an increased risk of hospitalization, lower treatment adherence and lower medical satisfaction compared to patients with high activation [10, 14, 15].

Patient education is a significant factor influencing activation levels. Educational investments in CKD are critical for enhancing patient activation and promoting engagement in kidney health [16]. Existing studies also indicate that social support is a social factor that influences patient activation. Marianne et al. found that social support among patients with chronic pain was positively correlated with patient activation [17]. Zhu et al. also found that in elderly patients with coronary heart disease, the higher the level of social support, the higher the level of patient activation [18]. The relationship between social support and psychological distress has been well established. Social support theory suggests that social support can alleviate anxiety, depression, and other negative psychological states when individuals face high levels of stress [19]. In addition, psychological factors may also affect patients’ activation levels [20]. Psychological factors that are significantly positively correlated with patient activation include satisfaction with social roles, hope, and optimism. Psychological factors that are significantly negatively correlated with patient activation include depression or depressive symptoms, anxiety, fatigue, and fear of disease recurrence [20]. Existing studies on the relationship between psychology and patient activation has mainly been conducted among patients with heart disease [21], spinal surgery [22], and cancer [23]. PD patients require long-term PD treatment, and both the procedural complexity and infection risks are higher compared to other chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension and diabetes) [24]. There are few studies exploring the factors that influence the activation in PD patients, making it difficult to develop effective strategies to improve their activation levels [25–27]. Current studies have focused more on the impact of patient activation on outcome measures, with insufficient exploration of the underlying mechanisms of activation [10, 15].

Thus, this study aims to explore factors influencing activation levels in PD patients. At the same time, it investigates the relationship between social support, psychological distress, and patient activation levels, as well as the mediating role of psychological distress. This study will provide theoretical basis and scientific foundation for developing intervention measures for PD patients to improve their activation levels and self-management. For this purpose, we proposed the following hypotheses: (1) Social support is directly associated with patient activation; (2) Psychological distress is directly associated with patient activation; (3) Psychological distress serves as a mediating factor in the relationship between social support and patient activation in PD patients. Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of self-reported data collected from March 2025 to June 2025 at a peritoneal dialysis center in a hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province. This peritoneal dialysis center is the first of its kind in Hubei Province, staffed by specialized physicians, nurses, and nutritionists. The dialysis center maintains a comprehensive follow-up and management system, serving approximately 1,000 patients.

Consecutive convenience sampling was used to select PD patients at peritoneal dialysis centers. Eligible participants (a) patients receiving PD treatment (including dialysis patients with newly placed PD catheter); (b) patients who signed the informed consent and participated voluntarily. Exclusion criteria included (a) combined HD and PD therapy; (b) acute cardiocerebrovascular events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke within 3 months), malignancies, immunocompromised status, or hematologic disorders; (c) cognitive impairment or psychiatric comorbidities; (d) pregnancy or lactation period.

Researchers distributed questionnaires to PD patients who met the inclusion criteria. For any questions or concerns raised by PD patients, explanations and clarifications were provided until the patients fully understood. After completing the questionnaire, researchers verified its completeness and entered the data into the database. Questionnaires with mismatched questions and answers, ambiguous responses, or multiple alterations will be excluded. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [28].

Study size

According to the sample requirements for constructing a structural equation model proposed by Stevens [29, 30], the sample size should be 5 to 20 times the number of items in the model (there are 35 items in this model). Considering 20% invalid questionnaires, the minimum sample size for this study should be 218 PD patients.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical variables

The personal data form was compiled by the researchers after discussion. It included information on age, gender, BMI, educational level, living status, residence, marital status, employment status, monthly household income, smoking and drinking habits, exercise status, comorbidities and dialysis duration. Complications were grouped into “yes” and “no.” The categories of the rest of indicators are depicted in Table 1.

Assessment of psychosocial distress

The Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) was developed by Kessler in the United States [31]. K10 has been tested and applied among Chinese patients with kidney disease [32]. It is an assessment tool used to evaluate an individual’s mental health status over the past month. K10 includes dimensions of anxiety and depression. This scale employs a 5-point Likert scale. Each item is rated on a scale from 1 to 5, representing ‘almost never’ to ‘all the time,’ respectively. The scale consists of 10 items, and the total score is calculated by summing all items. The higher the patient’s score, the more severe their psychological distress. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated as 0.90.

Assessment of social support

The perceived social support scale (PSSS) was developed in 1988 [33] and translated into Chinese in 2001 [34]. The Chinese version of the PSSS has been tested in kidney patients and is psychometrically sound [35, 36]. The PSSS involves three dimensions, namely, family support, friend support, and other support. A 7-point Likert scale was used. Each item is rated on a scale of 1 to 7, ranging from ‘very poorly’ to ‘very well.’ The sum of the scores of each item is the total score of the scale, which ranges from 12 to 84. Higher scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support. The Cronbach’s a coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.76.

Assessment of patient activation

The patient activation-13 items (PAM-13) was developed by Hibbard in 2005 and consists of 13 items [13]. The PAM-13 used in this study is the Chinese version provided by the authors, which has been validated and applied in Chinese CKD populations [37]. The PAM-13 includes four dimensions: (1) the belief that active role is important; (2) the level of confidence and knowledge to take action; (3) the ability to take action, and (4) the ability to stay the course under stress. The scale uses a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree,’ scored from 1 to 4 points. If an item is not applicable, it is scored as 0 points. The score of the 13 items is the raw score, which is then converted to a 100-point positivity score by the original authors based on a point comparison table. The activation level is divided into four stages: stages 1 (score ≤ 47.0), stages 2 (score 47.1 to 55.1), stages 3 (score 55.2 to 67.0) and stages 4 (score ≥ 67.1). The higher the score, the higher the activation level. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated as 0.92.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized SPSS 27.0 for statistical analysis and R 4.5.0 with ggplot2 package for data visualization. Count data were statistically described using frequency (n) and composition ratios (%). The Jarque-Bera test was used to test for normality. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presents as mean ± standard deviation (SD); non-normal variables were reported as median (interquartile range). Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to examine the correlation between the study variables.

Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was employed to identify predictors of patient activation. Include candidate variables with P-values < 0.1 from univariate analysis and variables that were clinically relevant according to reports or clinical experience, into the model. Specifically, age, gender, income, and complications were first included as covariates in the regression model; next, the social support variable was added; finally, the psychological distress variable was incorporated. The main effects of the included variables were assessed by comparing changes in R2 values and F values across the regression models. The severity of multicollinearity in the model is measured by variance inflation factor (VIF). VIF value greater than five was considered to have a collinearity problem.

In addition, mediation analysis was used to examine whether psychological distress (mediator variable) mediated the impact of social support (independent variable) on patient activation (dependent variable). Mediation analysis was conducted using causal stepwise regression testing and bootstrap testing. The SPSS PROCESS macro 4.0 was used to test the mediating effect, and model 4 was selected. Using 5,000 Bootstrap resampling, the direct effect, indirect effect, and total effect were estimated using 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CI). When the 95% CI does not include 0, the effect is considered statistically significant. The sociodemographic correlates were entered as covariates to control the mediation model. All tests were bilateral, and the level of significance was set as a P-value < 0.05.

Results

Summary of characteristics and current status of patient activation in PD patients

A total of 239 questionnaires were distributed. Seven individuals declined to participate and 232 questionnaires were collected, achieving a response rate of 97.07%. Nine questionnaires were excluded due to poor quality and only 223 questionnaires were finally included.

In this study, the PAM-13 score of PD patients was 58.10 (51.00, 75.00) points. Of these, 47.08% of PD patients were in a low activation level (Stages 1 and 2); 22.42% of PD patients had moderate activation levels (Stages 3); 30.49% of PD patients were at high activation levels. Details can be found in Table 1.

Of the 223 PD patients included, the average age was 45.57 ± 17.75 years, the mean BMI score was 21.34 ± 3.68, and the median time on dialysis was 43.00 (36.00, 54.00) months. The majority of PD patients had a middle school and belove education (n = 113, 50.67%). 62.23% (n = 141) of PD patients were female, 12.11% (n = 27) of PD patients lived alone, 54.71% (n = 122) of PD patients resided in countryside, 14.35% (n = 32) were unmarried, and 69.51% (n = 155) were currently unemployed. 14.35% (n = 32) of patients still smoke, 8.97% (n = 20) of PD patients still drink alcohol, and only 44.84% (n = 100) of PD patients exercised regularly. The social support and psychological distress scores for PD patients were 61.90 ± 11.76 points and 19.00 (14.00, 24.00) points, respectively.

The results of the univariate analysis showed that gender, marital status, monthly household income, and complications were all statistically significantly different in terms of social support scores (P < 0.05). Residence and complications were also found to have statistically significant differences in psychological distress scores (P < 0.05). There were statistical differences in patient activation scores with different monthly household income, and complications (P < 0.05). Details are shown in Table 2.

Correlation analysis and regression analysis

The correlation between age, dialysis duration, BMI, social support, psychological distress, and patient activation was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation. The results showed that only the anxiety dimension of psychological distress was associated with dialysis duration (r = 0.22, P < 0.05). There was a significant correlation between social support and psychological distress and patient activation. The higher the level of social support for PD patients, the higher their level of activation (r = 0.68, P < 0.01). The higher the level of social support for PD patients, the lower their psychological distress (r = -0.59, P < 0.01). The psychological distress of PD patients was significantly negatively correlated with their activation levels (r = -0.55, P < 0.01). See Fig. 2 for details.

Mediation analysis

To investigate the mediating effect of psychological distress, causal stepwise regression test and bootstrap test involving 5,000 random samples were performed. In addition, we included statistically significant demographic factors (age, gender, residence, marital status, comorbidities and monthly household income) as control variables in the mediation model.

In stepwise regression test, the results of Analysis I showed that social support had a significant impact on patient activation (β = 0.627, P < 0.01). Analysis II showed that social support was also significantly related to psychological distress (β = -0.520, P < 0.01). In Analysis III, after adding psychological distress, social support (β = 0.525, P < 0.01) and psychological distress (β = -0.196, P < 0.01) also had a significant effect on patient activation, indicating the existence of a mediating effect. Discussion

This study explored the factors influencing patient activation in PD patients and further identified the underlying relationship between social support, psychological distress, and patient activation.

Wide variations in activation levels have been reported in patients with different renal diseases. Lunardi et al. investigated activation levels in patients with CKD stage 5, and showed that 73% of patients had low activation levels (stage 1 and 2) [25]. Low activation levels also occur in HD patients, which is confirmed by Sara’s and Hussein’s study [27, 38]. Central HD patients reported lower activation rates (53% for stage 1 and 2) compared to PD patients (32%), CKD patients (40%) and kidney transplant patients (25%) [39]. The results of this study showed that 47.08% of PD patients were in a low activation state (stages 1 and 2), indicating that they lack awareness and belief in participating in health management. Most PD patients are in a highly activated state, with 22.42% classified as Stage 3 and 30.49% as Stage 4. This may be related to the fact that PD patients in this study must participate in theoretical and practical training and assessments provided by medical staff before initiating home PD. Home PD treatment is only permitted once patients meet the criteria. The activation levels of PD patients vary across different regions and hospitals, which may be influenced by factors such as medical policies, medical resources, remote monitoring systems and mobile health (mHealth) [27]. Regular follow-ups by medical professionals and the use of mHealth have resulted in higher levels of awareness and self-management among PD patients [40, 41].

Demographic factors have a particular impact on patient activation. In this study, monthly household income, complications, psychosocial distress, social support were independent influences on activation levels in PD patients. Several studies have shown that the older the patient, the lower the level of activation [25, 27]. This may be related to the fact that older patients have less willingness in maintaining healthy behaviour or are more adept at accepting paternalistic healthcare [42]. In this study, no significant differences were found between age and patient activation. This may be related to our teaching and assessment of theory and skills for all PD patients. Household income is an important factor influencing patient activation, mainly because economic conditions directly affect patients’ ability to access medical resources, the time cost of participating in health management, health literacy levels, and psychological stress. Household income is a positive predictor of patient activation, which has been confirmed in many studies [26, 43]. Patients with complications face increased treatment difficulties and a heavier overall burden, which can lead to a loss of confidence in treatment and a decline in health-related behaviour [26, 44]. In this study, there was no significant correlation between dialysis duration and patient activation. This finding is consistent with Bulck et al.‘s investigation of activation levels among HD patients [42]. The relationship between PD patient activation levels and dialysis duration may be influenced by multiple complex factors, such as individual differences, treatment adherence, and patient education. Dialysis duration warrants further investigation as a potential factor in future studies.

Social support refers to emotional, practical, and behavioral support provided from multiple sources, such as family members or friends [45]. Social support encompasses both structural support (e.g., the number of people in social networks, the frequency of social interactions, or the diversity of networks) and functional support (e.g., perceived availability of companionship, emotional, or tangible support) [45, 46]. Numerous studies have found that higher levels of structural and functional support may independently exert significant protective effects across various diseases through distinct mechanisms [46, 47]. In this study, the content of PSSS aligns more closely with functional support. This study indicates that lower PSSS scores correlate with lower activation levels in PD patients. This may be related to the lack of emotional support among PD patients, leading to increased loneliness and reduced confidence in treatment. Additionally, for families facing financial hardship, the lack of economic support may lead to reduced motivation for treatment and decreased adherence among PD patients. The relationship between social support and patient activation has also been confirmed across multiple studies involving diverse populations (e.g., chronic pain patients, systemic lupus erythematosus) [17, 48]. Changes in family structure and lack of social support may lead to reduced communication and trigger adverse emotions (e.g., anxiety and depression) [49]. In this study, the worse the patient’s psychological condition, the worse their activation level. A systematic review conducted by Golubinski et al. also demonstrated that psychosocial/psychological factors can explain variations in patient activation [20]. This study also found that psychological distress mediates the relationship between social support and patient activation. Lin et al. also reached similar conclusions, perceived social support among elderly hospitalized patients influences chronic disease self-management through psychological resilience [50]. High-level social support can alleviate patients’ psychological stress, help them build confidence, and make them more inclined to actively participate in health management [51]. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to the mental health of PD patients, alleviate their negative psychological states, thereby improving their activation and enhancing their self-management abilities.

The research conducted by Hussein et al. provides specific recommendations for improving the activation levels of dialysis patients [11]. Identifying high-risk groups is key. Dynamically adjusting the frequency and content of health education based on the PAM scale [11]. There is limited experience with self-management programs in dialysis care. The Stanford Chronic Disease Self-Management Program (CDSMP), a well-established self-management programs, enhances patient self-management through a 6-week course (2.5 h per week), with 10–15 patients forming a support group, and coaches (all chronically ill) collaborating. CDSMP has been shown in several studies to improve self-management, self-efficacy, and adherence in dialysis patients [52]. Social support may serve as a modifiable factor to improve psychological symptoms and activation levels in PD patients. Family members can not only accompany PD patients to medical appointments, provide emotional support and daily care, but also help patients systematically master key points of disease management (including interpreting biochemical indicators, mastering self-care techniques, and preventing complications). The medical team should proactively communicate disease progression, collaboratively develop personalized treatment plans, and identify the patient’s support needs through regular follow-up. However, patients may not be able to attend offline interventions on time due to various factors. Therefore, online-based intervention strategies and programs should be developed. Chao et al. conducted a systematic review on the application of mHealth in PD patients [53]. The results indicate that multifunctional mHealth applications show promising potential in enhancing PD patient efficiency, satisfaction, and effectiveness compared to traditional care [53]. In the future, our interventions for PD patients should be focused on mHealth to enable remote monitoring of PD patients.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. Firstly, the study used a single-center design, limiting its generalizability to larger populations and settings. In addition, the study was a cross-sectional design and was unable to assess temporal effects or potential reverse causality. Second, the small sample size affected the ability to detect statistical relationships. Third, since this study involved voluntary participation, self-selection bias may be present. Patients may have biased responses due to socially desirable reactions. To minimize social desirability bias, this study implemented the following measures: patients were explicitly informed prior to the survey that all data would undergo anonymization; family members were prohibited from participating in the questionnaire completion process at any stage; and all materials were immediately stored confidentially after patients completed the questionnaire. Future studies should validate the robustness of these findings through multicenter, large-sample research.

Conclusion

The relationship between social support and patient activation is partially mediated by psychological distress. Therefore, medical staff at peritoneal dialysis centers must not only assess patients’ physical functioning but also evaluate their social support and psychology, developing appropriate follow-up interventions accordingly. Intervention programs should emphasize strengthening social support networks and providing psychological counseling support (such as cognitive behavioral therapy or group support) to alleviate patients’ psychological distress and enhance their health management capabilities.
