Preoperative patient-reported depression and anxiety may affect short-term surgical outcomes in women with hallux valgus
Authors: Zhihong Nie, Shenglong Ding, Chunlin Zhu, Jiashuo Liu, Lin Zhang, Wei Wang, Mingzhu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative anxiety may influence surgical outcomes, but its specific impact on hallux valgus (HV) surgery remains unclear. This study evaluated the association between preoperative anxiety and postoperative outcomes in female patients who underwent HV correction.MethodsIn this retrospective comparative study, 163 female patients who underwent primary HV correction were included. Patients were categorized into anxiety (Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale [SAS] ≥ 40, n = 48) and nonanxiety (SAS < 40, n = 115) groups. The outcomes included radiographic parameters (hallux valgus angle [HVA], intermetatarsal angle [IMA]), pain (visual analog scale [VAS]), functional outcomes (Foot and Ankle Ability Measure [FAAM], and American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society [AOFAS] score), quality of life (36-Item Short Form Health Survey [SF-36] Physical Function), complications, and length of hospital stay. Multivariate analyses adjusted for potential confounders.ResultsThere were no significant differences in age, body mass index (BMI), education level, or radiographic parameters between the groups. Anxiety patients had longer hospital stays than nonanxiety patients did (7.03 ± 3.23 vs. 5.16 ± 2.53 days; p < 0.001). Postoperatively, the anxiety group had a higher VAS score (4.14 ± 1.56 vs. 1.24 ± 1.37; p < 0.001) and lower FAAM Activities of Daily Living (ADL), FAAM Sports, AOFAS (85.42 ± 11.54 vs. 91.38 ± 10.11; p = 0.001), and SF-36 PF (83.72 ± 7.36 vs. 94.62 ± 5.42; p < 0.001) scores. Anxiety was moderately correlated with HVA (r = 0.625; p < 0.01). Anxiety severity showed a strong dose-dependent association with functional impairment (FAAM Sports, r = − 0.913) and pain (VAS, r = 0.821).ConclusionPreoperative anxiety is associated with short-term suboptimal postoperative outcomes following hallux valgus surgery, particularly greater pain and worse functional recovery. These findings suggest that preoperative psychological assessments and targeted psychological support may be valuable components of comprehensive surgical care for female patients undergoing hallux valgus correction.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: Hallux valgus, Anxiety, Patient-reported outcomes, Orthopedic surgery, Psychosocial factors

Introduction

Hallux valgus is a common triplanar deformity characterized by the lateral deviation of the hallux and medial prominence of the first metatarsal head [1]. Evidence indicates that hallux valgus occurs more frequently among females and elderly individuals. The estimated global combined prevalence and incidence of HV is 19%, although the prevalence differs substantially by sex: 23.74% among females compared with 11.43% among males [2, 3]. Patients often experience pain, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life, making HV a significant orthopedic concern [4, 5]. Surgical correction, despite being the primary treatment for structural deformity and encompassing more than 100 described techniques, yields highly variable clinical outcomes [6–9]. Minimally invasive approaches may reduce the operative time and accelerate recovery, however, higher-level evidence is needed to establish definitive recommendations [10–12]. Up to 15–30% of patients experience persistent pain, restricted joint motion, or dissatisfaction with cosmetic results, indicating that anatomical correction alone does not guarantee optimal recovery [13, 14].

While biomechanical factors, surgical approaches, and radiographic correction have been extensively studied, emerging evidence highlights the critical role of psychosocial factors in orthopedic surgery outcomes [15]. Mental health comorbidities, fear of reinjury, and kinesiophobia have been shown to adversely affect pain levels, functional outcomes, and return-to-sport rates following ankle fracture [16, 17], chronic ankle instability [18], anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [19, 20], shoulder instability [21], and Achilles tendon rupture [22–24]. Despite this recognition, the influence of preoperative anxiety on hallux valgus surgery outcomes remains poorly characterized. In foot and ankle surgery cohorts, anxiety is a common psychological problem and warrants investigation, however, its sex-specific impact remains poorly characterized [25]. For example, Klein et al. reported no significant association between preoperative anxiety/depression scores and 12-month postoperative foot function, as measured by the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS), following scarf bunionectomy for HV [26]. Notably, patients diagnosed with mood disorders had a significantly increased risk for postoperative pain after ankle and hindfoot reconstruction [27, 28]. A scoping review also revealed that poor preoperative mental health is associated with significantly inferior outcomes following foot and ankle surgery [29]. Recently, Cotchett et al. conducted a cross-sectional study and reported that pain catastrophizing preoperatively is a significant predictor of foot pain and foot function [30]. However, that study did not evaluate the longitudinal impact of pain catastrophizing on postoperative outcomes. Specifically, female HV patients may face unique challenges because of heightened aesthetic concerns and sociocultural pressures regarding footwear norms, potentially exacerbating preoperative anxiety [31, 32]. Preoperative anxiety and depression have been consistently associated with worse postoperative outcomes, including greater pain, prolonged recovery, and increased opioid consumption [33–35]. The aforementioned studies highlight the importance of considering psychosocial assessments preoperatively for individuals planning to undergo foot surgery.

Psychological factors have been increasingly recognized as important determinants of surgical outcomes across various orthopedic subspecialties. However, the specific relationship between preoperative anxiety and postoperative outcomes in patients after hallux valgus surgery remains comparatively understudied [26, 33]. This knowledge gap limits the development of personalized preoperative optimization strategies. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to retrospectively evaluate the association between preoperative anxiety and multidimensional postoperative outcomes following HV surgery, including pain, function, quality of life, and radiographic alignment. To this end, we analyzed the relationships between preoperative anxiety and surgical outcomes in female patients with hallux valgus. We hypothesized that higher levels of anxiety are associated with poorer expected recovery prior to surgery. To our knowledge, this represents one of the few investigations to systematically assess these associations in female patients undergoing hallux valgus correction.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective comparative study of patients who underwent surgery between October 2020 and May 2024 at Beijing Tongren Hospital of Capital Medical University, which is nationally recognized as a specialized center for the treatment of HV. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the hospital (IRB No. TRECKY2020-146), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Participants

Only female patients were enrolled, as they represent approximately 80% of hallux valgus surgical cases at our institution and have a higher prevalence of anxiety and depression than males in this demographic. This approach minimized selection bias and ensured a homogeneous, clinically representative sample. Generalizability to male patients is noted as a limitation. Specifically, we initially screened 374 consecutive patients who underwent hallux valgus surgery at our single academic center. Of these, 163 patients met the inclusion criteria and were ultimately enrolled in the final analysis. The remaining 211 patients were excluded based on the following predetermined criteria. male sex (n = 42), incomplete preoperative anxiety assessment (n = 68), previous foot or ankle surgery (n = 65), revision hallux valgus surgery (n = 21), incomplete postoperative follow-up data (n = 10), and concomitant inflammatory arthritis or neuromuscular disorders (n = 5). All patients visiting the Foot and Ankle Surgery Center ward who received surgical treatment of their hallux valgus were assessed for eligibility. Hallux valgus was diagnosed based on a combination of patient history, physical examination, and imaging. Surgery was performed using either Chevron/Akin/Scarf osteotomy or first metatarsophalangeal joint/first tarsometatarsal joint arthrodesis, depending on individual patient characteristics and disease severity [36–38]. Briefly, surgical approach was determined by standardized criteria: (1) age and activity level—younger, active patients received joint-preserving osteotomy; older (> 65 years), lower-demand patients underwent arthrodesis; (2) deformity severity—mild to moderate (HVA < 40°, IMA < 16°): distal Chevron or Scarf osteotomy; moderate to severe (HVA 40–50°, IMA 16–20°): proximal Scarf with Akin osteotomy; severe (HVA > 50°, IMA > 20°) or joint instability: arthrodesis; (3) first metatarsophalangeal joint arthritis—preserved cartilage: osteotomy; advanced arthritis: arthrodesis; and (4) prior failed surgery: arthrodesis. Surgeries were performed by certified Foot and Ankle surgeons, who were members of the Association for Foot and Ankle Surgery. The inclusion period ensured a minimum follow-up duration of one month for all participants. The exclusion criteria included revision surgery, severe concomitant toe deformities, morbid obesity (body mass index > 35 kg/m²), surgical cancellation for nonmedical reasons, and language barriers precluding effective communication.

Data collection

We collected the clinical data of each patient 2 days before surgery; the data was collected face-to-face and computerized using an HIS case system by dedicated data collectors. Standardized, validated tools, including the Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), visual analog sale (VAS), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) score, and 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), were used for all outcome assessments to reduce measurement bias [39–42]. The paper version of the questionnaire collected information on demographic factors and included the standardized SAS. Demographic information, including age, BMI, education level, marital status, number of children, and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic heart failure (CHF), coronary artery disease (CAD), peripheral artery disease (PAD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and previous foot/ankle surgery, was recorded. The anxiety level of each of patient was assessed using the standardized SAS 2 days before HV surgery [43]. During hospitalization, and at 1 month and 6 months after surgery, follow-up data, including surgery related information and postoperative pain scores, were collected, and postoperative outcomes were evaluated. Surgical information, including length of hospital stay, surgical side, complications and reoperation, was collected during hospitalization. Patients self-reported their pain using a VAS at the 1-month postoperative follow-up evaluation by telephone. Data collectors collected objective postoperative outcome information, including hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA) via an outpatient computer HIS system at 6-month postoperative follow-up evaluation. At the 6-month postoperative follow-up visit, the patients completed the FAAM with ADL and sports subscales, AOFAS scale, and SF-36 with physical function (PF) and bodily pain (BP) subscales via onsite administration at the outpatient clinic.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were compared between groups using the independent-samples t test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between preoperative Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) scores and various demographic, radiographic, and clinical outcome variables. All the statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 163 patients were included in the study, with 115 in the no anxiety group (SAS < 40) and 48 in the anxiety group (SAS ≥ 40). There were no significant differences in age (49.88 ± 13.61 years vs. 52.13 ± 12.14 years, p = 0.322), BMI (26.38 ± 8.85 kg/m² vs. 26.57 ± 9.14 kg/m², p = 0.902), education level (p = 0.908), hypertension (50 vs. 26, p = 0.212), diabetes mellitus (26 vs. 11, p = 0.966), CHF (7 vs. 2, p = 0.910), CAD (10 vs. 3, p = 0.835), COPD (14 vs. 5, p = 0.750), or previous foot/ankle surgery (26 vs. 15, p = 0.246) between the two groups. However, marital status (p = 0.024) was significantly different (Table 1).

Surgery-related information

There was no significant difference in the proportion of unilateral surgeries (82.61% vs. 87.50%, p = 0.437) between the two groups. The incidence of postoperative complications was comparable between the groups, with no significant differences in hemorrhage (anxiety group: 13 [11.30%] vs. non-anxiety group: 6 [12.50%]), surgical site infection (9 [7.83%] vs. 5 [10.42%]), or other complications (4 [3.48%] vs. 2 [4.17%]). Similarly, the reoperation rate did not differ significantly between the groups (3 [2.61%] vs. 2 [4.17%], p = 0.978). In contrast, the length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the anxiety group than in the non-anxiety group (7.03 ± 3.23 days vs. 5.16 ± 2.53 days, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes

Patients with anxiety had higher HVA (11.64 ± 9.05° vs. 9.25 ± 8.58°; p = 0.113) and IMA (5.51 ± 2.96° vs. 4.94 ± 3.15°; p = 0.286), although the differences were not statistically significant. However, patients with anxiety experienced significantly greater postoperative pain, as indicated by higher VAS scores (4.14 ± 1.56 vs. 1.24 ± 1.37; p < 0.001). The functional outcomes were also worse in the anxiety group, with lower FAAM ADL (83.36 ± 8.04 vs. 94.26 ± 7.20; p < 0.001), FAAM Sports (76.85 ± 11.86 vs. 88.98 ± 12.53; p < 0.001), and AOFAS (85.42 ± 11.54 vs. 91.38 ± 10.11; p = 0.001) scores. Additionally, patients with anxiety had lower SF-36 PF scores (83.72 ± 7.36 vs. 94.62 ± 5.42; p < 0.001), indicating worse physical function postoperatively (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate the concurrent effects of preoperative anxiety on postoperative outcomes following HV surgery in female patients. Notably, preoperative anxiety was not significantly associated with postoperative radiographic alignment, as measured by HVA and IMA. This finding indicates that the technical outcome of surgical correction may not correlate with patients’ preoperative psychological state. Elevated preoperative anxiety was associated with markedly greater postoperative pain (VAS score of 4.14 ± 1.56 vs. 1.24 ± 1.37; p < 0.001) and poorer functional outcomes. This pattern highlights the clinical relevance of addressing psychological states prior to hallux valgus surgery. Consistent with prior studies on joint arthroplasty and spine surgery, our findings suggest that preoperative anxiety may intensify pain perception and impede functional recovery [35, 44]. Our study extends this evidence to the domain of foot and ankle surgery, specifically hallux valgus correction, for which the psychological role in recovery has been comparatively understudied. Our findings demonstrate that preoperative anxiety is significantly associated with poor postoperative outcomes in female patients undergoing hallux valgus correction.

Preoperative anxiety, despite being a natural reaction to impending surgery, can become maladaptive when severe. Growing evidence indicates that heightened stress responses to invasive procedures may compromise physiological healing and psychological well-being, thereby adversely affecting postoperative recovery [25, 45]. Preoperative anxiety can negatively affect patient satisfaction with surgery and is associated with postoperative complications [46]. Interestingly, postoperative complication rates did not differ significantly between the groups in our study. These findings suggest that while preoperative anxiety warrants attention for pain and functional outcomes, it may not substantially alter the risk of technical complications in standard hallux valgus surgery performed by experienced surgeons. For example, percutaneous techniques without internal fixation have shown promising preliminary results with durable correction and high patient satisfaction [47]. In addition, our observation that anxiety prolongs hospital stay by nearly two days (7.03 days vs. 5.16 days) echoes similar reports in joint arthroplasty cohorts, where heightened preoperative anxiety correlated with slower postoperative mobilization and extended care requirements. These results corroborate prior reports linking psychological distress to delayed discharge in orthopedic populations [48].

While the radiographic parameters (HVA and IMA) did not significantly differ between groups, we observed a moderate correlation between preoperative anxiety and residual HVA (r = 0.625). These intriguing findings, which contrast with patients’ functional outcome assessments, suggest that psychological factors may influence postoperative alignment through subtle biomechanical (e.g., altered gait patterns) or biological (e.g., healing response) mechanisms. Our results align with the established literature, demonstrating a poor correlation between radiographic angles and patient-reported outcomes in hallux valgus surgery [49, 50]. However, on the basis of the current data, the specific mechanism underlying this correlation—whether primarily biomechanical, biological, or a confounding effect of baseline deformity severity—remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. These findings contrast with recent evidence demonstrating no significant association between mental health status and surgical outcomes in patients with end-stage ankle arthritis [51]. However, we note that the patient cohort in the referenced study exhibited a low prevalence of baseline anxiety disorder; additionally, the authors explicitly recognized their limited capacity to evaluate anxiety’s impact on clinically meaningful improvements. Clinically, this finding provides reassurance that this finding provides reassurance that preoperative anxiety may not elevate surgical risk in standard hallux valgus procedures, suggesting that psychological interventions may be best directed toward optimizing pain and functional recovery rather than preventing complications.

Our results are consistent with those of existing studies highlighting the adverse effects of preoperative anxiety on functional outcomes across all assessment metrics, including FAAM ADL, FAAM Sports, AOFAS, and SF-36 physical function scores. For example, Shakked et al. reported that patients with depressive symptoms were less satisfied with their pain levels postoperatively despite reporting lower mean VAS pain levels [52]. Moreover, evidence of preoperative emotional distress predicted worse pain and function at early foot and ankle surgical follow-up [28]. Importantly, our results contrast with those of Klein et al.’s report that personality traits and symptom reports showed limited predictive value for HV surgery outcomes [26]. We further extended these observations by establishing preoperative anxiety as an independent predictor of postoperative recovery in HV patients. In support of this, a recent study confirmed that presurgical psychological factors (anxiety, depression, and pain catastrophizing) strongly correlated with pain and functional status [30]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential benefits of implementing psychological interventions—such as perioperative counseling or peer support programs—to optimize surgical outcomes in this population. These findings underscore the clinical value of integrating psychological care into standard hallux valgus surgical pathways.

Preoperative anxiety, as a modifiable risk factor, warrants routine screening to enable targeted interventions and optimize patient-reported outcomes. The strong dose-dependent associations between anxiety severity and both pain intensity and functional impairment suggest that even a modest reduction in anxiety may yield clinically meaningful improvements in recovery. Furthermore, our predominantly female cohort represents a high-risk population with an elevated prevalence of baseline anxiety, highlighting the particular need for proactive psychological assessments in this population.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support routine preoperative anxiety screening in hallux valgus surgical candidates. Patients with elevated anxiety may benefit from enhanced postoperative protocols, including multimodal analgesia, earlier physical therapy initiation, and closer follow-up intervals, to mitigate functional decline. Importantly, our findings for complications and radiographic alignment offer a reassuring context: surgeons can inform patients that, in this cohort, preoperative anxiety may not increase surgical risk or compromise technical correction. This nuanced messaging may reduce patient catastrophizing while simultaneously validating the genuine impact of anxiety on recovery experience, thereby enhancing shared decision-making and satisfaction. Given the clear association between preoperative anxiety and worse patient-reported outcomes, addressing psychological distress prior to surgery could improve early mobilization and the overall recovery trajectory. In resource-limited settings, these data support risk-stratified triage, prioritizing psychological support for highest-risk patients. With respect to surgical training programs, our findings support the integration of psychological assessments into foot and ankle curricula, recognizing that technical proficiency alone does not guarantee optimal patient-reported outcomes. Future studies should explore whether targeted interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy or relaxation techniques) can mitigate the negative effects of anxiety on HV surgery outcomes, ideally through randomized controlled trials. Additionally, future studies should investigate whether these findings extend to other foot and ankle procedures, for which psychosocial factors are similarly understudied despite their likely clinical relevance.

A key strength of this study is the use of a validated, anxiety-specific assessment tool (SAS) in a well-defined cohort of hallux valgus patients, providing more granular insight than generalized psychosocial instruments do. However, our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design inherently limits our ability to establish temporal causality and may introduce selection bias; throughout the manuscript, we have therefore framed our findings as associative rather than causative. Second, nonresponse bias may exist, as 163 of 374 screened patients were ultimately included, and excluded individuals may have differed systematically from those enrolled. Third, the relatively short follow-up duration (one to six months) represents a notable limitation; our findings reflect short-term outcomes only, and future prospective studies with follow-up exceeding one year are warranted to determine whether these associations persist long term. Moreover, the 5–7 days hospital stay reflects standard Chinese healthcare protocols, where inpatient preoperative anesthesia evaluation and postoperative observation are routine. This exceeds short-stay protocols in other systems and should be interpreted within its cultural context. Fourth, surgical management varied, and we were unable to perform subgroup analyses by procedure type because of the limited sample size; this variability may have confounded the outcome comparisons. Fifth, the use of AOFAS scores is a limitation, as the AOFAS scale has recognized reliability and generalizability concerns compared with newer legacy measures [53]. Sixth, measurement bias may exist, as patients with differing anxiety levels may interpret survey questions differently. Seventh, we were unable to ascertain whether certain patients received ongoing psychiatric treatment for anxiety during the perioperative period, which could independently influence outcomes. Finally, the study focused exclusively on female patients, limiting its generalizability to male populations. Future research should employ prospective designs, longer follow-up durations, larger samples for subgroup analyses, and broader demographic inclusion to validate and extend these findings. Despite these limitations, our results underscore that psychological well-being should be considered alongside anatomical correction in the management of hallux valgus, advocating for a more holistic approach to postoperative care. Integrating psychiatric and rehabilitative strategies could enhance recovery in anxious patients, ensuring that functional outcomes match radiographic success.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that preoperative anxiety is associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes in female patients undergoing hallux valgus correction. While these results support the consideration of preoperative psychological screening in clinical practice, further prospective research with longer follow-up is warranted to confirm these associations and assess the efficacy of targeted interventions.
