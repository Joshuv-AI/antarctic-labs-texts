Neuromelioidosis in Rajasthan: A rare and fatal manifestation of Burkholderia pseudomallei infection
Authors: Meenakshi Pareek, Aditi Gupta, Shailendra Prajapat, Taruna Yadav, Kumar S. Abhishek, Deepak Kumar, Gopal Krishna Bohra, Mahesh Devnani, Vibhor Tak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMelioidosis, an often-fatal infection caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, is typically endemic to wet, tropical regions. However, its occurrence in dry, arid zones like northwest India is underrecognized. Neuromelioidosis, the rare neurological form of this disease, presents with high morbidity and mortality. This report details a confirmed case of neuromelioidosis diagnosed in Rajasthan, India.Case PresentationA 65-year-old male with a background of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presented with prolonged fever, respiratory symptoms, and altered mental status. Neurological examination revealed increased limb tone and extensor plantar responses. Imaging revealed subcortical white matter hypodensity and multiple centrilobular nodules. Initial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was suggestive of tubercular meningitis; however, subsequent blood and CSF cultures confirmed Burkholderia pseudomallei. Despite initiation of intravenous meropenem and co-trimoxazole, the patient succumbed to multi-organ failure after 14 days.DiscussionNeuromelioidosis remains a diagnostic challenge due to its ability to mimic other CNS infections, particularly tuberculosis. This case underscores the critical role of microbiological confirmation in guiding treatment. While the standard treatment protocol includes an intensive phase with meropenem followed by maintenance with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, delays in diagnosis significantly worsen outcomes.ConclusionClinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for neuromelioidosis in patients with unexplained neurological symptoms, even in non-endemic, arid regions like Rajasthan. Timely diagnosis and targeted therapy are key to improving prognosis. This case highlights the need for greater awareness and diagnostic vigilance in atypical geographic settings.

Introduction

Melioidosis, caused by the bacterium Burkholderia pseudomallei, is an emerging infectious disease in India, particularly in the dry and arid northwestern region, where its occurrence is rare. This Gram-negative, aerobic, and non-spore-forming bacterium is commonly found in contaminated soil and water. The rising incidence of melioidosis in this region highlights the potential impact of climate change on its epidemiology [1].

Melioidosis is a life-threatening disease with a high mortality rate, often challenging to diagnose due to its ability to mimic other conditions. In many cases, patients succumb to the infection before an accurate diagnosis is made. The disease typically manifests with abscess formation, particularly in the lungs, liver, spleen, skin, and skeletal muscles. Neuromelioidosis refers to central nervous system (CNS) involvement in melioidosis. Although involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) is rare (3–5 %), it is statistically linked to a higher mortality rate of up to 20 % [2].

Neuromelioidosis, a rare neurological manifestation of melioidosis, is even less commonly reported in Rajasthan. Our study presents the first confirmed case of neuromelioidosis in the region, identified through the detection of Burkholderia pseudomallei in the patient’s cerebrospinal fluid and blood culture. The diagnosis was established based on radiological and microbiological findings, along with the patient’s clinical presentation, strongly indicating the presence of neuromelioidosis.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old male from Hemawas, Pali, with a two-year history of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), was admitted to the Emergency High-Dependency Unit (HDU). He presented with complaints of fever, productive cough, and shortness of breath persisting for two months, along with altered sensorium (irrelevant speech) for two days before admission. He had a history of chronic bidi smoking for 10 years and was a former tobacco chewer, having quit a decade ago. By profession, he was a tailor and there was no significant history of contact with contaminated soil or water.

Prior to admission, he had been hospitalised at an external facility for 7 days and treated with ceftriaxone (1 g BD). On admission, his vital signs were stable, with normal blood pressure, a pulse rate of 118/min, and SpO₂ of 99 %. However, he was pale and exhibited bilateral occasional crepitation on auscultation. Neurological examination revealed increased tone in all four limbs, with an extensor response in the left plantar reflex.

Initial investigations showed hypodensity in the subcortical and deep white matter of the right high parietal lobe, along with altered CSF signal density in the right lateral ventricle on NCCT head. HRCT thorax revealed multiple centrilobular nodules with a "tree-in-bud" pattern. CSF analysis showed elevated protein levels (736 mg/dL), low glucose (6.79 mg/dL), and an increased cell count with lymphomononuclear predominance (80 %) and polymorphs (20 %), initially suggestive of tubercular meningitis, for which Anti-Tubercular Therapy (ATT) was initiated.

Blood and CSF cultures grew creamy, non hemolytic on blood agar colonies which were oxidase positive as depicted in Fig. 1. On MacConkey agar, colonies appeared pink, dry, and wrinkled after 48 h of incubation, whereas growth on Ashdown agar demonstrated characteristic purple colonies (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). This was confirmed by both automated Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization-Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) and conventional PCR for Burkholderia pseudomallei, establishing a diagnosis of neuromelioidosis. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing using disk diffusion was performed on Mueller–Hinton agar according to EUCAST guidelines, which demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to ceftazidime and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, with sensitivity to meropenem. A triple-disk test revealed resistance to colistin and gentamicin, while the isolate remained sensitive to amoxicillin–clavulanate characteristic of Burkholderia pseudomallei shown in Fig. 4. ATT was discontinued, and the patient was started on high-dose meropenem 2 gm IV and two tablets of double strength trimethoprim - sulphamethoxazole (160/800 mg) twice a day. Despite treatment, his condition worsened, necessitating intubation and mechanical ventilation, and he was transferred to the Trauma Intensive Care Unit (TICU) for further management. The FiO2 requirements progressively increased (33–100 %). Tracheal cultures revealed carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, for which appropriate antibiotic therapy was administered.

During his ICU stay, he developed severe complications, including worsening Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) and hypotension requiring inotropic support. Contrast-enhanced imaging of the chest and abdomen showed multiple confluent patches of ground-glass opacities, consolidation with air bronchograms in both lungs, but no evidence of abscesses. MRI brain findings were indicative of multiple micro abscesses and micro bleeds in the right cerebral hemisphere, along with obstructive hydrocephalus, ventriculitis, and ependymitis as shown in Fig. 5. Over time, the patient developed a new-onset focal neurological deficit. A repeat NCCT head revealed increasing edema with a midline shift of approximately 10 mm to the left. A neurosurgical consultation was obtained, and anti-edema treatment was continued. His neurological status continued to decline.

During the course of his ICU stay, the patient's hypotension worsened despite escalating inotropic support. His ARDS progressed further, and he was unable to maintain oxygen saturation even on full ventilatory support.

On 14th day of admission at mid night, his hypotension deteriorated rapidly, requiring an increase to triple inotropes. His cardiac monitor showed bradycardia, which later progressed to asystole. CPR was initiated immediately as per the modified Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) protocol, with intravenous adrenaline administered every 3–5 min. Despite resuscitative efforts, he succumbed to cardiac arrest.

The cause of death was refractory hypotension, severe metabolic acidosis, and multi-organ failure secondary to septic shock and neuromelioidosis.

Discussion

Neuromelioidosis, a rare but serious manifestation of melioidosis, involves the central nervous system and is caused by the environmental pathogen Burkholderia pseudomallei. This condition represents about 3–5 % of all melioidosis cases, with a notably high mortality rate of around 20 % [2]. The infection is more commonly observed in individuals with pre-existing conditions and is often under-recognized, particularly in non-endemic regionsIn India, melioidosis has emerged as a growing public health concern, particularly in humid, tropical, and coastal areas. Arid zones, such as those in the northwest, tend to report fewer cases [1], [3].

Geographically, neurological involvement due to melioidosis is frequently documented in regions such as northern Australia, Thailand, and parts of South and Southeast Asia, including India and Malaysia [4]. A common challenge in managing this disease is the lack of clinical suspicion, which often leads to diagnostic delays. The pathogen typically enters the body through skin abrasions, inhalation, or ingestion of contaminated soil or water [5], [6]. The infection is known to spike during the monsoon season and after flooding events, accounting for up to 80 % of cases during these periods [2]. Risk factors like diabetes mellitus, renal impairment, chronic alcoholism, and immunosuppressive therapy significantly increase susceptibility [7].

Clinically, melioidosis may range from localized skin lesions to severe pneumonia and sepsis [8]. The Most prevalent presentation is the development of focal or multifocal abscesses accompanied by bacteraemia. Neurological involvement may be misdiagnosed due to similarities with more common endemic diseases such as tuberculosis. The presence of multiple abscesses and bloodstream infection is a frequent finding [9]. Laboratory diagnosis remains challenging as the organism can resemble other non-lactose fermenting Gram-negative bacilli like Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter. Inadequate microbiological alertness can further contribute to misidentification [10]. Earlier, the organism was often misidentified on automated systems, but with updated Vitek 2 and MALDI-ToF MS, identification has now become faster and more reliable. [11].

The standard treatment for melioidosis includes a two-phase antibiotic regimen: an initial intensive phase followed by a prolonged eradication phase. Intravenous ceftazidime or meropenem is recommended during the acute phase for a minimum of 2 weeks, followed by oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) for at least 12 weeks. Cases with CNS involvement, require an extended intensive phase of intravenous therapy for up to 8 weeks, followed by a prolonged eradication phase of 6 months as per latest Darwin guidelines.In cases of TMP-SMX intolerance, doxycycline is a suitable alternative. Despite appropriate therapy, relapse can still occur, although the recurrence rate is reduced to below 5 % with adherence to treatment guidelines [12].

Globally, similar cases of neuromelioidosis have been reported. A systematic review of 120 CNS melioidosis patients revealed a male predominance (70 %) and a high frequency (60 %) of underlying comorbidities. Brain imaging often showed ring-enhancing lesions, and two-thirds had multi-organ involvement. The reported mortality rate remained at 20 % [13]. Europe documented its first case in 2022, with diagnosis achieved after extensive testing. Although the patient survived, they were left with permanent neurological deficits [14].

Sri Lanka and China have also contributed case reports. In Sri Lanka, the patient recovered with some neurological sequelae [15]. A case series from Hainan Province, China, reported seven patients—five had major neurological deficits, one died, and one was discharged prematurely [16].

In India, southern regions have seen more reports. A case series from Vellore documented 18 cases between 2008 and 2019, which included abscesses, cerebritis, and meningeal involvement. Three of these patients did not survive [17]. Six cases from Bhubaneswar included presentations of brain abscess, meningitis, and spinal epidural involvement, with a 50 % mortality rate [18].

From Rajasthan including ours only two cases have been documented. A 28-year-old woman from Rajasthan presented at AIIMS Delhi with left hemiparesis following a 4-month history of fever, headache, and seizures initially misdiagnosed as tubercular granuloma and later suspected CNS aspergillosis and vasculitis. Only after brain biopsy and culture was the correct diagnosis of CNS melioidosis established. She was treated with prolonged meropenem and ceftazidime therapy, followed by oral doxycycline. After a year, she remained severely disabled (mRS 5), requiring full nursing care, though with minimal neurological improvement [19].

Conclusions

Diagnosing neuromelioidosis remains a significant clinical challenge due to its nonspecific symptoms and the rarity of the condition in many regions. Early identification is essential, as delayed diagnosis increases the risk of adverse outcomes, including death and long-term neurological impairment. Given its ability to mimic other central nervous system infections, especially tuberculosis, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion—particularly in patients from endemic areas or with relevant risk factors.

Timely and accurate diagnosis often depends on a combination of imaging findings and advanced microbiological techniques. MRI patternssuch as ring-enhancing lesions with tunnel sign, hemorrhagic foci, ventriculitis, and microabscessescan offer critical diagnostic clues in suspected cases. Treatment must be promptly initiated and appropriately prolonged to prevent recurrence.

As awareness increases and diagnostic capabilities improve, more cases are likely to be identified. Early intervention with targeted antibiotic therapy has the potential to reduce mortality and minimize long-term disability. Recognizing neuromelioidosis as part of the differential diagnosis in appropriate clinical contexts will ultimately lead to better patient outcomes.
