Benefits and risks of consuming Cannabis sativa L. in food
Authors: Ewa Matyjaszczyk, Maria Śmiechowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cannabis sativa L. or hemp is a cosmopolitan, multi-purpose plant and area of its cultivation has been growing in recent years. Its public perception is influenced by potential to produce narcotic substances. However, hemp seed can be a valuable source of nutrients for humans and may be of particular interest in more sustainable food consumption patterns, including vegetarian and flexitarian diets. The paper presents benefits and risks of hemp seeds in case of their consumption in food. Low cannabinoids varieties are considered only. The biological activity of cannabinoids and related effects are considered out of scope of the study. Nutritional value of hemp seeds regarding content of amino acids is comparable to the value of egg whites and soya. Oil is a source of two essential fatty acids: linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid in a desirable ratio of 3:1. Hemp seeds are also a source of minerals, vitamins and carbohydrates, including fiber. The most characteristic hazardous properties of hemp are ability to accumulate toxic heavy metals and produce anti-nutritional substances. Breeding hemp to reduce the anti-nutritional compounds content may be promising in view of its growing popularity as a food source.

1. Introduction

Cannabis sativa L. or hemp has been cultivated since ancient times and has a wide range of uses. During its growth the plant can be used for phytoremediation and as a pest repellent [1,2]. After being harvested it serves among others as food, feed, as well as a raw material for obtaining fiber for clothing, paper, and technical products such as ropes, strings, and construction materials [3,4]. It can also be used for medicinal purposes, by cosmetic industry, and as a source of energy (bio-fuels) [5,6]. Cannabis sativa L. can also be a source of narcotic substances. For that reason its cultivation is regulated in many countries [7,8] and its taxonomy is based on psychoactive properties [9]. The narcotic effect is mainly related to the content of cannabinoids, especially Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC), which has mildly addictive properties, but is also used in medicine [10].

The interest in Cannabis is growing. In recent years there has been a significant increase in its cultivation area worldwide. Hemp is produced on all continents. In Asia, the largest producer is China, in Europe, France, in North America, Canada, and in South America, Chile [11].

This paper attempts a narrative review of scientific articles on hemp. The focus here is on one way of use of this multi-purpose plant only - consumption by humans as food - in order to highlight related benefits and risks.

The assumption was made that legally grown, low THC Cannabis sativa L. varieties are used to produce food for humans [12]. Hence the risk analysis is focused on factors different that cannabinoids. Abundant literature on risks related to consumption of cannabinoids is, however, available and many reviews focus on them [13,14].

The data in Table 1 show that Cannabis sativa L. seeds contain the most fat and the least phytocannabinoids. For this reason, the part of the plant used for human consumption is almost exclusively seeds [15]. Hemp seed is an achene containing only one seed that does not split when the seeds are mature. The shelled seeds have an oval or spherical shape with green spots, a greenish-gray color, and a pleasant, nutty taste [95].

Since antiquity, hemp seeds have been consumed in China (since 2500 BC) and other regions of Asia (since approx. 500 BC) as evidenced by, among others, artifacts found in tombs [16]. Hemp seeds can be consumed whole, shelled, or processed [17,18].

3. Nutrients in Cannabis sativa L. seeds

3.1. Protein content and amino acid composition

The protein content in Cannabis seeds varies greatly depending on the variety and form of the seed. Raw seed contains 18.3–23.0% [average 20.4%] of protein, while dehulled one contains 24.6–28.4% [average 26.0%] using the conversion factor of 5.3 [14]. In the study by Franek et al. [19], the protein content in hemp seed was 22.5–26.4% [conversion factor 6.25]. In case of hemp as well as other plant raw materials the conversion factor of 5.3 may be me appropriate as plant proteins are less easily digestible than animal proteins. Hence using conversion factor of 6.25 may lead to overestimation of protein content [20].

The two main proteins in hemp seeds are albumin and edestin. Edestin is the most abundant and constitutes approximately 82% of the hemp protein content. Hemp protein contains most of the essential amino acids needed by the human body. Regarding content of amino acids the nutritional value of hemp seeds can be compared to the value of soy proteins and egg whites. Comparative analysis of hemp protein with soy protein showed that hemp proteins are rich in aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine, arginine, and leucine. In turn, the content of exogenous amino acids isoleucine, lysine, and phenylalanine was lower in hemp protein than in soy protein [21]. Hemp protein is characterized by high arginine and phenylalanine content. The limiting amino acid in hemp protein is tryptophan [22]. Hemp protein can be considered a good source of sulfur amino acids [23,24]. Rizzo et al. [25] pointed out that the edestin protein contains aromatic amino acids, which are usually poorly represented in a vegetarian diet. Moreover, the albumin fraction contains fewer disulfide bonds, which gives the protein a less compact structure and does not inhibit trypsin, which increases digestibility. The availability and content of protein in hemp seeds also increases after roasting [26].

Xu et al. [27] evaluated hemp protein in comparison to soy, pea, whey, and egg proteins. Hemp protein contained the lowest levels of total and essential amino acids but the highest levels of arginine. Furthermore, hemp protein is characterized by a compact structure, which may explain the protein’s low water absorption capacity and solubility.

3.2. Fatty acid profile of Cannabis seeds

Hemp seeds contain an average of 30% fat, but the fat content of the seeds depends on the latitude of cultivation. Research shows that it may remain within quite wide limits, from 24.3% for hemp oil grown in the USA, 26.9% for hemp oil in Pakistan and Canada, to 33.3% for hemp oil from Ukraine [24].

Pre-roasting the hemp seeds significantly affects the oil yield and linoleic acid content in the pressing process [26]. Hemp seeds provide oil containing unsaturated fatty acids such as linolenic acid and linolenic acid. It contains omega-6 and omega-3 acids in a ratio of 3:1, which is considered the most correct from a nutritional point of view [28,29]. This desirable ratio of omega-6 and omega-3 acids in Cannabis sativa seed oil was confirmed by research conducted in Turkey, where it was additionally shown that the geographical region may influence the composition of the oil [30]. LA and ALA are precursors of polyunsaturated fatty acids [PUFA] n-6 and n-3, biologically active arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid. In a study conducted in Lithuania on the fatty acids contained in oils from Cannabis sativa, Linum usitatissimum, and Camelina sativa. Razmaité et al. [6] showed that all oils consisted mainly of two essential acids, namely linoleic and α-linolenic acid. In all seeds, the PUFA/SFA [saturated fatty acids] ratio was much higher than recommended in a healthy diet. The ratio was the highest in case of hemp seeds, while the lowest in linseeds.

Among omega-6 acids, attention is drawn to gamma-linolenic acid 18:3[n-6], which has been shown in studies to have anti-inflammatory effects in many diseases. As shown by Rosso et al. [31], depending on the hemp variety, the content of gamma-linolenic acid remained in the range of 0.54–1.8 g/100 g of total fatty acid content.

3.3. Carbohydrates, including fiber, in Cannabis seeds

The carbohydrate content of Cannabis seeds varies considerably. Callaway [32] reports that the total carbohydrate content in whole hemp seeds of the Finola variety was 27.6 g per 100 g of seeds. Mattila et al. [33] determined the carbohydrate content in whole hemp seeds at 34.4 ± 1.5 g/100 g. It was pointed out that the main fraction of carbohydrates is insoluble fiber, i.e., crude fiber. Fiber constitutes 98% of total carbohydrates and the remainder is starch [33]. The high content of insoluble fiber in hemp seeds is reported by Vonapartis et al. [34] at 35.7 g/100 g, with most of the IDF found in the hemp seed husk.

The significant variation in carbohydrate content of hemp reported by the literature is influenced by a number of factors, including the variety, location and growing conditions, as well as the form of the seed, i.e. whether the seed is with or without a husk. It is also worth to keep in mind that carbohydrate content is influenced by other components e.g. the variation in oil content affects carbohydrate composition [33].

3.4. Vitamins in Cannabis sativa seeds

In addition to the extremely rich composition of fatty acids, hemp seeds contain as well unsaponifiable fractions, which include tocopherols, and phytosterols. The most common isomer of tocopherol, namely γ-tocopherol ensures high oxidative stability of hemp seeds, especially their oil, while the α isomer is considered to be the only bioactive form of tocopherol, i.e., the form showing vitamin E activity in the human body [17], α-tocopherol and δ-tocopherol isomers occur in smaller amounts in hemp seeds. Research conducted by various authors indicate diverse content of tocopherols in hemp seeds. This diversity may be due to various factors such as the influence of seed genotype, year of cultivation, geographical origin, and extraction method [34]. In addition to tocopherols and vitamin E, Cannabis sativa oil contains vitamin A and carotenoids [which can be converted to vitamin A in human body]. The content of vitamins E and A in hemp seed oil was determined to be 56.28–92.97 and 7.8 mg/100 g, respectively [35].

3.5. The content of mineral substances in Cannabis sativa

Hemp seeds are a good source of minerals [32,35]. The content of microelements and macroelements in hemp seeds can vary within a wide range of 4–7.6%. Studies on the mineral composition of hemp seeds conducted by Montero et al. [35] show that the content of individual elements can remain in a wide range, which may be influenced by the hemp variety and the place of cultivation. Rizzo et al. [25] pointed out that Cannabis sativa seeds provide elements such as calcium, iron and zinc, which play an important role in the diet, especially in diets that exclude animal products. The content of minerals in whole and shelled hemp seeds may also vary. Alonso-Esteban et al. [17] showed that whole hemp seeds have higher Ca, Mn, and Cu contents than shelled seeds, while shelled hemp seeds have higher P, K, Mg, and Zn contents than whole seeds. However, the iron content was almost the same in whole and shelled hemp seeds. In the study by Siano et al. [36] all macroelements had a higher content in flour than in seeds. Fedora seed flour contained more calcium, potassium, and magnesium than the grain. The content of trace elements: Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu was also higher in hemp flour. The above-cited authors explain that the varying mineral content in hemp seeds results from factors such as plant variety, soil conditions, and year of cultivation. As showed by Barčauskaitė et al. [37], the content of macro and microelements in hemp seeds is also influenced by sowing density and intensity of fertilization.

4. The use of raw materials from hemp in food production and dietary supplements

In recent years, a rapid development of the market for products obtained from hemp seeds is observed. One of the key factors that sparked the increased interest in Cannabis sativa L. was the search for alternative sources of protein. This is influenced by changes in the human nutrition model, an increase in consumer interest in plant-based, vegan, and vegetarian diets, or diets with a predominance of plants, as is the case in semi-vegetarian diets, which are associated with limiting the consumption of animal protein. Hemp seeds have great potential and can be used in various branches of food and non-food industries [3]. A number of processes used in food technology affect the properties and composition of hemp seeds. Liu et al. [38] demonstrated that hemp seed germination improves the functional properties of protein, increasing the hardness of hemp seed gels. Aloo et al. [39] demonstrated that not only germination but also the fermentation process improves the functional properties of hemp seeds. Germination and fermentation affect the levels of anti-nutritional substances such as tannins, saponins, acid, and trypsin inhibitors [39].

The nutritional value of hemp seeds is positively influenced by roasting, which has already been used in ancient times [25]. In recent years, the seed roasting process has undergone significant innovations, resulting in new attractive products, such as hemp seeds roasted with sea salt. Roasted hemp seeds can also be combined with other food products such as blueberries, barberries, sour cherries, mulberries, cranberries, vanilla, cocoa, and figs to create new snack products [35].

Another product obtained from hemp seeds that arouses great interest is hemp flour. Comparative studies show that hemp flour has a higher nutritional value and higher content of protein, fat, minerals, fiber, essential fatty acids, and essential amino acids than wheat flour [40]. Moreover, hemp flour does not contain gluten, therefore it does not cause related food allergies and is safe for people with celiac disease. Studies have shown that hemp protein isolates do not contain thaumatin-like proteins with allergenic properties, which allows their use as a hypoallergenic food ingredient. Guided by these advantages of hemp flour, it was used, among others, for making pasta. It has been shown that the addition of 30–40% hemp flour to durum wheat flour improves the physicochemical properties, texture parameters, and sensory properties of pasta [41]. Bonacci et al. [42] showed that the rheological and sensory quality, as well as nutritional value of pasta made with hemp flour is influenced not only by the size of the addition, but also by the grinding degree of the flour. Similar conclusions were drawn by Mikulec et al. [43] as regards baking bread.

In addition to bread and pasta, there are various snacks, desserts, bars, muesli and granola with the addition of hemp seeds, drinks, milk, chocolate, sweets and confectionery products, meat substitute, and sprouts [44].

Bukowska [45], Trovato et al. [46] and Kamle et al. [47] indicated that hemp seed products can be classified as healthy foods because they contain many bioactive compounds, such as polyphenols and vitamins. They also stressed as desirable the content of secondary metabolites such as terpenes, phytosterols, tocopherols, and cannabinoids.

Numerous research studies show that hemp seeds have nutraceutical potential. Studies have shown that hemp oil can be helpful in the treatment of skin diseases, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis and reduces the risk of cancer [28,45]. Hemp seed oil also contains gammalinolenic acid, which is beneficial in preventing osteoporosis [48,49]. Therapeutic results in the treatment of central nervous system diseases such as epilepsy, neurodegenerative diseases, and multiple sclerosis are promising [47,50]. The studies of Pinto and Martel [51] confirm anti-obesity effects of cannabidiol. They also believe that the high content of insoluble dietary fiber in hemp seeds and flour has a functional role influencing food digestibility, prevents obesity, reduces appetite, lowers cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels. On the other hand Rogers et al. [52] studied use cannabis-infused food products in the treatment of anorexia.

5. Safety issues related to Cannabis sativa L. consumption

Among the factors that may pose risk for humans consuming hemp, heavy metals, pesticides, as well as microorganisms and mycotoxins are frequently mentioned in the literature [53–55]. In addition the seeds contain anti-nutritional compounds.

There are also other threats, such as the presence of THC higher than 0.3%, due to contamination with seeds of different varieties or cannabinoid contamination. Cannabinoid contamination may result from contact with the resin secreted by trichomes during harvest, or cleaning of seeds. Processing hemp must therefore be controlled. Analytical techniques already exist that enable rapid and sensitive methods for detecting cannabinoids [9].

5.1. Presence of anti-nutritional compounds in hemp seeds

While discussing use of Cannabis sativa L. in food, it is necessary to stress certain anti-nutritional compounds that may negatively affect its value as a food product. Antinutritional compounds bind to nutrients, causing a decrease in their bioactivity, reduce protein digestibility and mineral utilization [56]. Antinutritional compounds include phytic acid, condensed tannins, saponins, cyanogenic glycosides, and trypsin inhibitors [57]. Phytic acid is a natural compound found in plants. However, the high content of phytates in hemp seeds may limit the bioavailability of P, Fe, Mg, Zn, Mn, and Cu by forming complexes with these elements. They also affect the activity of digestive enzymes. Therefore phytic acid and other phytates have long been considered antinutritional compounds. The phytic acid content in hemp seeds ranges from 4.5 to 7.6 g/100 g of dry, defatted matter [58]. Matila et al. [59] showed that the phytic acid content in hemp seeds is on average 5.3 g/100 g. To reduce the adverse effects of phytic acid contained in Cannabis sativa, soaking, germination, fermentation, cooking, extrusion, dehulling, radiation and ultrasound techniques are used [60]. However, recent research indicates that phytic acid may have beneficial effects on the body. Phytic acid has been shown to have anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antibacterial properties, as well as antidiabetic properties. Phytic acid has free radical scavenging properties, and research is underway to use it in cancer and neurodegenerative diseases [61].

Condensed tannins, also known as proanthocyanidins, form complexes that are insoluble together with proteins and minerals. Tannins are phenolic compounds that have a positive impact on health by acting as antioxidants, anticancer, cardioprotective, antimicrobial and antidiabetic compounds [62]. Following Cosme et al. [63] the content of condensed tannins in hemp seeds ranged from 88.6 to 13.8 mg/g in terms of catechin equivalents.

Trypsin inhibitors are commonly found in seeds of Brassicaceae, Solanaceae, Fabaceae and Poaceae plants. They limit the utilization of proteins in food by blocking the proteolytic enzymes trypsin and chymotrypsin. Trypsin inhibitors also adversely affect the digestion of peptides and proteins in the small intestine. Their content in hemp seeds depends on various factors such as variety and genotype, cultivation conditions and is in the range from 10.8 to 27.8 of enzymatic activity units per mg of defatted flour [58].

Cyanogenic glycosides are secondary metabolites produced by plants as pests protective substances. They are classified as toxic to humans because, under unfavorable conditions, they may undergo hydrolysis in the gastrointestinal tract, which ultimately leads to the release of hydrogen cyanide. It is believed that in concentrations below 100 ppm they are not harmful to the human body. However, their content varies significantly depending on the genotype and variety [64].

Saponins include glycoside compounds containing non-sugar steroid or triterpenoid aglycone. They have an irritating effect on the stomach and may have toxic properties that cause hemolysis of red blood cells. Due to the toxic effects, FAO/WHO proposed limits for the saponin content in quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) to 0.12%. Quinoa contains the most saponins among other seeds and grains, such as linseed or soybean [65]. The amount of saponins in hemp seed flour can vary greatly owing to numerous factors [64,66]. Tasie et al. [67] determined the content of saponins in three non-defatted and defatted varieties of industrial hemp seeds. The saponin content for non-defatted seeds was in the range of 0.16–0.18 g/100 g, and in defatted seeds 0.18–0.20 g/100 g.

In the future, it may appear possible to reduce or eliminate the content of certain anti-nutrients mentioned above through breeding methods. Such a direction in hemp breeding may be promising in view of its growing popularity as a food source.

5.2. Microorganisms and mycotoxins

Cannabis sativa L. is associated with various epiphytic as well as endophytic microbial communities. This includes organisms that may potentially pose a threat to human health. Some of the bacteria species inhabiting Cannabis are opportunistic human pathogens [e.g. Escherichia coli, Salmonella sp., Listeria sp.]. Among fungi identified in Cannabis are species with both the ability to cause human infections as well as produce mycotoxins. Fungi such as Aspergillus sp., Fusarium sp., and Penicillium sp. that were found in Cannabis, are potentially infectious to humans (in particular for groups with compromised immune system) and at the same time a potential source of mycotoxins [53,68].

Microorganisms may be eliminated or neutralised during the process of food preparation, however some of them may survive and pose risks, in particular for vulnerable groups of consumers. On the other hand, mycotoxins generally survive food processing as they are chemically stable. Production of mycotoxins can occur either before harvest or during storage of harvested seeds, in particular while drying and storing in humid environments. The long-term effects of mycotoxins occurring in food may include the induction of cancers and immune deficiency [69].

Several Fusarium species were isolated from hemp seeds, some of which were found in the seeds and some on the seed surface [70]. Mycotoxins produced by Fusarium species include deoxynivalenol, zearalenone, fumonisin B and T-2. Mycotoxins were also detected in hemp flour, with more toxins in flour stored in plastic packaging than in a paper bag. Sacco et al. [71] found that hemp flour contained four times more mycotoxins than wheat flour.

5.3. Pesticides

There are harmful organisms that may affect hemp production [72] and pesticides may be used to control them. It is generally considered that detection of pesticides below maximum residue levels [MRL] confirms their use in the food chain, while detection residues above MRL may mean incorrect application and risk to consumers [73]. Pesticides residues are mentioned as potentially hazardous compounds detected in Cannabis sativa L. [54]. Arena et al. [74] showed presence of pesticides above MRL in four out of ten analyzed samples of hemp seed oil. The remaining samples [six out of ten] showed no detectable levels of pesticide residues.

It is worth stressing that pesticide residues are present in numerous plants [75,76] and the ability to accumulate pesticide residues although worth noting may not be a characteristic specific to hemp. On the other hand, hemp is known in agronomy as a plant with potential to inhibit growth of certain weeds and produce pests repelling substances, as well as potential source of biopesticides. In horticulture hemp is known to be introduced to the gardens in order to inhibit the growth of soil nematodes and repel pests from the neighbouring plants of different species enabling elimination or reduced use of pesticides [2,77]. It is therefore possible that compounds produced by hemp may have potential to be commercialized for use in plant protection as biopesticides [78]. The protective substances produced by hemp against pests may influence its value as food source and were discussed above as anti-nutritional compounds.

5.4. Heavy metals

Cannabis have deep root system and through it is able to absorb and accumulate various elements from the soil. This includes toxic heavy metals, as well as other elements of potential toxicity for humans. According to the available literature, cannabis can absorb among others lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic, antimony, tin, selenium, strontium, bismuth, titanium, nickel, chromium, as well as cobalt [1,79]. The absorption level depends on a number of factors including concentration in the soil, type of soil, fertilization level, and the Cannabis variety. The concentration of heavy metals and other toxic element in the plant organs and tissues differs. Some studies show quite high content of heavy metals in the plant parts that could be potentially consumed by humans [80]. According to Flajšman et al [79], Golia et al [81] and Ćaćić et al. [82] heavy metals bioaccumulate mainly in the underground part, while the stem, leaves and seeds contain lower concentration of pollutants. Zafeiraki et al. [83] and Gauvin et al. [84] report that higher concentrations of Cd are found in leaves than in seeds. Considering the literature, it is not unlikely that the Cannabis plant parts consumed by humans as food may contain elevated levels of toxic elements. It seems that two heavy metals - lead and cadmium - may be of particular concern. The reason is their high toxicity for humans as well as the fact that both are accumulated by edible parts of Cannabis.

However the data on heavy metals in hemp available in the literature often come from the studies focused on phytoremediation potential of Cannabis and/or accumulation of elements in the plant parts. Consequently, many samples come from plants grown on wastelands, near abandoned mines or other areas known to be contaminated and not intended for food production, rather than from land in agricultural use. The use of such data in the context of food safety may lead to an overestimation of health risk scenarios.

The data on the content of toxic elements coming from Cannabis grown in laboratory conditions on a substrate very high in heavy metals, or on agricultural soil purposefully enriched with toxic elements shall be not considered for risk assessment as unrealistic. The concentration levels of lead and cadmium coming from the samples collected in the areas known to be polluted, but still used as agricultural land, may give an indication regarding the worst-case scenario. Such approach was used in other works on risk assessment of heavy metals in plants consumed by human and possessing the ability to accumulate toxic elements [85,86].

What are the maximum levels of cadmium and lead as food contaminants? According to WHO guidelines on quality of herbal medicines and products [87], the limits are 0.3 mg/kg dry weight for cadmium and 10 mg/kg dry weight for lead. The European Union Commission Regulation 2023/915 sets the maximum levels of metals in food for different groups of products. Cannabis seeds are not mentioned, however, it seems that regarding the pattern of use of hemp seeds the levels set for cereals (0.1 mg/kg wet weight for cadmium and 0.2 mg/kg wet weight for lead) may be relevant [88].

A number of studies performed on Cannabis as agricultural crop, where results come from fields in agricultural use, report heavy metals concentration below detection level or no risk for the population exposed to Cannabis [83,89–91].

Therefore, it seems that additional studies focused on heavy metals in Cannabis sativa consumed in food may be useful. Furthermore, in order to assess the health risks associated with contaminants in Cannabis sativa, data on the consumption of cannabis in food are needed. To date, such data are lacking in the literature. For this reason, preparing quantitative risk-benefit model with contaminants in Cannabis sativa requires further research.

6. Conclusions

The rapid growth observed in recent years in the market for products derived from hemp seeds can be linked to their interesting properties, as well as to the growing consumer interest in vegetarian and flexitarian diets. Hemp seeds present high nutritional value. They contain proteins with amino acid profile comparable to egg whites and soya. Hemp oil is a source of two essential fatty acids: linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid in desirable ratio of 3:1. Hemp seeds contain also carbohydrates, including fiber, minerals and vitamins.

Consumption of Cannabis seed may have positive health effects that include anticancer and anti-mutagenic effects, obesity prevention, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects as well as prevention of cardiovascular diseases.

Pesticides as well as mycotoxins are mentioned in the literature among the factors that may pose risk for humans consuming hemp. Those contaminants may however occur in numerous plants. A hazardous characteristic of hemp is its ability to absorb toxic heavy metals from the soil. Consequently, hemp seeds may contain elevated levels of heavy metals, in particular cadmium and lead. In addition, the seeds contain anti-nutritional compounds produced by the plant that include inhibitors of trypsin, condensed tannins, phytic acid, saponins, and cyanogenic glycosides.

The risks associated with consuming hemp in food can be reduced in various ways. Cultivation of hemp intended for human consumption only in the fields where the level of heavy metals is known to be safe seems quite an effective tool to address the risk regarding heavy metals. There is also significant potential in breeding. As regards hemp intended for human and possibly also for animal consumption breeding could be focused on development low-accumulation varieties as well as of varieties that enhance nutritional benefits while producing fewer anti-nutritional compounds.

The increase in production and, consequently, the availability of hemp-based food products on the market may be associated with a number of problems, including issues related to the supply chain, economic feasibility and the harmonisation of regulations around the world. There appear to be no technical obstacles to increase agricultural production. As regards its soil and climate requirements, hemp can be cultivated and indeed be a useful addition to crop rotation in many countries around the world. It therefore seems that a more harmonised regulatory environment may be crucial for the popularisation of this plant.
