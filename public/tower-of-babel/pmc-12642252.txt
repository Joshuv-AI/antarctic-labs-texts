Rational utilization of antibiotic prophylaxis in oral and maxillofacial surgery: a large scope review of recent data
Authors: Danial Mohammadpour, Farhad Ghorbani, Farzaneh Bozorg-Ghalati, Iraj Mohammadpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical site infections raise the risk of serious health issues following oral and maxillofacial surgeries. There is full consensus that using systemic antibiotics as a prophylactic measure can reduce infection risk in oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures, but the optimal duration and effective usage of these antibiotics remains debated. In light of growing antibiotic resistance, prophylactic use of antibiotics should be evaluated carefully and limited to certain scenarios. Thus, the aim of this scoping review was to summarize the available data regarding the prudent and evidence-based application of prophylactic antibiotics in the realm of oral and maxillofacial surgical interventions.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted using published studies from 2000 to 2025 across the Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar databases. We included 25 randomized controlled trials, 60 cohort studies, and 40 systematic reviews.ResultsAccording to the included articles, infection rates ranged from 0% to 62% depending on surgery type. Moreover, various antibiotic types and regimens were used, and in most studies, the follow-up period was short, and the outcomes were not clearly defined. The choice of prophylactic antibiotics depends on the specific surgical procedure and the level of contamination involved. The use of prophylactic antibiotics is advised for surgical extractions of third molars, temporomandibular joint replacements, and clean-contaminated tumor removal surgeries. In compound fractures of the mandible, maxillofacial fractures, and those communicating with paranasal sinuses, prescribing a postoperative course of antibiotics for at least 24 h is necessary, but extended use doesn’t lower infection rates. For dental implants, the evidence supporting prophylaxis in healthy individuals is weak. Prophylactic antibiotics are not routinely advised for fractures of the upper or midfacial thirds.ConclusionThe included studies exhibited substantial differences in design and objectives, making direct comparisons challenging. The findings underscore the importance of short-course, targeted prophylaxis over prolonged empirical regimens to curb resistance. Attention to antimicrobial stewardship and strict adherence to guidelines for antibiotic prophylaxis in oral and maxillofacial surgery are essential to reduce antibiotic misuse and improve the quality of study. More studies are required to provide recommendations for maxillofacial surgical interventions in immunocompromised patients.Keywords: Antibiotic prophylaxis, Oral, Maxillofacial, Surgery, Infections

Background

Millions of individuals worldwide are impacted by oral and maxillofacial surgeries annually [1]. Encompassing the area from the skull base to the hyoid bone, the maxillofacial region is of critical physiological significance [1]. It is connected to auditory, optic, speech, and olfactory systems, as well [1, 2]. Facial traumatic surgical interventions involve the maxillofacial bones, including those in the upper face, midface (zygoma, maxilla, orbit, and nose), and mandible [1, 3]. Non-traumatic procedures include third molar removals, implants, cleft lip and palate, and orthognathic surgeries [1, 3]. Currently, open reduction and internal fixation are employed to manage facial traumatic procedures to enhance stabilization, injury repair, and cosmetic outcomes. Although only 12% of the body’s surface area is comprised of the head, neck, and maxillofacial regions, they account for nearly 50% of all traumatic injuries [1, 4, 5]. One of the main objectives for the surgical treatment of maxillofacial injuries is to create conditions that support effective healing and reduce the risk of infection [6]. One frequent side effect of oral or maxillofacial procedures is surgical site infections. Typically, infection rates after oral and maxillofacial surgeries fall between 0% and 62% [7]. Surgical site infections occur either within a month after surgery or, in cases where implanted materials are used, within a year of the procedure [7].

Prophylactic antibiotic treatment is believed to have a significant impact on promoting ideal recovery after oral and maxillofacial surgery [8–11]. The decision to prescribe antibiotics is influenced by the patient’s oral and systemic health. The risk of infections is increased by periapical abscesses, severe periodontal disease, traumatic injuries with mucosal breach, and odontogenic diseases. Diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hematological disorders, cancer, malnutrition, obesity, smoking, alcohol use, aging, and a compromised immune system are all factors that raise infection risk, as do longer operations, urgent surgeries, and contaminated surgical wounds [7, 8, 10]. Moreover, acute viral infections can compromise the immune system and allow secondary bacterial infections that may require antibiotics [2]. In surgery, antibiotic prophylaxis is typically used only for procedures that carry a high risk of bacterial infection or when patient characteristics substantially raise the likelihood of an infection [7, 10, 11]. These consist of open mandibular or maxillary fractures, clean-contaminated intraoral mucosal surgeries, reconstructive flap-based oncological resections, and interventions in immunocompromised patients [7–11]. Clean-contaminated referred to an incision made through which the digestive or respiratory tracts were accessed under closely monitored conditions without any contamination [7, 10, 11].

Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics are typically given to patients with clean and clean‒contaminated facial fractures who are undergoing surgery [12, 13]. The perioperative period lasts from 2 h before surgery up to 24 h after surgery [14]. Various studies have confirmed that administering antibiotics perioperatively is effective in reducing infection rates associated with facial fractures [12, 13, 15]. Moreover, preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, given before the perioperative period, is thought to reduce the infection rate associated with mandibular fractures and is commonly employed in managing traumatic injuries [7, 16, 17]. Yet, there is ongoing debate surrounding the effectiveness of administering prophylactic antibiotics postoperatively for a prolonged period exceeding 24 h, with significant differences in practice depending on the surgeon’s preference [18–20].

Other variables that may affect the risk of infection and the choice of postoperative prophylaxis include the type of fracture, the reduction technique, and any preexisting clinical conditions. Patients with traumatic fractures of the maxillary, orbital, ethmoid, and frontal sinuses may face a higher risk of infection if they also have acute or chronic rhinosinusitis or a sinus outflow obstruction [21, 22]. Furthermore, fractures in teeth with openings connecting to the mouth and bacterial flora, along with fractures exposed to the skin, are more susceptible to infections and can hinder the healing process of the fracture [14, 23, 24]. Eventually, infections may occur four times more frequently in open reduction and internal fixation surgeries compared to closed reduction [19, 25, 26].

Nonetheless, administering antibiotics unnecessarily can result in potential dangers like toxicity, bacterial resistance, allergic reactions, antagonistic drug effects, perilous drug interactions, and infectious complications from superinfection [22–24]. Additionally, problems associated with antibiotic treatment can also lead to high expenses [10, 11]. Therefore, it is crucial to implement a rational approach supported by scientific evidence when prescribing these medications to determine the optimal timing of prophylactic antibiotic therapy, improve quality of life, and minimize complications. While multiple systematic reviews exist for specific procedures (e.g., third molars, mandibular fractures), there is a lack of a broad, overarching map of the evidence across the entire spectrum of oral and maxillofacial surgery. This scoping review aims to systematically map the available literature from 2000 to 2025 on antibiotic prophylaxis in oral and maxillofacial surgery, summarize key findings for different procedures, identify consensus and controversies, and highlight gaps for future research.

Methods

This large scope review was carried out following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (2020 edition).

A certified librarian peer-reviewed the search strategy. A comprehensive search of the existing literature was performed, and articles from the Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Web of Science (Clarivate) databases published between 2000 and 2025 were retrieved and evaluated. Boolean operator “AND” was employed to refine the search strategy. The keywords used for search were “antibiotic prophylaxis” AND “oral” AND “mandibular” AND “maxillofacial” AND “fracture” AND “surgery”. Furthermore, a manual search of more recent research articles and systematic reviews was carried out to improve the study’s accuracy and scientific backing.

We included published randomized controlled trials, cohort, pre-, peri-, and postoperative prophylactic antibiotic studies, and excluded case series and case reports due to inadequate patient numbers and the absence of a comparison group. Moreover, published abstracts, conference posters, letters, articles in non-English languages, and articles of which the full text was not available, were excluded. We placed no limitations on the type, dosage, duration, or method of antibiotic prophylaxis. All human prospective and retrospective clinical studies are included.

Participants in the study consisted of male and female patients of all ages who underwent surgical treatment for facial fractures. Facial fractures included non-pathological traumatic fractures of the mandible, maxilla, zygoma, nasal, lacrimal, vomer, palatine, orbital, ethmoid, or sphenoid bones. Treatment for facial fractures may involve open or closed reduction, either with or without internal fixation. Studies involving non-human subjects, patients with preexisting infections, or facial fractures related to animal bites (which have a greater risk of infection) were excluded. It is important to emphasize that the assessment criteria for undertaking this study encompassed published articles related to the use of antibiotic prophylaxis in oral and maxillofacial surgeries, sourced from trustworthy databases and available resources.

We utilized EndNote to build our reference list, taking extra care to remove redundancies and preserve the accuracy of the data. The search yielded references, and two authors (DM and FG) independently screened the titles and abstracts. A third reviewer (IM) was available for further discussion in the event of disagreement (i.e., unclear description of which fractures were treated surgically and which were treated non-surgically). Potentially pertinent articles were added for full-text screening after the title and abstract were chosen. The overall quality of evidence was assessed using the GRADE guidelines. A strong recommendation was established when the majority of the published literature of level I and IIa supported the recommendation. The recommendation was deemed weak when it was endorsed by the vast majority of level IIb published literature. This systematic review was not registered.

Results

A broad survey of published literature on this subject is presented in this study. The review process identified 542 papers. Of these, 261 duplicates, not full-text, and non-English articles were excluded. Of the remaining 281 papers, 125 were included in the final comprehensive review. Among these, 25 were randomized controlled trials, 60 were cohort studies, and 40 were systematic reviews. Categorization of surgical injuries based on contamination‒infection risk

Type I: clean injuries

Injuries that do not disrupt mucous membranes, such as those in the oral cavity, have an infection rate ranging from 1% to 4%. No prophylaxis or prophylaxis lasting no more than 24 h with amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (co-amoxiclav or Augmentin) is recommended, as there is no benefit (grade III) to administering postoperative antibiotics [27, 28].

Type II: Clean-contaminated injuries

Injuries that involve the disruption of the mucosa, such as those occurring in the oral cavity or surgeries performed in inflamed areas, exhibit an infection rate ranging from 5% to 15%. It is recommended to utilize co-amoxiclav, cefazolin in combination with clindamycin, or metronidazole for prophylaxis against Gram-positive and anaerobic bacteria. Co-amoxiclav is administered as 2 g; if surgery is prolonged, a repeat dose of 1 g every 4 h is recommended. For those who have an allergy to β-lactam antibiotics, a combination of clindamycin and gentamicin is administered, with a repeat dose every 4 h for extended surgical procedures [29–32].

Type III: contaminated injuries

Oncological surgery involving the oral cavity and the neck has an infection rate ranging from 16% to 25%. Prophylactic strategies for Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria not addressed in clean and clean-contaminated procedures are administered using medications such as ampicillin/sulbactam or piperacillin/tazobactam. Co-amoxiclav is administered as 2 g; if surgery is prolonged, a repeat dose of 1 g every 4 h is recommended. A combination of clindamycin and cefazolin is administered, with a repeat dose of clindamycin every 6 h and 1 g every 8 h of cefazolin for long-term surgical procedures [33, 34].

Type IV: infected injuries

Dirty and infected injuries exhibit an infection rate of 25%. Antibiotic treatment is consistently preferred over prophylaxis [35].

The application of antiseptics in the oral cavity decreases the bacterial count at the surgical location, though it has not demonstrated significant effectiveness in preventing bacterial colonization [36, 37].

Prophylactic antibiotics in various maxillofacial and oral surgical interventions

Pre-, peri-, and postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis is advised for patients who are at high risk of infection or who exhibit symptoms of infection. The selected antibiotic should work against the typical bacteria present in the oral cavity and cervicofacial skin, like Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Treponema, Prevotella, Aggregatibacter, Tannerella, Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium, Actinobacillus, and Eikenella. Therefore, co-amoxiclav and ampicillin/sulbactam provide broad coverage, although other choices such as first-generation (cephalexin, cefazolin, cefadroxil, and cefazedone), second-generation (cefuroxime, cefprozil, cefmetazole, cefotetan, cefoxitin, cefonicid, and ceforanide), and third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, cefoperazone, cefixime, ceftizoxime, cefpodoxime, ceftibuten, cefditoren, and cefdinir), quinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, delafloxacin, gemifloxacin, and ofloxacin), or clindamycin can also be utilized [10, 11, 27].

Preoperative and postoperative antiseptics like chlorhexidine and iodine can help increase wound healing and decrease complications from surgical trauma in the oral cavity, especially in patients with heart valve diseases, alloplastic implants, bone grafts, immunosuppression, the elderly, or poor oral hygiene [36, 37].

Oral surgery

Due to the low infection rate, healthy patients undergoing oral procedures generally do not require antibiotic prophylaxis. Usage will be limited to situations involving active infection (dental abscesses), patients with comorbidities, or immunosuppression [10, 11, 27, 38].

Third molar removals (clean‒contaminated surgery)

Seventeen studies contributed to the information on surgical site infection and antibiotic use in patients with third molar removals [10, 27, 39–53]. The surgical site infection rate after third molar removal varies, with reported rates typically ranging from 1% to 11% [45]. Lower third molar extractions, specifically, show infection rates from 1.5% to 5.8% in some studies [47, 49, 51, 53]. Prophylactic antibiotics might be prescribed for third molar extractions depending on the impaction depth, necessity of osteotomy, tissue trauma, and postoperative inflammation [10, 27, 42, 45]. Insufficient clear evidence about the best timing for prophylaxis (pre-, peri-, and postoperative) was obvious [42, 49]. Potential surgical complications consist of infection and alveolar osteitis [46, 49, 51]. Antibacterial drugs such as amoxicillin (500 mg PO q8h), co-amoxiclav (625 mg PO q8h), levofloxacin (500‒750 mg PO/IV once daily), moxifloxacin (400 mg PO/IV once daily), azithromycin (500 mg PO once daily), cefazolin (500 mg–1 g IM/IV q6‒8 h), ceftazidime (500 mg IM/IV q8‒12 h), clindamycin (600 mg PO q8h), and metronidazole (500 mg PO/IV q8h) have been studied in various publications [41, 44, 48, 50]. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have assessed the efficacy of systemic antibiotics in preventing dry socket and infections after third molar extractions [43, 44, 53]. They indicate that prophylactic antibiotics lowered the infection risk by 60% to 70% after extraction. Nevertheless, the side effects of antibiotics and the possible risks of antibiotic resistance should be taken into account [43, 44, 53]. Some authors appear to show the advantage of utilizing preventive antibiotics like co-amoxiclav for osteotomy procedures [42, 48, 50]. Dental implants

Nine clinical, systematic reviews and meta-analyses studies contributed to the information on surgical site infection and antibiotic use in patients undergoing dental implant procedures [27, 54–61]. The incidence of surgical site infection following dental implant placement is usually about 4% to 10% [53]. Dental implants serve as a durable cosmetic solution for individuals with partially or fully edentulous jaws. Success rates for implants after surgery range from 95 to 99%. Infection can significantly increase the risk of implant failure, and factors such as poor oral hygiene, surgical trauma, insufficient initial stability, smoking, diabetes, and the use of rough-surfaced implant collars can elevate this risk [54, 58, 60, 61]. Several studies indicate that preoperative preventive antibiotics can decrease the chance of implant failure by as much as 2% [27, 58, 59]. Consequently, the administration of prophylactic antibiotics is likely unnecessary for single-implant surgeries in healthy and low-risk individuals [55, 57, 61].

When prophylactic antibiotics are necessary, administering 2 g of preoperative amoxicillin has demonstrated a similar decrease in implant failure rates compared to 1 gram of preoperative amoxicillin followed by an extra 2-day postoperative regimen [60]. Considering that the chance of side effects from antibiotics increases with prolonged use, it is recommended to administer them preoperatively in this situation [27, 58, 60]. Prolonged antibiotic therapy following implant surgery has not been shown to improve the outcome of postoperative infections [54, 56]. Cleft lip and palate surgery

There is currently no strong consensus or conclusive evidence to firmly determine the effectiveness of prophylactic antibiotics in surgeries for cleft lip, palate, or alveolar bone grafting [62–67]. The rate of postoperative infection after surgery for cleft lip or palate is between 1.1 and 4.4% or even 5‒10% in more general oral and maxillofacial surgery contexts. Factors such as the specific surgical protocol, patient age, and type of cleft (e.g., complete or bilateral clefts) can influence infection risk [65].

According to some studies, most surgeons use antibiotic prophylaxis for cleft lip repair, but far fewer use it for cleft palate repair. Until recently, two studies assessed postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis (amoxicillin 500 mg PO q8h) for cleft palate surgical procedures. In this cohort of patients, giving antibiotics for 5 days postoperatively reduced the incidence of fistulas [66, 67]. In a retrospective study, all patients received a single intravenous dose of cefuroxime (750 mg if < 50 kg, 1.5 g if > 50 kg q4h) during the perioperative period [64]. Maxillofacial traumatic fractures

Twenty-six studies contributed to the information on surgical site infection and antibiotic use in patients with maxillofacial fractures [3, 7–13, 21, 24, 26–28, 68–80]. The overall pooled surgical site infection rate for maxillofacial fracture surgery is 5.6% in one meta-analysis [4], but range from 1% to 33% depending on the study and fracture type [4, 28]. Factors influencing infection risk include the fracture’s location, and patient-related issues like diabetes, smoking, compulsive drug abuse, and alcohol use. Complications can range from minor cellulitis treatable with antibiotics to severe long-term issues like plate loosening, chronic osteomyelitis, and pseudarthrosis, particularly with severe infections [3, 26–28]. The choice to apply antibiotic prophylaxis in maxillofacial surgeries relies on the specific kind of fracture (open or closed), the intended treatment, and the site of the fracture [24, 26]. In this context, antibiotic prophylaxis is believed to combat bacteria that infiltrate the wound before the operation [27, 68]. Multiple systematic reviews revealed a significant reduction in infection rates when preoperative antibiotics (either 2 g of amoxicillin or penicillin V potassium) were used in patients suffering from maxillofacial fractures [8, 27, 71]. Postoperative antibiotics given over 24 h after maxillofacial fracture surgery showed no substantial advantage [8, 24, 27, 28, 73, 76]. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that adding postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis to a typical pre- and/or perioperative antibiotic schedule revealed no notable difference in the risk of infection [71]. Postoperative treatment often includes drugs like co-amoxiclav, ampicillin/sulbactam, cephalosporins (second or third generation), and quinolones. For individuals with penicillin allergies, clindamycin is used to prevent infection [27].

Nasal bone fracture is the most prevalent type of fracture in maxillofacial injuries. The surgical site infection rate in nasal bone fracture surgery is between 2% and 4% for closed reductions. Open fractures have significantly higher infection rates, as high as 16% or more, but this is less common. Nasal bone fracture is typically managed through closed reduction, necessitating a splint within the nasal cavity to support the repositioned bone piece [21, 24]. To maintain the reduced condition, external materials such as Merocel or Vaseline-gauze are frequently used as internal splints. Using nasal packing introduces a possibility of infection and may cause trauma to the nasal mucosa [78–80]. Postoperative prophylactic antibiotic treatment is carried out because of worries regarding infection-related issues like toxic shock syndrome and acute sinusitis following nasal packing. Cephalexin (Keflex, 500 mg PO q12h) and cefazedone (Refosporen, 500 mg IM/IV q8h) are commonly administered as postoperative prophylactic antibiotics to prevent infections caused by nasal packing [78–80]. Temporomandibular joint replacements

The information on antibiotic use and surgical site infection in patients undergoing temporomandibular joint surgery was compiled from seven studies [81–87]. Surgery on the temporomandibular joint was characterized as encompassing total joint replacements and arthrocentesis, arthroscopic surgery, and condyle removal. After a complete replacement of the temporomandibular joint, infections occur in a small percentage of patients, specifically between 1.5 and 4.5%. These infections are primarily caused by S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and Peptostreptococcus species. Some authors recommend that giving antibiotic prophylaxis intravenously during the perioperative phase, beginning within the first hour before surgery, lowers the chance of surgical site infection in total joint replacement [81–87]. Possible prophylactic antibiotics for temporomandibular joint replacement surgery consist of the perioperative application of cefazolin (0.5–1 g IV q6‒8 h), cefuroxime (1.5 g IV q4h), clindamycin (300‒900 mg IV q8h), and vancomycin (1 g IV q12h). Therapeutic levels of these antibiotics must be sustained until the surgical closures are completed. Mandibular and dentoalveolar fractures

Surgical site infection rates for mandibular fractures varied from 3.3% to 43.9%. Twenty-six studies contributed to the information on surgical site infection and antibiotic use in patients with mandibular and dentoalveolar fractures [15–20, 23, 25–28, 31, 35, 88–100]. Mandibular fractures usually occur due to trauma. This could entail a tumble onto the chin or a hit from the side. Rarely, osteonecrosis or bone tumors may be the cause of them. Data indicates that condyle fractures account for 36% of all fractures in this area, with the body, angle, and symphysis comprising 21%, 20%, and 14% respectively. Infrequently, a jaw fracture may occur in the ramus (affecting about 3% of cases) or even less frequently, in the coronoid process (around 2%). Dentoalveolar fractures refer to injuries impacting the teeth and the adjacent bone that secures them in position. Surgery to correct the issue might involve methods like wiring the jaws together or using plates and screws to fix the bones [19, 25, 26, 31, 89, 93, 94].

Established protocols for peri- and postoperative antibiotic administration in the management of mandibular and dentoalveolar fractures typically include agents such as penicillin (500 mg PO q12h), co-amoxiclav, ampicillin/sulbactam (1.5 g to 3 g IM/IV q6h), piperacillin/tazobactam (3.375 g IV q6h), ticarcillin/clavulanic acid (3.2 g IV q6‒8h), and third-generation cephalosporins [92, 93, 98, 99]. Many surgeons continue to use antibiotics for non-complicated fractures because they may reduce the risk of infection, even though there is no apparent improvement in treatment outcomes [20, 92]. During the first 72 h, antibiotic treatment is not required [97]. Antibiotics are widely agreed upon for treating infection complications such as abscesses, pseudarthrosis, osteomyelitis, and assault victims [26, 27, 99, 100]. However, in this scenario, the focus is on treatment rather than prevention [99]. Orbital wall fractures

The surgical site infection rate for orbital wall fractures varies by study, but a meta-analysis indicates it is around 1.77% [73]. However, other studies have reported higher rates, such as 3.92% in one analysis of orbital floor fractures, and 6.8% in another research [6, 22]. Orbital wall surgery is considered a clean-contaminated procedure due to communication with the airway, making infection a concern that can lead to serious complications like orbital cellulitis or blindness [101].

Different authors have conflicting opinions on the use of antibiotics, with some advocating for it while others oppose it [6, 22, 101–105]. Cephalexin and co-amoxiclav are the most commonly prescribed oral antibiotics that are similarly effective. Courses of antibiotics lasting 5 to 7 days showed no higher rates of infection than those lasting 10 to 14 days [104].

Third midface and upper fractures

Surgical site infection rates for fractures of the midface and upper facial third varied from 1% to 12.5%. Third-generation cephalosporins are given for treating liquorrhea immediately after these fractures occur, even though many fractures in this facial region are deemed complex or compounded [6, 14, 19, 24, 106–108]. In a retrospective study, the use of prolonged postoperative antibiotics for uncomplicated midfacial fractures revealed no consequential benefit in lowering infection rates [107].

Orthognathic and pre-prosthetic surgeries

The likelihood of getting an infection after orthognathic surgery varies quite a bit, with studies showing infection rates between roughly 1.4% and 33.4% [109, 110]. Twelve studies contributed to the data on surgical site infection and antibiotic use in patients with orthognathic and pre-prosthetic surgeries [27, 109–119].

Orthognathic surgery was characterized to encompass reconstructive methods, maxillomandibular progress, and surgical adjustments for facial asymmetry. These surgeries are classified as clean-contaminated [27]. It seems that surgeries involving the reconstruction of mandible have a higher risk of postoperative infection when compared to other facial surgeries [110, 112, 114, 115]. Certain problems have shown the advantages of using postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis (such as penicillin and cephalosporins), but this does not enhance the outcome and is costlier [112, 114, 115]. Other researchers suggest that there is no evidence supporting the effectiveness of prolonged postoperative antibiotics, particularly when administered orally, in enhancing infection prognosis [113, 114, 116–118]. A greater number of infections occur in bimaxillary surgery cases when preoperative antibiotic treatment is not given [111]. More studies are needed to evaluate the use of pre-, peri-, or postoperative prophylactic antibiotics for orthognathic surgery.

In mandibular osteotomies, cefazolin has been administered intravenously, or levofloxacin and moxifloxacin orally; however, it is preferred to use co-amoxiclav in the latter due to the significant resistance of bacteria to cefazolin [109, 110, 112, 114, 119].

The previous guidelines recommended a five-day course of antibiotic prophylaxis, but there is no difference in postoperative infection rates between one day and five days [113, 118]. However, there is a particular decrease in morbidity when continuing prophylaxis for five days [113, 116]. Salivary glands

Surgical site infection rates after parotidectomy can vary significantly in studies, with reported rates ranging from approximately 2% to over 11%. Factors that can increase the risk of infection include concomitant neck dissection, high drain output, smoking, and the presence of a malignant tumor [120–124].

Current medical practice does not support the routine use of prophylactic antibiotics for parotid gland surgery [120–124]. To reduce the risk of infection, perioperative antibiotic therapy is indicated for patients having extensive parotidectomy and neck dissection [120]. Potential prophylactic antibiotics for surgeries involving salivary glands include cefazolin or clindamycin for patients with penicillin allergies [120, 121].

Oncological, reconstructive, and cervical surgery

Surgical site infection rates for general head and neck cancer procedures are about 22.7%, while rates for head and neck reconstructive surgeries are about 46%. This increased risk is due to the clean-contaminated nature of the surgical field, where the oropharyngeal bacterial flora can easily contaminate the surgical wound, especially after large cancer resections. Factors increasing the risk include preoperative radiotherapy, higher tumor stage, comorbidities, longer surgery duration, and the complexity of microvascular reconstruction [29–33, 125–130].

Saliva, skin, teeth, and the tumor itself are the origins of bacteria in these diseases. Hence, the selected antibiotic must be able to target the common saliva bacteria such as Gram-positive and anaerobes, as well as the Gram-negative bacteria commonly present in tumors [125–130]. Most studies in head and neck oncological surgery do not focus on Gram-negative bacteria, but there is growing concern that preventing infections from these bacteria can lead to better results [34, 129].

Pre- and perioperative antibiotic administration has been demonstrated to reduce the risk of post-surgical infections. Potential options for antibiotic prophylaxis in the field of cervical pathology and oncological surgery include clindamycin combined with cefazolin, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, quinolones, or penicillin derivatives with β-lactamase inhibitors [31–33, 125–127].

Cefuroxime plus metronidazole, and gentamicin (5 mg/kg IM/IV daily) plus clindamycin are prophylactic antibiotics that effectively target Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria. Cefazolin is ineffective against anaerobic bacteria. Additional options include cefoperazone (2 g IV q8h), cefotaxime (2 g IV q8h), moxalactam (2 g IV q8h), co-amoxiclav and ampicillin/sulbactam, which possess similar properties. The length of the treatment varies depending on the surgeon and is not well standardized. Postoperative antibiotics will be continued until drainage ceases, and may be extended in cases of surgical site infection, wound reopening, or fistula formation [27, 31–33, 125, 126].

Discussion

This review investigates the rationale behind the use of prophylactic antibiotics in oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures. We believe that the rigorous inclusion criteria improved our study’s strength and produced a more accurate representation of the available data. Nonetheless, there was notable heterogeneity even among the included studies due to differences in study design, antibiotic type, surgical treatment, fracture type and location, follow-up period, and outcome. Prophylactic antibiotic therapy is characterized by administering antibiotics before, during, or following a diagnostic, therapeutic, or surgical intervention to avert infectious complications. A careful assessment is needed when deciding to give prophylactic antibiotics after surgery. This should weigh the risks, like promoting antibiotic-resistant bacteria and adverse drug reactions, against the potential benefit of preventing a postoperative surgical site infection at the fracture site. Certain patients may gain advantages from antibiotic prophylaxis after surgery due to underlying health issues that increase their infection risk. This involves a fracture in a tooth-supporting area of the mandible, a notable lag between the injury and the surgical intervention, a decomposed tooth present at the fracture site, severe periodontal disease, and a background of malnutrition, tobacco use, diabetes, or compromised immune function [23, 97, 111]. Miles et al. showed that the concurrent use of tobacco and alcohol was more common among patients with infection [89]. Moreover, they determined that race had a significant correlation with the occurrence of infections and proposed that socioeconomic factors might influence a patient’s ability to follow their medication regimen [89].

Several oral traits might affect the possibility of infection and the ensuing requirement for prophylactic antibiotics after surgery. Existing periodontal disease and the extraction of third molars can heighten the risk of postoperative surgical site infections in facial fracture repairs, particularly for maxillomandibular fractures that undergo fixation [93, 94, 111, 115]. Fractures occurring in a tooth-supporting area have a greater risk of infection compared to other regions, like the subcondyle [23]. Domingo et al. discovered that individuals with traumatic dental injuries faced a markedly elevated risk of surgical site infection in contrast to those with intact teeth, likely due to subsequent contamination from oral bacteria [96]. Research indicates that the position of fractures in the dental arch holds clinical importance, with the area of the third molar being the most prone to infection [23]. Ultimately, a patient’s oral care habits can significantly influence the effective recovery from oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures [96].

The gender and age of patients can affect their immunity and healing process. Older patients aged over 65 years frequently have multiple coexisting conditions such as diabetes, chronic hypertension, heart failure, and kidney disease, all of which can affect postoperative morbidity. Moreover, older patients tend to experience more periodontal disease, and the disease is often more severe. A significant risk factor for surgical site infections after maxillomandibular surgeries in older adults is existing periodontal disease [110–112, 119].

The presence of overlying soft tissue injuries, the surgical approach taken, the fracture type, and the exact fracture site all significantly influence a surgeon’s choice regarding the use of prophylactic antibiotics and may affect the occurrence of surgical site infections after surgery [8, 14, 20]. A significant number of surgeons consistently give prophylactic antibiotics irrespective of the fracture and patient traits. Even though every study used β-lactam antibiotics, the specific antibiotic agents that were given differed significantly. Cefazolin is the preferred antibiotic when the source of pathogens is skin flora, as it targets Gram-positive cocci. If the oropharyngeal flora is the origin of pathogens, broad-spectrum antibiotics might be necessary (co-amoxiclav, or cephazolin with metronidazole) [10, 27, 28]. Abubaker et al. used penicillin G and penicillin V, antibiotics that solely target Streptococcus species, which may account for their inconsequential study findings [18].

According to the evidence available at present, the use of prophylactic antibiotics is advised for the surgical removal of the third molar, crushed mandibular fractures, temporomandibular joint replacements, complex implants requiring grafts or multiple implants, and in cases of clean-contaminated tumor excisions. There is no consensus on whether continuing antibiotic prophylaxis beyond 24 h is beneficial for patients with upper or midface fractures [71, 131].

In dentoalveolar surgery, research indicates that administering prophylactic antibiotics can greatly decrease the occurrence of surgical site infections [51, 52]. Prophylactic antibiotics can be utilized to avert dry alveolar sockets and thus lessen postoperative complications [42–44, 53]. In specific situations where perioperative antibiotic treatment is necessary, amoxicillin is equally effective in preventing infection as co-amoxiclav [44, 48, 50]. Essentially, when it comes to preventing surgical site infections, the medications amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav, clindamycin, cefazolin, and azithromycin combined with metronidazole all performed similarly [44, 48, 50].

In dental implant procedures, antibiotics serve a double purpose: to ward off infections following surgery and to boost the chances of the implants remaining stable and functional long-term. To reduce the risk of implant failure, there are issues related to the extensive use of antibiotics, as adverse effects can happen [54]. Additionally, it appears that antibiotic prophylaxis is warranted when dental implants are to be inserted right after tooth extraction in areas affected by chronic periapical infections [58, 61]. In complicated situations such as prompt placement in extraction sockets and/or a patient with health issues, the possible positive impact of antibiotic prophylaxis shouldn’t be disregarded. Administering a single dose of 2 g of amoxicillin either intravenously or orally one hour before surgery could decrease the risk of implant failure and help avoid postoperative pain [59–61].

For individuals experiencing dental abscesses, moxifloxacin demonstrated comparable effectiveness to clindamycin in alleviating pain, achieving positive clinical outcomes, and exhibiting a similar safety profile. In cases of drug-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, prolonged treatment with ampicillin/sulbactam could result in full recovery, thus minimizing postoperative infections and the likelihood of disease recurrence [10, 12, 27].

Recent findings indicate that periodontal disease present at the time of orthognathic surgery is a major risk factor for infections at the surgical site. Multiple studies have shown that prophylactic antibiotics administered before orthognathic surgery are effective in reducing the incidence of postoperative infections [110, 112, 115]. Danda et al. discovered that extended use of antibiotics is more effective in reducing the risk of postoperative infections [132]. For bilateral sagittal split osteotomy, a short postoperative antibiotic regimen is more beneficial than just one preoperative dose [133]. In contrast, Oomens et al. determine that extra continuous antibiotics after surgery provide no advantages [110]. Furthermore, Tan et al. assessed the use for an extended postoperative period and found it to be not advisable [109]. Several studies assessed that cefazolin is more effective than penicillin and clindamycin when antibiotic treatment is advised [111, 112]. Preoperative antibiotics are generally recommended in orthognathic surgery; however, it is still unknown which regimen is most effective and whether longer treatment is beneficial [112, 114].

In facial fractures, the site and intensity can differ and may encompass various wound categories such as clean, clean-contaminated, contaminated, and dirty [68]. Often, surgical wounds become contaminated with bacterial flora from the oral cavity and sinuses. Consequently, these procedures are categorized as clean-contaminated wounds, which puts them at a higher risk for postoperative wound infections [29, 31, 32]. Overall, prophylactic antibiotics are both warranted and commonly utilized [24, 93, 94]. As a result, several studies showed that prophylactic antibiotics were given during the perioperative period for Le Fort-I/II fractures [23, 24]. Furthermore, sufficient evidence supports the use of prophylactic perioperative antibiotics for all procedures involving open reduction and internal fixation, which are defined as antibiotics given less than 2 h before the start of surgery and not extending beyond 24 h after surgery [21, 24, 28, 68, 93, 94]. Moreover, a single day of postoperative antibiotics proved effective in preventing infection complications; however, no further advantages of prolonged postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis were identified [28, 68, 71, 73, 97–99, 107]. Extended antibiotic prophylaxis might lead to postponed infectious difficulties and antimicrobial resistance. Zosa et al. reported a markedly higher risk of infection for patients undergoing extended antibiotic prophylaxis lasting more than 7 days [134, 135]. Miles et al. showed that patients who underwent prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis experienced late infectious difficulties [89]. Delaplain et al. verified an increased incidence of surgical site infection when antibiotic prophylaxis exceeded 72 h [73].

Given that the infection rate was zero in orbitozygomatic fractures, a study challenges the necessity of using antibiotics in this context [6]. Conversely, in the case of temporomandibular joint replacement, perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis effectively minimizes surgical site infection [81–83, 87]. Several studies revealed no notable differences among various antibiotic regimens such as amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav, cefazolin, cefotaxime, clindamycin, and piperacillin/tazobactam [9, 10, 106, 107]. As a result, prolonged postoperative antibiotic therapies cannot be advised [85–87].

For surgery involving cleft lip, palate, and alveolus, only limited data could be identified. Postoperative fistula formation rate served as the primary determinant of the procedure’s effectiveness. In this context, antibiotic prophylaxis administered as a single dose (cefuroxime) or a 5-day regimen (amoxicillin) demonstrated a decrease in the frequency of fistula formation [66, 67]. Moreover, the occurrence of wound infections was minimal, even in the absence of postoperative antibiotics [64]. Antibiotic prophylaxis is not currently recommended.

Multiple investigations on mandibular fractures did not indicate whether they encompassed open or closed fractures [19, 23, 70, 98, 107]. Many studies have indicated that an open fracture could result in a four times greater infection rate [8, 96]. It is advised in mandibular fractures to maintain antibiotic prophylaxis for 24 h after the lesion has been closed, and not to extend beyond that period [15, 18, 19, 23, 70, 89, 96, 98, 107]. Several studies have shown that a 24-hour course of antibiotic prophylaxis provides adequate antibacterial protection for mandibular fractures [8, 10, 20, 21, 27, 28, 71, 73, 92, 99]. Recent surveys have verified that surgeons do not consistently follow these guidelines, and a significant proportion still extend the duration of antibiotic prophylaxis [20, 21]. A survey of surgeons performing open reduction and internal fixation of mandibular fractures showed that the vast majority use systemic antibiotics. The study indicated that approximately three-quarters of surgeons prescribe antibiotics for up to three days (around 44%), while slightly more than half (around 55%) continue antibiotic treatment for a week. A very small percentage (just over 1%) extend antibiotic use beyond a week when treating open fractures of the mandible [20]. Additionally, when treating closed fractures, surgeons show varying preferences for antibiotic duration. Just over half (51%) prescribe antibiotics, with three-day (50.5%) and one-week (48.6%) regimens being the most frequent choices. Prolonged use of antibiotics, exceeding a week, is uncommon (1%) [20].

It’s worth asking why the recommendations mentioned above have not been incorporated into our everyday clinical practice. Current guidelines rely primarily on a small number of clinical studies, leaving some uncertainty about the best choice and duration of systemic antibiotic prophylaxis. Thus, there remains global heterogeneity in prevention protocols. Due to the microbiome found in the oral cavity and sinuses, maxillofacial surgeons worry about the fracture site becoming contaminated with normal flora, which could result in infection, and they naturally believe that there might be an advantage to extended antibiotic treatment [20]. Given the limited high-quality data on procedures that involve clean-contaminated conditions, this issue remains controversial within the maxillofacial community.

Present guidelines do suggest the preventive use of antibiotics for patients having head and neck surgery involving an incision through the oral, nasopharyngeal, or esophageal mucosa. The suggested protocol is cefuroxime along with metronidazole before the surgical incision for procedures involving mucosal surfaces [29, 31, 32]. Besides surgical technique, the risk of wound infection after head and neck cancer surgery is influenced by several other key factors. These factors include how much the tumor has progressed, any previous radiotherapy or chemotherapy, the length of the patient’s hospital stay before surgery, whether there is a permanent tracheostomy, and whether the cancer originated in the pharynx or larynx [125, 127, 129]. Furthermore, risk factors such as the level of contamination (from clean to dirty) play a role in influencing the rate of infection. In clean-contaminated oncologic head and neck surgeries, high-risk patients, such as those with complex congenital cyanotic conditions, should receive perioperative antibiotics [125]. Thus, perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis holds critical significance in head and neck surgery [31, 125].

Additionally, prophylactic antibiotics are recommended for patients having reconstructive procedures for head and neck cancer [30, 34, 126, 128–130]. The regimens offered consist of ampicillin/sulbactam, co-amoxiclav, cefazolin, or clindamycin [29–31, 34, 125]. There is little evidence that perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis can last more than 24 h [31, 33, 125, 130]. A potential indication for extended therapy might be observed in microvascular free-flap reconstruction, with inconsistent findings about whether perioperative prophylaxis or a 3–5-day regimen is more successful in lowering surgical site infection [30, 128, 129]. In clean head and neck surgeries, the use of antibiotic prophylaxis is unnecessary [136].

Giving antibiotics before tympanostomy might improve outcomes, based on current evidence [136]. However, prophylactic antibiotics did not have any effect on outcome in clean myringoplasty [137]. In parotid gland surgery, a single dose of cefazolin as prophylaxis is adequate to lower the risk of surgical site infection [120–124].

For patients at high risk of endocarditis who are undergoing oral and maxillofacial surgery and face a risk of bacteremia, co-amoxiclav is the antibiotic of choice. Alternative treatments for high risk endocarditis patients with penicillin allergies include clindamycin, clarithromycin, or azithromycin. Any procedure involving the oral or maxillofacial region should be preceded by prophylactic treatment for patients who are at high or moderate risk. The recommendations advise taking the medication either orally one hour before the procedure, or intravenously thirty minutes before [138–140].

Eventually, extended courses of systemic antibiotic prophylaxis may be linked to a range of adverse effects, including skin rashes, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, general malaise and fatigue, and Clostridioides difficile infection [72]. Prolonged use of antibiotic prophylaxis might also lead to delayed infectious complications [73, 89, 135]. As far as we know, no potential explanation for this phenomenon has been identified in the literature to date, and more investigation is needed to confirm these results.

Finally, excessive and/or inappropriate antibiotic use is a key driver of the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance [141, 142]. Antimicrobial resistance develops as a natural evolution to antimicrobial exposure, enabling bacteria to resist drugs through mutations in their own chromosomal genes and through the horizontal transfer of genes [141, 142]. The global rise of antimicrobial resistance could threaten our capacity to treat current and rising infectious diseases, and could also undermine numerous other advances in healthcare [141]. Maxillofacial surgeons, just like other healthcare professionals, must recognize that antimicrobial resistance is a worldwide public health issue. Antimicrobial stewardship ensures antibiotics are used properly according to globally recognized guidelines, enabling effective infection prevention and treatment while minimizing the adverse effects associated with antibiotic use [143].

Limitations

The principal restriction of this scoping review is the heterogeneity in the evidence present in the literature. The considerable heterogeneity observed was caused by differences in study design, lack of randomized controlled trials, the characteristics of patient populations, the type and duration of antibiotics used, the chosen surgical treatments, the fracture types and locations, how surgical site infection was defined, the length of follow-up, and the reported outcomes. The outcome measures were largely defined with vague criteria and insufficient follow-up durations. Follow-up durations differed considerably, spanning from under one week to six months, with the median around six weeks. This implies that many studies likely missed infections that appear later. Retrospective cohort studies employed a broad range of antibiotics or combinations, and they did not explain why different patients received different antibiotic types.

Many studies on mandibular fractures and fractures of the midface and upper facial third did not specify whether the fractures were open or closed. There is a requirement for rigorous research to assess the advantages of initiating antibiotic prophylaxis at the time of admission for patients with open maxillofacial fractures. In many of the studies encompassed by this review, details about surgical fracture stability and the timing of fracture fixation were frequently inadequate or insufficiently described. The existing literature does not provide data on the pathogens identified by culture at the time of infection diagnosis. As a result, the available evidence is limited, so the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In our view, the rigorous inclusion criteria improved the study’s robustness and yielded a more accurate representation of the existing evidence. Current evidence supports giving prophylactic antibiotics in certain dental and maxillofacial surgical scenarios, including third molar extraction, smashed mandible fracture, temporomandibular joint replacement, complex implant procedures that involve grafting or multiple implants, and clean-contaminated tumor resections.

Prophylactic antibiotics during the perioperative period may decrease the risk of surgical site infections in significant operations like clean-contaminated head and neck oncological surgery. In orthognathic surgery, the use of preoperative antibiotics may lower the risk of surgical site infections; however, the advantages of extended therapy and the most effective regimen are still unclear. In maxillofacial surgery, using antibiotic prophylaxis during the perioperative period for mandibular and Le Fort-I/II fractures may lower the risk of surgical site infections, but longer postoperative antibiotic courses beyond 24 h do not produce the same effect.

For dental implants, a single dose of prophylactic antibiotic given before placement could decrease the risk of dental implant failure, though it may not impact surgical site infection. In the case of third molar extraction, the advantages of perioperative antibiotic treatment for healthy individuals remain unclear. Future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials, especially in less-explored fields such as cleft lip and palate surgery.

Maxillofacial surgeons should adjust antibiotic prophylaxis and treatment plans based on the patient’s overall health, the type of injuries, and the status of the bone and surrounding soft tissues. Treatment options must take into account the available standardized guidelines concerning the specific situations and risks associated with each patient.

The excessive and inappropriate use of antibiotics is a key factor fueling the development and propagation of antimicrobial resistance. Antimicrobial resistance represents a major health issue on a global scale. Antimicrobial stewardship advocates for the proper use of antibiotics in line with globally recognized guidelines, supporting effective infection prevention and treatment while reducing the negative consequences of antibiotic use.
