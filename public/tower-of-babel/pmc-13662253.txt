Effect of Hand Grip Exercise on Rotator Cuff Strength and Shoulder Function Recovery after Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Byeong Sun Park, Seung Ik Cho, Da Hui Jeong, Hailey Nam, Ji Seung Yoo, Dong Hwan Lee, Seok Won Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of hand grip exercise on rotator cuff strength and shoulder function after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair.MethodsNinety patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair were randomly allocated into the grip exercise group (n = 45) and control group (n = 45). The hand grip strengthening exercise (3 sets of 15 repetitions, twice daily) was applied only to the grip exercise group immediately after surgery up to 3 months. From 3 months postoperatively, the same rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strengthening exercises were applied to both groups. The isometric strength of the shoulder joint (abductor, external rotator, and internal rotator) and periscapular muscles, tone and stiffness of the upper trapezius muscle, range of motion (ROM), pain visual analog scale (VAS) score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, Constant score, and University of California, Los Angeles score were measured preoperatively and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Changes in the supraspinatus muscle atrophy and repair integrity were assessed via magnetic resonance imaging at 6 months postoperatively.ResultsAt 3 months, abductor, external rotator, and internal rotator muscle strengths were not significantly different between groups, but at 6 months, the grip exercise group showed significantly higher external rotator and internal rotator strengths (all p < 0.05). Similarly, the periscapular muscle strengths were not different at 3 months, but the serratus anterior muscle strength was higher in the grip exercise group at 6 months (p = 0.034). At 12 months postoperatively, the differences in the external rotator and internal rotator muscle strengths, and serratus anterior muscle strength disappeared. There were no significant differences in the tone and stiffness of the upper trapezius muscle, ROM, pain VAS, and functional scores. In addition, changes in the supraspinatus muscle atrophy and the repair integrity were not different between groups.ConclusionsHand grip exercise affected the rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strengths at 6 months after rotator cuff repair. The grip exercises can effectively and safely promote a faster recovery of shoulder muscle strength and function, without compromising rotator cuff healing.Keywords: Rotator cuff tear, Muscle strength, Rehabilitation

Rotator cuff tears are one of the primary causes of shoulder pain, with the incidence of rotator cuff injuries increasing with age. Treatment options for rotator cuff tears initially involve conservative treatment including medication, local steroid injections, and physical therapy. If conservative treatments are ineffective, surgical intervention may be considered, and arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) has been reported to effectively improve shoulder function and alleviate pain for patients with rotator cuff tears.1)

Traditional postoperative protocols for ARCR recommend immobilization for approximately 4 to 6 weeks to promote tendon healing, which can induce muscle weakness around the shoulder joint. While safe exercise interventions to minimize muscle weakness after ARCR have not been clearly established, standard rehabilitation protocols do not focus on the recovery of strength until adequate early tendon-to-bone healing has occurred in the initial phase of up to 3 months.2) The prolonged rotator cuff muscle weakness can lead to an imbalance in muscle action during movement, potentially resulting in secondary compression to the tendon, and delaying the return to daily activities. Therefore, it is important to strengthen shoulder muscles within a range that does not place an excessive load on the surgical site.

Meanwhile, hand grip strengthening exercise is known as an effective intervention that does not impose a significant load on the tendon while increasing activation of deep shoulder muscles.3) Grip strength reflects upper limb function and has been linked to overall health outcomes, including disease and survival risk.4) Previous studies have shown a positive relationship between rotator cuff strength and grip strength in healthy individuals, and a recent study by AlAnazi et al.5) reported that adding hand grip exercise to conventional intervention effectively improves shoulder function, pain, muscle strength, and range of motion (ROM) in patients with subacromial impingement syndrome.

However, evidence regarding the effects of grip exercise following ARCR is limited. Thus, we hypothesized that hand grip exercise during the early postoperative period would result in greater recovery of rotator cuff strength and shoulder function at mid-term follow-up compared with conventional treatment after ARCR.

METHODS

This was a prospective randomized controlled study. The Institutional Review Board of the Konkuk University Hospital approved the study protocol (IRB No. KUMC 2023-11-045), and all patients provided informed consent. Written informed consent for publication of the clinical images was obtained from all patients whose images are included in this article.

Patient Enrollment and Allocation

Between February and September 2023, 100 patients who received ARCR for repairable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears were evaluated for study eligibility, and 10 of them were excluded (history of shoulder surgery, n = 2; workers’ compensation status, n = 2; isolated subscapularis tears, n = 3; and patients who did not agree to participate in the study, n = 3). The remaining 90 patients were randomly assigned to the grip exercise group or conventional treatment group (45 patients in each group) according to an automatically generated randomization list (www.randomizer.org), and allocation concealment was maintained until group assignment. Blinding of patients and therapists was not feasible because the rehabilitation protocol required active patient participation. Among these 90 patients, 4 in the grip exercise group and 2 in the conventional group were lost before the 1-year postoperative follow-up and were excluded. Finally, 41 in the grip exercise group and 43 in the conventional group were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart.

Demographic and Clinical Data

We prospectively collected and assessed multiple demographic and clinical factors that might impact the results: age, sex, height, weight, body mass index, preoperative stiffness, hand dominance, tear size, bone mineral density, and fatty infiltration of each rotator cuff muscle. We defined shoulder stiffness as passive forward elevation under 120°, passive external rotation less than 30°, and internal rotation limited below the L3 level.6) Arthroscopic measurement of tear size was performed intraoperatively using a calibrated probe, and the results were categorized using the DeOrio and Cofield system.7) The patients’ bone mineral density values were measured just before surgery by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (Horizon W, version 13.6.0.7; Hologic) and the minimal T-score between the proximal femur and lumbar spine was noted, with the Ward area of the femur excluded. Fatty degeneration in the rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis) was analyzed from preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) following the Goutallier classification8) as modified by Fuchs et al.9) Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a 2-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Measurements were independently performed once by 1 musculoskeletal radiologist (SGM) and 1 orthopedic surgeon (SWC) with more than 20 years of experience, both blinded to clinical information and outcomes. The reliability of the measurement of the fatty infiltration in each rotator cuff muscle was generally acceptable, with an ICC of 0.823 for the supraspinatus, 0.711 for the infraspinatus, and 0.654 for the subscapularis (all p < 0.001).

Surgical Procedures and Postoperative Rehabilitation

All surgeries were conducted arthroscopically with patients positioned in the beach-chair position, and a single surgeon (SWC) performed all procedures. To create a flat acromial surface, subacromial decompression and acromioplasty were carried out in every case. Depending on the presence of dislocation, subluxation, and tears exceeding 50% of the long head of the biceps tendon or for a symptomatic degenerative superior labral anterior and posterior lesion, as well as age or activity level, a biceps tenotomy or tenodesis was selectively performed. A suture-bridge technique in a double-row configuration was applied for all repairs, and none of the patients required distal clavicle excision or coracoplasty.

An abduction brace was used for shoulder immobilization postoperatively, typically for 4 to 6 weeks depending on tear size measured at the time of surgery. Shrugging of shoulders, active forearm supination and pronation, and active hand and wrist motion were encouraged immediately after surgery.

ROM exercises were allowed after weaning of the brace and gradually progressed in the order of passive ROM, active assisted ROM, and active ROM until 3 months postoperatively. During the period up to 3 months postoperatively, only the grip exercise group performed grip strengthening exercises. Hand grip exercises were instructed under therapeutic supervision in addition to the conventional ROM exercises when patients removed the brace. The exercise was performed in a relaxed seated position; the elbow joint was flexed at 90 ° in a neutrally rotated state; the wrist was at the neutral position; and the patient was asked to squeeze the hand gripper for 7 seconds and relax for 3 seconds. With this constituting 1 exercise, the patient repeated the exercise 15 times for 3 sets twice a day (in the morning and the afternoon) until 3 months after surgery at a convenient time for the patient.10) An adjustable hand gripper (Nuzamas) was used to perform the exercises, and the exercise intensity was set at a moderate level, with a rating of perceived exertion (RPE) of 13 (somewhat hard).11) Participants were instructed to reset the resistance by using RPE and record their exercise adherence (Fig. 2). Beginning 12 weeks postoperatively, both groups began resistance band exercises to strengthen the rotator cuff and periscapular muscles. Sports activities and heavy labor were allowed after 6 months. Except for the grip exercise, the same rehabilitation protocol was applied to all patients.

Guide to hand grip exercise.

Patient Assessment

The isometric strength of the shoulder joint and periscapular muscles, muscle tone and stiffness of the upper trapezius (UT), ROM, various functional scores, and pain visual analog scale (VAS) score were evaluated preoperatively, and at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after surgery by a certified clinical exercise specialist. MRI assessments at 6 months following surgery were performed to evaluate changes in supraspinatus muscle atrophy and the condition of repair integrity; muscle atrophy was assessed by an orthopedic surgeon (SWC), while repair integrity was independently evaluated by both a musculoskeletal radiologist (SGM) and an orthopedic surgeon (SWC).

Isometric strength of shoulder joint and periscapular muscles

The isometric strength of the shoulder joint (abductor, external rotator, and internal rotator) and periscapular muscles (UT, middle trapezius [MT], lower trapezius [LT], and serratus anterior [SA]) were measured using a digital hand-held dynamometer (Model-01165, Lafayette Instrument Company).

The peak torque of abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation was recorded in pounds (lb), following the testing method described in the study by Beshay et al.12) (Fig. 3). Isometric strength testing of the UT and LT muscles was performed as described by Hislop and Montgomery13) (Fig. 4A and C), MT strength according to Kendall et al.14) (Fig. 4B), and SA strength according to Michener et al.15) (Fig. 4D).

Evaluation of isometric strength of periscapular muscles. (A) Upper trapezius. (B) Middle trapezius. (C) Lower trapezius. (D) Serratus anterior.

Tone and stiffness of the UT

UT muscle tone and stiffness were assessed using the Myoton Pro (Myoton AS) according to previously published protocols.16) Measurements were obtained with participants seated in a neutral posture, and the measurement site was standardized at the midpoint between the C7 spinous process and the lateral acromion. Tone (Hz) and stiffness (N/m) were recorded as the mean of 3 consecutive measurements (Fig. 5).

Evaluation of muscle tone and stiffness of the upper trapezius.

Functional outcomes: shoulder ROM, pain, and functional scores

To assess the angle of forward flexion, the patient was asked to raise the arm straight ahead with the thumb pointing upward while standing, lifting it as high as possible without pain or compensatory trunk movement. The inclinometer was positioned proximal to the lateral epicondyle of the elbow for measurement (Fig. 6A).17) External rotation measurement was taken by passively placing the patient’s arm at the side and having the patient rotate their arm backward as far as possible, with their palm facing the ceiling, while the inclinometer was placed on the anterior aspect of the forearm, parallel to the midline of the ulna and resting on the soft tissue of the flexor mass (Fig. 6B).17) To measure internal rotation behind the back, the vertebral level reached by the tip of the patient’s thumb was recorded (Fig. 6C). For the analysis, vertebral levels were numbered serially as follows: 12 for the T12, 13 for L1, 17 for L5, and 18 for levels below the sacrum.18) The pain VAS score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Constant score, and University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score were evaluated in all patients at each follow-up period. The pain VAS score was assessed from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the highest level of pain).

Evaluation of shoulder range of motion. (A) Forward flexion. (B) External rotation. (C) Internal rotation.

Anatomical outcomes: rotator cuff integrity and the change of the supraspinatus muscle atrophy

MRI-based evaluation of postoperative cuff healing was conducted according to the Sugaya classification: type I, sufficient thickness with homogeneously low intensity; type II, sufficient thickness with partial high-intensity areas; type III, less than half the thickness without discontinuity; type IV, minor discontinuity; and type V, major discontinuity. Healing failure was defined as either type IV or V.19) The supraspinatus muscle atrophy was measured on the scapular Y-view of the oblique sagittal images of MRI at the location where the body of the scapula, scapular spine, and medial border of the coracoid process form a Y-shape. The supraspinatus muscle atrophy was calculated by measuring the occupation ratio according to the method of Thomazeau et al.,20) defined as the ratio between the cross-sectional area of the supraspinatus muscle and that of the supraspinatus fossa on the scapular Y-view, using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS) workstation. The changes in ratio between before surgery and 6 months after surgery were compared among the groups.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the ASES score, which was used for sample size calculation. The primary endpoint was defined as the mid-term postoperative follow-up. Secondary outcomes included shoulder muscle strength assessed at multiple postoperative time points. A sample size was calculated to detect a significant difference in ASES score (mean difference, 8 points; standard deviation, 12 points) based on similar previous studies.21,22) The ASES score has been validated and widely used for the evaluation of outcome after ARCR.23) A sample size of 45 patients in each group was required for a power of 80% at a type I error level of 0.05 and a dropout rate of 20%. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software version 22.0 (IBM Corp.), with a p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. For comparison of continuous variables, the independent t-test was applied, while categorical variables were analyzed using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test to assess group differences.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we aimed to analyze the effects of hand grip strengthening exercises on the shoulder strength and function after ARCR, and found a significantly higher external rotator, internal rotator, and SA strength in the grip exercise group compared with the control group at 6 months after surgery. Following rotator cuff repair, many surgeons recommend wearing a brace for 4 to 6 weeks to protect rotator cuff repair sites and promote tendon healing by restricting shoulder joint movement in the early postoperative period. However, it has been reported that after this immobilization period, shoulder muscle strength can decrease by a minimum of 3% to 4% and up to 47%. In the early stages after surgery, rehabilitation primarily focuses on protecting the rotator cuff tendons, reducing shoulder pain, and improving ROM, rather than on muscle strength recovery.24) Nevertheless, regaining shoulder strength is a fundamental goal for all patients after rotator cuff repair. Weakness of the rotator cuff and altered muscle activation patterns have been shown to increase superior translation of the humeral head, leading to greater mechanical compression of subacromial structures.25) If muscle weakness persists, it may delay functional recovery, making an early return to daily activities more difficult. In our study, grip exercises resulted in improved muscle strength; however, this did not lead to a statistically significant improvement of functional scores, even though the ASES and Constant scores were higher in the grip exercise group than the control group at 6 months. Meanwhile, Lee and Choi26) showed that hand grip exercises increased the muscle activation of the infraspinatus and the cross-sectional area of the supraspinatus when active strength exercises could not be applied after rotator cuff repair. However, we failed to show significant changes in the supraspinatus muscle, measured at 6 months after surgery. According to the study by Chung et al.,27) the supraspinatus volume continued to increase for at least 1 year after surgery. Therefore, it may not be sufficient to observe changes and group differences at 6 months after surgery, and long-term follow-up is necessary.

Several components in hand grip strengthening exercises may contribute to positive results in a way in which hand grip strength influences the rotator cuff. Firstly, the neurological connection was between the hand grip and rotator cuff. Simply, during hand grip exercises, the brain would facilitate the rotator cuff muscles to turn on for the arm to function properly and avoid injury. Therefore, the strong hand grip would increase the neural drive between the hand and shoulder. In previous clinical studies, it was found that concurrent activation of indirect propriospinal pathways in grip strengthening exercises provides adaptable movement control, allowing integration of sensory information from the shoulder and hand.28) Another study reported that the link between proximal stability and distal mobility could explain the relationship between hand grip strength and shoulder strength.10) These findings are consistent with our results, which demonstrated a significant improvement in external and internal rotator strength at 6 months postoperatively, indicating a positive relationship between rotator cuff strength and hand grip strength. Restoration of shoulder strength following rotator cuff repair often takes up to a year or more,29) and in this study, the differences in shoulder muscle strength disappeared at 1 year after surgery. Therefore, it can be interpreted that grip exercises would have more meaning in facilitating faster recovery of shoulder strength. The absence of a significant difference in abductor strength could be attributed to the fact that following surgery, abductor strength is more influenced by postoperative pain and stiffness rather than the recovery of shoulder muscle strength, which may have minimized the noticeable differences in the abductor strength.

Also, grip exercises have been reported to increase the activation of scapular stabilizing muscles, particularly the SA and trapezius muscles.30) For successful functional recovery after rotator cuff repair, it is essential to restore appropriate neuromuscular control of the shoulder complex. However, abnormal scapulohumeral movement due to weakened periscapular muscles after surgery can place stress on the rotator cuff tendons and increase the risk of compression forces. The coordinated action of the UT and LT, along with the SA, provides stability during shoulder joint movement, making the strengthening of these muscles crucial after surgery. In a study by Lee et al.,30) it was explained that grip exercises enhance muscle activation in the shoulder complex, as the stabilization muscles contract in advance to facilitate finger movement. This proximal muscle activation is necessary for distal movement, highlighting the effectiveness of grip exercises. In our study, similar results were observed. Although there was no significant difference between the 2 groups by 3 months and 6 months postoperatively, the grip exercise group showed greater strength in the UT, MT, LT, and SA compared to the control group, with a statistically significant difference in the SA strength.

The hand grip exercise performed during the early postoperative period (up to 3 months) may have enhanced the effectiveness of subsequent rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strengthening exercises performed between 3 and 6 months postoperatively. At 3 months after surgery, shoulder strength recovery may still be limited by postoperative pain, stiffness, and protective rehabilitation protocols, which could explain the absence of between-group differences at this time point. Accordingly, greater rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strength was observed in the grip exercise group at 6 months compared with the control group. However, these differences were no longer evident at 12 months postoperatively, likely because most patients had reached a ceiling effect of strength recovery regardless of rehabilitation strategy. To date, limited data are available regarding the recovery of periscapular muscle strength after rotator cuff repair. The present findings suggest that periscapular muscle strength continues to improve over time, resulting in comparable strength levels at 1 year irrespective of additional rehabilitation strategies. Taken together, these results suggest that hand grip exercise may facilitate faster recovery of rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strength—rather than superior long-term outcomes—leading to earlier stabilization of the shoulder complex compared with conventional rehabilitation protocols.

Increased stiffness in the UT muscle may cause an imbalance of scapular motion during shoulder motion, and a decrease in trapezius stiffness or activity has been reported to be closely related to a reduction in shoulder pain. A previous study recommended that a mean value of 319.1 N/m can be considered as a reference for UT stiffness, and it is essential to quantify UT stiffness in the assessment of shoulder-related pathology and development of optimal therapy.31) In this study, both groups showed higher UT stiffness than the reference value until 12 months. Even though it was not statistically significant, the grip exercise group demonstrated a gradual decrease in the UT stiffness, with lower stiffness compared to the control group; we think the decreased UT stiffness in the grip exercise group may have a potential effect on the decrease in postoperative pain and functional recovery.

In terms of anatomical outcomes, there were no differences in the healing failure rates between the 2 groups, suggesting that performing grip strength exercises in the early period after surgery did not impair rotator cuff repair healing without placing excessive load on the repair site, and can be considered a safe additional rehabilitation protocol. In addition, it has been reported that the atrophy of the supraspinatus muscle was one of the most important prognostic factors after a rotator cuff repair in many studies, and we may expect more enhanced surgical results if the atrophy of the rotator cuff muscle is improved by the grip strength exercise. Previously, Lee and Choi26) observed an increased cross-sectional area of the supraspinatus muscle after hand grip exercise compared to the control group. However, we failed to detect significant differences in the changes in the occupation ratio of the supraspinatus muscle at 6 months after surgery. This may be explained by the studies that demonstrated that the changes in the supraspinatus muscle area or volume were detected at least 1 year after surgery.27) The meaningful changes in muscle recovery may require longer observation periods, emphasizing the need for long-term follow-up assessments.

This study was the first to evaluate the effect of hand grip exercise on shoulder muscle strength and function, and anatomical outcomes in the early period after rotator cuff repair surgery. Nevertheless, there are several limitations in this study. First, the follow-up time of 1 year may be relatively short. However, we could find differences in the shoulder muscle strengths at 6 months, which disappeared at 1 year. Thus, a 1-year follow-up period may be enough to show the effect of the hand grip exercises on the shoulder muscle strength and function. In addition, the evaluation time for the anatomic outcome at 6 months may be relatively early for the final diagnosis of cuff healing. However, previous preclinical and clinical studies32) have suggested that a 6-month postoperative period is sufficient for assessing rotator cuff healing. Therefore, we believe that extending the follow-up period would not result in a significant difference in the healing rate that could undermine the reliability of our findings. Nevertheless, there could be further changes in the supraspinatus muscle atrophy after 6 months postoperatively. A longer follow-up period may be necessary to confirm the anatomical outcomes. Second, the intensity of grip strength exercise was not consistent among patients. While previous studies set exercise intensity relative to maximum grip strength,26) measuring maximum grip power immediately after brace weaning is challenging and may lack accuracy. Therefore, we tried to standardize the exercise by instructing patients to progressively increase the resistance of an adjustable hand gripper to a moderately challenging level during exercises. In addition, although hand dominance was balanced between groups, residual confounding related to dominance cannot be completely excluded. Direct assessment of neuromuscular activation, such as electromyography analysis, was not performed in this study; therefore, the proposed neurological effects of hand grip exercise could not be directly verified. Furthermore, it would be better to perform the isokinetic strength test, which is a proven objective and reliable method accurately reflecting rotator cuff status. However, due to the postoperative shoulder stiffness and pain, we could not perform the isokinetic strength test accurately; thus, we conducted an isometric strength test instead. Finally, postoperative grip strength was not serially assessed in this study, as the hand grip exercise was intended to facilitate proximal shoulder muscle activation rather than to increase grip strength itself. Nevertheless, the absence of serial grip strength measurements limits the ability to fully elucidate the mechanistic link between the intervention and the observed changes in shoulder muscle strength.

In conclusion, the present study suggests that implementing hand grip exercise during the first 3 postoperative months may be associated with greater rotator cuff and periscapular muscle strength at 6 months after rotator cuff repair. While no long-term differences were observed, these findings indicate a potential role of hand grip exercise in facilitating mid-term strength recovery without adversely affecting rotator cuff healing. Given the methodological limitations of this study, these results should be interpreted with caution, and further well-powered studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
