A Global Intra-city Commuting Origin-Destination Flow Dataset for Urban Sustainable Development
Authors: Can Rong, Jingtao Ding, Meng Li, Yong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommuting Origin-Destination (OD) flows capture movements of people from residences to workplaces, representing the predominant form of intra-city mobility and serving as a critical reference for understanding urban dynamics and supporting sustainable policies. However, acquiring such data requires costly, time-consuming censuses. In this study, we introduce a commuting OD flow dataset for cities around the world, spanning 6 continents, 179 countries, and 2,358 cities, providing unprecedented coverage of dynamics under diverse urban environments. Specifically, we collected fine-grained demographic data, satellite imagery, and points of interest (POIs) for each city as foundational inputs to characterize the functional roles of urban regions. Leveraging these, a deep generative model is employed to capture the complex relationships between urban geospatial features and human mobility, enabling the generation of commuting OD flows between urban regions. Comprehensively, validation shows that the spatial distributions of the generated flows closely align with real-world observations. We believe this dataset offers a valuable resource for advancing sustainable urban development research in urban science, data science, transportation engineering, and related fields.Subject terms: Sustainability, Geography, Interdisciplinary studies

Background & Summary

Cities serve as the primary hubs of social and economic activities1–4, including manufacturing in factories, service provision in commercial establishments, trade in marketplaces, and technological innovation within research and development centers. To optimize efficiency, these activities are often spatially organized into functional zones5,6, such as industrial zones for manufacturing and residential zones for housing. This spatial organization necessitates daily commuting between residential and workplace regions. Such critical behavior is typically captured in form of commuting Origin-Destination (OD) flows, data that record population movements between residential and workplace regions7–10. Furthermore, commuting OD flows reflect peak mobility patterns during morning and evening rush hours, which constitute a significant component of urban dynamics11,12. Building on this, commuting OD flows are integral to advancing United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)13,14, and our systematic validation (in Section of Technical Validation) for different goals demonstrates their reliability for sustainability:

Urban Planning (SDG-11): Commuting OD flows reveal inefficient long-distance travel patterns, enabling planners to optimize zone allocation and reduce travel costs15–17.

However, global-scale commuting OD flow data covering diverse urban environments remains unavailable, hindering the universality and comprehensiveness of studies on urban dynamics and sustainable development. This limitation has resulted in existing works focusing on a small number of cities within specific urban contexts8,10,30–37, leading to varied and context-dependent conclusions. The primary obstacle lies in the significant challenges associated with data collection. Traditionally, there are two main approaches to collecting commuting OD flows. The first is conducting travel surveys, which, while accurate, are time-consuming and expensive due to extensive population coverage required7,37–39. The second involves using location records collected from mobile phone usage9,21,40–43, which is more cost-effective but faces barriers such as commercial conflicts among mobile service providers and privacy concerns, severely limiting global availability. Consequently, collection-based methods for acquiring commuting OD flows on a worldwide scale remain impractical and currently infeasible.

In this work, we present a large-scale commuting OD flow dataset for global cities, generated using deep graph generative models based on public data like population counts, demographics, satellite imagery, and points of interest (POIs). The generation involves two processes: urban region profiling and commuting OD flow generation. First, we use public data to characterize urban regions’ functions and attributes. Second, we model commuting flows as a weighted directed graph, where regions are nodes and flows are edges, and use graph generative models36,44 to generate flows based on spatial characteristics extracted from multi-source global public data. This data-driven approach enables realistic flow generation for specific urban spaces. We collect data for 2,358 cities across 179 countries and 6 continents, as shown in Fig. 2, enabling research on urban dynamics and supporting sustainable policy development globally.

An overview of the globally distributed cities included in the dataset.

We systematically validate our dataset from five perspectives to ensure its accuracy and robustness: (1) regression analysis based on mobile location data (correlation coefficients 0.74-0.82 for various cities); (2) error analysis against Census data (CPC=0.572, significantly higher than previous works); (3) geospatial visualization analysis in cities across Asia, Latin America, and Africa; (4) downstream tasks for predicting work population (R2>0.84 for all test cases) and carbon emission (correlation=0.66); and (5) attribute reproduction in scale-free commuting networks. The results demonstrate that our dataset is both reliable and valuable.

Methods

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the methodology used to construct the dataset. The construction pipeline, illustrated in Fig. 1, comprises three main steps. First, we select the cities to be included in the dataset and divide them into urban regions. Next, we profile these urban regions by extracting features from multi-source global public data using specific feature extraction techniques. Finally, we generate commuting OD flows between the urban regions using graph generative models.

An overview of the dataset construction pipeline.

Determining City Boundaries and Geographic Units

To comprehensively cover cities and their geographic boundaries across diverse urban environments worldwide, we integrated multiple datasets to select cities with detailed boundary information. Specifically, we began by collecting cities from the WhosOnFirst (WOF) database, a global open-source gazetteer of places. This data is available in GeoJSON format at https://whosonfirst.org/. From this dataset, we selected all cities with explicitly defined boundaries represented by polygons. To further enhance coverage, we supplemented our dataset with cities from the GUI dataset provided by Han et al.45, ensuring no duplication of cities already included in the WOF database. Finally, we add cities defined in the GHS-FUA dataset46 by removing too small cities (less than 100, 000 people or 25 km2) and the repeated cities. Through this integration process, we compiled a comprehensive dataset containing a total of 2,358 cities, each with detailed geographic boundary information.

After determining the cities and their boundaries, we divided each city into individual urban regions using a unified, adaptive grid-based method commonly employed in modeling urban dynamics7,10,47–49. To balance detail and computational efficiency, we dynamically adjusted the grid size for each city based on its geographic extent. This approach enables finer resolution for smaller cities and coarser grids for larger urban areas. The grid size was determined by the city’s bounding box, defined by the distance between its minimum and maximum latitude and longitude. Specifically, the grid size was set to 5% of the city’s total boundary length, with an upper limit of 5 kilometers and a lower limit of 500 meters. Figure 3 illustrates examples of the region division for various cities, demonstrating the adaptability and effectiveness of this approach. This configuration ensures that the resulting urban regions are sufficiently detailed to capture the city’s structure while remaining computationally manageable. Moreover, avoiding excessively small grids minimizes the impact of randomness and noise. Smaller cities typically exhibit finer-grained community structures and functional divisions, where smaller grids are more appropriate; in contrast, larger cities tend to have more spatially aggregated communities, for which coarser grids better capture meaningful commuting flows. The 5% rule enables consistent scale adaptation across cities, while the bounds of 500m and 5km are informed by empirical testing, corresponding respectively to neighborhood-scale structures and the typical spatial extent of large urban functional zones50,51.

Multi-source Global Public Data for Profiling Urban Regions

We utilize a variety of globally available public data sources, each rigorously validated for their effectiveness in describing the functional characteristics of urban regions52–67, to generate commuting OD flows. Specifically, we use the following data sources:

Population and Demographics: WorldPop68 provides fine-grained global population counts and demographic data at a 100m resolution. Using this dataset, we calculate the total population and demographic structure (age and gender distributions) within each urban region. This information is essential for estimating the generation of population flows35,69,70. This dataset is available on the WorldPop data catalog platform at https://www.worldpop.org/datacatalog/. The “Total Population” dataset can be found under the “Population Counts” data category, while demographic details are listed under the “age and sex structures” category. The data is organized by country, with each dataset corresponding to a specific country. Users can navigate to a given category, select a country of interest, and download the relevant data files. In this work, we use the data of constrained individual countries 2020 UN adjusted.

It is worth noting that the temporal coverage of the different data sources used in this study is not fully aligned. While this may introduce some degree of temporal inconsistency, it is unlikely to significantly affect the derived spatial commuting patterns. Achieving perfect temporal consistency across all data sources would indeed be ideal; however, it is extremely challenging due to limitations in data availability. Nonetheless, we emphasize that these temporal discrepancies are not expected to materially impact the validity of the spatial commuting patterns that our dataset aims to capture, because of the relatively slow pace of commuting patterns and urban structural change77–83. In detail, William et al.77 found that individuals’ commuting patterns shifted by less than 4.2% over five years, and the overall structure remained unchanged over fifteen years. Similarly, Peter et al.79 reported that commuting patterns in cities like New York, Los Angeles, and Chicago changed by less than 5% over five years. Rashid et al.84, over a 30-year span, found only subtle adjustments in urban functional zoning, with minor refinements slightly increasing spatial complexity. Rachel et al.82 reported that Brisbane’s residential land use changed by merely 10% between 2001 and 2021.

From each data source, we extract a feature vector for every urban region. These feature vectors are then concatenated to form the final semantic representation of each region, resulting in a 1,094-dimensional vector. This vector comprises 36 dimensions derived from population and demographic data, 1,024 dimensions from satellite imagery, and 34 dimensions from POIs, which are derived 34 POI categories by aggregating fine-grained OpenPOIMap tags into semantically coherent groups. This manual grouping enhances interpretability and balances functional specificity with generalizability across urban contexts. The resulting feature vector serves as the node feature and acts as the conditional input for the graph generative model. The detailed contents of the feature vectors are shown in Table 3.

Generating Commuting OD Flows via Generative Graph Diffusion Models

Using the extracted semantic features of urban regions as node-level inputs, we employ graph diffusion models to generate commuting OD flows, represented as weighted edges between nodes. Specifically, we adopt WEDAN44, a state-of-the-art model specifically designed for the generation of commuting OD flows. WEDAN has been demonstrated to outperform other methods for this task. As the primary focus of this work is to provide a synthetic dataset of commuting OD flows, we do not delve into the technical details of WEDAN. Seeking a deeper understanding of WEDAN’s design and the principles of diffusion models can be referred to prior works85,86. For completeness, we provide a brief overview of WEDAN’s utilization in this study. The detailed introduction of WEDAN is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary File 1).

WEDAN is a graph diffusion-based model that generates the weighted edges of a graph, representing commuting OD flows, conditioned on the node features. The framework of WEDAN comprises two primary processes: forward diffusion and reverse denoising. The forward diffusion process is used to prepare the training data by progressively adding small amounts of noise to the edge weights (commuting OD flows) until the data is entirely disrupted and reduced to pure noise. The reverse denoising process then iteratively reconstructs the original data from the noise, guided by the node features. During each iteration of the reverse process, a portion of the noise is removed from the data produced in the previous iteration. The partially denoised data is then fed into the next iteration. This process continues until the noise is fully removed, resulting in data that conforms to the underlying data distribution. A key aspect of WEDAN is that the generation process is guided by the node features, which encapsulate the semantic representations of urban regions. This ensures that the generated commuting OD flows align closely with real-world urban dynamics.

In this study, we adopt the same training and optimization strategy as the original WEDAN implementation44. The model is trained using a standard denoising diffusion loss, which involves predicting the Gaussian noise added at each diffusion step85. The training process uses commuting OD data from 3,333 counties and metropolitan areas across the United States, partitioned into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing. We set the diffusion steps to 256, used 3 graph Transformer layers with a hidden dimension of 32, and a learning rate of 1e-5. Unlike the original WEDAN paper, the node features used in our setting are constructed from the multi-source global public data described above. This enables broader applicability across cities worldwide. While we do not explicitly model uncertainty or bias, we rely on a well-established strategy in Artificial Intelligent Generated Content (AIGC), training on diverse and large-scale urban data, to promote generalizability87. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that some biases may persist, especially in global scale and learned from the U.S., and we discuss these in detail in the Limitations section.

This method assumes that semantic features derived from global public data are sufficiently expressive to capture the spatial patterns of commuting flows, and that training on diverse urban forms allows the model to generalize across different city sizes, structures, and income levels88–90. Prior research52–67 supports the representational capacity of such data sources in capturing key socioeconomic, demographic, and environmental characteristics. Accordingly, WEDAN serves as a powerful and transferable engine for generating synthetic OD flows aligned with real-world urban mobility dynamics.

Commuting OD Flows

The commuting OD flows are provided in matrix form and stored as a NumPy array, as illustrated in Table 2. In this matrix, the element at position [i, j] represents the number of people residing in the i-th region of the city who commute to the j-th region for work. The indices i and j correspond to the unique identifiers (indices) of the urban regions within the city’s boundary and region divisions, as defined in the associated Shapefile. The matrices contains commuting OD flows between all urban regions within the specific cities’ boundary. We visualize the commuting OD flows in three representative cities in Fig. 5 to give a intuitive physical representation of the commuting OD flows. It is worth noting that the diagonal values of the OD matrices represent intra-zone flows, which are not estimated by our model. In the stored .npy files, these values are set to zero for format consistency, but they should be interpreted as missing (NA) rather than zero flow.

Technical Validation

We conduct a comprehensive technical validation of the generated commuting OD flows to ensure their accuracy and reliability. This validation encompasses evaluations across five aspects: (1) regression analysis based on mobile location data; (2) error analysis of generated flows against UK census data; (3) geospatial visualization analysis; (4) downstream tasks for predicting population distribution during work hours; and (5) attribute reproduction in scale-free commuting networks.

Comparison Analysis with Mobile Location Data

Existing studies have utilized mobile location data, such as call detail records (CDRs) and cellular network accesses (CNAs), to estimate commuting flows92–94. For our validation, we collected mobile location data from various sources for representative cities worldwide, including Beijing and Shanghai in China, Rio de Janeiro in Brazil, and Senegal in Africa. These cities were selected not only for their global representativeness but also because commuting-related reference data, such as CDRs or travel surveys, are available, which is a prerequisite for validation. The mobile location data used for validation in this study were sourced from various providers and differ in format. For Beijing, we obtained 2019 location service data provided by a major Chinese internet-based location service provider. For Shanghai, we used 2016 CNAs data collected by the largest mobile operator in China. The dataset for Rio de Janeiro was provided by Julio et al.95, which is a synthetic dataset generated by aggregating CDRs data from Rio de Janeiro. For Senegal, we used the D4D-Senegal dataset96, which is an anonymized dataset generated by aggregating CDRs data from Senegal. The datasets for Beijing and Rio de Janeiro were pre-aggregated into OD matrices, while the Shanghai and Senegal datasets consist of anonymized individual-level cellular base connection records. We compared the generated commuting OD flows with commuting flows estimated from the mobile location data by calculating Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SCC) between the two datasets. As shown in Fig. 6, the SCC values for Beijing, Shanghai, Rio de Janeiro, and Senegal are 0.741, 0.641, 0.816, and 0.477, respectively. These results indicate a high degree of consistency between the generated commuting OD flows and the mobile location data. It is important to emphasize that while strong correlations validate the quality of the generated data, weaker correlations do not necessarily indicate poor quality. Such discrepancies may stem from limitations or biases inherent in the reference data. For instance, the relatively low SCC value of 0.477 for Senegal can likely be attributed to the lower coverage of mobile phone usage in Senegal compared to other cities.

Comparison between generated commuting OD flows and mobile location data. (a) Beijing, (b) Shanghai, (c) Rio de Janeiro, (d) Senegal.

To further evaluate the structural similarity between the generated commuting OD flows and collected multi-source mobility data, we introduce two additional metrics: network connectivity similarity and OD pattern similarity. Specifically, we compute the Jaccard similarity between the binary connectivity structures of the generated and collected mobility data-derived commuting flows. Regarding OD pattern similarity, we normalize the outflow distribution to all destinations and calculate the cosine similarity between the generated and data-sourced transfer probability vectors. Results show that the network connectivity similarities are 0.746, 0.690, 0.901, and 0.875 for Beijing, Shanghai, Rio de Janeiro, and Senegal, respectively. The OD pattern similarities are 0.740, 0.797, 0.830, and 0.691, respectively. These findings indicate that the generated commuting OD flows share approximately 70 ~ 90% structural similarity with the mobility patterns derived from independent data sources. Importantly, this demonstrates that even without considering exact flow magnitudes, the generation model is able to capture the underlying spatial structure of commuting behavior effectively. We also count the travel range in the commuting OD flow data from the generation and mobile location data. The results indicate a close alignment between the generated data and mobile phone-derived mobility data: 11.4 km vs. 17.2 km in Beijing, 8.9 km vs. 8.7 km in Shanghai, 14.4 km vs. 13.2 km in Rio de Janeiro, and 11.1 km vs. 11.8 km in Senegal.

Cross-continental Error Analysis of Generated Flows Against Census Data in the UK and US

Thanks to the availability of comprehensive commuting OD flow data from national censuses in the UK and US, this provides a valuable benchmark for evaluating the model performance on generalizing to unseen cities within the same distribution, and transferability across countries in different continents. We give an introduction of the data used in these experiments first. There are commuting OD flows within 3,333 urban areas including counties and metropolises in the US, and 200 local authority districts (LADs) in the UK, which can act as the cities. The regions within these cities are defined at a finer spatial granularity, corresponding to census tracts in the US and Middle Layer Super Output Areas (MSOAs) in the UK. The data of the US is collected in 2018 and the data of the UK is collected in 2021. We conduct three experiments to evaluate the model performance: (1) splitting the US data into training, validation, and test sets (8:1:1), and then train the model on the training set and evaluate the model on the validation and test sets; (2) treating the US data as the training set, and the UK data as the test set, i.e., the model is trained on the US data and evaluated on the UK data; (3) treating the UK data as the training set, and the US data as the test set, i.e., the model is trained on the UK data and evaluated on the US data. Specifically, we utilize three metrics to quantitatively evaluate the deviation between the generated and census data: (1) common parts of commuters (CPC), (2) root mean square error (RMSE), and (3) normalized root mean square error (NRMSE). Downstream Tasks in Multiple Urban Applications and Corresponding SDGs

To further validate the generated commuting OD flows, we perform downstream tasks to predict population distribution during work hours. This is achieved by integrating the nighttime residential population distribution with the commuting OD flows to model daytime transitions. Specifically, we use nighttime population distribution data from WorldPop as the baseline and incorporate transitions from residential locations to workplace locations during commuting hours as the variation to derive the population distribution during work hours. To further enhance the validation, we compare the predicted daytime population distribution against real-time population heatmap data from Baidu Map’s Huiyan urban population geography platform. The heatmap data is from Sep 9th 2024 and scaled to approximate the full population distribution during work hours using the method proposed by Xu et al.99. As illustrated in Fig. 14, the results demonstrate that our generated commuting OD flows accurately predict population transitions from nighttime residential periods to daytime work periods, with coefficients of determination (R2) of 0.95, 0.89, 0.84, and 0.87 for Chengdu, Guangzhou, Jinan, and Qingdao, respectively, while directly using the nighttime population distribution data from WorldPop as the baseline, the coefficients of determination are 0.71, 0.66, 0.63, and 0.65. These findings provide strong evidence for the quality and reliability of the generated commuting OD flows.

Downstream task results of population distribution prediction during work hours. (a) Chengdu, (b) Guangzhou, (c) Jinan, (d) Qingdao.

To further validate the practical utility of our generated commuting OD flows, we apply them to estimate urban transportation carbon emissions as a downstream task (SDGs-9,13)29. Specifically, we first calculate commuting distances based on OD matrices and regional centroids, incorporating city-specific detour factors to account for different road network characteristics. We then combine these with modal shares (60% private cars, 35% public transit, and 5% cycling/walking) and their respective emission factors (171, 104, and 0 gCO2/km) to compute the overall transportation carbon emissions for each city. As shown in Fig. 16, we compare our estimates with actual measurements from Carbon Monitor Cities100. From the results, our estimates demonstrate strong correlation with the measured data (correlation coefficient of 0.66). This carbon emission estimation method based on commuting OD flows not only validates the reliability of our generated data but also provides a novel approach for rapid assessment of urban transportation carbon emissions. This approach is particularly valuable for cities lacking detailed carbon emission monitoring data, offering a practical way to estimate their transportation-related carbon footprint.

Comparison between estimated and measured urban transportation carbon emissions. (Downstream task to achieve SDGs-9,13).

We also employ them to analyze urban mobility network resilience (SDGs-9,11). Specifically, we develop a comprehensive resilience assessment framework that incorporates multiple dimensions of urban networks. We first calculate the network connectivity based on OD matrices, measuring both the strength and distribution of commuting flows. We then evaluate system vulnerability by simulating node failures and quantifying their impacts on the overall network performance. Additionally, we assess network redundancy through alternative path analysis and recovery potential based on the distribution of critical nodes101–105. As shown in Fig. 17, we analyze the relationship between city size and urban mobility resilience for 21 major global cities. We apply K-means clustering (k=2) on the computed resilience index to identify two groups of cities in a statistical manner. The results reveal two distinct patterns: cities exhibiting higher levels of measured resilience index (R2=0.679) and cities exhibiting lower levels of measured resilience index (R2=0.272). Notably, several megacities like Shanghai, Chicago, and Paris exhibit resilience characteristics that align more closely with the higher-resilience group despite their large populations, suggesting that well-planned urban structures can maintain system resilience even under the pressure of massive population. Furthermore, there is a significant correlation between city size and urban resilience, with larger cities generally exhibiting lower resilience index, which can clearly be profiled by our generated commuting OD flows.

Existing studies examining the commuting behavior as networks from the perspective of complex networks have established that these networks are scale-free, meaning the number of commuters between two regions follows a power-law distribution. To validate this property in the generated commuting networks, we analyze and visualize the distribution of commuting flows. As shown in Fig. 15, the distribution of the generated commuting flows closely follows a power-law distribution, indicating that the generated commuting networks exhibit the scale-free property. Sensitivity Analysis of the Input Data

To evaluate the relative importance of different types of input data, we conduct an ablation study focusing on the three data sources: population and demographics, satellite imagery, and POIs. In this experiment, we assess the sensitivity of the model to each input modality by systematically replacing one of the three feature sets with random noise while keeping the other two unchanged. The model used for this analysis is the same as in the main experiments, trained on the U.S. dataset. For the evaluation, we apply the model to generate commuting OD flows in the United Kingdom, a region not seen during training. The ground-truth OD data for the UK is sourced from the 2021 national census provided by the Office for National Statistics (ONS), covering all commuting flows between every two MSOAs. We define 200 cities across the UK based on LADs boundaries. As shown in Fig. 18, the results reveal that population and demographic features contribute the most to the model’s performance, indicating their critical role in capturing commuting behaviors. Satellite imagery also provides a meaningful performance gain, reflecting its utility in encoding the physical structure of urban environments. In contrast, POIs have a comparatively smaller impact, suggesting that they may offer more supplementary information rather than broad structural cues.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations of this dataset, which can be categorized into dataset, modeling, and validation perspectives. From the characteristics of the dataset, we have the following limitations:

Intra-city Commuting OD Flows: The dataset exclusively focuses on intra-city commuting OD flows, meaning that inter-city commuting flows are not included.

Static Spatial Interactions: The dataset represents static commuting OD flows and does not account for temporal variations. While differences in development stages across cities may serve as a limited proxy for temporal variations, this approach lacks the precision and granularity offered by city-specific historical development information, which remains a limitation.

Regarding the modeling framework, there may be following limitations:

Model Bias: Although we adopt a state-of-the-art model for generating commuting OD flows, it inevitably carries certain inductive biases due to its architecture, learning assumptions, and the distribution of the training data. For example, treating inherently discrete trip counts as continuous variables often “smears” near-zero OD pairs–true zeros are inflated into tiny positive flows, while very small real flows may be rounded away altogether. Moreover, due to the inherent smoothness in the model, extremely large flows in edge cases may be underestimated, as they differ significantly from other flows in the data. However, this is a challenge faced by all other existing models. As a result, the generated flows may not perfectly match real-world commuting patterns. While the model aims to approximate actual flows as closely as possible, discrepancies between the synthetic and real data are expected and should be acknowledged.

However, such data cannot always reflect the most recent changes in the real world due to delays in updates in global coverage. Consequently, the generated OD flows may exhibit a time lag relative to real-world conditions. For the scientific credibility and effective reuse of the dataset, we also acknowledge the following limitations from the validation perspective:

Validation Concentrated in High-income Settings: The quantitative validation of the generated commuting data relies exclusively on data from the United States and the United Kingdom due to the data availability. Both are high-income countries, and the spatial resolutions of the validation units are relatively coarse (census tracts and MSOAs). This may not sufficiently test the robustness of the model for generating OD flows in low- and middle-income areas, where urban forms, mobility infrastructure, and data availability differ substantially. Although we provide supplementary validation using mobile location data in Brazil and Senegal, which represent upper-middle-income and low-income contexts respectively, these comparisons are based on sampled mobile phone records rather than comprehensive census data, and thus offer less rigorous validation. In the future, validation using real-world commuting OD data from a broader range of countries and income levels should be conducted to more thoroughly validate the model’s global applicability.

Atypical Commuting Patterns in the UK Census Data: The UK census data used for validation was collected in 2021, during the COVID-19 pandemic, when commuting patterns were significantly disrupted by widespread remote working and travel restrictions106,107. In terms of future improvements, integrating road network data (e.g., from OpenStreetMap) and built-up footprints could refine the spatial realism of the generated OD flows by excluding uninhabited or inaccessible regions. Additionally, adopting the recently updated WorldPop population estimates (2015-2030), which incorporate the latest censuses and covariates, could further improve the accuracy of the input demographic data. We will keep maintaining this dataset to improve both its coverage and accuracy.

Data Loading and Processing

For datasets containing multiple file types, we recommend using Python for loading and processing the data. Specifically, GeoPandas is ideal for loading city boundaries and region division data provided in Shapefile format, while NumPy is well-suited for loading the commuting OD flow data. Additionally, the Shapefile data can be loaded using GIS software such as ArcGIS. Python, integrated within ArcGIS, can also be used to process the commuting OD flow data, enabling seamless handling of both data types in a geospatial context.

To facilitate easy access for researchers across different domains, we also provide a web-based graphical interface at https://fi.ee.tsinghua.edu.cn/worldod/ that allows users to generate commuting OD flows for any region of interest without writing code.
