Next-generation osteoarthritis models: integrating biological, computational, and engineering approaches
Authors: Luminita Labusca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a complex degenerative joint disease with substantial global health implications, yet effective disease-modifying treatments remain elusive. This review explores next-generation models revolutionizing OA research, including microfluidic organ-on-a-chip (OOAC) platforms, organoid systems, computational modelling, finite element analysis (FEA), and artificial intelligence (AI). OOAC systems replicate joint microenvironments, integrating biomechanical stimulation and dynamic tissue interactions, thereby enabling precise investigations of inflammatory and degenerative processes. While organoid technologies capture cellular heterogeneity and self-organization, they primarily serve as static, multicellular models rather than dynamic biomechanical systems. FEA provides high-resolution, patient-specific simulations of joint mechanics and cartilage degeneration, offering insights into mechanical stress distribution and OA progression. Computational modelling and AI enhance predictive capabilities, facilitating precision medicine approaches and optimizing treatment strategies. Despite significant advancements, critical challenges remain, particularly regarding biological fidelity, cross-model integration, and clinical translation. Ensuring computer-based model validation against curated, high-quality datasets—including patient-derived biomechanical, imaging, and molecular data—is imperative for increasing accuracy and translatability. By improving early diagnosis, treatment personalization, and cost-effective therapeutic screening, these advanced technologies can inform healthcare policies, optimize resource allocation, and shape evidence-based guidelines for OA management. This review underscores the necessity of interdisciplinary collaboration to refine these advanced platforms, bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical research, and accelerate the development of patient-specific OA interventions while informing adequate healthcare policies.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Organ on a chip, Organoids, Artificial intelligence, Motion analysis

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a complex degenerative joint disease that can affect individuals across their lifespans, although its prevalence tends to rise with the advancing years. OA progressively involves all joint compartments leading to destruction of articular surfaces and loss of joint function. OA is not exclusively linked to aging but can result from various factors such as joint misalignment, overuse, trauma, and metabolic syndrome, leading to joint structure destruction and functional loss even in younger individuals [1].

Given its widespread, OA has emerged as a significant global health issue with a rapidly increasing incidence. Each year, over 86 million individuals aged 20 and above are affected by OA, making it the most prevalent degenerative disease worldwide [2]. The reported cases have escalated from 247.51 million in 1990 to 527.81 million in 2019, according to the Global Burden of Disease study [3].

Currently, the primary focus of OA treatment is symptom management, typically involving pain relief through non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or opioids [4 However, there is no identified disease-modifying drug capable of halting OA progression. For severe cases of OA where the joint function is significantly impaired, total joint replacement (TJR) is the ultimate treatment option. Nonetheless, this surgical procedure is invasive, associated with considerable healthcare costs, and poses risks of complications.

A key concern in current OA treatment is the therapeutic gap that exists between the onset of OA and the point at which total joint replacement becomes necessary. Many OA patients, especially young and active ones, fall into this gap, experiencing substantial pain and joint dysfunction without qualifying for TJR.

Advanced therapeutic approaches such as regenerative medicine, nanomedicine, and precision medicine are potential innovative solutions that could diversify and improve OA treatment. Regenerative medicine (RM) seeks to repair or replace damaged tissues using stem cells, growth factors, or tissue engineering. Nanomedicine employs nanomaterial-based systems for drug delivery and tissue regeneration. Precision medicine is set to tailor treatments based on individual characteristics, such as genetic background, disease progression, individual or group particularities and possible co -morbidities.

Despite the promise of these advanced therapies, their progress toward clinical application has been slow. Some regenerative solutions may provide symptomatic relief but may not justify the associated costs, requiring further research to enhance efficacy and safety. Similarly, while nanomaterial-based systems have shown potential in preclinical studies, their clinical translation necessitates more technological advancements and rigorous validation.

Two major conceptual advances in understanding OA have been marked in the last decade consistently impacting the field of therapeutic design. The long-held perspective of a disease of cartilage has been replaced by the current understanding of OA multifaceted disease affecting the whole joint. Synovial joints are currently perceived as complex organs composed of a diversity of tissues with intricated immune and metabolic features, each of them impacting OA occurrence and progression [7]. Particularly the role of systemic and intraarticular adipose tissue and its pathological states – obesity, metabolic syndrome as well as cachexia is increasingly acknowledged [8, 9] offering potential therapeutic targets [10]. Several pathological mechanisms have been proposed to explain OA onset and progression. These theories align with distinct OA subtypes, each characterized by specific molecular and biomechanical alterations. Chronic low-grade inflammation underlies inflammatory OA. This theory suggests that synovial inflammation, mediated by increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, plays a crucial role in cartilage degradation and joint destruction. Unlike autoimmune-driven arthritis, inflammatory OA is driven by innate immune activation, leading to progressive deterioration of all tissues that compose diarthrodial joints.

Another widely recognized mechanism is biomechanical stress and joint overloading, which is central to overloading-associated OA. This theory posits that excessive mechanical strain, frequently observed in athletes, individuals with obesity, or those with occupational joint overuse, leads to cartilage microtrauma, subchondral bone remodelling, and the eventual breakdown of joint structures. The inability of chondrocytes to effectively repair microdamage exacerbates disease progression. Posttraumatic OA (PTOA) as well as joint malalignment-induced OA stands as the most relevant representative of the biomechanical disturbances introduced by either traumatic destruction or compartmental overuse of articular joint tissue components, respectively.

Oxidative stress and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction can act as potent drivers of OA. An imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defences results in chondrocyte apoptosis, extracellular matrix degradation, and inflammation. ROS accumulation not only damages cartilage directly but also alters chondrocyte metabolism, further contributing to disease progression.

A lubrication failure hypothesis has also been proposed. This theory suggests that disruptions in synovial fluid composition, including reduced levels of hyaluronic acid and lubricin, lead to increased frictional forces within the joint. The loss of adequate lubrication accelerates cartilage wear, impairing its ability to withstand mechanical stress and promoting further degeneration.

A growing body of evidence supports the metabolic theory of OA, proposing that systemic metabolic dysregulation—driven by obesity, insulin resistance, and altered lipid metabolism—creates a pro-inflammatory and catabolic joint environment. Adipokines and metabolic intermediates contribute to chronic inflammation and chondrocyte dysfunction, leading to cartilage degradation and abnormal subchondral remodelling.

Another important progress is acknowledging that OA consists in a diversity of clinical phenotypes produced by the alteration of certain molecular mechanisms, possibly shared by several phenotypes that could be targeted more specifically for therapeutic purposes. The possibility that clinical phenotypes of OA have different underlying aetiologies and pathogenic pathways that could be described and classified at a molecular level has important ramification for future design of targeted OA therapeutics [11].

One of the causes of current unavailability of disease modifying drugs is considered to be the lack of relevant models that can simultaneously represent tissue interaction within the joint as well as the intricated OA phenotype diversity [12]. Preclinical models should accurately represent the complex composition of the joint organ, including its diverse cell populations (chondrocytes, osteocytes, synovial fibroblasts, and synovial macrophages) and structural organization. In plus, models that integrate the interactions with the immune system and metabolic factors would reflect the interdependence with whole organism functioning and adaptative responses. In such, high fidelity as well as high throughput in vitro models are expected to facilitate novel drugs and/or regenerative therapies testing in initial phases of product development. This narrative review will summarize current availability of OA models based on advanced technological and scientific breakthroughs that aim to address these complex challenges. Micro and nano fluidics-based devices, organoids, computer modelling and artificial intelligence approaches will be described together with existent opportunities and roadblocks.

Joint on a chip- microfluidics

Rapid advances in microfluidics technology combined with accelerated improvement in additive manufacturing are offering interesting alternatives in modelling complex tissues and organs. The development of such in vitro micro-scaled biomimetic platforms aims to closely reproduce cellular composition and well as normal and pathological physiology of various organs [13]. Their major advantages rely on device miniaturization, integrative functionalization, low consumption and possibility for accurate control of certain physical and biochemical parameters (of which concentration gradient, fluid shear stress, cell interaction, tissue–tissue interface) [14]. Organ-on-a-chip devices (OOAC) and micro-physiological systems (MPS) are of large interest in drug discovery as they can facilitate compound and phenotype screening, toxicology, physiology and pharmacokinetic studies [15]. In the field of human joint modelling, microfluidics is regarded as being of unpreceded value in replicating cellular crosstalk as well as biomechanical stimuli existent during joint development and function [16].

Modelling the articular joint tissue complexity using ooacs

The development of OOAC systems for modelling articular joints replicates the evolving school of thought regarding joint functioning and OA pathogeny. Among the firstly reported OOAC-like models focused on chondrocyte metabolism, introducing the concept of biomechanical cell stimulation within microchips using highly controllable pneumatic actuators. Chondrocyte embedded in alginate hydrogels were submitted to cyclic compression using an array of pneumatic actuated by transparent polydimethylsiloxane (PMDS) balloons. Such device allows for life cell imaging and was reported to increase cell viability upon dynamic compression but not under static compression [17]. potentially offering a means for studying chondrocyte biology. Another approach intended to improve the accuracy of three-dimensional micro tissue models of cartilage consists in modelling nutrient gradient across self-organizing hydrogel embedded primary equine chondrocytes. The microfluidic 3D chondrocyte culture-on-a-chip array was reported to reproduce the low proliferating metabolic environment, a degree of cyclic sheer stress with regular media exchanges, and most of all a reproducible nutrient gradient across 3-mm-long and 1 mm-high micro pellets allowing for their spontaneous organization within layers such as superficial zone [18].

Increasing in complexity, an osteochondral construct composed of in situ developing bone and cartilage tissue from human bone marrow derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) was cultivated within a bioreactor. Spatially defined chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation was shown to be differentially influenced by inflammatory cytokine exposure (interleukin 1-beta- IL 1β) with increased catabolic response in the osteogenic tissue, demonstrating active communication between compartments. Technically, the system consisting of a multichambered bioreactor fitted within a microfluidic base which resulted in two separate chambers that could be supplied with different (differentiation) media flows. The differentiating BMSCs expressed specific bone and cartilage markers and were able to form a basophilic interface corresponding to a tide mark proof of a crosstalk between the metabolic features of the two forming tissues [19]. To counteract the limited expansion capability of serial passaged MSCs, the same team further developed a similar device populated with induced pluripotent stem cell derived MSCs embedded within photo crosslinked gelatine scaffolds. When exposed to chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation media respectively, newly formed tissues responded to IL 1β stimulation by acquiring an OA like phenotype [20]. The group subsequently patented an upgraded device containing synovial and adipose tissue as well as macrophage compartments contained within microbioreactors interconnected with miniaturized tubes circulating synovial fluid, hypoxic and normo-oxic culture medium [21].

A microfluidic chip investigated the interaction between chondral and synovial tissue aimed to prospect the simulation of cartilage interaction with blood derived monocytes extravasated through synovial endothelium. Fibrin gel embedded articular cartilage derived chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts from OA patients populated the respective chondrocytes and synovial microfluidic chambers communicating by means of microchannels simulating synovial fluid flow. To mimic the endothelial wall of synovial postcapillary venules, endothelial cells were seeded in the microchannels. The system was tailored in terms of fibrin concentration, cell density and fluid flow and the endothelization of the micro channels demonstrated its ability to resist dextran diffusion and to permit cytokine accumulation within synovial compartment. The platform is sought to allow for testing compounds targeting chemokine signalling axes involved in monocyte extravasation from systemic blood stream during OA synovial inflammation [22]. Even if this model cannot still reproduce the complexity of cellular interaction within the synovial membrane, its merit consists in providing a method for reproducing lining and sub lining layers [16].

Modelling joint biomechanics with OOAC

One of the crucial advantages of microfluidic OOAC consists in the ability to incorporate biomechanical stimuli within the modelling system. Constant biomechanical stimulation regulates cell behaviour in vivo, governing development, growth and maturation as well as tissue homeostasis. Biomechanics are able to enhance, eliminate or even reverse the response to a given biochemical cue or specific drug candidate or a regenerative therapeutic intervention. The incorporation of different platforms for providing physiologically relevant biomechanical stimuli is one of the main advantages of OOAC systems [23]. Particularly modelling normal and OA articular joint requires provision of highly controllable multiparametric biomechanical stress at both physiological and/or pathological levels [24]. Technically, the challenge to design mechanical stimulation methods that are adaptable for microfluidics has been addressed in the beginning by miniaturization of existent devices used for cell stimulation. Axial compression, substrate flexion or direct abutment have been micro engineered to accommodate OOAC with increased spatiotemporal control [25]. Pneumatically actuated PMDS membranes were chosen by many teams developing joint OOAC platforms due to their optical properties, gas permeability, deformability, and ease of fabrication [26]. PMDS applied compressive stresses and strains can vary from physiological (approximately 5–25%) or hyper physiological (up to approximately 30%) commonly delivered to hydrogel laden chondrocytes or progenitor cells within OOAC chambers. The delivery of hyper physiological compression was proven to increase expression of catabolic and inflammatory markers in chondrocytes and shift to a hypertrophic phenotype, proving its utility in studying OA pathophysiology [27].

Several technical issues are currently challenging technical development of biomechanically active joint OAC devices. The modality of delivering microscale loads at a cellular level, the choice of direction of stress application (horizontal versus vertical) has proven technically demanding. The necessity of resolving time as well as life imaging options further complicates the choice of material. Further development of OOAC devices asks for advanced materials that are simultaneously optically transparent, permeable to gas, with high deformability and elasticity and easy to miniaturize and fabricate [24, 28]. One of the most commonly used material for fabricating load delivering solutions- PMDS- has good biocompatibility, flexibility, optical transparency, proved compliant with most microfabrication techniques, is known to absorb hydrophobic molecules interfering with toxicity and pharmacokinetic studies [29]. Plasma or UV preconditioning, metal or sol-gel coating can be used for reducing PMDS surface energy making it more suitable for joint-on-a-chip device [30]. A particular challenge has proven to be the fact that commonly used hydrogels cannot reproduce simultaneously cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM) biomechanics and bioactivity (protein adsorption and cell adhesion). Synthetic poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG)-based hydrogels that can be tuned to mimic matrix stiffness decrease chondrocyte adhesion and protein adsorption [31]. Choice of natural or synthetic hydrogel for embedding cells might be oriented by the scope of OOAC device, a fact that needs further development {32]. To date the field of biomaterials suitable for modelling bone, synovial, adipose tissue micro compartments is expanding due to increasing interest in the design and manufacture of OOAC.

Modelling inflammation using ooacs

Local and systemic inflammation is one of the most common pathological mechanisms involved in OA occurrence and progression. Since synovial tissue is the main inflammatory tissue within the joint, OOACs that mimic synovial lining or the addition of synovial tissue within a micro joint device introduced the ability to study the effect of inflammation on OA occurrence, progression as well as possible modalities to circumvent it. A three-dimensional microfluidic device including rheumatoid arthritis patient derived synovial and chondral organoids simulating tissue cross talk during development and disease that could be easily replicated to include OA samples [33]. The synovial OOAC-like device developed for studying inflammatory dynamics during rheumatoid arthritis (RA) included a label free optical monitoring bio sensing device that was able to detect transforming necrosis alpha (TNF alpha) induced changes within RA patient derived synovial organoids [34]. A miniaturized device of increased complexity containing human MSC denominated Mini Joint was developed aiming to more closely reproduce articular joint complexity. The Mini Joint containing tissue engineered osteochondral complex, synovial-like fibrous tissue, and adipose tissue integrated into a microfluidic-connected bioreactor was shown to mimic tissue interaction and to exhibit relevant changes when exposed to inflammatory mediators (Il 1β) [35]. The Mini Joint, the first microdevice incorporating mostly all tissues within synovial joints, can be adapted to test different routes of drug administration (intra articular or systemic) by choice of fluid stream. The same group further developed the neu-micro-Joint, a 3D printed microdevice that can incorporate dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons from organ donors in order to model sensory neuron innervation of joint tissues. Neural activity is assessed with micro electrode arrays (MEAs) and calcium ions imaging. The device, aimed at investigating physiology as well as potential novel therapies for treating pain during OA [36].

OOAC for modelling genetic factors involved in OA occurrence and progression

Genetic factors are known to be involved in OA occurrence and progression [37]. Since OA is a multifactorial disease, concomitant and combinatorial alteration in multiple gene expression is likely to be involved. Environmental driven epigenetic factors that are operational especially during early OA stages [38] modulate a large variety of clinical phenotypes. It has been proposed that microfluidics can model gene involvement by using gene editing technology such as CRISPR-Cas on patient-derived cells to understand their role in OA progression. Hypo-chondrogenesis, could be modelled in patient-derived IPSCs by editing the collagen type II gene, COL2A1 [39] Such edited cell lines used to populate OOAC devices could offer insights regarding interaction with other joint tissue and OA progression serving as early models for drug testing. OOAC could be used as well to compare transcriptomics of microtissues generated from different patients. Comparative transcriptomics of de novo cartilage and bone matrix deposited tissues from different patients in advanced stages of OA cultivated within microfluidic devices could be used to identify potential OA risk genes and unknown predispositions to develop the disease [40].

Emerging technologies such as spatial transcriptomics and single-cell mechanistic analyses are leveraging the ability to dissect the complex microenvironments and cellular interactions that drive OA progression. They provide unprecedented resolution in characterizing molecular and cellular heterogeneity within joint tissues, offering valuable insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Spatial transcriptomics enables gene expression profiling while preserving tissue architecture, making it possible to map cellular transcriptional activity in situ [41] This technology is particularly relevant for OA, as it facilitates the study of differential gene expression in chondrocytes, synovial fibroblasts, and immune cells across various stages of the disease. By integrating it with histopathology, various molecular signatures can be correlated with specific joint microenvironments, identifying novel biomarkers and molecular pathways associated with cartilage degeneration and inflammation.

Single-cell sequencing approaches, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell proteomics, allow for the high-resolution analysis of individual cells within complex tissues. In OA, these methods have been instrumental in identifying heterogeneous cell populations, such as pro-inflammatory synovial macrophages and chondrocytes undergoing hypertrophic differentiation. Single-cell analyses have also provided insights into cell-to-cell communication networks, revealing key signalling pathways involved in cartilage breakdown, synovial inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation​ [42].

Currently, OOAC systems are developed using advanced materials and fabrication techniques to closely mimic human physiological environments. One of the most widely used materials in OOAC fabrication is polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), known for its optical transparency, gas permeability, and biocompatibility, which facilitates both gas exchange as well as live-cell imaging. However, PDMS tends to absorb small hydrophobic molecules, which can impact experimental outcomes. To counteract this, additional biomaterials such as hydrogels are frequently used. These include natural hydrogels like collagen and fibrin, which replicate extracellular matrix properties, and synthetic hydrogels such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), which provide structural support and influence cell behaviour.

The fabrication of OOAC systems relies on microfluidic technologies, allowing the creation of intricate networks of microchannels that control nutrient and oxygen supply, simulate shear stress, and guide cell differentiation. Soft lithography remains one of the most widely used microfabrication techniques, enabling the precise manipulation of cellular microenvironments. In addition to microfluidics, three-dimensional bioprinting has gained traction as a technique for constructing OOAC models with complex tissue architectures, providing a high degree of spatial control over multiple cell types and biomaterials. As these systems grow in complexity, they increasingly incorporate real-time monitoring sensors capable of tracking environmental conditions such as pH, oxygen concentration, and electrical activity. This integration allows for the continuous collection of physiological data, improving the accuracy and predictive power of these models. Furthermore, 3D bioprinting is prone to automation fact that increases reproducibility and potentially lowers costs. (Fig. 1).

OOAC models advantages and disadvantages

The microfluidic devices offer interesting alternatives for compound screening and validation of novel therapies in initial phases of development. Their degree of complexity is continuously increasing by the addition of advanced materials and methods of collecting data. Major advantages in using OOAC consist in their ability to incorporate a variety of tissue within 3D environment. OOAC can be rather cheap and simple to fabricate especially using additive manufacturing methods and offer the advantage of testing simultaneously multiple drugs [43]. The disadvantages are related to the technical complexity of such models that require specialized equipment and expertise as well as to the limited time viability and function of the cells included, fact that limits relevance for the study of chronic diseases such as OA. Moreover, collecting output data oftentimes requires termination of the experiment, challenging reproducibility as well as cost efficiency. A technical challenge is the presence of surface effect within device microcapillaries. The amount of the circulating fluids is very small, the surface effects dominate the volume effect potentially impeding the quality of the data provided. Depending on the material used for fabricating OOAC, the metabolite of interest can be absorbed on its surface and bias the results. Data collection and cross validation with patient derived samples are currently one of the biggest challenges to overcome [44]. To ensure the reliability and relevance of OOAC models, validation methods focus on multiple parameters. One approach involves comparing the functionality of OOAC systems to conventional two-dimensional cell cultures and in vivo models. Evaluating the physiological and biochemical responses of these systems helps determine how closely they mimic human organ function. Additionally, biomarker analysis plays a crucial role in assessing model accuracy, measuring the presence of specific proteins and signalling molecules indicative of normal or pathological tissue behaviour. Drug response testing further validates OOAC models, as their ability to replicate human tissue reactions to pharmacological compounds establishes their potential for preclinical drug screening and toxicity testing (Fig. 2).

Validation and relevance of OOAC models

As joint-on-a-chip models continue to evolve in complexity and fidelity, there is a growing need to integrate monitoring systems capable of non-destructive, high-throughput data collection. Similar to OOAC platforms designed to model other complex organs, joint-on-a-chip devices can incorporate physical, chemical, and biochemical sensors that enable both in-line and on-line analysis of automatically collected samples. Unlike early OOAC models, which relied on manually collected samples requiring subsequent processing, advanced systems now facilitate real-time, multiplexed analysis. This continuous and potentially automated data acquisition enhances experimental efficiency and provides dynamic feedback to inform decision-making throughout the study.

The parameters that can be monitored in OOAC systems are factors of the cell microenvironment: physicochemical properties (e.g., oxygen, pH, temperature, CO2, and osmolarity), physical (e.g., mechanical cell-cell and cell-ECM interaction), and biochemical cues (e.g., soluble metabolites, cytokines, growth factors) [45]. Cross validation of samples collected from OOAC in real time with patient derived samples is a powerful modality to establish clinical relevance of such models. Immunoassays (ELISA, multiplexed ELISA) and/or mass spectrometry proteomic analysis of patient derived synovial fluid and from OOAC circulating fluid can be matched for inquiring the clinical relevance in investigating disease and/or reparatory biomarkers. The combination of single-cell analyses and spatial transcriptomics with OoC models can further contribute to understanding OA pathophysiology. Integrating single-cell transcriptomics with joint-on-a-chip devices can help validate in vitro models against patient-derived data, improving their predictive value for drug screening and regenerative medicine applications Bulk and/or single cell RNA-seq and proteomics of patient derived cartilage, bone, synovium or adipose tissue samples can be cross compared with respective tissues generated within OOAC devices. Populating such devices with patient derived mesenchymal or IPSCs could serve as an important tool for patient stratification, for OA phenotype characterization as well as for testing stage-specific precision medicine drug or regenerative therapy. A major obstacle to overcome in the process of cross validation is the scarcity of normal and/or early OA stages patient samples that could inform further OOAC development and validation. The establishment of disease specific tissue repository including curated samples and information from normal donors could possibly address, at least in part, such issues [46].

Organoid models

Combining recent advances in stem cell and developmental biology, organoids have emerged as promising biotechnology for in vitro modelling of an increasing variety of organs and tissues [47]. Organoids are micrometer-sized three-dimensional self-organized structures that replicate basic microarchitecture and physiology of the tissue of origin. Generally, organoids are obtained from suspension cultures of pluripotent stem cells (embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, progenitors derived from IPSCs). They can contain multiple progenitors and/or adult cell types mimicking the heterogenicity of the modelled organ/tissue. When exposed to specific environmental cues, the spontaneously formed cell assembly reproduces the developmental stages of organ generation, growth and, to some extent, maturation. Starting 2009, organoid technology has gained momentum offering modalities for the study of development, disease as well as for compound testing in pharmacological studies. Among one the first reported, the long-term 3D culture of Lgr5 + stem cells lead to the development of in vitro intestinal organoids generally resembling intestinal microarchitecture and function [48]. Embryonic-stem-cell-derived retinal epithelium was shown to generate in vitro optic cup morphogenesis enabling high molecular studies of developmental cascades with potential use for drug screening and high throughput testing [49]. The process of generating organoids is becoming increasingly technologically sophisticated, progressing from relatively simple approaches such as gel encapsulation and air-liquid interface methods [50] to the creation of heterogeneous structures that model multi-tissue miniaturized systems, as well as to organoid bioprinting [51]. Generation of organoids that model articular joint reflected both technological advances in the field as well as the shift from the classical understanding of OA as merely “cartilage degeneration” to the concept of a disease of the whole joint as an organ.

Cartilage organoids

Cartilage organoids were among the first in vitro models developed to replicate articular joint structures and study the developmental processes of joint embryogenesis. These models have been useful in investigating stem cell dynamics during chondrogenesis, in defining cartilage maturation stages at a molecular level, and in providing insights into occurrence of degenerative joint diseases such as OA [52, 53].

Early cartilage organoids generated using limb bud mesenchymal cells from embryonic mice, cultured at high density on a membrane-air interface were able to closely reproduce in vitro the stages of chondrogenesis preceding endochondral ossification. This model derived detailed immunologic, immunohistochemical, and electron microscopy analyses of stages of collagen type I and II dynamics during cartilage formation, maturation and degeneration [54].

Further advancements introduced 3D aggregates formed by self-assembling chondrocytes or chondroprogenitor cells, structured as spheroids [55] or cell sheets (organoid-sheets) with the aim of producing implantable cartilage grafts [56].

Recent studies have optimized the cell suspension method combined with a cell-derived extracellular matrix, yielding cartilage organoids that contain collagen type II and proteoglycans while maintaining comparable proliferation rates between osteoarthritic (OA) and non-degenerate (ND) chondrocytes​ [57] Using such 3D structures as modular units combined with extracellular matrix mimicking scaffolds could be used for bioprinting models that reproduce cartilage layered architecture and increase potential for therapeutic applications​ [58].

Cartilage organoids obtained from human OA-like chondrocytes. An organoid model of human OA was developed by engineering pathological cartilage from its developmental progenitors. Given that human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (hBMSCs) naturally acquire hypertrophic traits upon chondrogenic priming, it was hypothesized that exposing hypertrophic hBMSC-derived micro-cartilage to pro-inflammatory cytokines at OA-relevant concentrations (similar to synovial fluid from patients with OA) would induce key disease features. The model’s applicability was assessed using mass spectrometry-based proteomics to identify potential druggable targets for reversing OA progression [59].

Osteocartilaginous organoids

To more accurately replicate the heterogeneous cellular composition and architectural complexity of articular joint perceived as an organ, efforts have advanced toward the generation of bone and cartilage organoids. Using murine induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), a sequential differentiation approach was employed to mimic bone development via endochondral ossification. Cells were first exposed to chondrogenic factors, including transforming growth factor β-3 (TGF-β3) and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2), for 15 to 45 days. This was followed by osteogenic induction for up to 73 days, resulting in structures with a chondrogenic core expressing collagen type II, aggrecan, and Sox-9, surrounded by a shell of collagen type I-expressing cells capable of depositing a mineralized matrix. This organoid model of endochondral ossification demonstrated that mature chondrocytes could further differentiate into osteoblasts and contribute to mineralized matrix deposition upon osteogenic induction. Notably, osteocytes within this model, but not chondrocytes, were found to regain pluripotency upon exposure to Yamanaka factors [60].

A scaffold-free approach using bone marrow-derived human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) embedded within their self-deposited extracellular matrix (ECM) was also shown to generate osteochondral organoids that mimic endochondral ossification. Exposure of MSC-ECM constructs to chondrogenic media induced hypertrophic ossification, both in vitro and in vivo following subcutaneous implantation in mice. This process closely resembled endochondral ossification, occurring through an N-cadherin-mediated pre-cartilage condensation mechanism [61].

To model intramembranous ossification, human iPSCs and iPSC-derived MSCs were used, with the addition of osteoinductive bio ceramic nanoparticles to enhance the osseous component. A parallel strategy involving cell-ECM structures derived from iPSCs and iPSC-derived MSCs enabled the generation of osteocartilaginous organoids through sequential exposure to osteogenic and chondrogenic factors. Additionally, adipose tissue organoids were successfully derived from both iPSCs and iPSC-derived MSCs using adipogenic media. This reflected the increasing understanding of the intraarticular fat pad as an important determinant in joint health and disease and succeeded in expanding the range of joint tissues that can be replicated through this approach [62].

Synovial organoids

Enabling the study of synovial tissue formation and inflammatory remodelling in a controlled environment have been used mainly in the context of inflammatory arthritis such as RA. A micro mass 3D synovial organ culture system was able to reproduce in vitro spontaneous synovial fibroblast organization and remodelling in inflammatory conditions closely resembling characteristic synovial hyperplasia and pannus formation in RA [63]. Such an approach could be further expanded to reflect synovial changes during OA. The development of organoid-based joint-on-a-chip co-culture systems incorporate both chondral and synovial compartments. These systems facilitate the study of reciprocal inflammatory interactions between cartilage and synovium, providing a platform to investigate joint inflammation relevant to OA [34, 35]. A proof-of-concept study reported the 3D bioprinting of synoviocyte-laden scaffolds, to develop of a 3D synovial membrane in vitro model demonstrating good cell viability. However, further research is required to validate the construct as a reliable synovial tissue model [64].

Mini joint organoids

To enhance model fidelity, ideally the model would be required to reflect the multiple cell types within the joint as well as their interactions. A “mini joint model” incorporating various joint-associated cell types and extracellular matrix (ECM) components was proposed as a potential platform for testing disease-modifying drugs for inflammatory joint diseases. This model was developed using primary adult cells from human cartilage and bone, as well as craniofacial tissues from paediatric patients, to generate self-assembling skeletal organoid tissues. Suspension culture of bone and cartilage organoids (BORG and CORG) in Matrigel resulted in the formation of osteoblast- and osteocyte-like cells embedded in a mineralized matrix, alongside osteoclast-like cells and putative endothelial cells expressing CD31 and von Willebrand factor (VWF). Cartilage organoids expressed key chondrogenic markers, including Aggrecan and Collagen type II. These models were employed to investigate responses to in vitro inflammatory-like conditions induced by interleukin-6 (IL-6), as well as the effects of adenosine receptor agonists as potential anti-inflammatory therapeutics [65].

The spontaneous emergence and consistent growth of hyaline cartilage within human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived multiple tissue organoids (MTOs) were reported in xeno- and feeder-free, chemically defined cultures. At 15 weeks, RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis demonstrated that the cartilaginous tissue within MTOs closely resembled human foetal limb bud and growth plate chondrocytes, indicating the spontaneous adoption of a growth plate cartilage phenotype [66].

The process of generating organoids typically involves 3D culture techniques, in which stem cells or progenitor cells are embedded within extracellular matrix-like hydrogels. Over time, these cells self-organize, mimicking the structural and functional characteristics of the tissue they are derived from. Advances in bioprinting have further enhanced organoid technology, enabling the controlled deposition of cells and extracellular matrix components to form highly organized, multilayered structures. Additionally, microfluidic platforms have been integrated into organoid culture systems to ensure the continuous exchange of nutrients and removal of metabolic waste, improving tissue viability and functional outcomes.

Validating the physiological relevance of organoid models requires multiple approaches. Histological analysis allows for the evaluation of tissue architecture, ensuring that organoids accurately replicate the cellular composition and organization of the target organ. Functional assays assess the metabolic activity, enzyme production, or hormone secretion of organoids, verifying that they exhibit appropriate physiological responses. Gene and protein expression profiling provides further confirmation of cellular identity and differentiation status, ensuring that the organoids accurately model specific developmental and disease-related processes.

By integrating advanced biomaterials, fabrication techniques, and validation methods, both OOAC and organoid technologies continue to evolve, providing increasingly reliable in vitro models for studying human physiology and disease. These developments hold significant promise for drug discovery, regenerative medicine, and personalized therapeutic strategies, helping to bridge the gap between preclinical research and clinical applications.

Advantages and disadvantages; progress and challenges in organoid technology

Organoid models offer several advantages, justifying their increasing relevance in OA research. They enable high-throughput genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses, providing insights into tissue-specific gene expression and regulatory networks. Organoid technology can be used to generate patient-specific in vitro models while performing gene editing offers an unprecedented possibility for describing particularities of joint development, regeneration as well as OA occurrence and progress. Additionally, the ability to perform gene editing in organoids facilitates functional genomic studies and the investigation of disease mechanisms. Unlike traditional 2D cultures, organoids better recapitulate the 3D architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and microenvironment of native tissues, improving physiological relevance. Their adaptability for co-culture systems, integration with microfluidics, and compatibility with high-content screening further strengthens their potential for drug discovery and regenerative medicine.

In sum, organoids can recapitulate the complex architecture and functionality of native articular joint tissues, providing a more accurate representation of physiology compared to traditional monolayer cultures. ​ These models facilitate the study of OA pathogenesis by potentially mimicking cell and tissue cross talk as well as the microenvironment of human joint, enabling the investigation of cellular interactions and molecular pathways involved in disease progression. ​Organoids offer a platform for high-throughput screening of potential therapeutic agents, allowing for the assessment of drug efficacy and toxicity. ​Utilizing organoids can decrease reliance on animal models, addressing ethical concerns and potentially providing more human-specific data.​.

Despite these advantages, organoid technology faces several challenges, including limited reproducibility, variability in morphology and function, and genetic instability over prolonged culture periods [67]. Addressing these issues requires the establishment of standardized protocols for organoid generation, quality control, and data collection to improve reliability and cross-study comparability. One potential approach to enhancing standardization is the use of microaggregates embedded in biomaterials, such as agarose gel, which has been reported to improve the efficiency of periosteum stem cell-derived chondrogenic micro-mass formation, reducing the dependency on chondrogenic growth factors [68]. Such high-throughput platforms enable the production of organoids with controlled cellular composition and predictable phenotypes, offering significant potential for both disease modeling and therapeutic applications.

To improve the accuracy of data collected from organoid models, comprehensive gene and protein expression analyses must be performed with statistical rigor. The inclusion of appropriate control samples, such as foetal or paediatric tissues, while ethically challenging, should be prioritized whenever feasible. Since organoid formation recapitulates the developmental stages of a given tissue, modelling age-related diseases presents inherent limitations, as these models often lack maturity and cellular changes associated with aging. One of the most common strategies to address this limitation is the comparison of organoids derived from young and aged donors. Reduced efficiency of organoid formation has been documented in aged mice and humans, often linked to stem cell dysfunction and epigenetic modifications affecting Wnt signalling [69, 70].

To model age-related changes, attempts have been made to induce “hallmarks of aging”—a set of biological alterations associated with aging, including genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic modifications, proteostasis decline, metabolic dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and impaired intercellular communication [71]. Organoid models of the gut, liver, and skin have demonstrated the feasibility of simulating several age-associated alterations [72], and this approach could potentially be applied to study degenerative joint diseases in the future.

Mechanical stimulation is well recognized to have a fundamental role in the modelling of biomechanically responsive tissues, including articular joints [73]. The integration of organoid technology with microfluidics and 3D printing has been proposed to construct complex models that enable micro- and nanoscale mechanical stimulation [74]. In another approach, a system has been developed that combines microfluidic technology with cellular aggregates, allowing mechanical stimulation and characterization of cartilage micro-pellets. A custom-designed device delivers pressure to the confined cartilage microspheres while allowing for mechanical and molecular characterization [75]. Furthermore, the combination of microfluidics and organoid technology enables the incorporation of multiple tissue types within a single modelling system [33], facilitating the inclusion of synovial, adipose, and endothelial components to better mimic joint physiology and disease progression.

In summary, limitation exists, many of them currently in the process of being addressed, Structural limitation existent in earlier models relying on cartilage tissue or cartilage and bone lacking the full complexity of native joint structures, are addressed by attempts in adding vascularization and innervation, which are crucial for comprehensive OA modelling.

Batch-to-batch variability in organoid cultures can lead to inconsistencies in experimental outcomes, posing challenges for reproducibility and data interpretation. Culture automation and complex laboratory workflow digitalization consistently increase reproducibility while addressing variability. (Fig. 3).

The generation and maintenance of organoids, however, still require specialized skills and equipment, which limit their accessibility to some research laboratories. Organoids may not fully capture the systemic aspects of OA, such as immune system interactions and whole-joint biomechanics, limiting their applicability in certain research contexts.

Computer modelling and artificial intelligence – from organoid research to clinical disease predictive models

Computer modelling

Could be potentially used for adjusting mechanobiology to organoid research. A computation system biology approach was found able to predict complex molecular interactions that convert physical signals, especially biomechanics into cellular response(s) resulting in cartilage development, homeostasis or degeneration. Large scale representations of such interactions in health and disease of articular joints inform OA management, especially in the case of early OA that is only modestly reproduced by existent models. A network-based model was proposed in the form of a continuous dynamical system integrating interactome represented by mechano-sensitive cellular receptors, second messengers, transcription factors and proteins. The network model demonstrated that under physio-osmotic initial conditions, the cell achieves an anabolic state. In contrast, perturbations induced by pro-inflammatory and supraphysiological mechanical loads lead to a catabolic profile, as represented by catabolic chondrocyte profile. The model replicated 94% of experimental results available from literature and confirmed the fact that anti-inflammatory therapies are the most efficient way to restore anabolic states. Noticeably, this finding holds true for the situations where structural protein production has not yet been impaired [76].

Systems modelling

using network-based models (NBM) is regarded as having an increasing impact in the field of regenerative medicine due to scalability and versatility in accommodating extensive biological information [77]. A mathematical model of the regulatory network responsible for phenotypic switch was proposed for the in-silico screening of the effect of various factors on chondrocyte maintenance and hypertrophy. An elaborate gene network was built to incorporate many factors considered crucial for chondrocyte maintenance and hypertrophy, centred on the primordial transcription factors chondrogenic SRY-Box 9 SOX9 and osteogenic Runt-related transcription factor 2 RUNX2. By combining multiple signaling pathways the model was presented to enable a more holistic view of cartilage biology, and the extent to which different factors influence phenotypic switch to hypertrophy [78].

ECHO, the executable chondrocyte, uses a dynamic network ANIMO (Analysis of Networks with Interactive Modelling) to integrate 7 signalling pathways in a network containing 50 nodes and about 200 interactions. This In silico modelling of was able to predict chondrocyte fate decision during stem cell differentiation and OA progression and validated using wet lab transcriptomics. Dickkopf-1 DKK1 previously associated with inflammation-based bone loss, Wnt antagonist Frizzled related protein FRZB and gremlin 1, BMP antagonist (GRM1) was found to prevent Interleukin 1 beta (IL1β)-induced matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression in human chondrocytes offering a basis for therapeutic targeting [79]. A computer simulation model was able to predict age related changes in chondrocytes. Data derived from histology and quantitative immunohistology in cartilage samples of mice for 3 to 30 months with respect to morphology, cellular changes, matrix loss were used to inform a computer model. The model, designed as a biochemical network encoded in the Systems Biology Markup Language combined with stochastic simulations. demonstrated a progressive age-related loss of cartilage matrix and cellular changes. increase in oxidative stress and MMP-13, Oxidative damage and the IL-1 pathway were found important in initiating changes that lead to OA development [80].

Metabolic flux analysis and computer modelling

Were combined in an effort to elucidate chondrocyte metabolism fluctuation in response to dynamic loading. The model was able to reveal synchronization of energy metabolism and increased protein synthesis as result of dynamic compression in human chondrocyte line [81]. A network-based computer model was built to predict the effect of soluble cytokines on early OA onset. The model, based on computing molecular interactions during biosynthesis, inflammation and degradative chondrocyte activity, aimed to build an optimal regulatory network that controls chondrocyte metabolism based on detectable soluble proteins [82].

Computational models that simulate OA processes require validation on multiple scales

Comparative analysis with experimental cell and tissue models

Computer models predicting chondrocyte mechanotransduction, cartilage breakdown, and inflammatory cascades are validated against in vitro organ-on-a-chip and organoid systems. Gene expression, proteomic signatures, and cytokine profiles from these biological models are compared to computationally simulated responses.

Systems biology validation against transcriptomic and proteomic data

Network-based models that simulate osteoarthritis at a molecular level are tested using large-scale omics datasets, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics. By ensuring the model-generated interaction networks align with real biological pathways, researchers confirm their predictive robustness.

Cross-referencing with clinical information on disease progression

Computational models predicting osteoarthritis onset and severity are validated against longitudinal patient studies. For instance, algorithms simulating how joint inflammation triggers cartilage erosion are assessed using synovial fluid biomarker profiles, clinical imaging and patient record data over time.

Multi-tissue and whole-joint simulation

Joint-level models incorporating cartilage, synovium, and bone interactions are validated by multiscale modelling approaches, where cell- and tissue-level experimental data (such as cell culture, organoids, explant tissue and cadaveric joints) is integrated with whole-joint computational models to ensure consistency across scales.

While currently the proposed computer models are operational in predicting several specific changes in chondrocyte activity, there is still the need for an integrated perspective that allows for combination of multiple factors (mechano-transduction, inflammation, age-related changes).

A significant challenge will involve computationally predicting the reactivity of various cell and tissue types present in articular joints and their dynamic responses to systemic, local and regional metabolic and inflammatory fluctuations.

In summary, computer models can offer patient-specific predictions aiding clinical decision making in regard to timing of intervention. Models facilitate the exploration of complex interactions within joint structures, enhancing understanding of OA pathogenesis.​.

Such models can process extensive datasets, identifying patterns and potential biomarkers for early OA detection. ​ Of particular relevance relies on assistance for evaluating the burden of OA, informing public health strategies and preventive measures. Main disadvantages, similar to other computer-based modelling are represented by the quality of the data needed to inform existent and incoming algorithms. Moreover, ensuring computational models accurately reflect biological reality requires extensive validation against experimental and clinical data. Developing and running detailed models can be resource-intensive, necessitating specialized expertise and computational infrastructure. In strict dependence on the data fed for their establishment, computer models may not account for individual patient variability, limiting the applicability of findings across diverse populations (Fig. 4).

Current advantages of using computer modeling in osteoarthritis research and clinical applications

Finite element (FE) models and finite element analysis (FEA)

Computer models can be used to predict the onset and progression of OA degenerative processes at a whole joint level. FE models can assess musculoskeletal tissue loading and predict their degradation during various forms of normal or pathological loading [76]. Based on patient derived gait analysis data (gait kinematic and kinesis) musculoskeletal models can be used to identify zones of excessive loading therefore predicting the risk of overuse and OA onset. Knee adduction moment (KAM) in the first gait peak was increased in patients with medial compartment knee OA. KAM was found to be strongly correlated to medial knee contact force (mKFC). Authors reasoned that using modalities of gait alteration aimed at reducing mKFC could prevent OA progression. Such models can only estimate overall joint loading and fail to identify tissue specific force distribution. Moreover, since input data such as bone lengths, muscle attachment sites, joint centres are based on reference anatomic values, results are difficult to be adapted to be patient specific [83]. The introduction of computer tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data within the gait analysis workflow could be used to personalize this approach [84]. Since the acquisition of patient specific MRI data is laborious and resources consuming, a population-based statistical shape model was proposed to offer an efficient alternative. Creation of a musculoskeletal atlas project (MAP) could aid reconstruction of subject-specific tibiofemoral bone and cartilage models by means of a generic data set and several personalized geometric features [85].

In order to model tissue specific mechanical stress and therefore to potentially predict OA onset, FE models are employed for calculating bone and cartilage mechanical loads thought to be involved in joint degeneration. FE analysis (FEA) is presented as a non-invasive approach to study the impact of various biomechanical risk factors on articular joints [86]. FEA found its place in linking cartilage maintenance and degeneration mechanisms to whole joint organ biomechanics under various normal and pathological loading conditions. Thus, FEA algorithms have been proposed to predict cartilage proteoglycan loss during normal and pathological loading [87], ground substance softening and collagen damage during excessive loading [88], collagen fibre degeneration [89]. A more recent development modelled serval major cartilage tissue changes during OA (proteoglycan content, fluid flow, and collagen fibril network) tested for normal and abnormal loading conditions. The model was able to predict zonal changes in cartilage composition and could enable prediction of knee collagen degeneration and proteoglycan loss during the OA [90]. FE models simulating the effect of strain rate on collagen matrix and on non-fibrillar ECM showed that predictions are dependent on the theory used to build the model (local damage versus nonlocal approach) [91]. Phenomenological damage models consider tissue failure as a result of cell and matrix loss adjusted to several parameters such as body mass index, age and metabolic activity [92].

FE models of subchondral bone loss during OA make use of information obtained from quantitative computer tomography (QCT) in terms of bone structural characteristics (density, geometry). QCT-FE models integrate the contribution of all factors influencing bone characteristics at subchondral interface non-invasively and in a patient-specific manner [93]. Patient specificity could be improved using advanced imagistic such as MRI/CT combined with contrast agents deriving non-invasive methods for predicting early OA onset [94]. Combined FE models of bone and cartilage have been proposed. Models consisting of articular cartilage, calcified cartilage, subchondral cortical bone and subchondral trabecular bone were built aiming to investigate the effect of increased tissue permeability during OA. Two poro-elastic FE models were composed, one of the bone cartilage units during axial compression and of articular cartilage (AC) behaviour during spherical indentation, respectively. The model indicated that AC permeability stands as the primary determinant of the poromechanical response. In simulation including osteoarthritic AC proprieties found that fluid velocities increased in magnitude and were distributed over a smaller area compared to normal AC potentially leading to cell death, impaired joint lubrification and increased remodelling [95].

Use and limitations of FE models and FEA

As general workflow, FEA is carried out in three stages: a pre-processing stage that involves preparing the model in terms of geometry, mesh and materials that best mimic the constitutive tissue structure and physical behaviour. The processing stage involves the use of adequate algorithms to solve the mathematical problem, while post-processing involves analysing the results and drawing conclusions. Verification of results and validation is required to ascertain the degree of predictability inferred by the model. For the situation of major joint (such as knee joint FEAs), the time required to generate models, with accurate three-dimensional (3D) geometries and feasible FE meshes that preserves subject specificity is the main drawback for clinical use, since it can prove resource intensive. Several working days required to predict FEA based prediction could interfere with clinical decision making and patient welfare. Validation remains, however, one of the most challenging aspects of FEA. In contrast with models reporting generic specificity or specificity on in vitro testing specimens, validation on in vivo models has proven challenging to include [96]. Imagistic data such as MRI and fluoroscopy combined with gait analysis records have been used to validate predictions of forces acting on tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints, displacement of knee anatomic components during motion as well as cartilage deformation [97–99]. A proper model of OA progression should, however, include predictions regarding qualitative and quantitative aspects of joint components at macro and microscopical levels. In this case, validation against existing OA grading systems would require, aside Imagistics (Rx or MRI), direct observation of joint components (such as during surgery or arthroscopic surgery) or histopathological examination that are ethically challenging to perform for the only purpose of validating a model. In this context, engaging in establishment and validation of FE models within the context of larger, cohort studies or gaining access to large patient electronic medical records or databases is not only desirable but, perhaps, mandatory. Osteoarthritis Initiative database is already available and includes open access imagistic, biomarker and clinical assessment data results from 4796 patients with a 8 year follow up [100]. Expanding availability of such information for a worldwide representation of patient diversity could consistently improve FEA design, validation and implementation. Open or at least on demand access to public tissue repository with representative curated tissue specimens backed by consistent and coherent digital records regarding patient demographics, co-morbidities, specific and concurrent therapies, would facilitate design and validation of OA models as well. Multicentred Osteoarthritis study (MOST) for example, shares biospecimen data for researchers on demand by means of National Institute on Aging (NIA) research databank [101].

FE models and FEA have been used to understand the effect of meniscectomies on knee force distribution and consecutive cartilage stress [102], or for predicting the effect of alignment osteotomies around the knee on force distribution [103]. Such approaches could inform operative decision making regarding intra or extraarticular surgeries aimed at preventing OA progression [104] FEA was used as well to inform non operative interventions such as foot orthosis or gait altering. FEA based gait retraining for reducing knee abduction moment (KAM) in patients with medial knee OA was used in a clinical trial to provide real time feedback to patients during the training [105]. Expanding such approaches to other rehabilitation protocols and combining them with patient records data and prospective studies on duration of medication free interval or time-to total knee prosthesis could serve as efficient models on OA prediction .

FEA models must be rigorously validated against real-world biomechanical data and patient-derived information. Several validation strategies address key parameters of the model and consist of experimental validation via biomechanical testing; FEA predictions of cartilage and subchondral bone stress distribution are often compared with mechanical testing on cadaveric joints or in vitro tissue-engineered constructs. Stress-strain relationships, deformation patterns, and fracture mechanics are measured using pressure-sensitive films, digital image correlation, or nanoindentation techniques, ensuring that FEA outputs align with experimental observations. Motion analysis and gait data correlation: in clinical settings, motion capture systems coupled with force plate measurements are used to assess joint loading and movement patterns in trauma or OA patients. FEA models incorporating subject-specific gait data can be validated by comparing predicted contact forces, ligament strain, and cartilage stress distribution with measured ground reaction forces and joint kinematics.

Radiographic and MRI-based verification

Advanced imaging techniques, such MRI and CT are used to validate FEA-based cartilage degradation models. Changes in cartilage thickness, bone density, and joint morphology observed in longitudinal patient MRI scans are compared to FEA-predicted structural deterioration, establishing the model’s ability to replicate OA progression.

Cross-validation with population-based statistical models

to personalize FEA simulations, patient-specific data is incorporated using statistical shape modelling (SSM), which generates probabilistic variations of joint anatomy and loading. These models are validated against large datasets, such as the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) cohort, ensuring they generalize across diverse populations.

In summary, FEA facilitates the simulation of mechanical behaviour within articular joints, allowing for the identification of regions susceptible to mechanical failure associated with OA.

Subject-Specific Modeling: By integrating individual anatomical and mechanical properties, FEA enables personalized assessments of joint mechanics. FEA can estimate stress distribution and potential cartilage degeneration, aiding in the prediction of OA onset and progression, of importance in early OA diagnosis and intervention strategies. ​FEA allows for the assessment of biomechanical outcomes of various surgical procedures, particularly after high tibial osteotomy (HTO), providing insights into the effectiveness and potential risks associated with this intervention. Main disadvantages are that, to date, FEA are computationally as well as time intensive, limiting their clinical applicability. The accuracy of FEA outcomes heavily relies on the quality of input data, including precise anatomical geometries and material properties, which may not always be readily available or accurately measurable. ​Ensuring that FEA models accurately represent real life conditions necessitates extensive validation against experimental or clinical data, which can be resource-intensive and complex. ​FEA models incorporate assumptions and simplifications, such as homogeneous material properties, which may not fully capture the complex, heterogeneous nature of biological tissues, potentially affecting the accuracy of simulations. (Fig. 5).

FE and FEA can offer valuable insights into OA biomechanics contributing to treatment planning. Appropriate validation, accurate data input, and proper interpretation of results are mandatory to increase model fidelity. FE models that include prediction of structural changes within the joint at tissue, cell and molecular level could be further developed. Artificial intelligence (AI)

Solutions have been proposed for predicting articular joint functioning using baseline input data. Using data from the National Institute of Health Osteoarthritis Biomarkers Consortium, a machine learning approach was able to predict the onset of knee osteoarthritis with a relatively high area under curve (AUC) and confidence interval indexes. The history of knee injury and medial knee narrowing on plain radiographs were identified as having the highest Depp Lift gradient hence the highest predictive impact of knee OA with smoking and metabolic syndrome scoring immediately below [106]. Using 8-year data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI): plain radiographs joint space width measurements pain scores from the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) questionnaires, statistical modelling was used to predict longitudinal knee OA progression [107]. Disease progression in terms of structural deterioration (joint space narrowing) and symptoms could be formulated with high AUC values. Deep learning (DL) has been identified as an efficient tool for automatic interpretation of knee radiographs. In a systematic review of the literature, AI and machine learning were found promising in automatic grading of plain knee radiographs and in predicting the need for TKA as well as in predicting postoperative outcomes [108]. Currently, imagine analysis, image segmentation and feature extraction using ML and deep learning have been used to construct tools for automatic diagnostic and grading in OA. Prediction of disease progression as well as early biomarkers identification (especially extracted from high through put data generated with omics technologies) have a remarkable potential in impacting the field of OA modelling, drug target discovery and compound testing.

Generative modelling, such as generative adversarial neural networks, has been employed to create synthetic medical images. Generative models were able to generate high-quality synthetic DeepFake knee X-rays that reflect varying degrees of OA severity. A survey on medical experts resulted in more DeepFake knee X-rays considered to be the real ones than the reverse These synthetic images were used to augment training datasets, enhancing the performance of machine learning models in diagnosing the stage and predicting OA progression [109].

Multimodal data fusion involves integrating diverse data types—such as clinical records, imaging studies, and genetic information aimed at improving the accuracy of predictive models. Combining data from multiple sources has led to more precise assessments of disease progression and patient outcomes. For example, a study proposed a fusion model that integrates X-ray, MRI, and clinical information into a single network, resulting in improved classification of knee OA severity [110].

Another multimodal fusion machine learning model incorporated raw radiographic data, clinical examination results (namely WOMAC score) anthropometric data and patient medical history to predict OA progression. This model demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional logistic regression methods [111].

AI model validation AI models are increasingly used to predict disease onset, progression, and treatment response and can offer consistent upgrade in OA modelling particularly in combination with other existent technologies. However, ensuring their reliability requires strict validation methodologies based on:

Training on large, high-quality datasets

AI models must be trained and validated using extensive, diverse datasets, such as those from electronic health records (EHRs), imaging databases, and biomarker repositories. Ensuring data diversity prevents biases and improves generalizability across populations.

Cross-validation and external testing

Machine learning models undergo k-fold cross-validation, where the dataset is split into training and testing subsets multiple times, reducing overfitting. Additionally, validation on independent, external datasets from different geographic regions or clinical settings confirms their applicability beyond the original training data.

Explainability and interpretability

Validation AI models, particularly deep learning networks, are often criticized as black-box systems. To enhance interpretability, methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) are used to validate whether AI-generated predictions align with known OA pathophysiological mechanisms.

Benchmarking against expert clinical assessments

AI algorithms developed for grading OA and progression prediction are validated by comparing their outputs with expert radiologists’ assessments using standardized scoring systems, (e.g. the Kellgren-Lawrence RX grading scale) Interobserver agreement analysis between AI models and human experts ensures diagnostic accuracy.

Longitudinal validation through predictive modelling

AI models trained to forecast O A progression are validated using time-series analysis. By comparing predicted joint space narrowing, pain progression, and functional decline with real patient follow-up data, their ability to anticipate disease trajectories is tested.

Synthetic data generation and augmentation validation

To improve AI model performance, GANs create synthetic OA imaging datasets, allowing for validation in cases where real data is limited. These synthetic datasets are tested against real-world images and/or presented to expert clinical assessment to ensure consistency in model learning. (Figures 6 and 7).

Advantages and disadvantages. Challenges and limitations in the development of AI based models

AI models, particularly ML and deep learning (DL) algorithms, have the ability to radically improve the prediction of OA onset and progression by analysing large-scale datasets from medical imaging, genomic data, and patient records. AI enhances radiographic OA assessment by enabling precise segmentation and classification of joint structures, improving early detection of cartilage degeneration, can integrate patient-specific data to tailor treatment strategies, predicting responses to pharmacological and surgical interventions. AI can rapidly process vast amounts of clinical, biomechanical, and omics data, accelerating drug discovery and biomarker identification for OA. By relying on objective data-driven approaches, AI minimizes inter- observer variability in OA diagnosis and grading compared to traditional manual assessments.

However, as for any computer-based model, several existent disadvantages are still limiting their widespread application. AI models require large, well-annotated datasets, while biases in training data can lead to inaccurate predictions or overfitting. (Fig. 8).

Current advantages and disadvantages of using machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) in modeling osteoarthritis

Limitation in developing AI based models for diagnostic, disease modelling and therapeutic follow up begin therefore with data availability and quality: AI models, particularly deep learning algorithms, require large amounts of high-quality data to achieve performance [112]. Obtaining diverse and comprehensive datasets that cover various stages of OA progression and include longitudinal data can be challenging. The accessibility of such large data sets is hampered by ethical, regulatory and financier reasons that need to be addressed. Interpretability represents another important challenge. Deep learning models, especially complex ones like convolutional neural networks, are often considered “black boxes” as they lack transparency in their decision-making process. Interpreting the results and understanding why the model makes a particular prediction can be difficult, hindering its adoption in clinical settings where interpretability is crucial [113]. Furthermore, AI models may struggle to generalize well to unseen data or data from different populations. Models trained on data from one demographic or geographical region may not perform as effectively on data from another region, limiting their widespread applicability. In strict relation with interpretability and applicability, AI models can inherit biases present within the training data, potentially leading to biased predictions or recommendations. It’s essential to ensure that AI models are fair and do not disproportionately impact certain groups of patients [114]. Persistent lack of longitudinal data is continuously affecting AI based disease prediction regarding OA as well as other late onset diseases. Long-term studies with consistent follow-up data are essential for understanding disease progression accurately. However, such longitudinal datasets can be scarce, resource intensive, making it challenging to develop models that effectively capture the dynamic changes in OA over time [115]. While AI models may perform well in performing image interpretation, diagnostic or prediction of disease progression, it is essential to enable translation of these predictions into clinically meaningful outcomes that can guide treatment decisions and improve patient outcomes [116]. The set up of clinical studies that validate AI based diagnostic and disease progression on large groups of patients with long term follow up that inform clinical decision making presents similar time and financing constraints as those already presented. (Fig. 9).

Regulatory Approval and Adoption: Integrating AI models into clinical practice requires regulatory approval and acceptance from healthcare professionals. Ensuring that AI tools meet regulatory standards and are easy to use in a clinical setting is a significant challenge and needs to be backed by evidence-based medicine generating good quality clinical trials. Last but not least, creation, training and validation of AI models often require access to sensitive patient data, raising concerns about patient privacy and data security. Developing robust data privacy protocols is crucial to address these concerns.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has recently emphasized the development and implementation of New Alternative Methods (NAMs) to reduce reliance on animal testing. In 2023, the FDA established the New Alternative Methods Program, aiming to “spur the adoption of alternative methods for regulatory use that can replace, reduce, and refine animal testing (the 3Rs).” This initiative focuses on qualifying alternative methods, providing clear guidelines, and conducting applied research to advance new policies and guidance.

Additionally, the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, enacted in 2023, allows for the use of advanced technologies, including artificial intelligence and organ-on-a-chip systems, as alternatives to animal testing in drug development. This legislative change reflects a shift towards more humane and scientifically advanced testing approaches.

These developments reflect the FDA’s commitment to integrating innovative, humanistic value-based methodologies into regulatory science, aimed at enhancing the predictivity and efficiency of safety and efficacy assessments while reducing animal use.

Overcoming these limitations will require collaboration between AI researchers, clinicians, and policymakers. Addressing data challenges, improving model interpretability, and conducting well-designed longitudinal studies are some of the key areas that need attention to unlock the full potential of AI in disease progression modelling for OA.

An integrative approach- combining organoid, OOAC and AI for articular joint modelling

The combination of organoid, OOAC systems, and AI has the ability to introduce significant advancement in modelling diseases. Each approach provides distinct benefits: OOAC enables biomechanical stimulation and microenvironment control, organoids capture cellular heterogeneity and self-organization, while AI can be used for predictive modelling, pattern recognition, and therapeutic optimization. AI-driven computational modelling has been employed to simulate mechanotransduction and inflammatory cascades, revealing that pro-inflammatory cytokine perturbations accelerate OA progression, whereas early intervention with anti-inflammatory agents can restore chondrocyte homeostasis [76]. A FE based computational modelling that integrates information regarding proteoglycan loss and inflammatory cytokine presence to predict OA-like matrix degradation [117] could be used together with OOAC devices to optimize experimental conditions and drug screening protocols [118]. AI-based algorithms have been used to enhance microfluidic flow control, ensuring precise nutrient diffusion and biomechanical stimulation to replicate in vivo joint loading conditions.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The fields of scientific advancement and disease modelling are undergoing radical changes with accelerating technological progress and the rise of the information age. While in vitro tests and animal models provide insight into disease mechanisms, their ability to fully capture human pathology is limited. Osteoarthritis modelling stands to benefit tremendously from innovative approaches that overcome such constraints, pushing products more rapidly to the phase of clinical testing. Through sophisticated computational techniques incorporating vast amounts of multi-omics data, models may soon emulate the complexity and timescales of slowly progressing, early-life onset conditions in ways traditional methods cannot. By refining products virtually before intensive human studies, modelling holds promise to accelerate therapeutic development while avoiding unnecessary risks to participants. Continued innovations hold potential to revolutionize how insights into disease aetiology, progression and treatment response are gained.

Micro- and nano-physiological models, such as OOAC systems, are becoming increasingly sophisticated, successfully replicating the intricated structural and functional features of the articular joint. However, these models still face limitations in supporting large-scale testing and high-throughput screening of compounds. As organoids grow in complexity, the challenge will be to enhance their reproducibility and phenotype stability, while also developing approaches to simulate the complex and decisive interactions with the immune, circulatory, and metabolic systems [119].

To advance computer models of cellular interactions, they must be integrated hierarchically to accurately capture changes across tissue, organ, and whole-body levels. In addition, the articular joint, as a mechanosensitive organ, must have its responses to mechanical stimuli represented in both bottom-up and top-down processes. The challenge lies in tracking the molecular and cellular turnover driven by mechanical stimulation, as well as the disruptions caused by both excessive load and insufficient mechanical stress, all the way up to the level of the entire joint. Existing technologies have yet to overcome this hurdle effectively. It is expected that a combination of omics technologies, spatial biology and computer modelling will be able to integrate this finding into a detailed model of joint function reflecting both health and disease.

Capturing the impact of immune and metabolic systemic disorders on articular joint activity, along with its connections to an individual’s social and psychological environment, requires an integrated approach. This would likely involve combining finite element analysis (FEA), advanced computer modelling, gene expression studies, and insights into epigenetic changes. To date, the articular joint has yet to be modelled comprehensively as a whole. A significant gap exists between computer models that rely on biological data (such as omics), clinical data (patient records), and epidemiological data (including longitudinal studies on health and disease).

It is worth noting that so far, social and psychological dimensions have not been sought as being a part of joint functioning despite the obvious importance of mobility in individual functioning within respective ecosystems.

For a model to be truly effective, it must encompass all levels of system integration as well as all stages of testing, much like the process used for developing new drugs. From proteins and cells to the full range of tissues that make up the articular joint—and even patient wellbeing and satisfaction—all aspects must be realistically represented in a successful OA model.

Advanced technologies, such as micro-physiological systems, organoids, computer models, and artificial intelligence, have the potential to drive this integrative approach forward, despite the challenges it presents. In particular, AI and computational modelling offer powerful tools to enhance our understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying osteoarthritis. By analysing large and diverse datasets—from gene expression profiles to patient-reported outcomes—these technologies can reveal patterns and connections that might remain hidden using traditional methods. Psychological profile and social integration are increasingly recognized to influence immunity and metabolism. Integrating available data and planning for the systematic longitudinal collection of relevant information will add another dimension to successful modelling. In the quest for a multiscale, patient specific OA model, integration of already existent as well as emerging technologies and AI could add depth while adding the ability to offer an multi-hierarchical integrative approach.

The integration of “physical” models within silico modelling from gene and protein networks to gait analysis, and image-based prediction of OA progression has already made possible. Integration of neurological and neuropsychic components, impact of environmental challenges or socio-economic status, have yet to be sought. Such complex multileveled perspective in modelling normal and diseased joints are yet to be conceived. Combining these approaches into a “virtual patient” framework is undoubtedly challenging, but it is achievable with existing technologies.

To achieve successful models, several fundamental conditions need to be met. First, the extension of large omics data availability stands as a mandatory prerequisite. Additionally, ensuring secure access and sharing of patient record information is essential. Disease-specific, well-curated tissue repository banks that encompass demographic and geographic diversity are also important for both the establishment as well as for model validation. Furthermore, successful models require multidisciplinary and transdisciplinary collaboration in each and every step, from design to validation and verification.

Overall, with the right advancements in data availability, access, and collaboration, establishing, testing, and validating such complex models becomes feasible, largely opening an era of novel, aiming animal free testing technologies.
