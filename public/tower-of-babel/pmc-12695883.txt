Quantum walk comb in a dual waveguide quantum cascade laser
Authors: Alessio Cargioli, Miguel Montesinos Ballester, Sonja Gantner, Emilio Gini, Mattias Beck, Jerome Faist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRing quantum cascade lasers (QCLs) have recently demonstrated very stable active comb formation in the form of a quantum walk comb which takes advantage of very short gain recovery mechanisms, inducing liquid properties to the light. Due to the ring geometry, applications that require high output power remain limited. Here we show a 2 orders of magnitude light outcoupling improvement, up to a maximum of 120 mW at a laser heatsink temperature of 253 K, while still reaching a clean quantum walk comb state. These results are achieved by homogeneously integrating a racetrack QCL with a passive waveguide, which lies on top of the active region plane and therefore can be designed fully independently from the laser geometry. We also prove that we can change the light dispersion by tuning the parameters of the passive waveguide, with a direct impact on the behavior of the generated comb. Time-resolved spectral measurements in such devices demonstrate the ballistic expansion characteristic of quantum walk combs up to a bandwidth of 30 cm−1.Subject terms: Quantum cascade lasers, Photonic devices

Introduction

Frequency combs have become fundamental tools for many research fields1 from spectroscopy2, to telecommunications3,4 and high precision metrology5,6. Therefore, it is of great importance to obtain compact and bright frequency comb sources. To this purpose, quantum cascade lasers (QCLs)7 offer an interesting solution in the mid-infrared (mid-IR) and THz spectral range. Since the first demonstration of self-starting combs in Fabry–Perot QCLs8,9, the field expanded both from a fundamental understanding of the physics of the system10,11, which consists of a complex interplay of dispersion, Kerr nonlinearity, and an ultra-fast gain recovery time, and from a technological point of view, with the aim of achieving broader and more stable combs12–15. More recently, the study of QCL ring cavities has attracted increasing attention, since initial experiments showed self-starting frequency comb formation16,17, including soliton creation both in the Mid-IR18–20 and THz21 spectral region, allowing the study of a different physics inside the laser22. Subsequently, it was theoretically shown that residual distributed backscattering from the sidewall imperfection leads to soliton generation23, which is in good agreement with observations regarding very clean QCL rings that remain single mode for their entire operation range. It was shown that single-mode devices of this type can produce a comb via RF current modulation at the cavity roundtrip frequency, akin to a quantum walk in synthetic space24. This new laser state can be accurately described by a complex Ginzburg-Landau equation25 and is characteristic of systems such as QCLs due to their fast gain recovery time, which tends to suppress amplitude fluctuations, favoring frequency-modulated frequency comb formation allowed by the AM to FM conversion through the laser linewidth enhancement factor. The generated comb is fully controllable by changing the RF power and by detuning the modulation, making this an extremely attractive platform. Despite this, up to now, applications have remained limited due to the low output power of first-generation devices. Although this approach allows extremely low levels of backscattering, it leads to very small outcoupling efficiencies and less than ideal beam shapes imposed by the ring geometry16,18,24, limiting the total extracted power to hundreds of μW. It was recently shown that, by coupling a QCL racetrack (RC) with an active bus element that could be biased independently, it was possible to reach quantum walk comb (QWC) operation with an extraction power up to 100 mW26. This is an important step towards high power and highly controllable on-chip frequency combs but presents the limitation of power dissipation. To address this aspect, one attractive option would be to use optical integration techniques on silicon27–29, although challenges such as residual backscattering, power dissipation, or device alignment still remain. It is more appealing to look for a homogeneous integration where it is possible to integrate passive and active elements during the same fabrication process. Some works showed how this can be done by coupling a Fabry–Perot QCL to a plasmonic waveguide30, or through a but-coupled InGaAs waveguide31, or by evanescence coupling with an InGaAs waveguide grown below the QCL active material32,33. Using InGaAs as passive material remains a promising idea due to the relatively low losses in the mid-IR region34 compared to a plasmonic element that is better suited as a sensing area. In this work, as shown schematically in Fig. 1, we couple a QCL RC to a passive InGaAs waveguide, which is regrown on top of the active material via metal organic vapor phase epitaxy (MOVPE), in order to extract the light trapped inside the ring. By using a narrow top waveguide, we first prove that it is possible to improve the extraction efficiency of the laser by two orders of magnitude while still producing a QWC state, as reported in Fig. 2. Afterwards, by using a wide top waveguide, we study the more complex output power, which can reach a maximum of 120 mW, and the effect of the change in dispersion on the comb state35,36. Compared to the previously cited work of Letsou et al.26, we reduce the complexity of the electrical drive and the total power dissipation by requiring electrical injection only in the ring resonator, while passive waveguides are used to couple the light out of the active devices as well as to extract it from the outcoupling facets. The ability to redirect the light generated in the ring through a structure which is totally independent of the geometry of the active region (AR) opens up the possibility of monolithically integrating arbitrary passive elements on the same laser chip, pointing towards applications that can benefit from having one or more fully tunable QWCs on the same chip. Lastly, since the waveguide coupling mechanism is very general, well described by coupled mode theory37, and is governed by the relative size of the active and passive waveguide, we argue that our approach could be easily scaled at higher wavelengths by properly tuning the passive waveguide. Our development therefore has significant potential impact for spectroscopy applications in the full mid-IR spectral range, where frequency comb spectroscopy is already extensively used2,14,38, covering applications from environmental monitoring39 to biomedical applications40.

Results and discussion

The laser fabrication consists of a variation of the buried heterostructure process41, which extensively uses MOVPE regrowths to define the different layers of the device. The AR is first patterned by wet-etching, and subsequently an insulating layer of Fe-doped InP is regrown laterally. Afterwards, a layer of conductive Si-doped InP and Si-doped InGaAs is grown and then patterned into a shape that can be chosen independently from the active material geometry. Finally, a layer of Si-doped InP is grown to complete the burying of the structure. We refer to the Supplementary Fig. 1 for a more detailed description of the processing steps. A schematic of the final device, with a U-shaped passive waveguide as an example, is reproduced in the 3D model in Fig. 1a, where two cut-views from the front and the side of the device are also reported.

The final device mounted on a printed circuit board (PCB), which is needed to independently bias the laser through a DC and RF line, is shown in Fig. 1b, where a top view of a finished device is also displayed. Below the gold contact, it is possible to distinguish the RC shape, corresponding to the AR, and the U-shaped passive waveguide which allows extraction of the light at the facet. In Fig. 1c, d, we report the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the cross section of two test double waveguide lasers cleaved in their central region (plane A indicated by the dotted line) with two different passive waveguide widths. We show that, even if the laser has undergone multiple regrowth, it is possible to achieve a flat InGaAs structure that can be well aligned to the bottom AR, and that it is possible to tune both the width of the passive element, and the spacing of the two waveguides which can be freely chosen by the MOVPE regrowth. We first prove that it is possible to reach a clean QWC state in a double waveguide device with the top waveguide (typical width ~3 μm) narrower than the AR (typical width ~5 μm), which for brevity it will be referred to as narrow waveguide in the rest of the work. The narrow waveguide lasers reach an outcoupled power of tens of mW (Fig. 2a), showing an increase in extraction efficiency of roughly 2 orders of magnitude (see also the reference device in the Supplementary Fig. 2). We remark that, even if the lasers shown in Fig. 2 are processed on different active materials compared to the reference device, the significant change in output power is relatable to the geometry of the laser. To further corroborate our statement, the light-current-voltage (LIV) curves of a RC with and without an extractor processed on the same material are shown in the Supplementary Fig. 3 and the Supplementary Table 2. We show that the extractor improves not only the light outcoupling but also that there is no significant increase in the laser current threshold, indicating that the material regrowth is optimal, it does not impact the heat dissipation, and it does not introduce additional waveguide losses. We also underline that the light-power curves of the narrow waveguide laser tune smoothly until the roll-over region, which is to be expected given the shape of the lasing mode reported in Fig. 2b. In particular, the electric field shape in the passive waveguide is single lobed since the only supported mode in such a narrow waveguide at the typical emission wavelength of 4.5 μm is the fundamental TM00, as also showed by the beam profile measurement reported in the inset. Further details on the beam profile measurement can be found in “Methods”. In addition, we specify that all measurements are performed with a DC current source and at a heat sink temperature of 253 K to ensure a fair comparison with the reference RC laser, which is kept at a low temperature to ensure a better signal to noise ratio due to the low output power (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for further details).

An important condition to produce a quantum walk state is the CW laser state, which should present a monochromatic emission25. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the spectral behavior of the lasers as a function of the applied DC bias. In Fig. 2c, the CW spectral map is reported, showing two spectral regimes. At lower currents the emission is monochromatic, whereas at higher currents the device presents a multimode behavior, which is induced by residual back-reflection in the counterpropagating direction caused by the finite reflectivity at the extraction facet. To reduce this effect, an anti-reflection coating was applied to all devices reported in this work. Specifically, a λ/4 film of Al2O3 was evaporated to lower the facet reflectivity from 27% to approximately 0.8%, which mitigated the back-reflection contribution, but did not cancel it completely. We refer to the Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10 for a more direct comparison of the spectral behavior of the lasers before and after coating. Once a stable CW state is found, a current modulation at the repetition frequency of the laser is applied to generate the comb state (see “Methods” for the modulation conditions). The spectral tuning as a function of the modulation frequency has a characteristic shape, which is evaluated in Fig. 2d, and that perfectly matches what was originally shown in the work of Heckelmann et al.24 as well as the RF spectral map of our reference device (see Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). To further corroborate that the created state is indeed a QWC state, time-resolved spectral measurements were performed with the same modulation condition used to generate the spectra in Fig. 2d, both at resonance (Fig. 2e) and at a 2 MHz detuning (Fig. 2f). The measurements show the formation of the spectrum as a function of the time delay Δt after which the RF signal is turned on. The one at the resonant frequency presents a clear ballistic expansion that stabilizes after ~700 ns, which is in perfect agreement with the literature standard24. The same can be said for the out-of-resonance measurement, where Bloch oscillations are measured as already reported in ref. 42. Therefore, we can conclude that the state generated by the double waveguide laser is indeed a QWC state. More details on the time resolved acquisition can be found in the Supplementary Fig. 11.

From the theory of QWCs25 we know that the maximum comb bandwidth is proportional to M/D, where M is the modulation depth and D is the group velocity dispersion (GVD). Therefore, we designed lasers with a passive waveguide which is wider than the AR size (for brevity, it will be referred to as wide waveguide, typical width ~9 μm) in order to reduce the GVD and enhance the comb bandwidth. The wide waveguide lasers show an output power that can reach tens of mW, up to a maximum of 120 mW, but with a power dependence that presents significant power oscillations Fig. 3a, which clearly limit the stability of the platform. This effect can be understood by looking at the mode propagation along the passive element. Compared to the narrow waveguide case, the lasing mode shape (Fig. 3b) has two lobes that are pushed to the side of the passive waveguide. This is to be expected since the width of the passive waveguide is roughly twice the typical emission wavelength. The mode propagation is shown in Fig. 3c, where the intensity of the electric field is displayed in the xy plane passing through the middle height of the passive waveguide. The lasing mode is initialized in the 3D system on the left of the geometry, where the two waveguides are coupled, and it is propagated towards the right side, where the waveguides are not coupled anymore, and the light is extracted through the passive element. Due to the index matching condition, the preferred extraction mode is the TM20 of the passive waveguide. This not only explains the mode shape of the emitted light, as can be seen by the mode cross-section comparison in the inset of Fig. 3c, but also the power oscillations. In particular, the mode conversion efficiency depends on the refractive index of the AR and the InGaAs waveguide, which tune at different rates as a function of the current bias, since the top waveguide is fully passive and is at a lower temperature than the AR. This implies that while tuning the current, the power outcoupled via the passive element can also leak to other supported waveguide modes (TM00, TM10), which is not the case for the narrow waveguide lasers, since the passive waveguide can only support the fundamental TM00 mode. Further details on the 3D simulation for both laser designs are provided in the Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7.

As for the narrow waveguide case, the CW spectral map is acquired (Fig. 3d) and it presents a lower single mode dynamical range, which is again connected to the amount of light back-reflected from the laser facet. We underline that this is a different effect compared to the one causing the power oscillations in the light-current curves, which is mostly connected to the mode conversion mechanism. This is supported by the fact that power oscillation problem can be solved by choosing a narrow waveguide, while the multimode spectral behavior is present in both typologies of devices, and it can be reduced by applying an anti-reflection coating to the laser facet. Even if the CW behavior is less regular, it is still possible to obtain an operating range where the emission is monochromatic, and therefore, we are able to test the response of the device under modulation as reported in Fig. 3e. The expansion resembles a standard one until the resonant frequency is reached, where a collapse of the spectrum is present. In particular, the narrow waveguide case is designed to keep the dispersion similar to the of the reference RC, while the wide waveguide is designed to reduce the GVD term while trading off for a higher third order dispersion term (see also Supplementary Fig. 8c). In fact, it is the combination of the last two factors that causes the spectral jump at resonance24,25,43. In particular, we also argue that the high third order term is the reason why, even if the GVD is supposed to be lower than the other devices, the bandwidth does not improve.

Discussion

By homogeneously integrating a passive InGaAs waveguide with a QCL RC geometry, we have shown that it is possible to improve the light extraction efficiency by two orders of magnitude compared to the single-ring case. In addition, a QWC expansion was demonstrated for both double waveguide geometries under consideration. By tuning the width of the passive element, it is also possible to change the light dispersion, highly impacting the comb shape, which is influenced by higher-order dispersion terms. In general, narrow waveguide devices are better suited for achieving a reliable and tunable comb condition. The narrower cavity impacts the dispersion less and presents a better mode matching between the coupled cavity region and the passive element one, as proven by the far field measurement and by the regularity of the Power-Current curve. The back-reflections induced by the facet reflectivity remain problematic for the stability of the CW state of the laser, which can be mitigated by an appropriate anti-reflection coating. However, for achieving fully homogeneous active-passive integration, the back-reflection issue is less relevant since there would not be facets with high reflectivity. In particular, this work can be a first step towards a monolithic active-passive integration with possible applications in spectroscopy, taking advantage of the QWC tunability. Furthermore, the ability to design completely independent passive structures can be exploited to integrate photonic components that are nowadays common in the telecommunication spectral range29,44, particularly benefiting applications in on-chip dispersion compensation for generating short pulses45. Finally, we remark that the laser operation at 253 K has been chosen to ensure a better comparison with the reference RC, but we also prove the double waveguide laser operation at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 5), which makes our approach competitive with the approach of using an active outcoupler26. In particular, it is not possible to evaluate the performance of the two approaches by directly comparing the absolute laser power because the active material used in the two cases is drastically different since the QCLs operate at different wavelengths. It is therefore more correct to compare the outcoupling efficiency with respect to an uncoupled ring, which, to the best of our knowledge, is comparable. It is also worth noting that, while the approach of Letsou et al. provides additional control on the laser state through electrical driving on the extractor waveguide, our approach is more efficient in terms of power consumption, especially in the perspective of a fully integrated lab-on-chip.

Methods

LIV and beam profile measurements

The light from the QCL is collimated using a high numerical aperture lens (Thorlabs C037TME-E) and directed onto a powermeter (Ophir Starlite). The DC is supplied via QCL2000 (Wavelength Electronics). For the beam profile measurement a WinCamD-IR-BB by DataRay is used to image the collimated beam. The beam profiler is placed at 8 cm from the lens.

SEM pictures

The SEM pictures have been acquired using a SEM ZEISS ULTRA 55. We specify that both SEM pictures do not directly belongs to the lasers measured in this work, since to obtain them one has to cleave the chip in half (along line A), thus destroying the laser. Hence, they are acquired from sacrificial lasers fabricated in the same batch and they are reported to give an example of narrow and wide waveguide lasers and an example of the quality of the process with multiple successive MOVPE regrowths. The passive waveguide widths, also used to perform the 3D simulation, are estimated by looking at the facet of the real device, since it is possible to measure it without breaking the laser in half.

Spectral map

The spectral maps of the reference RC are measured with a commercial Fourier Transform Interferometer (FTIR) Bruker-VertexV80 using an external nitrogen-cooled HgCdTe detector. The double waveguide devices, instead, are measured with a home-made FTIR. More details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

RF modulation

The PCB allows an independent biasing of the laser via two ports. One is used for biasing the laser with a DC current (QCL2000 Wavelength electronics), while the other one is optimized for RF modulation. The RF signal is generated with a Rohde&Schwarz (SMF100A), sent to an RF amplifier (Mini-Circuits ZVE-3W-183+). Because the RF port is shorted with the DC port through the laser, we use a DC-block (MCL BLK-89-S+) to protect the Amplifier. The wide waveguide LIV characteristic of the laser used for measuring Fig. 3e is reported in the Supplementary Fig. 3. The laser spectra reported in Fig. 2c, d are acquired using narrow waveguide-2. The modulation conditions are: Reference RC: I = 540 mA, frep = 15.691 GHz, P = 27 dBm. Wide waveguide: I = 666 mA, frep = 11.091 GHz, P = 23 dBm. Narrow waveguide laser: I = 1.015 mA, frep = 10.15 GHz, P = 23 dBm.
