The Hard Lefschetz Theorem on Kähler Lie Algebroids
Shane Rankin

Abstract
. Compact Kähler manifolds classically satisfy the Hard Lefschetz Theorem, which gives strong control on the underlying topology of the manifold. One expects a similar theorem to be true for Kähler Lie Algebroids, and we show for a certain class of them that this is indeed true, with an added ellipticity requirement. We provide examples of Lie Algebroids satisfying this, as well as an example of a Kähler Lie Algebroid that does not meet this ellipticity requirement and fails to satisfy the Hard Lefschetz condition.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

There are a number of celebrated and named theorems in classical Kähler Geometry demonstrating the excellent behavior of such objects, i.e. The Kodaira and Nakano Vanishing Theorems, The Hard Lefschetz Theorem, The Hodge Index Theorem, etc; though in this paper the Hard Lefschetz Theorem will be our particular focus. Classically on an oriented compact manifold of dimension
n
we have that

\dim(H_{dR}^{k}(M))\cong\dim(H_{dR}^{n-k}(M))\qquad\forall k\in\{0,\dots,n\}

by Poincaré Duality, where
H_{dR}^{k}(M)
are the deRham cohomology groups of
M
. The Hard Lefschetz Theorem can be viewed as saying that on a compact Kähler manifold, wedging with an appropriate power of the Kähler class is an isomorphism witnessing this phenomenon. Recently, there has been attention towards Kähler Lie Algebroids, see
[
4
]
, where it was shown that both the Kodaira Vanishing Theorem and the Hodge-Riemann Bilinear relations still hold true.

1.2.
Results

The general outline of the proof and paper is as follows: The notions of Kähler Lie algebroids and some of the necessary Kähler identities have been previously established in
[
4
]
; separately, Hodge theory for Lie algebroids has been established in
[
1
]
. In section 2, we recall these results, and show that the two papers results are compatible, though they use different definitions. In section 3, we establish Lie algebroid analogues of some necessary relations from classical Kähler geometry, and use them to prove the main results. In section 4, we discuss impacts of this on the Betti numbers of the algebroid, provide a method of constructing examples, and provide an example of a Kähler Lie algebroid with non-elliptic anchor that fails to satisfy the Hard Lefschetz Theorem. Before we proceed to section two, we present the main results here. Collecting all the necessary conditions to make the aforementioned papers communicate with each other, we say that a Kähler Lie Algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
over a manifold
M
is
Hodge-admissible
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

M
is compact, orientable, and closed.

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is elliptic.

(3)

\mathcal{A}
carries an integrating section.

With such a Lie algebroid, we regain a Dolbeault-type decomposition of the Lie algebroid cohomology as well as the Hard Lefschetz Theorem.

{thm}
[]
Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
be a Hodge-admissible Kähler Lie algebroid. Then the harmonic forms are bigraded, that is

\mathcal{H}^{k}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\mathcal{H}^{p,q},

where
\mathcal{H}^{p,q}=\ker(\Delta)\cap\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{p,q}
is finite-dimensional. We also have that

H_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})\cong\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\mathcal{H}^{p,q},

where
H_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})
is the Lie Algebroid cohomology.

{thm}

[]
If
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
is a Hodge-admissible Kähler Lie Algebroid of rank
2m
, then
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the
\mathcal{A}
-Hard Lefschetz Theorem. That is, the map

\displaystyle[L]^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{m-k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H_{\mathcal{A}}^{m+k}(M)

\displaystyle[\alpha]

\displaystyle\mapsto[\omega^{k}\wedge\alpha]

is an isomorphism for all
k\geq 0
, where
\omega
is the
\mathcal{A}
-Kähler form associated to
h
.

{thm}

[]
If
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
is a Hodge-admissible Kähler Lie algebroid, then
\mathcal{A}
is formal.

A classical corollary of the Hard Lefschetz Theorem is that the odd Betti numbers of a compact Kähler manifold are even. If we assume that the relevant pairing
I
provided by Poincaré Duality carries over to the Algebroid setting, then we get an analogous result:

{thm}
[]
If
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
is a Hodge-admissible Kähler Lie algebroid such that
I
is non-degenerate, then the odd degree cohomology groups are even dimensional.

This is discussed in Section 4.1, and provides an obstruction for a Kähler Lie Algebroid to satisfy the Hard Lefschetz Theorem.

2.
Elliptic Lie Algebroids

2.1.
Complex Lie Algebroids

Throughout the paper, let
(M,\tilde{g})
denote a compact, closed, orientable, Riemannian manifold of real dimension
n
. All vector bundles are assumed to be complex unless otherwise indicated. The following definition is found in
[
4
]
, where the author makes reference to
[
5
]
as well as
[
9
]
.

Definition 1
.

[
4
, Definition 1]

A
Complex Lie Algebroid on
M
is a tuple
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],J)
where:

•

\mathcal{A}
is a complex vector bundle on
M
.

•

\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow TM_{\mathbb{C}}
is a vector bundle morphism called the
anchor of
\mathcal{A}
.

•

[\cdot,\cdot]
is a bracket on
\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
giving the space of sections the structure of a Lie algebra.

•

J\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
is the complex structure of
\mathcal{A}
, regarded as a bundle morphism over the identity such that
J^{2}=-\text{id}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

such that:

(1)

\rho
induces a Lie algebra homomorphism on sections, i.e. for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
, we have

\rho([X,Y])=[\rho(X),\rho(Y)]_{TM_{\mathbb{C}}}.

(2)

For all
X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
, we have

[X,fY]=f[X,Y]+(\rho(X)f)Y.

(3)

For all
X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})

\mathcal{N}_{J}(X,Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[JX,JY]-[X,Y]-J([JX,Y]+[X,JY])\equiv 0

That is,
J
is necessarily integrable.

Splitting
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
into its eigenbundle decomposition with respect to the complexified
J
allows us to write out

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{A}^{1,0}\oplus\mathcal{A}^{0,1},

where

\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{1,0})=\{X-iJ_{\mathcal{A}}X|X\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})\},\qquad\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{0,1})=\{X+iJ_{\mathcal{A}}X|X\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})\}.

This splitting extends to the dual bundle
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}=(\mathcal{A}^{1,0})^{*}\oplus(\mathcal{A}^{0,1})^{*}
. We then set

\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})=\Gamma\left(\bigwedge^{p}(\mathcal{A}^{1,0})^{*}\otimes\bigwedge^{q}(\mathcal{A}^{0,1})^{*}\right)

which splits the standard complex into

\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{k}\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{p,q}\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})

Where
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})=\Gamma(\bigwedge^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}})
. Much like in the classical setting of the tangent bundle,
\mathcal{A}
carries a natural differential defined as follows. For
\eta\in\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})
and
X_{i}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})
we define
d_{\mathcal{A}}
as

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{A}}\eta(X_{1},\dots,X_{k+1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+1}\rho(X_{i})\eta(X_{1},\dots,\widehat{X}_{i},\dots,X_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\eta([X_{i},X_{j}],X_{1},\dots,\widehat{X_{i}},\dots,\widehat{X_{j}},\dots,X_{k+1}).

The
deRham-Chevalley-Eilenberg Cohomology of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as the cohomology of the complex

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}(M;\mathbb{C})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}(M;\mathbb{C})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}

that is

H_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\ker(d_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}(M;\mathbb{C}))}{\im(d_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}(M;\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C}))}.

As in the case of classical complex geometry, the differential also splits into four separate components, though we’ll only consider analogues of the standard two for the following reason.

Theorem 2

(
[
5
]
,Theorem 2.1)
.

For a Complex Lie Algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],J)
, we have that

d_{\mathcal{A}}\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\subset\Omega^{p+1,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\oplus\Omega^{p,q+1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
.

This theorem ensures that our differential decomposes

d_{\mathcal{A}}=\partial_{\mathcal{A}}+\overline{\partial_{\mathcal{A}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{p+1,q}(M)\oplus\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{p,q+1}(M),

and comparing types we have that

\partial_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}=0,\qquad\partial_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial_{\mathcal{A}}}=-\overline{\partial_{\mathcal{A}}}\partial_{\mathcal{A}},\qquad(\overline{\partial_{\mathcal{A}}})^{2}=0.

One can define the analogue of the Dolbeault complex in this setting, though we will not need it for our purposes.

2.2.
Ellipticity

On a compact and
closed Hermitian manifold, the description of the deRham and Dolbeault cohomologies in terms of harmonic forms is possible because of the ellipticity of the ensuing differential complexes. The following definition of ellipticity for Lie algebroids can be found in
[
1
, Definition 1.15]
.

Definition 3
.

A Complex Lie Algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot])
is
Elliptic
if

\rho(\mathcal{A})+\overline{\rho(\mathcal{A})}=TM_{\mathbb{C}}.

Example 4
.

Let
X
be a real manifold with an almost complex structure
J
, and consider

\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{X-iJX|X\in TX\}\subset TX_{\mathbb{C}}.

The inclusion provides a natural candidate for an anchor map, and the complexified Lie bracket restricts to a bracket on
\mathcal{A}
if
J
is integrable. In this case, we have that

\rho(\mathcal{A})\oplus\overline{\rho(\mathcal{A})}=T^{1,0}X\oplus T^{0,1}X=TX_{\mathbb{C}}.

Note that in this case the differential corresponding to
T^{1,0}X
is
d_{\mathcal{A}}=\partial
which is indeed an elliptic operator.

Example 5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
regarded as an abelian Lie Algebroid over a point
M=*
. Define
J\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
by
Jv=iv
, the standard (integrable) complex structure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This is trivially elliptic as
\rho(\mathfrak{g})\oplus\overline{\rho(\mathfrak{g})}=TM_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a Lie algebroid over a smooth manifold
M
. Let
h
be a Hermitian Metric on
\mathcal{A}
and
\tilde{g}
be a Riemannian metric on
M
. Let
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}
denote the adjoint of
d_{\mathcal{A}}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product,

\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{L^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{M}h(\alpha,\beta)d\text{vol}_{\tilde{g}},

where we extend
h
to a hermitian inner product on
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})
by defining it on decomposable elements
\alpha_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_{k},\beta_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_{k}
as

h(\alpha_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_{k},\beta_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_{k})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\det(h(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j})).

Definition 6
.

Let
d_{\mathcal{A}},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}
be defined as above. Then the
Laplacian
is defined as

\Delta=d_{\mathcal{A}}d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}+d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}d_{\mathcal{A}}

which is a degree 0, second order differential operator on
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})
for all
k\geq 0
.

Definition 7
.

We say that
\alpha\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})
is
harmonic
if
\alpha\in\ker(\Delta)
or equivalently if
\alpha\in\ker(d_{\mathcal{A}})\cap\ker(d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger})
. The space of harmonic elements in
\Omega^{k}(M)
will be denoted
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.

Theorem 8

(van der Leer Durán)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is elliptic, then for all
k\geq 0
we have that

\Gamma(\bigwedge^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*})=\mathcal{H}^{k}\oplus\im(d_{\mathcal{A}})\oplus\im(d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}).

Moreover we have that

\ker(d_{\mathcal{A}})\cap\ker(d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger})=\mathcal{H}^{k}\cong H^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C}),

which are all finite dimensional.

Proof.

See
[
1
, Theorem 1.45]
.
∎

3.
Kähler Lie Algebroids

3.1.
Integration Compatibility

The following definition of a Kähler Lie algebroid can be found in
[
4
, Definition 2]
:

Definition 9
.

A
Kähler Lie algebroid

(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
is a complex Lie algebroid equipped with a hermitian metric
h
on
\mathcal{A}
whose associated
(1,1)
-
\mathcal{A}
-form
\omega
is
d_{\mathcal{A}}
-closed. That is,

h=g-i\omega.

We refer to
\omega
as an
\mathcal{A}
-Kähler form
.

Example 10
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold, and identify
TX
with
T^{1,0}X
via the complex linear isomorphism
X\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(X-iJX)
, where we regard
TX
as a complex vector bundle, with complex structure provided by
J
. Earlier, we discussed that this is an elliptic Lie algebroid. The hermitian metric from the Kähler structure also serves as a hermitian metric here, with the (1,1)-
\mathcal{A}
-form just the typical Kähler form. Note then that
d_{\mathcal{A}}\omega=\partial\omega
, which does vanish as
d\omega=0
implies that
\partial\omega=0
as well.

Example 11
.

We previously discussed the abelian Lie algebra
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, regarded as a Lie algebroid over a point, and that this was an elliptic Lie Algebroid.
In this case, the standard hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
gives rise to a Kähler Lie algebroid structure. In this case, the imaginary part of the hermitian metric resembles the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. It is indeed closed as
d\equiv 0
in this example, though it is not exact for the same reason.

The classical Kähler identities still hold in this setting, as well as a number of other familiar properties as shown in
[
4
]
. In order to see these in the context of the ellipticity already laid out, we spend a bit of time showing that the notions of integration defined in
[
4
]
agree with what we have already done. The central issue that occurs is the following: it would be convenient to define adjoints to the algebroid exterior derivative against something like the following map

\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\int_{M}\alpha\wedge\overline{\star\beta}=\int_{M}h(\alpha,\beta)\frac{\omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{m}}{m!},

where
\frac{\omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{m}}{m!}\in\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}
. Unfortunately,
\frac{\omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{m}}{m!}
is not a volume form on
M
so this fails. Moreover, we have already defined integration of algebroid forms using the Riemannian metric on
M
in order to discuss the Hodge decomposition. This can be remedied though, and to do so we introduce the following definition, which is used in defining integration in
[
4
]
:

Definition 12
.

An
integrating section
for a Complex Lie Algebroid
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
is a nonvanishing global section
\eta\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}\otimes\bigwedge^{top}T^{*}M_{\mathbb{C}})
.

To explain the name, let
\langle,\rangle
denote the map

\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{p}\otimes\left(\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}_{p}\otimes_{C^{\infty}(M)}\bigwedge^{top}T_{p}^{*}M_{\mathbb{C}}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bigwedge^{top}T_{p}^{*}M_{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\xi\otimes(X\otimes\mu)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\xi(X))\mu,

where
\xi(X)\in C^{\infty}(M)
denotes the pairing between
\mathcal{A}
and its dual. Using this we can define an (a priori different) integral.
If
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
is a Lie Algebroid over a compact oriented manifold
M
, and
\xi\in\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}
. Then we define the integral of
\xi
to be

\int_{M}\langle\xi,\eta\rangle,

where
\eta
is an integrating section of
\mathcal{A}
. Note that in particular, if we are working with a fixed element of
\xi\in\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}
then we can modify
\eta
to give us something particular:

Definition 13
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot])
be a Lie Algebroid over an oriented Riemannian Manifold
(M,\tilde{g})
with an integrating section
\eta
, and fix
\xi\in\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}
non-vanishing. Then
\eta
is called
\xi
-normal
if
\langle\xi,\eta\rangle=d\text{vol}_{\tilde{g}}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
be a Lie Algebroid with an integration section
\eta
, fix
\xi\in\bigwedge^{top}\mathcal{A}^{*}
non-vanishing, and let
\tilde{g}
denote the Riemannian metric on
M
. Then we can always choose an integrating section to be
\xi
-normal.

Proof.

Let
d\mathrm{vol}_{\tilde{g}}
be the standard volume form, and note that as
\xi,\eta
are both non-vanishing, we have that

\langle\xi,\eta\rangle=fd\mathrm{vol}_{\tilde{g}}

for some non-vanishing
f
. Then choosing
\chi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{f}\eta
, we have that
\chi
is an
\xi
-normal integrating section.
∎

In light of this, from now on we assume all integrating sections
\eta
are
\omega^{m}/m!
-normal where
\omega
is the Kähler form associated to the chosen hermitian metric
h
on
\mathcal{A}
, and
m
is half of the real rank of
\mathcal{A}
. With this assumption in place, the two notions of integration defined agree, that is for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M;\mathbb{C})
we have:

\int_{M}\langle h(\alpha,\beta)\frac{\omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{m}}{m!},\eta\rangle=\int_{M}h(\alpha,\beta)d\mathrm{vol}_{\tilde{g}}.

3.2.
Kähler Identites

On a Kähler Lie algebroid, if we denote by
\omega
the
\mathcal{A}
-Kähler form we can define a map

\displaystyle L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Omega^{p+1,q+1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega\wedge\alpha.

Classically this operator is part of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
representation, and the same is true in the Algebroid setting. Let
\Lambda
be the formal adjoint to
L
as defined in
[
4
]
. If we define
H
as

H|_{\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})}=(p+q-m)\text{id}_{\Omega^{p,q}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})}

where
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})=2m
, and extend it to all forms linearly, we have the following:

Proposition 15
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
be a Kähler Lie algebroid,
L,\Lambda
, and
H
be as above. Then the assignment

\rho(x)=L,\quad\rho(y)=\Lambda,\quad\rho(z)=H,

is an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
representation on the space of
\mathcal{A}
-forms, where
\{x,y,z\}
is the basis of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
[x,y]=z,[x,z]=-2x,[y,z]=2y
.

Proof.

The fact that
[L,\Lambda]=H
is the content of
[
4
, Lemma 5]
. The other two relations follow readily from the definition of
H
.
∎

Theorem 16
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[,],h)
is a Kähler Lie Algebroid, the the following equalities hold

\displaystyle[\partial_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger},L]=-i\overline{\partial}_{A},\qquad[\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger},L]=i\partial_{\mathcal{A}},

where adjoints are taken with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product previously discussed.

Proof.

See
[
4
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

On a Complex Lie algebroid, one can define more sensitive notions of the Laplacian than discussed in the previous section using the
\partial_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\overline{\partial_{\mathcal{A}}}
operators:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\partial}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\partial_{\mathcal{A}}\partial_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}+\partial_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}+\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}.

On a Kähler Lie algebroid not only do these two maps agree, but they agree up to a nonzero constant with the standard Laplacian.

Proposition 17
.

On a Kähler Lie Algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
we have that
\Delta=\Delta_{\partial}+\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}
. Moreover, we have that
\Delta_{\partial}=\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta
.

Proof.

This follows identically to the classical proofs as found in
[
3
, page 115-116]
.
∎

There are a few other relations that will be convenient to have on hand for upcoming proofs.

Lemma 18
.

On a Kähler Lie algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h)
, the following relations hold:

(1)

[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}]=0
,

(2)

[L,\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}]=0
,

(3)

[L,\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}]=0
,

(4)

[L,\Delta]=0
,

where the commutators are regarded in the graded sense.

Proof.

The first relation follows from the fact that
\omega
is closed, and implies the second relation. To see the third relation, note that we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}L\alpha

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{A}}L\alpha+\overline{\partial}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}L\alpha

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}(L\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}+i\partial_{\mathcal{A}})\alpha+\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}L\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha

\displaystyle=L\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\alpha+i\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\partial_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha+(L\overline{\partial}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{A}}+i\partial_{\mathcal{A}})\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha

\displaystyle=L\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\alpha+i\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\partial_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha+L\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha+i\partial_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\partial}_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha

\displaystyle=L\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}\alpha.

The final relation then follows from Proposition
17
, as we have

[L,\Delta]=2[L,\Delta_{\overline{\partial}}]=0.

∎

At this point, there are a a few necessary conditions for all the previous results to both work and agree with each other, so for convenience sake we’ll introduce a new definition to combine them all:

Definition 19
.

We say a Kähler Lie Algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot],h_{\mathcal{A}})
over a manifold
M
is
Hodge-admissible
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

M
is compact, orientable, and closed.

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is elliptic.

(3)

\mathcal{A}
carries an integrating section.

Remark 20
.

In light of Lemma

4.1.
Betti Parity

A classic consequence of the Hard Lefschetz Theorem is that the odd Betti numbers of a compact Kähler manifold are even. A similar fact is true for some Hodge-admissible Kähler Lie algebroids. We begin by defining a bilinear paring
I
on
H^{m-k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M;\mathbb{C})
as

\displaystyle I\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H_{\mathcal{A}}^{m-k}(M;\mathbb{C})\otimes H_{\mathcal{A}}^{m-k}(M;\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle([\alpha],[\beta])

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{M}\langle L^{k}(\alpha)\wedge\beta,\eta\rangle.

Classically, Poincaré duality ensures that this bilinear form is nondegenerate, however the Lie Algebroid analogue of the perfect pairing in Poincaré duality fails at times, see
[
2
, Examples 5.7 and 5.8]
. In the case that
I
is nondegenerate, we have the following theorem:
See
1.2

Proof.

By construction of
I
, we have that

\displaystyle I([\alpha],[\beta])

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\langle L^{k}(\alpha)\wedge\beta,\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\langle\omega^{k}\wedge\alpha\wedge\beta,\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(k-m)^{2}}\int_{M}\langle\omega^{k}\wedge\beta\wedge\alpha,\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(k-m)^{2}}I([\beta],[\alpha]).

When
k-m
is odd,
I
is then a nondegnerate, alternating form, forcing the dimension of

A natural question is what are sufficient and necessary conditions on a Lie Algebroid for

4.3.
Failure of the Kähler
b
-Sphere

b
-geometry was developed largely by Melrose in 1993
[
7
]
to study differential operators on manifolds with boundary.

Definition 24
.

A
b
-manifold
is a pair
(M,Z)
with
M
a compact smooth manifold, and
Z\subset M
a codimension
1
submanifold. By the Serre-Swan Theorem, there is a vector bundle
{}^{b}TM
, referred to as the
b
-tangent bundle
such that

\Gamma({}^{b}TM)=\{X\in\Gamma(TM)|X\text{ is tangent to }Z\}\subset\Gamma(TM).

b
-geometry naturally sits inside the Lie Algebroid philosophy, as the inclusion map
{}^{b}TM\hookrightarrow TM
serves as an anchor for a Lie Algebroid structure, and sections possesses the same Lie Bracket as
TM
. The notion of
b
-cohomology is also defined and studied:

Definition 25
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a
b
-manifold, and define

{}^{b}\Omega^{k}(M,\mathbb{R})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Gamma\left(\bigwedge^{k}({}^{b}TM)^{*}\right).

This forms a cochain complex with a natural differential
{}^{b}d
. We define the
b
-cohomology
of
(M,Z)
as

{}^{b}H^{k}(M;\mathbb{R})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\ker({}^{b}d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{}^{b}\Omega^{k}(M;\mathbb{R})\rightarrow{}^{b}\Omega^{k+1}(M;\mathbb{R}))}{\im({}^{b}d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{}^{b}\Omega^{k-1}(M;\mathbb{R})\rightarrow{}^{b}\Omega^{k}(M;\mathbb{R}))},

which is the Lie algebroid cohomology of
{}^{b}TM
with the aforementioned Lie algebroid Structure. We also have the following theorem originally in
[
7
, Section 2.16]

Theorem 26

(Mazzeo-Melrose)
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a
b
-manifold. Then we have

{}^{b}H^{k}(M)\cong H^{k}(M)\oplus H^{k-1}(Z).

For example, consider the
b
-sphere, where
M=S^{2}
and
Z=S^{1}
is an equatorial band, which we will denote
{}^{b}S^{2}

b

H^{k}({}^{b}S^{2};\mathbb{R})\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}\quad&\text{if }k=0,1\\
\mathbb{R}^{2}&\text{if }k=2\end{cases}

demonstrating that Poincaré duality can also fail in the

Proposition 27
.

On the Kähler
b
-sphere, the Hard Lefschetz Theorem fails.

Proof.

The only map we would need to check is the map
[L]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{}^{b}H^{0}({}^{b}S^{2};\mathbb{C})\rightarrow{}^{b}H^{2}({}^{b}S^{2};\mathbb{C})

∎

Remark 28
.

In this example, the ellipticity condition needed for a Hodge Decomposition fails as the inclusion map into the complexified tangent bundle of
S^{2}
is not elliptic.

Remark 29
.
