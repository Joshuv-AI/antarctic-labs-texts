Effects of vitamin D deficiency and supplementation on myocardial and hepatic iron status and function in children with beta-thalassemia
Authors: Hüseyin Avni Solğun, Mustafa Özay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and to assess the effects of vitamin D supplementation on myocardial and hepatic iron burden and organ function in children with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia (TDT).MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the pediatric hematology department of our institution. Patients aged three years and older with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia were included. Baseline and post-treatment laboratory assessments included serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels. All patients received vitamin D supplementation according to their deficiency status. Cardiac and hepatic iron deposition were evaluated using T2* magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed by echocardiography.ResultsA total of 88 pediatric patients were enrolled. The largest proportion of patients was aged 4–9 years (n = 53), followed by those aged 10–14 years (n = 22) and 15–19 years (n = 13). The cohort consisted of 54.5% males (n = 48) and 45.5% females (n = 40). Patients were categorized into three groups based on serum 25(OH)D levels: severe deficiency (< 10 ng/mL), mild deficiency (10–20 ng/mL), and sufficiency (≥ 20 ng/mL). Three months after vitamin D supplementation, statistically significant improvements were observed in both cardiac and hepatic MRI T2* values (p < 0.05).ConclusionVitamin D supplementation was associated with significant improvement in myocardial and hepatic iron parameters, suggesting a potential beneficial role in reducing iron-induced organ dysfunction. These findings highlight the importance of routine vitamin D monitoring and management in children with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia.

Introduction

Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent among patients with beta-thalassemia major. Several contributing factors have been identified, including reduced outdoor activity, limited sunlight exposure, dietary insufficiency, and genetic or ethnocultural influences, such as clothing practices that limit sun exposure. In this patient population, hepatic iron overload represents a particularly important etiological factor, as excessive iron deposition in the liver may impair the synthesis of 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the principal circulating form of vitamin D [1].

In addition to hepatic involvement, iron accumulation in the parathyroid glands may lead to hypoparathyroidism, further aggravating vitamin D deficiency [1, 2].

Iron-induced secondary hypoparathyroidism has been associated with subclinical systolic and diastolic myocardial dysfunction, particularly during adolescence.² Although vitamin D deficiency is commonly observed in individuals with congestive heart failure of various etiologies, the relationship between vitamin D status and cardiovascular involvement in pediatric patients with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia remains insufficiently understood due to the limited number of high-quality studies available [3, 4].

At the cellular level, iron enters cardiomyocytes predominantly through L-type voltage-dependent calcium channels (LVDCCs), a pathway influenced by both parathyroid hormone (PTH) and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)₂D] [5, 6]. Vitamin D and PTH stimulate calcium influx through these channels, suggesting a mechanistic link between vitamin D status and myocardial iron loading in patients with thalassemia [7].

Beyond its classical role in calcium absorption and bone metabolism, vitamin D functions as a pleiotropic hormone, with receptors expressed in nearly all human tissues [8]. It plays a critical role in maintaining intracellular calcium homeostasis, and deficiency has been associated with skeletal muscle weakness, chronic heart failure, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired insulin secretion [9].

In addition to its skeletal effects, vitamin D influences gene transcription, mitochondrial metabolism, and oxidative stress within cardiac tissue.⁸ Deficiency has been linked to heart failure, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired insulin sensitivity.² Given these systemic effects and the complex interplay between vitamin D, PTH, and iron metabolism, children with transfusion-dependent thalassemia represent a particularly vulnerable population.⁹.

Given the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in the general population and the additional physiological burden imposed by chronic disease, iron overload, and frequent medical interventions, patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia are especially susceptible to vitamin D insufficiency [10]. Emerging evidence linking L-type calcium channel activity to myocardial iron uptake highlights the need to explore whether vitamin D deficiency contributes to myocardial and hepatic iron overload and whether correction of this deficiency may reverse or ameliorate iron-related organ dysfunction [11, 12].

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the association between vitamin D deficiency and iron-related cardiac and hepatic dysfunction in pediatric patients with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of vitamin D supplementation in this context [13, 14].

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the Pediatric Hematology Department of our institution. Patients aged ≥ 4 years with a confirmed diagnosis of transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia were eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria included the presence of comorbid chronic conditions such as type 1 diabetes mellitus, asthma, obesity, epilepsy, liver disease, kidney disease, malabsorption syndromes, hypercortisolism, malignancy, or any contraindication to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Baseline and post-treatment laboratory assessments included serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), parathyroid hormone (PTH), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels. Cardiac and hepatic iron deposition were assessed using T2* MRI, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was evaluated by echocardiography. All laboratory and imaging evaluations were repeated three months after vitamin D supplementation.

Patient selection and registration

A total of 88 pediatric patients aged 4–17 years diagnosed with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia and followed at the Pediatric Hematology and Oncology Clinic of our institution between 2024 and 2025 were included in the study. Demographic characteristics, number of follow-up visits, clinical findings, and bleeding symptoms (including number and anatomical distribution) were recorded.

Patients with liver disease, kidney disease, type 1 diabetes mellitus, malabsorption, hypercortisolism, malignancy, or contraindications to MRI were excluded. Serum calcium, phosphate, PTH, and 25(OH)D levels were measured before and after vitamin D treatment. Cardiac T2*, liver iron concentration (LIC), and LVEF were determined.

Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Chelation therapy and vitamin D supplementation

All patients were receiving iron chelation therapy with either deferasirox (20–40 mg/kg/day) or deferoxamine (30–50 mg/kg/day, 5 days per week), based on iron burden and clinical judgment. Chelation regimens remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Supplementation was administered for 12 weeks. Dietary counseling was provided to maintain a daily calcium intake of approximately 1 g/day.

Laboratory and imaging evaluation

Baseline and 3-month post-treatment measurements included serum calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, ferritin, and 25(OH)D levels, measured using chemiluminescent immunoassay methods (total vitamin D).

Cardiac and hepatic iron deposition were quantified using T2* MRI (1.5 Tesla, Siemens Avanto). Cardiac T2* values < 12 ms and liver T2* values < 3.6 ms were considered indicative of significant iron overload, as defined by Wood et al. (2015). LVEF was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography. MRI examinations were not performed in children younger than 6 years due to breath-holding limitations.

Data collection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed using the keywords pediatric, transfusion-dependent thalassemia, vitamin D deficiency, cardiac function, and iron overload. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. The search yielded articles meeting the inclusion criteria and relevant to the objectives of the present study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-supplementation values.

Regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of myocardial and hepatic iron burden. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical committee approval

The study was approved by the Health Sciences University Başakşehir Çam and Sakura Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2021.09.193).

Results

A total of 88 pediatric patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia were evaluated (48 males and 40 females), with a mean age of 8.38 ± 4.2 years. Vitamin D deficiency (< 20 ng/mL) was detected in 76 of 88 patients (86%), including 13 patients (17%) with severe deficiency (< 10 ng/mL).

Baseline pre-treatment mean cardiac T2* was 30.21 ms, liver iron concentration was 3.42 mg/g dry weight, serum calcium level was 9.57 mg/dL, and mean PTH level was 36.7 pg/mL. No statistically significant differences were observed at baseline in cardiac or hepatic T2*, calcium, PTH, or ALP levels among vitamin D status groups (all p > 0.05).

Risk stratification identified 26 patients (30%) as high-risk, defined by cardiac T2* <12 ms and/or liver T2* <3.6 ms. Among these patients, 38% had severe vitamin D deficiency and 30% had mild deficiency, suggesting a trend toward increased iron burden in patients with lower vitamin D levels.

Regression analysis demonstrated that serum ferritin was the strongest independent predictor of cardiac T2* (β = −0.42, p = 0.002) and liver T2* (β = −0.36, p = 0.011). A weak negative correlation between serum 25(OH)D and PTH levels was observed (r = − 0.25).

Patients were classified into three groups based on 25(OH)D levels: severe deficiency (< 10 ng/mL), mild deficiency (10–20 ng/mL), and sufficient levels (≥ 20 ng/mL). Although numerical differences were observed, Kruskal–Wallis analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in cardiac T2*, liver iron concentration, calcium, PTH, or ALP among the groups (all p > 0.05).

In risk group analysis, patients with cardiac MRI T2* <12 ms or liver MRI T2* <3.6 ms were classified as high-risk (n = 26), while the remaining patients were classified as low-risk (n = 50). Approximately 38% of patients in the severe deficiency group and 30% in the mild deficiency group were classified as high-risk. High-risk patients were also identified among those with sufficient vitamin D levels, primarily due to hepatic iron accumulation.

The relationship between vitamin D levels and liver iron concentration was evaluated using regression analysis, yielding the equation:

Liver iron = 3.36 + 0.033 × 25(OH)D.

This association was not statistically significant (R² = 0.011, p = 0.367).

Following vitamin D supplementation, patients with severe deficiency (13 of 76; 17%) demonstrated improvements in cardiac and hepatic T2* values of 85% and 23%, respectively. Patients with mild deficiency (33 of 76; 44%) showed improvements of 76% and 42%, respectively. No statistically significant improvements were observed in patients with sufficient vitamin D levels (25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL; 30 of 76; 39%) (p > 0.05). Differences among groups were not statistically significant.

Multivariable regression analysis identified serum ferritin as the variable with the most significant effect on cardiac MRI T2* (p = 0.002), with increasing ferritin levels associated with a significant reduction in cardiac T2*. A positive and statistically significant association was found between serum calcium levels and cardiac MRI T2* (p = 0.042). No other variables were identified as significant predictors. For liver iron concentration, ferritin remained the primary determinant (p = 0.011).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a weak negative correlation between vitamin D and PTH levels (r = − 0.25). Although biologically plausible, the strength of this association was limited. All clinical and laboratory parameters are presented in Fig. 1.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to emphasize the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among patients with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia and to evaluate the effectiveness of vitamin D replacement therapy. Previous reports have suggested that vitamin D levels may be directly related to hemoglobin concentration, reflecting disease severity, as well as to the adverse effects of iron accumulation. However, the relationship between vitamin D status and cardiac and hepatic iron burden and function has not been clearly defined to date.

In the present study, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among patients with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia (44/76; 58%) was comparable to rates reported in previous studies, which ranged from 60% to 80%. These studies originated from diverse geographic regions, including North America, Italy, and North India [15, 16]. Severe vitamin D deficiency has been reported in 12.8–37.2% of patients with thalassemia in cohorts from North America and Tehran [16, 17]. The prevalence of severe deficiency observed in our study (13 of 76 patients; 17%) was consistent with these earlier findings.

This study included 88 pediatric patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia, providing comprehensive data on demographic characteristics, biochemical parameters, and the association between vitamin D status, iron overload, and cardiac health. The largest proportion of patients belonged to the 4–9-year age group (n = 53), followed by the 10–14-year (n = 22) and 15–19-year (n = 13) groups. The gender distribution was relatively balanced (54.5% male, 45.5% female), consistent with previous pediatric thalassemia cohorts. [18] Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of 8.38 years indicates that most patients were in early to mid-childhood, a critical developmental period during which metabolic and endocrine disturbances may have long-term consequences.

The reliability of MRI-based iron assessment has been well established. John C. Wood et al. evaluated noninvasive MRI-derived T2 and T2* techniques in 102 patients with iron overload and demonstrated strong correlations with hepatic iron concentration (r² ≥ 0.95) across clinically relevant ranges. [19] Their findings confirmed that both T2 and T2* are accurate and reproducible measures of tissue iron burden when appropriate acquisition protocols are used.

In alignment with the study by Wood et al., our investigation assessed cardiac MRI T2*, liver MRI T2*, serum iron indices, calcium, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). The mean cardiac T2* value was 30.21 ms, and the mean liver iron concentration was 3.42 mg/g dry weight. Mean serum calcium and PTH levels were 9.57 mg/dL and 36.71 pg/mL, respectively (Table 2). These findings suggest that, despite iron overload, biochemical markers related to bone metabolism and calcium homeostasis were not significantly different across vitamin D status groups, as supported by the absence of statistical significance in Kruskal–Wallis testing (p > 0.05).

Risk stratification based on cardiac MRI T2* (< 12 ms) and liver MRI T2* (< 3.6 ms) classified 26 patients as high-risk and 50 as low-risk. Notably, 38% of patients with severe vitamin D deficiency and 30% of those with mild deficiency were categorized as high-risk. The identification of high-risk patients even among those with sufficient vitamin D levels suggests that vitamin D status alone is insufficient to fully counteract iron-related organ damage. This observation highlights the complex and bidirectional relationship between iron overload and vitamin D metabolism, wherein hepatic iron accumulation may impair vitamin D activation, while vitamin D deficiency may exacerbate oxidative stress and dysregulate iron metabolism [19, 20].

Regression analysis assessing the association between vitamin D levels and liver iron concentration demonstrated a weak, non-significant relationship (R² = 0.011, p = 0.367). Similarly, the weak inverse correlation between vitamin D and PTH levels (r = − 0.25) indicates limited predictive value, underscoring the need for further mechanistic studies to clarify these interactions.

In patients with severe vitamin D deficiency, substantial improvements in cardiac and hepatic MRI T2* values were observed following supplementation, with improvement rates of 85% and 23%, respectively. These findings suggest that vitamin D repletion may exert a beneficial effect on iron burden, particularly in patients with severe deficiency. In contrast, no significant improvements were observed among patients with sufficient baseline vitamin D levels, indicating that vitamin D alone is unlikely to be sufficient for optimal iron overload management.

Multivariable regression analysis identified serum ferritin as the most significant predictor of cardiac MRI T2* (p = 0.002), with increasing ferritin levels associated with worsening myocardial iron burden. This finding confirms the established association between iron overload and cardiac dysfunction in thalassemia. [17] Similar results were reported by Parale et al. [21] and Seliem et al. [22], who demonstrated that chronic iron overload leads to elevated ferritin levels and increased left ventricular mass index, while systolic function remains preserved until advanced stages of disease.

A positive association between serum calcium levels and cardiac MRI T2* (p = 0.042) was also identified, suggesting that calcium metabolism may influence myocardial performance in this population. While serum ferritin levels remain above 2500 µg/L, echocardiographic parameters may exhibit compensatory changes, maintaining systolic function despite early ventricular remodeling. However, as iron burden increases, these compensatory mechanisms become insufficient, ultimately leading to systolic and diastolic dysfunction.

Clinical manifestations of cardiac dysfunction therefore tend to emerge later in life rather than during childhood. According to the Frank–Starling mechanism, ventricular dilation initially preserves cardiac output, but this compensation deteriorates over time. Consequently, imaging-based assessment remains the most accurate approach for early detection of myocardial involvement. T2* MRI is currently the gold-standard noninvasive modality for evaluating cardiac and hepatic iron burden and for monitoring response to chelation therapy [23].

Consistent with our findings, Mrdula et al. conducted a cross-sectional hospital-based study in children with beta-thalassemia major and demonstrated a significant negative correlation between vitamin D and serum ferritin, along with a positive association between vitamin D levels and echocardiographic indices of systolic function (EF and FS) [24].

Post-treatment MRI assessments performed three months after vitamin D supplementation demonstrated significant improvements in both cardiac and hepatic MRI T2* values (p < 0.05). The improvement rates are illustrated in Fig. 4. These results further support a potential adjunctive role for vitamin D in mitigating iron-induced organ damage. Persistently higher ferritin levels in the high-risk group at both time points (p < 0.01) emphasize the necessity of continued iron monitoring and optimized chelation strategies.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies reporting vitamin D deficiency prevalence rates of 60–80% in transfusion-dependent thalassemia [25]. The severe deficiency rate of 17% observed in our cohort aligns with previously reported rates from North America and the Middle East [26, 27]. Iron overload remains the primary determinant of myocardial dysfunction in thalassemia, and the inverse association between ferritin and cardiac T2* observed in our study corroborates earlier MRI-based evidence demonstrating increased cardiac siderosis at ferritin levels exceeding 2500 µg/L.

Mechanistically, vitamin D deficiency may facilitate myocardial iron uptake by altering calcium channel regulation, increasing oxidative stress, and impairing mitochondrial function. [28] Vitamin D supplementation may counteract these effects by restoring intracellular calcium balance and reducing reactive oxygen species. These mechanisms are supported by the findings of Mrdula et al. [29], who demonstrated improved cardiac function with higher vitamin D levels.

Conclusion

This study confirms the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among pediatric patients with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia and provides preliminary evidence that vitamin D repletion is associated with short-term improvements in myocardial and hepatic iron parameters. Vitamin D supplementation resulted in significant improvements in cardiac and hepatic MRI T2* indices, particularly among patients with severe deficiency.

However, the findings also highlight that adequate vitamin D levels alone may not fully prevent iron-related organ damage, underscoring the need for comprehensive, individualized management strategies that integrate optimized chelation therapy, regular imaging surveillance, and metabolic monitoring. Vitamin D supplementation may serve as a valuable adjunctive therapy in reducing iron-induced organ toxicity, but larger, prospective, controlled studies are required to establish causality, long-term benefits, and optimal dosing strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and the short follow-up duration of three months may have limited the ability to fully evaluate the long-term effects of vitamin D supplementation on myocardial and hepatic iron burden. Second, cardiac T2* MRI could not be performed in children younger than four years of age due to breath-holding requirements, which restricted the assessment of cardiac iron status in this subgroup.

Third, although all patients were managed under standardized transfusion and iron-chelation protocols, minor variations in chelator type, dosage, and transfusion frequency may have independently influenced hepatic and myocardial iron levels, irrespective of vitamin D status. Fourth, the absence of a placebo-controlled group precludes definitive causal inference regarding the observed effects of vitamin D supplementation on organ iron content, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Fifth, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D measurements reflected total vitamin D concentrations and did not differentiate between vitamin D₂ and vitamin D₃ isoforms. Finally, potential confounding factors such as growth velocity, dietary intake, sunlight exposure, and concurrent endocrine abnormalities were not fully controlled. Future studies involving larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and randomized controlled designs are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and to further elucidate the mechanistic pathways linking vitamin D metabolism with iron homeostasis in transfusion-dependent thalassemia.

Sixth, Echocardiographic evaluation, including ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS), was performed at baseline in patients for whom imaging was feasible, particularly in younger children (< 6 years) in whom cardiac MRI could not be obtained due to breath-holding limitations. At baseline, EF and FS values were within age-adjusted normal ranges, and no patient demonstrated evidence of systolic dysfunction. Following vitamin D supplementation, no statistically significant change in EF or FS was observed. Given the preserved baseline cardiac function and the limited sample size in this age subgroup, these findings were not considered clinically meaningful and therefore were not included in the main analysis.

Similarly, evaluation of liver function parameters (including transaminases and cholestatic markers where available) did not reveal consistent abnormalities at baseline, nor was a significant improvement observed after vitamin D supplementation. We believe that the young age of these patients likely represents an early disease stage, prior to the development of overt cardiac or hepatic dysfunction, which may explain the absence of detectable functional impairment or measurable response in these parameters.

However, we agree that systematic longitudinal cardiac and hepatic assessment in larger cohorts and at later disease stages may provide important insights and represents an important direction for future studies.
