Cross‐Cultural Adaptation, Reliability and Validation of the Mini‐OAKHQoL for Knee/Hip Osteoarthritis in a Syrian Arabic‐Speaking Population: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ruba Altahla, Jamal Alshorman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsOsteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease characterized. The Mini‐Osteoarthritis Knee and Hip Quality of Life (Mini‐OAKHQoL) scale is used to evaluate the impact of knee/hip osteoarthritis (KOA/HOA) on patients' quality of life (QoL). Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is recognized as the prevailing joint condition, primarily affecting older patients and serving as a major cause of pain and disability [1]. This results in a gradual decrease in functional capacity and overall quality of life (QoL) [2]. According to Neogi, individuals afflicted with OA commonly endure prolonged pain that escalates during physical activity [3]. Consequently, individuals with OA often curtail their participation in various activities to avoid exacerbating pain. The persistent pain associated with knee/hip osteoarthritis (KOA/HOA) significantly impacts overall QoL, leading to adverse effects on mood, social and recreational activities, and sleep patterns [4].

Despite the recognition of KOA/HOA as a significant health issue, quantitative data specifically contextualizing its impact in the Syrian and Arabic‐speaking population remains limited. Current literature indicates that approximately 30% of adults over the age of 50 in Syria are affected by KOA, with 65% of these individuals experiencing considerable limitations in daily activities [5]. Furthermore, the average score of 45 on the SF‐36 physical functioning scale underscores the substantial impairment in quality of life associated with this condition [6]. The economic burden of osteoarthritis, estimated at 23 million dollars annually in Syria due to healthcare costs and lost productivity, highlights the urgent need for culturally adapted assessment tools, such as the Mini‐OAKHQoL, to improve patient outcomes in this demographic.

Individuals with OA experience disruptions in their daily life routines. Notably, approximately 25% of those with OA exhibit a decline in functional capacity, which contributes to a reduction in their QoL [7]. The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Lequesne Index assess disease‐related factors, primarily focusing on pain and function. However, they lack the ability to evaluate other QoL dimensions, such as mental, social, and sexual domains [8, 9]. Nevertheless, these tools may not fully capture specific QoL aspects associated with KOA/HOA. Assessing patients' perspectives using Health‐Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) measures allows for a comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted disease burden. This includes outcomes that focus on patients' well‐being, social interactions, and psychological health [10]. Hence, HRQoL plays a crucial role in making treatment decisions, especially for individuals with OA. This condition imposes a substantial burden on social, health, and economic domains.

Several commonly used generic scales are designed to assess QoL across a wide range of diseases [11, 12, 13]. However, these general‐purpose scales may not have the required sensitivity to accurately capture disease‐specific HRQoL. Over time, this could result in them being unresponsive to changes or treatment [11, 14].

While generic QoL tools like the SF‐36 provide broad assessments of health status, they often lack sensitivity to OA‐specific impacts. This aligns with prior studies demonstrating that disease‐specific tools (e.g., OAKHQoL, WOMAC) capture 20–30% greater variance in OA‐related disability than generic measures [15, 16]. Additionally, the SF‐36's social functioning subscale omits key OA concerns like fear of dependency or sexual limitations, which the Mini‐OAKHQoL addresses directly. Such disparities underscore the need for condition‐specific tools in OA research, particularly in populations with high comorbid burden, where generic scales may conflate disease impacts with other health issues.

OA is a chronic condition that often manifests with age, and the occurrence of comorbidities is prevalent. Several factors may influence the assessment of QoL when using non‐specific HRQoL instruments to evaluate systemic impacts [17]. Moreover, treatment methods and strategies should vary depending on the specific body sites affected. Therefore, employing a QoL instrument specifically designed for the disease's site and nature can offer an alternative solution. Furthermore, despite its importance as a fundamental aspect of HRQoL measures, the absence of a dedicated assessment system focusing on social support is notable [10]. Therefore, using a standard, site‐specific tool has the potential to enhance clinical understanding, the impact on patients, and the evaluation of HRQoL in individuals living with KOA/HOA. Moreover, as indicated by previous research, such tools can effectively evaluate variations in HRQoL over time in OA [11].

The Mini‐OAKHQoL includes 20 items and is a condensed version derived from the original OAKHQoL scale. Both scales were developed to evaluate HRQoL in individuals with KOA/HOA. Recent evaluations have indicated that the Mini‐OAKHQoL scale demonstrates positive psychometric properties in validation studies conducted across diverse populations [18]. Currently, the Mini‐OAKHQoL is available in French [17] and Turkish language [19]. However, as a brief version, it reduces the time required to fill out patient data. This study fills a critical gap, as no validated OA‐specific QoL tools currently exist for Arabic‐speaking populations, particularly in conflict‐affected regions like Syria. Systematic reviews confirm this deficit—a 2021 MENA‐region analysis identified only 2 generic Arabic QoL tools with limited OA applicability [20, 21], while a 2023 WHO report highlighted Syria's complete lack of validated musculoskeletal PROMs post‐2011 [22]. This study intends to translate the original scale, tailor it to cultural needs, and assess the reliability and validity of the Syrian Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL in KOA/HOA contexts, for both research and clinical applications.

Methodology

This study included a cross‐sectional investigation of 87 patients with KOA/HOA between March and August 2023 at four outpatient rehabilitation clinics in Damascus, Syria. Rehabilitation sessions were conducted by skilled practitioners under the supervision of rehabilitation physicians. Among the 121 patients invited to participate, only 87 met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria required individuals to have either bilateral or unilateral KOA/HOA, diagnosed based on clinical and radiological criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR). These criteria included knee pain, radiographic osteophytes, and the presence of ≥ 1 of 3 specified symptoms/signs (Morning stiffness lasting ≤ 30 min, Crepitus, Bony tenderness or enlargement on palpation). Additionally, participants were required to meet the following criteria: aged > 30 years, experiencing morning stiffness lasting ≤ 30 min, demonstrating crepitus during active motion, and possessing proficiency in reading Arabic. Convenience sampling was employed in this study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Figure 1).

Sample and Data Collection

The sample size was determined using the methodology described by Hair et al. [23], which recommends a minimum of 5–10 participants per item for scale validation studies; since the Mini‐OAKHQoL consists of 20 items, our study included 87 participants, exceeding this guideline. For the test‐retest reliability analysis, a subsample of 30 patients was selected to achieve the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of ≥ 0.7 with statistical significance (p < 0.05), as recommended by Shoukri et al. [24] for reliability studies, ensuring the sample size was adequate to detect meaningful reliability estimates.

The Syrian Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL was administered to the patients. A sociodemographic survey (assessing age, gender, education, occupation, and KOA/HOA history) was conducted. Kellgren‐Lawrence classification scores and body mass index (BMI) measurements were recorded. In this study, radiographic images (anteroposterior and lateral views for knees; anteroposterior pelvis views for hips) were obtained for all participants. Two independent radiologists, blinded to clinical data, evaluated the images and assigned KL grades. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The KL classification was used to stratify participants by disease severity (Grade 0 to Grade 4. Grade 0 indicates no radiographic features of OA. In Grade 1, there may be doubtful joint space narrowing (JSN) and possible osteophytic lipping. Grade 2 presents with definite osteophytes and potential JSN. Grade 3 is characterized by moderate osteophytes, definite JSN, and some bony sclerosis, while Grade 4 displays large osteophytes, severe JSN, marked bony sclerosis, and clear deformity), and to correlate radiographic findings with clinical outcomes (e.g., Mini‐OAKHQoL scores). This approach aligns with prior validation studies of OA‐specific tools [15, 16] and ensures comparability with international OA research standards. Exclusion criteria included inflammatory diseases, cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorders, a history of knee or hip surgery, and other lower limb diseases. The investigator administered the survey to all patients and instructed them to complete it independently. The sample size was determined using the methodology described by Hair et al. [23].

The Mini‐OAKHQoL consists of twenty items and evaluates QoL over the previous month. It encompasses five dimensions: seven items for physical activities (I1–I5, I7, I11), three for mental health (I8, I17, I18), two for social support (I19, I20), three for pain (I12, I13, I16), and two for social functioning (I14, I15).

Additionally, the Mini‐OAKHQoL includes three specific items that focus on professional life [25], sexual life (I10), and the fear of dependency [I9], as indicated in previous studies [19, 26]. The items in the Mini‐OAKHQoL utilize numerical rating scales that range from 0 (worst) to 10 (best). The score for each category is obtained by calculating the average of the individual item scores within that specific category. Although social functioning and social support items are presented as inverse questions, they are scored consistently with the “0 (worst) to 10 (best)” concept used for the other categories. Cronbach's alpha of the overall Arabic version scale (α = 0.931) [22]. The scores for the final three independent items are used as their respective scores without being averaged with other items. The Short Form‐36 (SF‐36) is a frequently employed generic QoL scale consisting of 8 categories and 36 items. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better health status. The Cronbach alpha was good (a = 0.8) [22, 27, 28].

The WOMAC is a self‐administered scale specifically designed to evaluate the impact of OA. It uses Likert‐type response scales for participants to rate their experiences and assess symptom severity and disability across domains such as physical function and stiffness. The scores for each WOMAC category are combined, with the highest scores being 20 points for pain, 8 for stiffness, and 68 for physical function, respectively. However, higher scores on these scales indicate a less favorable health condition. Test–retest reliability was good to excellent with ICC of 0.91, 0.89, and 0.90, respectively [29, 30].

The ethics committee at Al Baath University approved this study (consent letter: IRB 2023151‐S). All study processes were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions. The purpose of the survey was clarified to each patient, who voluntarily agreed to complete the questionnaire. To ensure anonymity, blind forms were used, and data were provided in an anonymous format.

Translation and Validation Procedure

This study comprises two distinct phases. The first phase entails translating the Mini‐OAKHQoL scale and adapting it for Arabic use. The second phase involves evaluating the validity and reliability of the Arabic version.

Phase 1: Translating the Mini‐OAKHQoL Scale

The translation of the French Mini‐OAKHQoL scale into Arabic involved a comprehensive process that included translation and back‐translation. This meticulous adaptation followed widely accepted Beaton et al. (2000) guidelines to ensure accuracy and cultural relevance in the Syrian Arabic version [26]. Subsequently, an expert panel was established. The panel involved in the process consisted of three healthcare professionals, two language experts, and two patients. This panel rigorously examined all translations at every phase of the procedure. Two interpreters' native Arabic speakers proficient in French, with significant residency experience in France, independently translated the original scale into Arabic. Each interpreter was tasked with reporting any ambiguity in each item of the original version. Moreover, they were required to document changes to address the standard adaptation requirements for the Arabic language. Within the expert panel, patients were asked about the ease of answering and their understanding of each item's content.

Following the synthesis phase, the expert panel reviewed and combined the two translations, and an independent back‐translation from Arabic to French was conducted. Translators who were native French speakers with a high level of proficiency in Arabic were assigned to the task. In the final phase, the panel agreed on the pre‐final scale version. After comparing the back‐translated version with the original, it was determined that each item remained consistent and retained its intended meaning. Consequently, the Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL scale was finalized and made ready for the validation study.

Phase 2: Validation Steps

Validity Step

The authors thoroughly examined the pre‐final version of the scale, comprising two orthopedic specialists, three rehabilitation experts, and one rheumatologist, along with native French language speakers. Content validity was assessed to ensure the relevance of the items to the subject matter and QoL [25]. This study assessed construct validity by examining the correlation between the Mini‐OAKHQoL, WOMAC, and SF‐36 scales using Spearman's correlation coefficient. Face validity was assessed through cognitive debriefing interviews with 30 patients, aimed at evaluating the readability, comprehension, and feasibility of the Arabic version. These individuals were excluded from the final study sample.

Construct Validity

Convergent and divergent validity analyses were conducted to evaluate the scale's construct properties by examining its alignment with similar measures (convergent validity) and its differentiation from measures assessing different constructs (divergent validity). Convergent validity was established by demonstrating the alignment between the Mini‐OAKHQoL and other HRQoL tools, as evidenced by their correlations with the SF‐36 and WOMAC. Divergent validity analysis was conducted to assess the Mini‐OAKHQoL's ability to differentiate between theoretically distinct constructs.

Reliability Step

The psychometric characteristics of the Mini‐OAKHQoL scale were assessed using the principles of cross‐cultural adaptation [26]. Internal consistency was evaluated by calculating Cronbach's α, with values > 0.7 considered acceptable. Additionally, after 2 weeks was performed test‐retest reliability to determine using the ICC to estimate outcomes, with a correlation > 0.7 considered robust [25]. To evaluate test‐retest reliability, 30 patients were requested to complete the Mini‐OAKHQoL again after 2 weeks. Of these, 27 patients (90%) completed the follow‐up assessment and were included in the reproducibility analysis (Figure 1). As well as, to fulfill test‐retest reliability, the patients were invited for a second hospital visit during which they did not receive any treatment or intervention.

Phase 3: Scale Structure

The Mini‐OAKHQoL possesses a theoretically and empirically established five‐factor structure, originally confirmed through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in the French validation study [17] and subsequently supported in Turkish [19] and Spanish [31] populations. The five dimensions include: physical activities (7 items), mental health (3 items), pain (3 items), social support (2 items), and social functioning (2 items). Additionally, three items are treated as independent components assessing professional life, sexual life, and fear of dependency, as they represent distinct constructs not loading strongly on any single factor in the original validation [17]. In the present study, construct validity was assessed through convergent validity analysis by examining correlations with the WOMAC and SF‐36 scales, rather than conducting a de novo factor analysis, as the primary aim was cross‐cultural adaptation and reliability testing of the already validated instrument.

Internal Consistency

This study observed that the scale exhibited commendable content validity upon scrutiny of the penultimate Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL. Researchers conducted interviews with 30 patients to gain a deeper understanding of their interpretation of each item's meaning. The patients confirmed that all items were clear and easily comprehensible, and they attested that completing the scale was practical and feasible. Hence, no additional cultural adjustments were necessary. Consequently, the scale demonstrated suitable face and content validity, requiring no further cultural adjustments, and all items were found to retain their original meaning without alteration, as indicated in the Appendix.

Ethics Approval

All human subjects involved in this study were treated in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the ethics committee of Al Baath University with a consent letter – IRB No. 2,023,151‐S. Informed consent has been obtained from all individuals included in this study.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistical Software, version 29.0 (Copyright, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The statistical methods were conducted in accordance with the Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines [22]. Descriptive data consisted of frequencies and percentages for categorical data, while the group's mean and SD were calculated. The following pre‐specified primary analyses were performed to evaluate the psychometric properties of Syrian Arabic Mini‐OAKHQoL: Internal Consistency Reliability: This was done using Cronbach's Alpha (α). In addition, subgroup analyses: all comparisons between KL grades were carried out using the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups, and Kruskal–Wallis for comparisons between more than two groups. In addition, comparisons between joint types (knee, hip, both) were also carried out using Kruskal–Wallis.

Factor Analysis

Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was carried out in order to examine the underlying factor structure of the scale. Factors with eigenvalue > 1.0 were retained.

All the tests were two‐tailed tests, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant for all the tests conducted in this study. The level of significance for all the tests conducted in this study was set at 0.05. The sample size for the test‐retest reliability tests was calculated to be 30 for an ICC > 0.7 with 80% power at a 0.05 significance level using the sample size calculation tool for test‐retest reliability [24].

Scale Reliability

Internal Consistency

The internal consistency reliability of the pain, mental health, social support, physical activity, and social functioning dimensions was acceptable. Table 4 demonstrates that the test‐retest reliability for all categories was excellent. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) for test‐retest differences ranged from 1.02 (social function) to 3.07 (social support), indicating moderate‐to‐large stability across domains (Table 4). Moreover, removing any individual item did not significantly improve the overall Cronbach's alpha value. In fact, the internal consistency remained high, demonstrating that all items contribute meaningfully to the scale. Supporting this, items were carefully designed to reflect various dimensions of quality of life for patients with KOA/HOA.

Scale Validity

Items Validity

Thirty patients with OA participated in face‐to‐face interviews to assess the validity of the scale items, and the majority of patients were satisfied that all items were crucial for confirming the extent of KOA/HOA issues. The participants found the scale to be generally accessible, uncomplicated to comprehend, expeditious to complete, and requiring no modifications.

Structural Validity

The majority of the Mini‐OAKHQoL category showed a moderate correlation with the WOMAC and SF‐36. Physical activities have a moderate association with WOMAC (pain, r = 0.301, p = 0.005) and a weak association with SF‐36 (physical role strength, r = 0.242, p = 0.025; emotional role strength, r = −0.241, p = 0.025), respectively. Mental health has significant correlation with WOMAC (pain, r = −0.301, p = 0.005) and SF‐36 (vitality, r = −0.274, p = 0.010; emotional well‐being, r = 0.377, p = 0.010), respectively. Mini‐OAKHQoL pain have significant correlation with WOMAC (physical function, r = −0.251, p = 0.019) and SF‐36 (physical functioning, r = −0.310, p = 0.003, physical role strength; r = 0.230, p = 0.032; emotional well‐being, r = −0.283, p = 0.008, social functioning, r = 0.236, p = 0.028; general health perception, r = −0.223, p = 0.038). Moreover, the social support category has an association with WOMAC (physical function, r = −0.295, p = 0.006) and SF‐36 (emotional role strength, r = 0.368, p < 0.001; vitality, r = 0.259, p = 0.015), respectively. Social function of Mini‐OAKHQoL has significant correlation with WOMAC (pain, r = 0.308, p = 0.004) and SF‐36 (physical functioning, r = 0.374, p < 0.001) (emotional role strength, r = 0.289, p = 0.007) (vitality, r = 0.309, p = 0.004). Independent items of Mini‐OAKHQoL have significant correlation with WOMAC pain, r = −0.304, p = 0.004 and stiffness, r = 0.224, p = 0.037. SF‐36 physical functioning, r = −0.248, p = 0.020 and social functioning, r = 0.352, p < 0.001. While some correlations were modest (e.g., social support with physical function), these reflect the Mini‐OAKHQoL's broader scope, which includes domains (e.g., social functioning, fear of dependency) not fully captured by WOMAC or SF‐36. The pattern of stronger correlations for conceptually aligned domains (e.g., pain, physical function) and weaker correlations for divergent domains supports the scale's construct validity (Table 6).

Factor Analysis

The 8‐factor model explained 72.556% of the total variance in the data, which is considered an acceptable level of explained variance (Supplementary 1).

Discussion

The Mini‐OAKHQoL is an inclusive tool that encompasses the overall impact of KOA/HOA and the effects of treatment on QoL. The association of OA with concurrent illnesses can lead to a more disproportionate burden. Individuals with musculoskeletal diseases encounter a greater number of health issues compared to those without such conditions [32]. In contrast, the OA core set outlined in the International Classification of Functioning (ICF), Disability, and Health encompasses a wide range of dimensions. These include emotional well‐being, sleep quality, pain perception, intimate relationships, employment status, community engagement, participation in recreational activities, dynamics within the immediate family, and individual attitudes. Furthermore, it accounts for restrictions in physical activity [33]. A significant advantage of the OAKHQoL is its comprehensive coverage of ICF domains and its effective addressing of specific aspects relevant to patients with KOA/HOA. Moreover, the outcomes of this study demonstrate that the Arabic version of Mini‐OAKHQoL possesses satisfactory content validity and is culture‐adapted for OA use. The psychometric evaluation of the Arabic version yielded favorable statistical results, demonstrating that it is a robust, valid, and reliable instrument for individuals with OA. The psychometric evaluation of the Arabic Mini‐OAKHQoL aligns with established validation studies across cultures. Internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.931) exceeded the threshold of 0.7, matching the Turkish (α = 0.89) and French (α = 0.91) versions, while test‐retest reliability (ICC = 0.844) was comparable to the Spanish adaptation (ICC = 0.87) [19, 31]. Convergent validity was strongest for physical domains (r = 0.374 with SF‐36 physical function), replicating findings from prior studies (e.g., r = 0.35–0.42 in Guillemin et al., 2016) [17], but weaker for social support (r = 0.259), likely due to culturally distinct interpretations of familial roles in Syria [27]. These results confirm the Arabic version's robustness while highlighting domain‐specific considerations for clinical use. Findings in this study, the minimal mean differences (< 0.2 points) and large effect sizes (d > 1.0) support the scale's stability and practical utility.

The structural richness and dimensionality of the Mini‐OAKHQoL have been substantiated through factor analysis in previous validation studies [13, 14, 17, 19, 31]. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a five‐factor model among French, Turkish, and Spanish individuals with KOA/HOA. In the present study, we focused on assessing construct validity through convergent and divergent validity with established instruments (WOMAC and SF‐36), as the primary aim was cross‐cultural adaptation and reliability testing of the already validated instrument rather than exploration of its factor structure. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a five‐factor model among Spanish individuals with KOA/HOA, and the present research provides evidence for the feasibility of the Mini‐OAKHQoL. The influence of scale length on response rates remains a topic of ongoing debate, though shorter scales appear to offer advantages [29, 34]. Furthermore, the brief completion duration established that shorter scale versions can reduce administration time. The original OAKHQoL required an average completion time of 10 to 15 min [32], whereas the Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL could be completed in approximately 4 min. Similarly, the scoring process was streamlined, taking approximately 2 min for the Mini‐OAKHQoL. These outcomes are significant for the acceptance and utilization of the evaluation tool in both clinical research and patient care settings.

The Mini‐OAKHQoL exhibits satisfactory internal reliability and indicates a strong correlation among items within the categories. It displays excellent test‐retest reliability, suggesting minimal random measurement error. Based on consistent reliability analyses across various populations [19], the instrument can be inferred to be reliable and demonstrate reproducibility.

The physical activity, mental health, and pain categories showed notable interconnections with the corresponding subscales of the SF‐36 and WOMAC, providing strong evidence of convergent validity. Based on our findings, the most plausible explanation is that the Mini‐OAKHQoL incorporates a more in‐depth assessment of social aspects compared to other instruments. The Mini‐OAKHQoL evaluates both social functioning and social support, which distinguishes it from other HRQoL scales that often concentrate solely on aspects such as social isolation [28, 29, 30]. An alternative explanation may relate to guidance on the utilization of PROMs: if responses to individual questions do not align with those from a multi‐section scale, it suggests the scale may not comprehensively encompass all essential categories related to the concept under discussion [32]. The social subscale of the SF‐36 serves as a concrete example that strengthens this hypothesis. In contrast, items in the social category of the Mini‐OAKHQoL reflect activities such as getting out of the house, planning long‐term projects, and social support.

While the Mini‐OAKHQoL demonstrated strong reliability and validity overall, its performance in social and emotional domains warrants further discussion. Compared to other validation studies (e.g., the Turkish and Spanish versions), our Arabic version showed similar patterns of weaker correlations for social support and functioning with WOMAC/SF‐36 [15]. This aligns with the scale's design, which captures OA‐specific social concerns (e.g., fear of dependency, sexual limitations) not addressed by generic tools like the SF‐36. However, the modest correlations may also reflect cultural nuances in how social support is perceived or reported in Syrian populations, particularly in conflict‐affected settings where social dynamics may differ. For instance, items like ‘I feel supported by my relatives’ (I20) could be influenced by familial roles unique to Arab cultures, potentially limiting cross‐cultural comparability. Similarly, emotional domain correlations (e.g., mental health with SF‐36 vitality, r = −0.274) were weaker than those for physical domains, possibly due to overlapping but distinct constructs (e.g., OA‐related distress vs. general emotional well‐being). These discrepancies mirror findings in the French validation, where social items required careful cultural adaptation [18]. Future studies should explore qualitative insights to clarify these domain‐specific challenges.

The inconsistencies in content among various QoL scales explain why a strong relationship was not observed between the Mini‐OAKHQoL and other scales. Specifically, it is important to avoid interpreting the results as indicating inadequate validity for the scale in social aspects or domains. Instead, they may highlight the comprehensive nature and inherent features of the Mini‐OAKHQoL. Both the Spanish and original French studies showed evidence of the observed discrepancies regarding the convergent validity of social functioning and support [19]. The observed correlations align with the instrument's design: higher correlations for overlapping domains (e.g., pain, physical function) and lower correlations for distinct constructs (e.g., social support vs. physical health) confirm its ability to differentiate between dimensions. This mirrors findings in other cultural adaptations, where social domains exhibited weaker correlations due to their unique focus.

This study has several limitations. First, the convenience sampling method and recruitment from four outpatient clinics in Damascus may limit generalizability to the broader Syrian population, particularly those in rural or conflict‐affected areas, and a small sample size is a severe limitation in conducting a CFA or an EFA. Second, the cross‐sectional design precluded sensitivity‐to‐change analysis, which is critical for evaluating the scale's responsiveness to interventions. Future longitudinal studies are needed to address this gap. Third, potential selection bias and the lack of diverse regional representation may affect the external validity of our findings. While our translation process adhered to Beaton's guidelines, future studies could further strengthen validity by involving external experts earlier in the process (e.g., during initial translation synthesis). Finally, the impact of comorbidities on QoL was not assessed, warranting further investigation [19, 31]. In conclusion, the Arabic version of the Mini‐OAKHQoL demonstrates robust psychometric properties, including excellent reliability and validity, making it a suitable tool for assessing QoL in Syrian Arabic‐speaking patients with KOA/HOA. Its brevity and cultural adaptability enhance its utility in both clinical and research settings.
