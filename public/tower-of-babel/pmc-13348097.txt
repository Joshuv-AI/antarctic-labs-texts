Viscometric and UV–Visible spectroscopic characterization of L-Ascorbic Acid and Caffeine mixtures: insights into solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions
Authors: Tanika Dutta, Malabika Talukdar, Sulochana Singh, Siddhartha Panda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study equips significant molecular intuition into the compatibility of L-Ascorbic Acid (LAA, solute) and caffeine (CAF, co-solute), two bio-actives widely co-formulated in nutraceutical and pharmaceutical products. Varying concentrations of LAA (0.02–0.14 ) and CAF (0.025, 0.050, 0.075 ) in aqueous medium are used in this present study. A cohesive approach combining viscometric measurements, thermodynamic analysis and UV–Visible spectroscopic analysis was employed to elucidate their intermolecular interactions in aqueous medium at varying working temperatures (293.15 K-313.15 K) and at atmospheric pressure. Dynamic viscosity data were scrutinized through the Jone-Dole equation to assess solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions, while transfer parameters, solvation numbers and thermodynamic functions were employed to explain hydration behaviour and viscous flow. Positive Jones-Dole coefficient and solvation number above 2.5 confirm predominant ion–solvent interactions and enhanced hydration structuring. Uprising trend of viscosity with solution concentration and downward slope of temperature derivative of indicate strengthened molecular associations across all systems. Thermodynamic parameters reveal higher activation free energies and contrasting enthalpies signatures for LAA-CAF systems, reflecting different molecular reorganization patterns. UV–Visible spectra exhibit hypsochromic, hyperchromic, hypochromic and bathochromic shifts upon mixing validating hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interactions. Collectively, these findings significantly deepen the molecular-level understanding of LAA-CAF compatibility and support the rational design of more stable, synergistic multi-component formulations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01816-y.Keywords: Physicochemical properties, Bioavailability, Thermochemical properties, Viscometry, UV–visible spectroscopy

Introduction

The rising global demand for the rapid expansion of functional beverages, nutraceuticals and multi-component pharmaceutical products has intensified the need understand how bioactive compounds behave when combined in complex solution environments. Among these, LAA (vitamin-C) and CAF are the two most widely co-consumed species, frequently incorporated together in energy drinks, effervescent formulations, dietary supplements and fortified foods [1, 2]. Their concurrent presence in modern consumer products reflects current global trends toward formulations that simultaneously deliver antioxidant protection and metabolic stimulation. Yet, despite their widespread use, the fundamental molecular interactions governing their coexistence in mixed systems remain insufficiently elucidated.

Exploration of the interactions between LAA and CAF in mixed systems is essential for elucidating how their combined presence influences physicochemical properties, stability, compatibility, stability and potential intermolecular interactions and biological behaviour. The study revealed that variations in pH, temperature and concentration significantly influence the stability profile of LAA, particularly due to its well-known susceptibility to oxidative degradation. Such effects can in turn impact formulation performance, storage stability and the bioavailability of the active components. Spectroscopic and thermal analysis suggest possible non-covalent interactions including H-bonding and molecular association between LAA and CAF. These interactions may contribute to modified solubility behaviour. Such findings are particularly relevant in the development of co-formulated pharmaceutical and nutraceutical preparations. Some of the possible interactions are pictorially represented in Fig. 1.

Many researchers scrutinized different thermophysical properties of vitamins in aqueous medium along with solutes and co-solutes [3–9]. In such Banipal et al. has reported temperature-dependent apparent molar volumes, compressibility and viscosity coefficients of selected vitamins in water, which shows strong solute–solvent interactions, hydrolysis behavior differences, like LAA, thiamine HCl and pyridoxine HCl act as water structure breaker, whereas nicotinic acid behaves as a structure promoter [5]. Prachi et al. documented viscometric and conductometric results, which shows that increasing temperature lowers viscosity, LAA exhibits strong ion-ion and ion–solvent interactions acting as a structure breaker in aqueous KCl and a structure builder in DAP, viscous flow is spontaneous due to unfavorable transition-state formation and negative values confirm spontaneous ion association and ion-pair formation in KCl/DAP medium [3]. Jiang et al. concludes that densities and viscosities of Sorbitol/maltitol in aqueous LAA increase with solute and acid molality but decrease with temperatures, reflecting strong solute–solvent interactions dominated by hydrophilic effects, while maltitol exhibiting larger volumetric parameters but lower activation free energy than sorbitol due its structure and molar mass [6]. Richu et al. stated progressively stronger solute–solvent interactions from water to aqueous [Emim] [], dominance of pair over triplet interactions and chaotropic behavior of both LAA and thiamine hydrochloride in the studied solution [7]. A handful of papers were also available in different publications regarding the CAF thermochemical properties [10–14]. Sinha et al., Amirchand et al. and Ali et al. reported volumetric, viscometric, spectroscopic and theoretical analyses which reveal that CAF exhibits strong, temperature and concentration-dependent solute–solvent interactions, showing mild structure-breaking behaviour via hydrophobic hydration in Th()4 medium and dominant hydrophilic/ hydrogen-bonding interaction in aqueous [EABu]. In aqueous CAF-Amino acid system, strong solute–solvent and weak solute–solute interactions were observed and with CAF-induced shielding, electrostatic effect was reduced, promoting structure-building behaviour and increases system polarizability at higher concentrations [11, 12, 14].

Despite notable advances in solution chemistry, no contemporary study has systematically explored the coupled viscometric and UV–Visible spectroscopic behaviour of LAA in the presence of CAF. A distinct gap persists in the detailed assessment of thermochemical and physicochemical parameters of CAF in aqueous medium over a range of temperatures (293.15 K-313.15 K) along with all relevant derived functions. In this context, the present investigation offers a powerful and comprehensive approach. Viscometric study provides information regarding the flow behaviour of the liquids to govern molecular properties, such as polymer molecular weight, solute–solvent interactions and mixture compatibility [15]. Complementarily, UV–Visible spectroscopy detects variations in absorbance intensity or shifts in that arise from electronic perturbation induced by hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking or complex formation between LAA and CAF. When interpreted together, these techniques validate the interaction mechanisms and reveal how the coexistence of an antioxidant and stimulant alters the physicochemical environment of the solution. Such integrated evidence is highly relevant to real-world applications, as it directly supports the rational design of next-generation energy-drinks, effervescent preparations and multi-component pharmaceutical products with enhanced stability, compatibility and bioavailability.

In future, this research may further be extended to investigation of LAA-CAF systems at higher concentration and elevated temperature ranges under varying pH conditions, as well as in the presence of additional co-solutes to obtain deeper insight into their interaction dynamics. At elevated temperatures, changes in hydrogen bonding and hydration behaviour of the aqueous system can be inspected more visibly, helping to understand the nature of the solutes. It also permits estimation of the temperature dependence of the Jone-Dole coefficient, solvation nature and activation parameters of viscous flow. Other advanced spectroscopic methods (FTIR, NMR) could further elucidate the precise hydrogen-bonding network and microstructural organization of the ternary system.

Preparation of solutions

CAF solutions of predetermined molal concentrations (0.025, 0.050, 0.075 ) were prepared by accurately dissolving known masses of CAF in 1 kg of distilled water (density = 997.12 at 298.15 K). The resulting stock solutions, along with distilled water, were subsequently utilized as solvents for the preparation of LAA solutions with concentrations varying between 0.02 and 0.14 . All solutions were formulated under controlled laboratory conditions (298.15 K, 101 kPa). Solute masses were weighed with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mg using a Systronics digital analytical balance. The prepared solutions were stored in airtight polypropylene storage bottles and refrigerated for no longer than 2 days to prevent chemical degradation and moisture absorption. Apparatus and procedures

Density

Density measurements for LAA solutions prepared in both pure water and aqueous CAF medium were carried out using a vibrating-tube density analyser (Anton Paar 5000 M, Austria). This instrument quantifies liquid density through the oscillation U-tube principle, in which variations in oscillation frequency correlate with changes in sample density. Prior to analysis, the device was calibrated using dry air and deionized water, followed by rinsing with distilled water and anhydrous ethanol to ensure accurate and reproducible results. Calibration was performed before each set of measurements as recommended in the manufacture’s manual [5, 6, 16].

Temperature stability during experimentation was maintained at 0.01 K through an integrated Peltier thermostat, while the system pressure was controlled at 101 1 kPa. According to GUM guidelines, the instrument ensures a density accuracy of 0.005 and a density repeatability of 0.0001 , resulting in an overall measurement uncertainty of 0.5 .

Viscosity

For precise evaluation of molecular interactions within the studied systems, viscosity measurements were performed using a pre-calibrated Ostwald viscometer under rigorously controlled conditions. The apparatus, comprising a U-shaped capillary tube with an attached bulb of approximately 25 ml capacity, facilitated the determination of flow characteristics for each prepared solution. Prior to use, the instrument was meticulously cleaned and calibrated with deionized water at five distinct temperatures to account for temperature-induced variations in viscosity.

The flow time corresponding to the passage of the liquid meniscus between two fixed marks on the viscometer arm recorded using a digital stopwatch with an accuracy of 0.1 s. UV–Visible spectroscopy

The UV–Visible spectroscopy measurements were carried out in a Perkin Elmer lambda 365 spectrophotometer. It operates on the principle of molecular absorption of UV- visible radiation. A Xenon flash lamp serves as the radiation source and the selected wavelength is directed through the sample placed in a quartz cuvette. The absorbed and transmitted light is detected by a high-sensitivity detector and absorbance is calculated based on the Beer-Lambert Law. The instrument provides rapid, accurate measurements of absorbance over the UV–Visible range, enabling analysis of electronic transitions and solution-phase interactions. The spectra were obtained at room temperature within the range of 200–350 nm wavelength [17].

Results and discussion

Viscometric study

Understanding the transfer of energy, mass and momentum across different phases of matter forms the foundation of transport phenomena, a key concept in modern physical chemistry and molecular interaction studies. Properties such as thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, diffusion and viscosity serve as important indicators of a fluid’s internal resistance to flow. Among these, viscosity offers valuable insights into the nature of intermolecular forces and the degree of solute–solvent interactions within a system.

In this study, the viscosity behaviour of LAA in aqueous CAF solutions was examined across varying temperatures (293.15 K-313.15 K) to explore molecular interactions and structural associations. Thermodynamics of viscous flow

Rather than viewing viscosity merely as resistance to flow, it can be understood as a thermodynamically governed journey that solute particles undertake while moving through a liquid medium. As viscous flow is strongly influenced by temperature, the process is dictated by fundamental thermodynamic parameters- changes in free energy, enthalpy and entropy. These factors collectively determine the spontaneity and feasibility of molecular motion. For any viscous flow to occur, the particles must overcome an energy barrier by passing through a transition state, making the phenomenon both dynamic and deeply rooted in molecular energies.

The activation parameters for both solvent () and solute () are evaluated through relationships involving viscosity (), limiting molar volumes of solvent and solution () and universal constants () by using the recommended equation studied by Feakings et al. [24–26].

These parameters, and , reflect the energy cost required for molecules to shift from their ground state to an excited state. Essentially, they capture differences in Gibbs free energy for both solvent and solute as they approach the viscous transition state. Notably, larger values of solute activation free energy compared to that of the solvent () indicates that strong solute–solvent interactions restrict their movement through the fluid, making the overall flow less favorable.

Interpreted through a molecular lens, the positive activation energy values ( and ), reported in Table 4 highlight that solute and solvent molecules are tightly bound in the ground state, creating strong interactions that hinder their movement. This restricted mobility makes the initiation of flow more demanding. In the systems studied by Feakings et al. exceeds typically exhibit ion–solvent interactions. Such enhanced interactions suggest that during flow, the solute must overcome a more rigid structural environment, causing rupture and distortion within the solvent network as the transition state forms [26, 27].

The positive value of in aqueous LAA indicates that the transition state becomes more disordered, suggesting increased molecular freedom, Consequently, the viscous-flow mechanism would become favourable, as less structural organization is required for molecules to move fast, often accompanied by an exothermic (negative values) character of flow.

Negative entropy change for the (LAA + CAF + water) systems indicates that in the transition state the molecules arrange themselves in a more organized manner rather than remaining randomly distributed in the ground state. Such a decrease in randomness often occurs when strong intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding, ion–dipole, or dipole–dipole interactions are formed. A negative entropy change may also suggest enhanced structuring of water molecules around the solute, leading to the formation of an ordered solvation or hydration shell (structure forming character of LAA in the investigated system) Thus, negative entropy change signifies greater molecular association, restricted molecular motion, and increased structural organization in the system. Positive values for the mixture signify an endothermic viscous-flow mechanism. It implies that additional energy must be absorbed to disrupt existing solute–solvent interactions and to facilitate molecular movement.

UV spectroscopic study

UV–Visible spectroscopy serves as an important technique for examining molecular interactions through variations in electronic transitions. Each molecule possesses characteristic absorption maxima (), which act as a spectral signature. When molecular interactions occur, forces such as ion–dipole, ion–induced dipole and dipole–dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonding may develop, leading to shifts in . In this study, UV–Visible spectroscopic measurements were carried out to assess the interactions in LAA, CAF and their aqueous mixtures, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

UV- Visible Spectra of (a) 0.02 mol kg−1 aqueous LAA (b) 0.02 mol kg−1 LAA + 0.075 mol kg−1 aqueous CAF (c) 0.075 mol kg−1 aqueous CAF

CAF exhibits distinct absorption bands at 222 nm and 301 nm, corresponding to π–π* transitions of the carbonyl (C = O) group and n–π* transitions of the nitrogen atom in the five-membered ring, respectively [28]. LAA similarly shows absorption bands at 223 nm, attributed to π–π* transitions of the carbonyl group in the ring system, and at 288 nm, arising from n–π* transitions of the hydroxyl (–OH) group in the side chain [29]. Upon the addition of CAF to aqueous LAA, the absorption band at 223 nm displays a hypsochromic shift to 220 nm accompanied by a hyperchromic effect, suggesting enhanced intermolecular interactions that increase the energy gap between the ground and excited states. In contrast, the band at 288 nm undergoes a bathochromic shift to 302 nm with a hypochromic effect, indicating dipole–dipole interactions between LAA and CAF, which result in absorption at longer wavelengths [16].
