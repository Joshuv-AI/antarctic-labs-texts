A rare sequela of open cholecystectomy: gossypiboma presenting as a subhepatic mass with duodenal sinus – a case report

Introduction

Gossypiboma is derived from the Latin word gossypium, meaning “cotton,” and the Swahili word boma, meaning “place of concealment”[1]. It refers to a retained cotton-based surgical material inadvertently left inside the patient’s body after an operation, leading to a foreign-body granulomatous reaction[2]. Gossypiboma is also known by several other terms, including textiloma, cottonoid, cottonballoma, and gauzeoma[1]. The condition was first described by Wilson, a British surgeon, in 1884 following surgery for a fibroid tumor[3].

The actual incidence of gossypiboma remains uncertain, as many cases go unreported because of medicolegal concerns and asymptomatic presentations. The reported incidence ranges from 1:100 to 1:5000 for all surgeries and from 1:100 to 1:1500 for intra-abdominal procedures[4]. Herein, we report a case of gossypiboma presenting as a subhepatic mass with a pus-discharging duodenal sinus 4 months after open cholecystectomy, which was successfully managed with open surgical intervention. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for gossypiboma in postoperative patients presenting with abdominal pain, as early diagnosis and timely surgical management can help reduce associated morbidity and complications.

This work has been reported in line with the updated SCARE guidelines (2025)[5].

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man presented with dull, aching right upper abdominal pain for 2 months. There was no history of fever, abdominal distension, or vomiting. He had undergone an emergency open cholecystectomy for symptomatic cholelithiasis 4 months earlier. Physical examination revealed a body mass index of 22.5 kg/m2 with stable vital signs. The patient was icteric and had a well-healed 10-cm surgical scar over the right subcostal region (as shown in the image provided as a Supplemental Digital Content File, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A154). Mild tenderness was present in the right hypochondrium, with no evidence of rebound tenderness, guarding, or rigidity.

Laboratory investigations showed features of cholestasis, with a total bilirubin of 3 mg/dL (direct, 1.8 mg/dL), ALP 324 IU/L, ALT 291 IU/L, and AST 251 IU/L. Ultrasonography of the abdomen showed a dilated proximal common bile duct (8 mm) with distal choledocholithiasis (4 mm). On further evaluation, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed an intermediate- to high-signal-intensity subhepatic lesion measuring 9 cm × 7 cm × 7 cm with internal air foci, suggestive of a gossypiboma (Fig. 1), and distal choledocholithiasis measuring 4 mm (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis further confirmed a well-defined spongiform mass with curvilinear hyperdensities in the gallbladder fossa (Fig. 1). Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy demonstrated a ulcer with a whitish base measuring 12 mm × 12 mm in the first part of the duodenum, featuring a luminal opening that drained pus containing air bubbles (Fig. 2). The patient was managed conservatively with adequate intravenous fluid resuscitation, appropriate antibiotic therapy (inj. ceftriaxone), and analgesics for symptomatic relief. With this supportive treatment, the patient showed gradual clinical improvement, accompanied by resolution of cholestasis and improvement in the associated biochemical parameters. Subsequently, an exploratory laparotomy was performed, which revealed two tetras tied together that were densely adherent to the liver, omentum, and the first part of the duodenum, forming a localized inflammatory mass (Fig. 3). Approximately 30 mL of pus was identified within the cavity surrounding the retained surgical material. Further exploration demonstrated a perforation measuring 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm in the first part of the duodenum communicating with the sinus tract (Fig. 3). Careful adhesiolysis was performed, and the retained tetras were removed completely. The duodenal perforation was repaired using a modified Graham’s omental patch technique, followed by thorough peritoneal lavage and placement of an abdominal drain. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient gradually improved clinically, tolerated oral feeding well, and was discharged in stable condition. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrent infection or fistula formation. The sequence of key clinical events is summarized in Table 1 below. Discussion

Gossypiboma occurs most commonly in the abdomen (56%), followed by the pelvis (18%) and thorax (11%)[6]. Within the abdomen and pelvis, it is most commonly reported after cholecystectomy, followed by cesarean section, hysterectomy, and laparotomy. The subhepatic region is the most commonly involved site following cholecystectomy[7]. Gossypiboma has been reported more frequently in females than in males, likely due to the greater number of gynecologic and obstetric procedures performed. Its occurrence is reported to be nine times more common in emergency surgeries than in elective procedures and four times more common when there is an unexpected intraoperative change in the surgical plan[6]. Other risk factors associated with gossypiboma include high body mass index, rushed sponge counts, prolonged procedures, increased intraoperative bleeding, surgical inexperience, involvement of multiple surgical teams, and changes in the operating-room and staff during the procedure[1,8,9].

Retained surgical sponges provoke a foreign-body reaction that typically follows two patterns: an acute exudative inflammatory response, which may result in sepsis or abscess formation, and a chronic aseptic fibrotic response characterized by encapsulation and granuloma formation. The latter can lead to mass effect, adhesions, bowel erosion, and fistula formation[2].

The clinical manifestations of intra-abdominal gossypibomas vary depending on their location and the timing of presentation[10]. Abdominal pain (42%), palpable abdominal mass (27%), and fever (12%) are the most common presenting complaints[11]. The time to diagnosis of a gossypiboma can range from 1 day to 40 years, depending on the host tissue reaction[1]. Acute cases of gossypiboma typically present during the early postoperative period with intra-abdominal abscess formation, sepsis, or features of intestinal obstruction. Intestinal obstruction usually occurs when the retained gauze undergoes transmural migration into the bowel lumen without leaving an apparent bowel wall defect, secondary to pressure necrosis and a chronic inflammatory reaction[1]. This process may further lead to bowel perforation, adhesions, or fistula formation. In contrast, chronic cases may remain clinically silent for months to years after surgery and often present as encapsulated masses, internal or external fistulas, chronic abdominal pain, palpable abdominal swelling, or nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and weight loss[12].

Diagnosis of gossypiboma requires a high index of clinical suspicion, confirmed by appropriate imaging. Radiopaque sponges may be identified on plain X-rays. On ultrasound, gossypibomas typically appear as well-defined masses with wavy internal echoes, a surrounding hypoechoic rim, and strong posterior acoustic shadowing. However, CECT is considered the gold standard, offering the highest sensitivity and accuracy. Characteristic CECT features include a heterogeneous low-density mass with a high-density peripheral wall that enhances with contrast, often displaying a spongiform appearance with internal air bubbles[2]. CECT plays a central role in differentiating subhepatic gossypiboma from postoperative hematoma, bilioma, subhepatic abscess, gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), and other malignancies. When gossypiboma erodes into the intestinal wall, it may appear elongated or stretched due to bowel peristalsis and dragging forces, a radiological finding referred to as the “stretched feces sign.” Differentiating gossypiboma from a soft tissue tumor on CECT imaging can be challenging; in such cases, MRI may provide additional diagnostic value (as demonstrated in our case)[12].

Definitive management involves surgical removal through endoscopic, laparoscopic, or open approaches, thereby reducing complications. The morbidity and mortality associated with late diagnosis are 50% and 7%, respectively[8]. The laparoscopic approach offers the benefits of reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, smaller incisions, lower infection rates, and a reduced risk of hemorrhage compared to open surgery. Endoscopic removal can be performed if the gossypiboma is entirely intraluminal.

Prevention is better than cure, and it involves basic precautions, such as educating operating theater staff, strict adherence to the World Health Organization (WHO) Surgical Safety Checklist, use of radiopaque sponges, and meticulous perioperative sponge counts. Recently, newer technologies have been introduced to help prevent retained surgical sponges. These include electronic tagging systems, barcode-labeled surgical swabs, and radiofrequency identification (RFID) systems. In barcode systems, each sponge is scanned during the final count before wound closure. RFID systems use tagged sponges that can be detected by passing a special scanner over the operative field. These methods have shown high accuracy and can help reduce the risk of gossypiboma[13].

Conclusion

Gossypiboma should be suspected in patients with prior abdominal surgery who present with unexplained sepsis, obstruction, an abdominal mass, or persistent pain. A high index of clinical suspicion, followed by prompt surgical intervention, is crucial for early diagnosis and prevention of complications. This case highlights the importance of strict adherence to surgical safety checklist protocols, particularly meticulous sponge counting during surgery.

Limitations

This case report has several limitations. First, as a single case, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with gossypiboma. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 3 months, which may not be sufficient to assess long-term complications or recurrence. Third, the report lacks a comparison with alternative management strategies, and therefore, no conclusions regarding the superiority of the treatment approach can be drawn.
