MDP Planning as Policy Inference
David Tolpin

Abstract
We cast episodic Markov decision process (MDP) planning as Bayesian inference over policies . A policy is treated as the latent variable and is assigned an unnormalized probability of optimality that is monotone in its expected return, yielding a posterior distribution whose modes coincide with return-maximizing solutions while posterior dispersion represents uncertainty over optimal behavior. To approximate this posterior in discrete domains, we adapt variational sequential Monte Carlo (VSMC) to inference over deterministic policies under stochastic dynamics, introducing a sweep that enforces policy consistency across revisited states and couples transition randomness across particles to avoid confounding from simulator noise. Acting is performed by posterior predictive sampling, which induces a stochastic control policy through a Thompson-sampling interpretation rather than entropy regularization. Across grid worlds, Blackjack, Triangle Tireworld, and Academic Advising, we analyze the structure of inferred policy distributions and compare the resulting behavior to discrete Soft Actor-Critic, highlighting qualitative and statistical differences that arise from policy-level uncertainty.



1
Introduction

Casting Markov decision process (MDP) planning as Bayesian policy
inference offers a two-fold benefit.
First, it enables the use of general-purpose Bayesian inference
algorithms for planning, potentially obviating the need for
custom-designed and domain-specific solution methods.
Second, uncertainty about the optimal policy becomes explicit and is
represented as part of the inferred posterior, rather than being
implicitly absorbed into approximation artifacts or heuristic
regularization.

Despite these advantages, formulating MDP planning as Bayesian
inference presents fundamental challenges arising from the special
structure of the planning problem.
Most Bayesian statistical models assume a clear separation between
latent and observable random variables, and treat uncertainty as
purely epistemic—each random variable has a fixed but unknown value.
In contrast, MDP planning involves intrinsic aleatoric uncertainty due
to stochastic environment dynamics and future action realizations.
Moreover, identifying which components of the MDP—states, actions,
trajectories, or policies—should be treated as latent variables, and
which (if any) are observed, is not straightforward.

Several Bayesian formulations of planning and control have been
proposed, including approaches based on control-as-inference,
maximum-entropy reinforcement learning, and active inference

(
Dayan and Hinton 1997
;
Toussaint and Storkey 2006
;
Ziebart 2010
;
Levine 2018
)
.
While these approaches recast planning as probabilistic inference, they
do so by altering the optimization objective to fit standard Bayesian
modeling assumptions. In particular, the expected return is
augmented or replaced by entropy-regularized or evidence-based
objectives, and stochasticity of the resulting policies is introduced
as an intrinsic modeling preference rather than as a consequence of
explicit uncertainty. As a result, the inferred policies are optimal
with respect to surrogate objectives, and uncertainty over policies is
not directly interpretable as posterior uncertainty about the solution
of the original MDP planning problem.

In this work, we propose a formulation of MDP planning as Bayesian
policy inference that preserves the standard MDP objective. The
resulting posterior concentrates on policies that maximize expected
return, while simultaneously providing a principled
representation of uncertainty over policies. Unlike prior
control-as-inference approaches, our formulation does not rely on
entropy regularization as a surrogate objective, but instead derives
policy stochasticity from uncertainty about the agent’s preferences.

Building on this formulation, we adapt variational sequential
Monte Carlo (VSMC)
(
Naesseth et al. 2018
;
Maddison et al. 2017
)
to perform robust
approximate inference over policies in discrete MDPs with
stochastic dynamics. Furthermore, we interpret the reward scale
as a parameter controlling the agent’s uncertainty about its own
preferences, leading naturally to the notion of an

optimal stochastic policy under preference uncertainty
.
This interpretation provides a Bayesian justification for
stochastic optimal policies without departing from the
expected-reward criterion.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

A sound formulation of MDP planning as Bayesian policy inference
that preserves the classical expected-reward optimality criterion.

•

An adaptation of variational sequential Monte Carlo for Bayesian
policy inference in discrete MDPs with stochastic transitions.

•

An empirical study of reward scale as a measure of uncertainty
over agent preferences, and its effect on the induced optimal
stochastic policy.

2.2
Variational Sequential Monte Carlo

Variational sequential Monte Carlo
(
Naesseth et al. 2018
)
is an algorithm for
variational inference in models amendable to inference with sequential
Monte Carlo (SMC)
(

)

In standard SMC, the posterior distribution of latent variables is
approximated by
N

proposal distribution. A byproduct of SMC estimation of the posterior
is an estimate of the marginal likelihood of observations.
Variational SMC (VSMC) views the parametrized proposal distribution as
a variational family and maximizes a ‘surrogate ELBO’—the log
marginal likelihood estimate computed by SMC.

Formally, let

p(x_{1:H},y_{1:H})=p(x_{1})\prod_{t=1}^{H-1}p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t})\prod_{t=1}^{H}p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})

(2)

distribution, where
x_{1:H}
are the latent variables,
y_{1:H}
are
the observations, and
H
is the number of steps through the state
space (the horizon). SMC approximates the
evidence
, or
marginal likelihood,
Z=p(y_{1:H})
by a set of
N

particles

and their weights

\hat{Z}=\prod_{t=1}^{H}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{t,i},

(3)

where the weights are computed based on conditionals

p(x_{t+1}\mid p_{s})
,
p(y_{s}\mid x_{s})
and proposal
q(x_{t+1}\mid|x_{s},\lambda)
.

\hat{Z}
is an unbiased estimate of

is a biased

The proposal that minimizes the bias is the conditional distribution

p(x_{t+1}|x_{s},y_{1:H})

3
Probabilistic Model

A widespread setting for posterior Bayesian inference is a
graphical model involving a latent variable
x
, an observable
variable
y
, and a joint distribution
p(x,y)
. By Bayes’
rule,

p(x\mid y)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(y)},

(4)

and upon observing
y=y^{*}
, one obtains an unnormalized posterior over
x
,

p(x\mid y=y^{*})\propto p(x,y^{*}).

(5)

Posterior inference methods then aim to characterize this
distribution, exactly or approximately, by producing samples or
expectations consistent with the corresponding normalized
distribution. Although most practical algorithms do not evaluate the
normalizing constant
Z=p(y^{*})
explicitly, they can be viewed as
implicitly solving the normalization problem, in the sense that
their outputs behave as if such a normalization were available.

However, the presence of an explicit observable variable
y
is
not essential. Rather,
y
serves as a convenient way to parameterize
an unnormalized target distribution. From a more general perspective,
posterior inference only requires access to an unnormalized
probability (more precisely, a finite measure)
\tilde{p}(x)
. The objective is then to work with the corresponding normalized
distribution

p(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\tilde{p}(x),\quad Z=\!\!\int\limits_{\supp(x)}\!\!\tilde{p}(x)dx,

(6)

either by explicitly estimating
Z
or, more commonly, by implicitly
accounting for it through sampling or optimization procedures.

This formulation naturally accommodates cases in which
\tilde{p}(x)
—or, more typically,
\log\tilde{p}(x)
—is intractable and
available only through unbiased stochastic estimators. A range of
inference methods has been developed for such settings, including
pseudo-marginal Metropolis–Hastings
(
Andrieu and Roberts 2009
)
, stochastic variational
inference
(
Hoffman et al. 2013
)
,
and stochastic gradient Markov chain Monte Carlo
(
Nemeth and Fearnhead 2020
)
.
We cast policy inference within this general framework.

Since we are interested in inferring a policy, the
policy

\pi

is the latent random variable. The objective of MDP planning is to identify
policies that maximize expected return. To align the
probabilistic model with this objective, we assign to each policy an
unnormalized
probability of optimality
that is monotone in its
expected return.

Specifically, we define the unnormalized log probability of a policy
as the
expected return
obtained by the agent following
the policy in an episode, with expectation over trajectories

\tau_{\pi}
distributed according to the dynamics induced by policy

\pi
:

\log\tilde{p}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{\pi}}\sum_{t=1}^{H-1}R\left(s_{t},a^{(\pi)}_{t},s_{t+1})\right),

(7)

where
a^{(\pi)}_{t}=\pi(s_{t})
and
s_{t+1}=T\left(s_{t},a_{t}^{(\pi)}\right)
for all
t\in 1\ldots H
.
This bears similarity to a Boltzmann–Gibbs distribution over
trajectories, as in the maximum-entropy and control-as-inference
literature
(
Ziebart et al. 2008
;
Todorov 2006
;
Levine 2018
)
, however in our framework the
policies rather than the trajectories are Boltzmann-Gibbs distributed.

Note that neither actions nor states are treated as Bayesian random
variables for which a posterior is sought. Although the policy (if
stochastic policies are considered) induces a stochastic action
selection rule and the environment induces stochastic state
transitions, these are generative rather than inferential sources of
randomness: actions and state transitions are sampled forward from
their respective distributions rather than conditioned for the purpose
of posterior inference. The randomness they induce propagates into
the estimation of the unnormalized log probability of the policy, so
that
\log\tilde{p}(\pi)
is available only through noisy Monte Carlo
evaluations—by computing the return of a single episode:

\log\widehat{\tilde{p}}(\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{H-1}R\left(s_{t},a^{(\pi)}_{t},s_{t+1}\right).

(8)

Stochastic estimate (
8
) lays the basis for posterior
inference of the policy distribution.

4
Inference

We begin by specifying the object of inference. In this work, we
perform posterior inference over the space of
deterministic
policies
, equipped with a uniform prior
1
1

1

The proposed
formulation also admits posterior inference over stochastic policies.
We restrict attention to deterministic policies in this work to avoid
an additional layer of action-level randomness and to keep the focus
on policy-level uncertainty.
. While one may place a posterior over
stochastic policies, doing so introduces an additional and largely
redundant level of randomness. A posterior over stochastic policies
combines epistemic uncertainty over policies with intrinsic
action-level stochasticity, leading to mixtures of mixtures whose
behavioral interpretation is unclear and whose posterior geometry is
highly non-identifiable. In contrast, a posterior over deterministic
policies represents uncertainty solely at the level of coherent
behaviors, admits a clear decision semantics, and ensures that
posterior concentration corresponds directly to behavioral certainty.
Action stochasticity then arises naturally through marginalization
over deterministic policies (Section
4.2.3
), rather than
being embedded inside each policy.

4.1
Algorithm

A natural baseline for posterior inference over the policy
distribution specified by (
8
) is structured
variational approximation
(
Hoffman et al. 2013
)
. However, single-trajectory
variational inference tends to produce underdispersed approximations,
collapse onto a small subset of trajectory modes, and requires
problem-specific heuristics to remain stable under multimodal dynamics
or sparse rewards. The sequential structure
of (
8
) encourages application of variational
SMC, which multi-particle objective incorporates information from
diverse trajectory proposals, yielding a tighter and more robust
approximation to the policy posterior. These advantages, however,
require adaptation of VSMC to the policy inference setting, which is
the subject of this section.

VSMC optimizes a stochastic lower bound on the log marginal
likelihood, where the stochasticity arises from Monte Carlo sampling
of particle trajectories from the variational proposal. In the policy
inference setting considered here, the objective is additionally noisy
because the unnormalized log density of a policy is intractable and
can only be evaluated through an unbiased Monte Carlo
estimator (
8
). This second source of
randomness is independent of the variational proposal and therefore
does not alter the variational interpretation of the VSMC objective.
It can be treated as exogenous estimator noise on the target density,
analogous to the pseudo-marginal setting
(
Andrieu and Roberts 2009
)
, and incorporated
directly into the optimization without modification of the underlying
algorithm.

We constrain our setting to countable state spaces and finite
action spaces. We require a countable state space to be able to
identify previously visited states and to count the number of
visits. This assumption is for ease of caching and revisitation
bookkeeping; the probabilistic model itself does not depend on
discreteness. We require a finite action space to be able to
identify previously occurred state–action transitions and to
count the number of occurrences, as well as to express policies
by means of discrete distributions. In particular, for a
deterministic policy space, a policy
\pi
in state
s
is an
action assignment, drawn from a categorical distribution over
actions.

\pi(s)\equiv a|s\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{p}(s))\\

(9)

In a factored state space, where each state is represented by a
fixed-dimensional vector of state variables, the distribution of
policies can be parameterized by a neural network with the vector
representation of the state as the input and the vector of action
probabilities
\boldsymbol{p}
as the output. We adopt this parametrization
in our case studies.

SMC sweep

Two adjustments to vanilla SMC sweep are required:

1.

In order for particle policies to be deterministic, an action
must be drawn from the proposal distribution on the first visit to each
visited state only. If a trajectory revisits a state, both the prior
and the proposal probability of the action assigned on the first visit
are 1 (and of any other action are 0).

2.

Because the stochastic estimate
\log\hat{Z}
is induced by the
entire particle system rather than by individual particles,
stochasticity arising from environment dynamics must be coupled across
particles. In particular, random state transitions must be shared,
otherwise relative particle weights would be confounded by both
differences in their policies and independent realizations of the
transition noise. Concretely, whenever two particles visit the same
state
s
and select the same action
a
for the same
k
th time,
they are required to transition to the same successor state
s^{\prime}
.
This can be implemented by lazily sampling a shared transition tensor

\hat{T}
, where
\hat{T}_{s,a}^{k}\sim T(\cdot\mid s,a)
is sampled once
and reused by all particles within a sweep. The environment dynamics

T
are then replaced, during inference, by this shared random
realization
\hat{T}

2
2

2

This coupling of transition randomness
is related to the classical
common random numbers
variance
reduction technique
(
Mohamed et al. 2020
)
, however here it is applied for
correctness rather than for variance reduction.
.

Optimization objective

The original VSMC formulation
assumes reparameterizable SMC proposal distributions and omits score
function terms associated with the non-differentiable

resampling
operation due to their high variance and low
signal. In finite‐action MDPs, however, a categorical distribution
over actions cannot be reparameterized. Consequently, while the
resampling-induced score terms may still be dropped, the
score–function contribution arising from
sampling actions from
the proposal
must be retained in order to obtain meaningful
gradients. This yields the surrogate objective

\mathcal{L}=\log\hat{Z}+\overline{\log\hat{Z}}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log q(a_{t,i}\mid s_{t,i})

(10)

where the overline denotes a stop-gradient operation. The high
variance of this REINFORCE‐style term can be substantially reduced by
exploiting the temporal dependency structure of
trajectories
(
Schulman et al. 2015
)
. Defining

\log\hat{Z}_{t}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{H}\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{t^{\prime},i}\right),

(11)

an equivalent, temporally stratified objective is obtained:

\mathcal{L}=\log\hat{Z}+\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left(\overline{\log\hat{Z}_{t}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log q(a_{t,i}\mid s_{t,i})\right),

(12)

which replaces a single global learning signal with step-specific
signals, thus improving estimator stability.

In classical MDP planning, uncertainty over policies is typically
discarded by selecting a single optimal policy—namely, the policy that
maximizes expected return. From a Bayesian perspective,
this corresponds to selecting a maximum
a posteriori
(MAP)
policy under the inferred posterior.

In practice, MAP estimation is often approximated by annealing
techniques that encourage posterior concentration around a dominant
mode.

4.2.2
Annealing-Based MAP Selection

The variational objective underlying policy inference combines expected
return with a regularization term measuring the discrepancy between the
posterior and the prior. Increasing the relative weight of the return
term therefore leads to a more concentrated posterior and, in the
limit, to MAP-like behavior.

One common approach is to anneal the reward scale during optimization.
However, this strategy is undesirable for two reasons:

•

Rewards are part of the domain specification and encode the
agent’s preferences; modifying their scale alters the semantics of the
problem.

•

Increasing reward scale requires compensatory changes to
optimization hyperparameters, introducing an artificial coupling
between modeling assumptions and optimization dynamics.

An alternative is to anneal the regularization term by downscaling the
prior–posterior discrepancy. With this modification,
line
34
of Algorithm
1
becomes

w^{(i)}\leftarrow\text{{Reward}}(s,a,s^{\prime})+T\cdot(\log p_{a}-\log q_{a}),

(15)

where the temperature
T
decreases from
1
to
0
.
This procedure is formally equivalent to increasing the relative weight
of rewards and admits an interpretation as progressively collapsing
the prior onto the inferred posterior, reminiscent of variational
policy search methods
(
van de Meent et al. 2016
)
.

While effective for identifying a single high-probability policy,
annealing ultimately discards information encoded in the absolute
scale of rewards.

4.2.3
Stochastic Policy Selection via Posterior Predictive Sampling

Rather than collapsing the posterior, we may instead interpret it as a
representation of uncertainty over policies. In this view, rewards
encode the agent’s preferences, and differences in reward magnitude
reflect the degree of certainty associated with those preferences.

Under this interpretation, each policy drawn from the posterior may be
viewed as optimal for a particular realization of the agent’s
preferences, with posterior mass reflecting plausibility. Action
selection can then be performed by sampling from the posterior
predictive distribution, corresponding to recurrent Thompson sampling:
at each decision point, a policy is sampled from the posterior and an
action is drawn from that policy.

For a finite action space, this is equivalent to sampling an action

5
Related Work

This work connects to two broad and partially overlapping lines of research:

(i)

formulations of control and planning as probabilistic inference, and

(ii)

entropy-regularized reinforcement learning and policy search methods.

While both perspectives are well established, the present work differs
from prior approaches in the
probabilistic object being
inferred
—a posterior over policies—and in the resulting
implications for stochasticity, entropy regularization, and inference
algorithms.

5.1
Control and Planning as Inference

The idea of casting decision making and control as probabilistic
inference has a long history. Early formulations include
Attias 2003
,
who introduced planning as inference in graphical models, and later
developments by
Botvinick and Toussaint 2012
;
Toussaint and Storkey 2006
, and related work that formalized
optimal control problems as inference in structured probabilistic
models. In these approaches, optimality is typically encoded via
auxiliary variables or likelihood terms that bias trajectories toward
high return.

A closely related and influential line of work is the
active
inference
framework, which models action selection as approximate
Bayesian inference under a generative model that includes preferences
over outcomes. While differing in interpretation and objectives, these
methods similarly introduce probabilistic structure to express control
objectives.

More recently,
control-as-inference
formulations popularized
in the reinforcement learning community—most notably in the work of
Levine and colleagues—have shown that entropy-regularized RL
objectives can be derived as variational inference problems
(e.g.,
Levine 2018
). These formulations typically introduce either
fictitious observations or optimality variables, such that maximizing
expected return corresponds to maximizing the likelihood of optimality
under a suitably defined model.

The present work adopts the control-as-inference viewpoint but differs
in a fundamental modeling choice. Rather than introducing auxiliary
observations or trajectory-level optimality variables, we treat the

policy itself
as the latent random variable and interpret the expected
return of a policy as its unnormalized log probability. This yields a
posterior distribution over policies directly, without augmenting the
model with additional observation channels. As a result, the Boltzmann
distribution over policies arises naturally from the probabilistic
interpretation, rather than being imposed through likelihood design or
entropy penalties.

5.2
Entropy-Regularized Reinforcement Learning and Policy Search

A second closely related body of work concerns entropy-regularized
reinforcement learning and stochastic policy optimization, including
policy gradient methods
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
, soft Q-learning
(
Haarnoja et al. 2017
)
,
and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
;
Christodoulou 2019
)
. These methods optimize
objectives of the form

\mathbb{E}[R(\pi)]+\alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi),

(16)

where the entropy term encourages exploration and stabilizes learning.

Several works have established equivalences between these approaches.
In particular,
Schulman et al. 2017
showed that policy gradients
and soft Q-learning optimize the same underlying objective under
appropriate parameterizations. From the control-as-inference
perspective, entropy regularization is often interpreted as arising
from a variational bound, with the temperature parameter controlling
the trade-off between return and entropy.

While mathematically related, the interpretation in the present work
differs in three key respects. First, entropy regularization is not
introduced as a heuristic or tuning mechanism, but as a direct
consequence of placing a prior over policies. In particular, a uniform
prior leads to entropy-regularized objectives, while alternative
priors yield different regularization structures.

Second, stochasticity in prior approaches is typically a property of
the learned policy itself. In contrast, stochasticity here reflects

posterior uncertainty over deterministic policies
. Actions are
sampled by first sampling a policy from the posterior and then
executing it, rather than by directly sampling actions from a
stochastic policy parameterization. This distinction aligns the method
with Thompson sampling, though in a structured, state-conditional form
appropriate for sequential decision making.

Third, the inference formulation enables the use of
variational
sequential Monte Carlo (VSMC)

(
Naesseth et al. 2018
)
as a natural inference algorithm.
Unlike single-trajectory variational inference commonly used in RL,
VSMC maintains and resamples a population of policy hypotheses,
allowing posterior mass to concentrate on high-reward regions of
policy space while preserving uncertainty. As noted in prior work on
variational SMC, several existing methods emerge as limiting cases:
single-particle VSMC reduces to structured variational inference,
while multi-particle methods without resampling correspond to
importance-weighted objectives. Within the present framework,
previously introduced entropy-regularized algorithms can be
recovered as special or degenerate cases of the proposed inference
procedure.

Taken together, these connections position the proposed method
not just as an alternative optimizer for existing RL objectives,
but as a unifying probabilistic framework in which entropy
regularization, exploration, and policy uncertainty arise from
explicit Bayesian modeling choices.

6
Experiments

We evaluate the proposed policy inference framework across a
range of domains designed to expose different structural aspects
of decision making under uncertainty, and compare it to
entropy-regularized policy optimization. Throughout the
experiments we contrast inference over distributions of
deterministic policies (VSMC) with direct optimization of
entropy-regularized stochastic policies (SAC). The experiments
are designed to examine (i) qualitative structure of inferred
policy distributions, (ii) the role of multi-particle inference,
(iii) differences between deterministic-policy inference and
entropy-regularized optimization, and (iv) behavior under
increasing stochasticity and planning complexity.

We begin by exploring the proposed policy inference framework
and the algorithm on a grid world domain. The ease of static
visualisation and apparent simplicity of grid worlds facilitates
introspection into the properties of inferred policy
distributions and the induced policies. Then, we use the grid
world domain and three other domains from the
literature—Academic Advising, Blackjack, and Triangle
Tireworld—to compare policy VSMC to discrete Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC)
Haarnoja et al. 2018
;
Christodoulou 2019
, the state-of-the-art algorithm for
learning of entropy-regualized stochastic policies, outlining
differences in the resulting policies and suitability to
particular MDP types.

The selected domains span complementary structural properties of
Markov decision processes that are particularly relevant to policy
inference. Grid worlds provide fully observable environments with
transparent geometry, allowing direct visual inspection of policy
distributions. Blackjack represents a stochastic decision problem
with small state space but strong outcome randomness and well-known
optimal policies, enabling calibrated comparison to ground truth.
Triangle Tireworld introduces irreversible stochastic failures and
risk-sensitive planning, where early decisions affect downstream
recoverability. Academic Advising represents a large combinatorial
decision space with long horizons and delayed consequences.
Together, these domains allow us to examine how policy inference
behaves under multimodality, outcome stochasticity, risk management,
and combinatorial planning complexity.

Throughout the experiments, we run VSMC with 10 particles for

50\,000
iterations (SMC sweeps), adjusting the initial
learning rate between
10^{-5}
and
3\cdot 10^{-4}
for each
particular domain. We apply cosine learning rate decay to
0.1

of the initial rate. We adapt SAC implementation from
CleanRL
(
Huang et al. 2022
)
for discrete domains through replacing
the critic networks by multi-layer perceptrons with two hidden
layers. We train SAC for
1\,000\,000
time steps. In both VSMC
and SAC, the width of the hidden layers in all networks is set
to 64. Increasing the width of the hidden layers to either 128
or 256 slows down training and policy execution but does not
result in a noticeable change in performance.

Each algorithm–domain pair is evaluated over
25
independent
training runs. Policies obtained from each run are evaluated
using
10\,000
trajectories, allowing estimation of both
expected performance and variability across runs. The
temperature for VSMC and the entropy weight for SAC are kept at

1
except in the studies where the influence of the temperature
or the entropy weight is explored explicitly.

Blackjack

Blackjack is a card game where the goal is to beat
the dealer by obtaining cards that sum to closer to 21 (without going over 21)
than the dealers cards. Blackjack provides a stochastic control
problem with a compact state space and a known optimal policy,
making it possible to compare inferred policies against a
ground-truth solution while examining the effect of entropy
regularization in a domain where randomness arises primarily
from the environment rather than exploration.

The game starts with the dealer having one face up and
one face down card, while the player has two face up cards. All cards are drawn
from an infinite deck (i.e. with replacement). The card values are:

•

Face cards (Jack, Queen, King) have a point value of 10.

•

Aces can either count as 11 (called a ‘usable ace’) or 1.

•

Numerical cards (2–10) have a value equal to their number.

The player has the sum of cards held. The player can request
additional cards (hit) until they decide to stop (stick) or exceed 21 (bust,
immediate loss). After the player sticks, the dealer reveals their facedown
card, and draws cards until their sum is 17 or greater. If the dealer goes
bust, the player wins. If neither the player nor the dealer busts, the outcome
(win, lose, draw) is decided by whose sum is closer to 21.

7
Discussion

This work argues that classical MDP planning can be viewed as

posterior inference over policies
without altering the underlying
optimality criterion. The key modeling move is to treat a policy as the
latent variable and to assign it an unnormalized probability of
optimality that is monotone in its
expected return

(Eq. (
7
)). This yields a well-defined posterior
distribution over policies whose modes coincide with return-maximizing
solutions, while posterior dispersion provides an explicit and
interpretable notion of uncertainty about optimal behavior.

Why a Bayesian posterior over policies?

A posterior over policies is a natural object for planning because it
separates
solution uncertainty
from
environment randomness
.
In conventional approximate planning and RL, uncertainty about what to
do is often absorbed into heuristic choices (e.g., exploration bonuses,
entropy coefficients, or approximation artifacts), and is therefore hard
to interpret. Here, uncertainty is part of the model: policies with
similar expected returns plausibly coexist in the posterior, whereas
policies with substantially lower return are exponentially suppressed.
This framing makes it possible to use general-purpose inference tools
and to reason about decision-making as
probabilistic selection
among coherent behaviors
rather than as direct optimization of a single
parametric stochastic policy.

Three roles of uncertainty, made explicit.

Because the posterior is defined over deterministic policies but induces
stochastic action selection at execution time, the framework
disentangles three distinct sources of uncertainty that are commonly
conflated:

•

Aleatoric uncertainty
due to stochastic environment
dynamics is handled by the forward simulator and appears as noise in the
Monte Carlo estimate of policy log-probability
(Eq. (
8
)). This randomness is not ‘inferred’; it is
sampled forward.

•

Epistemic uncertainty about optimal behavior
is represented
by posterior dispersion over deterministic policies. Importantly, the
degree of dispersion depends on reward scale: when returns are sharply
separated, the posterior concentrates; when they are comparable, it
remains diffuse.

•

Action stochasticity for execution
arises from
marginalizing over deterministic policies. Executing the posterior
predictive distribution (Section
4.2.3
) corresponds to a
structured variant of Thompson sampling: uncertainty is expressed as
randomization across plausible deterministic policies, rather than as an
entropy term inside a single policy parameterization.

This separation clarifies what stochasticity
means
: it reflects
uncertainty about which deterministic behavior is optimal under the
specified preferences, not an intrinsic desire to behave randomly.

Reward scale as preference uncertainty.

A practical implication of the Bayesian framing is that reward magnitude
acquires an interpretation beyond shaping optimization. In the model,
rewards determine the relative posterior mass of policies: scaling
rewards up increases posterior concentration and yields near-deterministic
behavior; scaling them down makes multiple behaviors comparably plausible
and yields a more diffuse mixed policy. This supports the view that
reward scale encodes how sharply the agent distinguishes among outcomes,
i.e., how
certain
it is about its preferences. Under this
interpretation, the mixed policy induced by posterior predictive
sampling is an
optimal stochastic policy under preference
uncertainty
: it randomizes only to the extent that the preferences do
not decisively separate candidate behaviors. This differs from typical
entropy-regularized objectives, where stochasticity is introduced
directly as a design choice and tuned via a separate coefficient.

Inference mechanics and why VSMC fits the structure.

Once planning is cast as inference, the remaining question is how to
approximate the posterior when the target density is available only
through a stochastic estimator. The sequential factorization of returns
along trajectories makes sequential Monte Carlo a natural fit, and VSMC
provides a principled variational objective that leverages multiple
particles and resampling to avoid the collapse and under-dispersion
typical of single-trajectory variational objectives. However, planning
introduces additional structure not present in standard state-space
models, requiring two key adaptations for correctness and stability:
(i) enforcing determinism of particle policies by sampling each state’s
action only on first visit, and (ii) coupling transition randomness
across particles within a sweep so that particle weights reflect policy
differences rather than independent realizations of environment noise.
The grid-world study isolates both effects: degenerating VSMC to VIS or
single-particle VSA produces either high-variance solutions or inferior
modes, while removing shared dynamics yields ‘optimistic’ behavior
consistent with inferring both policy and dynamics rather than policy
alone.

Relation to control-as-inference and entropy-regularized RL.

The proposed framework sits between control-as-inference and
entropy-regularized policy optimization, while differing in what is
treated as latent and how stochasticity is interpreted. In much of the
control-as-inference literature, action/state sequences (or optimality
variables attached to trajectories) are inferred, often producing
policies whose stochasticity is tied to the variational construction and
which can become optimistic when model uncertainty is implicitly
absorbed. In entropy-regularized RL, stochasticity is typically embedded
inside the learned policy and tuned as a single knob that conflates
exploration during learning with uncertainty about optimal behavior.
Here, environment stochasticity is treated as in policy-gradient
settings (sampled forward through the simulator), while epistemic
uncertainty is represented as posterior uncertainty over deterministic
policies. The resulting stochastic behavior is therefore not an entropy
bonus but a posterior predictive effect, and it persists (or disappears)
according to how decisively rewards separate policies.

What the experiments suggest.

Across domains, empirical results reinforce these conceptual distinctions.
In grid worlds, policy inference captures multimodality in solution
space and produces behavior that differs qualitatively from SAC along
boundaries, where entropy-regularized optimization can prefer
boundary-directed actions that increase entropy even when they undermine
goal reachability. In Blackjack, where outcome randomness dominates and
the optimal policy is known, VSMC and SAC with comparable regularization
produce different trade-offs; matching VSMC’s performance requires
substantially weaker entropy regularization in SAC, supporting the claim
that posterior uncertainty and entropy-driven stochasticity are not the
same object. In Triangle Tireworld, aggregate performance is similar but
termination behavior differs, suggesting that the induced objectives
shape how policies manage ‘getting stuck’ versus lingering under a
horizon cap. Finally, in Academic Advising, both methods exhibit high
variance and heavy-tailed return distributions, but VSMC tends to produce
heavier tails, consistent with maintaining explicit uncertainty over
long-horizon decision sequences rather than collapsing to a single
stochastic policy.

Scope choice and practical extensions.

We focus on discrete state spaces primarily to keep the mechanics of the
sweep explicit: exact revisit detection, explicit state–action memories,
and simple caching of shared transition outcomes. This is a presentation
and implementation choice rather than a constraint of the policy
inference view. In continuous domains, the same semantics can be retained
with standard engineering choices. Deterministic policy behavior can be
enforced at the representation level (e.g., a deterministic policy
network) or via a hashable state abstraction used for ‘first-visit’
bookkeeping (tiling/binning, nearest-neighbor in an embedding space, or a
domain-provided context label). Shared environment stochasticity likewise
does not require a discrete transition table: it can be implemented with
common random numbers by driving the simulator with shared keyed random
streams, so that particles making equivalent transition queries receive
the same noise realization and therefore experience the same stochastic
outcome.

A second, orthogonal scope choice in this work is enforcing
strict determinism within each sampled policy. Inference over
deterministic policies yields a clean posterior over coherent
behaviors, but it also makes certain action choices unforgiving.
In particular, actions that have no effect in a state (e.g.,
banging into a wall) are effectively catastrophic under a
deterministic memoization rule: once such an action is chosen on
the first visit, the policy is committed to repeating it on
every revisit, and the resulting policies are exponentially
downweighted. This effect is most pronounced in deterministic or
nearly deterministic domains, where a single early ‘no-op’ can
lock the trajectory into low-return behavior. A practical
relaxation is to replace hard memoization with
stochastic
memoization
: upon revisiting a state, the particle usually
reuses the previously chosen action, but with a controlled
probability it is allowed to resample a new action.
