Phase-based Nonlinear Model Predictive Control for Humanoid Walking Stabilization with Single and Double Support Time Adjustments
Kwanwoo Lee, Gyeongjae Park, Jaeheung Park

Abstract
Balance control for humanoid robots has been extensively studied to enable robots to navigate in real-world environments. However, balance controllers that explicitly optimize the durations of both the single support phase, also known as step timing, and the Double Support Phase (DSP) have not been widely explored due to the inherent nonlinearity of the associated optimization problem. Consequently, many recent approaches either ignore the DSP or adjust its duration based on heuristics or on linearization techniques that rely on sequential coordination of balance strategies. This study proposes a novel phase-based nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework that simultaneously optimizes Zero Moment Point (ZMP) modulation, step location, step timing, and DSP duration to maintain balance under external disturbances. In simulation, the proposed controller was compared with two state-of-the-art frameworks that rely on heuristics or sequential coordination of balance strategies under two scenarios: forward walking on terrain emulating compliant ground and external push recovery while walking in place. Overall, the findings suggest that the proposed method offers more flexible coordination of balance strategies than the sequential approach, and consistently outperforms the heuristic approach. The robustness and effectiveness of the proposed controller were also validated through experiments with a real humanoid robot.



I
INTRODUCTION

Balance control is essential for humanoid robots to stably perform locomotion and tasks within human-centric environments.
Accordingly, balance control has been actively studied to address challenging real-world uncertainties, such as external forces and uneven terrain.
However, achieving robust balance control for humanoid robots is nontrivial due to their complex dynamics that arise from nonlinearity, high degrees of freedom, and an underactuated floating base that is indirectly controlled by external contact wrenches.

From the early stages of humanoid robot walking research, the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM)
[
1
]
has been used as a reduced model to simplify the complex dynamics of humanoid robots.
The LIPM approximates the dynamic relationship between the external contact wrenches and the floating base as a linear relationship between the Zero Moment Point (ZMP)
[
2
]
and the Center of Mass (CoM).
Using LIPM, walking pattern generators
[
3
,
4
]
have been proposed to plan CoM trajectories based on predefined footsteps.
Furthermore, some stabilizers
[
5
,
6
]
that use ZMP modulation have been introduced to regulate ground reaction forces through the support foot, which is known as the ankle strategy
[
7
]
.
However, the use of ZMP modulation alone has inherent limitations in coping with large external disturbances because the ankle strategy relies on the limited ankle torque capacity.

Snapshots of humanoid robot balance control in the real-world scenario: Recovery from a backward push while stepping in place.

The Divergent Component of Motion (DCM)
[
8
,
9
]
has been utilized to simplify trajectory generation
[
10
]
and control
[
11
]
by focusing solely on the unstable part of the LIPM.
In 2-D space, the DCM, also known as the Capture Point
[
12
,
13
]
, is defined as the point at which a robot comes to a complete stop if it places its foot at that location.
Based on this definition, various stepping controllers have recently been proposed to cope with large external disturbances by actively adjusting the step location of the swing foot rather than relying on pre-planned footsteps
[
14
,
15
]
.

In order to achieve a more robust and agile balance control, some studies
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
have proposed methods to adjust not only the step location but also the step timing.
This combined adjustment is commonly referred to as a stepping strategy
[
7
]
.
These approaches are based on a nonlinear analytical solution of the DCM-ZMP dynamics derived under the assumption of a constant ZMP.
Specifically, the seamless integration of the ankle and stepping strategies
[
18
]
reported better push recovery and greater flexibility than the sequential approach
[
17
]
, which applies the ankle strategy first and then the stepping strategy.
Note that this sequential coordination of balance strategies stems from the need to avoid nonlinearity.
Although studies on balance controllers combining ankle and stepping strategies have significantly improved balancing capabilities, balance controllers that explicitly optimize the durations of both the Single Support Phase (SSP), referred to as step timing, and the Double Support Phase (DSP) have not been widely explored.

The importance of DSP in the nominal gait schedule has been emphasized to enable proper weight shifting to unload the feet
[
19
]
.
Moreover, the absence of DSP can lead to discontinuous variations in robot CoM acceleration during support transitions, resulting in discontinuous joint torque commands
[
9
]
.
Although this may not be problematic for robots with high-bandwidth torque actuators, it can pose significant implementation challenges for most robots lacking such high-performance actuators.
Thus, DSP remains important for achieving feasible and smooth walking in such humanoid robotic systems.
However, when multiple steps are required instantaneously to maintain balance, a fixed DSP duration can delay the transition to the subsequent SSP.
Therefore, some studies have employed heuristics or a sequential approach to optimize the DSP duration
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
Specifically, Egle
et al.

[
21
]
proposed a sequential approach that optimizes ZMP modulation, step locations, step timing, and DSP duration using linear Model Predictive Control (MPC).
This study used a nonlinear analytical solution of DCM-ZMP dynamics derived from time-varying ZMP trajectories to capture the time-varying characteristics of ZMP during DSP.
However, the system was linearized by assuming that the stepping strategy is applied first before the ankle strategy, thereby sacrificing the model fidelity.
Hence, this assumption requires hand-crafted coordination, such as DCM error separation and projection; consequently, the sequential approach lacks flexibility in coordinating multiple balance strategies.

The main contributions of this study are as follows:
1) This study proposes a seamless balance control framework based on a phase-based nonlinear MPC (NMPC) that simultaneously optimizes ZMP modulation, step location, step timing, and DSP duration.
2) In a simulation environment that emulates compliant terrain, the proposed controller demonstrated more robust balance control and more flexible use of the aforementioned balance strategies than the sequential approach
[
21
]
.
In addition, the proposed method achieved more robust performance compared to the heuristic approach
[
20
]
.
3) The proposed framework was validated through real-world experiments on a humanoid robot.

II
PRELIMINARIES

II-A

Linear Inverted Pendulum Model

The LIPM has been widely utilized as a reduced model to simplify the complex dynamics of a humanoid robot into a linear relationship between the CoM and the ZMP.
In the LIPM, the total mass of the robot is assumed to be concentrated at the CoM.
Both the time derivative of the centroidal angular momentum and the vertical acceleration of the CoM are neglected.
Under these assumptions, the relationship between the CoM and the ZMP is given by,

\ddot{\bm{c}}=\frac{g}{c_{z}-z_{z}}(\bm{c}-\bm{z}),

(1)

where the
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the CoM and ZMP positions in the horizontal plane, respectively.
The variables
c_{z}
and
z_{z}
represent the vertical positions of CoM and ZMP, respectively.
The variable
g
is the gravitational acceleration.

II-B

Divergent Component of Motion

The LIPM in (
1
) contains both stable and unstable eigenmodes.
The DCM corresponds to the unstable mode associated with the positive eigenvalue.
The relationship between DCM and CoM is defined as,

\bm{\xi}=\bm{c}+b\bm{\dot{c}},

(2)

where the variable
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the DCM position and
b=\sqrt{\frac{c_{z}-z_{z}}{g}}
is the time constant.
By reordering (
2
), the relationship between the DCM and the ZMP is derived as,

\bm{\dot{\xi}}=\frac{1}{b}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{z}).

(3)

II-C

DCM-ZMP Dynamics

The nominal gait schedule of a bipedal walking robot consists of
n_{\varphi}
(single and double) support phases.
During the SSP, the robot supports itself with one foot while swinging the other foot toward the target location.
During the DSP, both feet are in contact with the ground as the robot shifts its weight to the next support foot.
For each phase
\varphi\in\{1,\cdots,n_{\varphi}\}
, the current position of ZMP
\bm{z}_{\varphi,t}
is interpolated in the horizontal plane from the starting point of ZMP
\bm{z}_{\varphi,0}
to the end point of ZMP
\bm{z}_{\varphi,T}
.
In this study, the ZMP trajectory is planned using linear interpolation within each phase
\varphi
:

\bm{z}_{\varphi,t}=\left(1-\frac{t}{T_{\varphi}}\right)\bm{z}_{\varphi,0}+\frac{t}{T_{\varphi}}\bm{z}_{\varphi,T}.

(4)

Here,
t\in[0,T_{\varphi}]
denotes the current time within the phase
\varphi
, and
T_{\varphi}
is the duration of phase
\varphi
.

Since the dynamics of DCM-ZMP (
3
) is a first-order differential equation and the ZMP trajectory in (
4
) is linear, the solution of (
3
) is obtained using partial integration as,

\bm{\xi}_{\varphi,T}=\mathbf{Z}_{\alpha}(T_{\varphi})+e^{\frac{T_{\varphi}-t}{b}}\left(\bm{\xi}_{\varphi,t}-\mathbf{Z}_{\beta}(T_{\varphi},t)\right),\\

(5)

where

\begin{split}&\mathbf{Z}_{\alpha}(T_{\varphi})=\bm{z}_{\varphi,T}+\frac{b}{T_{\varphi}}\left(\bm{z}_{\varphi,T}-\bm{z}_{\varphi,0}\right),\\
&\mathbf{Z}_{\beta}(T_{\varphi},t)=\bm{z}_{\varphi,0}+\frac{t+b}{T_{\varphi}}\left(\bm{z}_{\varphi,T}-\bm{z}_{\varphi,0}\right).\end{split}

(6)

Hereafter, the subscript
\varphi
is omitted for better readability.

II-D

DCM offset

Fig. 2:
Graphical diagram of the reference DCM trajectory; (a) the single support phase (SSP); (b) the subsequent double support phase (DSP).

The DCM end point
\bm{\xi}_{T}
represents the position of the DCM at the end of each phase. Fig.
2
illustrates a graphical diagram of the reference DCM trajectory.
During SSP,
\bm{f}_{0}
and
\bm{f}_{T}
represent the current position of the support foot and the target landing location of the swing foot, respectively.
The DCM end point
\bm{\xi}_{T}
during the SSP can be decomposed into the step location
\bm{f}_{T}
and a DCM offset
\bm{b}_{T}
as follows
[
16
]
:

\bm{\xi}_{T}=\bm{f}_{T}+\bm{b}_{T}.

(7)

According to the definition of the DCM, if the robot steps onto the DCM within the
N
-step capture region, it will eventually come to a complete stop
[
13
]
.
This observation implies that the reference DCM offset
\bm{b}^{\text{ref}}_{T}
should be determined from the reference step location
\bm{f}^{\text{ref}}_{T}
to maintain the desired walking velocity.
Therefore, minimizing the DCM offset error
\bm{b}_{T}^{\text{err}}
contributes to maintaining the target walking velocity and preventing the robot from falling due to disturbances.

In this paper, we further extend the definition of DCM offset to the DSP. The nominal gait schedule consists of a repeated cycle that begins with the SSP and ends with the DSP.
In an attempt to apply the relationship in (
7
) to the DSP as well, we define
\bm{f}_{0}
and
\bm{f}_{T}
in the DSP as the support foot position and the swing foot landing position from the previous SSP, respectively.
By the definition of the DSP, both
\bm{f}_{0}
and
\bm{f}_{T}
remain unchanged during the DSP.
Therefore, (
7
) is generally applied in all phases without loss of generality.
This allows the structure of the optimization problem and the variables to remain consistent in all phases.

III
Overall Framework

In this section, the proposed control framework is introduced.
Fig.
3
illustrates the overall control architecture.
First, the footstep planner generates the reference footstep poses
\bm{F}^{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{f}\times 12}
and the phase durations
\bm{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varphi}}
.
Based on these, the reference ZMP trajectory is planned such that it remains in the center of the support foot during SSP (i.e.,
\bm{z}^{\text{ref}}_{0}=\bm{z}^{\text{ref}}_{T}
) and is linearly interpolated between the centers of the previous and next support feet during DSP.

Fig. 3:
Overall control framework for humanoid balance control utilizing the proposed phase-based Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC).

The walking pattern generator in
[
4
]
produces the reference CoM trajectory
\bm{C}^{\text{ref}},\bm{\dot{C}}^{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times 2}
to track the reference ZMP trajectory
\bm{Z}^{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times 2}
while minimizing the CoM jerk within a preview horizon of
N_{p}
.
In general, a given ZMP trajectory can be realized by a CoM trajectory that diverges to infinity due to the nature of LIPM.
Since such behavior is considered infeasible for real humanoid robots, stability constraints are often introduced to ensure bounded CoM evolution
[
23
]
.
In this study, a capturability constraint,
\bm{c}_{N_{p}}+b\bm{\dot{c}}_{N_{p}}=\bm{z}^{\text{ref}}_{N_{p}}
, is incorporated into the walking pattern generator.
Then, the DCM trajectory
\bm{\Xi}^{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times 2}
is generated using (
2
).

The proposed NMPC optimizes the variable
\bm{v}_{i}
in Section
IV-A
for each phase
i=1,2,\cdots,n_{c}
, where
n_{c}
denotes the number of previewed phases.
The proposed NMPC aims to minimize the DCM offset error
\bm{b}^{\text{err}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}
.
Among the optimized variables, only the first control input
\bm{v}_{1}
is applied for balance control.

The ZMP control input
\bm{z}_{t,1}^{\text{ctrl}}
is linearly interpolated from the start point
\bm{z}_{0,1}^{\text{ctrl}}
to the end point
\bm{z}_{T,1}^{\text{ctrl}}
over the optimized phase duration
T_{\text{new},1}
following (
4
), where the superscript ctrl denotes control.
The ZMP distributor
[
6
]
converts the optimized target ZMP, defined as
\bm{z}_{t,1}^{\text{des}}=\bm{z}_{t,1}^{\text{ref}}+\bm{z}_{t,1}^{\text{ctrl}}
, into the desired contact wrenches
\bm{w}_{c}^{\text{des}}\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
applied to support feet.

The target landing position of the swing foot
\bm{f}_{T_{\text{new}},1}^{\text{des}}
is determined by adding the optimized step location adjustment
\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},1}
to the reference landing position
\bm{f}_{T,1}^{\text{ref}}
.
Similarly, the optimized phase duration
T_{\text{new},1}
is computed by adding the phase duration adjustment
\delta T_{1}
to the reference duration
T_{1}
.
The swing foot trajectory
\bm{f}_{t,1}^{\text{des}}
is then generated using a fifth-order spline.
When the current time
t
reaches the optimized phase duration
T_{\text{new},1}
, a phase transition is triggered; then,
t
is reset to zero, and the system progresses to the next phase.

The joint control module computes the desired angles of the lower body joints
\bm{q}_{lb}^{\text{des}}
using analytical inverse kinematics based on the optimized trajectories of the feet and CoM.
The desired angles of the upper body joints
\bm{q}_{ub}^{\text{des}}
remain constant.
The joint torque command
\bm{\tau}^{\text{cmd}}
is computed as the sum of the feedforward torque and the PD feedback torque.
Here, the feedforward torque
\bm{\tau}^{\text{ff}}
is computed by the QP-based whole-body inverse dynamics controller (WBC) from
[
22
]
described in Section
V
.
The WBC aims to to track the desired joint accelerations
\bm{\ddot{q}}^{\text{des}}
and the contact wrenches
\bm{w}_{c}^{\text{des}}
.

IV
PHASE-BASED NONLINEAR MPC

In this section, the main contribution of this paper, phase-based NMPC, is introduced.
The proposed NMPC aims to minimize the DCM offset error to maintain balance while preserving the nominal gait pattern of the robot.

IV-A

Optimization Variables

When the number of previewed phases is given by
n_{c}
, the optimization variable
\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{9}
is defined as follows:

\bm{v}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{0,i}}^{\top}\hskip-9.0pt&{\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T,i}}^{\top}\hskip-9.0pt&{\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}}^{\top}\hskip-9.0pt&{\bm{b}^{\text{err}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}}^{\top}\hskip-9.0pt&\delta T_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},\forall i=1,\ldots,n_{c}.

(8)

Here,
i=1
corresponds to the current support phase of the robot, while
i=2,\dots,n_{c}
represent the previewed phases within the MPC horizon.

IV-B

Nonlinear DCM Error Dynamics

The nonlinear DCM error dynamics for a new phase duration, derived under the assumption of a linearly interpolated ZMP trajectory, are expressed as follows:

\begin{split}\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{v}_{i},\bm{\xi}^{\text{err}}_{t,i},t)\ &=\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}}}+\bm{b}^{\text{err}}_{T_{\text{new}}}-\bm{Z}_{\alpha}^{\text{ctrl}}(T_{\text{new}})-\bm{Z}_{\alpha}^{\text{ref}}(T_{\text{new}})\\
&+\bm{Z}_{\alpha}^{\text{ref}}(T)-e^{\frac{T_{\text{new}}-t}{b}}\left(\bm{\xi}_{t}^{\text{err}}-\bm{Z}_{\beta}^{\text{ctrl}}(T_{\text{new}},t)\right)\\
&-\left(e^{\frac{T_{\text{new}}-t}{b}}-e^{\frac{T-t}{b}}\right)\bm{\xi}_{t}^{\text{ref}}\\
&+e^{\frac{T_{\text{new}}-t}{b}}\bm{Z}_{\beta}^{\text{ref}}(T_{\text{new}},t)-e^{\frac{T-t}{b}}\bm{Z}_{\beta}^{\text{ref}}(T,t)=\bm{0}.\end{split}

(9)

Equation (
9
) is derived under the assumption that the reference end point of the DCM remains unchanged despite the phase duration adjustments.
The objective is to better align the updated DCM trajectory with the current DCM measurement.
It serves as a nonlinear constraint to minimize the DCM offset error
\bm{b}^{\text{err}}_{T_{\text{new}}}
by optimizing the ZMP modulation, step location, and phase duration—namely,
\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{0}
,
\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T}
,
\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}}}
, and
\delta T
.
The detailed derivation of the nonlinear constraint (
9
) is provided in
APPENDIX
.

IV-C

Phase-based Nonlinear MPC

With the optimization variables
\bm{v}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{v}_{n_{c}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{9n_{c}}
, the phase-based NMPC is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{v}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\bm{v}_{i}}\bm{v}_{i}

(10)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{1}(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{t,1}^{\text{err}},t)=\bm{0}

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{v}_{i},\bm{\xi}_{0,i}^{\text{err}},0)=\bm{0}\quad\text{for }i=2,\ldots,n_{c}

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{T,i}^{\text{ctrl}}=\bm{z}_{0,i+1}^{\text{ctrl}}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n_{c}-1

(12)

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{z}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{0,i}\leq\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{0,i}\leq\overline{\bm{z}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{0,i}\quad\underline{\bm{z}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T,i}\leq\bm{z}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T,i}\leq\overline{\bm{z}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T,i}\quad\forall i

(13)

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{f}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i,j}-M(\bm{1}-\bm{y}_{j})\leq\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}\leq\overline{\bm{f}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i,j}+M(\bm{1}-\bm{y}_{j})\quad

\displaystyle\forall i\quad\forall j\in\{\text{SSP},\text{DSP}\}

(14)

\displaystyle\underline{\delta T}_{i}\leq\delta T_{i}\leq\overline{\delta T}_{i}\quad\forall i.

(15)

Here,
\bm{W}_{\bm{v}_{i}}
denotes a set of positive definite weighting matrices.
The underline and overline indicate the lower and upper bounds of the corresponding variable, respectively.
The weighting parameters are summarized in Table
I
.
The cost function regularizes the control inputs and minimizes the DCM offset error.
Note that the DCM offset error term is assigned the highest weight due to its critical influence on walking stability.

The constraints consist of two equality constraints and three inequality constraints.
The first equality constraint (
11
) represents the dynamics constraints of the proposed NMPC.
The first line of (
11
) enforces the nonlinear DCM error dynamics for the current support phase, while the second line of (
11
) ensures the continuity of the error dynamics across the future previewed phases.
Note that the DCM start point error of phase
i
is equal to the DCM end point error of the previous phase as,
\bm{\xi}_{0,i}^{\text{err}}=\bm{\xi}_{T_{\text{new},i-1}}^{\text{err}}=\bm{f}_{T_{\text{new},i-1}}^{\text{ctrl}}+\bm{b}_{T_{\text{new},i-1}}^{\text{err}}.

The second equality constraint (
12
) ensures the continuity of the ZMP control input between adjacent phases such that the ZMP transition remains smooth across phase boundaries.

The first inequality constraint (
13
) defines the ZMP feasibility bounds.
The ZMP control input at the start and end of each phase must remain within the local support foothold.
Thus, the bounds of the ZMP control inputs are determined by the foot size.
The second inequality constraint (
14
) imposes limits on the step location adjustment.
In the SSP, the robot must step within its reachable region.
This leads to the following constraint:

\underline{\bm{f}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i,\text{SSP}}\leq\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}\leq\overline{\bm{f}}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i,\text{SSP}}.

During the DSP, no step location adjustment is allowed.
This leads to the following constraint:

\bm{f}^{\text{ctrl}}_{T_{\text{new}},i}=~\bm{0}.

To accommodate the alternating SSP and DSP structures in the previewed phases, this constraint is formulated using the standard big-
M
method. Here,
\bm{y}_{j}\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
is a binary indicator:
\bm{y}_{j}=\bm{1}
if phase
j
is active, and
\bm{y}_{j}=\bm{0}
otherwise.
The vector
\bm{0}
and
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
consist of all zeros and ones, respectively.
The variable
M
is a sufficiently large constant that activates the corresponding constraints depending on the gait schedule.
The final inequality constraint (
15
) imposes limits on the phase duration adjustment
\delta T_{i}
.
These limits prevent an excessive increase in the swing foot speed during the SSP and impulsive ZMP shifts during the DSP.

The proposed NMPC problem is solved using Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)
[
24
]
, an iterative method for constrained nonlinear optimization.
The solution is iteratively updated as,

\bm{v}_{k+1}=\bm{v}_{k}+\beta_{k}\triangle\bm{v}_{k},

(16)

where
k
denotes the iteration step and
\beta_{k}
is determined via a line search algorithm in
[
25
]
.
To solve the nonlinear problem through multiple iterations, the active-set QP solver
qpOASES

[
26
]
is employed.
To meet the requirements of real-time implementation, the number of SQP iterations is limited to 20, and the iteration is terminated early if the 2-norm of the SQP search direction
\triangle\bm{v}_{k}
falls below
10^{-6}
.

V
WHOLE-BODY CONTROL

The desired ZMP
\bm{z}^{\text{des}}_{t,1}
is converted into the desired contact wrench
\bm{w}_{c}^{\text{des}}
for both feet using the ZMP distributor introduced in
[
6
]
.
The kinematic-level PD controller computes the desired joint acceleration as follows:

\ddot{\bm{q}}^{\text{des}}=\bm{K}_{p}^{\text{kin}}(\bm{q}^{\text{des}}-\bm{q})-\bm{K}_{d}^{\text{kin}}\dot{\bm{q}},

(17)

where
\bm{K}_{p}^{\text{kin}}
and
\bm{K}_{d}^{\text{kin}}
represent the kinematic-level PD gains.
To track the desired contact wrench, a QP-based whole-body inverse dynamics controller (WBC) from
[
22
]
is employed.
Given the joint acceleration command
\ddot{\bm{q}}^{\text{des}}
and the desired contact wrench
\bm{w}_{c}^{\text{des}}
, the WBC computes the feedforward torque
\bm{\tau}^{\text{ff}}
by solving a QP that incorporates the full-body dynamics and the soft contact constraints.

Finally, the following torque
\bm{\tau}^{\text{cmd}}
is commanded to the robot’s actuators at 2 kHz as

\bm{\tau}^{\text{cmd}}=\bm{\tau}^{\text{ff}}+\bm{K}_{p}(\bm{q}^{\text{des}}-\bm{q})-\bm{K}_{d}\dot{\bm{q}}.

(18)

The motor-level PD controller gains used in the simulations are set to
\bm{K}_{p}=\mathrm{diag}(160,\ldots,160)
and
\bm{K}_{d}=\mathrm{diag}(25,\ldots,25)
.

VI
RESULTS

VI-A

System Overview

The proposed method was validated through both simulation using MuJoCo
[
27
]
and hardware experiments on a humanoid robot.
The main control loop for generating torque commands operated at 2 kHz, while the walking pattern generator
[
4
]
and the proposed algorithm were executed in parallel at 100 Hz.
The number of previewed phases
n_{c}
in the NMPC was set to 3 to predict two upcoming steps, following the proposition in
[
28
]
.
In real-world experiments, the proposed method was deployed on an onboard computer mounted on the back of the robot, equipped with a 3.7 GHz octa-core processor and 16 GB of RAM.
The average computation time, including the walking pattern generator and the proposed method, was measured to be 4225
\mu s
.
This result satisfied the real-time requirements of the parallel control thread.

A larger positive

This simulation setting in MuJoCo has also been used in the reinforcement learning community to simulate compliant ground for robust policy training

As illustrated in Fig.

This can partially emulate the effect of compliant terrain, although the surface cannot fully serve the physical properties of real-world soft ground.

In this scenario, the robot performs forward walking on compliant terrain with a step length of 0.15

As shown in Fig.

In contrast, the sequential approach shown in Fig.

Evaluation of Robust Walking under External Push

In the first experiment, the robustness of the proposed method was evaluated under external push disturbances.
The disturbance rejection behavior of the proposed method was analyzed under external backward and lateral forces applied to the robot.
To assess the performance of the proposed method, physical forces were applied to the base of a humanoid robot walking in place.

for the backward push and 9.4
s

During the following SSP, the NMPC simultaneously optimized the ZMP modulation, step location, and step timing to minimize the DCM offset error induced by external forces.
The seamless integration of the balance control inputs enabled effective balance recovery under both types of perturbations.

In addition, we also conducted additional tests, including throwing a large yoga ball at the robot and pushing the robot with a stick.

VII
CONCLUSION

This study proposed a phase-based NMPC framework for robust humanoid balance control.
The proposed controller simultaneously optimizes ZMP modulation, step location, step timing, and the DSP duration, enabling the seamless integration of multiple balance strategies.
Simulation experiments on compliant terrain that emulate real-world environments demonstrated that the proposed method offers better flexibility than the sequential approach to apply the aforementioned balance strategies.
In addition, the proposed method achieved notable performance improvements over the heuristic approach across all experimental scenarios.
In future work, the framework will be extended to support loco-manipulation tasks, as the current study focused solely on achieving robust locomotion on flat terrain due to limitations inherent in the LIPM structure.

Phase duration adjustment is described as the process of finding an optimized duration

\bm{\xi}_{T}^{\text{ref}}=\bm{\xi}_{T_{\text{new}}}^{\text{ref}}

\bm{\xi}_{t}
