Multiscale assessment of transboundary water-food-energy nexus under climate change in Irtysh River Basin
Authors: Xialin Wang, Xiaoning Wang, Hanyu Jin, Hongrui Liu, Yanbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClimate change is intensifying water scarcity and amplifying upstream-downstream tensions in transboundary river basins, particularly in Central Asia. We present a multiscale framework integrating hydrological dynamics with the water-food-energy (WFE) nexus to assess climate-driven cross-sectoral trade-offs in Irtysh River Basin. By combining long-term hydroclimatic observations with climate projections, we quantified the effects of temperature and precipitation variability on runoff and evaluated how these changes propagate across interconnected WFE systems. Climate-induced hydrological changes increased irrigation demand, reduced hydropower potential, reshaped agricultural productivity, and intensified regional resource pressures, with distinct responses between upstream and downstream regions. These findings reveal pronounced spatial heterogeneity in climate vulnerability and adaptive capacity, highlighting the need for differentiated adaptation strategies and coordinated transboundary governance. Our study establishes a transferable framework for assessing climate change impacts across the WFE nexus, providing actionable insights for enhancing climate resilience and sustainable resource governance in transboundary river basins.

Introduction

Transboundary river basins, defined by hydrological systems that extend across national boundaries, play a critical role in the management of shared water resources.1 Under accelerating climate change, riparian countries face growing challenges in ensuring the sustainable allocation, governance, and utilization of these resources.2

Central Asia, characterized by its arid inland environment, is widely recognized as a hotspot for transboundary water challenges.3 Rising temperatures, enhanced evapotranspiration, and declining surface runoff associated with climate change have intensified regional water scarcity and exacerbated competition among water, food, and energy (WFE) sectors.4,5 Agriculture accounts for approximately 84% of total water withdrawals in the region and remains heavily dependent on irrigation.6 However, insufficient water infrastructure, inefficient irrigation practices, and extensive agricultural expansion continue to aggravate water shortages and threaten food security. At the same time, hydropower development and growing energy demand impose additional pressure on already limited water resources,7 further challenging regional WFE security.8

Since the WFE nexus concept was introduced at the 2011 Bonn Conference,9 related research has expanded rapidly, leading to the development of conceptual frameworks10,11,12 and diverse regional assessments.13,14 Previous studies have shown that cross-sectoral cooperation in transboundary river basins can generate environmental and economic benefits, promote integrated regional development, and reduce water-related conflicts.15,16,17,18

Despite these advances, important knowledge gaps remain regarding how the WFE nexus responds to climate change, particularly within upstream-downstream contexts.19,20 Existing approaches, including process-based analyses,21,22 integrated system evaluations,23,24 and coupled modeling frameworks,25,26 provide valuable insights but often fail to fully capture the interdisciplinary complexity required to optimize upstream-downstream interactions in transboundary river systems.2 Furthermore, many studies remain constrained by administrative boundaries and pay limited attention to cross-border hydrological and socioeconomic dynamics.27 Such state-centered perspectives restrict understanding of basin-scale system behavior by overlooking the interconnected processes and upstream-downstream linkages that shape transboundary outcomes.28

In Central Asia, existing research has primarily focused on hydrometeorological trends,29,30 transboundary water governance,31,32 and the socioeconomic implications of international policies.33,34 However, comprehensive assessments of climate-driven WFE nexus responses remain limited.24,35 Given increasing climate pressures and the complexity of transboundary river systems in the region, there is an urgent need for nexus-based approaches capable of supporting integrated and adaptive basin management.36

To address these gaps, this study focuses on the Irtysh River Basin (IRB), a major transboundary river system in Central Asia (Figure 1). Using long-term climatic, hydrological, and socioeconomic datasets combined with a multiscale assessment framework (Figure 2), this study investigates two key questions: (1) how are climate change impacts evolving across upstream and downstream regions? and (2) what forms of WFE cooperation can enhance the adaptive capacity of transboundary regions under climate change? Results

Climate change trends estimation

Spatiotemporal heterogeneity in hydrological availability are shown pronounced across the IRB from 2000 to 2023 (Figure 3). Although the magnitude of change differs among sub-basins, runoff decline is more evident in the lower-reach systems, particularly the Tobol River Basin (TRB), Konda River Basin (KRB), and Vagay River Basin (VRB). This pattern suggests that downstream areas may be more vulnerable to reduced runoff supply. This decline in discharge occurs alongside basin-wide warming, with stronger temperature increases observed in several upstream sub-basins, including the Ob River Basin (OBB), Middle Irtysh River Basin (MIRB), and Gol’koye Lake Basin (GLB). Meanwhile, decreasing precipitation, especially the anomalously dry conditions observed in 2022–2023,38 further constrains regional water availability. Together, these results indicate that recent hydroclimatic changes in the IRB are characterized by both reduced discharge and spatially uneven drought pressure across the basin.Figure 3Climate change trendsClimate change trends showing (A) monthly runoff variations (mm), (B) monthly temperature variations (°C), and (C) monthly precipitation variations (mm) across all sub-basins during 2000–2023.

Climatic drivers of river discharge

Multiple stepwise regression analysis revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the hydroclimatic controls of river discharge across the IRB. The detailed regression outputs are provided in Table S1 to support the subsequent analyses in this study.

In several sub-basins, including the KRB, TRB, and MIRB, precipitation emerged as the dominant driver of runoff variability, indicating that river discharge is primarily constrained by water availability. This finding suggests that reductions in precipitation can propagate into runoff deficits in these regions.

In contrast, temperature and evaporative demand became more influential in regulating discharge variability in other parts of the basin. The stronger association between discharge variability and temperature in the Lower Ob River Basin (LOBB) suggests that warming may increasingly influence runoff generation and flow regulation processes. This pattern is consistent with previous studies showing that regional warming can alter hydrological responses and reduce runoff stability in the IRB.39

Potential evapotranspiration exerted a significant negative influence on river discharge in the Ishim River Basin (ISRB) and GLB. Given the extensive grassland coverage in these sub-basins (Table 1), enhanced evaporative water losses are likely to reduce available runoff. Climate projections across sub-basins

To ensure the reliability of CMIP6 projections, model selection, bias correction, and validation procedures were further performed. The validation results showed good agreement between the climate-model simulations and ERA5-Land observations (Figures S1 and S2), supporting their use in the subsequent climate-projection analyses. Under SSP1-2.6, mean annual temperatures are projected to increase by approximately 1.5°C–3°C above historical levels, depending on the sub-basin. SSP2-4.5 produces intermediate warming of approximately 3°C–5°C, whereas SSP5-8.5 results in the largest increases, exceeding 6°C–10°C by the end of the century in several sub-basins. The KRB, OBB, VRB, and ISRB exhibit the steepest warming trajectories and the greatest inter-model spread. These projected temperature increases, particularly placing additional pressure on water allocation, agricultural productivity, and food security.

Precipitation projections exhibit considerable variability under both historical and future conditions. Interannual fluctuations remain pronounced across all SSP scenarios, resulting in wide confidence intervals and lower model agreement compared with temperature projection. Some sub-basins, including the VRB, ISRB, and GLB, show modest increases in mean annual precipitation under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 by the late century. In contrast, other sub-basins, including the OBB, LOBB, and MIRB, exhibit slight declines, most notably under SSP5-8.5. Nevertheless, the magnitude of projected precipitation changes remains relatively small compared with natural variability.

Overall, sub-basins located in the lower reaches of IRB (e.g., KRB and VRB) appear more sensitive to rising temperature, whereas precipitation projections remain highly uncertain and spatially heterogeneous. These findings highlight the importance of basin-specific climate impact assessments that consider both model uncertainty and localized hydroclimatic responses.

Projected changes in temperature and precipitation act as key external drivers of WFE nexus dynamics. Figure 5 further illustrates how these hydroclimatic forcings propagate through WFE systems, shaping cross-sectoral trade-offs and informing adaptation priorities across upstream and downstream regions. Specifically, rising temperatures and altered precipitation regimes are projected to affect water availability and increase the frequency of extreme events. These climate-driven changes modify cross-sectoral interactions and trade-offs, emphasizing the need for spatially differentiated adaptation pathways and basin-scale governance to support integrated WFE management. For instance, intensified warming in upstream regions may alter seasonal runoff regimes, thereby affecting the reliability of water supply for irrigation and hydropower generation. Downstream areas, characterized greater precipitation variability and increasing water scarcity, are likely to experience intensified competition among agricultural, energy, and ecological water demands. These findings demonstrate that spatially heterogeneous hydroclimatic changes have direct implications for basin-scale WFE resource pressures and necessitate tailored adaptation pathways.

WFE nexus assessment in the transboundary basin

As the core sub-basin encompassing our two administrative study regions, the MIRB experienced substantial precipitation variability alongside rising temperatures between 2014 and 2021 (Figure 6). Despite these climatic pressures, the upstream region in Xinjiang province implemented a series of adaptive measures, including adjustments in crop composition, revisions of irrigation quotas, and expanded adoption of water-saving irrigation technologies (Figure 7). Collectively, these measures generated approximately 422 million m3 of cumulative water savings, equivalent to an average of 52.73 million m3. Changes in WFE resources in the upstream region

Particularly, crop structure in Altay underwent substantial changes in response to hydroclimatic variability. Between 2007 and 2021, the total sown area across Altay’s seven administrative districts averaged 209,223 ha, expanding at an annual rate of 5%. Wheat cultivation increased by approximately 25% annually between 2007 and 2015 but subsequently declined by nearly 11% per year during 2016–2021. In contrast, soybean and vegetable cultivation areas decreased between 2007 and 2015 (−12% and −2% annually, respectively), followed by substantial expansion after 2016, with soybean cultivation area increasing by approximately 30% annually. Other major crops, including corn, potatoes, and oilseeds, increased by 23%, 2%, and 7%, respectively, over the 2007–2021 period.

Comparisons between agricultural transitions and local climate variations reveal a clear climate-crop relationship. During 2007–2015, mean temperature decreased slightly by 0.06 °C, while mean precipitation increased by 11.47 mm, conditions that favored water-intensive crops such as wheat. Between 2016 and 2021, precipitation declined by 18.19 mm, contributing to a transition toward more drought-tolerant crops, including corn, potatoes, and oilseeds. These results demonstrate the responsiveness of upstream agricultural systems to evolving hydroclimatic conditions and the effectiveness of adaptive management measures in maintaining WFE resource stability.

Comparison between upstream and downstream regions

Key WFE indicators reveal substantial differences in agricultural productivity, water-use efficiency, and energy demand between upstream and downstream regions (Figures 8 and 9; Table 2). Crop yields per unit area in Altay are approximately four times higher than those in East Kazakhstan, with cereal yields achieving an average of 7,485 kg/ha upstream compared with 1,388 kg/ha downstream. The upstream region also uses approximately 35% less irrigation water per hectare while consuming roughly five times more rural electricity, reflecting greater irrigation efficiency enabled by mechanized water-saving systems and greater energy inputs associated with agricultural intensification. In contrast, downstream regions in East Kazakhstan exhibit higher water consumption per unit of agricultural output, lower irrigation efficiency, and relatively low energy inputs in agriculture (e.g., 0.005 kWh/m3; 0.02 kWh/kg), indicating a continued reliance on traditional irrigation practices. Ecological water use was not included in the assessment because of data limitations; therefore, downstream WFE pressures may be conservatively estimated. The observed upstream-downstream disparities arise from the combined influences of hydroclimatic conditions, technological capacity, and socioeconomic factors. Upstream regions benefit from more efficient water infrastructure, optimized cropping structures, stricter water management policies, and diversified energy sources, which collectively reduce dependence on hydropower and enhance resilience to climate variability. In contrast, downstream regions face constrained water availability, lower agricultural productivity, and limited modernization of irrigation and energy infrastructure, resulting in greater exposure to WFE trade-offs under both climatic and socioeconomic pressures.

These disparities are likely to be further intensified under the hydroclimatic changes (Figure 4). Pronounced warming in upstream regions may alter seasonal runoff regimes, thereby affecting the reliability of water supply for irrigation and hydropower generation. Meanwhile, increasing precipitation variability and water scarcity in downstream areas are expected to intensify competition among agricultural, industrial, and ecological water demands. Combined with existing infrastructure and management limitations, these hydroclimatic stresses may further propagate cross-sectoral pressures, leading to reduced crop yields, higher water consumption per unit of agricultural output, and lower energy-use efficiency in downstream regions.

Overall, these findings demonstrate how hydroclimatic variability interacts with local management practices and technological capacity to shape differentiated WFE pressures across the basin. They reveal divergent developmental pathways within the transboundary system and underscore the importance of basin-scale coordination. Strengthening transboundary cooperation and promoting integrated WFE management will therefore be critical for enhancing downstream adaptive capacity, improving resource-use efficiency, and fostering more equitable resilience across the entire basin.

Discussion

Upstream-downstream relationships in transboundary basin cooperation

Transboundary river systems are shaped not only by hydrological linkages but also by institutional, political, and socioeconomic conditions.40 Regional dialog platforms and multilateral governance mechanisms therefore play essential roles in facilitating policy coordination and aligning national interests with shared basin objectives.41 The IRB, which serves as the case study in this study, reflects this complexity, as China and Kazakhstan occupy structurally different positions within the transboundary system.

As riparian states along the Irtysh River, China and Kazakhstan have maintained relatively stable cooperation in cross-border water management. In 2003, the two countries established the China-Kazakhstan Transboundary River Joint Committee, which remains the primary institutional platform for managing shared water resources. Between 2003 and 2015, the committee convened 12 official meetings and established a dedicated working group to draft the Transboundary River Water Resource Allocation Agreement. These mechanisms have contributed to maintaining ecological stability and supporting coordinated water allocation across the basin. Consequently, the Irtysh River is widely recognized as one of the best-protected transboundary rivers in Central Asia.8 The existing governance framework not only facilitates continued collaboration in water management but also provides an institutional foundation for expanding cooperation across the WFE nexus.

However, the findings of this study indicate that upstream and downstream regions exhibit substantially different levels of climate sensitivity and WFE vulnerability, creating new challenges for transboundary governance. Although the current institutional framework has been effective in addressing water allocation, it has not fully evolved to accommodate climate-induced hydrological changes, cross-sectoral WFE interdependencies, and trade-offs.42 Similar governance limitations have also been identified in other transboundary basins, including the Mekong and Syr Darya,43,44 where agreements focused primarily on water allocation have struggled to adapt to climate change and increasing resource pressures.

Within this context, the present study highlights both strengths and limitations of conventional transboundary water-sharing mechanisms. Future basin governance will require the integration of joint climate-risk assessments, shared data platforms, and multi-sectoral planning tools consistent with emerging WFE governance frameworks.1,45 The existing China-Kazakhstan partnership demonstrates substantial potential for expanding cooperation beyond water allocation toward integrated WFE governance and coordinated climate adaptation.

Hydroclimatic pressures and differentiated WFE vulnerabilities

Our results reveal significant warming trends, runoff reductions, declining precipitation in several sub-basins, and pronounced spatial heterogeneity in climate impacts across the IRB. These hydroclimatic pressures translate into markedly different WFE conditions between upstream and downstream regions.

The upstream region in China demonstrates relatively strong adaptive capacity, supported by more efficient water infrastructure, optimized cropping structures, expanded adoption of water-saving irrigation technologies, reduced irrigation quotas, and a gradual transition toward renewable energy sources. Collectively, these measures improve water-use efficiency and help alleviate pressure on regional water resources.

In contrast, downstream regions in Kazakhstan remain highly vulnerable to hydroclimatic stressors. Recurrent droughts, precipitation variability, and heat stress continue to threaten agricultural productivity, particularly within conventional wheat-based agricultural systems. In addition, irrigation systems still experience substantial water losses, while energy-use patterns reflect limited technological modernization.46 Similar conditions have also been documented in other downstream regions of Central Asia, such as the lower Amu Darya Basin, where irrigation inefficiencies and limited agricultural modernization further intensify climate-related risks.47

Hydroclimatic variability alone does not determine WFE vulnerability; rather, local management practices, infrastructure conditions, and adaptive capacity strongly influence how hydroclimatic stresses propagate through interconnected water, food, and energy systems. Our findings demonstrate that hydroclimatic variability interacts with governance capacity and technological development to shape differentiated WFE pressures across the basin. They further reveal divergent developmental pathways within the transboundary system and underscore the importance of adaptive basin-scale coordination under future climate change.

Opportunities for integrated transboundary WFE cooperation

The pronounced structural differences identified between upstream and downstream regions also indicate significant opportunities for asymmetric and complementary cooperation between China and Kazakhstan. Upstream advantages in technology, infrastructure, and financial capacity could support downstream adaptation efforts, particularly in irrigation modernization, water-saving technologies, and renewable energy development.

In recent years, Kazakhstan has initiated a series of agricultural reforms aimed at improving sustainability, including the promotion of modern irrigation systems, drought-resistant crop varieties, agricultural mechanization, and water-saving technologies.48 Although initial outcomes have been positive, substantial challenges remain in technology dissemination, financial support, and policy implementation, especially for smallholder farmers.49 Within this context, strengthened cooperation with China may provide important opportunities for improving downstream adaptive capacity and resource-use efficiency.

The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has further expanded the potential for cross-border cooperation in water, agriculture, and energy systems. Bilateral agricultural trade between China and Kazakhstan has increased substantially since the launch of the BRI,50 reflecting deepening regional economic integration and growing opportunities for agricultural modernization and cross-border investment. At the same time, coordinated investment in renewable energy infrastructure could help reduce downstream dependence on hydropower while supporting broader low-carbon energy transitions. Such cooperation is particularly relevant in light of Kazakhstan’s 2050 National Low-Carbon Energy Strategy and Xinjiang’s Clean Energy Medium- and Long-Term Development Plan for the Altay Region.51,52

Accordingly, the IRB is well positioned to pioneer integrated WFE cooperation strategies for transboundary basins in Central Asia. Effective long-term governance will require both countries to address water-related issues within the broader context of socioeconomic development and to prioritize implementable projects aligned with shared interests, mutual needs, and common objectives.53 Continued development of regional cooperation initiatives under the BRI may provide additional opportunities to support transboundary water-energy projects and strengthen agricultural resilience across the region.54

These findings suggest that effective transboundary cooperation should account not only for resource availability but also for structural differences in upstream–downstream WFE conditions. Integrating water management, agricultural trade, and energy transitions across national boundaries could help secure a stable transboundary water corridor, optimize cross-sectoral resource use, and generate mutual sustainability benefits for both countries. Importantly, the identified structural disparities indicate that hydroclimatic variability may further intensify upstream-downstream WFE trade-offs if transboundary coordination is not strengthened, underscoring the need for proactive and adaptive governance strategies.

Main findings

As climate change increasingly affect hydrological dynamics of transboundary river systems, strengthened cooperation among riparian countries is becoming essential for the sustainable management of shared water resources. Focusing on the Irtysh River Basin, a critical transboundary basin in Central Asia, this study assessed the evolving impacts of climate change and explored the potential of integrated WFE nexus strategies to enhance regional resilience.

The results reveal substantial temporal variability in basin runoff, characterized by more pronounced warming in upstream regions and more severe water scarcity in downstream areas. These spatial disparities highlight the need for a differentiated yet basin-wide approach to climate adaptation. The findings further demonstrate divergent adaptation pathways between upstream and downstream regions, underscoring the importance of coordinated governance strategies that address the interconnected challenges of water scarcity, energy demand, and agricultural stress under changing climatic conditions.

From a WFE nexus perspective, the study demonstrates that hydroclimatic variability alone does not determine regional vulnerability. Instead, governance capacity, technological development, and cross-border cooperation strongly influence how climate-related pressures are transmitted and managed across interconnected water, food, and energy systems. In this context, the BRI may provide an important platform for strengthening transboundary cooperation, promoting shared adaptation strategies, and supporting more integrated governance mechanisms between China and Kazakhstan.

Limitations of the study

This study also has several limitations. These include the limited availability of high-resolution downstream datasets and the absence of fully coupled simulation frameworks capable of representing climate-WFE interactions dynamically. Future research should improve the transparency and accessibility of environmental and socioeconomic data, develop integrated transboundary WFE simulation models, and incorporate participatory assessments involving farmers, policymakers, and energy stakeholders. In addition, evaluating the institutional feasibility and governance effectiveness of WFE cooperation mechanisms will be critical for supporting long-term transboundary collaboration. Overall, this study provides new insights into transboundary water governance in Central Asia and highlights the importance of adaptive management strategies that address distinct upstream and downstream challenges under climate change. Strengthened institutional cooperation and integrated basin-wide governance approaches will be essential for enhancing resilience and promoting sustainable development in the Irtysh River Basin and other transboundary river systems facing similar challenges.

Method details

Study area

Physical and hydroclimatic characteristics across the transboundary basin

The Irtysh River is a major international watercourse in Central Asia, extending approximately 4,248 km and flowing through China, Kazakhstan, and Russia before joining the Ob River and ultimately discharging into the Arctic Ocean. The Irtysh River Basin (IRB) covers about 165,000 km2 and supports nearly 15 million people, constituting a critical ecological and socioeconomic system in the region.55

Ecological and geographical conditions vary considerably along the upstream–downstream gradient across the IRB.56 The upper basin, located in Xinjiang Province in northwest China, is characterized by desert plains, alpine environments, and retreating glaciers.39 The middle basin, situated in northeastern Kazakhstan, is dominated by arid steppe and desert landscapes.57 In contrast, the lower basin, located on Russia’s Western Siberian Plain, consists primarily of grasslands and forest ecosystem.58

Over recent decades, the IRB has experienced pronounced climatic warming. Snow cover declined at an average rate of 0.3% per year between 1979 and 2016, reducing basin water storage capacity and weakening the natural regulation of runoff.59 These hydroclimatic changes have intensified concerns regarding water scarcity throughout the transboundary basin and provide an important environmental context for evaluating WFE interactions and resource vulnerabilities.39,59,60

Socioeconomic and sub-basin profiles

Geographical and demographic characteristics were integrated to delineate major socioeconomic regions and identify key upstream and downstream stakeholders within the WFE nexus. Socioeconomic and institutional conditions differ substantially among the three riparian countries of the IRB. Agriculture and pastoralism dominate land use in China’s Xinjiang Province and Kazakhstan, whereas industrial development plays a more prominent role in Russia.61,62,63 These differences, together with varying governance frameworks, add considerable complexity to transboundary water allocation and integrated WFE resource management.56

Spatial differences between upstream and downstream sub-basins further shape resource dependencies and development priorities. Upstream areas rely heavily on irrigation to sustain agricultural productivity, whereas downstream regions are more dependent on stable river discharge to support industrial activities, ecosystem services, and domestic water supply.

Baseline status of WFE resources across national boundaries

Due to limitations in data availability, this study focused on two representative administrative regions for the WFE assessment: the Altay region in Xinjiang Province, China (upstream), and East Kazakhstan Province, Kazakhstan (downstream). This comparative transboundary framework enables an evaluation of how climate-induced changes propagate through water, food, and energy systems across national boundaries.

The upstream region in Xinjiang is dominated by an “oasis economy”, where irrigated agriculture accounts for approximately 95% of total water consumption. Despite the critical role of irrigation in sustaining agricultural productivity, the development and utilization of water resources in the upper Irtysh River remain relatively limited.64 The adoption of water-saving irrigation technologies expanded substantially, increasing from 12,868 units in 2007 to 70,000 units in 2022 and covering nearly 67% of the total sown area. In parallel, irrigation quotas for major crops were revised in 2012, 2014, and 2023, resulting in an estimated 10% reduction in irrigation water use per hectare over the past decade (Figure 7).

In East Kazakhstan, cereal crops occupy about 46% of total sown area, with wheat accounting for nearly two-thirds of the cereal cultivation area. Oilseed crops represent 31%, reflecting increasing regional demand for vegetable oils.65 Between 2014 and 2021, the average annual production of cereals, potatoes, oilseeds, and vegetables reached approximately 1.96 million tons, requiring an estimated 6.8 billion m3 of irrigation water annually. These figures indicate relatively low irrigation efficiency and underscore the vulnerability of regional agriculture to water scarcity.65,66

Energy consumption patterns further demonstrate strong cross-sectoral linkages within the basin. In the Altay region, electricity demand increased by an average of 30.8 million kWh annually between 2007 and 2021, driven primarily by agricultural expansion and the mechanization of water-saving irrigation systems. During the same period, the contribution of renewable energy sources, particularly wind and solar power, increased steadily, whereas the share of hydropower declined from 17% to 7%, indicating an ongoing transition toward a lower-carbon energy structure.

In Kazakhstan, hydropower along the Irtysh River remains an important regional energy source, although the national energy mix is still dominated by fossil fuels (90%), with wind, solar, and hydropower contributing 2.1%, 1.6%, and 8.1%, respectively.67 Continued growth in electricity demand is expected to increase energy-related water consumption, particularly in downstream regions that rely heavily on hydropower generation.46,68

In addition to agricultural and energy-related water use, ecological water demand was explicitly incorporated into the assessment of upstream areas. Data from the Altay region indicate that approximately 13% of annual surface water is allocated to maintain riverine ecosystem functions between 2004 and 2021. In contrast, ecological water-use data for downstream regions in East Kazakhstan were unavailable; consequently, ecological flow requirements were not included in the downstream WFE assessment. This limitation should be acknowledged, as downstream WFE pressures may therefore be slightly underestimated.

Overall, the baseline WFE conditions demonstrate the complex interdependence among water availability, agricultural production, and energy systems across the basin. These conditions provide the contextual foundation for subsequent assessments of climate-induced resource pressures and potential adaptation pathways within the WFE nexus.

Data sources

An interdisciplinary dataset was compiled from global open-access platforms and regional statistical reports to support the multiscale assessment framework. The dataset includes climate, hydrological, agricultural, and energy-related variables, which provide essential inputs for evaluating cross-sector linkages within the WFE nexus.

Climate and hydrological data for the period 2000–2023 include monthly precipitation, temperature, potential evapotranspiration, and surface runoff. These datasets were primarily obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), with surface runoff extracted from the ERA5-Land reanalysis product. Collectively, these records provide consistent long-term observations for assessing basin-wide hydroclimatic variability. Preliminary analyses based on related hydroclimatic data were presented in a publicly available project report.37

Agricultural datasets, comprising crop types, sown areas, crop yields, and irrigation water quotas, were obtained from official public statistical yearbooks. For the Altay region of China, data were collected from the Altay Region Statistical Yearbook, the China Rural Statistical Yearbook, the Statistical Bureau of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, and the China Environmental Statistical Yearbook. Corresponding agricultural data for East Kazakhstan were retrieved from the Statistics Committee of the Republic of Kazakhstan.

Energy-related information were derived from regional agencies and publicly released annual reports. Hydropower production statistics for East Kazakhstan were sourced from Samruk Energy Annual Reports, whereas provincial energy generation data for Xinjiang Province were collected from the China Energy Statistical Yearbook.

Methods for assessing WFE nexus

Conceptual framework

This study is guided by the water–food–energy nexus framework developed under the Convention on the Protection and Use of Transboundary Watercourses and International Lakes.69 Building on this foundation, we employ a multi-scale participatory framework that integrates climate modeling, sectoral analysis, and transboundary governance to evaluate future hydrological and socio-environmental dynamics within the study regions (Figure 2).70

The framework begins with an assessment of historical and projected climatic conditions at the sub-basin scale. Future temperature and precipitation changes are analyzed using CMIP6 simulations under three Shared Socioeconomic Pathway scenarios—SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5, and SSP5–8.5—which represent low-, medium-, and high-radiative-forcing trajectories, respectively. These climate projections serve as the primary drivers of anticipated environmental change, influencing future water availability, hydrological variability, and associated socioeconomic risks.

Subsequently, sector-specific indicators related to water resources, agricultural production, and energy consumption are quantified using an index–matrix approach. This method captures sectoral exposure and sensitivity, enabling a comparative analysis of regional development pressures and potential vulnerabilities within the WFE nexus.

The framework further incorporates a transboundary governance dimension to identify cooperative pathways for enhancing adaptive capacity among riparian states. Proposed adaptation strategies emphasize climate-informed basin management, improved data-sharing mechanisms, and strengthened institutional coordination to support sustainable and equitable governance of the transboundary basin.

This multiscale assessment framework links natural hydrological dynamics with human and sectoral impacts, providing a basis for basin-scale governance and adaptation strategies. Although inconsistencies between hydrological boundaries and administrative jurisdictions remain a major challenge for integrated WFE nexus governance and resource allocation,71,72 particularly in transboundary settings, the proposed approach improves the representation of hydrological processes while facilitating cross-border comparisons that more closely reflect real-world governance and management conditions.

Climate modeling and scenario development

Climate trends were assessed using the Seasonal Mann–Kendall test (SMK) and Sen’s slope estimator, which together provide a robust evaluation of long-term changes in key climatic variables.73,74 These methods were applied to monthly temperature, precipitation, and potential evapotranspiration data to identify statistically significant trends at the sub-basin scale.

To examine the influence of climatic factors on river runoff, a multiple stepwise regression analysis was then performed at the 95% confidence level. This model quantified the relationships between river flow and selected climatic drivers, including precipitation, temperature, and potential evapotranspiration, across all delineated sub-basins of the IRB. The stepwise procedure enabled the identification of the most influential predictors of river flow while reducing potential multicollinearity among explanatory variables.

Future climatic conditions were derived from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) projections based on Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), which integrate assumptions regarding greenhouse gas emissions, land-use trajectories, and energy-system transitions.75 Three representative scenarios were further selected to capture a broad range of potential future conditions. SSP1–2.6 represents a sustainability-oriented pathway characterized by low emissions and a radiative forcing level of about 2.6 W m-2 by 2100. SSP2–4.5 represents an intermediate “middle-of-the-road” pathway, with moderate socioeconomic development and radiative forcing of approximately 4.5 W m-2. SSP5–8.5 portrays a fossil-fuel-intensive future with limited climate mitigation, leading to high emissions and radiative forcing approaching 8.5 W m-2. These scenarios enable a comparative assessment of climate-driven hydrological and socio-environmental risks under contrasting global development pathways.

CMIP6 model selection and climate data preprocessing

Five representative global climate models (GCMs) were selected: ACCESS-CM2, MIROC6, KACE-1-0-G, CESM2, and CNRM-CM6-1. For consistency across models, the first ensemble member (r1i1p1f1) was used for each model. Historical simulations for 1950–2014 and future projections under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 were included.

ERA5-Land, with a spatial resolution of 0.1 ° × 0.1 °, was used as the observational reference dataset.76 All GCM outputs were resampled to the ERA5-Land grid using bilinear interpolation and subsequently clipped to the IRB boundary to exclude grid cells outside the study area.

Temperature downscaling and validation

Temperature downscaling was performed using the Delta spatial bias-correction method, which assumes that systematic model biases remain approximately stationary over multi-decadal timescales. To evaluate the robustness and temporal transferability of the correction procedure, a split-sample test framework was applied.77

The period 1950–1984 was used for calibration. Monthly climatological bias fields were calculated from differences between the GCM simulations and ERA5-Land observations and subsequently applied to independent raw GCM simulations for 1985–2014. Downscaled temperatures during the validation period were evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE). Statistical metrics used for model evaluation are described under Quantification and Statistical Analysis.

Evaluation of raw GCM seasonal temperature climatology

To assess the ability of the uncorrected GCMs to reproduce the seasonal temperature cycle, raw GCM temperature outputs for 1995–2014 were independently evaluated. Daily temperature data were aggregated into long-term monthly climatological means for January through December and compared with ERA5-Land using Taylor diagrams.78

Evaluation of seasonal precipitation variability

Because precipitation exhibits strong spatial heterogeneity and temporal intermittency, precipitation analyses were based on the NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 high-resolution daily downscaled dataset, which has undergone bias-correction procedures.79

For validation, daily precipitation data for 1995–2014 were aggregated into long-term monthly climatological means. Model performance in reproducing seasonal precipitation variability was evaluated against ERA5-Land using Taylor diagrams.

Descriptive comparative analysis

Quantifying the WFE nexus using well-defined indicators is essential for characterizing interdependencies among nexus sectors and assessing regional sustainability pressures. The selected indicators emphasize resource availability, resource access, agricultural self-sufficiency, resource-use efficiency, and productive capacity.21

Core metrics representing water resources use, agricultural production, and energy utilization were compiled and analyzed using a descriptive comparative approach (Table 2). An index–matrix structure was eventually used to evaluate sectoral conditions and identify cross-sectoral interactions within upstream and downstream regions. This approach enables a transparent comparison of WFE nexus dynamics and supports the identification of priority areas for transboundary coordination.

Notably, the resulting hydroclimatic outputs were incorporated into the WFE nexus assessment by linking climatic stressors to irrigation demand, agricultural production, hydropower generation, and ecological water allocation. These interactions directly informed the estimation of WFE resource indicators. Building upon the climate modeling outputs, this study further demonstrates how hydroclimatic variability propagates through interconnected water, food, and energy systems and may further exacerbate upstream–downstream disparities in resource availability and management (Figure 9).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python and R, as appropriate for the specific analysis. Detailed descriptions of the statistical methods, software packages, sample sizes, and uncertainty measures are provided in this section and in the corresponding figures, figure legends, and supplemental information.

Historical hydroclimatic trend analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations of the historical hydroclimatic variables, including temperature, runoff, and precipitation, were performed in Python using the pandas, NumPy, and pyMannKendall packages. To identify and quantify long-term trends while accounting for annual seasonality, the seasonal Mann–Kendall test was applied to the monthly time-series data. Trend magnitudes were estimated using Sen’s slope estimator. For each sub-basin, the analysis included 288 monthly observations (n = 288) spanning the period from 2000 to 2023. A 12-month moving average was calculated to represent the central tendency and reduce short-term variability. The intercept of each Sen’s trend line was estimated using the median of the corresponding time series. Detailed statistical results, including the estimated slope values, are presented in Figures 3A–3C.

Stepwise regression for climatic drivers

Stepwise multiple regression analysis was performed in R to quantify the relationships between river runoff and the selected climatic drivers, including precipitation, temperature, and potential evapotranspiration. For each sub-basin, the regression model was fitted using 288 monthly observations (n = 288) spanning the period from 2000 to 2023. The statistical significance of the identified climatic drivers was evaluated using their corresponding p-values. Significance thresholds were defined as p < 0.10, p < 0.05, and p < 0.01. Complete regression results, including regression coefficients, intercepts, and the exact p-values for each sub-basin, are provided in Table S1 in supplemental information.

Climate model evaluation and projection uncertainty

All data processing and statistical analyses used to evaluate climate model performance and future hydroclimatic projections were conducted in Python using the xarray, NumPy, pandas, and scikit-learn packages. The selected CMIP6 global climate models were evaluated against the ERA5-Land dataset over the historical reference period. Similarities in spatial and temporal patterns were assessed using Taylor diagrams based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and the normalized standard deviation, calculated as the ratio of the modeled standard deviation to the observed standard deviation. The accuracy of temperature downscaling was further evaluated through cross-validation using the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2).

For the climatological evaluation, the analysis was based on 12 multiyear monthly climatological means (January–December; n = 12). For future climate projections under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios, the ensemble comprised five CMIP6 climate models (ensemble size = 5). The multi-model ensemble mean was used as the measure of central tendency, whereas the 5th and 95th percentiles of the ensemble projections were used to characterize inter-model variability and projection uncertainty, representing the central 90% ensemble interval. Specific evaluation statistics are reported in Figure S2.

Unless otherwise specified, statistical details, including sample size (n), statistical tests, and uncertainty measures, are reported in the corresponding figures, figure legends, Results, or supplemental information.
