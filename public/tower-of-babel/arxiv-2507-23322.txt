Non-equilibrium thermodynamics of the quantum Brownian motion: Anomalous non-equilibrium currents arising from complete positivity
Simone Artini, Gabriele Lo Monaco, Alberto Imparato, Mauro Paternostro, Sandro Donadi

Abstract
We rigorously analyze the non-equilibrium thermodynamic behavior of various formulations of quantum Brownian motion (QBM) using the framework of stochastic thermodynamics. While the widely used Caldeira-Leggett master equation exhibits desirable thermodynamic features — such as the fulfilment of a detailed balance — it fails to ensure complete positivity. In contrast, several completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) extensions turn out to be thermodynamically controversial. We show that such extensions introduce anomalous phase-space structures that violate detailed balance at the steady state, leading to non-vanishing entropy production and effective non-equilibrium current of unclear physical origins. Our results highlight a fundamental tension between quantum consistency and thermodynamic equilibration in open quantum systems.



The theory of Brownian motion is a cornerstone in modern physics. It played a decisive role in the consolidation of the atomic theory of matter
Einstein 1905
and, from a statistical mechanics point of view, it served as a starting point for the study of continuous Markov processes and their role in describing the mechanism that leads mesoscopic systems to thermal equilibrium, and links it to dissipation
Kubo 1966
;
Onsager 1931
.
The interaction between a particle and a thermal environment, the description of dissipation and the emergence of irreversibility are also of profound interest in the context of quantum mechanics, both from a fundamental and a technological perspective. The quantum description of Brownian motion was first introduced in the seminal work of Caldeira and Leggett
Caldeira and Leggett 1981
. Starting from a model of a bath of harmonic oscillators, they derived a master equation that correctly predicts the relaxation of the system’s state to a thermal state at the temperature of the bath. However, the dynamics described by the Caldeira-Leggett (CL) master equation are not of Lindblad form and do not preserve complete positivity (CP) (to be more precise, it does not preserve even simple positivity
Homa et al. 2019
). While the violation of CP is compatible with the approximations made in deriving the CL master equation—and thus does not lead to inconsistencies within its regime of validity—it is desirable to formulate an open quantum system dynamics that preserves complete positivity. In order to cast the master equation in Lindblad form, the addition of a term that is small in the high temperature limit is proposed
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. In order to go beyond arbitrary approaches and corrections, many works expanded on the original CL model either by relaxing the approximations, thus abandoning the Markovian framework
Hu et al. 1992
;
Hu et al. 1993
;
Vacchini and Hornberger 2007
;
Giovannetti and Vitali 2001
, or by implementing phenomenological Lindblad master equations based on global symmetries
Vacchini 2002
.

In this work, we show that the inclusion of the extra term in the CL model, while ensuring consistency with CP, implies that the steady state of the system is no longer an equilibrium one. In fact, the correction leads to the presence of persistent currents in the steady state, signaling a violation of the detailed balance condition. Thus, enforcing complete positivity introduces anomalous currents that prevent proper thermalization.
We then generalize our analysis to the most general Gaussian Lindblad master equation that is translationally covariant in space, finding again a violation of detailed balance by the steady state. Therefore, at least within the framework of Gaussian and Lindblad dynamics, the quantum description of a system coupled to a thermal bath fundamentally differs from the classical picture: preserving complete positivity—a genuinely quantum requirement—implies the presence of effective driving mechanisms that do not allow the steady state to be in true equilibrium.

General framework -

There are three, nearly equivalent, mathematical descriptions of Brownian motion: the Langevin equation, which describes the stochastic evolution of the paths of the particle by adding noise to the deterministic equations of motion; stochastic differential equations (SDE), which are
a mathematical refinement and generalization of the Langevin equation; and the Fokker-Planck equation (FP), which describes the evolution of the probability distribution of the position (and possibly momentum or velocity) of the particle
Gardiner 1985
. In this work, we focus on the latter description, and consider the Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t)=\left(-\nabla\!\cdot\!{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{B}\nabla\right)P(\bm{x},t)\,,

(1)

where
{\bm{x}}
encompasses all the degrees of freedom of the particles (positions and momenta),
\boldsymbol{A}
is the drift matrix and
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
the drift vector, which contains the deterministic motion and friction terms;
\boldsymbol{B}
is the diffusion matrix, which accounts for the stochastic part of the dynamics. The equilibrium condition is expressed by the notion of micro-reversibility in the steady state, meaning that each transition
\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\bm{x}^{\prime}
has the same probability of its time-reversed version. Time-reversal is performed via a diagonal matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}
such that
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}\bm{x}=\tilde{\bm{x}}
, with
\tilde{x}_{i}=\epsilon_{i}x_{i},\,\,\epsilon_{i}=+1
for positions and
\epsilon_{i}=-1
for momenta. Assuming the existence of a steady-state solution
P_{S}(\bm{x})
, the micro-reversibility condition holds
if and only if
Gardiner 1985

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}^{T}\bm{x}P_{S}(\bm{x})=-{\bm{A}}\bm{x}P_{S}(\bm{x})+{\bm{B}}\nabla_{\bm{x}}P_{S}(\bm{x})\,,

(2)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{B}}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}^{T}={\bm{B}},

and
P_{S}(\bm{x})
is said to satisfy the
detailed balance
(DB) condition. A thermodynamic theory of Brownian motion, known as stochastic thermodynamics
Seifert 2012
;
Van den Broeck 2013
, has also been developed by interpreting usual thermodynamic quantities -namely entropy, work and heat- as random variables of the trajectories of the particle. In particular, this approach helped expanding thermodynamics to nonequilibrium systems and shed light onto the emergence of irreversibility and its connection to entropy
Seifert 2008
;
Tomé and de Oliveira 2010
;
Imparato and Peliti 2007
. This approach usually roots itself in the SDE description of Brownian motion, but the FP approach can also be used to find the average of the thermodynamic quantities defined on trajectories. The main quantity of interest is the entropy production rate, which, in many relevant scenarios, uniquely characterizes the distance from equilibrium
Campisi et al. 2011
. Starting from the Shannon entropy of the probability distribution
S(t)=-\int\,d\bm{x}P(\bm{x},t)\ln P(\bm{x},t)
, one can split the entropy rate of the system as

\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=\Pi(t)-\Phi(t),

(3)

where
\Phi(t)
quantifies the rate at which entropy flows between system and environment, and
\Pi(t)
is the entropy production rate, which characterizes thermodynamic irreversibility
Landi and Paternostro 2021
. Such splitting is usually performed by separating the drift and diffusion matrices into symmetric and antisymmetric components with respect to time inversion, thus identifying the irreversible and reversible parts of the current, respectively. The entropy production rate is then identified with the term that is non-negative or null if and only if
P_{S}
satisfies the DB condition, provided the diffusion has no odd component with respect to time inversion. This is generally written as

\Pi=2\int\,d\bm{x}\,\,\frac{J^{I}(\bm{x})\,({\bm{B}}^{I})^{-1}J^{I}(\bm{x})}{P(\bm{x})}\,,

(4)

J^{I}(\bm{x})
being the irreversible part of the current. This expression provides the entropy production rate averaged over the ensemble of stochastic individual trajectories of the dynamics, thus establishing a link with well-known fluctuation relations
Gardiner 1985
;
Seifert 2008
;
Van den Broeck 2013
;
Seifert 2012
;
Tomé and de Oliveira 2010
.

In this paper we focus on exploring the relation between the DB condition and the irreversible currents in the stationary state. It is well known that, when the DB conditions hold, the stationary state exists, is unique and the total current vanishes
Van Kampen 1992
. In particular, the irreversible part vanishes as well. However, the absence of an irreversible current in the steady state does not imply DB, as shown in the following theorem (we offer a proof in
appendix

A
):

Theorem
.

For a homogeneous Fokker-Planck equation such that

{\mathcal{E}}{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{E}}^{T}={\bm{B}}
, if a stationary solution
P_{S}(\bm{x})
exists and is such that the irreversible component of the current is zero, then the DB conditions hold.

These considerations justify the choice of the entropy production rate as the figure of merit to characterize nonequilibrium when the hypothesis of this Theorem is satisfied. On the other hand, if there is a reversible component in the diffusion matrix, a zero irreversible current – and consequently a zero entropy production rate – does not guarantee DB in the steady state due to the possible onset of reversible currents.

Until now we discussed in the framework of classical thermodynamics, however our goal is to study models describing the Quantum Brownian Motion (QBM)
Caldeira and Leggett 1981
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. Restricting the study to potentials that are at most quadratic in the system’s degrees of freedom, and considering only states with positive Wigner function, we can apply the same construction of classical continuous Markov processes to build the stochastic thermodynamics of some of the incarnations of the QBM model. To provide our quantitative assessment, we replace the Shannon entropy
S(t)
with the Wigner entropy

S_{W}(t)=-\int\,d\bm{x}\,W_{\hat{\rho}}(\bm{x},t)\ln(W_{\hat{\rho}}(\bm x,t))

(5)

as the main figure of merit to characterize the thermodynamic properties of the considered models
Brunelli et al. 2018
;
Brunelli and Paternostro 2016
;
Santos et al. 2017
;
Santos et al. 2018
;
Artini and Paternostro 2023
;
Artini et al. 2025
(cf. Ref.
Colla and Breuer 2021
for a thermodynamic description of QBM based, instead, on the von Neumann entropy). Here,
W_{\hat{\rho}}(\bm{x},t)
is the instantaneous Wigner function associated with the state
\hat{\rho}
of a system.

Using this framework, we can identify anomalous terms in phase-space, with respect to classical Brownian motion, whose origin stems in the complete positivity requirement, and characterize their effects on the thermodynamics of the stationary state. In particular, we are able to demonstrate that all space covariant and completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) quadratic master equations lead to nonequilibrium steady states characterized by a constant entropy production rate or a reversible nonequilibrium current. As a consequence, thermalization, as intended classically i.e. satisfying the DB in
eq.

2
, cannot be achieved with these theories.

The CL master equation -
The master equation obtained in the weak-coupling and high-temperatures limit of the CL model reads

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]-\frac{\eta m}{\hbar^{2}\beta}[\hat{q},[\hat{q},\hat{\rho}]]-\frac{i\eta}{2\hbar}[\hat{q},\{\hat{p},\hat{\rho}\}].

(6)

Using the mapping rules summarized in
appendix

B
, this can be cast into the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial W_{\hat{\rho}}}{\partial t}=\bigl\{W_{\hat{H}},W_{\hat{\rho}}\bigr\}_{*}+\eta\frac{\partial}{\partial p}(pW_{\hat{\rho}})+D_{pp}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}W_{\hat{\rho}}

(7)

with
D_{pp}=m\eta/\beta
. The Moyal brackets
\{W_{\hat{A}},W_{\hat{B}}\}_{*}:=2W_{\hat{A}}\sin\small(\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{IJ}\overset{\leftarrow}{\partial_{I}}\overset{\rightarrow}{\partial_{J}})W_{\hat{B}}
originate from the unitary part of the evolution
Baker Jr 1958
;
Zachos et al. 2005
,

\Omega=i\sigma_{y}

being the symplectic matrix. In general, the unitary term can lead to higher-than-second order derivatives in the partial differential equation for the Wigner function, invalidating an attempt to perform an analysis along the lines of classical Markov processes. For this reason, our work focuses to the case of potentials that are at most quadratic in position and momentum, in which case the Moyal brackets and the Poisson brackets coincide. Under this assumption, the equation for the Wigner function has the same functional form of the Klein-Kramers equation
Kramers 1940
of classical Brownian motion. Thus, just like the classical case, the asymptotic state is of the Gibbs form, and the entropy production rate decreases in time, reaching zero asymptotically: the system reaches a true thermal equilibrium. Needless to say, this is consistent with the results obtained through the quantum master equation and could corroborate it as a good candidate for a model of thermalization of open quantum systems.

Despite having the desired thermodynamic features,
eq.

6
does not describe a proper quantum evolution as it is not completely positive. The addition of a
minimally invasive
(
i.e.
small in the high temperature limit) term such as
-\frac{\eta\beta}{16m}[\hat{p},[\hat{p},\hat{\rho}]]
has been proposed to ensure positivity, at the cost of having a stationary state that slightly deviates from a thermal one. From the phase-space perspective, the corresponding modified FP equation reads (we suppress the arguments of the Wigner function for easiness of notation)

\frac{\partial W_{\hat{\rho}}}{\partial t}=\bigl\{W_{\hat{H}},W_{\hat{\rho}}\bigr\}_{*}+\eta\frac{\partial(pW_{\hat{\rho}})}{\partial p}+D_{pp}\frac{\partial^{2}W_{\hat{\rho}}}{\partial p^{2}}+D_{qq}\frac{\partial^{2}W_{\hat{\rho}}}{\partial q^{2}},

(8)

with
D_{qq}=\frac{\hbar^{2}\eta\beta}{16m}
being the diffusion constant in the position variable. The additional term
D_{qq}\partial^{2}_{q}W_{\hat{\rho}}
keeps the system out of equilibrium and indeed leads to a violation of DB. With reference to
eq.

18
, in this model one has
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{m}\\
-m\omega^{2}&-\eta\end{pmatrix}
and
B=\begin{pmatrix}2D_{qq}&0\\
0&2D_{pp}\end{pmatrix}
in the case of an harmonic oscillator. Although the second condition in
eq.

18
is trivially satisfied, the first constraint – when considering at most quadratic potentials – leads to the following equations

\displaystyle\dfrac{p}{m}W_{S}=\biggl(\dfrac{p}{m}-2D_{qq}\partial_{q}\biggr)W_{S}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle(V^{\prime}(q)-\eta p)W_{S}=(V^{\prime}(q)+\eta p+2D_{pp}\partial_{p})W_{S}\,,

whose solution
W_{S}=\mathcal{N}_{0}e^{-\frac{\beta p^{2}}{2m}}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{0}
is a constant normalization, does not satisfy the stationary Fokker-Planck equation, which requires a dependence on the position.

To quantify the degree at which micro-reversibility is broken, we compute the entropy production rate following the procedure reported in
appendix

A
. The reversible part of the current gives zero contribution to the rate of the total entropy
dS(t)/dt
; the irreversible current has two components
J_{q}[W]=-D_{qq}\partial_{q}W
and
J_{p}[W]=-\eta pW-D_{pp}\partial_{p}W
, yielding the following form for the entropy flux and production rates

\Phi{=}-\frac{\eta}{D_{pp}}\int\,dq\,dp\,pJ_{p},\penalty\ \penalty\ \Pi{=}\int\,dq\,dp\left[\sum_{k=q,p}\frac{J_{k}^{2}}{D_{kk}W_{\hat{\rho}}}\right].

(10)

The steady-state entropy production rate can be easily obtained by substituting the steady-state Wigner function in the expressions for the current. The former is a Gaussian with zero mean value and covariance matrix
V_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}_{q,\infty}&\sigma_{pq,\infty}\\
\sigma_{pq,\infty}&\sigma^{2}_{p,\infty}\end{pmatrix}
where, in the case of an harmonic oscillator, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{q,\infty}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\eta D_{qq}}{\omega^{2}}+\dfrac{D_{qq}}{\eta}+\dfrac{D_{pp}}{\eta(m\omega)^{2}},\,\sigma_{pq,\infty}=-2mD_{qq},

(11)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{p,\infty}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(m\omega)^{2}D_{qq}}{\eta}+\dfrac{D_{pp}}{\eta}.

Noting that the mean values of
q
and
p
are always null, we readily find

\displaystyle\Pi_{\infty}=D_{qq}\eta m^{2}\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{D_{pp}}+\frac{3D_{qq}\eta^{2}m^{2}}{{\cal D}}\right)

(12)

with
{\cal D}=(D_{pp}+D_{qq}m^{2}\omega^{2})^{2}+D_{qq}m^{2}\eta^{2}(D_{pp}-3D_{qq}m^{2}\omega^{2})
.
As expected,
eq.

12
vanishes when
D_{qq}=0
.
In summary, due to the presence of the extra term that guarantees the positivity of the quantum map, in the steady state of the particle a spontaneous constant dissipation appears, keeping the system out–of–equilibrium. This diffusive term in the FP equation (
8
) can be associated with additional noise with variance
D_{qq}
acting only on the position degree of freedom, and not subject to a fluctuation–dissipation relation and thus preventing the system from reaching thermal equilibrium with the bath at temperature
1/\beta
.

Translation-covariant master equation -
We now consider the most general Lindblad dynamics that is bilinear in
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
, introduced and discussed in detail in Ref.
Vacchini 2002
, to show how such anomalous DB breaking emerges in the most general model as well. The master equation for the dynamics at hand can be cast in the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL)
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
form

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}{=}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\!\left[\hat{H}{+}\frac{\eta}{4}\{\hat{q},\hat{p}\},\hat{\rho}\right]+-\frac{i}{2\hbar}(\mu-2\eta)[\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\},\hat{\rho}]+

(13)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j}\Gamma^{ij}\!\left(\!\hat{X}_{i}\hat{\rho}\,\hat{X}_{j}^{\dagger}{-}\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{X}_{i},\hat{\rho}\}\!\right)\!\!,

where we have introduced the flipped coordinates
\hat{X}_{1}=\hat{p}
,
\hat{X}_{2}=-\hat{q}

and the matrix
\Gamma
as

\Gamma=\frac{2}{\hbar^{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}D_{qq}&D_{qp}-i\frac{\eta}{4}\\
D_{qp}+i\frac{\eta}{4}&\frac{\eta m}{\beta}\end{matrix}\right)\,.

(14)

Here,
\eta
,
\mu
and
D_{qp}
are free parameters, while
D_{qq}=\biggl(\dfrac{\hbar^{2}\eta\beta}{16m}+\dfrac{\beta D_{qp}^{2}}{\eta m}\biggr)
. In order to get a more tractable and instructive scenario, we choose
\mu=2\eta
. This choice cancels the second Hamiltonian term in
eq.

13
, making the Lindbladian translational-covariant
Vacchini 2002
. As such a term contributes to the reversible part of the drift matrix, the non-equilibrium behavior is the same for the two models and thus the conclusions will retain their general validity. Moving to phase space, we now face an additional cross-diffusion term described by
2D_{qp}\partial_{p}\partial_{q}W
. It is particularly easy to see that no steady-state can satisfy DB for this equation. To this end, it is sufficient to consider the diffusion matrix, which for this model takes the form
{\bm{B}}=\hbar^{2}\,{\rm Re}\,\Gamma
and realize that the condition
\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}B_{i,j}(\bm{\epsilon}\bm{x})=B_{ij}(\bm{x})
in
eq.

18
is violated. Indeed, as the diffusion matrix is constant, such requirement may hold only when the off-diagonal elements are null. This time-symmetric component of diffusion actually contributes to the reversible part of the current. Therefore, the functional form of the entropy production rate
\Pi
is the same as in
eq.

10
with
D_{qq}
defined as below
eq.

14
. Instead the entropy flux rate reads

\Phi=-\int\,dq\,dp\,\biggl[\frac{\eta}{D_{pp}}pJ_{p}+2D_{qp}\frac{\partial_{q}W_{\hat{\rho}}\partial_{p}W_{\hat{\rho}}}{W_{\hat{\rho}}}\biggr].

(15)

In order for
eq.

13
to preserve its translational-covariance, the unitary part must have the same symmetry, thus implying that the system must be free. In such a case, the stationary solution is unique and takes the expected canonical form
\hat{\rho}_{th}=\exp(-\beta\hat p^2/2m)/\mathcal{Z}
(with
\mathcal{Z}
the partition function). Any other Hamiltonian does not guarantee uniqueness of the steady-state solution
Vacchini 2002
. In this case, the steady-state entropy production rate is zero if the Einstein relation
D_{pp}=m\eta/\beta
holds, as in the Klein-Kramers equation. However, even if we impose this condition – thus achieving zero entropy production rate in the steady state – the stationary solution still would not satisfy the DB conditions due to the presence of the reversible diffusion introduced by the cross derivative (the term proportional to
D_{qp}
). So, although no dissipation is present, the stationary state is still driven out-of-equilibrium by another physical mechanism. This is usually associated to dynamical activity in active matter systems and other nonequilibrium phenomena, and is referred to as
frenesy
in the literature (cf. Ref.
Maes 2020
;
Basu and Maes 2015
for an overview).

Conclusions -
We have shown that the master equation derived from the original CL model - within its approximations - is thermodynamically valid as the steady-state of the dynamics is thermal. However, this feature is associated to a quantum master equation that does not preserve positivity, and hence requires corrections. On the other hand, in general, a Gaussian theory of QBM cannot lead to proper thermalization in the sense that the DB is always violated. This is due to the effects of anomalous currents in phase-space that drive the system out of equilibrium, either via an indefinite heating mechanism or in light of a dynamical, i.e. time-reversible, nonequilibrium contribution. Exploring non-Gaussian dynamics might thus be useful in the derivation of a QBM theory that is consistent from both a thermodynamic and quantum mechanical point of view. In order to study such models, however, one would need to resort to the fully quantum generalization of DB
Agarwal 1973
;
Alicki 1976
, and generalize the construction of the entropy production rate to states with negative Wigner functions as well. This could be done, for example, using the Wehrl entropy
Zicari et al. 2023
;
Artini et al. 2025
, which is always well defined, thus fully leaving the classical framework. The assessment of non-Markovian effects is a second important line of investigation. A potential approach would be to consider non-Markovian master equations derived from the microscopic dynamics without approximation, such as the one introduced by Hu, Paz and Zhang
Hu et al. 1992
, whose implications in the context addressed in this work remain fully unexplored.

Appendix A
Detailed Balance and stochastic thermodynamics

We construct the entropy production rate starting from a general Fokker-Planck equation and leveraging arguments based on time-reversal symmetries. We also show the connection between this quantity and micro-reversibility and identify the cases when it ceases to be the sole quantity that characterizes deviations from equilibrium.

Consider the general time-homogeneous Fokker-Planck equation for the evolution of the probability density function of a
N-
dimensional Markovian stochastic process without jumps

\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t)=\left(-\nabla\!\cdot\!{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\!\cdot\!{\bm{B}}(\bm{x})\nabla\right)P(\bm{x},t)\,,

(16)

where
{\bm{A}}(\bm{x})
is the drift vector and
{\bm{B}}(\bm{x})
is the diffusion matrix and assume there exists a stationary solution
P_{S}(\bm{x})
for such a process. Assume that these variables transform under the action of the time-reversal operator
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}=\textit{diag}(\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{N})
as
x_{i}\rightarrow\epsilon_{i}x_{i}
,
\epsilon_{i}=\pm 1
,
i=1,..,N
. Typically, one deals with position and momentum variables for which
\epsilon_{i}=1
and
\epsilon_{j}=-1
, respectively. Then, the stationary state is said to satisfy the DB
condition
if the micro-reversibility relation holds

P(\bm{x},\tau|\bm{x}^{\prime},0)P_{S}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=P(\bm{\mathcal{E}}\bm{x}^{\prime},\tau|\bm{\mathcal{E}}\bm{x},0)P_{S}(\bm{x})\,,

(17)

P(\bm{x},\tau|\bm{x}^{\prime},0)
being the conditional probability of finding the system in the state
x
at time
t
, given it was observed in position
x^{\prime}
at the initial time. It can be shown (cf. Ref.
Gardiner 1985
, for instance), that the necessary and sufficient conditions for DB to hold are

\begin{cases}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{A}}(\bm{\mathcal{E}}\bm{x})P_{S}(\bm{x})=-{\bm{A}}(\bm{x})P_{S}(\bm{x})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}({\bm{B}}(\bm{x})P_{S}(\bm{x})),\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{B}}(\bm{\mathcal{E}}\bm{x}){\bm{\mathcal{E}}}^{T}={\bm{B}}(\bm{x}).\end{cases}

(18)

In the following, we restrict to the case of (
i
) homogeneous diffusion matrix,
i.e.

{\bm{B}}(\bm{x})=\bm{B}
, with
\bm{B}
a constant symmetric matrix and (
ii
) drift vector linear in the coordinates,
{\bm{A}}(\bm{x})=\bm{A}\bm{x}
, the drift matrix
\bm{A}
being constant.
To get a thermodynamic description of the system, we start by writing
eq.

16
as a continuity equation. We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t)=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot J(\bm{x},t),

(19)

\displaystyle J(\bm{x},t)={\bm{A}}\bm{x}P(\bm{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t).

We can split the current into its time-symmetric component
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}J^{I}(\mathcal{E}\bm{x})=J^{I}(\bm{x})
and anti-symmetric one
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}J^{R}(\mathcal{E}\bm{x})=-J^{R}(\bm{x})
. We also define the reversible and irreversible components of a matrix
\bm{M}
as
{\bm{M}}^{R}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{M}}-{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{M}}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}^{T})
and
{\bm{M}}^{I}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{M}}+{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}{\bm{M}}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}^{T})
. Using this notation, the two components of the current are

\left\{\begin{matrix}J^{R}(\bm{x},t)={\bm{A}}^{R}\bm{x}P(\bm{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}^{R}\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t)\,,\\
J^{I}(\bm{x},t)={\bm{A}}^{I}\bm{x}P(\bm{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}^{I}\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t)\,.\end{matrix}\right.

(20)

We are interested in the rate
\dot{S}(t)
of the Shannon entropy of the system, where
S(t)=-\int\,d\bm{x}P(\bm{x},t)\ln(P(\bm x,t))
. Using
eq.

19
and integrating by parts (assuming vanishing probability distributions and currents at spatial infinity) we have

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\int d\bm{x}J(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln(P(\bm x))

(21)

\displaystyle=-\int d\bm{x}J^{R}(\bm{x})\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x})}{P(\bm{x})}-\int d\bm{x}J^{I}(\bm{x})\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x})}{P(\bm{x})},

where we understood the dependence on
t
on the right hand side.
In the first integral, we can substitute the definition of the reversible current, observing that the reversible part only contributes through its trace, which vanished since, by construction, the diagonal elements of
{\bm{A}}^{R}
are zero. In the second integral, we perform the substitution
\nabla_{\bm{x}}P=2P({\bm{B}}^{I})^{-1}{\bm{A}}^{I}\bm{x}-2({\bm{B}}^{I})^{-1}J^{I}
, finally getting the following expression of the rate of the Shannon entropy

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}=
