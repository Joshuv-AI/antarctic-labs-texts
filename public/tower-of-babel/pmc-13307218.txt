Quadriceps Injuries in Major League Soccer: A 10‐Year Analysis of Injury Rate, Return‐to‐Play, and Performance Metrics
Authors: Brian Forsythe, Catherine Hand, Camden Bohn, Daanish Khazi‐Syed, Joshua Chang, Francis Hand, Harmanjeet Singh, Andrew Savoia, Jonathan Spaan, Eric Giza, Margot Putukian, Bert R. Mandelbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate quadriceps injury rates, return‐to‐play (RTP) durations, and postinjury performance in Major League Soccer (MLS) athletes with quadriceps injuries.MethodsData from MLS players with quadriceps injuries (2010‐2021) were analyzed using the MLS Injury Surveillance database. Injury rates, RTP times, and reinjury rates were compared across 2 periods (2010‐2015 vs 2016‐2021). Uninjured players matched 2:1 by position, age, and experience served as controls. Postinjury performance metrics including games played, minutes played, goals, assists, and clean sheets were compared longitudinally across 3 seasons.ResultsA total of 1550 MLS players were included. The average age of injured players was 25.57 ± 4.03 years. Injury rates slightly decreased from 7.70% (2010‐2015) to 6.51% (2016‐2021, P = .111). RTP times significantly increased from 14.4 to 23.0 days (P < .001). Reinjury rates declined from 5.08% (2010‐2015) to 0.82% (2016‐2021) (P = .098). RTP after reinjury was longer in 2016‐2021 but nonsignificant (P = .647). Game‐related injuries had shorter RTP (16.4 vs 20.7 days, P = .030). Acute contact injuries resulted in shorter RTP (9.55 days) compared with noncontact (27.0 days) or chronic injuries (16.2 days, P < .001). Weather at the time of injury influenced RTP, with shorter durations in rainy/snowy conditions (11.0 vs 18.75 days, P < .0001). Field type had no significant impact (P = .361). Defenders experienced significant declines in minutes played per season and at 3 years after injury (P = .002, P = .004, respectively). No significant differences were found in goals, assists, or clean sheets.ConclusionsIn this study, reductions in injury and reinjury rates were not statistically significant. Longer RTP durations may reflect a cautious recovery approach or may also reflect end‐of‐season timing or off‐season transitions. Position‐specific rehabilitation, particularly for defenders, may enhance outcomes.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.

The quadriceps muscle group, comprising the rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and vastus intermedius, is critical to the biomechanical demands of professional athletes, especially in sports requiring explosive movements and high‐intensity activities. 1 In Major League Soccer (MLS), rapid accelerations, decelerations, directional changes, and repetitive kicking increase players’ susceptibility to quadriceps injuries, 2 ranging from minor strains to severe tears that significantly impact rehabilitation, career longevity, and performance. 3

Quadriceps injuries are among the most common muscle injuries in professional soccer, contributing substantially to time‐loss injuries in MLS athletes. The high‐intensity nature of the sport highlights the importance of effective injury prevention and management to minimize downtime and optimize outcomes. 4 Despite advancements in treatment protocols, return to play (RTP) remains a complex process with significant variability in recovery, underscoring the need for individualized rehabilitation strategies to ensure a safe return to preinjury performance. 5

Although research has extensively examined lower limb injury epidemiology and risk factors in professional soccer, 6 , 7 the specifics of RTP after quadriceps injuries remain underexplored. 8 , 9 Findings from RTP studies in other sports, such as the National Basketball Association, are limited in applicability due to soccer's unique biomechanical demands. 10 , 11 This study addresses this gap by analyzing RTP timing in MLS players with quadriceps injuries and comparing their postinjury performance to matched uninjured controls. For the purpose of this study, a quadriceps injury was defined as any strain or tear to the quadriceps muscle group that required medical intervention and resulted in time loss. The purpose of this study was to evaluate injury rates, RTP duration, and postinjury performance among MLS athletes with quadriceps injuries. We hypothesized that players returning to play will achieve performance metrics comparable to uninjured counterparts within 2 years.

METHODS

This retrospective cohort study analyzed MLS players with quadriceps injuries from 2010 to 2021. Quadriceps injuries were defined as those requiring medical intervention and affecting the quadriceps muscle group, identified via the MLS Injury Surveillance database and validated with public data from mlssoccer.com. Two periods, 2010‐2015 and 2016‐2021, were compared to assess injury rates, RTP durations, and reinjury rates.

Injured players were identified from the MLS Injury Surveillance database, requiring at least 1 full MLS season before and after injury for inclusion. Reinjuries were counted once per player. Matched noninjured controls were selected in a 2:1 ratio, based on position, age (±2 years), MLS experience (±1 year), and season (±1 year). The final cohort included 1550 injured players with matched controls. Two independent reviewers (F.H., C.H.) ensured data accuracy.

Demographic, injury, and performance data were extracted from MLS records. Demographics included age, position, team, and MLS experience. Injury details captured year, surface type (grass vs turf), context (match vs training), location (home vs away), age at injury, weather conditions, and RTP date. Performance metrics before and after injury included games played, games started, minutes, goals, assists, pass accuracy, interceptions, clean sheets, and goals conceded. Injured players were identified through the MLS Injury Surveillance database, which includes injury‐specific details such as laterality, timing, and injury onset. However, performance metrics (e.g., games played, minutes, goals, assists, clean sheets) are not recorded in the surveillance database. Therefore, to evaluate postinjury performance, we used publicly available data from MLSsoccer.com. This combined methodology has been used in prior MLS injury studies and ensures comprehensive analysis of both clinical and performance‐related outcomes. Delta values were calculated as the difference between each year of interest and the year prior to injury (Δ = Year_X − Preinjury Year), allowing assessment of longitudinal performance changes relative to baseline.

Injury type was variable recorded in the surveillance database, often described nonspecifically as “pain,” “tightness,” or “contusion.” Only 1 complete quadriceps rupture was documented. Severity labels such as “mild,” “moderate,” or “severe” were available in some cases but were absent or listed as “unknown” in more than one‐third of entries. Treatment protocol details were limited; most entries stated “referred to team physician” without additional information on whether surgery, physical therapy, or other interventions were used. Rehabilitation protocol, therapy type, and PRP use were not documented. As such, detailed evaluation of treatment influence on outcomes was not possible.

RTP was defined as days from injury to competitive play. Reinjuries were defined as a subsequent quadriceps injury occurring within 2 months of RTP. If a player sustained multiple qualifying reinjuries in different seasons, each episode was counted as a distinct reinjury event. Reinjury rates, based on Ekstrand's definition, reflected the percentage of players with a second quadriceps injury within 2 months. Injury rates were calculated annually as the proportion of unique injured players to total MLS players, with averages computed for each cohort. RTP durations represented the mean days missed per cohort, with comparisons between periods.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analyses compared injury rates, RTP durations, and reinjury rates between the 2 periods. Independent t‐tests assessed performance metric differences between injured players and controls. Results were expressed as means ± standard deviation, with significance set at P < .05. Analyses were performed using STATA 17 (StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. StataCorp LLC, 2021).

RESULTS

A total of 1550 MLS players with quadriceps injuries (2010‐2021) were analyzed and compared with matched noninjured controls. Uninjured players were matched 2:1 to injured players based on age (±2 years), position, MLS experience (±1 year), and season (±1 year) to serve as controls. This matching approach ensured comparability between groups when assessing postinjury performance differences. The mean age at injury was 25.57 ± 4.03 years. Midfielders represented the largest group of injured players (29.4%), followed by defenders (24.1%), forwards (16.1%), and goalkeepers (8.7%). RTP durations significantly increased from 14.4 days (2010‐2015) to 23.0 days (2016‐2021, P < .001). Field type did not significantly affect RTP (P = .361). Similarly, positions showed no significant RTP differences (P = .573).

Competitive setting significantly influenced RTP, with game‐related injuries resulting in shorter recovery (16.4 days) than practice‐related injuries (20.7 days, P = .0299). Injury onset was also a key factor: acute contact injuries had the shortest RTP (9.55 days), compared with acute noncontact (27.0 days) and chronic/gradual noncontact injuries (16.2 days, P < .001).

Field location and player age did not significantly impact RTP (P = .341; and .251, respectively). Time of injury during a game also showed no significant effect (P = .379).

Weather conditions were a significant factor, with sunny/clear injuries averaging 18.75 days, whereas rainy/snowy conditions had shorter RTP (11.0 days, P < .001). Although rainy or snowy conditions were associated with shorter RTP durations, these findings are exploratory and should not be interpreted as implying a protective or causal effect of weather. DISCUSSION

This study provides key insights into the epidemiology and outcomes of quadriceps injuries in MLS players, highlighting 3 injury rates, RTP durations, and reinjury rates across 2010‐2015 and 2016‐2021. Although the reduction in injury rates from 7.70% to 6.51% was not statistically significant, it may suggest potential advancements in injury prevention strategies and player care. Similarly, the minor increase in average RTP duration (12.6‐14.9 days) was not clinically relevant but may indicate a shift toward a more cautious recovery approach prioritizing player safety and long‐term health. In addition, weather was included as an exploratory variable based on its availability in the dataset. Although injuries occurring in rainy or snowy conditions were associated with shorter RTP, this is likely confounded by other unmeasured factors such as play intensity, injury mechanism, or sample size variability and these data should not be interpreted as suggesting that these conditions are inherently safer. Performance metrics showed no significant differences between injured players and matched uninjured controls for most positions; however, defenders experienced a significant long‐term decline in minutes played and assists 3 years after injury. These findings support the potential benefit of continued refinement of RTP protocols, particularly for position‐specific recovery strategies.

Reinjury rates decreased from 5.08% to 0.82% (P = .098). This decline likely results from enhanced rehabilitation protocols, stricter adherence to RTP guidelines, and improved player monitoring, aligning with literature supporting individualized recovery plans and robust injury surveillance systems. 12 , 13 , 14 The longer RTP following reinjury in 2016‐2021 (38.0 days) compared with 2010‐2015 (24.4 days, P = .647) may reflect more severe injuries or more conservative recovery strategies after initial failures, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive postrecovery monitoring. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 The absence of detailed rehabilitation data, including adherence and recovery adjustments, restricts insights into interventions affecting reinjury rates or RTP durations, limiting the findings’ applicability for refining clinical practices.

Performance metrics showed no significant differences for most positions, except for defenders, who exhibited marked declines in minutes played and assists 3 years after injury. The physically demanding nature of their role, requiring frequent tackling, sprinting, and rapid transitions, underscores the need for tailored recovery protocols to restore their ability to meet high‐intensity demands. 20 , 21 , 22 Prolonged recovery in noncontact injuries, often indicative of more severe or complex damage, highlights the necessity of meticulous and individualized rehabilitation approaches. 22 Midfielders, known for their high‐intensity workloads, had the longest RTP durations, although differences were not statistically significant.

Competitive settings significantly influenced RTP, with practice injuries requiring longer recovery (20.7 vs 16.4 days, P = .03). This may stem from less immediate intervention or a more cautious recovery approach during training, whereas game injuries often receive expedited treatment under competitive pressures. 23 Goalkeepers exhibited minimal performance impact, likely due to the unique demands of their role, which involve less continuous quadriceps strain and running compared with outfield players. 24 , 25

These findings align with prior studies, including those by Eckard et al. 8 and Ekstrand et al., 7 which emphasize the benefits of early intervention, individualized rehabilitation, and advancements in injury prevention. Observations from the UEFA Injury Study further reinforce the need for tailored recovery strategies for defenders, whose roles demand significant physicality and endurance. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of refining RTP protocols to optimize recovery, minimize reinjury risk, and ensure long‐term athletic performance and career sustainability in elite soccer players. By addressing the unique demands of each position and integrating individualized care, these strategies can enhance both player outcomes and team success.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it depends on the accuracy of the MLS Injury Surveillance database, which may introduce biases from inconsistent injury classification or underreporting of minor injuries, potentially underestimating injury and reinjury rates. RTP duration, although consistent, does not capture peak performance readiness or compensatory adaptations, underscoring the need for comprehensive recovery assessments. RTP calculations may be artificially prolonged in cases where injuries occurred near the end of the competitive season or during off‐season periods. Injury rates were not adjusted for player exposure, such as minutes played or training hours, limiting precision in incidence estimates. However, by analyzing annual unique injuries and accounting for the league's expanding player pool, this study captures data over time. Additionally, the surveillance dataset lacked comprehensive data on injury severity and treatment course. Although some severity descriptors were included, more than one‐third of injuries had missing or nonspecific labels. Treatment documentation was minimal and typically limited to referral to a team physician. Use of physical therapy, surgical intervention, or other rehabilitation strategies was not captured. These omissions limit our ability to assess how severity or treatment modality influenced RTP duration or performance. Lastly, results are specific to MLS and may not generalize to other leagues or sports due to differences in physical demands, travel, medical access, and injury management protocols, reducing external validity.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, reductions in injury and reinjury rates were not statistically significant. Longer RTP durations may reflect a cautious recovery approach or may also reflect end‐of‐season timing or off‐season transitions. Position‐specific rehabilitation, particularly for defenders, may enhance outcomes.
