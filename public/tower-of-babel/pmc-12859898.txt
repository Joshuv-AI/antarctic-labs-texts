The mediating role of thriving at work between emotional labor and presenteeism: a comparison between psychiatric nurses with and without sleep disturbances
Authors: Jianing Li, Xiaolian Li, Yaoying Chen, Mei Chen, Wen Shi, Huijun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmotional labor is a significant component of healthcare work, yet the mechanisms linking it to presenteeism are not fully understood. Existing research has centered on negative mediating pathways, leaving the potential role of the positive psychological state of thriving at work underexplored. This study aimed to examine the mediating role of thriving at work in the relationship between emotional labor and presenteeism, and to compare this pathway between psychiatric nurses with and without sleep disturbances.MethodsA multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted using convenience sampling among 1,311 psychiatric shift-work nurses from 19 specialized hospitals in Fujian Province, China, between January and March 2025. Participants completed the Emotional Labor Scale, Stanford Presenteeism Scale, Thriving at Work Scale, and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Mediation analysis was performed using structural equation modeling with bootstrap estimation.ResultsIn the overall sample, thriving at work partially mediated the positive associations of surface acting (β = 0.122, p < 0.001) and emotional display rules (β = 0.063, p < 0.001) with presenteeism, accounting for 36.5% and 28.0% of the total effects, respectively. By contrast, deep acting exerted a significant negative indirect effect on presenteeism through thriving at work (β = -0.118, p < 0.001). When stratified by sleep quality, the indirect effect of surface acting accounted for 45.7% of the total effect in the sleep disturbance group (vs. 19.9% in the no sleep disturbance group), while the mediated proportion of emotional display rules reached 34.1% (vs. 16.1% in the no sleep disturbance group).ConclusionThriving at work mediates the relationship between emotional labor (particularly surface acting and emotional display rules) and presenteeism among psychiatric nurses. Sleep disturbances significantly amplify this mediating pathway, indicating that sleep quality is a crucial boundary condition for this psychological mechanism. Interventions aimed at enhancing thriving at work and improving sleep quality may help mitigate the negative consequences of emotional labor.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

With mental health emerging as an increasingly prominent focus of national health strategies, psychiatric units are confronted with heightened service expectations, which intensifies the emotional regulation demands placed on nurses. Psychiatric nursing is characterized by intense and continuous emotional demands, arising from exposure to patient aggression, emotional volatility, and suicidality [1–4]. Unlike general medical settings, psychiatric nurses must frequently engage in de-escalation, therapeutic communication, and real-time risk assessment, making emotional regulation a central component of their work. This persistent exposure renders psychiatric nursing uniquely emotionally demanding within healthcare settings [5], increasing nurses’ risk of emotional exhaustion and work-related impairment.

Within this context, presenteeism—defined as attending work while physically or psychologically unwell, accompanied by reduced productivity—has emerged as a critical occupational health concern [6]. Compared with absenteeism, presenteeism is less visible yet more pervasive, with prevalence among nurses reported as high as 49.2% [7]. Given the centrality of emotional engagement in psychiatric care, these heightened demands may further increase nurses’ vulnerability to presenteeism, which not only compromises care quality and patient safety [8, 9] but also incurs substantial economic costs [10]. Thus, identifying the antecedents of presenteeism among psychiatric nurses represents a timely research priority.

A key antecedent is emotional labor, defined as the process through which employees regulate their behaviors, facial expressions, and emotions to align with organizational norms [11]. Emotional labor typically encompasses surface acting, deep acting, and emotional display rules. Surface acting, the modification of external expressions without altering inner feelings, has consistently been shown to elicit emotional dissonance, resource depletion, and stress-related outcomes [5, 12]. In contrast, deep acting involves modifying internal emotional states to align with expected expressions and has been linked to more adaptive outcomes, including personal accomplishment and better performance [13, 14]. Emotional display rules reflect organizational expectations; in psychiatric settings, strict expectations for calmness, empathy, and stability function as enduring contextual demands. Although recent studies have linked emotional labor to presenteeism [15–17], they have predominantly emphasized negative mediating pathways, such as burnout and fatigue [16, 17]. Importantly, evidence suggests that emotional labor, particularly deep acting, can also foster positive outcomes by building psychological resources [14, 18, 19]. However, this constructive perspective has been largely overlooked in presenteeism research, especially in emotionally intensive clinical contexts such as psychiatric nursing.

To address this gap, we introduce thriving at work, defined as a positive psychological state characterized by both vitality and learning [20], as a potential protective mediator. Rooted in Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [21], thriving at work represents a resource characterized by energy and growth. Accumulating evidence indicates that thriving is closely linked to enhanced psychological and energetic resources [22, 23], which meta-analytic findings identify as core protective factors against presenteeism [24]. This suggests that thriving at work may act as a key mechanism through which distinct dimensions of emotional labor are translated into reduced work productivity. Yet this mediating mechanism has not been examined among psychiatric nurses, whose emotionally intensive work may limit their ability to preserve or generate resources.

Beyond the psychological mechanism of thriving at work, we incorporate the physiological state of sleep disturbances as a critical boundary condition grounded in COR theory. Sleep disturbances—defined as persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep with daytime dysfunction [25]—represent a chronic form of physiological resource depletion within the framework of COR theory. Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among psychiatric nurses, with a prevalence rate of 55% [2], attributable to shift work, circadian misalignment, and the unique psychological burden of their roles. For instance, night shifts frequently involve managing acute exacerbations of patients’ symptoms such as hallucinations, impulsivity, or suicidality. This responsibility, which requires high vigilance under minimal staffing, creates conditions that severely disrupt sleep and exacerbate emotional demands. Poor sleep impairs cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and recovery processes [26–28], thereby potentially exacerbating the effects of emotional labor. Consequently, nurses with sleep disturbances may have a reduced capacity to engage in resource-enhancing emotional labor strategies or maintain thriving at work, indicating that the mediating pathway may vary across subgroups stratified by sleep disturbance status.

Taken together, these gaps highlight the need for an integrated resource-based framework. Guided by COR theory, this study investigates how surface acting, deep acting, and emotional display rules are differentially associated with thriving at work and, in turn, presenteeism among psychiatric nurses. Furthermore, by comparing nurses with and without sleep disturbances, this study identifies differential vulnerability profiles and clarifies the resource-based pathways underlying reduced work productivity.

Hypotheses

H1a

Surface acting is negatively associated with thriving at work.

H1b

Deep acting is positively associated with thriving at work.

H1c

Emotional display rules are negatively associated with thriving at work.

H2

Thriving at work is negatively associated with presenteeism.

H3a

Thriving at work mediates the association between surface acting and presenteeism

H3b

Thriving at work mediates the association between deep acting and presenteeism.

H3c

Thriving at work mediates the association between emotional display rules and presenteeism.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among psychiatric shift work nurses working in 19 specialized hospitals across Fujian Province, China, between January and March 2025, using a convenience sampling approach to ensure representation across different hospitals. Eligible participants were required to hold a valid nursing license and have at least one year of psychiatric nursing experience. Nurses who were on leave during the study period, those in training or internship positions, and non-shift workers were excluded. To ensure data collection consistency, site coordinators underwent standardized online training covering study objectives, eligibility criteria, and electronic questionnaire administration procedures before collaborating with charge nurses to distribute unique survey links to eligible participants. The electronic data collection system incorporated mandatory response fields and IP address restrictions to prevent duplicate submissions while maintaining participant anonymity.

Participant characteristics

The survey results of 1,311 psychiatric nurses showed that the sample was mainly female (91.69%), 72.84% of the nurses were married, and 59.79% held a bachelor’s degree or above. In terms of income distribution, 41.09% reported monthly earnings between 3,000 and 5,999 CNY. Professionally, 28.53% had accumulated 10–15 years of psychiatric nursing experience, while 47.37% held senior nurse positions, and 51.80% were formal employees. Based on sleep quality, participants were divided into a no sleep disturbances group (n = 552) and a sleep disturbances group (n = 759). Characteristics of the Participants

Measures

The demographic characteristic questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect the demographic characteristics of all the participants, including gender, marital status, educational background, personal monthly income (CNY), experience in psychiatric work (years), professional title, employment type, and monthly night shifts.

Emotional labor scale

This scale was developed by Grandey [29], and the Chinese version of the Emotional Labor Scale (C-ELS), adapted and developed by Luo et al. [30], aims to assess the level of emotional labor among nurses. The C-ELS includes three dimensions: surface acting (7 items, e.g., “In displaying the appropriate emotions and attitudes required at work, I sometimes need to act as if I were performing.”), deep acting (3 items, e.g., “To accomplish my work, I sometimes suppress my genuine emotions and display a professional attitude consistent with job requirements.”), and emotional display rules (4 items, e.g., “The hospital uses my emotional displays as an important criterion in performance evaluation.”). Responses are recorded on a 6-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating a higher level of emotional labor. The C-ELS has been widely adopted in China [31] and has also been applied to psychiatric nurses [32]. The Cronbach’s α values of the three dimensions in the current study were 0.746, 0.721, and 0.709, respectively.

Stanford presenteeism scale

Presenteeism was assessed using the Chinese version of the Stanford Presenteeism Scale (SPS-6) [33, 34], which measures productivity loss attributable to health issues. Participants rated their agreement with statements (e.g., “In the past month, due to health problems, it has become more difficult for me to regulate the stress at work”) on a 5-point Likert scale. Following reverse scoring of Items 5 and 6, the total scores ranged from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater productivity impairment. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.749.

Thriving at work scale

Thriving at work was measured using the Thriving at Work Scale, a validated self-report instrument developed by Porath et al. [35] and sinicized and adapted for the Chinese context [36]. The scale comprises two subdimensions, including vitality (5 items) and learning (5 items). It consists of 10 items (e.g., “I proactively acquire relevant knowledge in the workplace”) rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). After reverse-scoring items 4 and 8, the total score ranges from 10 to 70, with higher scores indicating a greater level of thriving at work. The scale has been successfully applied to Chinese nurse populations, demonstrating good reliability and validity [37]. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.815.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a validated self-report questionnaire assessing sleep disturbances over a 1-month interval [25]. One sample item is “During the past month, when have you usually gone to bed at night? ” The PSQI comprises 19 items grouped into 7 components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty), yielding a global score range of 0–21. Higher scores indicate worse sleep quality. Based on the established clinical cutoff of PSQI > 7 [38], participants were dichotomized into groups with and without significant sleep disturbances. In this study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.758).

Data collection

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board prior to initiation. Nursing department administrators from participating hospitals were first contacted to obtain institutional consent. Data collection was conducted using Wenjuanxing, a widely used and validated online survey platform in China. To ensure data quality, several measures were implemented: (1) restricting responses to prevent duplicates, (2) setting minimum completion time thresholds, (3) incorporating mandatory response fields for all questionnaire items, and (4) removing incomplete or inconsistent responses during data cleaning. After data cleaning, 1,311 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 27.0. Before conducting the main analyses, we examined the data for multicollinearity. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all predictor variables in the regression models were below 3, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were computed to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics. Bivariate relationships were assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. A grouped mediation analysis was conducted to examine the potential differential role of sleep quality. Specifically, we tested the mediating effect of thriving at work in the relationship between emotional labor dimensions (surface acting, deep acting, and emotional display rules) and presenteeism across two groups: nurses with sleep disturbances (PSQI > 7) and those without (PSQI ≤ 7). Mediation analysis was performed via structural equation modeling (SEM), employing maximum likelihood estimation with 5,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. Both direct and indirect effects were evaluated. The fit of the SEM models was assessed using multiple indices, including the χ²/df ratio, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), normed fit index (NFI), comparative fit index (CFI), incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Common method variance test

To assess the potential for common method variance, we performed a statistical check following Harman’s single-factor approach. An exploratory factor analysis including all study items was conducted. The results revealed seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. Critically, the first factor accounted for only 14.35% of the total variance, which is well below the 40% threshold, suggesting that common method bias is not a major concern.

Correlations analyses

Table 2 presents the Pearson’s correlation coefficients among the key study variables. As hypothesized, surface acting was significantly and positively correlated with presenteeism (r = 0.317, p < 0.01) and significantly and negatively correlated with thriving at work (r = -0.223, p < 0.01). In contrast, deep acting demonstrated a weak but significant positive correlation with both thriving at work (r = 0.100, p < 0.01) and presenteeism (r = 0.119, p < 0.01). The emotional display rules showed a pattern similar to surface acting and were associated with presenteeism (r = 0.286, p < 0.01) and negatively associated with thriving at work (r = -0.214, p < 0.01).

Likewise, the significant negative indirect effect of deep acting was of a larger magnitude in the sleep disturbances group (β = -0.142) than in the comparison group (β = -0.069) (see Table 4; Fig. 1B and C).

Discussion

The current study extends the existing literature on emotional labor and presenteeism by offering new insights into two underexplored aspects. First, it moves beyond the predominant focus on negative pathways by introducing thriving at work as a novel, positive psychological mediator. Second, by comparing nurses with and without sleep disturbances, it establishes sleep quality as a significant boundary condition, revealing that the detrimental effects of surface acting and emotional display rules on presenteeism are particularly pronounced among nurses with sleep disturbances.

The results of this study indicate that the presenteeism score among psychiatric nurses was 17.06 ± 4.06, which is higher than values reported in other studies among Chinese nurses [16, 39]. This elevated level may be closely related to their unique professional environment and psychological load. First, psychiatric nurses regularly face patients’ sudden, impulsive, or violent behaviors, where threats to personal safety constitute an ongoing occupational stressor [40, 41]. Consequently, psychological resource depletion is accelerated, leading to job insecurity and burnout, manifestations closely associated with high levels of presenteeism [39, 42]. Second, the recovery process of psychiatric patients is typically prolonged and recurrent, with treatment outcomes often not immediately apparent, making it difficult for nurses to derive timely and clear positive feedback from their work. This delay and lack of a sense of achievement can easily lead to diminished professional value and meaning, further undermining their intrinsic motivation for on-the-job engagement [43], thus exacerbating the mind-body dissociation characteristic of presenteeism.

The present study demonstrates that emotional labor does not exert a uniform effect on presenteeism. Instead, its influence is mediated through two distinct psychological pathways. The negative pathway was evident in both surface acting and emotional display rules, which increased presenteeism primarily through diminished thriving at work. This aligns with longstanding findings that surface acting is emotionally exhausting and undermines well-being [44]. Although emotional display rules represent a more implicit normative demand rather than an active regulatory behavior, our results reveal that both forms of negative emotional labor converge on the same resource-depletion mechanism, eroding thriving at work. This extends the literature by demonstrating that emotional labor erodes positive psychological functioning. Identifying thriving at work as the mediator thus reframes emotional labor research by complementing the dominant deficit-based perspective with a resource-based, promotive lens.

The positive pathway emerged through deep acting. While the total effect of deep acting on presenteeism was nonsignificant, its significant indirect effect via enhanced thriving at work illustrates the dual nature of this strategy. Prior meta-analytic evidence confirms that deep acting is associated with well-being and performance outcomes [14], reinforcing its potential to generate psychological resources. At the same time, deep acting requires sustained cognitive and emotional effort, suggesting that the strategy simultaneously incurs resource costs. These opposing processes explain why deep acting exhibited a suppression pattern: its resource-gaining and resource-consuming components counteracted each other in the total effect. Our findings refine conceptualizations of deep acting by positioning it within a dynamic resource equilibrium. Furthermore, context also shapes the extent to which deep acting produces net gains or losses. For instance, research in hospitality shows that deep acting behaviors, such as customer-directed warmth or flirting, can directly enhance tangible outcomes such as tip size and perceived rapport, offering immediate positive reinforcement and making deep acting a high-return investment [45]. In contrast, psychiatric nursing provides relatively few instances of such instant interpersonal rewards. Interactions such as building trust with a volatile patient over weeks illustrate a high-cost, low-return context in which the psychological benefits of deep acting accrue slowly, if at all. Consequently, the protective and draining effects of deep acting are more likely to counterbalance one another.

This study identifies sleep disturbances as a critical boundary condition that intensifies the mediating role of thriving at work in the association between emotional labor—particularly surface acting and emotional display rules—and presenteeism. This finding holds particular significance for psychiatric nursing, where intense emotional demands and strict display rules are inherent [32]. Consistent with COR theory [21], sleep functions as a core recovery mechanism; when disrupted, nurses begin the workday with diminished energetic and cognitive resources. In this compromised state, the demands of emotional labor accelerate resource depletion, creating a vicious cycle in which resources are consumed while the primary recovery pathway through sleep remains impaired. As thriving at work diminishes, nurses possess fewer psychological reserves to sustain role performance, making presenteeism a behavioral manifestation of chronic resource insufficiency. These results underscore the importance of safeguarding sleep health as a protective resource buffer in high–emotional-demand settings such as psychiatric nursing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and makes it difficult to determine the temporal sequence among variables. Second, the design cannot rule out alternative explanatory models; for example, sleep may serve as a mediator between emotional labor and presenteeism, rather than merely functioning as a grouping variable. Furthermore, although the study examined surface acting, deep acting, and emotional display rules as distinct dimensions, employees often integrate multiple strategies in practice, and the combined effects of these strategies were not thoroughly investigated. Finally, the exclusive reliance on a sample of Chinese psychiatric nurses and the omission of contextual factors such as ward type and psychiatric subspecialty differences limit the generalizability of the findings.

Based on these limitations, future research could proceed in the following directions. First, longitudinal or experimental designs should be adopted to establish causality and clarify temporal pathways among variables. Second, the configuration patterns of different emotional labor strategies should be further explored by examining interaction effects between dimensions. Finally, it is essential to test the theoretical model in more diverse occupational and cultural populations to enhance its external validity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study confirms that thriving at work serves as a significant psychological mechanism explaining how emotional labor influences presenteeism among psychiatric nurses. We found that the resource-depleting strategies of surface acting and emotional display rules increase presenteeism primarily by undermining nurses’ sense of thriving at work. Additionally, the mediating pathway through thriving at work was substantially stronger among nurses with sleep disturbances, underscoring that physiological resource depletion can intensify the detrimental effects of emotional labor.

These findings suggest that mitigating presenteeism requires integrated interventions addressing both psychological and physiological resources. First, at the psychological level, training should move beyond rigid “service with a smile” mandates and instead focus on deep acting techniques. These can be integrated into clinical supervision to help nurses reframe challenging interactions (e.g., aggression, self-harm) through a therapeutic lens, reducing the internal dissonance of surface acting. Second, at the physiological level, tailored sleep hygiene protocols are essential for shift workers. This includes providing restful on-call rooms, optimizing shift schedules, and offering cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) to address post-work rumination.
