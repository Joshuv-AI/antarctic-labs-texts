Membrane-bound guanylyl cyclase-G deficiency impairs nitric oxide–mediated epithelial repair in intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury in male mice
Authors: Hui-Chen Lo, Ruey-Bing Yang, Chien-Hsing Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Membrane-bound guanylyl cyclase (GC-G), a noncanonical receptor, has been implicated in intestinal epithelial homeostasis. This study investigated the role of GC-G in intestinal ischemia-reperfusion (IIR) injury and its involvement in nitric oxide (NO)–related responses using male wild-type (WT) and GC-G knockout (KO) mice. Mice underwent superior mesenteric artery occlusion followed by 3- or 24-hour reperfusion and were intraperitoneally administered arginine (NO donor), aminoguanidine (selective iNOS inhibitor), or L-NAME (non-selective NOS inhibitor). In WT mice, IIR reduced jejunal mass, increased IL-6 and TUNEL-staining at 3 h, and suppressed nitrate/nitrite (NOx) and Ki-67 at 24 h. Arginine reduced caspase-3 activity at 3 h and enhanced Ki-67 and DNA content at 24 h. Aminoguanidine suppressed early apoptosis and improved later proliferation. L-NAME reduced early injury but enhanced later proliferation without restoring DNA content. GC-G deficiency was associated with attenuated early apoptotic responses but impaired mucosal regeneration following IIR. In KO mice, arginine failed to enhance proliferation and was associated with elevated apoptosis and cytokines, while NOS inhibitors reduced systemic NOx and IL-6 without improving epithelial proliferation or altering caspase-3 activity or bulk cGMP levels. Notably, intestinal NOx levels remained elevated in KO mice. Together, these findings suggest that GC-G contributes to the coordination of NO-related epithelial responses and influences intestinal sensitivity to NO-associated interventions during IIR, without detectable changes in total tissue cGMP levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47089-2.

Introduction

Intestinal ischemia-reperfusion (IIR) causes significant mucosal damage characterized by epithelial apoptosis, inflammation, and barrier disruption, largely driven by oxidative stress and dysregulated nitric oxide (NO) production1,2. Guanylyl cyclases (GC) generate cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), a secondary messenger involved in epithelial turnover, apoptosis, and inflammatory regulation3. Among these enzymes, membrane-bound GC-G is expressed in multiple organs, including the intestine, and has been implicated in mucosal defense through mechanisms that appear to be independent of NO-soluble GC signaling4–6.

Evidence from renal ischemia-reperfusion models indicates that GC-G deficiency reduces tubular apoptosis and caspase-3 activation7. In the intestinal context, GC-G has been reported to contribute to jejunal integrity and to modulate apoptotic and inflammatory responses through pathways described as NO-independent and cGMP-associated4. Notably, GC-G-deficient mice exhibit increased plasma nitrate/nitrite (NOx) levels following IIR, suggesting altered NO homeostasis in the absence of GC-G activity4. However, the extent to which GC-G influences intestinal responses to pharmacological modulation of NO signaling—rather than directly mediating NO effects—remains unclear.

NO exerts context-dependent effects during IIR that vary according to the NOS isoform involved8. Under physiological conditions, neuronal NOS (nNOS)- and endothelial NOS (eNOS)-derived NO support vascular regulation and epithelial integrity, whereas inducible NOS (iNOS) is markedly upregulated during IIR, leading to excessive NO production that promotes oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cytokine release, and epithelial apoptosis9,10. Arginine supplementation can enhance NO bioavailability and has been shown to mitigate ischemia-induced organ injury, partly through modulation of oxidative and inflammatory responses10–12. Conversely, pharmacological NOS inhibition using aminoguanidine (relatively selective for iNOS) or NG-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME, non-selective) has been shown to reduce NO overproduction and tissue injury in experimental IIR models13–15. These interventions influence inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-62,16,17. Despite extensive investigation of NO dysregulation in IIR, how GC-G status modifies intestinal responses to NO-associated interventions has not been systematically examined.

In the present study, we examined WT and GC-G knockout (KO) mice subjected to intestinal ischemia followed by 3- or 24-hour reperfusion and treated with arginine, aminoguanidine, or L-NAME to modulate NO availability and NOS activity. We hypothesized that GC-G status influences the pattern of mucosal injury and repair following IIR and modifies intestinal responses to NO-associated interventions. Rather than presuming a requirement of GC-G for NO signaling, this experimental design was intended to assess whether GC-G deficiency influences the timing and extent of mucosal injury, apoptosis, inflammation, and regeneration in response to NO-associated modulation. Mucosal injury was assessed by tissue mass and histological indices; apoptosis and proliferation by caspase-3 activity, TUNEL staining, and Ki-67 immunostaining; and inflammatory responses by IL-6 and TNF-α levels in plasma and jejunal tissue. This approach allowed characterization of genotype-, treatment-, and time-dependent differences in intestinal responses to NO-associated interventions during IIR.

Results

Differential effects of GC-G deficiency and NO-related treatments on Jejunal mass after 3- and 24-hour IIR

After 3-hour reperfusion (Fig. 1), WT mice in the C3W group exhibited significantly reduced mucosal dry weight (Fig. 1A) and muscularis protein and DNA content (Fig. 1D and F) compared to WTS group, indicating early jejunal loss. No significant changes were observed among A3W, G3W, and N3W compared to C3W, suggesting that none of the NO-related treatments altered early jejunal mass parameters in WT mice at this time point.

In KO mice, KOS showed significantly lower mucosal and muscularis protein content (Fig. 1C and D) and reduced muscularis DNA (Fig. 1F) compared to WTS, indicating baseline genotype-dependent structural differences. Following IIR, C3K exhibited increased mucosal dry weight compared to C3W (Fig. 1A) and decreased muscularis dry weight compared to both KOS and C3W (Fig. 1B), consistent with a genotype-dependent response to reperfusion. Moreover, A3K, G3K, and N3K showed significantly higher mucosal dry weight than their respective WT counterparts (A3W, G3W, and N3W), reinforcing the gene effect. However, no significant differences were observed among C3K, A3K, G3K, and N3K, indicating no treatment efficacy in KO mice. Mucosal DNA content (Fig. 1E) remained unchanged across all groups.

At 24 h (Fig. 2), C24W mice had significantly decreased mucosal dry weight and muscularis protein and DNA (Fig. 2A, D, F), along with increased mucosal DNA (Fig. 2C), indicating progressive mucosal atrophy and muscularis loss. Among WT mice, only N24W exhibited reduced mucosal DNA compared to C24W, suggesting a modest L-NAME effect. No treatments altered mucosal or muscularis dry weight or protein content, indicating limited late-phase treatment effects in WT mice.

In KO mice, C24K exhibited significantly increased mucosal dry weight and reduced muscularis protein and DNA compared to C24W (Fig. 2A, E, and F), reinforcing the gene effect during reperfusion. G24K had significantly reduced mucosal protein and DNA compared to G24W (Fig. 2B and C); A24K and N24K had decreased muscularis protein and DNA to A24W and N24W (Fig. 2E and F). N24K also had significantly lower muscularis dry weight than N24W (Fig. 2D). Nonetheless, no significant differences were observed among C24K, A24K, G24K, and N24K, indicating persistent treatment ineffectiveness in KO mice.

Three-way ANOVA analyses demonstrated that genotype and reperfusion time were the primary determinants of jejunal mass parameters (Supplementary Table 1). Significant genotype × reperfusion time interactions were identified for both mucosal and muscularis indices, indicating that the magnitude and direction of IIR-induced changes differed between WT and GC-G–deficient mice over time. In contrast, treatment effects and higher-order interactions involving treatment were limited across most jejunal mass parameters.

Differential regulation of jejunal histomorphology by GC-G deficiency and NO-related treatments after IIR

Representative hematoxylin and eosin–stained jejunal sections illustrating overall morphological changes across genotypes, treatments, and reperfusion time points are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

After 3-hour reperfusion, WT mice in the C3W group exhibited significantly decreased villus height and increased crypt depth and muscularis thickness compared to WTS, indicating early IIR-induced structural alterations of the jejunum (Fig. 3A, C, and E). Arginine, aminoguanidine, and L-NAME significantly reduced crypt depth in A3W, G3W, and N3W compared to C3W (Fig. 3B), suggesting that both NO supplementation and NOS inhibition attenuated IIR-induced early crypt epithelial hyperplasia. However, villus height and muscularis thickness remained unchanged by treatment, indicating selective modulation of crypt architecture at this time point.

In KO mice, KOS showed significantly reduced crypt depth compared to WTS, indicating gene-dependent baseline differences in jejunal architecture. Following IIR, C3K mice exhibited significantly decreased crypt depth and muscularis thickness compared to C3W, reflecting a genotype-dependent response to reperfusion. G3K and N3K mice displayed significantly reduced crypt depth compared to their WT counterparts, and G3K also showed thinner muscularis, further supporting a dominant gene effect. However, no significant differences were observed among C3K, A3K, G3K, and N3K, indicating limited treatment responsiveness in the absence of GC-G during early reperfusion.

Consistent with the 3-hour analysis, morphometric parameters at 24 h (Fig. 3B, D, and F), villus height, crypt depth, and muscularis thickness in C24W did not differ from WTS, suggesting resolution of early IIR-induced morphological alterations. Among WT treatments, only N24W exhibited significantly increased crypt depth compared to C24W, indicating delayed crypt remodeling in response to L-NAME. No other treatments affected villus height or muscularis thickness at this later stage.

In KO mice, C24K and KOS did not differ in histomorphological parameters, suggesting minimal IIR impact on jejunal architecture in the absence of GC-G. Only N24K showed significantly reduced crypt depth compared to N24W. However, no significant differences were found among C24K, A24K, G24K, and N24K, indicating that neither IIR nor NO-related treatments affected jejunal histomorphology in GC-G–deficient mice at 24 h.

Three-way ANOVA analyses supported a predominant influence of genotype on jejunal histomorphology, with genotype- and time-dependent differences in crypt architecture between WT and GC-G–deficient mice (Supplementary Table 1). Treatment effects were comparatively limited.

Differential regulation of systemic and local inflammatory mediators by GC-G deficiency and NO-related treatments after IIR

At 3-hour reperfusion (Table 1), WT mice in the C3W group exhibited significantly elevated plasma IL-6 and TNF-α levels compared to WTS, indicating IIR-induced systemic inflammation. No significant differences were observed among A3W, G3W, and N3W, suggesting limited anti-inflammatory effects of treatments at this stage. In KO mice, KOS showed significantly decreased plasma NOx and increased IL-6 compared to WTS, reflecting a gene-dependent alteration in baseline inflammatory status. C3K exhibited significantly higher plasma NOx and TNF-α compared to KOS, and lower IL-6 compared to C3W, indicating both IIR and gene effects on systemic inflammation. Notably, A3K, G3K, and N3K showed significantly lower plasma NOx and IL-6, and G3K and N3K showed significantly elevated TNF-α compared to their respective WT counterparts, suggesting genotype-dependent modulation of systemic inflammatory mediators. Relative to C3K, A3K, G3K, and N3K showed distinct changes in plasma NOx, IL-6, and TNF-α, respectively, indicating treatment-specific effects within the KO genotype.

In jejunal mucosa after 24-hour reperfusion, NOx was significantly reduced in C24W compared to WTS and further in G24W, alongside decreased TNF-α, suggesting selective modulation of local inflammatory mediators. In KO mice, KOS exhibited increased NOx and decreased TNF-α compared to WTS. C24K, A24K, G24K, and N24K had significantly higher mucosal NOx than their WT counterparts, confirming a gene-dependent enhancement of local NO accumulation. Mucosal IL-6 was not significantly different between KOS and WTS or between C24K and KOS, but was significantly decreased in N24K compared to both C24K and N24W, reflecting combined gene and treatment effects. Mucosal TNF-α was lower in C24K than C24W but higher than KOS, consistent with both gene and IIR effects. A24K exhibited further reduced TNF-α compared to both C24K and A24W, suggesting a genotype-dependent suppressive effect of arginine on local TNF-α production. Collectively, these findings indicate that systemic and mucosal inflammatory mediators to IIR vary by genotype and reperfusion time, with treatment effects depending on the specific mediator and tissue compartment (Supplementary Table 1).

Differential regulation of mucosal cGMP and caspase-3 activity by GC-G deficiency and NO-related treatments after IIR

After 3- and 24-hour reperfusion, mucosal cGMP did not differ significantly among WTS, C3W, A3W, G3W, and N3W groups (Fig. 4A). Similarly, no significant differences were observed between KOS and WTS, or among KOS, C3K, A3K, G3K, and N3K groups (Fig. 4B). Accordingly, group-wise comparisons did not reveal treatment- or time-dependent modulation of mucosal cGMP levels. However, three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of genotype on mucosal cGMP levels, whereas reperfusion time and treatment-related interactions were not significant (Supplementary Table 1), indicating that GC-G deficiency was associated with a modest but consistent difference in overall mucosal cGMP content across the experimental design rather than discrete group-specific effects.

Discussion

IIR induces intestinal injury characterized by mucosal atrophy, apoptosis, and inflammatory activation. Using WT and GC-G-deficient mice treated with arginine, aminoguanidine, or L-NAME at early (3 h) and late (24 h) reperfusion stages, this study examined how GC-G status influences intestinal responses to NO-associated interventions under IIR conditions. Rather than presuming GC-G as a canonical NO receptor or excluding the involvement of soluble guanylyl cyclase, this approach was designed to evaluate whether GC-G deficiency alters the timing, magnitude, and coordination of injury and repair responses following pharmacological modulation of NO availability. The results demonstrate that, under IIR conditions, GC-G contributes to genotype- and time-dependent intestinal responses to NO-associated interventions, while bulk mucosal cGMP levels remain largely unchanged. IIR elicits robust inflammatory responses with elevated TNF-α and IL-6 in both intestinal tissue and plasma, a pattern commonly associated with mucosal and systemic injury2,4. In this study, WT mice exhibited early villus shortening and increased TUNEL-positive cells indicating rapid epithelial loss and apoptosis, whereas sustained alterations in jejunal mass and selected structural parameters persisted at both 3 and 24 h after reperfusion. By 24 h, reduced Ki-67 and jejunal NOx levels suggested impaired regeneration. Jejunal IL-6 increased at 3 h, while plasma IL-6 remained elevated at both time points; plasma TNF-α also increased at 3 h, reinforcing concurrent local and systemic inflammatory activation. Increased crypt depth and muscularis thickening at 3 h, along with elevated mucosal DNA content at 24 h, may reflect partial compensatory remodeling responses2,4. Together, these findings delineate a temporally organized pattern of epithelial apoptosis, impaired proliferation, persistent inflammation, and NOx dysregulation, underscoring an incomplete and dysregulated recovery following IIR in WT mice.

Although GC-G is not a canonical NO receptor like soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC), accumulating evidence indicates that GC-G has been implicated in the modulation of NO–related signaling and epithelial homeostasis4,7. Our previous work showed that GC-G deletion reduced jejunal cGMP levels and attenuated phosphorylation of NF-κB and MAPKs (JNK2/3 and p38), accompanied by decreased apoptotic responses4. Consistent with these findings, GC-G-deficient mice under basal conditions (KOS) exhibited villus shortening, reduced jejunal protein and DNA content, and elevated plasma TNF-α, indicating compromised epithelial integrity at baseline. Following IIR, KO mice displayed a distinct pattern of responses characterized by increased jejunal NOx and reduced mucosal TNF-α, suggesting altered local inflammatory signaling and disrupted spatial regulation of NO pathways rather than coordinated epithelial protection or repair.

In contrast to WT mice, GC-G-deficient mice exhibited blunted and temporally limited responses to IIR. Accordingly, all IIR-associated changes were interpreted relative to the corresponding sham-operated controls within each genotype, rather than assuming a shared baseline physiology between WT and GC-G–deficient mice. Despite elevated plasma IL-6 levels, jejunal mass and overall architecture were relatively maintained, with transient increases in mucosal caspase-3 activity and reductions in mucosal IL-6 and Ki-67 observed at 3 h but not sustained at 24 h. At later stages, KO mice retained higher jejunal protein and DNA content together with lower plasma NOx, whereas jejunal NOx remained elevated and TNF-α suppressed across time points. These findings indicate a delayed and poorly coordinated injury–repair response, marked by impaired coupling between inflammatory modulation, apoptotic control, and epithelial regeneration. Notably, GC-G has been predominantly studied in sensory and reproductive systems5,18; however, its expression in kidney and intestine suggests broader regulatory roles in epithelial stress responses4,7. Importantly, the present findings do not imply that GC-G functions as a canonical cytoprotective guanylyl cyclase or replaces sGC signaling. Rather, they are consistent with a model in which GC-G contributes to the maintenance of intestinal structure and basal NO–cGMP signaling under physiological conditions, and in which GC-G deficiency is associated with altered regulation of the spatial coordination and temporal integration of NO-related signals during IIR.

In WT mice, arginine exerted time-dependent protective effects following IIR, reducing jejunal caspase-3 activity at 3 h and enhancing Ki-67 expression and DNA content at 24 h, consistent with attenuation of early epithelial apoptosis and promotion of late mucosal regeneration. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that arginine upregulates Bcl2, suppresses Bax, reduces lipid peroxidation, and facilitates NO-mediated tissue repair11,19,20. However, given that L-arginine serves as a shared substrate for NOS and arginase, the protective effects of arginine observed in this study should be interpreted as the net outcome of competing metabolic pathways rather than preferential activation of NO–dependent signaling. Notably, mucosal cGMP, TUNEL, and cytokine profiles were not significantly altered, suggesting that arginine-associated protection may involve compartmentalized NO signaling or cofactor-dependent activation rather than changes in bulk NO-cGMP levels21.

Selective iNOS inhibition with aminoguanidine also produced stage-specific effects in WT mice. At 3 h, aminoguanidine reduced jejunal crypt depth and TUNEL-positive cells, supporting the notion that excessive iNOS-derived NO contributes to early inflammatory and apoptotic injury22–24. By 24 h, aminoguanidine was associated with increased Ki-67 expression and altered plasma and mucosal TNF-α and NOx profiles, while mucosal cGMP and caspase-3 activity remained unchanged. These mixed responses indicate that although iNOS inhibition limits early tissue damage, iNOS-derived NO may also participate in later repair processes, underscoring the dual-phase nature of iNOS signaling during IIR.

In contrast, non-selective NOS inhibition with L-NAME similarly reduced crypt depth and TUNEL-positive cells at 3 h, indicating early suppression of NO-mediated injury, consistent with reports in cerebral, hepatic, and renal ischemia–reperfusion models14,16,25. However, at 24 h, L-NAME increased jejunal crypt depth and Ki-67 expression without restoring mucosal DNA content, cGMP levels, or caspase-3 activity, suggesting dysregulated proliferation and impaired regeneration. Given the systemic route of administration, the observed intestinal effects should be interpreted as responses to whole-body modulation of NO-related pathways rather than intestine-specific actions. Together, these findings suggest that while early NOS inhibition is protective, pan-NOS blockade may disrupt eNOS–cGMP–PKG–dependent repair pathways25,26. Importantly, neither aminoguanidine nor L-NAME significantly altered mucosal cGMP or caspase-3 activity, indicating that their effects likely involve alternative or spatially restricted NO signaling mechanisms beyond canonical pathways.

The effects of arginine in GC-G–deficient mice were further examined to delineate gene-dependent modulation of NO-mediated repair. In contrast to WT mice, arginine elicited disorganized and stage-dependent effects in KO mice. At 3 h, A3K mice showed elevated plasma IL-6, jejunal TNF-α, and Ki-67, suggesting concurrent inflammation and proliferation. By 24 h, A24K mice displayed increased mucosal TUNEL staining and reduced mucosal protein and DNA content, despite lowered jejunal TNF-α and plasma NOx, consistent with impaired recovery. Compared to WT counterparts, A3K mice displayed lower plasma NOx and IL-6 but higher jejunal NOx and caspase-3 activity, whereas A24K mice displayed elevated jejunal NOx, increased apoptosis, and reduced jejunal mass. Notably, Ki-67 expression in A24K remained significantly lower than in A24W, underscoring defective regenerative signaling. Collectively, these gene-dependent divergences indicate that GC-G is involved in the coordination of arginine-associated NO modulation and epithelial repair during IIR. Although arginine promotes epithelial survival via NO signaling11, its protective efficacy appears to be dependent on intact downstream cGMP–PKG signaling. Previous studies have shown that GC-G deficiency disrupts NO–cGMP balance and impairs MAPK/NF-κB pathways, and that effective cGMP-mediated repair requires functional guanylyl cyclase4,27,28. Accordingly, the present findings suggest that in the absence of GC-G, arginine fails to promote mucosal repair despite unchanged bulk cGMP levels. Thus, unchanged bulk cGMP levels do not preclude a functional role of GC-G, as GC-G may primarily regulate spatially restricted or stress-responsive cGMP signaling that are not captured by total tissue measurements.

Beyond arginine, the effects of selective and non-selective NOS inhibition were evaluated in GC-G–deficient mice to assess NO modulation under impaired cGMP signaling. At 3 h, aminoguanidine increased plasma NOx and jejunal TNF-α, while L-NAME reduced plasma NOx but elevated plasma TNF-α, suggesting divergent effects on systemic NO metabolism and inflammation. Ki-67 expression was relatively preserved in G3K but was reduced in N3K, indicating that selective iNOS inhibition may better support early proliferative responses in the absence of GC-G. However, neither treatment altered caspase-3 activity or TUNEL staining, indicating limited effects on early apoptosis. By 24 h, both aminoguanidine and L-NAME reduced plasma NOx and partially suppressed inflammatory mediators—aminoguanidine decreased plasma IL-6, whereas L-NAME reduced jejunal IL-6. Nonetheless, mucosal cGMP levels, caspase-3 activity, TUNEL staining, Ki-67 expression, and DNA content remained unchanged, and Ki-67 expression in G24K and N24K was markedly lower than in WT counterparts, indicating ineffective epithelial regeneration. Notably, jejunal NOx levels remained elevated across treatments despite reduced plasma NOx, consistent with impaired NO compartmentalization, a phenomenon reported in other ischemia-reperfusion models with dysregulated iNOS activity22,29. Together, these findings suggest that in the absence of GC-G, neither selective nor non-selective NOS inhibition is sufficient to restore coordinated mucosal repair, possibly reflecting impaired integration of downstream cGMP-related signaling30. Although iNOS inhibition can reduce oxidative injury and eNOS-derived NO supports epithelial recovery31, these protective effects appear ineffective without GC-G, and GC-G may additionally participate in noncanonical NO-mediated cytoprotective pathways beyond classical NOS-dependent mechanisms32.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, although the IIR model used in WT and GC-G-deficient mice captures key features of early and late phases of intestinal injury, the analysis was restricted to two discrete time points (3 and 24 h), which may not fully capture the dynamic progression of mucosal damage, inflammation, and repair. Second, while multiple NO-related parameters were measured, including NOx, cytokines, caspase-3, TUNEL, and Ki-67, downstream cGMP–PKG signaling and its spatial compartmentalization were not directly evaluated. Accordingly, the present study was not designed to delineate the relative contributions or potential interactions of GC-G and sGC in NO-mediated epithelial responses. Third, tissue-level analyses were conducted using whole jejunal segments, which may mask cell-type–specific or region-specific differences in NO responsiveness and GC-G signaling. In addition, because only male mice were included in the present study, the applicability of these findings to females remains to be determined, particularly given known sex differences in NO signaling and inflammatory responses. Finally, although GC-G deficiency clearly altered responses to IIR and NO-associated interventions, compensatory activation of other guanylyl cyclases or redox-sensitive pathways may have contributed to the observed phenotype and warrant further investigation.

Taken together, the present data indicate that GC-G does not function as a canonical NO-activated guanylyl cyclase or a determinant of bulk intestinal cGMP levels, but instead modulates basal intestinal physiology and shapes the coordination of epithelial injury and repair responses to NO-associated interventions during intestinal ischemia–reperfusion.

In conclusion, IIR induced a clear time-dependent pattern of mucosal injury and impaired repair in WT mice, characterized by early epithelial apoptosis and inflammation followed by compromised regenerative signaling. Under these conditions, arginine supplementation attenuated early apoptotic responses and enhanced late-phase epithelial proliferation, whereas NOS inhibition effectively reduced early epithelial injury; notably, selective iNOS inhibition by aminoguanidine partially attenuated late inflammatory responses, as reflected by reduced NOx and TNF-α levels at 24 h. In contrast, combined inhibition of constitutive and inducible NOS by L-NAME alleviated early apoptosis but failed to restore effective mucosal recovery, indicating a dissociation between proliferative signaling and structural repair.

Paradoxically, although GC-G deficiency was associated with attenuated injury features after IIR, it profoundly altered jejunal responsiveness to NO-associated interventions. In GC-G–deficient mice, NO-associated treatments modulated cytokine and NOx levels without restoring coordinated epithelial regeneration, and intestinal NOx remained elevated despite reduced systemic NOx, indicating disrupted spatial regulation of NO signaling. The absence of detectable changes in bulk mucosal cGMP suggests that these effects may involve cGMP-independent or spatially restricted signaling mechanisms. Collectively, these findings support a role for GC-G in coordinating intestinal responses to NO modulation after IIR, without acting as a primary mediator of classical NO–cGMP signaling.

Methods

Animals and experimental design

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Changhua Christian Hospital (Approval No. CCH-AE-95010). All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research. Male C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from the National Taiwan University Laboratory Animal Center (Taipei, Taiwan). GC-G KO mice, backcrossed onto the C57BL/6J background, were provided by Dr. Yang’s laboratory at Academia Sinica (Taipei, Taiwan) and genotyped by PCR as previously described7.

After one week of acclimation, mice were fasted overnight and randomly assigned into 18 groups (n = 12/group). WT and KO mice underwent either sham operation or IIR procedures, consisting of 45 min of superior mesenteric artery occlusion followed by 3–24 h of reperfusion. WT mice were assigned to sham (WTS) or IIR groups and received subcutaneous administration of either 0.1 ml saline (C3W and C24W), arginine (300 mg/kg, A3W and A24W), aminoguanidine (100 mg/kg, G3W and G24W), or L-NAME (10 mg/kg, N3W and N24W). KO mice underwent parallel treatments (KOS, C3K/C24K, A3K/A24K, G3K/G24K, N3K/N24K). Group designations and experimental flow are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 2.

All compounds were freshly prepared in saline and administered subcutaneously to achieve systemic modulation of NO availability and NOS activity rather than localized intestinal delivery. In the 24-hour reperfusion groups, treatments were administered repeatedly at 0, 4, 8, 16, and 23 h after reperfusion, following a dosing schedule adapted from previously established intestinal ischemia–reperfusion protocols1,2 and consistent with repeated post-ischemic dosing strategies reported in other ischemia–reperfusion models33. In contrast, 3-hour reperfusion groups received only the initial post-reperfusion treatment, reflecting the acute phase of injury. This experimental design enabled comparison of intestinal responses across genotypes, reperfusion times, and NO-associated treatment conditions.

Induction of intestinal ischemia and reperfusion

Mice were anesthetized with intramuscular injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), then underwent a 2-cm midline laparotomy. Buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneously) was administered 30 min before surgery for pre-emptive analgesia and repeated at 12 h post-operatively. Intestinal ischemia was induced by occluding the superior mesenteric artery with a microvascular clamp, as previously described4. Successful induction of ischemia was confirmed by a visible change in intestinal color from pink to dark red or brown, indicating reduced perfusion. After 45 min, the clamp was removed to initiate reperfusion, which was verified by restoration of intestinal color. The abdominal wall was closed in two layers using chromic gut sutures for the muscle layer and surgical staples for the skin. Following surgery, mice were returned to their cages with free access to water and chow and were monitored regularly for signs of distress. Animals were euthanized at 3–24 h after reperfusion for tissue and blood collection.

The overall survival rate was 97.2%. Six mice (one each in A3W, C3K, A3K, G3K, N3K, and C24K) died postoperatively due to intra-abdominal hemorrhage, confirmed by necropsy.

Tissue harvesting and processing

At 3- or 24-hours post-reperfusion, mice were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (150 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg) via intramuscular injection and euthanized by exsanguination. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture and centrifuged at 3,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain serum and plasma, which were stored at − 80 °C. The entire small intestine was removed and placed on ice. A jejunal segment (5 cm distal to the stomach to 10 cm proximal to the cecum) was isolated for analysis.

Two 3-cm jejunal segments were excised, rinsed with ice-cold saline, opened longitudinally, and mucosa scraped with glass slides. Wet and dry weights were recorded, and mucosal protein content (Pierce Chemical Co., Rockford, IL) and DNA content were measured34.

A 1-cm mid-jejunum segment was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for morphometric analyses. Villus height, crypt depth, and muscularis thickness were measured in ≥ 10 well-oriented villus–crypt units per sample4.

Measurement of inflammatory markers, cGMP, and Caspase-3

Plasma and jejunal levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Plasma NOx was assessed using a colorimetric assay (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI). Jejunal cGMP and caspase-3 activity were quantified via ELISA (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY) and colorimetric assay (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), respectively, and both were normalized to total protein content, determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Detection of apoptotic and proliferating cells in Jejunal sections

Apoptotic cells in jejunal tissue were identified using a commercial TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling) immunofluorescence staining kit (Hoffmann-La Roche Inc., Branford, CT). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and the apoptotic index was calculated as the number of TUNEL-positive cells per five randomly selected villi.

Cell proliferation was evaluated via Ki-67 immunohistochemistry using a monoclonal antibody (clone SP6, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA; 1:100 dilution). The proliferation index was defined as the percentage of Ki-67–positive nuclei in 10 randomly selected crypts. Negative controls used 0.5% bovine serum albumin in PBS instead of primary antibody.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using the general linear model procedure in SAS (SAS 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). To evaluate the effects of genotype (WT vs. GC-G KO), treatment (saline, arginine, aminoguanidine, or L-NAME), and reperfusion time (3–24 h), three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. When significant main effects or interaction terms were detected, post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test. For comparisons involving sham-operated controls and treatment effects within each genotype and reperfusion time point, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was applied. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
