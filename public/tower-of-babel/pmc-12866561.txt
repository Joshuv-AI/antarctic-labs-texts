High prevalence of depression in symptomatic knee osteoarthritis: a cross-sectional study on risk factors and therapeutic implications
Authors: Mahamadou Sagara, Abderrahim Majjad, Laila Taoubane, Ahmed Bezza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a major public health concern, particularly in Morocco. Its association with mental health, especially depressive symptoms, remains insufficiently documented in North African/Mediterranean populations. This study investigated the prevalence of depression and associated factors among patients with symptomatic KOA.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included patients with KOA diagnosed according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria. Clinical and psychological assessments were performed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Radiographic severity was assessed on available and interpretable knee radiographs using the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grading system. Clinically significant depression was defined as HADS-D ≥ 11. Multivariable logistic regression (restricted to participants with available radiographs) was used to identify factors independently associated with depression.ResultsA total of 410 patients were included; 61.9% were women, with a mean age of 61.8 ± 9.5 years and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 28.1 ± 4.4 kg/m². Depression (HADS-D ≥ 11) was observed in 130 patients (31.7%), of whom 72.3% were women. Multivariable analysis (n = 280, participants with available radiographs) identified factors independently associated with depression: female sex (aOR = 1.45; 95% CI 1.10–2.10; p = 0.010), higher BMI (aOR = 1.06; 95% CI 1.03–1.09; p < 0.001), higher WOMAC pain score (aOR = 1.04; 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p < 0.001), greater functional limitation (WOMAC function score) (aOR = 1.01; 95% CI 1.01–1.02; p = 0.002), presence of comorbidities (aOR = 1.40; 95% CI 1.15–1.75; p = 0.004), and physical inactivity (aOR = 1.25; 95% CI 1.05–1.60; p = 0.020). In contrast, KL grade was not significantly associated with depression (aOR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.98–1.06; p = 0.32).ConclusionNearly one-third of patients with symptomatic KOA had clinically significant depressive symptoms, with a higher proportion among women. Depression was associated with both non-modifiable (female sex) and potentially modifiable factors, including BMI, pain severity, functional limitation, comorbidities, and physical inactivity. The absence of a significant association between KL grade and depression supports a comprehensive management approach that addresses pain, physical activity, and mental health.

Introduction

Scientific background and rationale

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a chronic, degenerative, and multifactorial joint disease that affects nearly 22% of individuals over the age of 40 worldwide [1]. It is characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, leading to pain, stiffness, and functional limitations that may result in long-term disability [2]. Beyond joint symptoms, KOA is frequently accompanied by comorbid conditions, including mental health disorders particularly depression which are increasingly recognized as clinically relevant in this population. A systematic review reported a pooled prevalence of depression of approximately 30% among patients aged 45 to 75 years with knee osteoarthritis, with variability across studied populations [3–5]. The chronic pain depression relationship may create a vicious cycle: pain can exacerbate depressive symptoms, while depression may increase pain perception and reduce adherence to treatment [6, 7]. Despite this bidirectional association, routine depression screening remains limited in osteoarthritis care, particularly in middle-income countries such as Morocco [8, 9]. Furthermore, most available evidence originates from Western settings, with fewer data from North African populations, where cultural and socioeconomic factors may influence symptom expression, health behaviors, and access to mental health care.

Identifying factors associated with depression in KOA is therefore essential for implementing comprehensive and context-adapted management strategies. Several correlates have been reported, including female sex, elevated body mass index (BMI), cardiometabolic comorbidities (e.g., diabetes and hypertension), low physical activity, and greater pain severity [10, 11]. However, the relative contribution of these factors in non-Western populations remains insufficiently explored.

Context specific to North African populations

The prevalence and clinical characteristics of depression among patients with symptomatic KOA are poorly documented in North African settings. Most studies have been conducted in European, North American, or Asian cohorts, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other sociocultural and healthcare contexts [12]. The North African region has distinct demographic and health-system characteristics that may affect both the expression and the management of depression in osteoarthritis [13, 14]. Disparities in access to care, varying levels of education, and cultural perceptions of chronic illness and mental health may influence both depressive symptom reporting and its functional impact [15].

Studies from the Maghreb have reported a high burden of depressive disorders in populations living with chronic diseases, with clinical presentations that may differ from those observed elsewhere [16]. In this context, identifying locally relevant factors associated with depression in KOA could inform tailored care pathways and psychosocial interventions. Our study, conducted in a North African cohort of patients with symptomatic KOA, aims to contribute new evidence to better characterize these interactions and support integrated care approaches.

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study was to estimate the prevalence of depression and identify factors associated with depression among North African patients with symptomatic KOA managed in a Moroccan tertiary care hospital.

Secondary objectives were:

To analyze the relationship between pain severity and functional limitations (assessed using the WOMAC score) and depressive symptoms (assessed using the HADS-Depression subscale);To evaluate the influence of body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, and physical activity level on the risk of depression.

Hypothesis

Depression is associated with higher pain and functional impairment, the presence of comorbidities, and reduced physical activity among patients with knee osteoarthritis.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional, descriptive, and analytical observational study designed to identify factors associated with depression among patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis (KOA). The study was conducted in the Rheumatology Department of the Mohammed V Military Teaching Hospital (HMIMV) in Rabat, a national referral center serving both civilian and military populations.

Setting, location, and study period

The study was carried out over 12 months (January to December 2023). Patients were recruited consecutively among outpatients presenting with chronic knee pain during scheduled rheumatology consultations. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires administered by trained physicians. All participants received detailed information about the study and provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. The study protocol was approved by the HMIMV Institutional Ethics Committee.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and symptomatic KOA meeting the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) clinical classification criteria. Standard weight-bearing anteroposterior knee radiographs were obtained when available and interpretable; radiographic KOA was defined as a Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade ≥ 2 and was used for radiographic severity analyses. Exclusion criteria were inflammatory rheumatic diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis), post-traumatic or crystal-induced osteoarthritis, current antidepressant treatment, an active psychiatric disorder requiring specialized care at the time of inclusion, or refusal to participate.

Eligibility was confirmed by a rheumatologist with more than five years of clinical experience. The patient selection process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Sampling strategy

Participants were enrolled consecutively as they attended outpatient consultations. No random sampling or randomization was performed; all eligible patients meeting inclusion criteria were included.

Study variables

Outcome

The primary outcome was clinically significant depressive symptoms, defined as a score ≥ 11 on the depression subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-D). Participants were classified as depressed (HADS-D ≥ 11) or non-depressed (HADS-D < 11).

Potential confounders

Potential confounders considered a priori included education level, symptom duration (months), and sleep disturbances (e.g., chronic insomnia), when available. Variables included in the final multivariable model were selected based on clinical relevance and completeness of data across participants with available radiographs.

Data sources and measurement tools

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire including sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, medical history, weight, height, BMI), HADS, and WOMAC. Physical activity was assessed according to WHO criteria (≥ 150 min/week considered regular; <150 min/week considered insufficient). Comorbidities were recorded based on medical history and/or ongoing treatment. Radiographic severity was graded using the Kellgren–Lawrence classification on standard weight-bearing anteroposterior knee radiographs when available and interpretable.

All questionnaires were completed in the presence of the investigator to ensure standardization and minimize missing data.

Bias management

Several measures were implemented to minimize bias: consecutive recruitment to reduce selection bias, training of physicians in standardized data collection, double data entry to limit transcription errors, and multivariable analysis to adjust for relevant confounders identified in the literature.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using the Schwartz formula for cross-sectional studies. Assuming an estimated depression prevalence of 30%, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a minimum of 323 participants was required. To account for exclusions and missing data, 410 participants were included.

Quantitative variable handling

Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, WOMAC scores) were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Where clinically relevant, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² defined obesity. For analyses, WOMAC pain and WOMAC function were handled as continuous variables.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with depression, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Missing data were handled using mean imputation for continuous variables and complete-case analysis for categorical variables. Analyses involving Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade were restricted to participants with available and interpretable knee radiographs. A sex-stratified analysis was performed to explore the association between radiographic severity (KL 2 vs. KL 3–4) and depression. Statistical analyses were performed using jamovi software (version 2.26.6).

Results

Participants

Figure 1 presents the participant selection flow diagram. Between January and December 2023, 430 patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis were assessed for eligibility. Twenty patients were excluded (n = 6 due to an active psychiatric disorder or current antidepressant treatment, n = 8 due to refusal to participate, and n = 6 due to incomplete data). In total, 410 participants were included in the final analysis and classified into two groups: depressed (HADS-D ≥ 11; n = 130) and non-depressed (HADS-D < 11; n = 280).

Descriptive data

The study population included 254 women (61.9%) and 156 men (38.1%), with a mean age of 61.8 ± 9.5 years. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 28.1 ± 4.4 kg/m².

In the depressed group (n = 130), 72.3% were women (n = 94), compared with 57.1% (n = 160) in the non-depressed group (p = 0.004). Mean BMI was higher in the depressed group (29.7 ± 4.8 kg/m²) than in the non-depressed group (27.4 ± 3.9 kg/m²; p < 0.001). Mean symptom duration was 26 ± 7 months in the depressed group versus 23 ± 8 months in the non-depressed group (p = 0.010). These characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with depression

Multivariable logistic regression was performed among participants with available and interpretable knee radiographs (n = 280), allowing inclusion of radiographic severity (KL grade) in the adjusted model (Table 4). The model adjusted for age, sex, education level, BMI, cardiometabolic comorbidities, pain severity, functional limitation, and physical activity, and identified several independent associations with depression. Female sex was associated with higher odds of depression (aOR = 1.45; 95% CI 1.10–2.10; p = 0.010). Higher BMI was associated with depression (aOR = 1.06 per unit increase; 95% CI 1.03–1.09; p < 0.001). Each additional point on the WOMAC pain score was associated with higher odds of depression (aOR = 1.04; 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p < 0.001), and greater functional limitation was also associated (aOR = 1.01 per point; 95% CI 1.01–1.02; p = 0.002). Having at least one comorbidity was associated with depression (aOR = 1.40; 95% CI 1.15–1.75; p = 0.004), as was physical inactivity (aOR = 1.25; 95% CI 1.05–1.60; p = 0.020). Radiographic severity assessed using the Kellgren–Lawrence grade was not significantly associated with depression (aOR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.98–1.06; p = 0.32). These results are shown in Table 4.

Therapeutic management

Regarding treatment strategies, 73.2% of patients were receiving analgesics (paracetamol or mild opioids), 62.9% were using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and 20.0% had undergone intra-articular injections. Moreover, 36.5% participated in therapeutic education programs including weight management strategies, and 24.9% were undergoing physiotherapy. Only 10.8% of patients were receiving psychological care despite the high prevalence of depressive symptoms. Surgical intervention was performed in 4.9% of cases. Discussion

Key findings

In this cross-sectional study of patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis, depressive symptoms were frequent and consistent with prevalence ranges reported in the international literature [17]. This finding supports the relevance of routinely considering mental health in the clinical assessment of knee osteoarthritis.

In multivariable analysis, depressive symptoms were statistically independently associated with female sex and higher body mass index, in line with prior evidence showing a higher burden of depression among women and among individuals with excess weight [18–20]. Depressive symptoms were also associated with greater pain and functional limitation, which is clinically plausible given the close and potentially bidirectional relationship between chronic pain, disability, and psychological distress [21]. In addition, cardiometabolic comorbidities and physical inactivity were associated with depressive symptoms, reinforcing the importance of a multidimensional approach to osteoarthritis management that addresses both physical and psychological factors [22].

Radiographic severity assessed using the Kellgren–Lawrence grade was not independently associated with depressive symptoms. This may indicate that symptom burden and perceived functional impact are more closely related to psychological distress than radiographic changes alone, consistent with evidence on clinical–radiographic discordance in knee osteoarthritis [23, 24]. The sex-stratified analysis should be interpreted cautiously given its subgroup nature and the availability of radiographic data for only a subset of participants.

Interpretation and comparison with existing literature

Overall, our findings emphasize the close relationship between KOA symptom severity, functional limitation, and depressive symptoms. The observed associations likely reflect a complex, potentially bidirectional interplay between pain, disability, health behaviors (physical inactivity), and mental health, as described in prior work [25, 26]. The association between higher BMI and depressive symptoms may involve inflammatory pathways and the psychosocial burden of excess weight [27]. Similarly, the association with physical inactivity supports integrated strategies combining pain control, tailored exercise programs, and psychological support to improve both functional and mental health outcomes [28, 29].

An important clinical observation is the low rate of psychological care (10.8%) despite frequent depressive symptoms, suggesting barriers to mental health access such as limited availability of services, stigma, and the absence of routine screening protocols. This gap has also been reported in other settings and argues for patient-centered multidisciplinary care pathways [30].

Clinical implications

Given the high prevalence of depressive symptoms, routine screening using brief validated tools (e.g., HADS-D) could be considered during KOA consultations to facilitate early identification and referral when needed. Multidisciplinary models integrating rheumatology care with physiotherapy and mental health support may help address pain, disability, inactivity, and psychological distress simultaneously. Importantly, these recommendations should be viewed as pragmatic approaches aligned with the observed associations rather than as evidence of causal effects in this cross-sectional design.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large sample size and the use of widely validated instruments (WOMAC and HADS). However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; depression may contribute to pain and disability and may also result from chronic symptoms. Second, WOMAC and HADS are questionnaire-based measures and may be subject to reporting bias. In addition, depressive symptoms were assessed using the HADS-D screening scale rather than a structured clinical psychiatric interview; therefore, misclassification is possible. Third, recruitment from a single tertiary center may limit external validity by overrepresenting patients with greater symptom severity or better access to specialized care. Fourth, some psychosocial determinants (e.g., social support, stress, detailed psychiatric history, and socioeconomic variables beyond those captured) were not comprehensively assessed and may have confounded some associations, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, radiographic data were available only for a subset of participants, and multivariable analyses including KL grade were restricted to those with available and interpretable radiographs (n = 280), which may have introduced selection bias and should be interpreted with caution.

Generalizability and future research

Although derived from a single Moroccan tertiary center, these findings may be relevant to similar populations in middle-income countries where KOA, obesity, and limited mental health resources coexist. Multicenter studies are needed to confirm these results across diverse settings and to better characterize cultural and health-system factors influencing depression in KOA. Longitudinal studies would help clarify temporal relationships, and interventional trials could evaluate whether integrated strategies (exercise programs, weight management, and psychological interventions) improve both depressive symptoms and KOA outcomes.

Conclusion

In this Moroccan cohort of patients with symptomatic KOA, nearly one-third had clinically significant depressive symptoms. Depression was associated with female sex and several potentially modifiable factors, including BMI, pain severity, functional limitation, comorbidities, and physical inactivity. Despite this burden, psychological care was infrequently used. These findings support the consideration of routine screening and integrated care approaches addressing both musculoskeletal symptoms and mental health.
