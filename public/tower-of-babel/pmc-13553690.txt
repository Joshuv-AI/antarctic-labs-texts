Effects of heart failure pharmacotherapy on natriuresis and diuresis: A narrative review
Authors: Berenika Jankowiak, Piotr Ponikowski, Jan Biegus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurohormonal overactivation and the resulting sodium avidity, driving sodium retention, are important mechanisms underlying the signs and symptoms of heart failure (HF). Blockade of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and adrenergic systems therefore represents a central therapeutic target and cornerstone of HF pharmacotherapy, theoretically counteracting sodium avidity. Paradoxically, evidence on how neurohormonal blockade affects markers of sodium avidity, natriuresis, and diuresis remains fragmented, with no integrated summary to date. This review aims to synthesize available evidence on the impact of guideline-directed medical therapy and other HF therapies on sodium excretion/diuresis in HF [Fig. 1]. Here we present comprehensive review of available data on this topic. The scarcity of high-quality studies, small sample sizes, and conflicting results preclude definitive conclusions; accordingly, we refrain from formulating any. Interpretation is further limited by the lack of a standardized method for assessing sodium excretion independent of confounders such as renal function and dietary sodium intake, which restricts reproducibility across studies.Keywords: Heart failure, Neurohormonal blockade, GDMT, Diuresis, Natriuresis, Sodium excretion.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a major challenge for contemporary medicine, being associated with high morbidity and mortality [1], while HF–related hospitalizations impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems [2, 3]. For patients, HF represents a substantial physical as well as psychological burden and is associated with a significant reduction in quality of life [4, 5].

Impaired regulation of water and electrolyte homeostasis is a key component of the disease’s pathophysiology. Pathologically excessive sodium retention, known as sodium avidity, plays a crucial role in the development of HF symptoms and congestion development [6, 7]. Sodium retention is believed to lead to volume overload and subsequently fluid accumulation [8]. These abnormalities primarily result from disease progression and are believed to be strongly correlated with excessive neurohormonal activation, including overactivation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, the adrenergic system, the natriuretic peptide system, endothelin, and others [9–13].

Natriuresis (defined as the amount of sodium excreted over a given period) appears to be the most reliable objective marker for assessing sodium avidity [14]. It is to some extent disconnected from glomerular filtration rate [15] and correlates with urinary output [16–19]. Moreover, a decline in sodium excretion has been shown to precede an episode of AHF [20]. Given these characteristics, natriuresis and spot urine Na (uNa) may be used as tools to monitor treatment effectiveness in the context of counterbalancing the body’s sodium avidity [21]. Higher natriuresis (lower sodium avidity) has been associated with more favorable outcomes [8, 15, 22–24].

Based on our understanding of pathophysiology, one may hypothesize that neurohormonal (NH) blockade should lower sodium avidity, decrease diuretic demand, and, therefore, lower the risk of HF decompensation [25, 26].

In HF, blockade of this excessive neurohormonal activation is achieved through pharmacological agents, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, (and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors) which constitute the pillars of therapy for heart failure with reduced and mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [27–33]. In this narrative review, we summarize the effects of these pillars, as well as GLP-1 RA, and SGLT-2i, on urinary sodium excretion in HF patients.

Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors

Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I) are one of the key drugs in the HF pharmacotherapy, which potentially, may indirectly and time-dependently influence urinary sodium excretion [34, 35].

Bauersachs et al. [36] provided evidence that the combination of ACE-I (trandolapril) and MRA (spironolactone) increased natriuresis and diuresis in animals with CHF.

In 1982, Hornych et al. investigated the effects of captopril in 10 patients with hypertension. Natriuresis in these patients, after initiating ACE-I, was increased, whereas the diuresis was not [26, 37]. Subsequently, Daskalopoulos et al. [38] and Gentilini et al. [39] investigated the effects of captopril in patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites, respectively. The former administered captopril at doses ranging from 50 to 150 mg to 11 patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites and observed a reduction in both urine output and urinary sodium excretion. The latter conducted a double-blinded, placebo-controlled, randomized, crossover trial involving 38 patients with liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension (13 of whom also presented with ascites) as well as 13 healthy controls. Oral administration of 12.5 mg of captopril led to a significant decrease in natriuresis in patients with ascites.

In a study conducted by Massin et al. [40], 76 patients with worsening HF were examined and treated with intravenous loop diuretics. It was demonstrated that patients receiving ACE inhibitors exhibited significantly higher diuresis and natriuresis compared with those not treated with ACE inhibitors. Importantly, neither the dose of chronically administered diuretics nor the dose of loop diuretics administered during the study influenced total urine output or urinary sodium concentration.

Marenzi et al. [41] investigated the effect of ACE-I on urinary electrolyte excretion in 33 patients with chronic HF; 15 receiving long-term ACE-I therapy and 18 not receiving ACE-I at baseline. Samples were collected at baseline and after a single session of extracorporeal ultrafiltration. At baseline, urinary output and urinary sodium concentration were similar in both groups, although urinary potassium concentration was lower in the ACE-I-treated group. After ultrafiltration, the ACE-I-treated group exhibited higher urinary potassium excretion, while urinary sodium levels remained unchanged. In contrast, the group not receiving ACE-I showed a decrease in urinary sodium concentration, and urinary potassium levels remained unchanged.

Nowack et al. [42] conducted a study assessing the effects of an ACE inhibitor (cilazapril) on natriuresis and diuresis following intravenous administration of a loop diuretic in 18 healthy, normotensive men aged 23–36 years. Patients were randomized into two groups: a low-salt diet group and a normal-salt diet group. 24 h urinary sodium excretion was measured on the sixth day after sham injection and on the seventh day after furosemide injection. A significant increase in natriuresis was observed 2–3 h after furosemide administration in participants receiving cilazapril, regardless of dietary sodium intake, compared with placebo.

Sacubitril/valsartan (angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi))

In addition to conferring the benefits associated with angiotensin receptor blockade (via valsartan), sacubitril inhibits neprilysin activity, thereby preventing the degradation of natriuretic peptides and other endogenous vasoactive peptides that counteract the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ultimately attenuating the detrimental effects of its overactivation [6, 43–46]. It is believed that this, among other effects, leads to a reduction of sodium retention and consequently to increased diuresis and natriuresis.

In the biggest study assessing the impact of ARNi on sodium avidity, Mebazaa et al. [47] in a prospective, mechanistic, non-randomized, open-label, observational study involving 216 patients with stable chronic HFrEF, investigated the effects of initiating Sacubitril/Valsartan therapy (switching from ACE inhibitors/ARBs to ARNi) over a 3-month period on natriuresis, diuresis, and congestion in response to fluid and sodium load followed by intravenous administration of 40 mg furosemide. The study had two co-primary endpoints: the average change in natriuretic response to intravascular volume expansion and to intravenous diuretic administration. Secondary endpoints included average changes in diuretic response, patient-reported dyspnea, and natriuretic peptide levels. ARNi therapy was associated with significantly increased natriuresis and diuresis both before and after diuretic administration. Additionally, patients reported less dyspnea and fewer symptoms of congestion after the IV fluid challenge. This is an important study as it was prospectively designed to detect the mechanistic signals of ARNi initiation in the CHF population, with predefined natriuretic and diuretic endpoints. The findings also suggest that ARNi therapy may be effective in preventing HF decompensation, as it enhances the ability to adapt to changes in sodium intake and increases in fluid volume. However, this was an open-label observational study with unavoidable risk of bias.

Setoguchi et al. [48] demonstrated that ARNi therapy initiation, in 60 patients with decompensated HF after hemodynamic stabilization, slightly and transiently increased urinary volume and diuretic response without impairing renal function. The effect was observed only on the first day of therapy. Younger age, higher baseline NT-proBNP levels, and abnormal body mass index were associated with a more pronounced diuretic response to ARNi therapy.

Wang et al. conducted a study [49] involving 72 Asian patients with salt-sensitive hypertension—a condition also associated with impaired sodium excretion—and compared natriuresis and diuresis between patients treated with ARNi and those receiving valsartan alone. A significantly higher level of urinary sodium excretion was observed in patients treated with the ARNi on day 1 but not on day 28. Moreover, those patients exhibited better blood pressure control and a lower level of NT-proBNP.

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists

The ATHENA-HF trial was conducted among 360 AHF patients (who previously were not treated with MRA or were treated with low dose of spironolactone), who were randomized in intervention to 100 mg spironolactone versus placebo or 25 mg spironolactone daily for 96 h. the primary endpoint was the change of NT-proBNP level and the secondary endpoints included clinical congestion, dyspnea assessment, urine output, and weight change, the additional, safety endpoints included potassium serum level and renal function. There was no significant change in log NT-proBNP level reduction, in clinical outcomes, including diuresis (the median 24-hour urine volumes were 1,100 mL and 1,183 mL, respectively (p = 0.76)), 30-days-all-cause-mortality, potassium serum level or GFR between both groups [50].

In the study by van Vliet et al., 16 patients with congestive HF and diuretic resistance, after 5 days of insufficient treatment with combination of loop diuretic and the maximum tolerable dose of an ACE-I were treated with spironolactone at a dose of 100 mg for 7 days, resulting in increased natriuresis and diuresis in 13 (81%) of patients, accompanied by a reduction of clinical symptoms [51].

A comparable therapeutic effect was reported by Ferreira et al. [52] in a study in which patients with decompensated HF received 100 mg of spironolactone daily in addition to standard therapy. Compared with the group treated with standard of care (SoC) alone, this cohort demonstrated increased spot uNa levels and a sodium-to-potassium ratio > 2. Similar conclusions were reported by Hensen et al., in whom oral administration of spironolactone at a dose of 200 mg in patients with CHF led to a significant increase in urinary sodium excretion [53].

The impact of the new non-steroidal MRA finerenone on natriuresis is unknown. Moreover, a new class of drugs, Aldosterone synthase inhibitors, has been developed and tested and, from a pathophysiological perspective, also has potential to affect sodium avidity in HF, but this needs further well-designed studies [54].

Beta-blockers

Despite their well-established clinical benefits [55–59], there is no conclusive evidence demonstrating an effect of beta-blockers on natriuresis in the HF population [60–62]. Indirect evidence, however, is provided by experimental studies; in a study by DiBona and Sawin [63] conducted in rats with myocardial infarction–induced HF, urinary sodium excretion was compared between animals treated and not treated with beta-blockers. Administration of metoprolol resulted in a statistically significant increase in urinary sodium excretion in the beta-blocker–treated group.

Sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors

SGLT-2 inhibitors are recommended in HF, irrespective of ejection fraction [64–68]. They act through multiple pathways; however, not all mechanisms of their action are known and understood [69, 70]. One of their best-characterized mechanisms of action is increased urinary glucose excretion, resulting in osmotic diuresis and increased urine output, which may be accompanied by natriuresis and collectively contribute to decongestion. Importantly, the mechanisms underlying osmotic diuresis and natriuresis are distinct, although both may increase urine output. Osmotic diuresis results from an increased amount of osmotically active solutes in the tubular part of the nephron, which promotes water content within the tubular lumen and thereby increases urine volume. In contrast, natriuresis-based decongestion results from reduced tubular sodium reabsorption, leading to increased urinary sodium excretion, with water following the excreted sodium. Of note, osmotic diuresis may also increase total urinary sodium excretion; however, this may occur predominantly through an increase in urine volume rather than an increase in urinary sodium concentration.

The EMPA-RESPONSE-AHF study [71], conducted in 80 patients, compared empagliflozin 10 mg with placebo for 30 days and demonstrated a marked and statistically significant increase in urine output in patients receiving the SGLT2 inhibitor. However, the diuretic response (defined as weight change per 40 mg furosemide) did not change significantly. In a predefined mechanistic substudy of EMPA-RESPONSE-AHF [72], empagliflozin administration in patients with AHF significantly increased urinary glucose excretion, with a peak observed at 24 h; however, it had no significant effect on urinary sodium excretion.

The SiRENA project was conducted in 49 patients without HF, including individuals with type 2 diabetes and/or chronic kidney disease. Administration of 10 mg empagliflozin was associated with a reduction in extracellular body water, an increase in copeptin, renin, and aquaporin-2 excretion, a non-significant increase in aldosterone levels, and a decrease in free water clearance. No significant changes in natriuresis or diuresis were observed [73].

This phenomenon was further elucidated in the study by Rao et al. [74], which observed a significant reduction in proximal tubular sodium reabsorption in HF patients receiving empagliflozin. However, this effect was counterbalanced by distal part adaptations: increased sodium reabsorption in the loop of Henle and the distal tubule; consequently, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors did not result in a significant change in urinary sodium concentration.

It is crucial to note that SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with a nephron adaptation effect analogous to the one observed with loop diuretics [75–77], whereby the initial natriuretic and diuretic response is progressively blunted—or even abolished—by compensatory adaptation mechanisms within the nephron [70, 78]. The best-characterized and most consistently confirmed effect of SGLT2 inhibitors, glucosuria, is accompanied by a concomitant increase in diuresis, at least during the initial period after treatment initiation, which in turn drives a transient rise in natriuresis [79, 80]. However, this increase in urine volume does not appear to be durable. This attenuation may be further compounded by a second mechanism: compensatory activation of vasopressin, which reduces urine volume and thereby offsets the initial diuretic effect of SGLT2 inhibitors.

GLP-1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists are widely recognized for their effectiveness in treating obesity [81, 82]. Their beneficial effects in obesity have been attributed, among other mechanisms, to attenuation of the inflammatory state observed in these patients [83, 84]. Over time, these properties have also been shown to confer benefits in HF, making GLP-1 receptor agonists promising therapeutic candidates for HFpEF in obese patients [85–89]; in part due to their ability to mitigate obesity-induced excessive activation of the RAA system [90] and reducing level of serum aldosterone level [91] and low-grade inflammation that is part of the disease pathophysiology [92, 93]. Wajdlich and Nowicki [94] investigated the impact of GLP-1 agonist (a single dose of 1.2 mg liraglutide) in diabetic patients (n = 34) with impaired kidney function, 17 patients with GFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 and 17 - with GFR > 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 on blood pressure profile, natriuresis, hydration and serum concentration of renin, aldosterone and atrial natriuretic peptide. Liraglutide administration resulted in increased urinary sodium excretion in both groups, with a more pronounced (median + 42 mmol/daily) effect observed in patients with GFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m².

Iron

Iron deficiency is common in HF patients and is associated with multiple comorbidities [93, 95–101]. Despite its clinical relevance, the role of iron deficiency in kidney function assessed in the context of natriuresis and diuresis remains insufficiently studied. Notably, sodium handling within the nephron is an energy-dependent and energy-consuming process, and iron supplementation may provide the energetic support required to sustain this process. This mechanism may help account for the reduced risk of decompensation, cardiovascular mortality and HF hospitalization observed in patients receiving iron supplementation in large clinical trials [102–105] but the exact mechanism of this observation remains unknown.

Clinical implications

Although the data on the direct impact of comprehensive GDMT up-titration on sodium handling in HF are not available, several clinically important findings can be derived from the STRONG-HF trial. The trial has demonstrated that a high-intensity care strategy involving rapid initiation/up-titration of comprehensive GDMT (including RASi, MRA, and beta-blockers) reduced the composite risk of HF readmission or all-cause death at 180 days in clinically stabilized patients following hospitalization for AHF [106]. A subsequent analysis showed that high-intensity care was associated with greater symptom relief, NYHA class improvement, body weight reduction, and more effective and sustained decongestion at 90 days [28]. These effects were achieved despite a lower mean dose of loop diuretics at 90-days [28]. Urinary sodium excretion was not assessed in the trial, and it remains unknown whether these effects were mediated by enhanced natriuresis. Importantly, SGLT2 inhibitors were not part of the protocol-mandated treatment strategy; therefore, we can only speculate that their addition to neurohormonal therapy might have resulted in an even greater decongestive effect Fig. 1.

Nevertheless, the available evidence is currently too limited and heterogeneous to determine whether such differences are consistent or clinically meaningful, and no firm conclusions can therefore be drawn.

Inflammation as a potential target for improvement of decongestion

Accumulating evidence implicates smoldering inflammation and adipokine dysregulation as important components of the pathophysiology of AHF, with substantial contributions from these pathways to the development of congestion [12, 13, 92]. Accordingly, therapies targeting these mechanisms have attracted growing interest, with the most extensive body of evidence for the oldest and most widely used class of anti-inflammatory drugs, namely corticosteroids. Early studies suggested that steroid treatment may enhance diuretic response in HF patients [107–111] (Table 1). A meta-analysis of 17 observational studies and 6 RCTs, comprising 22,815 patients (PROSPERO CRD420200620029), linked steroid use with greater weight loss and higher urine output; natriuresis also tended to be greater with steroid therapy, although a formal meta-analysis of this endpoint was not feasible [109]. The impact of complex HF therapies on loop diuretic requirements

Loop diuretic requirement may be regarded as an indirect measure of sodium avidity. Consistent with this, evidence from large, randomized trials across several therapeutic classes indicates that initiation and optimization of GDMT reduces diuretic demand. This has been observed with angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, SGLT-2 inhibitors, MRA and, more recently, GLP-1 receptor agonists, in which treatment was associated with lower loop diuretic doses or a reduced need for diuretic intensification during follow-up [25]. Taken together, these observations suggest more effective sodium elimination and improved homeostatic control under optimized neurohormonal blockade [25].

Challenges in detecting changes in sodium avidity induced by GDMT

Several considerations limit the ability to detect such an effect, if it exists. First, the expected magnitude of change is likely modest, and therefore difficult to demonstrate within the short follow-up and small sample sizes typical of mechanistic studies; at the same time, even a small effect may accumulate over time and eventually become clinically relevant. Second, urinary sodium excretion is an inherently noisy measure: it shows substantial interindividual variability and marked day-to-day fluctuation within the same patient, and is influenced by congestion status, renal function, background diuretic therapy, timing of assessment and, above all, dietary sodium intake, which is difficult to control even under study conditions. Third, no standardized methodology exists; studies differ in the timing of urine sampling, duration of collection, concomitant diuretic regimens and the definition of natriuretic endpoints, which limits comparability across reports. Fourth, the effect of therapy on natriuresis may not be stationary over time. Compensatory mechanisms such as nephron adaptation may attenuate, or even reverse, an initial response, so that early and late effects differ substantially. By analogy with the initial decline in eGFR observed after initiation of RASi, MRA or SGLT-2 inhibitors, the acute natriuretic signal may be qualitatively different from, or opposite in direction to, the chronic effect; assessment at a single time point may therefore be misleading. This to some extent might also explain inconsistent findings reported to date.

Additional limitations concern diuretic demand as a surrogate of sodium avidity. Although clinically meaningful, loop diuretic requirement is an imperfect reflection of sodium avidity, since titration is usually performed in relatively coarse, clinically driven steps (for example, one versus two tablets of furosemide) and is further influenced by prescriber inertia; smaller but physiologically relevant changes in the underlying need for diuretic therapy may therefore remain undetected. Finally, the available data derive from heterogeneous populations, encompassing different HF phenotypes as well as non-HF cohorts such as patients with hypertension, and no prospective study has been specifically designed to assess the impact of disease-modifying therapies on natriuresis in HF. The present observations should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present review should be acknowledged. First, the number of studies specifically designed to evaluate the effects of the discussed drug classes on natriuresis and diuresis is small. Second, the available investigations were conducted over several decades, during which background HF therapy evolved substantially; effects observed in earlier trials were therefore obtained against a therapeutic background that differs markedly from contemporary comprehensive GDMT, which limits their direct extrapolation to present-day practice. Third, the studies typically enrolled small and heterogeneous patient populations, and direct head-to-head comparisons between drug classes are essentially absent, precluding any ranking of their relative natriuretic potency. Finally, this review was conducted as a narrative rather than a systematic synthesis; no formal search strategy or quantitative pooling was applied, and selective reporting of positive natriuretic findings cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Despite the clinical importance of natriuresis as a marker of disease severity and treatment response in HF, many aspects of sodium handling in this population remain incompletely understood, and studies comprehensively addressing the impact of neurohormonal blockade on urinary sodium excretion are lacking. The available data suggest that ACE-I, ARNi, and MRA may enhance urinary sodium excretion, whereas a consistent chronic natriuretic effect has not been demonstrated for SGLT-2 inhibitors, despite their documented acute diuretic/natriuretic action.

These observations should, however, be interpreted with caution, as the heterogeneity and methodological limitations of the available evidence preclude any definitive conclusions.
