B7H6 is the predominant activating ligand driving natural killer cell-mediated killing in patients with liquid tumours: evidence from clinical, in silico, in vitro, and in vivo studies
Authors: Sunyoung Lee, Seok Joo Chae, In-Hwan Jang, Se-Chan Oh, Seok-Min Kim, Soo Yun Lee, Ji Hyun Kim, Jesang Ko, Hang J Kim, Ik-Chan Song, Jae Kyoung Kim, Tae-Don Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNatural killer (NK) cells are a subset of innate lymphoid cells that are inherently capable of recognizing and killing infected or tumour cells. This has positioned NK cells as a promising live drug for tumour immunotherapy, but limited success suggests incomplete knowledge of their killing mechanism. NK cell-mediated killing involves a complex decision-making process based on integrating activating and inhibitory signals from various ligand-receptor repertoires. However, the relative importance of the different activating ligand-receptor interactions in triggering NK killing remains unclear.MethodsWe employed a systematic approach combining clinical, in silico, in vitro, and in vivo data analysis to quantify the impact of various activating ligands. Clinical data analysis was conducted using massive pan-cancer data (n = 10,595), where patients with high NK cell levels were stratified using CIBERSORT. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis were performed based on activating ligand expression. To examine the impact of ligand expression on NK killing at the cellular level, we assessed surface expression of five major activating ligands (B7H6, MICA/B, ULBP1, ULBP2/5/6, and ULBP3) of human tumour cell lines of diverse origins (n = 33) via flow cytometry (FACs) and their NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity on by calcein-AM assay using human primary NK cells and NK-92 cell lines. Based on this data, we quantified the contribution of each activating ligand to the NK killing activity using mathematical models and Bayesian statistics. To further validate the results, we performed calcein-AM assays upon ligand knockdown and overexpression, conjugation assays, and co-culture assays in activating ligand-downregulated/overexpressed in liquid tumour (LT) cell lines. Moreover, we established LT-xenograft mouse models to assess the efficacy of NK cell targeting toward tumours with dominant ligands.FindingsThrough the clinical analysis, we discovered that among nearly all 18 activating ligands, only patients with LT who were NK cell-rich and specifically had higher B7H6 level exhibited a favorable survival outcome (p = 0.0069). This unexpected dominant role of B7H6 was further confirmed by the analysis of datasets encompassing multiple ligands and a variety of tumours, which showed that B7H6 exhibited the highest contribution to NK killing among five representative ligands. Furthermore, LT cell lines (acute myeloid leukemia (AML), B cell lymphoma, and T-acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL)) with lowered B7H6 demonstrated decreased susceptibility to NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity compared to those with higher levels. Even within the same cell line, NK cells selectively targeted cells with higher B7H6 levels. Finally, LT-xenograft mouse models (n = 24) confirmed that higher B7H6 results in less tumour burden and longer survival in NK cell-treated LT mice (p = 0.0022).InterpretationWhile NK cells have gained attention for their potent anti-tumour effects without causing graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), thus making them a promising off-the-shelf therapy, our limited understanding of NK killing mechanisms has hindered their clinical application. This study illuminates the crucial role of the activating ligand B7H6 in driving NK cell killing, particularly in the context of LT. Therefore, the expression level of B7H6 could serve as a prognostic marker for patients with LT. Moreover, for the development of NK cell-based immunotherapy, focusing on increasing the level of B7H6 on its cognate receptor, NKp30, could be the most effective strategy.FundingThis work was supported by the National Research Council of Science & Technology (NST) grant (CAP-18-02-KRIBB, GTL24021-000), a National Research Foundation grant (2710012258, 2710004815), and an Institute for Basic Science grant (IBS-R029-C3).Keywords: B7H6, Natural killer cell, NK killing, Activating ligand, Major activating ligand, Liquid tumour

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed on April 22, 2024, for relevant articles published from database inception using the search terms “NK cells” AND “activating ligands”. Although over 100 studies have evaluated individual activating ligands, none have yet analyzed the comparative potency across various activating ligands, which reflects the physiological state. We also searched for relevant articles using the search terms “NK cells”, “Clinical”, “in vitro”, and “in vivo” with proper Boolean operators in the same databases, on the same dates. While over 10,000 studies employed a single-level approach, no studies utilized a combined approach incorporating clinical data analysis alongside in silico/in vitro/in vivo experiments.

Added value of this study

Our study utilized a clinical analysis to assess the significance of all identified activating ligands. Among these ligands, B7H6 emerged as the sole correlate with survival in patients with LT. To support this finding, we quantified the contribution of each activating ligand to the NK killing activity with in silico analysis. This analysis also predicted that B7H6 is the most potent activating ligand inducing NK cell-mediated killing against LT. This was validated by high-quality evidence across multiple scales. This evidence included calcein-AM assays, conjugation assays, and co-culture assays in B7H6-downregulated/overexpressed LT cell lines. Finally, the established LT xenograft mouse models confirmed that higher B7H6 levels enhance NK cell killing efficiency against LT.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our data demonstrate that the B7H6-NKp30 interaction is a dominant inducer of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Unlike other activating ligands, high B7H6 expression correlated with improved prognosis in patients with LT. Furthermore, in vitro NK-mediated cytotoxicity against LT cell lines and in vivo survival of LT-xenograft models significantly increased with higher B7H6 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that the B7H6-NKp30 pathway is the most promising for further development of NK-based immunotherapies.

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphoid cells that can recognize and kill transformed cells, including virus-infected cells and tumour cells. Due to their high efficacy while causing minimal side effects,1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 NK cells represent a promising form of tumour immunotherapy. However, using NK cells in their natural state as a living drug has had limited success in various clinical trials due to the difficulty of controlling NK killing activity.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 To overcome this challenge and develop effective anti-tumour therapies utilizing NK cells, it is crucial to understand how various activating and inhibitory signals regulate NK killing activity.8,11,12

The exploration of the NK killing mechanism commenced over half a century ago with the identification of “missing-self” by Karre and Ljunggen.13 They observed that NK cells target cells lacking expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-Class 1 molecules. In cases where cells express MHC-Class 1 and are recognized as “self,” activating signals from various activating ligands such as MICA/B, ULBP families, and B7H6 induce NK killing if they outweigh inhibitory signals.9,10,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Despite the fact that activating ligand-receptor pairs are not equally potent, no studies have investigated which pairs are the most potent (Fig. 1a).10 This is because existing studies have regulated only one ligand at a time, and focused solely on the qualitative effect (i.e., either activating or inhibitory) on NK killing activity. Thus, a fundamental question about NK killing remains unanswered: Among the various types of activating signals, which signal is the major determinant of NK killing activity?

A systematic approach is required to identify key activating ligands in NK killing. (a) NK cells recognize tumours via specific and varied ligand-receptor interactions (denoted by different shapes and colors). NK cells make the decision to kill tumours or not by integrating signals from various receptors. However, the specific contribution (impact) of each activating ligand on this NK cell killing decision remains unclear, impeding the identification of key activating ligands (red question mark). (b) To address this, our study utilizes a systematic approach combining in clinical, in silico, in vitro, and in vivo data analyses.

To address this gap, we conducted a quantitative and systematic investigation into the impact of activating signals on NK cell killing (Fig. 1b). Our clinical analysis (n = 10,595) showed that only B7H6 among nearly all identified activating ligands (n = 18) is correlated with a better prognosis. To further validate this unexpected result, we integrated the data on surface ligand expressions and NK-mediated cytotoxicity from various tumour cell lines (n = 33), and then quantified the contribution of each ligand to NK killing using mathematical models and Bayesian statistics. Our in silico analysis revealed that B7H6 predominates in NK killing among five major activating ligands, especially in liquid tumour (LT) cell lines. This discovery was further validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments, where regulating B7H6 most strongly affected NK-mediated cytotoxicity. Our approach suggests that B7H6 is the most promising therapeutic target in NK cell-based therapy for LT and may also be a high value predictive marker of response to NK cell-based therapy.

Methods

Study design and data sources for clinical analysis

RNA-sequencing expression data and clinical information of the 10,595 patients with 37 types of tumours were downloaded from the genomic data commons (GDC) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). The patients with solid tumours (ST; n = 9960) were derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) dataset for the various tumour types (n = 33) (See Table 1, Table 2 for details). The patients with LT (n = 2406) were used from 2 types of TCGA datasets and 5 independent datasets: The Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET)- acute myeloid leukemia (AML) as AML1 (n = 187), TCGA as AML2 (n = 151), TARGET-acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL)-P2 as B-ALL (n = 267) and T-ALL (n = 265), Oregon Health and Science University (OHSU)- Chronic myeloproliferative disorders (CNL) as CNL (n = 51), BEATAML1.0 (OHSU-AML) as AML3 (n = 450), diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBC) from TCGA (n = 47), the Cancer Genome Characterization Initiative-Burkitt Lymphoma Genome Sequencing Project (CGCI-BLGSP) as mature B-cell lymphoma (MBL) (n = 112), and The Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF)-COMMPASS as plasma cell tumours (PCL) (n = 787). We classified all kinds of lymphoma as LT because of their cellular origin. In TARGET-ALL-P2, because T-and B-ALL are phenotypically and molecularly different, we analyzed them separately. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million (FPKM) values transformed to a log 2 scale were used for analysis.

The workflow for clinical analysis is presented in Fig. 2. It involves the use of TCGA pan-cancer data for ST (n = 9289) and five datasets for LT (n = 1306), all of which are primary tumour samples with available vital records in biospecimen or clinical files. These datasets were used to estimate hazard ratios and conduct survival analysis. Among the nine LT datasets, four datasets (T-ALL in TARGET-ALL-P2, OHSU-AML, OHSU-CNL, and TCGA-DLBC) were excluded from risk ratio and survival analysis due to challenges in assessing survival. Specifically, the T-ALL dataset in TARGET-ALL-P2 was excluded because the survival rate for the NK cell-rich T-ALL group was 100%. The OHSU-AML was excluded from clinical analysis due to the absence of duration of survival, and the OHSU-CNL and TCGA-DLBC datasets were excluded due to the low number of NK cell-rich samples (n < 10). To avoid data bias from different datasets, each tumour type or dataset was normalized using standardization separately before integrating datasets.

Workflow of clinical data analysis. RNA-sequencing expression data of the 10,595 patients with 37 types of tumours were downloaded from the genomic data commons (GDC) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Using the computational quantification of immune cells via CIBERSORT, the samples were divided into two groups: an NK cell-rich group, with NK cell ratios ranging from 50-80%–100% (statistically significant correlation, p < 0.05), and an NK cell-poor group, with NK cell ratios up to 50–80%. TCGA pan-cancer for ST (n = 9289) and 5 datasets for LT (n = 1306), which are samples of primary tumours and existing information of vital records in biospecimens or clinical files, were used to perform survival analysis. In detail, T-ALL was excluded because the survival rate for NK cell-rich patients was 100%, and TCGA-DLBC and OHSU-CNL were excluded due to an insufficient number of NK cell-rich patients for meaningful survival analysis (n < 10). The association between NK cell activating ligands and CD8 T cell or CD4 T cell-rich patients was also investigated to confirm whether the effect was dependent on NK cells or all tumour-infiltrated lymphocytes.

Sex information was obtained from the publicly available datasets in the GDC Data Portal, which aggregates clinical data from multiple studies. The data used in this study primarily rely on medical records rather than self-reported information.

Procedures for clinical analysis

To construct the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) plot of expression profiles for pan-cancer, the genes of the NK cell activating ligands involved in NK killing (n = 18) were used as input data.19,20 The expression level of each sample was standardized before merging the expression profile of pan-cancer. The perplexity parameter was set to 30 and the plot was embedded in two dimensions. Expression patterns were shown in dot plots from the expression of ligands.

CIBERSORT was used for the computational deconvolution of immune cells for the 10,595 patients from gene expression data (https://cibersort.stanford.edu).21 Among the expression values available through the GDC portal, we employed log2-scaled FPKM values for the deconvolution method.22 The created gene expression file with the 10,595 cases was uploaded to CIBERSORT as a mixture file, and CIBERSORT was run with the following options: LM22 reference file, 500 permutations, and quantile normalization disabled. The frequency of NK cells included activated NK cells and resting NK cells in an LM22 reference file. The samples, which were the distribution of NK cell ratios from 50 to 80%–100% with statistically significant correlation (p < 0.05), were called the NK cell-rich group, and the others (up to 50–80%) were called the NK cell-poor group. To avoid data bias, we performed the division of groups based on the relative proportion of NK cells separately for each dataset.

Multivariate Cox regression models (95% confidence intervals) were performed using 18 genes of known activating ligands to estimate the significance of overall survival in patients. For detailed information on the activating ligands, refer to Appendix 1 p 1. Survival curves were visualized using Kaplan–Meier (KM) plots and log-rank tests, comparing the ligands-high and ligands-low groups divided by the median cut-off for each activating ligand.

Study design for in vitro/in vivo analysis

For the quantitative study of different activating ligands (n = 5), we performed in silico analysis based on the surface ligand expression data and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity data. We utilized flow cytometry (FACs) to investigate the surface expression of activating ligands (n = 5) on various human tumour cell lines (n = 33) from various origins. We also measured NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against each tumour cell line using human primary NK (pNK) cells and the NK-92 cell line. With the obtained experimental data, the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity prediction model was constructed and analyzed to confirm which ligand was dominant. We then performed in vitro experiments including calcein-AM assay, conjugation assay, co-culture assay, and western blotting, and in vivo experiments including blood analysis, bioluminescence imaging (BLI), and survival analysis in LT-xenograft mice (n = 24).

Mathematical modeling and Gibbs variable selection (GVS)

To quantify the contribution of five representative activating ligands to NK killing, we developed a mathematical model predicting cytotoxicity with activating ligand expression, specifically, the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity of a cell line assuming additive impact of individual ligands, following previous studies.23,24 Then, we fitted the model to incorporate the relation between the NK-mediated cytotoxicity and surface ligand expression levels. In addition to the model, we also constructed 25 models as its variants. Then, each model was fitted to account for the experimentally measured pNK (or NK-92)-mediated cytotoxicity of LT and ST groups. Subsequently, we utilized the Akaike information criterion (AIC)25, 26, 27 to assess each model and identify the key combinations of activating ligands in each group. Finally, the statistical significance of each activating ligand in predicting pNK-mediated cytotoxicity was assessed using the GVS with the model.28

Construction of mathematical models predicting NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity with surface expression of tumour ligands

To assess the contribution of representative activating ligands to NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, we developed a mathematical model predicting cytotoxicity with activating ligand expression. Since NK cells express a sufficient amount of activating receptors, including NKG2D and NCR families, we assumed that receptor expressions are saturated, and thus the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity is mainly determined by ligand expression. Specifically, the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity of a cell line is described by a sum of the contribution of each ligand, as in previous studies.23,24 The contribution of each ligand was described by a Hill function following a previous study,29 with the Hill exponent of 2. We assumed that the Hill function describing the contribution of each ligand has the same normalized expression at half of the maximum contribution to reduce the number of parameters used in the fitting. Measuring NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity

NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity was measured with the Calcein-AM (Thermofisher, C1430) release assay as previously described.32 Target tumour cells were stained with Calcein-AM, a cell-permeant dye, for 1 hr at 37 °C. The labeled cells (1 × 104 cells) and serially diluted effector cells were co-cultured in 96 well round-bottom culture plates (SPL, 34096) for 4 hr at 37 °C after staining. Released Calcein-AM into the supernatant was measured using a multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) after removing the supernatant to 96 well black plates (SPL, 30496). The maximum level of released Calcein-AM was estimated based on the addition of 2% Triton X-100 (Merk, 108643), final volume to the target cells. The spontaneous level of released Calcein-AM was estimated by adding culture medium to the target cells. The percentage of specific lysis was calculated according to the following formula: (sample release—spontaneous release)/(maximum release—spontaneous release) × 100 (%). In addition, when measuring the cytotoxicity of 33 cell lines, we set the effector:target (E:T) ratio that can best show the difference of cytotoxicity between cell lines. In particular, since pNK kills target cells more sensitively than NK-92, we used lower ratios (0.25:1, 0.5:1, and 1:1) than NK-92's ratios (1:1, 5:1, and 10:1) in the assay.

In vivo experiments

Since B7H6 is expressed specifically in humans, a humanized mouse model was required. Therefore, a humanized mouse was constructed by injecting a human tumour cell line expressing B7H6 into NSIG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid II2rgtm1Sug/Jic, 5–8 weeks) mice. The humanized mouse model allowed us to investigate the killing of human NK cells against human tumour cells under in vivo conditions, but it was limited in investigating the unique response of NK cells in the mouse body. Before the experiment, we established all the protocols and proceeded as follows. Due to the nature of the in vivo experiment, six mice per group were used to account for the number of outliers. Specifically, a total of NSIG mice were purchased from Saeronbio, and mice were randomly divided into two groups before tumour injection. Random numbers were generated using the standard = RAND () function in Microsoft Excel. U937-shControl-Luc (n = 6) or U937-shB7H6-Luc cells (n = 6) (1 × 106 cells) were intravenously (i.v) injected into mice (6 weeks) on day 0. pNK (1 × 107 cells) or PBS were injected i.v. on days 0, 3, 5, and 8. Successful differentiation of pNK cells was confirmed by FACs before injection. Tumour burden was estimated every 2–3 days with BLI. XenoLight D-luciferin, potassium (Perkin Elmer, 122799) was applied by intraperitoneal (i.p) injection and incubated 15 min before capturing. No mice were excluded from the experiment. Mice with BLI values that differed too much were excluded from representing BLI data, but no mice were excluded in analyzing survival rate. Blood analysis was proceeded on day 20. Blood was extracted from the tail vein. After treating ACK, 5 min at 37 °C to remove RBC, human CD45 was stained and analyzed by FACs. Survival rate analysis, BLI measurements, and blood analysis were analyzed by individuals who did not undergo tumour and NK cell injections, and the individual didn't know which group received U937_ctrl or U937_shB7H6. The study did not have a humane endpoint because of the survival analysis. To minimize potential confounders, injection and tumour growth measurements were always performed in the same order and cages for each group were always located in the same place.

Quantification and statistical analyses

In vitro experimental samples were tested in duplicate or triplicate, and experiments were performed three to eight times independently. Statistical significance was assessed with Pearson correlation test and Student's t-tests. The Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) Test was used to assess the significance of the in vivo survival rate. All experimental data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism (Version 5.01, GraphPad Software), and clinical data were analyzed by the R software package (ver. 3.6.3). Results

Only B7H6 levels among numerous activating ligands correlate with the prognosis of patients with LT

To explore the impact of activating ligands on NK cell activity for patients with various types of tumour, we used the RNA expression profiles of the ligands from patients with 37 types of tumour (n = 10,595) using data from the GDC data portal (Table 1, Table 2). We observed distinct gene expression profiles between LT and ST (Fig. 3a; Appendix 1 p 2). This prompted us to perform survival analysis for the LT and ST groups separately, to assess which ligands are most sensitively associated with patient survival. For each group (LT and ST), we categorized patients based on their relative NK cell levels to confirm whether the clinical relevance of activating ligands is attributed to NK cell activity (Fig. 3b). Specifically, for each group, we quantified the proportion of NK cells in each sample via a computational quantification method, CIBERSORT. Patients were further subdivided into two categories: those with a relatively high proportion of NK cells among immune cells (referred to as NK cell-rich) and those with a relatively low proportion of NK cells (referred to as NK cell-poor) (Fig. 3b), employing various thresholds (top 50%, 40%, 30%, and 20%). After dividing patients into these subgroups, we conducted multivariate cox regression for each activating ligand to identify ligands associated with patient survival (Fig. 3b). To avoid data bias from different datasets, we divided patients into NK cell-rich and NK cell-poor groups based on the relative proportion of NK cells within each dataset. We then normalized the ligand expression levels before splitting patients into ligand-high and ligand-low groups (Appendix 1 p 3).

Among nearly all identified activating ligands (Appendix 1 p 1, Supplemental Table S1), B7H6 was the only activating ligand whose increased expression significantly reduced the risk ratio for LT across various thresholds (Fig. 3c; Appendix 1 p 2, panel a–b). Notably, as the threshold for the NK cell-rich group increased from the top 50% to the top 20%, the risk ratio decreased more drastically (Appendix 1 p 4). This indicates that B7H6, uniquely among the 18 activating ligands examined, significantly influences the survival of patients with LT. In contrast, for NK cell-rich ST groups, no activating ligand demonstrated clear relevance to survival (Appendix 1 p 5). While MICB and B7H6 were correlated with increased survival of patients with ST, the association was weaker compared to the correlation between B7H6 and survival of patients with LT (Appendix 1 p 5). In addition, B7H6 expression was not associated with improved survival when analyzed in relation to CD4 or CD8 T cell proportions (Fig. 3c–d; Appendix 1 pp 6–7). This indicates that the prolonged survival in patients with LT who have higher B7H6 levels is primarily due to NK killing activity.

To further characterize the observed association between B7H6 expression and survival, we generated KM curves for patients with LT. We divided the LT group into B7H6-high and B7H6-low groups based on the median B7H6 level. Consistent with our earlier findings (Fig. 3c), patients with high B7H6 expression in the NK cell-rich group (especially the top 30%) showed significantly improved survival (Fig. 3e, p = 0.0069, Log-rank test). This association became weaker as the threshold for the NK cell-rich group decreased from 20% to 50% (Appendix 1 p 5, panel c). Finally, for the NK cell-poor group, there was no difference in survival depending on the B7H6 expression level (Fig. 3e). This further supports a model in which higher B7H6 levels lead to prolonged patient survival via NK killing.

We next explored whether this survival benefit of high B7H6 expression applies across all LT types. We analyzed survival benefits by each dataset: PCL, B-ALL, MBL, AML1, and AML2. The PCL, B-ALL, and MBL types displayed a similar trend, with patients in the B7H6-high group showing better prognoses (Fig. 3f–h, p = 0.0111, 0.0041, and 0.0332, respectively, Log-rank test). While the AML types did not exhibit a statistically significant effect, the B7H6-high group within the AML types still exhibited a trend toward better prognosis compared to the B7H6-high group in the NK cell-poor patients (Fig. 3i–j).

Mathematical analysis predicts that B7H6 is a representative activating ligand inducing the NK killing response in LT

Although only B7H6 was found to be significant for survival, our clinical analysis could be influenced by potential dataset bias. To determine whether the significant role of B7H6 is independent of such biases, we conducted additional in vitro, in silico, and in vivo studies. As a first step, we investigated how B7H6 expression affects the susceptibility of tumour cell lines to NK cell-mediated killing. We selected five well-investigated activating ligands, including B7H6 (B7H6, MICA/B, ULBP1, ULBP2/5/6, and ULBP3),34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 from those considered in the clinical study (Appendix 1 p 1, Supplemental Table S1).9,10,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 We then measured the levels of these five major NK cell-activating ligands and the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity of 33 tumour cell lines using two types of NK cells: primary NK cells differentiated from human CB (pNK) (Appendix 1 p 8, panel a–c) and the NK-92 cell line (Appendix 1 p 8, panel d–e), with varying effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (E:T = 0.25:1, 0.5:1 and 1:1 for pNK, E:T = 1:1, 5:1 and 10:1 for NK-92, see Appendix 1 p 9 for details) (Fig. 4a–b).

Consistent with the bulk mRNA sequencing data (Fig. 3a), hierarchical clustering40 based on surface ligand expression levels separated the cell lines into two subgroups, LT and ST (Fig. 4b). To understand how these ligands contribute to NK killing activity, we analyzed the relationship between the expression levels of the five major NK cell-activating ligands and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity in each group separately (LT and ST). For the LT group, B7H6 emerged as the sole activating ligand whose surface expression strongly correlated with cytotoxicity mediated by both pNK and NK-92 cells (Fig. 4c; Appendix 1 p 9, panel a–c) across varying E:T ratios, which is consistent with the survival analysis (Fig. 3). As other ligands (MICA/B, ULBP1, ULBP2/5/6, and ULBP3) share a common receptor, NKG2D, we also investigated the correlation between the combined expression of NKG2D ligands and NK-mediated cytotoxic value. However, their combined expression did not correlate significantly with cytotoxicity (p > 0.8 for every E:T ratio, Pearson's correlation test). Additionally, we also examined three other activating ligands that tend to work effectively as activating ligands in our analysis (Fig. 3). These ligands did not exhibit any significant correlation, further underscoring the importance of B7H6 (Appendix 1 p 9, panel d). To determine whether this dominance is restricted to short-term cytotoxicity (less than 4 h), we extended our investigation to longer-term cytotoxicity. The dominance of B7H6 remained robust over the extended time period of 24 hr (Appendix 1 p 9, panel e). In contrast, no such strong correlation was observed in ST (Fig. 4c; Appendix 1 p 9, panel f–h).

We further investigated the nonlinear combined contribution of the five activating ligands to NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, which the simple correlation cannot detect, by developing mathematical models (n = 25) based on all possible combinations of the five activating ligands (Fig. 4d). Across all combinations, models that accurately reproduced NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity for LT consistently showed B7H6 as a highly effective ligand in influencing cytotoxicity, while MICA/B was identified as only weakly effective (Fig. 4e). Notably, models including B7H6 demonstrated up to a 25% reduction in error, as measured by AIC, compared to those that did not include B7H6 as an effective ligand. In contrast, the other three ligands did not show a consistent pattern of effectiveness (Fig. 4e). The strong role of B7H6 in LT was also observed when the models were fitted to the NK-92-mediated cytotoxicity (Appendix 1 p 10, panel a). On the other hand, in ST, no ligand, including B7H6, showed a significant contribution to NK killing (Fig. 4e; Appendix 1 p 10, panel a). The dominant role of B7H6 in LT but not in ST was also supported by the GVS, which quantifies the statistical significance of each ligand to predict cytotoxicity (Fig. 4f; Appendix 1 p 10, panel b–c).28 Moreover, 77% of the variance in pNK-mediated cytotoxicity for LT was well explained by a model focusing solely on the relationship between B7H6 expression and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity in LT (Fig. 4g). To test whether the dominant role of B7H6 is due to a bias in the dataset we used (Fig. 4b), we utilized additional LT cell lines (n = 5) for the validation. The pNK-mediated cytotoxicity of these additional cell lines was also accurately predicted by the model (Fig. 4h).

The dominant effect of B7H6 was confirmed in various LT cell lines

To support the clinical and in silico analyses that B7H6 plays a predominant role in the NK killing decision, we knocked down (KD) B7H6 in various LT cell lines with high expression of B7H6 (U937 in AML, Raji in B-cell lymphoma, and Jurkat in T-ALL) and assessed its effect on the NK killing response, including the formation of cellular conjugates between NK cells and tumours, an indicator of the initial stage of the killing process where the NK cell forms an immune synapse with the target, and the phosphorylation of NK cell activation signals pI3K and ERK (Fig. 5a; Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel a). B7H6 KD resulted in a significant decrease in pNK-mediated (U937; p < 0.0001, Raji; p < 0.0001 Jurkat; p < 0.001, two-tailed Student's t-test) and NK-92-mediated cytotoxicity (U937; p < 0.001, Raji; p < 0.001, Jurkat; p < 0.0001, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Fig. 5b; Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel b), the formation of cellular conjugates between NK cells and tumours (U937; p = 0.005 for 2 hr, p = 0.004 for 3 hrs, p = 0.014 for 4 hr, Raji; p = 0.0068 for 1 hr, p = 0.0017 for 2 hr, p = 0.00625 for 4 hr, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Fig. 5c; Appendix 1 p 11–12, panel c), and phosphorylation levels of the NK cell activation signaling molecules,41,42 pI3K and ERK (Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel d). Consistently, when we overexpressed (OE) B7H6 in the low-B7H6 LT cell line THP-1 (Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel e), we observed both an increase in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity (pNK; p < 0.01, NK-92; p < 0.001, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel f–g) and cellular conjugation (p = 0.0013 for 4 hr, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel h). In addition, when co-cultured with pNK cells, B7H6 KD U937 cells showed low dead cell populations (7-AAD+) compared to controls (p = 0.0064 for 1 hr, p = 0.0122 for 2 hr, p < 0.0001 for 4 hr, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 pp 11–12, panel i–j). Consequently, we conclude that NK cells target and kill specifically B7H6-high LT, which is consistent with the clinical analysis (Fig. 3c–j).

NK killing is related not only to the expression of B7H6 but also to its interaction with its cognate receptor, NKp30. To determine whether the B7H6-NKp30 axis effectively regulates NK killing, we examined the NKp30 KD pNK cells (Appendix 1 p 13, panel a)-mediated cytotoxicity changes on B7H6 KD LT cells (Raji and U937) (Appendix 1 p 13, panel b). At this time, NKp30 KD pNK cells showed cytotoxicity values that were independent of the expression level of B7H6 (Raji; p = 0.69, U937; p = 0.49, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 p 13, panel c), different from control pNK cells (Raji; p = 0.015, U937; p = 0.002, two-tailed Student's t-test), indicating that the B7H6-NKp30 axis effectively influences NK killing.

To confirm the influence of the B7H6-NKp30 axis on NK killing in patient cells, we examined the population of dead cells (7-AAD+) after coculture of NKp30 KD pNK cells with patient-derived AML cells (Fig. 5d–f, Appendix 1 p 13, panels d–e). These AML cells exhibited a significantly lower percentage of dead cells compared to the control group (p = 0.04, paired t-test) (Fig. 5f), supporting the effect of the B7H6-NKp30 axis in NK killing against LT cells.

We used cytokine-differentiated human pNK cells and NK-92 cell lines to assess the importance of B7H6. To explore whether pNK cells in their natural state, without cytokine differentiation, have a distinct receptor subset compared to these two types of NK cells, we examined their NK cell receptor expression levels. We found that fresh pNK cells without cytokines (Appendix 1 p 14, panel a) had lower levels of activating receptors and higher levels of inhibitory receptors than differentiated NK cells (Appendix 1 p 8, panels a–c). To determine whether these differences in receptor subsets influence the impact of B7H6 on NK cell-mediated killing, we measured the cytotoxicity of fresh pNK cells against B7H6 KD Raji cells. The results showed that fresh pNK cells also experienced a significant decrease in cytotoxicity when B7H6 was downregulated (Appendix 1, p. 14, panel b; Pearson's correlation = 0.90, p-value = 0.04), indicating that all three types of NK cells with different receptor subsets rely on B7H6-dependent killing.

We further investigated the amount of B7H6 expression needed to achieve optimal NK killing capacity. To do so, we conducted experiments with varying levels of B7H6 expression in two different LT cell lines (U937 and Raji) and assessed NK killing at each B7H6 level (Fig. 5g–h). Surprisingly, both U937 and Raji exhibited a sharp increase in NK killing when B7H6 expression was between MFI ratios of 2.5 and 4.5 (Fig. 5h). This suggests that despite the diversity among LT subtypes, a similar amount of B7H6 is needed to trigger optimal NK cell killing.

Finally, we conducted a comparative analysis to assess the impact of modulating B7H6 on NK killing efficiency compared to MICB, one of the well-known activating ligands. Specifically, we modulated differential expressions of B7H6 and MICB in THP-1 and evaluated their sensitivity to NK killing (Fig. 5g). Compared to B7H6, varying MICB expression had a lesser effect on NK killing against THP-1 cells, suggesting a less pronounced influence on NK cells (Fig. 5i). In both Jurkat and Raji cells, downregulation of ULBP2 and MICAB led to a reduction in NK cell killing, but this effect was less pronounced than when B7H6 was downregulated. In Jurkat cells, the reduction in killing was significant for B7H6 (p = 0.003, two-tailed Student's t-test) and MICAB (p = 0.0038, two-tailed Student's t-test), but not for ULBP2 (p = 0.157, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 p 15, panels a–b). Similarly, in Raji cells, downregulation of B7H6 significantly reduced NK killing (p = 0.02, two-tailed Student's t-test), while ULBP2 had a weaker, non-significant effect (p = 0.108, two-tailed Student's t-test) (Appendix 1 p 15, panels c–d). This observation aligns with the clinical data analysis (Fig. 3c–d) and the predictions derived from the mathematical analysis that B7H6 has a greater effect on NK killing than other activating ligands including MICA/B and ULBP 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 4d–f).

NK cells sensitively target B7H6-high tumours in LT-xenograft mice

To explore the anti-tumour activity of NK cells against B7H6-high LT in vivo, we established LT-xenograft mouse models by transplanting either control (shControl-Luc) or B7H6-KD (shB7H6-Luc) U937 cells (Fig. 6a). In addition, we intravenously injected human pNK cells, which were expanded in vitro with cytokines (IL-15, IL-21, and HC) over four doses across 8 days. After tumour transplantation, tumour growth was monitored by blood analysis (on day 20 after post-transplant), BLI every 2–4 days, and survival analysis. Treatment of mice with pNK resulted in a reduction of CD45+ (human leukocyte common antigen) cells in the blood (Fig. 6b) of control U937-transplanted mice (p < 0.0001, two-tailed Student's t-test). In addition, the tumour burden, as measured by BLI, decreased in pNK-treated mice compared to those treated with PBS (Fig. 6c). The median survival of mice transplanted with shControl-Luc cells increased from 20.5 days to 24 days following pNK treatment (p = 0.0022, ordinary one-way ANOVA) (Fig. 6d), highlighting the efficacy of NK cells. However, the changes in tumour growth in shB7H6-Luc-transplanted mice following pNK treatment were minimal (Fig. 6e–g). Treatment of shB7H6-Luc-transplanted mice with pNK cells reduced the CD45+ cells in the blood (p = 0.035, two-tailed Student's t-test), but the reduction was not significant compared to control mice (Fig. 6e). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in tumour burden, as assessed by BLI (Fig. 6f), or in survival (Fig. 6g). These results suggest that NK cells can effectively target B7H6-high LT cells also in vivo.

Discussion

Our study reveals B7H6 as a predominant factor in promoting NK killing of LT cells. Through our comprehensive analysis of pan-cancer data, we have elucidated that individuals with elevated levels of B7H6 manifest a more favorable prognosis compared to those with lower levels. The dominance of B7H636,43, 44, 45 in patients with LT is further supported by previous studies where expression levels of NKp30, the specific receptor for B7H6, were found to correlate with the survival of patients with LT.46 This was further supported by in silico analysis based on the data from 33 tumour cell lines of various origins, which predicted that B7H6 has a dominant effect on NK killing against LT, which we further confirmed with in vitro and in vivo experiments. This suggests that B7H6 is the sole feasible target candidate, at least among the 18 activating ligands we examined, for treating patients with LT using NK cell-based therapies. In addition, because B7H6 is expressed specifically in tumours, targeting B7H6 has the added benefit of providing a safe and effective treatment that targets and kills tumour cells without killing normal cells.

This study highlights the potential role of B7H6 expression in selecting patients for NK cell-based immunotherapy in LT (Fig. 3). Previous studies have shown that B7H6 is expressed in many different tumour types, including lymphoma and leukemia.47 Our findings suggest that patients with high B7H6 expression in their tumour cells may benefit more significantly from this therapy (Fig. 3c). This aligns with recent research highlighting B7H6's role in promoting NK cell activation and tumour cell death, particularly in hematological malignancies.48 Therefore, B7H6 expression could serve as a potential biomarker to predict a patient's response to NK cell-based therapy for LT. However, some AML datasets show bad prognoses with B7H6 expression, which is likely due to B7H6's intrinsic cell signaling (Fig. 3i). B7H6 promotes cell proliferation and migration via various signaling pathways such as STAT3 and RAS in hematological tumour cells.49,50 It is also associated with immune cell activation through genes like MYC, IFNG, TNF, JUN, and FOS.48,51 Given these findings, while B7H6 generally promotes favorable outcomes through NK cell activation, its role in some AML cases may be more complex, potentially driving tumour progression through intrinsic signaling pathways. This complexity underscores the need for further investigation into the context-dependent effects of B7H6 in AML.

To identify and confirm a major ligand, we primarily utilized two types of NK cells: differentiated primary NK (pNK) cells from umbilical CB and the NK-92 cell line. Despite differences in NK receptor subsets (Appendix 1 p 8, panels a–e), results from both NK cell types consistently indicated that B7H6 strongly induces NK cell-mediated killing. Notably, freshly isolated pNK cells from CB exhibited B7H6-dependent cytotoxicity even with low expression levels of activating receptors, such as NKp30. There was a strong correlation between B7H6 expression and the cytotoxicity levels mediated by fresh pNK cells (Appendix 1 p 14, panels a–b). In patient analyses, those with higher NK cell counts in their peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) demonstrated a lower hazard ratio when B7H6 expression was high (Fig. 3e–i). This suggests that B7H6 may similarly influence NK cell-mediated killing in PBMCs, regardless of differences in NK cell subsets. This suggests that B7H6 may influence NK cell-mediated killing across various NK cell subsets, but it remains possible that it preferentially binds different ligands in different subsets.

In contrast, the efficacy of NK cells for treating patients with low B7H6 levels may be limited (Fig. 3e). A potential direct therapy could involve increasing B7H6 expression in these patients. This could be achieved by utilizing an oncolytic virus to deliver B7H6 to tumour cells, thereby enabling NK cells to effectively target the tumours. However, since B7H6 signaling itself can enhance tumour cell proliferation,47,52 it would be necessary to use a mutant form of B7H6 that prevents this intrinsic signaling and avoids promoting tumour growth. Alternatively, to increase the therapeutic effect without modulating the expression of B7H6, it is recommended to use CAR-NK cells targeting B7H6. Our in vitro experiments (Fig. 5h) indicate that the killing activity of natural NK cells requires a certain threshold of B7H6 expression (MFI<3.5) to effectively target LT cell lines. CAR-NK cells that specifically target B7H6 will be able to sensitively detect even tumour cells that express less B7H6 than a certain threshold of B7H6 expression (MFI < 3.5). Another way of exploiting the potential of the B7H6-NKp30 pair for CAR-NK is to apply the structure of NKp30 to CAR. The robust NK cell response to B7H6-high LT cells suggests that the activation mechanism via its receptor, NKp30, is highly potent. Therefore, our findings suggest that alongside B7H6, NKp30 could serve as a valuable target for CAR design in NK cells. This approach may also address a current challenge in CAR-NK development by identifying the most potent target for CAR construction.

Unlike LT, no ligand could be identified that dominantly affected NK killing in ST because activating ligands showed a correlation with NK killing that varied depending on the conditions (Fig. 4c; Appendix 1 p 9, panel d). Even the simple summation of activating ligand expression failed to accurately predict pNK-mediated killing in ST (p > 0.2 for every E:T ratio, Pearson's correlation test). This indicates that a complex synergistic effect of ligands may regulate NK cell activity in ST. Indeed, a properly weighted sum of the expressions of MICA/B, B7H6, and ULBP1 showed a higher correlation than the individual values in ST (Appendix 1 p 9, panels i–j). Analysis of the clinical data from patients with ST also showed that both MICB and B7H6 affected survival, which was different in patients with LT (Appendix 1 p 5). Therefore, unlike LT, to understand the mechanism of NK killing in ST, it is essential to investigate both the expression of multiple activating ligands and their synergistic effects. Investigating synergistic effects of activating ligands in ST might be possible by applying scaled-up data with a sufficient number of tumours to our extendable framework in the in silico analysis.

The strength of our study is that we analyzed the effects of several activating ligands using a variety of methods, including clinical data analysis and in vitro/in vivo/in silico analysis. By using multiple methods, we were able to quantify and compare the relative strengths of the ligands and validate our results in several ways. Although our study focused on five major ligands out of the 18 known activating ligands, our mathematical model is scalable, allowing for the analysis of a larger number of ligands across a broader range of cell lines. Expanding the mathematical analysis to incorporate a more extensive set of ligands would be a valuable direction for future research.

Our study has certain limitations. We utilized bulk RNA sequencing data from multiple datasets which we analyzed with CIBERSORT software. However, CIBERSORT is unable to distinguish NKp30-expressing NK cells within cell types. This limitation prevents us from fully confirming the dominant effect of B7H6 in the clinical analysis of patients with LT. Additionally, due to the characteristics of RNA data, it is not possible to determine the absolute amount of B7H6 expression and the number of NK cells required for B7H6 to affect the survival of patients with LT. This is because activating ligands are likely regulated at various levels, from post-transcription to post-translation, including RNA splicing, transport to the cell surface, retention in the ER/Golgi, or secretion through shedding or exosomes. Therefore, it is necessary to utilize immunohistochemistry data to confirm the relationship between the protein amount of B7H6 and the killing of NK cells. In addition, it is necessary to investigate the surface expression of B7H6 in blasts from patients with LT to establish the expression level of B7H6 as a criterion for NK cell therapy in clinical practice. Furthermore, since we do not have a dataset of patients with hematologic malignancy who received NK cells and concurrently confirmed the expression of B7H6, we were unable to investigate the function of B7H6 under direct NK cell therapy conditions. Finally, the activation of NK cells is not determined by activating signals alone, but by the interaction of activating and inhibitory signals. In this paper, we only addressed the relative strength of activating signals, but addressing the interaction of activating and inhibitory signals would be a good future study.

In conclusion, our systematic approach showed that only B7H6 strongly triggers NK killing in patients with LT among nearly all activating ligands (n = 18). The significance of this study is that, unlike most other studies that analyze activating ligands separately, we took a systematic approach and analyzed most of the activating ligands together to confirm the predominance of B7H6. We propose B7H6 as the sole feasible therapeutic target and a predictive marker for response to NK cell-based therapy.
