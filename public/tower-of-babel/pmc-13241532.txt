Reliability of foundation models for image retrieval in histopathology
Authors: Abubakr Shafique, Xiaoli Qin, Amanda Dy, Najd Alshamlan, Dimitrios Androutsos, Susan J Done, April Khademi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring fairness and explainability is essential for the development of ethical, reliable, and effective AI systems in healthcare. Bias in AI models can contribute to disparities in clinical outcomes, challenging equity in medical decision-making. Content-Based Image Retrieval (CBIR) offers interpretable, visual tools to support diagnostic processes; however, these tools remain susceptible to biases inherent in the data. This study investigates covariate bias arising from differences in scanning devices within Foundation Models (FMs) used for CBIR in histopathology. We introduce a unique dataset comprising spatially co-registered images derived from the same histopathology slides scanned using two distinct scanners. This design enables a targeted analysis of scanner-induced variability in FM representations. Among the FMs assessed, Vision Transformer (ViT)-based architectures such as UNI, Virchow2, and GigaPath, demonstrated top performance and best generalization properties across scanners.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Digital Pathology (DP) is experiencing rapid growth, fueled by ongoing technological innovations in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Deep Learning (DL)1,2. Content-Based Image Retrieval (CBIR) is a well-established AI tool for pathology3, as it enables pathologists and researchers to retrieve visually or semantically similar images automatically4. The user inputs a query image, and the CBIR tool returns the correspondent images from the reference archive. There are many high-value clinical applications for CBIR, including virtual secondary review, decision-support, as well as for education purposes, exposing trainees to diverse datasets and images5–7. This could improve quality in pathology and education. CBIR can assist with data selection for clinical trials and cancer progression studies7; it also provides an efficient mechanism of curating specialized databases for AI applications8.

Histopathology AI models are usually developed using images from one scanner, and on deployment, the images are likely to be acquired by a different scanning platform9. Although DL has demonstrated remarkable performance in histopathology tasks, data variability across scanners reduces model robustness and is the leading barrier to wide-scale implementation of computational pathology systems10. As a result, varying scanning devices can lead to inequitable performance for patients and prevent wide-scale adoption of AI for pathology. Recent efforts within the digital pathology AI community have focused on developing robust models using self-supervised Foundation Models (FMs)11–15, which have shown considerable promise in histopathological image analysis. FMs alleviate the dependency on large-scale annotated datasets and can achieve robust representations across diverse tasks. FMs are attractive for a wide range of medical imaging tasks; however, emerging evidence suggests that performance may not be as invariant as expected10,16 as FMs are sensitive to inter-centre and inter-scanner variations10,17 and transformations18. This poses challenges to real-world implementations.

As AI-driven systems become increasingly integrated into clinical decision-making19,20, ensuring algorithm fairness is essential for ethical and reliable systems in healthcare10,21. Fairness, defined as the absence of systematic disparities in model performance across different patient subgroups or devices, helps prevent the amplification of healthcare inequities10,21,22. In this work, we examine fairness in terms of reliability and robustness of state-of-the-art FMs across scanning devices for CBIR applications to accelerate adoption. Using the same tissue slides, retrieval is examined across scanners, i.e., Scanner A as the archive and Scanner B images are the queries (and vice versa). This permits for isolated analysis of scanner-effects and is the first study of its kind. This study aims to uncover vulnerabilities in FMs for retrieval applications to scanner-bias, which would contribute to the development of more fair and equitable CBIR systems.

Results

In this study, fairness and reliability of histopathology CBIR applications is examined between two different scanning devices. Patch-level retrieval is performed using OICR and UHN co-registered patches, and TMA-level retrieval is performed using UHN co-registered TMAs. In this study, we primarily focused on a binary classification task distinguishing tumor from non-tumor (normal) tissue to specifically evaluate retrieval performance across scanners at both the patch- and TMA-level. To complement this, a breast cancer subtype retrieval experiment is also performed to demonstrate proof-of-concept.

Retrieval within the same scanner (i.e., Scanner A query and Scanner A archive or Scanner B query and Scanner B archive) is referred to as in-distribution (ID). Retrieval across scanners is regarded as out-of-distribution (OOD), i.e., Scanner A as the archive and Scanner B images are the queries (and vice versa). This enables an isolated analysis of scanner-induced bias in AI models. ID performance is reported as the mean retrieval performance when the query and archive originate from the same scanner (A → A and B → B). In contrast, OOD performance represents the mean performance when the query and archive come from different scanners (A → B and B → A). Representative examples of retrieval results are illustrated in Fig. 1. Additional retrieval examples for other models are shown in Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2.

Mean macro-averaged F1-scores for MV-5 retrieval using (a) the OICR dataset and (b) the UHN dataset

To evaluate the generalization capability of the models, TOST equivalence analysis was performed on the MV-5 macro-averaged F1-scores across the two scanners. The results are presented in Fig. 3 for (a) the OICR dataset and (b) the UHN dataset, using Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance metrics. Table S1 in the supplementary file shows the numerical representation of the TOST analysis. Because co-registered images with consistent underlying tissue morphology are evaluated across scanners in the CBIR experiments, models that generalize well are expected to exhibit minimal performance differences across scanners. Across both datasets, (a) OICR and (b) UHN, the largest performance differences between ID and OOD are observed for the KimiaNet, HistoLite, Virchow, and Hibou-B models, whereas UNI, Virchow2, and GigaPath exhibit the smallest performance drops across scanners.

TOST analysis.

Patch level TOST equivalence analysis of five-fold cross-validated, macro-averaged F1-scores for MV-5 retrieval using Euclidean, Cosine, and Hamming distances is shown for a the OICR dataset and b the UHN dataset. Highlighted models indicate those exhibiting generalization, whereas grayed-out models denote non-generalizing models.

The equivalence bounds were automatically determined for each experiment, with outlier FMs excluded from the bound computation (differences between ID and OOD exceeding 10%). This criterion was selected to avoid biasing the equivalence bounds and producing excessively wide intervals that would trivially encompass all models. For the OICR dataset, the equivalence bounds (±ϵ) were found to be 0.0384 for Euclidean distance, 0.0322 for cosine distance, and 0.0457 for Hamming distance. For the UHN dataset, the equivalence bounds (±ϵ) were determined as 0.0189 for Euclidean distance, 0.0238 for cosine distance, and 0.0388 for Hamming distance. Figure 3 highlights the models that demonstrate generalization across scanners with grayed-out points indicating models that are not statistically equivalent across scanners. Notably, GigaPath consistently demonstrates generalization across both datasets and all three distance metrics.

The number of patch level Common Retrieved Instances (CRIs) between scanners for top-5 retrievals (combined for the OICR and UHN datasets) using Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance metrics is shown in Fig. 4a. This metric reflects the consistency and stability of retrieval behavior across scanners. Unlike conventional performance metrics, which primarily assess retrieval correctness, CRI specifically quantifies the stability of model outputs under domain shift. While several models demonstrate reasonable consistency between ID and OOD retrievals across different similarity metrics, patch level retrievals based on Hamming distance exhibit the fewest common retrieved instances. Euclidean and cosine distances show similar trends, with models such as UNI and HIPT achieving a median of two CRIs within the top-5 retrieved patches. In contrast, HistoLite, KimiaNet, and Virchow display the lowest consistency across ID and OOD retrievals.

TMA-level evaluation

TMA-level retrieval was evaluated using a leave-one-patient-out sampling strategy within the Yottixel framework, employing a pairwise median-of-minimums matching approach for TMA-to-TMA comparisons. The leave-one-patient-out design mitigates the risk of data leakage between query and archive samples from the same patient. While the Yottixel5 framework by default computes the median-of-minimum Hamming distance using barcoded embeddings, we additionally evaluated retrieval performance using median-of-minimum Euclidean and cosine distances computed on the original, non-binarized feature embeddings to assess consistency across different similarity measures. Figure 5 and Table 2 presents the macro-averaged F1 scores for MV-5 retrievals across both intra- and inter-scanner archives for the UHN dataset, along with performance differences. Highlighted models indicate those achieving the best performance under ID or OOD retrieval for the respective similarity metrics, as well as those exhibiting the smallest performance degradation between ID and OOD conditions. Moreover, TMA level MV-5 retrieval classification accuracy, and macro averaged recall are provided in the supplementary material in Fig. S7. TMA level mAP@5, and MV-5 per-class precision is also provided in the supplementary material in Figs. S8a and S8b, respectively. Figure 5 illustrates that several FMs, including GigaPath, Virchow2, Virchow, and UNI, achieve high F1-scores while exhibiting minimal performance drops across scanners. Models such as ResNet50-MoCoV2 and PathDino show slightly greater performance differences across scanners. In contrast, HistoLite, KimiaNet, and Hibou-B experience the largest performance drops between scanners. Notably, while the macro-averaged F1-score differences between scanners may vary slightly across Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance metrics, the overall trend in performance is consistent across all models.

Discussion

This work evaluates the effects of different scanners on histopathology FMs for CBIR applications, a leading barrier to widescale implementation. Patch-level and TMA-level results demonstrate similar trends, with some FMs generalizing across scanners, datasets and similarity metrics. TOST analysis was employed to assess statistical equivalence between the patch-level F1-scores obtained from five-fold cross-validation across scanners. TMA-level retrieval performance was analyzed by comparing the differences between retrievals across scanners. Furthermore, MV-5 classification metrics, including accuracy and macro-averaged recall, were evaluated to provide additional insights into retrieval-based classification performance. To assess the stability and consistency of the models under domain shift, the number of CRIs across scanners was also computed. Finally, mAP@5 was analyzed for both patch-level and TMA-level retrieval tasks across scanners.

When examining the patch-level evaluation across commonly used similarity metrics for retrieval tasks, such as Euclidean and cosine distances, we observe a consistent performance trend across the evaluated models, although the magnitude of the performance differences varies. Euclidean distance measures the absolute geometric distance between feature vectors in the embedding space, whereas cosine distance measures the angular dissimilarity between vectors based on their normalized orientation. Consequently, Euclidean distance often produces larger absolute distance values compared to cosine distance, while preserving the same relative ordering of similarity among samples. In contrast, patch-level retrieval results based on Hamming distance exhibit a slightly different trend compared to those obtained using Euclidean and cosine distances. This difference is due to the additional post-processing required for Hamming distance similarity computation. Specifically, the continuous feature embeddings are first transformed into binary barcodes using the MinMax binarization algorithm prior to calculating the Hamming distance. This binarization step nonlinearly modifies the representation of the original embedding space by reducing continuous feature information into binary patterns. As a result, the similarity relationships among samples differ from those computed directly on the raw embeddings, leading to observable variations in retrieval behavior relative to Euclidean and cosine distance-based evaluations. Transforming high-dimensional deep feature embeddings into binary barcodes provides an efficient approach for reducing computational and memory requirements in large-scale applications. In image retrieval scenarios, where a query must be compared against millions of images, representing images as compact binary codes can substantially improve storage efficiency and accelerate similarity computations.

When evaluating TMA-level retrieval performance, a similar performance trend is observed across all three distance metrics. This consistency can largely be attributed to the median-of-minimum distance aggregation used in the retrieval framework. In this approach, pairwise similarities are first computed between patches from the query TMA and those from candidate TMAs, after which the minimum distances are identified and aggregated using the median operator. This pairwise aggregation strategy reduces the influence of individual patch-level variations and stabilizes the overall similarity estimation between TMAs. As a result, the retrieval behavior becomes less sensitive to the specific choice of similarity metric, leading to more consistent trends across Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance-based evaluations.

For the breast cancer subtype retrieval analysis, when evaluating retrieval based on raw feature embeddings using Euclidean and cosine distances, GigaPath and UNI demonstrate clear generalization with minimal performance degradation between ID and OOD settings. However, when barcode-based post-processing is applied and Hamming distance is used for retrieval, these models no longer appear to satisfy the generalization criteria. In contrast, models such as Virchow and HIPT exhibit evidence of generalization under the Hamming distance-based retrieval framework. Additionally, we observe wider confidence intervals in the subgroup patch-level analysis compared to the binary tumor versus non-tumor evaluation. This is primarily attributable to the increased task complexity and the comparatively smaller and more imbalanced sample sizes within ILC subtype, which introduce greater variability in the estimated performance metrics. Despite these limitations, the results provide useful insights into how different feature representations and similarity metrics influence model robustness when evaluating more fine-grained histopathological distinctions.

GigaPath emerges as one of the most generalizable models, exhibiting the smallest performance drops across scanners and datasets, at both patch-level and TMA-level retrieval tasks, with the exception of subgroup retrieval using Hamming distance. This robustness is consistently observed across all evaluated metrics, including Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distances, as demonstrated by the TOST equivalence analysis in Fig. 3. In addition, GigaPath demonstrates strong retrieval stability, maintaining a high number of CRIs across scanners, as illustrated in Fig. 4, which indicates the model is selecting the same images from both scanners most often. This observation is further supported by additional evaluation metrics (including accuracy, recall, and mAP@5) computed across different similarity metrics and datasets.

In addition to GigaPath, several other FMs (including Virchow2, UNI, IBOT-ViT, and PathDino) were found to be generalizing across multiple metrics and datasets, spanning both patch-level and TMA-level retrieval tasks, as indicated by the TOST analysis (Fig. 3). Although HIPT does not rank among the top-performing models in terms of retrieval accuracy, it nonetheless exhibits stable and consistent behavior across domains. Both the CRI and TOST analysis indicate that HIPT is generalizing across scanners at both patch and TMA levels. This observation highlights that model stability and robustness under domain shift do not necessarily imply superior retrieval accuracy. Similar results were found in another study9, where HIPT was consistent across scanners, but had lower performance for inter- and intra-scanner classification tasks.

Among the CNN-based models evaluated in this study, ResNet50-MoCoV2 shows the smallest performance difference across scanners, particularly for TMA-level retrieval. At the patch level, ResNet50-MoCoV2 also generalizes when using the Hamming distance across both datasets. This is contrast to the other two smaller CNN models, HistoLite and KimiaNet. Most FMs available in the literature are based on ViT architectures, although several histopathology-specific models built on CNNs also exist. In our experiments, ViT-based FMs generally demonstrate stronger generalization compared to CNN-based models. This difference may be attributed to the underlying architectural characteristics of the two approaches. CNNs rely on convolutional filters with local receptive fields to learn hierarchical representations, which introduces strong inductive biases such as spatial locality and translation invariance. In contrast, ViT-based models divide images into patches and employ self-attention mechanisms to model long-range dependencies and global contextual relationships across the image. This capability often enables more expressive representation learning and improved robustness when trained on large-scale datasets. Notably, several of the top-performing models in our study (such as GigaPath, Virchow2, and UNI) are ViT-based models that have been pretrained on large and diverse histopathology datasets, which likely contributes to their stronger cross-scanner generalization performance.

At the patch-level, models such as HistoLite, KimiaNet, Hibou-B, and Virchow are substantially affected by domain shift as shown by large performance changes across scanners in both the CRI and TOST analyses. In contrast, the behavior at the TMA-level differs for some models; for instance, IBOT-ViT, which performs well at the patch-level demonstrates a pronounced performance drop across scanners at the TMA-level; similarly, Virchow performs poorly at the patch-level and shows comparatively greater robustness for TMA-level retrieval. To qualitatively explore these trends, Fig. 7 presents 2D t-SNE projections of the feature embeddings obtained from co-registered patches scanned by two different scanners. These visualizations illustrate the distribution of learned feature representations across models and scanning devices. For models such as HIPT, GigaPath, UNI, and Virchow2, the feature embeddings from both scanners appear closely clustered, suggesting the models learn similar representations across scanning platforms. This observation is consistent with the improved generalization performance observed for these models in the quantitative analyses. Interestingly, HIPT demonstrates overlapping feature distributions across scanners even when considering both datasets jointly, whereas other strong-performing models such as UNI and GigaPath exhibit scanner-wise feature overlap within each dataset (OICR and UHN), but show some separation when comparing across datasets. The lower-performing models display clearer separation between features derived from different scanners, indicating greater sensitivity to scanner-specific variations. These qualitative observations align with the quantitative findings reported in this study. PathDino, on the other hand, exhibits relatively sparse embedding distributions in the t-SNE projection, suggesting a substantial shift in feature representations between retrievals from each scanner. It is important to note that 2D t-SNE visualizations are intended primarily for qualitative interpretation and do not provide a precise quantitative measure of embedding compactness or similarity. In a separate study10, we defined a robustness metric that measures the domain shift, and dispersion of the features in the feature space using mean squared error, Kullback-Leibler divergence, and Calinski-Harabasz index. It was found that models such as HIPT, GigaPath, Virchow2, and UNI are less affected by scanner-induced domain variation. These findings are consistent with the observations presented here.

The generalization performance of a FM is not solely determined by its parameter size or the scale of its training data. For example, UNI, which is smaller than the models like Virchow, Virchow2, and GigaPath, and trained on a comparatively smaller WSI dataset, also exhibits robust generalization across scanners. PathDino, despite being compact, consistently demonstrates strong retrieval performance across different scanners and datasets, outperforming larger models such as HIPT, Hibou-B, and Virchow. These results suggest that architectural design, training objectives, and data diversity play a more critical role in model generalization than size alone. Models such as Virchow and Virchow2, which share identical architectures and parameter counts, show different generalization behavior. Although the number of training images was increased in Virchow2, another key difference is the Virchow2 model is trained with multi-resolution data which lead to better and broader representations. Other top generalizing models, such as UNI and GigaPath, also incorporate multi-resolution images during training, suggesting that perhaps the different resolution data is aiding generalization by allowing the model to learn image features at different scales.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations should be considered. First, the analysis was limited to images of breast tissue acquired using only two whole-slide scanners (Aperio and VisionTek), which may limit the applicability of the findings to other scanner types and tissue specimens commonly encountered in clinical practice. Future investigations should therefore include a broader range of scanning platforms, tissue types, and acquisition conditions (such as variations in staining protocols, inter-laboratory differences, and diverse patient cohorts) to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of FMs across heterogeneous imaging environments.

Second, the evaluation focused on patch-level and TMA-level retrieval reliability, while WSI-level retrieval experiments were not conducted due to the highly imbalanced nature of the available dataset. In the future, larger WSI datasets, with more balanced labels and classes will be considered.

Third, the VisionTek slides were originally digitized at 20× magnification and subsequently upsampled to match the spatial resolution of the Aperio scans. Although this harmonization step ensured consistent input magnification and spatial dimensions for all evaluated FMs, upsampling cannot recover genuine high-frequency information and may alter fine-grained texture statistics. In this work, we consider the upsampling factor to be part of the scanner shift. Consequently, the observed scanner-related effects may be partially confounded by interpolation artifacts introduced during rescaling. Nevertheless, all FMs were evaluated under an identical pre-processing pipeline, and several models were found to produce similar outputs across scanners suggesting robustness to the scanner-related domain shifts. Therefore, while absolute performance values may be affected by these factors, the relative comparison of model robustness remains internally consistent. Further validation across additional scanning platforms and acquisition settings will be necessary to establish broader generalizability.

Further research is required to better understand the architectural and training factors that contribute to the improved generalization capabilities of certain FMs. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the potential of FMs as robust parameter initializations for downstream histopathology tasks. Overall, the results support ongoing efforts toward the development of clinically deployable AI systems and contribute to advancing the translational potential of AI in digital pathology.

Methods

Tissue specimens

Two clinical datasets are used to study scanner-induced domain shift in CBIR tasks. The first dataset was collected from the Ontario Institute for Cancer Research (OICR) in Toronto, ON, Canada, and consists of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) slides from 111 breast cancer patients, with one slide per patient. The cohort is predominantly female (97%), with patient ages ranging from 30 to 94 years. Several breast cancer subtypes are represented, including invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), encapsulated papillary carcinoma, and invasive lobular carcinoma. A detailed breakdown of the breast cancer subtypes in the OICR dataset is provided in the supplementary material (Table S2). Patients exhibit diverse hormone receptor (HR) and HER2 expression profiles, as well as a range of Nottingham grades and clinical stages. Of 111 slides, 105 slides are tumorous, and six slides are normal. The WSIs are scanned with an Aperio AT2 scanner at 40× magnification and a resolution of 0.25 Microns Per Pixel (mpp).

The second dataset was collected from the University Health Network (UHN) in Toronto, ON, Canada, and comprises 529 breast cancer tissue microarrays (TMAs) from 189 patients. Some subjects had normal-appearing adjacent tissue slides, resulting in 371 Tumour TMAs and 158 Non-Tumour TMAs. The cohort consists of female patients, all diagnosed with HR+ and HER2-, encompassing a range of clinical stages. WSIs were acquired using an Aperio ScanScope scanner at 40× magnification, corresponding to a spatial resolution of 0.25 μm per pixel (mpp). Clinical information was used to assign TMA-level labels.

This study was conducted in accordance with all applicable ethical guidelines and regulations, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the research ethics boards of OICR (Reference No. 2018-278-4) and UHN (Reference No. 2023-424-2). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the respective ethics boards, as the study utilized archival samples only. No clinical trial procedures were involved, and the study was conducted as a retrospective analysis of archival data.

Digital database

OICR and UHN slides were rescanned on a Sakura VisionTek digital scanner at 20× magnification with a resolution of 0.27 mpp and upsampled to match the resolution, 0.25 mpp, of Aperio images. Affine registration was performed using the Fast Sample Consensus (FSC) feature matching algorithm23, with the Aperio images as the reference. Registration quality was assessed using the Jaccard Index23, where binary tissue masks were generated from H&E-stained images acquired from both scanners using a pre-trained U-Net model24. The resulting masks were compared across scanners to evaluate the accuracy of alignment. Both datasets demonstrated high-quality registration, with average Jaccard indices exceeding 94%.

Figure 8 shows images across scanners, with patches extracted from identical coordinate locations. For patch-level analysis, an expert pathologist selected 10 high-power fields (HPFs) of tumour and 10 of normal (non-tumour) tissue, each measuring 21,2000 μm2, from the Aperio OICR dataset. Spatial registration enabled transfer of the HPF coordinates to the VisionTek OICR dataset, from which patches of the same size (512 × 512), resolution (0.5 mpp at 20x), and spatial correspondence were extracted. In total, 4952 patches (2959 tumour and 1993 normal) were extracted from the HPFs for each scanner, yielding 9904 patches for evaluation. Similarly, from the UHN dual-scanner dataset, 1521 tumour and 520 normal patches of size 512 × 512 at 20× (0.5 mpp) were obtained from each scanner, resulting in a total of 4082 patches for cross-scanner analysis. Given the pathologist-provided annotations for OICR WSIs and tissue segmentation mask for UHN TMAs, patches were extracted from the annotated/masked regions using a sliding-window approach with a 5% patch overlap. Additionally, each extracted patch was required to contain at least 70% tissue content.

Histopathology foundation models

This work investigates the use of FMs as a feature extractor for CBIR in histopathology. The term “Foundation Model” refers to large-scale, deep neural networks that are pre-trained on extensive and diverse datasets serving as a general-purpose backbone to produce robust and transferable features for a wide range of downstream applications14,15,25,26. Many histopathology FMs have been introduced, including UNI27, Hibou-B28, Virchow29, Virchow2/2G30, GigaPath31, IBOT-ViT32, Hierarchical Image Pyramid Transformer (HIPT)33, PathDino34, KimiaNet35, and ResNet50-MoCoV236. In addition to the FMs, a light-weight model, HistoLite9, is also employed. The majority of the evaluated FMs are built upon the Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture, which captures long-range spatial dependencies by modeling relationships between image tokens (sub-patches) through self-attention mechanisms. In contrast, three of the assessed models (HistoLite, KimiaNet, and ResNet50-MoCoV2) are based on convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures. The models are trained on extensive datasets that are both private and public. Table 4 summarizes the FMs considered in this study. All models evaluated in this study (publicly accessible versions) are available through their respective repositories on GitHub or Hugging Face.

Content-Based-Image Retrieval (CBIR)

CBIR systems extract features from query images and compare them to images from an archival database using a distance metric. The images from the archive that are most similar to the query (smallest distance) are retrieved and returned to the user. To extract features, the FMs listed in Table 4 were utilized without any additional fine-tuning. As each FM was developed using different pre-processing schemes, the images were used directly for inference without applying any pre-processing steps such as stain normalization, color normalization, or intensity normalization.

Based on prior literature, the most commonly used retrieval distance measures were adopted, including Euclidean distance, cosine distance, and Hamming distance2,5,37,38. Consistent with related work, the feature embeddings were used directly as generated by the models, without applying additional post-processing steps. The only exception was for computing the Hamming distance where the continuous feature embeddings were converted into binary barcode representations using the MinMax algorithm5,39. The Euclidean and cosine distances were computed directly from the raw (non-binarized) embedding vectors.

In this work, we conduct two main analyses to examine the generalization properties of FMs for CBIR. First, we investigate patch-level CBIR using identical tissue patches from two scanners. Next, we extend the CBIR application to retrieval in TMAs using image-level labels. WSI-level retrieval was not conducted due to the highly imbalanced cohort of the OICR dataset.

Patch-Level Retrieval – To examine patch-level CBIR fairness across scanners, we constructed two query sets and two reference archives comprising spatially co-registered patches with similar tissue morphology. These datasets correspond to images digitized using Scanner A (Aperio; AP) and Scanner B (VisionTek; VT). To provide a more realistic assessment of retrieval performance, we adopted a two-stage evaluation protocol. In the first stage, Scanner A was used as the archive, and the query images were from the held-out Scanner A data and the corresponding patches from Scanner B for the cross-scanner retrieval. In the second stage, this was repeated but with Scanner B as the archive. Five-fold cross-validation was used, in which for each fold, 80% of the patients were assigned to the set of data (reference) in the archive, and the remaining 20% were used as the query set. Using patient-level stratification, we ensured that no data leakage occurred between the query set and the archive set. Experiments examine patch-level CBIR performance using patches from the same tissue slides across scanners. These experiments leveraged co-registered images, ensuring consistent tissue morphology across scanners.

Features from the query patches were compared to the features from the archive patches using the k-nearest neighbour (k-NN) with Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance2,5,37. This retrieves the most histologically similar embeddings from the archive for each query sample. Euclidean, cosine, and Hamming distance are widely adopted in retrieval tasks, and provides an effective measure of geometric proximity between representations2,5,37. Figure 9a shows the CBIR setup for patch-level retrieval. Evaluations were conducted using patches extracted from corresponding spatial locations across WSIs acquired with Scanner A and Scanner B.

Illustration of a patch-level and b TMA-level retrieval framework for intra- and inter-scanner retrievals using spatially co-registered images.

TMA-Level Retrieval – For TMA retrieval, the Yottixel search engine with the leave-one-patient-out setup was used5,40,41. All patches from each TMA are directly utilized for TMA-to-TMA retrieval, which are transformed into embeddings, and subsequently converted into barcodes using a MinMax algorithm5,38,39. Similarity is estimated using the median-of-minimums strategy, which computes pairwise Hamming distances between two sets of patches and assigns the median of the minimum distances as the final similarity score5. Beyond the Hamming distance applied to barcode representations within the Yottixel pipeline, we additionally computed pairwise Euclidean and cosine distances using the original (non-binarized) embedding vectors. For each of the three distance metrics, we then derived the median-of-minimum distances to obtain a robust summary measure for retrieval evaluation.

In the case of TMAs, their relatively smaller size makes the mosaic step unnecessary; instead, all patches from each TMA are directly utilized for TMA-to-TMA retrieval. The TMA CBIR setup is shown in Fig. 9b. Yottixel’s flexible architecture supports the evaluation of diverse FMs with only minimal modifications to the framework41. Evaluations were performed using the same TMAs across Scanner A and Scanner B (with patient level stratification).

Breast Cancer Subgroup Retrieval – An additional evaluation on breast cancer subtypes was completed using the OICR dataset. There are breast cancer subtype labels for 81 IDC and and 16 ILC slides. Within these 97 slides, the normal and tumour patches are extracted from the annotated region for a total of 1933 normal patches, 411 ILC patches, and 1944 IDC patches. The same patch level CBIR experimental setup is used, with one scanner as the archive, and the other scanner as the query, with three possible output labels (normal, IDC or ILC). The dataset was partitioned using patient-level stratification to prevent any potential data leakage between training and evaluation samples. Specifically, the data were divided into archive (80%) and query (20%) sets, and a five-fold cross-validation protocol was employed to ensure robust performance estimation.

Evaluation metrics

For each query (patch or TMA), the top-k most similar patches/TMAs were retrieved from the archive. The “majority-k accuracy” was utilized, wherein a retrieval is deemed correct only if the majority of the retrieved samples are accurate (i.e., of the same class)2,41. In this study, we report results for k = 5. A majority voting strategy was applied across the k = 5 retrieved labels to obtain a single consensus label per query. This consensus label was then compared with the corresponding query label to measure classification performance. Accuracy, recall, per-class precision, macro-averaged F1-score at majority vote-5 (MV-5) are measured from the majority vote retrieval. The macro-averaged F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and assigns equal weight to all classes regardless of their frequency, making it well-suited for imbalanced datasets40–42. Additionally, the mean average precision (mAP@5) is also quantified which considers both classification accuracy, and the position of the ranking within the top-5 retrievals37,38.

To examine generalization performance of the models, the method proposed by ref. 43 is adopted to examine whether the retrieval performance is statistically equivalent across scanners. The unique dataset leveraged in this work is the same tissue regions acquired by two different scanners, and the change in performance across scanners will be a strong indication of generalization capabilities of the model. The method uses the Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) for testing of performance equivalence, where models that have a performance drop between scanners that are within an equivalence bound are significant, indicating similar performance in both scanners (generalizing).

The method measures drops in performance metrics between same scanner (ID) and cross scanner (OOD) data, which corresponds to two different scanners in this work. If there is equal performance across the two scanners, the mean difference would be zero. To determine whether the change in performance of a model is statistically significant, the equivalence bounds are estimated using the data-driven method from ref. 43 based on the 95% Confidence Interval (CI) from the performance differences in all models. Lastly, we introduce a complementary metric termed common retrieved instances (CRI) to assess the stability and consistency of retrievals between scanners. This metric quantifies the overlap between the sets of retrieved patches using each scanner as the training set (ID), and testing on held-out within scanner data (ID) and the cross-scanner data (OOD). Specifically, the number of common retrievals is measured per query, independent of their relative ranking within the top-k results (with k = 5 in our experiments). This metric captures the model’s consistency under domain shift and is distinct from retrieval accuracy; that is, a model may be stable and consistent even if its retrieved patches are not strictly correct.
