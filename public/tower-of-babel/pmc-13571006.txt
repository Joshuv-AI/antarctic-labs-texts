A compound heterozygous combination of SLC34A2 variants in pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Zhizhen Zou, Ping Mei, Lixu Yan, Hongmei Wu, Yue Xing, Xiaosong Ben, Weifeng Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary Alveolar Microlithiasis (PAM) is a rare hereditary lung disorder characterized by the intra-alveolar deposition of calcium phosphate microliths. It is primarily familial and follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, with no significant gender disparity in incidence. In its early stages, PAM is often asymptomatic, and most cases are detected incidentally through abnormal imaging findings during routine health examinations. We report a case of a male patient in his mid-50 s with a 10-year history of exertional dyspnea and cough unresponsive to conventional therapy. Initially diagnosed and treated for emphysema in early 2024, the patient was readmitted two months later with progressive dyspnea and cyanosis. The diagnosis of PAM was confirmed by typical medical imaging and pathological examination. Genetic analysis identified a previously unreported compound heterozygous combination of SLC34A2 variants: a c.910A > T (p.Lys304*) nonsense variant in exon 8 and a heterozygous ∼5.5 kb copy-number deletion at 4p15.2 (encompassing exons 2–6), thereby expanding the catalogue of reported PAM-associated genetic combinations. No recurrence was observed during one-year follow-up after bilateral lung transplantation.

Introduction

Pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis (PAM) is a rare genetic lung disease characterized by the deposition of calcium phosphate microliths within the alveolar spaces.1,2 It is primarily familial and follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, with no significant difference in incidence between male and female patients.3,4 Although environmental factors may influence disease development, variants in the SLC34A2 gene are considered the main cause. 5

The SLC34A2 gene is located on chromosome 4p15 and consists of 13 exons. It encodes the sodium-phosphate cotransporter NPT2b.6–8 This transporter is highly expressed in the lung, especially on the surface of type II alveolar cells. Pathogenic variants in SLC34A2 lead to abnormal structure and function of NPT2b, impairing phosphate transport. As a result, phosphate levels rise in the alveoli. The phosphate then combines with calcium to form flake-like crystals, which deposit in the alveolar walls.9,10

Patients with early-stage PAM often show no obvious clinical symptoms. Most cases are diagnosed after abnormal findings are detected during physical examinations or imaging studies. Imaging findings often reflect disease severity more accurately than subjective symptoms. This lack of correlation is known as the clinical-imaging dissociation.2,11 We report a case of PAM in a middle-aged man with chronic hypoxic respiratory failure who successfully underwent lung transplantation.

Case presentation

The reporting of this study conforms to CARE guidelines. 12 A male patient in his mid-50 s presented with a 9-year history of recurrent chest tightness and shortness of breath. His symptoms worsened over the six months prior to admission, accompanied by cough and sputum production. He was hospitalized din mid-2024 due to exacerbated dyspnea, headache, and cyanosis. The patient had a history of pulmonary heart disease with pulmonary arterial hypertension for more than 10 years and had not received any prior pharmacological treatment. His younger brother, a first-degree relative, was previously diagnosed with pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis. Signed informed consent for treatment and for publication of this case and accompanying images was obtained from the patient. All patient details have been de-identified. Institutional review board approval was not required for this single case report.

At admission, the patient presented with finger cyanosis and resting hypoxia. Arterial blood gas analysis on room air showed pH 7.38, PaCO2 48 mmHg, PaO2 52 mmHg, and HCO3− 28 mmol/L, indicating chronic respiratory acidosis with moderate hypoxemia. Pulmonary function tests revealed a severe restrictive ventilatory defect: forced vital capacity (FVC) 45% predicted, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) 52% predicted, and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) 32% predicted. Echocardiography estimated a systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 72 mmHg. The patient required continuous low-flow oxygen (2 L/min) at rest. Indications for lung transplantation included progressive respiratory failure despite optimal medical management, World Health Organization functional class IV, and an anticipated 2-year survival of less than 50% based on the composite prognosis score.

Chest radiography revealed diffuse increased density in both lung fields, with widespread patchy high-density shadows. The pulmonary hilum, cardiac silhouette, bilateral diaphragm, and costophrenic angles were obscured (Figure 1). Chest high-resolution CT (HRCT) showed bilateral diffuse, fine, sand-like (“sandstorm”) calcified micronodules (<3 mm in diameter), predominantly in the subpleural and perifissural regions. Interlobular septal thickening and ground-glass opacities (GGO) were present, forming a characteristic “crazy-paving” pattern (Figure 2). Subpleural small cysts and a “black pleural line” (a thin radiolucent line between the calcified nodules and the inner chest wall) were also observed. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were visible in the mediastinum and bilateral hilar regions, but these were incidental and not the cause of the crazy-paving pattern. 6

In early 2024, the patient received a diagnosis of emphysema with pulmonary interstitial changes, pericardial effusion, and aortic atherosclerosis at another institution. After anti-infective and symptomatic therapy, his condition improved, and he was discharged. He was readmitted two months later due to recurrent dyspnea, headache, and cyanosis.

After multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient was considered a candidate for lung transplantation. He subsequently underwent bilateral lung transplantation. The explanted lungs showed a gritty, sand-like cut surface and a granular pleural surface due to protruding calcified nodules. The parenchyma had markedly increased resistance and decreased elasticity (Figure 3 and 4). Histological examination revealed diffuse calcifications (50–5000 μm) and numerous calcified lamellar bodies within alveolar spaces (Figure 5). Architectural distortion and interstitial fibrosis were also observed.

Post-transplant follow-up at 6 and 12 months: Pulmonary function tests showed marked improvement: FVC increased from 45% predicted to 78% predicted, and DLCO from 32% to 67% predicted. HRCT at 12 months revealed clear lung fields without evidence of recurrent microlithiasis. Transbronchial biopsies at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no acute or chronic rejection. The patient experienced mild cytomegalovirus reactivation at month 4, which was successfully treated with valganciclovir. No other significant complications occurred.

Genetic analysis

Genetic analysis was performed on peripheral blood samples using whole-exome sequencing (WES) on the Illumina platform. Secondary analysis was conducted using Sentieon software aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions were annotated using VEP and classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using in-house bioinformatics software based on NGS read-depth data.

WES identified a heterozygous SLC34A2 variant, c.910A > T (p.Lys304*), in exon 8, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 6). Additionally, a heterozygous ∼5.5 kb copy-number deletion at chromosome 4p15.2, encompassing exons 2–6 and adjacent introns of SLC34A2 (reference transcript: NM_006424.3), was detected via NGS-based CNV analysis. This deletion was not detected in the patient's daughter. Family history was limited to the patient's younger brother, who had been previously diagnosed with PAM at another institution. Family segregation analysis revealed that the proband's daughter carries only the heterozygous c.910A > T variant but does not carry the 4p15.2 deletion. This segregation pattern definitively confirms that the two pathogenic variants are in trans (compound heterozygous configuration). The proband's parents were deceased, and his younger brother, previously diagnosed with PAM at another institution, was unavailable for testing. No radiological screening was performed on asymptomatic relatives due to limited access and patient preference. It should be noted that the CNV result is based on NGS data and has not been independently validated by orthogonal methods (e.g., MLPA), as acknowledged in the limitations of the clinical laboratory report.

Discussion

Pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis (PAM) is a rare diffuse lung disease characterized by extensive deposition of calcium phosphate microcrystals in the alveoli. It is an autosomal recessive disorder associated with variants in the SLC34A2 gene. This gene encodes the type IIb sodium-phosphate cotransporter (NPT2b) in the alveolar epithelium. Pathogenic variants lead to the formation of calcium phosphate microcrystals in the alveoli, which is the primary disease mechanism.7,10,13

Diagnostic criteria for PAM include: (1) characteristic radiological findings (bilateral diffuse sand-like calcifications, crazy-paving, subpleural cysts, black pleural line); (2) histopathological confirmation (lamellated calcific microliths within alveoli); and (3) genetic confirmation (biallelic pathogenic SLC34A2 variants). A robust differential diagnosis is essential. No effective medical therapy exists for PAM. Corticosteroid treatments are largely ineffective. Bronchopulmonary lavage is used for symptomatic management. For end-stage disease, bilateral lung transplantation is the only intervention that can change the clinical outcome. 14 Transplant recipients typically show better quality of life and respiratory function. However, the underlying pathogenesis of PAM is not well understood. The long-term outcomes and recurrence risk after transplantation require further investigation.15–17

The favorable post-transplant outcome in our case is consistent with a recent report by Vallejo et al., 18 who described a 36-year-old male PAM patient with similar pre-transplant hemodynamic parameters - including a PaO2 of 57 mmHg, DLCO of 33% predicted, and pulmonary artery pressure of 71 mmHg - who underwent successful bilateral lung transplantation with excellent graft function at 9 months of follow-up. Both cases reinforce that lung transplantation offers a favorable outcome for end-stage PAM patients with severe hypoxemia and pulmonary hypertension, and support the observation that disease recurrence has not been reported post-transplantation. These findings suggest that transplantation should be considered early in PAM patients with progressive respiratory failure and overt pulmonary hypertension, as delayed referral may compromise transplant candidacy.

PAM shows a clear familial predisposition. Published data indicate that about one-third of cases are familial. These often involve consanguineous marriages, and disease incidence is higher among siblings.19,20 In 2006, Corut et al. located the causative gene to a 4.2-Mb region on chromosome 4p15 by studying a large consanguineous Turkish family. They confirmed PAM is linked to SLC34A2 gene variants. 6

The SLC34A2 gene is located on chromosome 4p15.2 and has 13 exons. It encodes the NPT2b cotransporter. Gene variants cause abnormal NPT2b structure and function, impairing phosphate transport.21,22 This raises alveolar phosphate levels. Phosphate then binds calcium to form crystals that deposit in the alveoli. According to the most recent systematic review by Jönsson et al., 21 34 allelic variants have been documented in at least 68 patients worldwide, with the majority present in the homozygous state and only four cases described as compound heterozygous.

In the present case, we identified a previously unreported compound heterozygous combination of SLC34A2 variants: a c.910A > T (p.Lys304*) nonsense variant in exon 8 and a heterozygous ∼5.5 kb deletion encompassing exons 2–6 (NM_006424.3). To our knowledge, this specific allelic combination has not been documented in any public variant database (ClinVar, gnomAD, HGMD) or in the literature. The c.910A > T variant has been recurrently reported exclusively in Chinese patients,22–24 establishing it as a population-specific mutational hotspot in the Chinese PAM population. The ∼5.5 kb deletion involving exons 2–6 was previously described only in the homozygous state in a Japanese patient. 25 Notably, among the four previously reported compound heterozygous cases documented in the systematic review, only one involved c.910A > T, which was combined with a missense variant (c.1363T > C, p.Tyr455His) rather than a large deletion. 26 A more recent report by Liu et al. 27 described another Chinese PAM patient with a compound heterozygous combination of c.910A > T and a novel missense variant c.1267G > A (p.Gly423Arg) in exon 11. In that case, the patient presented with a milder phenotype at age 36 with preserved pulmonary function and did not require lung transplantation, whereas our patient exhibited a more severe phenotype with progressive respiratory failure and underwent bilateral lung transplantation. This comparison raises the possibility that the co-occurrence of c.910A > T with a large deletion resulting in complete loss of function of the affected allele may confer a more severe disease course than when c.910A > T is combined with a missense variant. Our case thus represents the first description of a compound heterozygous combination consisting of a nonsense variant and a large deletion involving exons 2–6, expanding the mutational spectrum of PAM and highlighting the importance of comprehensive genetic testing that includes both sequencing and copy number variation analysis.

The c.910A > T variant introduces a premature termination codon, truncating the protein and ablating its function.22,23 Functional studies by Jönsson et al. 24 demonstrated that NaPi-2b mutant constructs carrying this variant were non-functional when expressed in Xenopus laevis oocytes, confirming its pathogenic effect. The 5.5 kb deletion removes critical upstream exons including the promoter region and N-terminal domain, likely resulting in complete allele inactivation. 25 Previous studies have established that homozygous deletions of this region are pathogenic; our case confirms that heterozygous deletions can cause disease when combined with another pathogenic variant on the alternate allele, consistent with recessive inheritance.

The compound heterozygous nature of PAM may be underrecognized in non-consanguineous populations. Liu et al. 27 noted that the clinical manifestations, imaging, and pathological features of patients with compound heterozygous mutations were similar to those of patients with homozygous mutations, suggesting that the compound heterozygous state should be actively sought in genetic testing, particularly in patients without evidence of consanguinity. Our case supports this observation and further demonstrates that compound heterozygosity can lead to end-stage disease requiring transplantation.

The absence of the deletion in the patient's daughter suggests either a de novo event or germline mosaicism. This finding has important implications for genetic counseling. While the c.910A > T variant could have been inherited from one parent, the deletion likely occurred spontaneously. We recommend the daughter undergo genetic testing for the c.910A > T variant to determine her carrier status. If she carries this variant, prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing should be considered to assess transmission risk. For at-risk relatives, cascade testing and low-dose HRCT screening may be considered after appropriate genetic counseling.

Limitations

Several limitations of this report should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-case study with a relatively short follow-up period (12 months). Second, parental DNA was unavailable (both parents deceased), preventing full segregation analysis across three generations. However, the daughter's carrier status successfully confirmed the trans configuration of the two variants. Third, while the CNV analysis was performed using robust NGS-based algorithms, the clinical laboratory report explicitly states that the accuracy and range of CNV detection have not been independently confirmed by orthogonal experimental methods (such as MLPA or qPCR) due to technical limitations of NGS data, and thus the exact breakpoints require further validation. Fourth, the full pedigree was incomplete as the affected brother could not be recruited for testing. Finally, while we compared our patient's clinical features with those of recently reported Chinese PAM cases, 27 the lack of standardized phenotypic reporting across studies limits the robustness of genotype–phenotype comparisons. Longer follow-up and functional studies are needed to assess long-term outcomes and the molecular consequences of this specific genotype.

Conclusions

This report expands the SLC34A2 mutational landscape by documenting a previously unreported compound heterozygous combination of variants, and demonstrates the importance of comprehensive genetic analysis incorporating both sequencing and copy number variation detection in PAM diagnosis. The compound heterozygous state identified here may represent an underrecognized genetic pattern in non-consanguineous populations. Our case, together with the recent literature, 27 reinforces that lung transplantation is a viable and effective treatment for end-stage PAM, with favorable short-term outcomes and no evidence of recurrence. Future studies should focus on genotype–phenotype correlations and the development of targeted therapies that address the underlying phosphate transport defect.
