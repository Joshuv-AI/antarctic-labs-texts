A spectral quantum algorithm for numerical differentiation and integration
Jordan Cioni, Fabio Semperlotti

Abstract
This paper presents low-complexity quantum algorithms for numerical differentiation and integration in partially bounded domains. The algorithms are based on a spectral approach that allows taking advantage of the computationally efficient quantum Fourier transform algorithm. Full derivations and gate-level circuit examples are presented. As part of the integration algorithms, we also develop an encoding technique to efficiently perform element-wise multiplication operations and unitary operators to perform partial summations of information encoded in state amplitudes.

1.1
Background on Quantum Differentiation Algorithms

Algorithmic Differentiation is a computational implementation of the chain rule
[
6
]
and, as such, it is not accompanied by the approximation errors of traditional numerical methods
[
12
]
.
In both quantum and classical implementations, AD takes an input function and a point within its domain, and reduces it to a composition of primitive functions and operations (i.e. logarithms, trigonometric functions, addition, multiplication, etc.) with known derivatives
[
13
,
14
]
.
Using quantum implementations of the primitive functions
[
15
,
12
,
16
]
, the derivative is evaluated at the given point
[
6
]
.
It follows that the error associated with this technique is primarily associated with the quantum implementation of each primitive function
[
12
]
.
Likewise, its complexity is given by the sum of each primitive function’s quantum complexity
[
6
]
.
In comparison to other numerical differentiation techniques, AD is not capable of estimating a derivative from a set of function samples taken at points in the function domain.
Furthermore, because AD only returns the input function’s derivative at a single point, the technique must be repeatedly applied to estimate a function’s derivative throughout a computational domain.

In the field of quantum chemistry, the VQE is commonly used to estimate energy derivatives with respect to atomic variables such as nuclei position and external fields
[
3
,
17
,
18
,
2
]
, which correspond to a variety of physical properties including nuclear forces, vibrational frequencies, and radiation intensities
[
17
,
18
]
.
VQEs use a parameterized circuit,
U(\theta)
, and a corresponding ansatz state,
\ket{\psi(\theta)}=U(\theta)\ket{0}^{\bigotimes n}
, where
\ket{0}^{\bigotimes n}
is an
n
-qubit initial state vector, and
\theta
is a vector of circuit parameters.
The parameters are variationally optimized until the expectation value of the circuit,
E
, is minimized.
Then, the derivative of the expectation value is obtained from previously known relationships with the optimal circuit parameters

[
3
,
19
]
.
For this approach to be applicable, the differentiand must be encoded as the expectation value of a Hamiltonian operation. For this reason, this approach is especially suitable for studying atomic energy derivatives because the Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+V(x)
, where
V(x)
is the potential energy of the system, already corresponds to an atomic system’s the total energy.
Given that this quantum derivative technique requires problem-specific information to encode a suitable Hamiltonian operator, it is not readily generalized to other problems and disciplines.
Due to the analytical nature of this technique
[
3
]
, the VQE approach requires prior knowledge of the input’s functional form; therefore, this method also cannot estimate a derivative from a set of samples.

Jordan’s algorithm for numerical gradient estimation
[
10
]
approximates the input function,
f
, over a small domain centered at the origin using a first-order Taylor series,
f(\bm{x})=f(\bm{0})+\bm{x}\cdot\nabla f
.
The input function is evaluated using an oracular function,
O_{f}=e^{i2\pi\frac{N}{ml}f(\bm{x})}
, where
l
defines a sufficiently small region to enable a linear approximation of
f
,
m
is an estimate of the largest partial derivative, and
N=2^{n}
where
n
is the number of qubits used to describe the partial derivatives along each dimension.
For a
d
-dimensional function, a single query is made to the oracle function to evaluate
f
at
d
points, one along each dimension, surrounding the origin.
Using addition modular
N
, the result is added to an output register prepared in an eigenstate of addition mod
N
.
Consequently, the input registers receive a phase-kickback proportional to
f
[
20
]
.
Recalling the first-order Taylor expansion of
f
, the global phase of
f(\bm{0})
is ignored, and the phase terms proportional the function’s gradient are extracted using a QFT.

Using the implementation of Jordan’s algorithm described above, a function’s gradient may be estimated efficiently; however, it is limited to returning gradient estimations at the origin of the input domain
[
10
]
.
Owing to the prevalence of gradient descent methods in variational quantum algorithms and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithms (QAOA)
[
21
,
22
]
, efforts have been made to expand the capabilities of Jordan’s algorithm for these purposes
[
5
,
4
]
.
Using separable variable quantum addition and subtraction techniques, the input state is shifted by an arbitrary
\Delta x
, allowing Jordan’s algorithm to be used for a region centered beyond the origin of the input domain
[
5
]
.
Consequently, fully quantum gradient descent is achievable
[
5
]
.
Noting that Jordan’s algorithm encodes the objective function using a single call to a phase oracle function
[
10
]
, the gate-level complexity of the algorithm is undetermined without an estimate of the phase oracle’s complexity.
Efforts to derive an efficient phase oracle for Jordan’s algorithm have shown that, to evaluate a general
d
-dimensional function,
f
, with precision
\epsilon
, an oracle may be constructed with complexity
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon^{2}}\big)

[
4
]
; however, for smooth functions, the same oracle has complexity
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon}\big)

[
4
,
23
]
.
Recalling that Jordan’s algorithm relies on first-order terms of the given function’s Taylor expansion
[
10
]
, it has been shown that incorporation of higher-degree central difference formulas enables extension of Jordan’s algorithm to functions possessing non-negligible higher-order terms
[
4
]
.

Based on the above discussion of currently available algorithms for quantum differentiation, it is seen that most existing methods require significant prior knowledge of the input function.
AD requires the functional form of the input to be known
[
6
]
, VQE approaches require a known input function encoded as a Hamiltonian
[
3
]
, and Jordan’s algorithm requires a phase oracle constructed from the input function
[
10
]
.
At the same time, no existing techniques exploit quantum entanglement to return simultaneous derivative estimates at multiple points. It appears that quantum algorithms capable of performing numerical derivatives in a traditional sense, that is taking function samples as input and returning the derivative estimates as output without additional prior knowledge, are needed.

1.2
Background on Quantum Integration Algorithms

A classical Monte Carlo Integration (MCI) approximates the values of bound integrals by estimating the function’s mean over the specified domain using successive function evaluations at random points in the specified domain. Then, the integral value, which is proportional to the function’s mean, is easily obtained
[
24
]
.
In quantum implementations, QMCI follows a nearly identical framework. Several researchers have employed quantum computing to improve upon the
\mathcal{O}\big(N\big)
complexity of classical MCI, where integration is performed over
N
discrete domain points
[
25
,
8
,
9
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Using Grover’s algorithm to improve mean estimation resulted in a quadratically improved complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{N}\big)
; however, this implementation requires a Boolean input function
[
25
]
and efficiency of the Grover’s algorithm step critically depends on the function’s uniformity over the input domain
[
29
]
.
In the Quantum Supersampling method, QMCI successfully improved efficiency by simultaneously evaluating the input function at all discretized domain points stored in a superposition of states
[
8
]
.
Building on the Quantum Supersampling method, the quantum coin method was developed for functions that map inputs to
\{0,1\}
with quadratic improvements on the classical MCI complexity
[
9
]
.
Similar complexity improvements are also seen in QMCI algorithms developed for periodic functions
[
26
]
, Sobolev-like higher-dimensional functions
[
27
]
, and Holder class functions
[
30
]
.
Noting that these examples all strictly limit the input function, a generalized QMCI algorithm with similar
\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{N}\big)
complexity was recently developed using oracular polynomial function approximations to simultaneously evaluate the function over a superposition of discrete domain points
[
28
]
.
As highlighted for the quantum derivative algorithms, QMCI algorithms require significant prior knowledge of the problem. In particular, the integrand must be known in advance. These integration algorithms also restrict the type of integrations that may be performed: namely, they only perform definite numerical integration between specified limits.

Recently, a new quantum integration algorithm based on Riemann summations has been suggested as an alternative to QMCI
[
11
]
.
This novel quantum integration technique assumes normalized samples of the input function,
f
, at uniformly spaced domain points,
x_{i}
, can be efficiently encoded into the
N
state amplitudes of an
n
-qubit system, where
N=2^{n}
.
The integral over the first
M
terms, where
1\leq M\leq N
, may be approximated by a Riemann sum,
\Delta x\sum_{0}^{M-1}f(x_{i})
.
In this quantum algorithm, the partial summation is performed using a single unitary operation with complexity
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}M\big)
for a general value of
M
.
The unitary operator’s circuit is designed such that the corresponding matrix’s first column contains only ones in the first
M
rows while the remaining rows contain only zero.
Applying the operator stores the result of the partial summation in the amplitude of the system’s first state.
Once the first state is measured, the result is scaled by
\Delta x
and the input function’s
L2
norm during post-processing to recover the correct integral estimate.
Compared to the
\mathcal{O}\big(N\big)
complexity of common classical numerical integration algorithms, and the
\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{N}\big)

[
28
]
complexity of the most efficient QMCI algorithms, the quantum Riemann sum algorithm’s maximum complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)

[
11
]
represents a profound efficiency improvement which enables this algorithm to serve as a subroutine of more complex quantum algorithms. The primary limitation of this algorithm stems from its inability to estimate all partial summations simultaneously. In cases where the integral is required at all points along the domain, rather than a single exact integral estimate between two specified limits, this algorithm must be applied iteratively and will incur a complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(M\log_{2}M\big)
.

Based on the above considerations, it appears that there is only one algorithm capable of estimating the integral from a set of function samples, while no methods are available to evaluate the integral point-wise over the computational domain.
In this work, we present a technique for computing partially bound integrals, which yield the integral value at all domain discretizations between the specified limits.
Additionally, this article’s contributions to quantum integral calculation build off of its quantum derivative results to demonstrate a quantum implementation of the trapezoidal numerical integration technique, potentially demonstrating a route to developing higher-order quadrature-based quantum integration algorithms.

2
Overview of the Technical Approach

Based on the above considerations and on the shortcomings of current approaches for differentiation and integration, this study focuses on the development and implementation of a quantum algorithm for the numerical evaluation of derivatives and integrals.

The quantum derivative algorithm presented in this paper builds on the properties of the Fourier transform and re-envisions a classical algorithm originally presented in
[
31
,
32
]
. Given a complex-valued integrable Lebesgue function
f:R\to\mathbb{C}
defined on the real line such that
f\equiv f(x)
), the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
of the derivative of
f(x)
is given by
[
33
]
:

\mathcal{F}\bigg[\frac{df}{dx}\bigg]=i\omega\mathcal{F}\big[f(x)\big]

(1)

where
i
denotes the imaginary value,
i=\sqrt{\text{-}1}
, and
\omega
takes the meaning of either the pulsation or the wavenumber depending on the nature of the independent variable (either time or space, respectively).

By applying the inverse Fourier transform to Eq. (
1
), the function’s derivative is computed purely via the application of multiplications and Fourier transform operations, that is:

\frac{df}{dx}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigg[i\omega\mathcal{F}[f(x)]\bigg]

(2)

While Eq. (
2
) represents a trivial technique for analytical derivative calculation and a computationally expensive method for the classical calculation of numerical derivatives, it provides an effective blueprint for a quantum derivative algorithm. In fact, the methodology described above can form the foundation of an efficient quantum derivative algorithm, given that the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) is, to-date, one of the most efficient quantum algorithms available.

2.1
Discrete Fourier Transform-based Derivatives

By leveraging discrete Fourier transforms (DFT), researchers have implemented classical derivative algorithms based on the discrete analogue of Eq. (
2
)
[
31
,
32
]
. In this latter case, Eq. (
2
) is approximated as:

\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]_{j}=DFT^{-1}\big[i\omega_{k}~DFT[f]_{k}\big]_{j},\qquad\text{where }\omega_{k}=\begin{cases}2\pi k/N,&\quad\text{if }0\leq k<\frac{N}{2}\\
2\pi(k-N)/N,&\quad\text{if }\frac{N}{2}\leq k<N\end{cases}\,.

(3)

where
\big[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\big]_{j}
denotes the approximate derivative at the point
x_{j}
,
DFT[f]_{k}
denotes the
k^{\text{th}}
element of the discrete Fourier transform of
f(x)
, and
\omega_{k}
denotes the
k^{\text{th}}
discrete equivalent of the wavenumber from Eq. (
1
).

Remarking that the identity provided in Eq. (
1
) is obtained by evaluating the Fourier transform of a function’s derivative through purely analytical methods, the identity cannot be immediately extended to discrete applications where the function is known only numerically. Consequently, erroneous high-frequency oscillations were observed near domain boundaries when Eq. (
3
) was applied to calculate numerical derivatives
[
31
,
32
]
.
In the case of non-periodic input functions, the same erroneous end behavior was exacerbated.
These works also found that end behavior discrepancies are minimized by adapting Eq. (
3
) to use a modified wavenumber. While this modified wavenumber is well-documented in the existing literature
[
31
,
32
]
, its derivation exemplifies a robust process for development of QFT-based quantum algorithms. As such, a brief derivation will be presented.

To obtain the modified wavenumber appropriate for discrete Fourier transform-based derivatives, we begin with the central difference method.
For a function
f(x)
sampled at discrete points
x_{j}
with uniform separation given by
\Delta x
, the central difference method approximates the derivative at each point with the expression:

\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]=\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x-\Delta x)}{2\Delta x}

(4)

Taking the Fourier transform of Eq. (
4
):

\mathcal{F}\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]=\frac{\mathcal{F}\big[f(x+\Delta x)\big]-\mathcal{F}\big[f(x-\Delta x)\big]}{2\Delta x}

(5)

Applying the Fourier shift theorem, which states
\mathcal{F}\big[f(x+a)\big]_{k}=e^{\text{-}i\omega_{k}a}\mathcal{F}\big[f(x)\big]_{k}
, and denoting
\mathcal{F}\big[f(x)\big]_{k}
as
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we obtain the following relationship:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]_{k}&=\frac{e^{\text{-}i\omega_{k}\Delta x}-e^{i\omega_{k}\Delta x}}{2\Delta x}\mathcal{F}_{k}\\
&=\frac{i\sin(\omega_{k}\Delta x)}{\Delta x}\mathcal{F}_{k}\end{split}

(6)

where the definition of
\omega_{k}
is given in Eq. (
3
).
Noting that
\sin\big(\frac{2\pi(k-N)}{N}\big)=\sin\big(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\big)
, Eq. (
6
) does not need to be treated in a piecewise manner to account for the piecewise nature of
\omega_{k}
.
Thus, the modified wavenumber takes the following form:

\frac{i\sin(\frac{2\pi k}{N})}{\Delta x}

(7)

Finally, the discrete equivalent of the Eq. (
2
) is obtained:

\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]_{j}=DFT^{-1}\bigg[\frac{i\sin(\frac{2\pi k}{N})}{\Delta x}DFT[f\big]_{k}\bigg]_{j}

(8)

In the following, we will provide the mathematical description of both the Quantum Fourier Transform-based Derivative (QFTD) and the Quantum Fourier Transform-based Integral (QFTI) algorithms based on the general concept presented above.
For each algorithm, it will be assumed that the input function
f
can be efficiently encoded using amplitude encoding, or that
f
is encoded as a result of previous circuit operations.
We also highlight that, with appropriate encoding and bit ordering, the algorithms presented herein may be extended to higher-dimensional problems. However, to clearly illustrate the basic methodology, we choose the simple case of a one-dimensional function.

2.2
The Quantum Fourier Transform Derivative (QFTD)

The basic QFTD algorithm includes three key steps: 1) mapping the input function to the frequency space, 2) scaling it by an appropriate modified wavenumber, and 3) mapping the result back to the input domain.
In the context of quantum computing, where the QFT is used instead of the DFT, the modified wavenumber used to calculate a derivative is identical to the classical case.
Seeing that the modified wavenumber takes the form of a trigonometric function, the element-wise multiplication between the modified wavenumber vector and the frequency space states may be performed using a sequence of controlled rotation gates.

Figure 1
:
Three qubit example of the QFTD circuit. QFTs are applied to the
k
-register, which contains amplitude encoded function samples. Modified wavenumber scaling is accomplished by performing a series of controlled
Rx
gates on an ancillary qubit. Following the ancilla-controlled inverse QFT operations, the QFTD states are measured.

In the following, we show the viability of this process. First, consider the input state
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
of the QFTD circuit expressed as a summation over all
N
states of an
n
-qubit system:

\ket{\Psi_{0}}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f_{j}}{|f|}\ket{j}

(9)

where
|f|
denotes the
L2
norm of the input function, and the total number of samples,
N
, is given by
N=2^{n}
. Here we also define the superposition of input states as
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
and use
j
to denote individual states; thus,
f_{j}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
function sample which is stored in the amplitude of state
\ket{j}
. Throughout the remainder of this paper, we will follow the convention that, in the input domain (position, time), superpositions of states are denoted by
\ket{\Psi}
and individual states are indicated by
j
, while in the frequency domain, superpositions are given by
\ket{\Phi}
and individual states are indicated by
k
.

Applying a QFT to the quantum state induces the mapping
f_{j}\ket{j}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{k}\ket{k}
, hence resulting in:

\ket{\Phi_{0}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}

(10)

Now, we consider the desired output of the wavenumber multiplication circuit.
From Eq. (
8
), we see that the
k^{\text{th}}
must be scaled by
i\sin(\frac{2\pi k}{N})/\Delta x
. Noting that the application of the
1/\Delta x
multiplier can be performed classically during the post-processing phase, it is temporarily neglected. The output of the wavenumber multiplication circuit becomes:

\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}i\sin\bigg(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\bigg)\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}

(11)

In order to achieve the desired form of the quantum state as in Eq.
11
, we choose to incorporate an ancilla qubit initialized to the
\ket{0}_{a}
state, where subscript
a
denotes the ancilla register. We use the ancilla qubit to perform a conditional rotation of the quantum state that implements the mapping
\ket{k}\ket{0}_{a}\rightarrow A\ket{0}_{a}+i\sin(\theta_{k})\ket{k}\ket{1}_{a}
with the objective of isolating terms of the quantum state that did not receive a
\sin(\theta_{k})
term; in the ancilla state,
A
is some probability amplitude associated with the unused final states.
Given that the wavenumber introduces an imaginary number
i
, we perform the controlled rotation via a
R_{x}
gate defined as:

R_{x}(\theta)=e^{-iX\theta/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta/2)&-i\sin(\theta/2)\\
-i\sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)\end{bmatrix}

(12)

It is immediate to verify that, when this gate operates on a qubit in the
\ket{0}
state, the resulting qubit carries an
-i\sin(\theta/2)
coefficient on the
\ket{1}
state. Indeed, assuming a rotation of magnitude
-2\theta
and leveraging the parity properties of the trigonometric functions, the gate transforms the state
\ket{0}
as:

R_{x}(-2\theta)\ket{0}=cos(\theta)\ket{0}+i\sin(\theta)\ket{1}

(13)

Therefore, we seek to perform the following transformation:

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}\ket{0}_{a}\rightarrow\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigg[\cos(\theta_{k})\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}\ket{0}_{a}+i\sin(\theta_{k})\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}\ket{1}_{a}\bigg]

(14)

or equivalently:

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}\ket{0}_{a}\rightarrow\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}e^{iX\theta_{k}}\ket{0}_{a}\,.

(15)

where
\theta_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{N}
. In binary form,
\theta_{k}
is expressed as:

\begin{split}\theta_{k}&=\frac{2\pi}{2^{n}}(2^{n-1}k_{n-1}+2^{n-2}k_{n-2}+...+2k_{1}+k_{0})\\
&=\pi(2^{0}k_{n-1}+2^{-1}k_{n-2}+...+2^{2-n}k_{1}+2^{1-n}k_{0})\end{split}

(16)

and our desired state becomes:

\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{k}e^{iX2\pi(2^{\text{-}1}k_{n-1}+2^{\text{-}2}k_{n-2}+\cdots+2^{1-n}k_{1}+2^{\text{-}n}k_{0})}\ket{0}_{a}\,.

(17)

We now express
\ket{k}
as
\ket{k_{n-1}}\ket{k_{n-2}}\cdots\ket{k_{1}}\ket{k_{0}}
, and rearrange the exponential terms of Eq. (
17
):

\begin{split}\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}&e^{iX2^{0}\pi k_{n\text{-}1}}\ket{k_{n-1}}~e^{iX2^{\text{-}1}\pi k_{n\text{-}2}}\ket{k_{n-2}}~\cdots\\
&\cdots~e^{iX2^{2\text{-}n}\pi k_{1}}\ket{k_{1}}~e^{iX2^{1\text{-}n}\pi k_{0}}\ket{k_{0}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{k}}{|f|}\ket{0}_{a}\end{split}

(18)

To observe how gates must be implemented at the individual qubit level, we wish to express
\ket{\Psi_{2}}
in its product state. However,
\mathcal{F}_{k}
varies with each state therefore requiring it to be written in a different form that may be factored into the product state.
To that end, we define
\mathcal{F}_{k}
as the product:

\mathcal{F}_{k}=b_{k_{n\text{-}1}}^{(n-1)}b_{k_{n\text{-}2}}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{k_{1}}^{(1)}b_{k_{0}}^{(0)}\,,

(19)

where the superscript of each
b
coefficient denotes a particular qubit, and the subscript denotes that qubit’s
\ket{0}
or
\ket{1}
state. For example, if
k=1
, then
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the coefficient of the state
\ket{00\cdots 01}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{k}=b_{0}^{(n-1)}b_{0}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{0}^{(1)}b_{1}^{(0)}
. Using this notation,
\ket{\Phi_{1}}
may be expressed as:

\begin{split}\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum_{k_{n\text{-}1}=0}^{1}\sum_{k_{n\text{-}2}=0}^{1}\cdots\sum_{k_{0}=0}^{1}&\bigg[b_{k_{n\text{-}1}}^{(n-1)}e^{iX2^{0}\pi k_{n\text{-}1}}\ket{k_{n-1}}~\otimes~b_{k_{n\text{-}2}}^{(n-2)}e^{iX2^{\text{-}1}\pi k_{n\text{-}2}}\ket{k_{n-2}}~\otimes~\cdots\\
&\cdots\otimes b_{k_{1}}^{(1)}e^{iX2^{2\text{-}n}\pi k_{1}}\ket{k_{1}}~\otimes~b_{k_{0}}^{(0)}e^{iX2^{1\text{-}n}\pi k_{0}}\ket{k_{0}}\bigg]\otimes\ket{0}_{a}\,,\end{split}

(20)

Then, the final product state is obtained by evaluating each summation:

\begin{split}\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\frac{1}{|f|}\bigg[b_{0}^{(n-1)}\ket{0}&+b^{(n-1)}_{1}e^{iX2^{0}\pi}\ket{1}\bigg]\otimes\bigg[b_{0}^{(n-2)}\ket{0}+b_{1}^{(n-2)}e^{iX2^{\text{-}1}\pi}\ket{1}\bigg]\otimes\cdots\\
&\cdots\otimes\bigg[b_{0}^{(1)}\ket{0}+b_{1}^{(1)}e^{iX2^{2\text{-}n}\pi}\ket{1}\bigg]\otimes\bigg[b_{0}^{(0)}\ket{0}+b_{1}^{(0)}e^{iX2^{1\text{-}n}\pi}\bigg]\otimes\ket{0}_{a}\,.\end{split}

(21)

At this point, it is important to recognize that, as
\ket{\Phi_{0}}
is an entangled state, the
b
coefficients appearing in Eq. (
21
) cannot be algebraically obtained
[
20
,
34
]
; however, determining their exact values is not needed in the present approach.
Focusing on the exponential terms in Eq. (
21
), it is observed that exponential terms only appear when the
p^{th}
qubit is in state
\ket{1}
, that is
\ket{k_{p}}=\ket{1}
. It follows that the
k
-qubits can serve as controls for a sequence of ancillary qubit rotations in order to generate the correct final state.
Furthermore, we see that the
p^{th}
qubit in the
k
-register must control an ancilla rotation of
\phi_{p}=2^{p-n+1}\pi
radians.
Recalling Eq. (
13
), to produce a rotation of
\theta
using an
R_{x}
gate, the argument passed to the gate must be
-2\theta
.
Accordingly, the wavenumber rotation circuit presented in Fig.
2
shows the
p^{\text{th}}
qubit controlling an
R_{x}
gate with the argument
-2^{p-n+2}\pi
radians.

Figure 2
:

\ket{0}_{a}
when the
k^{\text{th}}
superposition state is scaled by
i\sin(2\pi k/N)
.

To illustrate more clearly the effects of the controlled rotations, we restate Eq. (
21
) using the associative property of the Kronecker product, that is
\ket{a}\otimes\ket{b}\otimes\ket{c}=\big(\ket{a}\otimes\ket{b}\big)\otimes\ket{c}
. In the proceeding expression, note that the ancilla qubit is only subjected to a rotation of an angle
\phi_{p}
when the
p^{th}
qubit is in state
\ket{1}
:

\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\frac{1}{|f|}\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}^{(n-1)}b_{0}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{0}^{(1)}b_{0}^{(0)}\\
b_{0}^{(n-1)}b_{0}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{1}^{(1)}b_{0}^{(0)}~e^{\text{-}iX\phi_{0}}\\
b_{0}^{(n-1)}b_{0}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{1}^{(1)}b_{0}^{(0)}~e^{\text{-}iX\phi_{1}}\\
b_{0}^{(n-1)}b_{0}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{1}^{(1)}b_{1}^{(0)}~e^{\text{-}iX(\phi_{1}+\phi_{0})}\\
\vdots\\
b_{1}^{(n-1)}b_{1}^{(n-2)}\cdots b_{1}^{(1)}b_{1}^{(0)}~e^{\text{-}iX(\phi_{n\text{-}1}+\phi_{n\text{-}2}+\cdots+\phi_{1}+\phi_{0})}\\
\end{pmatrix}\otimes\ket{0}_{a}

(22)

Referring to
\theta_{k}
’s binary form in Eq.
16
, we may express
\theta_{k}
as a function of the controlled rotation angles:

\theta_{k}=\phi_{n-1}k_{n-1}+\phi_{n-2}k_{n-2}+\cdots+\phi_{1}k_{1}+\phi_{0}k_{0}

(23)

Combining Eq. (
19
), Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
23
), we perform the following simplification:

\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\frac{1}{|f|}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{0}e^{iX\theta_{0}}\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}e^{iX\theta_{1}}\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}e^{iX\theta_{2}}\\
\mathcal{F}_{3}e^{iX\theta_{3}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{N\text{-}1}e^{iX\theta_{N\text{-}1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}

(24)

Finally, the last Kronecker product is evaluated and the
Rx
operators included in the
k
vector are allowed to act on the ancilla qubit:

\ket{\Phi_{1}}=\frac{1}{|f|}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{0}e^{\text{-}iX(\text{-}2\theta_{0})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}e^{\text{-}iX(\text{-}2\theta_{1})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}e^{\text{-}iX(\text{-}2\theta_{2})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}\\
\mathcal{F}_{3}e^{\text{-}iX(\text{-}2\theta_{3})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{N\text{-}1}e^{\text{-}iX(\text{-}2\theta_{N\text{-}1})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}_{a}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta_{0})\mathcal{F}_{0}\\
i\sin(\theta_{0})\mathcal{F}_{0}\\
\cos(\theta_{1})\mathcal{F}_{1}\\
i\sin(\theta_{1})\mathcal{F}_{1}\\
\cos(\theta_{2})\mathcal{F}_{2}\\
i\sin(\theta_{2})\mathcal{F}_{2}\\
\cos(\theta_{3})\mathcal{F}_{3}\\
i\sin(\theta_{3})\mathcal{F}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
\cos(\theta_{N\text{-}1})\mathcal{F}_{N\text{-}1}\\
i\sin(\theta_{N\text{-}1})\mathcal{F}_{N\text{-}1}\end{pmatrix}

(25)

Because the ancilla qubit was incorporated in the least significant bit (LSB) position, its state alternates between
\ket{0}_{a}
and
\ket{1}_{a}
. Noting that the
\sin(\theta_{k})
terms only appear when the ancilla qubit is in the state
\ket{0}_{a}
, Eq. (
25
) reveals that we have successfully obtained a state in the form of Eq. (
14
). Note, the ancilla qubit was included in the LSB position while presenting the underlying theory of the QFTD circuit; however, the ancilla is encoded in the most significant bit (MSB) position in all circuit examples so that states receiving the proper modified wavenumber are easily identified when viewing the full set of circuit observations.

Once the
\ket{\Phi_{1}}
is obtained, the algorithm is completed by applying an inverse QFT to the
k
-register. Given that only the
\ket{1}_{a}
states have received the correct phase, the inverse QFT must be controlled by the
\ket{1}_{a}
state:

\ket{\Psi_{1}}=QFT^{-1}\Bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}i\sin(\theta_{k})\mathcal{F}_{k}\ket{k}\ket{1}_{a}\Bigg]

(26)

Following the controlled inverse-QFT,
\ket{\Psi}_{3}
is proportional to the numerical derivative of the input function.
After measuring all qubits, observations with
\ket{0}_{a}
are discarded and the correct amplitudes are recovered though post-processing.
The input function’s norm and the constant multiplier
1/\Delta x
must be factored in to recover the amplitude.
Finally, given that each state’s probability amplitude is proportional to the squared state coefficient, the measured values are proportional to the square of the numerical derivative. Therefore, the correct amplitude is recovered by:

\bigg[\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\bigg]_{j}^{2}=\bigg(\frac{|f|}{\Delta x}\bigg)^{2}~\psi_{j}^{2}\,,

(27)

where
\psi_{j}^{2}
is the measurement output associated with the
j^{th}
state. Using Eq. (
27
), the minimum resolution of the QFTD algorithm may be obtained. For a circuit simulated using
M
shots, the measurement least count is
\psi^{2}=1/M
. Substituting into Eq. (
27
), the QFTD resolution is obtained:

\epsilon=\frac{|f|^{2}}{M\Delta x^{2}}

(28)

where
\epsilon
denotes the QFTD algorithm’s resolution. In regions where the square of the input function’s derivative is less than
\epsilon
, the associated states are not expected to be observed when measuring the circuit. At such points, a value of zero is assumed for the QFTD output during post-processing.

2.3
The Quantum Fourier Transform Integration (QFTI)

2.3.1
Derivation of the QFTI Modified Wavenumber

Using a procedure similar to that shown for the QFTD modified wavenumber, a QFTI modified wavenumber algorithm can also be obtained.
Beginning with the discrete approximation of the area of a function
f
given by the trapezoidal method,
\Delta A_{j}=\frac{\Delta x}{2}\big[f(x_{j}+\Delta x)+f(x_{j})\big]
, where
\Delta A_{j}
is the area beneath the
j^{\text{th}}
section of
f
, a convenient form of the wavenumber will be obtained by considering a step size of
2\Delta x
:

\Delta A_{j}=\Delta x\big[f(x_{j}+\Delta x)+f(x_{j}-\Delta x)\big]

(29)

As before, applying a Fourier transform to Eq. (
29
) results in a trigonometric modified wavenumber:

\mathcal{F}[\Delta A]_{k}=\Delta x\cos\bigg(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\bigg)\mathcal{F}_{k}

(30)

and the differential area under the
j^{th}
section of
f
may be represented as:

\Delta A_{j}=\Delta x~QFT^{-1}\bigg[\cos\bigg(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\bigg)\mathcal{F}_{k}\bigg]_{j}\,.

(31)

2.3.2
The QFTI Procedure

A direct comparison of Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
31
) reveals that the differential and integral modified wavenumber formulations differ only by a constant multiplier, and by the presence of a phase term.
This observation allows using a nearly identical wavenumber rotation circuit to scale each term with the appropriate modified wavenumber.
Referring to the definition of the
Rx
gate in Eq. (
12
), scaling by the term
\cos(\frac{2\pi k}{N})
can be accomplished by modifying the QFTD circuit to initialize the ancilla qubit in the
\ket{1}_{a}
state. Then, upon measuring the final output, observations with the ancilla qubit in the state
\ket{1}_{a}
have received the proper phase, while observations in the
\ket{0}_{a}
should be discarded.

Figure 3
:
Three qubit example of the QFTI circuit. Following a similar procedure used in the QFTD algorithm, the differential area of the input function is encoded for each point. Partial summations of each differential area are performed using the partial summation matrix product operator (PsMPO) gate to approximate the integral at each point.

Unlike the QFTD algorithms, the QFTI algorithm’s application of the QFT, the wavenumber rotation, and the inverse-QFT only encodes a vector of the differential areas of the function
f
.
In the continuous case, an integral with a fixed lower bound and an upper bound located arbitrarily within the problem domain is given by:

\begin{split}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}f(x)~dx&=F(x)-F(x_{0})\\
&=I(x)\end{split}

(32)

where
F(x)
is the functional form of the integral of
f(x)
,
F(x_{0})
is the constant of integration obtained by evaluating
F(x)
at
x_{0}
, and
I(x)
is introduced to denote the difference
F(x)-F(x_{0})
.
Evaluating Eq. (
32
) for a particular value of
x
yields the area under
f
between
x
and
x_{0}
.
Therefore, to discretely approximate the result of Eq. (
32
) at an arbitrary
x_{j}
in the problem domain, a partial summation is performed from
x_{0}
to
x_{j}
:

I(x_{j})=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Delta A_{i}

(33)

Using a unit lower triangular matrix denoted by
\Sigma
, a single matrix product operation may be applied to evaluate
I(x_{j})
, abbreviated
I_{j}
, for all domain values:

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}I_{0}\\
I_{1}\\
\vdots\\
I_{N-1}\end{pmatrix}&=\Sigma\begin{pmatrix}\Delta A_{0}\\
\Delta A_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\Delta A_{N\text{-}1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta A_{0}\\
\Delta A_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\Delta A_{N\text{-}1}\end{pmatrix}\\
\end{split}

(34)

Assuming that the lower triangular matrix may be encoded as a unitary gate, the above equation may be implemented within the QFTI circuit.
We begin by constructing a Hermitian matrix
H
as follows:

H=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Sigma^{\dagger}\\
\Sigma&0\end{bmatrix}

(35)

where the symbol
\dagger
denotes the complex conjugate transpose operation. Following the procedure for block encoding of a matrix product operator
[
35
]
, it is possible to transform the hermitian matrix
H
into a unitary matrix
U_{H}
having the form:

U_{H}=\begin{bmatrix}H/\eta&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}

(36)

where
\eta
is the spectral norm of
H
, while
B
,
C
, and
D
are particular matrices described in the following. Given that
U_{H}
is unitary,
B
must be a matrix such that
BB^{\dagger}=I-HH^{\dagger}
, where
I
is the identity matrix. Expressing
H
as the product of a diagonal matrix
D
and its eigenvector matrices
P
and
P^{\dagger}
, we obtain:

H=PDP^{\dagger}

(37)

The expression for
BB^{\dagger}
may be written in terms of known quantities; therefore,
B
may be found by evaluating:

B=\sqrt{I-D^{2}/\eta^{2}}P^{\dagger}

(38)

Finally, defining the rectangular matrix
W=\begin{bmatrix}H/\eta\\
B\end{bmatrix}
and performing QR-decomposition allows
W
to be written as the product of the unitary matrix
Q
, and the rectangular matrix
R
:

\begin{split}W&=QR\\
&=U_{H}R\end{split}

(39)

where
Q
is a matrix of the form
U_{H}
given in Eq. (
36
).

During construction of
U_{H}
, the overall size of the problem was increased multiple times. Starting from the lower-triangular matrix
\Sigma
, the operator’s size was doubled to construct the hermitian matrix
H
. While constructing
U_{H}
, the size of the partial sum operator was doubled again. Each doubling of operator’s size corresponds to inclusion of an ancilla qubit to the system.
In Fig.
3
, the two additional qubits were encoded in the
b
and
c
registers and initialized to states
\ket{0}_{b}
and
\ket{0}_{c}
, respectively.
To ensure the operation only includes states which received the appropriate phase during the wavenumber rotations, we control the summation operation using the
\ket{0}_{a}
state. Then, the input to the partial summation matrix product operator (PsMPO) is
\ket{0}_{b}\ket{0}_{c}\ket{\Psi_{3}}
, where ancilla qubits are initialized in the most significant bit positions for convenience. Performing the operation
U_{H}\big(\ket{0}_{b}\ket{0}_{c}\ket{\Psi_{1}}\big)
yields the expression:

\ket{\Psi_{2}}=\ket{0}_{a}\begin{bmatrix}U_{H}&C\\
B&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{A}\\
\vec{0}\end{pmatrix}=\ket{0}_{a}\begin{bmatrix}0&\Sigma^{\dagger}/\eta&C_{11}&C_{12}\\
\Sigma/\eta&0&C_{21}&C_{22}\\
B_{11}&B_{12}&D_{11}&D_{12}\\
B_{21}&B_{22}&D_{21}&D_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{A}\\
\vec{0}\\
\vec{0}\\
\vec{0}\end{pmatrix}

(40)

where
\vec{A}
denotes the vector of differential areas, and the subscripts
11
,
12
,
21
, and
22
respectively indicate the top-left, top-right, lower-left, and lower-right quadrants of
B
,
C
, and
D
matrices.
Evaluating the matrix product and Kronecker product, the final state is obtained:

\ket{\Psi_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\Sigma\vec{A}/\eta\\
0\\
B_{12}\vec{A}\\
0\\
B_{22}\vec{A}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(41)

From the final expression of
\ket{\Psi_{2}}
, it is observed that, for the bit-ordering used in this algorithm, the partial summation results are stored in states where the three most significant bits are
\ket{001}
. At this time, the coefficients of the
\ket{001\cdots}
states are proportional to
I(x_{j})
.

As was the case for the QFTD, upon measuring the statevector, the results must be scaled to recover the correct amplitudes. Referencing Eq. (
31
), we see the coefficients must be scaled by a factor of
1/\Delta x
to account for its absence during the wavenumber rotation procedure. Additionally, the norm of the input function
|f|
, and the spectral norm of the summation matrix
\eta
must also be factored into the result. Finally, we recall that the measured quantities are not the state coefficients but their squared values. It follows that the circuit output is given by:

\big[I(x_{j})\big]^{2}=\big(|f|\eta\Delta x\big)^{2}\psi_{j}^{2}

(42)

where
\psi_{j}^{2}
again denotes the
j^{\text{th}}
state’s measurement output. Again, the QFTI algorithm’s resolution for a simulation using
M
shots is obtained by evaluating Eq. (
42
) at
\psi^{2}=1/M
:

\epsilon=\frac{\big(|f|\eta\Delta x\big)^{2}}{M}

(43)

As before, in regions where the analytical output is lesser than the QFTI resolution, the associated states are not expected to be observed when measuring the circuit. Therefore, when a state is not observed its QFTI output is assigned a value of zero during post-processing.

.

Second, because two additional qubits are required to perform the cumulative summations, the number of
k
-register qubits used to store function samples is restricted to 6 for simulation efficiency. Thus, QFTI simulations presented herein employ 6

Discussion

The previous numerical results illustrated the effectiveness of the QFTD and QFTI algorithms for a different range of sample functions. In the following, we briefly address the complexity of these algorithms.
Using two applications of the QFT circuit with complexity
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)

n=\log_{2}N

Rx

Referring to Eq. (
4

Comparing the QFTD’s complexity to that of the central difference method,
\mathcal{O}\big(N\big)

In the case of the quantum integration algorithm, the complexity of the partial summation operation must also be accounted for.
In the examples presented above, the PsMPO was implemented using Qiskit’s Unitary Gate function.
This additional unitary operation adds a single layer to the circuit’s overall depth; however, the gate level complexity introduced by the PsMPO is not clearly defined. Indeed, only a lower bound of
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)
may be used to estimate the QFTI algorithm’s gate-level complexity.
Comparing to the quantum Riemann sum method’s maximum complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)

[

]

To the author’s best knowledge, at this time the QFTI algorithm is the only applicable technique for numerical integration throughout an arbitrary domain; however, in cases where only the exact integral between two points is required, the Riemann sum technique is generally more efficient.

While assessing the practical potential of the quantum numerical calculus techniques presented in this work, the error associated with each algorithm is also of interest.
Because the results are stored as probability amplitudes, the total number of shots used to simulate the circuit inversely affects the precision of probability amplitude estimation.
Additionally, the number of shots required to capture a function’s behavior depends strongly on the function itself.

For both algorithms presented, the final result is encoded in the state amplitudes, which means that the quantity obtained by measuring the probability of a given state is proportional to the square of the result (either the derivative or integral of the function).
Hence, during the state measurement process, the sign information is lost. This aspect could ultimately hinder the applicability of the algorithms in practical applications where the final result sought is the actual derivative or integral of a function. However, it is unlikely that quantum algorithms will ever be used for simple data processing operations, like differentiation or integration, which are well-addressed by classical algorithms.
On the other side, the correct signs are encoded in the quantum state coefficients, which means that the information is available in the quantum state at any level of the computation.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we presented a quantum implementation of a DFT-based algorithm that enables numerical differentiation of a generic function. Starting from a uniform sampling of the function, the algorithm returns derivative estimates at discrete points selected within the domain of interest.
For an input consisting of N function samples, the quantum derivative algorithm was found to have a complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)
and an error of
\mathcal{O}\big(N^{\text{-}2}\big)
.
Expanding on the spectral technique presented for function differentiation, a quantum algorithm for partially bound trapezoidal integration was also developed.
The integration method was found to have a minimal complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(\log_{2}N\big)
, and an error of
\mathcal{O}\big(N^{\text{-}1}\big)
.
Additionally, this paper presented a technique to perform inner-product-like element-wise multiplication between a vector of arbitrary values and vector containing trigonometric function samples. The technique may be extended to element-wise multiplication with other compositions of trigonometric or complex exponential functions in future work.
To build upon the quantum numerical calculus algorithms presented herein, additional work is required to preserve the sign information of differentiation and integration results, which is lost as a result of the measuring process.
Finally, future work may consider adapting these algorithms to include frequency-based signal filtering by detecting high-frequency noise prior to the inverse QFT portion of the algorithm and discarding noise results during post-processing.
