Intraocular and ocular perfusion pressure changes during pediatric laparoscopic appendectomy
Authors: Yasemin Dere Gunal, Nesrin Buyuktortop Gokcinar, Serap Yorubulut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate intraocular pressure (IOP) and ocular perfusion pressure (OPP) changes during pediatric laparoscopic appendectomy performed in the Trendelenburg position and to identify potential perioperative ocular risks.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, IOP and OPP were measured in 25 ASA I pediatric patients undergoing emergency laparoscopic appendectomy. OPP was calculated as the difference between mean arterial blood pressure (MABP = diastolic BP + 1/3 [systolic BP – diastolic BP]) and IOP. Measurements were obtained with a Tono-Pen Avia® tonometer at eight standardized intraoperative timepoints (T1–T8), including the pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg phases. Statistical analysis was performed using repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction.ResultsA significant rise in IOP was observed during Trendelenburg positioning, with peak values at T6 (approximately the 10th minute in Trendelenburg). Paired-samples comparisons demonstrated significantly higher left-eye IOP during Trendelenburg (T5–T6), with mean interocular differences of 1.32 mmHg (T5, p < 0.001) and 1.32 mmHg (T6, p = 0.00001). A mixed-effects model (fixed factors: time × eye; random intercept for subject) confirmed a strong time effect (p < 0.001) and a trend-level interaction (p ≈ 0.08), supporting side-dependent IOP elevation. OPP showed a transient decline during pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg, with a minimum mean of 48.5 ± 4.0 mmHg at T6 (p < 0.01). Transient IOP elevations above 21 mmHg occurred in 5 of 25 patients (20%), all resolving within minutes after desufflation. No postoperative ocular complications were observed.ConclusionTrendelenburg positioning during pediatric laparoscopy causes transient but statistically significant alterations in IOP and OPP. Although these changes were short-lived and clinically asymptomatic in healthy children, perioperative ocular risk assessment remains important in patients with glaucoma, elevated baseline IOP, or optic nerve vulnerability.

Introduction

Minimally invasive surgery has become the standard approach for many pediatric surgical procedures, including appendectomy. Laparoscopic techniques offer numerous advantages, such as shorter recovery time, reduced pain, and improved cosmetic outcomes [1]. However, these benefits are accompanied by physiologic challenges, particularly due to pneumoperitoneum and patient positioning. The Trendelenburg position—frequently employed to enhance visualization of the pelvic organs—alters venous return, intracranial dynamics, and respiratory mechanics, and has been shown to affect intraocular pressure (IOP) in adult patients [2–6]. Despite its widespread use, the implications of this positioning on IOP in the pediatric population remain poorly understood [7, 8].

IOP is a dynamic parameter influenced by aqueous humor production and outflow, as well as episcleral venous pressure. Physiologic or surgical factors that increase venous pressure—such as head-down positioning or elevated intraabdominal pressure—can transiently elevate IOP [2, 9]. In adult patients, Trendelenburg position during laparoscopic or robotic surgeries has been associated with significant IOP increases and, in rare cases, postoperative visual loss or ischemic optic neuropathy, especially in patients with preexisting ocular conditions [9–15]. These effects are well documented across procedures such as prostatectomy and gynecologic laparoscopy [10, 11, 14]. By contrast, only a few pediatric studies have addressed this issue, showing transient IOP elevations related to pneumoperitoneum and positioning [7, 8]. However, these investigations assessed limited intraoperative timepoints, and detailed patterns of IOP and OPP fluctuation in children remain insufficiently characterized [16, 17].

Noninvasive methods such as handheld applanation tonometry allow real-time IOP monitoring under general anesthesia without interrupting surgical workflow, offering a unique opportunity to track pressure fluctuations throughout surgery [17, 18].

Although IOP fluctuations during Trendelenburg positioning are well documented in adults, pediatric eyes may present unique vulnerabilities due to anatomical and physiological differences. The scleral wall and lamina cribrosa are thinner and more compliant in children, which could potentially render the optic nerve more sensitive to transient IOP spikes. Moreover, autoregulatory mechanisms of ocular blood flow are still maturing during childhood, possibly increasing susceptibility to perfusion fluctuations [13, 16]. While most healthy children may tolerate transient IOP elevations, those with congenital glaucoma, optic nerve hypoplasia, or vascular dysregulation could face a greater theoretical risk of optic nerve injury or postoperative visual disturbances.

This study aims to evaluate the changes in IOP and ocular perfusion pressure (OPP) during pediatric laparoscopic appendectomy performed in the Trendelenburg position. By assessing bilateral IOP at multiple standardized timepoints throughout the surgery and correlating these with mean arterial blood pressure, we sought to identify whether positioning or intraabdominal pressure has a greater influence on intraoperative ocular dynamics. To our knowledge, this is one of the first prospective studies addressing this question in a pediatric population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study was approved by the Kırıkkale University Clinical Research Ethics Board on 07/11/2017 with the approval number 21/08. Written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all pediatric participants.

A total of 25 pediatric patients scheduled for emergency laparoscopic appendectomy with a preoperative diagnosis of acute appendicitis were included. All patients had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I and no known ocular pathology or systemic comorbidities.

Because this study involved emergency pediatric appendectomy cases, comprehensive ophthalmic examinations such as optical biometry or axial length measurement could not be performed. However, bedside direct ophthalmoscopy and handheld autorefraction were conducted to rule out any overt ocular pathology or significant refractive error. A brief medical and family history was also obtained from parents regarding previous eye examinations and the use of corrective lenses. None of the children had a known ocular disorder or wore glasses, and all were considered to have emmetropic or only mild refractive status (≤ ± 2.0 D).

Exclusion criteria included any history of ocular disease (e.g., glaucoma), chronic medication use, or conversion to open surgery. We included only emergent daytime appendectomy cases to ensure that the same ophthalmologist was consistently present for standardized measurements, thereby minimizing interobserver variability. This focused sampling strategy allowed for uniform data collection across all cases.

Anesthesia technique

All patients included in the study were classified as ASA physical status I. To eliminate variability related to anesthetic agents and ventilatory parameters, a standardized anesthesia protocol and mechanical ventilation settings were applied to all patients.

Following standard monitoring—electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate, pulse oximetry, noninvasive blood pressure, neuromuscular transmission, entropy, and body temperature—premedication was administered with intravenous midazolam at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg. Anesthesia induction was achieved with intravenous propofol (2–5 mg/kg), rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg), and fentanyl (1 µg/kg). After ensuring adequate muscle relaxation, endotracheal intubation was performed.

Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane administered in a 50% oxygen–air mixture. Patients were mechanically ventilated with a tidal volume of 8–10 mL/kg, and end-tidal CO₂ (PetCO₂) was maintained between 30 and 40 mmHg. A positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cm H₂O was applied, and peak airway pressure (Ppeak) values were recorded.

Intravenous fluid therapy was administered using calculated maintenance fluid requirements and intraoperative replacement with dextrose-saline solutions (D5W + saline). At the conclusion of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was reversed with 0.04 mg/kg neostigmine co-administered with atropine. Extubation was performed when adequate spontaneous respiration was confirmed.

During laparoscopic insufflation, a gradual rise in end-tidal CO₂ was observed, accompanied by a transient increase in intra-abdominal pressure. With the transition to the Trendelenburg position, PetCO₂ plateaued, and blood pressure stabilized. Although PEEP was applied to prevent alveolar collapse and atelectasis, it may have transiently reduced venous return, potentially affecting systemic blood pressure.

Surgical procedure and positioning

All surgeries were performed laparoscopically using three trocars. Pneumoperitoneum was established using CO₂ insufflation, initially set to 12 mmHg. After establishing pneumoperitoneum in the supine position, patients were tilted into a 15–20° Trendelenburg position with a leftward tilt to optimize exposure of the lower right quadrant. At a later point during the surgery, intraabdominal pressure was increased to 14 mmHg for a second measurement phase. Following the completion of the surgery, patients were returned to the supine position and extubated after recovery of spontaneous respiration.

Intraocular pressure and perfusion measurements

Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured using a Tono-Pen Avia® handheld applanation tonometer (Reichert Technologies®, Depew, NY, USA), a validated portable device suitable for intraoperative use [17, 18]. Each measurement comprised ten applanations automatically averaged by the device, and only readings with ≥ 95% confidence were accepted. The right and left eyes were assessed at eight standardized intraoperative time points:

To minimize interobserver variability and potential measurement bias, all IOP assessments were performed by a single experienced ophthalmologist.

Statistical analysis

Prior to data collection, an a priori power analysis was performed for within-subject IOP changes across the eight intraoperative timepoints (α = 0.05, desired power = 0.80).

Assuming an expected mean difference of approximately 2.5 mmHg (SD ≈ 2.0) based on previous pediatric and adult laparoscopic Trendelenburg studies [7], a minimum of 24–28 participants was required.

The final sample of 25 children met this threshold, providing adequate power for repeated-measures comparisons.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Differences in IOP, OPP, and MABP across the eight intraoperative timepoints (T1–T8) were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction.

Because right and left eyes represent within-subject measures, paired-samples t-tests were used to compare eyes at each stage.

In addition, a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects of time (T1–T8), eye (left/right), and their interaction (time × eye), and a random intercept for subject, was fitted to account for within-subject correlation and to assess whether interocular differences varied by stage.

For paired comparisons, standardized effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s dz (dz = t/√n) with 95% confidence intervals.

A post-hoc observed-power analysis based on the repeated-measures ANOVA (within-subject Time effect, 8 levels) confirmed excellent sample sensitivity to detect time-dependent changes in IOP (η²ₚ = 0.69–0.76; Cohen’s f = 1.49–1.77; observed power ≈ 1.00 at α = 0.05).

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

A total of 25 pediatric patients (9 girls, 16 boys) were included in the study. The mean age was 12.32 ± 3.01 years (range: 8–16), and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 17.44 ± 2.12 kg/m². The mean duration of surgery was 34.4 ± 3.9 min.

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of surgical stage on IOP in both eyes (p < 0.001).

IOP decreased significantly after anesthesia induction (T2) compared with baseline (T1) (p < 0.001) and showed a mild transient rise following intubation at T3 (p = 0.049 for the right eye), remaining below baseline levels.

With the onset of Trendelenburg positioning at 12 mmHg pneumoperitoneum (T5), IOP increased markedly in both eyes (p < 0.01 vs. T1). This early elevation reflects the initial hemodynamic response to positional change and venous pressure increase.

When intra-abdominal pressure was subsequently elevated to 14 mmHg in the same position (T6), IOP reached its peak values (mean = 18.9 ± 2.2 mmHg in the right eye and 20.2 ± 1.6 mmHg in the left eye; both p < 0.001 vs. T1).

The progressive increase from T4 to T6 indicates a cumulative effect of pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg tilt on intraocular pressure.

Following desufflation and return to the supine position (T7–T8), IOP rapidly declined toward baseline, confirming that these elevations were transient and reversible.

In 5 of 25 patients (20%), left-eye IOP briefly exceeded 21 mmHg at T6 (four patients 22 mmHg, one patient 24 mmHg); all normalized within minutes and were not associated with postoperative ocular symptoms.

Post-hoc power analysis for the repeated-measures ANOVA (Time effect, 8 levels) confirmed that the sample provided excellent sensitivity to detect within-subject IOP changes. The Time effect was large for both eyes (right: F(7,161) = 51.10, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.690; left: F(7,161) = 72.06, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.758), corresponding to Cohen’s f values of 1.49 and 1.77, respectively (observed power ≈ 1.00 at α = 0.05).

Right–left eye comparison of IOP

Paired-samples t-tests (Table 1) showed slightly but consistently higher IOP in the left eye during several intraoperative stages.

A small interocular difference first appeared at T4 (supine, after pneumoperitoneum; Δ (L–R) = 0.72 mmHg, p < 0.001), preceding the Trendelenburg position. Although statistically significant, this early asymmetry was minor and likely reflected transient hemodynamic variation associated with pneumoperitoneum.

The difference became more pronounced during Trendelenburg positioning, reaching its maximum at T5–T6 (Δ = 1.32 mmHg, both p < 0.001) and partially resolved toward the end of surgery.

To account for repeated measures and inter-eye correlation, a linear mixed-effects model was applied with fixed effects of time (T1–T8), eye (left/right), and their interaction (time × eye), and a random intercept for subject. The model confirmed a strong overall effect of surgical stage (p < 0.001), indicating dynamic intraoperative changes in IOP. The main effect of eye side was not significant (p = 0.73), but a trend-level time × eye interaction (β = −1.16 ± 0.66, p = 0.079) suggested that the left–right difference was stage-dependent, becoming most evident during Trendelenburg (T5–T6).

Under these conditions—with slight leftward tilt, elevated intra-abdominal pressure, and venous congestion—the left eye consistently exhibited slightly higher IOP than the right. This asymmetry resolved after desufflation and repositioning, confirming that it was transient and posture-related rather than a baseline characteristic.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) also showed a significant time-dependent variation (p < 0.001, Table 2).

MAP decreased slightly after anesthesia induction (T2), remained relatively stable during pneumoperitoneum (T3–T4), and then exhibited a further transient decline during Trendelenburg positioning (T5–T6)—the same stages where OPP reached its minimum.

This reduction in MAP, combined with elevated IOP, contributed to the transient OPP decrease observed at these points.

Following desufflation and extubation (T7–T8), MAP increased toward or slightly above baseline levels, paralleling the recovery of OPP.

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrated that intraocular pressure (IOP) significantly increases in pediatric patients undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy in the Trendelenburg position, particularly at higher intra-abdominal pressures. Left-eye IOP values were consistently higher than right-eye values during Trendelenburg, likely due to the slight leftward tilt used to optimize surgical access. This asymmetry was confirmed by paired analyses and supported by a mixed-effects model, indicating a physiological effect of gravitational orientation. Trendelenburg positioning was also associated with transient reductions in ocular perfusion pressure (OPP), both parameters normalizing rapidly after desufflation and extubation. These findings may be clinically relevant particularly for children with pre-existing optic nerve or vascular vulnerability.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to evaluate IOP and OPP at multiple intraoperative stages during pediatric laparoscopic appendectomy. The standardized anesthesia, repeated bilateral measurements, and clearly defined timepoints allowed a detailed characterization of intraoperative ocular hemodynamics in a pediatric cohort.

Adult studies have extensively documented IOP elevations during laparoscopic or robotic procedures. A 2019 meta-analysis by Van Wicklin et al. [6] including 18 studies (n = 762) reported an average intraoperative IOP rise of 13.6 mmHg, with peak values up to 28 mmHg, though no pediatric cases were included. Our data show similar trends but with lower absolute values (peaking at 20–21 mmHg), suggesting partial age-related protection against severe pressure elevations. This milder response likely reflects the shorter Trendelenburg duration, lower intra-abdominal pressures, and the consistent performance of all procedures by an experienced pediatric surgeon under standardized conditions, minimizing venous congestion and physiologic stress.

Age-related anatomic and vascular factors may also contribute. Children exhibit lower scleral rigidity, greater ocular compliance, and an immature vascular autoregulation system. Experimental and Doppler studies have shown that retinal and choroidal vascular tone and myogenic responsiveness are not fully mature in childhood [13, 16], and even modest IOP changes may transiently influence optic nerve head blood flow when autoregulation is challenged [14]. Although these fluctuations are typically well tolerated in healthy children, theoretical susceptibility persists under combined influences of anesthesia, pneumoperitoneum, and positional changes.

Our results are consistent with previous adult studies by Awad et al. [5] and Joo et al. [9], which showed significant IOP increases and OPP decreases during robotic and gynecologic laparoscopies. Comparable trends were observed in pediatric cohorts by Murabito et al. [8] and Astuto et al. [7], who also noted IOP elevations during Trendelenburg but without assessing OPP. Our study extends these observations by quantifying bilateral IOP and OPP changes across eight standardized intervals and identifying a consistent lateralized increase, likely attributable to surgical tilt. The occurrence of transient IOP elevations above 21 mmHg in 20% of patients—though clinically asymptomatic—supports the need for awareness in children with pre-existing ocular or vascular compromise. All IOP values in our cohort remained well below the 30–35 mmHg range reported to compromise optic nerve perfusion [13], supporting the absence of any clinically relevant ischemic risk.

These findings suggest that transient intraoperative IOP surges may temporarily reduce ocular perfusion reserve. While adult reports describe rare cases of postoperative visual loss and ischemic optic neuropathy after prolonged steep Trendelenburg exposure [3, 15], such severe outcomes are unlikely in short pediatric procedures. Nevertheless, minimizing the steepness and duration of Trendelenburg, and intermittently adjusting pneumoperitoneum when feasible, may further reduce ocular risk in vulnerable patients.

Our OPP results highlight the balance between systemic blood pressure and ocular perfusion. The lowest OPP values occurred during Trendelenburg at 14 mmHg insufflation (T6), reflecting transient reductions in ocular blood flow that may become relevant in patients with glaucoma or vascular dysregulation [12, 14]. Although short-lived OPP declines are unlikely to cause damage in healthy eyes, prolonged reductions can impair optic nerve perfusion, particularly when combined with anesthetic-induced hypotension. Therefore, perioperative awareness of ocular hemodynamics may be warranted in selected high-risk children.

Although this study focused on intraoperative physiological changes rather than postoperative outcomes, the clinical relevance of these transient alterations remains to be clarified. Trendelenburg positioning during pediatric laparoscopy leads to transient but statistically significant changes in IOP and OPP; however, the clinical effects have not been clearly demonstrated in this study, as functional or structural assessments were beyond its scope. In future studies, the use of emerging portable or bedside OCT systems may enable pre- and postoperative evaluation of optic nerve and retinal structures, providing better understanding of whether prolonged or repeated exposure to elevated IOP and reduced OPP could have measurable long-term consequences, particularly in longer or more complex procedures.

From a practical standpoint, several factors likely contributed to the modest and transient IOP elevations observed in this study: the brief Trendelenburg phase (approximately 8–10 min), uniform anesthetic protocol, and the surgeon’s experience. Longer operative durations or complex cases could extend exposure time, leading to higher or more sustained IOP elevations. Prior studies have shown that both the magnitude and duration of elevated IOP determine the risk of optic nerve hypoperfusion [2, 12, 14]. Thus, although all changes observed in our study were physiologically tolerable, careful intraoperative management of position and intra-abdominal pressure remains advisable, especially during prolonged or technically demanding surgeries.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths that enhance the validity of its findings. The key strengths of this study include its prospective design, standardized anesthetic and surgical protocol, use of a validated measurement device (Tono-Pen Avia®), and repeated bilateral assessments performed by a single ophthalmologist. These features ensured methodological consistency and minimized observer variability.

The study was conducted at a single center and included only ASA I children undergoing emergency appendectomy, which limits generalizability to higher-risk populations. The modest sample size (n = 25) may have limited the detection of subtle time × eye interactions. Nevertheless, the use of repeated-measures ANOVA and mixed-effects modeling ensured robust within-subject analysis and provided sufficient statistical power (> 0.99) to detect time-dependent changes in intraocular pressure across surgical stages.

Central corneal thickness, optical biometry, and axial length measurements were not performed because of the emergency setting, in which cooperation for instrument-based testing was not feasible. However, preoperative history confirmed the absence of any known ocular disease or refractive error exceeding ± 2.0 D, minimizing the likelihood of structural bias. Hydrostatic height correction for OPP was not applied, as all measurements were performed in the supine or slight Trendelenburg position, where vertical reference differences are physiologically negligible and already reflected in systemic pressure changes.

Another limitation is the lack of long-term postoperative follow-up and the absence of objective structural or functional assessments such as OCT or perimetry, which would help evaluate subtle optic nerve changes. Future studies using portable or bedside OCT systems may allow ethically feasible pre- and postoperative assessment of ocular structures in pediatric surgical settings. Lastly, our cohort did not include children with ocular comorbidities such as congenital glaucoma, limiting direct evaluation of risk in vulnerable subgroups.

Conclusion

Trendelenburg positioning during pediatric laparoscopic appendectomy causes transient but statistically significant elevations in intraocular pressure (IOP) and concurrent reductions in ocular perfusion pressure (OPP), which rapidly normalize after desufflation. These transient changes appear physiologically tolerable in healthy children but may warrant attention in those with optic nerve or vascular vulnerability. The left–right asymmetry observed underlines the potential influence of surgical tilt. Awareness of intraoperative ocular dynamics and cautious control of both Trendelenburg duration and insufflation pressure are advisable. Future multicenter studies with larger samples and structural follow-up will be essential to establish safe intraoperative thresholds and clarify the long-term significance of these transient ocular changes.
