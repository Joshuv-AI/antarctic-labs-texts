Optical coherence tomography assessment of rapidly progressive pulmonary vein stenosis in fibrosing mediastinitis: a case report
Authors: Haijuan Wang, Zhaofen Wang, Xiuli Li, Peng Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFibrosing mediastinitis is a rare disease caused by benign proliferation of fibrous tissue within the mediastinum encircling the mediastinal organs, leading to compression of the surrounding bronchial and blood vessels. We report a case of fibrosing mediastinitis with rapidly progressive pulmonary vein stenosis evaluated using multimodality imaging. Computed tomographic pulmonary angiography demonstrated multiple pulmonary vein stenoses, while intravascular optical coherence tomography provided high-resolution characterization of the lesions, showing smooth vessel walls without evidence of thrombosis or significant intimal hyperplasia, suggesting an extrinsic compressive mechanism. These imaging findings complemented conventional angiography and influenced interventional strategy. This case highlights the incremental value of optical coherence tomography in assessing pulmonary vein stenosis associated with fibrosing mediastinitis.Keywords: Optical coherence tomography, Compression of pulmonary veins, Fibrosing mediastinitis, Pulmonary hypertension, Pulmonary vein intervention

Introduction

Fibrosing mediastinitis (FM), also known as sclerosing mediastinitis or mediastinal fibrosis, is a benign but often progressive and potentially lethal disorder [1]. The proliferating fibrous tissue encases, infiltrates, and compresses adjacent mediastinal structures, resulting in narrowing of the large mediastinal blood vessels such as the pulmonary vasculature, superior vena cava, and bronchial tubes, as well as other structures in the mediastinum such as the oesophagus [2]. FM causing stenosis of the pulmonary vessels within the mediastinum can lead to pulmonary hypertension (PH) and right heart failure, which are the main causes of death in patients with FM. In this article, we report a case of pulmonary vein stenosis due to FM, resulting in PH and bilateral pleural effusions. Although computed tomographic pulmonary angiography is commonly used to evaluate pulmonary vein stenosis, its ability to characterize lesion morphology is limited.

The application of intravascular optical coherence tomography (OCT) for detailed assessment of pulmonary vein involvement in fibrosing mediastinitis has rarely been reported. OCT, an imaging technique based on near-infrared light, has gained popularity because of its superior spatial resolution (10–20 μm), which is about 10-fold higher than that of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) (≈150 μm) [3]. OCT can provide more informative and clinically valuable images [4]. Accordingly, we present a case highlighting the role of OCT in evaluating rapidly progressive PV stenosis.

Case report

Initial presentation

A 66-year-old male patient has presented with a 3-year history of progressively worsening shortness of breath and a 1-month history of cough. He had a history of working in a coal mine for 8 years. One month ago, he was diagnosed with bilateral pleural effusions at another hospital. Following thoracentesis drainage, his shortness of breath was not relieved. The physical examination in our institution revealed dullness on percussion in both lungs and decreased breath sounds in both lungs. His complete blood count, C-reactive protein, routine analysis of pleural and abdominal fluids, and urinalysis results were within normal limits, and tuberculosis smear examination, bacterial and fungal cultures were negative, whereas immunoglobulin E was 463.72 IU/mL, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 20 mm/h.

Echocardiography revealed elevated pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) of 55 mmHg, tricuspid regurgitation, and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. Computed tomographic pulmonary angiography showed severe stenoses of the pulmonary veins in the left and right superior pulmonary veins and the left inferior pulmonary vein (Fig. 1), clearly showing the anatomical locations and severity of the lesions, thereby guiding the identification of pulmonary veins selected for treatment. multiple fibrotic nodules and calcified foci were also observed in both lungs, along with multiple enlarged hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes bilaterally, which were consistent with FM. Echography detected left pleural effusion. Combined with the patient’s history and relevant investigations, these findings led to the clinical diagnosis of chronic FM with pulmonary vein stenosis, resulting in PH and pleural effusion.

First intervention

The patient underwent pulmonary venography, in which there was no significant stenosis in the pulmonary arteries, with a PAP of 45 mmHg, while multiple pulmonary veins showed moderate-to-severe stenosis (Table 1). Intravascular OCT was performed in selected pulmonary veins to further characterize lesion morphology. OCT demonstrated preserved luminal contours with smooth and continuous vessel walls, without evidence of intraluminal thrombus or marked intimal hyperplasia (Fig. 2). The stenotic segments showed luminal narrowing consistent with external compression rather than intrinsic vascular pathology. These findings provided complementary information beyond angiography and supported lesion-specific interventional planning. Subsequently, interventional therapy was performed on the pulmonary veins. The superior segment of the left inferior pulmonary vein was treated with pharmacological balloon dilatation, and 3.5 × 22 mm and 6.0 × 24 mm bare metal stents (BMS) were placed in the superior segment of the right inferior pulmonary vein and in the right superior pulmonary vein, respectively. OCT was performed in the stented superior segment of the right inferior pulmonary vein, demonstrating good apposition of the stent to the vessel wall (Fig. 3). The images (Fig. 4) demonstrated significant changes in the vessels before and after treatment and provided a basis for identifying the pulmonary veins selected for intervention and determining the appropriate stent sizes, thereby guiding procedural planning and execution. On repeat echocardiography, PAP decreased to 27 mmHg. Additionally, the patient’s shortness of breath was significantly relieved. He was discharged from hospital and treated with aspirin (100 mg once daily) and rivaroxaban (20 mg once daily).

Clinical deterioration within 1 month

After 1 month, he was admitted for a second intervention due to worsening shortness of breath. C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 26.12 mg/L. Echocardiographic showed that the PAP was 52 mmHg and the right atrium was enlarged to 41 mm. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a small amount of pleural effusion on the left side. However, after 10 days in the hospital, a marked increase in bilateral pleural effusions was observed on chest X-ray.

Second intervention

A second intervention was performed. The PAP was 50 mmHg. Left pulmonary venography revealed significant progression of pulmonary vein stenosis (Table 1). Notably, the anterior segment and lingular segment of the left superior pulmonary vein, which previously showed no significant stenosis, had progressed to approximately 90% severe stenosis within one month. In addition, the left inferior pulmonary vein was completely occluded. After pre-dilating the apicoposterior, anterior segments, as well as the lingular segment of the left superior pulmonary vein, a 6.0 × 18 mm BMS was deployed in the apicoposterior segment, a 7.0 × 15 mm BMS in the anterior segment, and a 6.0 × 15 mm BMS in the lingular segment (Fig. 5). Figure 5 demonstrates findings similar to those shown in Fig. 4. Meanwhile, approximately 800 ml of yellowish pleural fluid was also drained. The echocardiogram was repeated 5 days after the operation and PAP was 44 mmHg. The patient’s shortness of breath was significantly relieved again. The same medication regimen was continued after the second discharge.

Discussion

FM is a rare group of benign proliferative diseases of fibrous tissue in the mediastinum. In Western countries, FM is most commonly a complication of granulomatous disease caused by histoplasmosis [5]. The mediastinal structures most commonly involved are the pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, superior vena cava, trachea and bronchus [6]. The most common clinical manifestations in patients with FM are dyspnoea, cough and haemoptysis [2, 7], which often depend on the structures of mediastinum that are affected [8].

Dense fibrotic tissue within the mediastinum can infiltrate adjacent vessels and adhere to the vessel wall, leading to increased vascular stiffness and a heightened risk of rupture [9]. Furthermore, the presence of dense, calcified hyperplastic fibrotic tissue within the mediastinum may increase the risk of vascular injury, stent migration, stent underexpansion, and procedural failure during percutaneous pulmonary angioplasty. In this case, OCT was performed to assess the selected pulmonary veins, showing preserved luminal contours with smooth and continuous vessel walls, without evidence of intraluminal thrombus or marked intimal hyperplasia. OCT provided important information for optimizing for pulmonary veins intervention, as follows: (i) it can accurately measure the cross-sectional area of the pulmonary veins; (ii) it can identify intimal dissection or rupture and comprehensively assess the vascular lumen and vessel wall using high-resolution imaging, including intraluminal thrombus, luminal surface regularity, and intimal hyperplasia; (iii) it is possible to observe whether the stent adheres to the vessel wall after placement (which is closely related to thrombus formation within the stent); (iv) it can help anticipate potential risks during interventional procedures; (v) it can distinguish venous stenosis due to intimal hyperplasia from that due to extrinsic compression, providing a reliable basis for differentiating pulmonary vein stenosis caused by FM from other chronic obstructive pulmonary vascular diseases. Recently, studies have highlighted the pivotal role of OCT-guided stent optimization in significantly improving clinical outcomes in patients undergoing PCI for complex coronary lesions [10]. In summary, preoperative OCT enhances procedural safety and operator confidence, and helps reduce the risk of intraoperative complications.

Pulmonary vein stenosis is typically attributed to intimal hyperplasia or extrinsic compression. In the present case, OCT demonstrated a smooth vessel wall without evidence of significant intimal proliferation, suggesting that the underlying mechanism is more likely related to extrinsic compression, although the exact etiology remains to be further elucidated. Rapid progression of pulmonary vein stenosis may be driven by elevated systemic and local inflammatory activity, as supported by increased inflammatory markers upon readmission. Inflammatory mediators can promote fibroblast proliferation, thereby exacerbating mediastinal fibrosis and leading to compression of the pulmonary vasculature [11].

Pulmonary vein intervention has proven to be an effective therapeutic option for pulmonary stenosis caused by fibrosing mediastinitis and may lead to significant improvement in clinical symptoms. However, this strategy is associated with certain limitations, including the risk of restenosis and vascular remodeling, which may complicate future interventions [12]. There is still a lack of robust evidence supporting long-term anticoagulation therapy after pulmonary vein stenting. Therefore, long-term follow-up is warranted to monitor for disease progression or recurrence.

Conclusion

In summary, multimodality imaging is essential for evaluating pulmonary vein intervention in FM. OCT may provide incremental value over conventional imaging modalities by enabling detailed characterization of lesion morphology and underlying mechanisms. This approach may facilitate appropriate treatment planning.
