Gross and microscopic anatomical study of the esophagus in Gasgie ecotype chickens in the Alefa District, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Kefale Ambachew Shiferaw, Mebrie Zemene Kinde, Bemrew Admassu Mengistu, Abebe Tesfaye Gessese, Yitayew Demessie, Zerihun Getie Wassie, Genetu Kassahun Berie, Merawi Kindu Birhanie, Dejen Takele Shiferaw, Zenebe Jemere Aragaw, Amsalu Chanie Mersha, Addisu Workie Miliket, Mohammed Yesuf, Firdyawukal Abuhay Tafere, Asnakew Mulaw Berihun, Mastewal Birhan, Elias Melkamu Tsehay, Abraham Belete Temesgen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Gasgie chicken is an important indigenous ecotype of Ethiopia that has adapted to diverse agroecological conditions and contributes substantially to the livelihoods of rural communities. However, despite its significance, comprehensive information on its anatomical features remains limited, particularly regarding the esophagus, which plays a vital role in feeding adaptation and digestion. This study aimed to investigate the gross and microscopic anatomy of the esophagus in Gasgie ecotype chickens from the Alefa District, Northwest Ethiopia. An exploratory experimental study was conducted at the University of Gondar from February to September 2025. Ten healthy chickens were purposively selected from the Alefa market and acclimatized for two weeks. They were humanely euthanized using 100% carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation, and the cervical, crop, and thoracic esophagus were examined grossly, microscopically, and morphometrically. Gross characterization revealed that the esophagus is a long muscular tube divided into cervical, crop, and thoracic regions, showing age- and sex-related differences in regional prominence, especially in one-year-old males. Gross morphometry demonstrated significantly greater esophageal dimensions in older and male chickens compared with younger and female counterparts (P < 0.05). Microscopic characterization showed that the esophageal wall consisted of tunica mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and adventitia/serosa, lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with mucus-secreting glands, and showed more complex mucosal folds in older chickens. Histomorphometric analysis revealed significant age- and sex-related differences in Gasgie chicken esophagus, with one-year-old and male chickens exhibiting consistently thicker epithelial and connective tissue layers across all regions (P < 0.05). This study provides baseline data for anatomical, physiological, and pathological reference. Further histochemical and ultrastructural studies are recommended, along with comparative analyses among ecotypes, and the findings should be integrated into veterinary anatomy curricula in Ethiopian universities.

Introduction

Chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus) are the most widely raised poultry species worldwide, managed under both commercial and backyard production systems1. In Ethiopia, poultry production is predominantly based on indigenous chickens, which constitute approximately 88% of the national flock, while hybrid and exotic breeds account for only 6.25% and 5.25%, respectively2,3. Indigenous chickens are mainly reared under traditional scavenging systems and are distributed across diverse agro-ecological zones4. Through long-term adaptation, these local ecotypes have developed unique genetic traits that confer resilience to environmental stressors and manifest in distinctive morphological and physiological characteristics that differentiate them from commercial breeds5,6.

Among the indigenous ecotypes, the Gasgie chicken holds particular importance. These chickens are typically housed indoors during the night and allowed to scavenge freely during the day, with their diet supplemented by grains and household leftovers7,8. Morphologically, Gasgie chickens are most commonly characterized by red body plumage, although variations such as white, red-brownish, white with red tips, black with white tips, and tri-colored (white-black-red) feathers are also observed. Approximately 60.7% of the population exhibit rose combs. Distinctive features of this ecotype include relatively long necks in males, short weaning periods, docile temperament, and high productivity and reproductive efficiency, which together render the Gasgie chicken well-adapted to local farming conditions9.

Poultry species are generally capable of adapting to a wide range of environmental conditions, and diet and food availability are major determinants of this adaptability10,11. The digestive system, particularly the esophagus, plays a central role by enabling the intake, moistening, temporary storage, and regulated passage of ingesta to the stomach12. Morphological and histological variations of the esophagus among poultry species and breeds reflect influences of age, sex, diet, genetic background, and environmental conditions13.

The esophagus of the chicken is a flexible muscular tube extending from the pharynx to the proventriculus. It lacks upper and lower sphincters and is divided into three regions: cervical, crop, and thoracic14. The cervical segment, measuring approximately 11.5 cm, contains longitudinal mucosal folds that facilitate food passage. The crop, about 3 cm long, serves as a temporary food reservoir, while the thoracic segment, approximately 6 cm in length, is characterized by straight mucosal folds and a whitish epithelial lining15.

Histologically, the esophageal wall is composed of four distinct layers: tunica mucosa, tunica submucosa, tunica muscularis externa, and either adventitia or serosa, depending on the region. The mucosa is lined with stratified squamous epithelium, which varies in thickness and keratinization in response to functional and environmental factors. Mucus-secreting glands within the lamina propria and submucosa provide lubrication and protection of the lining10. Differences in mucin composition and glandular distribution are also important determinants of feed efficiency and digestive health16.

Comparative studies from Egypt17, Turkey18, and Iraq19 have demonstrated significant morphological and histological differences in the esophagus of chickens, associated with age, diet, breed, sex, and genetics. These findings highlight the value of anatomical investigations for improving poultry health, nutrition, and productivity through targeted management and breeding strategies20–22. Despite this, gross and microscopic anatomical data on Ethiopian indigenous ecotypes, including the Gasgie chicken, remain scarce. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to characterize the gross and microscopic anatomy of the esophagus in the Gasgie ecotype chicken and to quantify and compare the morphometric parameters of the cervical, crop, and thoracic regions across different age and sex groups.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted from February to September 2025 in the Alefa District, located in the Amhara Regional State of Ethiopia (Fig. 1). Geographically, the district is bordered to the southwest by the Agew Awi Zone, to the west by Quara, to the north by Takusa, to the east by Lake Tana, and to the southeast by the West Gojjam Zone. Alefa is situated approximately 144 km from Gondar town, with its administrative center at Shaura. The district lies between 11°45′ and 12°30′ N latitude and 36°30′ and 37°10′ E longitude, at an elevation ranging from 750 to 2250 m above sea level23. The Gasgie chicken, the dominant poultry ecotype in this district, is primarily found in the midland and lowland agro-ecological zones and is commonly managed under a traditional scavenging production system9.

Study animals

A total of ten apparently healthy Alefa Gasgie chickens, representing both sexes and two age groups, were purposively purchased from local farmers at the Alefa market. The chickens were categorized into two age groups: six months and one year, with five individuals in each group. The body weight ranged from 1.3 to 1.5 kg in the six-month-old group and 1.7–2.0 kg in the one-year-old group. Inclusion criteria were based on apparent health, characterized by normal plumage, smooth and intact comb, wattle, and shank, healthy mucous membranes, and normal behavior within the specified age and weight ranges. Chickens showing any clinical signs of illness or external deformities were excluded. After acquisition, the chickens were transported to the University of Gondar Veterinary Farm and housed under appropriate environmental conditions. They were acclimatized for two weeks, during which they were fed sorghum and maize, provided with clean drinking water, and observed daily for health status using routine physical examination tools, including a stethoscope and thermometer. The exploratory experimental study commenced after all chickens were confirmed to be clinically healthy and suitable for data collection.

Study design and period

An exploratory experimental study was conducted at the University of Gondar from February to September 2025. This design was selected to allow detailed investigation of the gross and microscopic anatomy of the cervical, crop, and thoracic regions of the esophagus in Gasgie ecotype chickens due to limited prior research.

Sample size and technique

A total of 10 apparently healthy Gasgie chickens were purposively selected, taking into account availability, health status, sex, and age group. The sample size was determined based on previous international studies, as limited research has been conducted in Ethiopia. Comparable investigations have reported similar sample sizes, including six chickens24, five chickens25, twelve chickens26, twenty pigeons27, and six turkeys28.

Sample collection and processing

Gross anatomical analysis

Each Gasgie ecotype chicken underwent a thorough physical examination using a stethoscope and thermometer at the University of Gondar Veterinary Farm to confirm overall health. Body weight (kg) was recorded prior to sampling using a portable hand balance (YUYUSO, China). Humane euthanasia was performed following Underwood and Anthony29 guidelines, using 100% carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation in a sealed chamber. Following euthanasia, the esophagus was carefully dissected and separated into cervical, crop, and thoracic regions for detailed gross anatomical assessment. Photographs were captured using a Leica digital camera, and morphometric parameters, including length (mm), circumference (mm), diameter (mm), and weight (g), were measured immediately using a calibrated digital vernier caliper (Dasqua, China) and an electronic precision balance (Citizen, Japan). The mucosal surface and internal folds were further examined using a hand lens to document regional variations.

Microscopic anatomy and histometry

A total of ten normal esophageal tissue samples, each comprising the cervical, crop, and thoracic regions and measuring approximately 3 cm in length, were collected, flushed with normal saline, and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. The tissues were dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethanol alcohol, cleared in xylene and then impregnated and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany), stained with Harris hematoxylin and eosin Y (H&E) (Cell Path, UK), mounted with dibutyl phthalate xylene (DPX), and cover-slipped The prepared slides were thoroughly dried and examined under a light microscope at objective magnifications ranging from 10× to 40×, following the procedures described by Malede et al.30 and Temesgen et al.31. The histological architecture and tissue organization of the esophagus were assessed, and photomicrographs were obtained using a Leica microscope equipped with a digital camera system. Quantitative histological measurements of the esophageal layers were performed using ImageJ software.

Data management and analysis

The data were organized using Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into Stata version 18 for descriptive statistical analysis of the esophageal parameters in Gasgie chickens. Comparisons between sexes and age groups were conducted using independent sample t-tests, with statistical significance established at p < 0.05. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Additionally, images obtained from gross anatomical and histological examinations were incorporated to support and complement the descriptive statistical findings.

Results

Gross anatomical findings

Gross characterization

The esophagus of the Gasgie chicken was identified as a long, muscular tube extending from the pharynx to the proventriculus and anatomically divided into cervical, crop, and thoracic regions. The cervical portion extended from the pharyngo-esophageal junction to the thoracic inlet, where it expanded to form the crop. Initially, the cervical esophagus was positioned along the midline dorsal to the larynx and trachea but became laterally displaced in the middle cervical region, lying on the right ventro-lateral aspect (Fig. 2). The crop originated from the cervical esophagus at the thoracic inlet and exhibited a gentle spindle shape with a slight bulge. It was located subcutaneously, anterior to the breast muscles. The thoracic esophagus was located within the thoraco-abdominal cavity, dorsal to the trachea and syrinx, and cranial to the proventriculus. Fine longitudinal mucosal folds were consistently observed in the cervical and thoracic regions, while the crop showed irregularly arranged folds (Fig. 3).

Gross morphometry

Gross morphometric analysis revealed that the esophageal dimensions of Gasgie chicken were significantly influenced by both age and sex. Across all esophageal regions, one-year-old chickens exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) larger and heavier esophageal structures than six-month-old chickens. Notably, the cervical, crop, and thoracic portions showed marked age-related increases in all measured parameters, including length, width, circumference, and weight (Table 1).

Sex also had a distinct effect on the gross morphology of the esophagus. Males generally possessed significantly (P < 0.05) larger and heavier esophageal structures compared with females. In all regions, including the cervical, crop, and thoracic segments, males exhibited greater length, width, circumference, and weight than their female counterparts (Table 2).

Microscopic anatomy and histometry findings

Microscopic characterization

The walls of the cervical, crop, and thoracic esophagus in six-month-old and one-year-old male and female Gasgie chicken were composed of four tunics: tunica mucosa, tunica submucosa, tunica muscularis, and tunica adventitia in the cervical region, and tunica serosa in the crop and thoracic regions, respectively. In the cervical esophagus, the mucosal folds were lined with non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Beneath the epithelium lay the lamina propria, followed by a muscularis mucosa, submucosa, and muscularis, with glands present in the lamina propria and submucosa. In six-month-old chicken, the muscularis mucosa was poorly developed or absent, whereas in one-year-old chicken, longitudinal smooth muscle bundles were observed within the folds, together with thicker lamina propria and more developed glands (Fig. 4).

In the central region of the crop, the mucosal folds were lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. The underlying layers consisted of lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, submucosa, and muscularis. In six-month-old chicken, the mucosal folds appeared relatively simple and primary, while in one-year-old chicken they developed into broader, leaf- or club-shaped folds with thicker supportive layers (Fig. 5). In the lateral diverticulum of the crop, the mucosal folds were also lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, supported by lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, submucosa, and muscularis. In six-month-old chicken, the folds were less complex and uniform, whereas in one-year-old chicken they were larger, more irregular, and structurally reinforced (Fig. 6).

In the thoracic esophagus, the mucosal folds were lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Beneath this epithelium lay the lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, submucosa, and muscularis, with the outermost layer being the adventitia or serosa, depending on the region. The adventitia consisted of loose connective tissue containing blood vessels and nerves, serving to anchor the esophagus to adjacent structures within the thoracic cavity. Glands were present within the lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, and submucosa. In six-month-old chicken, the folds and glands were less developed, whereas in one-year-old chicken, the mucosal folds were more prominent, the lamina propria denser, the muscularis mucosa and muscularis more developed, and the glands larger and more abundant (Fig. 7).

Microscopic morphometry

Histomorphometric analysis demonstrated that the thickness of the esophageal layers in Gasgie chicken was significantly influenced by both age and sex. Across all esophageal regions, one-year-old chickens exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) thicker epithelial and muscular layers compared with six-month-old chickens. The cervical, crop, and thoracic portions showed consistent age-related increases in the thickness of the epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, tunica submucosa, and tunica muscularis (Table 3).

Sex also exerted a significant (P < 0.05) influence on the histomorphometric characteristics of the esophagus. Males generally exhibited thicker epithelial and connective tissue layers than females across all regions. In the cervical, crop, and thoracic segments, the thickness of the epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, tunica submucosa, and tunica muscularis was consistently greater in males than in females (Table 4).

Discussion

The chicken esophagus is a muscular tube connecting the pharynx to the proventriculus and plays a key role in feed transport, temporary storage, and initial digestive processing. In Gasgie chicken, the esophagus exhibited a tripartite organization consisting of cervical, crop, and thoracic regions, consistent with observations in Columba livia, quails, house sparrows, ducks, and cattle egrets27,32–36. Some species, such as homing pigeons and partridges, possess only cervical and thoracic regions37.

The cervical esophagus extended from the pharyngoesophageal junction to the thoracic inlet, where it formed the crop. Its midline course deviated slightly to the right ventrolateral side in the mid-cervical region, particularly in one-year-old males and females, likely to accommodate the crop and facilitate smooth passage of ingesta. Such lateral deviation has also been reported in shrikes, pigeons, partridges, quails, and moorhens24,32,37–40. Prominent longitudinal mucosal folds in the cervical region, similar to those in pigeons, quails, ducks, and cattle egrets32,34. The cervical esophagus was longer than the thoracic portion, supporting the idea that cervical length correlates with ingesta transport efficiency36,41,42.

The crop originated from the cervical esophagus at the thoracic inlet and exhibited a gentle spindle-shaped form with pronounced curvature and occasional bulge. Its subcutaneous position allows easy probing and surgical access, in agreement with El-Naseery et al.35. Morphologically, the spindle-shaped crop of Gasgie chicken is similar to that of ducks and geese34, whereas pigeons and doves possess bilobed crops43–54, and some species, including Garganeys, moorhens, shrikes, owls, gulls, and penguins, lack a crop entirely24,40,41. Functionally, the crop serves as a temporary storage site, facilitating grain softening, initial enzymatic digestion, and limited nutrient absorption46–49. Rapid increases in crop weight and volume during the first postnatal week highlight its importance in early digestive development and growth, mirroring findings in other granivorous birds55,56.

The thoracic esophagus extended caudally, dorsally to the trachea and syrinx, and cranially to the proventriculus and gizzard. Morphological variations in mucosal folds, such as branched, wave-like, finger-like, and leaf-like forms, align with observations in pigeons, rock pigeons, cattle egrets, and ducks36. Age-related thickening of muscular layers in the thoracic esophagus likely enhances peristaltic efficiency, reflecting functional maturation similar to that reported in other species44.

Gross morphometric analysis revealed significant age- and sex-related differences in all esophageal regions, with one-year-old chickens and males showing larger dimensions than six-month-old chickens and females33,57,58. Enlargement of the cervical esophagus may improve ingesta transport from the pharynx to the crop. Increased crop size provides greater storage capacity for ingesta, supporting more efficient feeding and digestion. The thoracic esophagus of older and male chickens may facilitate more efficient transport of ingesta toward the proventriculus and gizzard. Comparable age- and sex-related differences have been reported in quails, house sparrows, ducks, partridges, and pigeons32,34–36,59–61. These morphometric variations may also be influenced by dietary and genetic factors, as diet-induced changes in gut morphology and microbial colonization can interact with age- and sex-related patterns, while quantitative trait loci associated with digestive efficiency and gastrointestinal anatomy have been identified62–64.

Histologically, the walls of the cervical, crop, and thoracic esophagus were composed of four distinct tunics: mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and adventitia in the cervical region, with serosa present in the crop and thoracic regions35,51,52. Mucosal folds were lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, providing protection against mechanical stress during feed passage. The lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, and esophageal glands were consistently observed, exhibiting regional specialization across the three segments34,39.

In the cervical esophagus, muscularis mucosa was poorly developed in six-month-old chickens but developed into longitudinal smooth muscle bundles by one year of age, indicating structural maturation52. The crop showed similar age-dependent changes: younger chickens had primarily simple folds, whereas older chickens developed leaf- or club-shaped folds36. The lateral diverticulum of the crop was lined with non-keratinized epithelium and contained lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, and muscularis, reflecting regional diversity in fold morphology51.

In the thoracic esophagus, older chickens exhibited prominent mucosal folds, denser lamina propria, well-developed muscularis mucosa, a serosal covering, and more abundant glandular structures, reflecting functional maturation with age34,39. Similar adaptations have been reported in other avian species, where esophageal morphology aligns closely with dietary habits and feeding behavior35,36. In granivorous birds such as pigeons and homing pigeons, abundant mucous glands coat ingested grains to facilitate swallowing and protect the epithelium37,39. In Gasgie chicken, mucous glands in the thoracic esophagus likely serve a similar lubricating role34,51.

Histomorphometric analysis confirmed that age and sex significantly influenced esophageal structure in Gasgie chicken. One-year-old chickens exhibited thicker epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosa, muscularis, and submucosa across all regions compared with six-month-old chickens. Males consistently showed greater thickness in these layers than females65. Age-related increases in epithelial thickness, denser lamina propria, and more developed muscular layers were observed across all esophageal regions, mirroring patterns in pigeons, where the crop showed the greatest histometric adaptation, the thoracic esophagus moderate changes, and the cervical portion the least27. Sex-related differences were also evident, with males exhibiting greater epithelial and muscular thickness than females, consistent with observations in common quail, where the muscularis is composed entirely of smooth muscle, epithelial thickness is greatest in the crop and cervical regions, the lamina propria is thinnest in the crop, and muscular layers are thickest in the thoracic esophagus66.

Conclusion

The study confirms that the esophagus of Gasgie chicken varied significantly in gross morphology, morphometry, microscopic organization, and histomorphometry across age and sex. Grossly, the esophagus was a long muscular tube divided into cervical, crop, and thoracic regions, with age- and sex-related variations in regional prominence. Gross morphometric analysis revealed significantly larger esophageal dimensions in one-year-old and male chickens compared with six-month-old and female counterparts (P < 0.05). Microscopically, the esophageal wall comprised the tunica mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and adventitia/serosa, lined with non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium containing mucus-secreting glands. Histomorphometric evaluation demonstrated that all layers were significantly thicker, and mucosal folds were more complex in older and male chickens across all regions (P < 0.05). This study provides valuable baseline data for anatomical, physiological, and pathological reference. Further histochemical and ultrastructural investigations are recommended to enhance the understanding of glandular secretions. Comparative analyses among different ecotypes should also be conducted, and the generated data are suggested to be incorporated into the veterinary anatomy curriculum of Ethiopian universities.
