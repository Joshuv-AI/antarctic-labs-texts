Determination of contraceptive knowledge, attitude, and behavior among nursing students in Northern Cyprus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jonathan Niciza, Kemal Elyeli, Ümran Dal Yilmaz, Dilay Necipoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aims to determine the levels of knowledge, attitude and behavior on contraceptive methods among nursing students.MethodsThe study was conducted as a descriptive and cross-sectional study. In the study, face-to-face data collection forms were completed by n = 297 nursing students included in the sample between January and June 2025. The data were collected using a data collection form consisting of four parts: (1) the student information form (2), the Contraceptive Knowledge Assessment (CKA) (3), the Contraceptive Attitude Scale (CAS), and (4) the Contraceptive Behavior Scale (CBS). Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0.ResultsParticipants in the study 72.4% was female. 34.7% of the participants reported being sexually active. A significant portion of the participants (86.2%) reported not using any contraceptive methods. In this study, nursing students had a mean score of 8.39 ± 4.93 (min-max: 0–22; median: 8.0) on the CKA measurement tool. The CAS mean score was 106.11 ± 16.86 (min-max: 62–156; median: 101.0) and the CBS mean score was 7.56 ± 6.92 (min-max: 0–20; median: 8.0). In addition, a positive correlation was found between age and the CKA, between the CKA and CAS and CBS, and between CAS and CBS (p < .05).ConclusionsFrom the study findings, we conclude that the respondents in the study had poor knowledge of contraceptives, poor contraceptive behaviour and positive attitude towards contraceptives use. A significant weak positive correlation between age and contraceptive knowledge was found among the respondents. The findings showed the need for strengthening contraceptive education and targeted interventions in the sampled nursing students.Implications for research, education and policyBased on the findings of the study, we recommend comprehensive education and training on contraception for the respondents using periodic evaluations and review sessions that are integrated to enhance learning retention. Researchers should design and test structured contraceptive education interventions using pre- and post-assessment designs to measure their impacts on knowledge, attitudes, and behaviour.

Introduction

According to the National Cancer Institute (NCI), contraception encompasses the use of pharmacological agents, devices, or surgical interventions to prevent pregnancy [1]. The importance of contraception is cited in its efficacy in reducing unintended pregnancy rates, mitigating the incidence of unsafe abortions, and limiting the transmission of infections [2].

The World Health Organization (WHO, 2021) reported that 164 million women of reproductive age lacked access to contraception.

It is estimated that 9% (1.9 billion) of women aged 15 to 49 all over the world had unmet contraceptive needs, and 270 million women lacked access to modern methods of birth control [3]. In the year 2022, an estimated 874 million women, representing 77.5% of reproductive-aged women worldwide, used modern contraceptives. This estimate represents a 10% increase from the year 1990, when the prevalence was reported as 67%. Despite the aforementioned, the WHO figures show that annually about 74 million women in low- and middle-income countries experienced unintended pregnancies [4]. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) have a significant effect on the sexual and reproductive health of women, and it is estimated more than a million new infections that can be treated are transmitted every day [5]. WHO, in the year 2020, reported that 374 million new infections that form four of the most common STIs were chlamydia (129 million), gonorrhoea (82 million), syphilis (7.1 million), and trichomoniasis (152 million) [6]. Worldwide, STIs present an enormous problem affecting society, economy and public health; the illnesses have affected humans for ages and persist across several geographical and cultural settings, with more than 38 million people all over the world affected by HIV/AIDS [7].

In addition, bacterial infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis remain problematic, with millions of new cases reported daily worldwide [8]. Elevated fertility rates contribute to maternal mortality and morbidity, posing health risks for both mothers and offspring [9]. Newborns may be born into families unprepared for their arrival or unable to provide adequate care [10]. Parenthood entails significant demands, including financial obligations, and can affect families, communities, and societies [11]. Limited access to education can restrict opportunities for young people growing up in families facing such challenges. Parents may experience increased emotional and psychological distress [12].

Nursing students in university are young individuals between the ages of 18 and 24; this is the period where many students explore sexuality through romantic and sexual relationships. This exploration predisposes them to various risky behaviours (unprotected sex, multiple sexual partners, frequent partner changes, unwanted pregnancies, and abortion attempts) [13] during educational pursuits, which can negatively impact their health, social well-being, and learning outcomes [14, 15]. Findings from cross-sectional studies showed university students had poor knowledge of contraceptives [16, 17]. Other studies have also reported negative attitudes toward contraceptive use among students [18, 19] with suboptimal usage rates [20, 21] and frequent attempts to terminate pregnancies, suggesting a high prevalence of unintended pregnancies and persistent gaps in contraceptive behaviour [21]. Some researchers have opined that among young people in tertiary institutions, knowledge of contraceptives alone may not necessarily lead to increased use. To reduce the risky sexual behaviours among university students and improve their chance of making informed decisions on sexuality, age-appropriate interventions that will target contraceptive knowledge, attitudes and behaviours should be implemented [22]. Even though some of the earlier cited studies included other students from fields apart from nursing, there is a need to conduct a cross-sectional study among the nursing students. This investigation is crucial because these students are potential future healthcare workers that will be significant in educating individuals about reproductive health promotion.

The gap between existing literature and this study is that recent research measures single variables such as contraceptive knowledge [23, 24]. General contraceptive attitude [25, 26], and for other categories of people such as women [27, 28], men and women, or community members not specifically for university students [2]. The contribution of this study is not only to conduct the study on university students but also to measure the entire three variables at the same time: knowledge, attitude and behavior. It is significant that this study evaluates how one variable affects the other given that this category of individuals are exposed to enormous health risks since they are among the sexually active people, and they need to be informed to protect themselves and prepare to educate families for a good future of society. In addition, these students are future health providers who will be playing valuable roles as educators, counsellors, and influencers in their specific communities.

Methods

Research design

The study was conducted as a descriptive and cross-sectional study. The reporting of the study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines recommended for cross-sectional studies.

Setting

The study was conducted at Near East University, in the Nursing Faculty, located in Northern Cyprus, from January to June 2025. The target population is international students in the nursing faculty. However, Near East University has 21 faculties comprising 220 departments and programs, 5 graduate schools with around 200 graduate and postgraduate programs, and 28 research institutes.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of all international undergraduate students studying at the Faculty of Nursing of Near East University (N = 349). The study aimed to reach the entire population. However, after approaching all the target population during the study period, a total of 297 respondents, representing an 85.1% response rate, completed the questionnaires; therefore, the sample for the study is 297. Our sample size was determined based on the recommendation to use a sample between 160 and 300 as valid observations for various statistical analyses; this approach will mitigate issues associated with both small and excessively large samples, thereby minimising type I and type II errors and supporting a reliable conclusion [29].

We selected the students using convenience sampling because the students were readily accessible during the registered academic session, and it allowed efficient recruitment of respondents. Moreover, the sampling technique is often used in educational and behavioural health research that utilises a descriptive, cross-sectional, or correlation design to identify patterns or associations rather than population-wide generalizability [30].

Data collection tools

Given that the nursing student group received education in English, the data collection forms were provided in English. The data were collected using a data collection form consisting of four parts: (1) the student information form (2), the Contraceptive Knowledge Assessment (CKA) (3), the Contraceptive Attitude Scale (CAS), and (4) the Contraceptive Behavior Scale (CBS). The data collection instruments are:

Student information form

The student information form used to gather demographic data had 12 questions, including age, gender, religion, sexual activity, marital status, contraceptive method uses and having children. We prepared the “Socio-Demographic Section Form” based on reviewing the literature on related study [16, 26].

Contraceptive knowledge assessment (CKA)

The Contraceptive Knowledge Assessment Form (CKA) was developed in English by Haynes and colleagues in 2017. It consists of 25 multiple-choice questions aimed at assessing knowledge about contraceptive methods. Each correct answer receives 1 point, while an incorrect answer receives 0 points. The total score is obtained by summing the correct answers across all 25 items. Total possible scores range from 0 to 25. High summative scores reflect good knowledge, while low summative scores reflect poor knowledge. The original scale had an internal consistency reliability of 77% based on a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient; this was confirmed in a related study with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 [14]. In this study the Cronbach alpha value is 0.822.

Contraceptive attitude scale (CAS)

The Contraceptive Attitude Scale (CAS), developed by Black, was used. The 32-item instrument measures general attitudes toward contraception; it has a five-point Likert scale. “Strongly disagree” is scored as 1, and “Strongly agree” is scored as 5. The instrument consists of 17 positive and 15 negative items, for which participants indicate either agreement or disagreement. For positive statements, “Strongly disagree” is scored as 1, and “Strongly agree” is scored as 5. For negative statements, all questions are reversed to ensure they are scored positively; for ‘strongly disagree’, which is scored 5, this score is reversed to 1 as in the positive statement; it was also applied to the remaining (disagree, undecided, agree and strongly agree). A total score is obtained by summing all responses. Scores range from 32 to 160, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward contraception and lower scores indicating more negative attitudes toward contraception. The scale has a validated Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82 [15]. The Cronbach’s alpha value for this scale in this study was 0.863.

Contraceptive behavior scale (CBS)

The Contraceptive Behavior Scale (CBS) was developed by Wang and colleagues. The instrument consists of five items intended to measure two dimensions of contraceptive behavior. The scale’s scoring criteria are a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 20. Higher scores indicate positive contraceptive behaviors, while lower scores indicate negative or unfavorable behaviors. The scale is valid and reliable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 [19]. The scale was validated in another separate study and the alpha value was 0.73 [31]. In this study the Cronbach’s alpha value is 0.891.

Data collection procedure

The researcher met with the students face-to-face in the classroom and distributed the questionnaires. The researcher explained the purpose of the study and the details of the questionnaires to the students. During the process of data collection, we inform the students to avoid writing their names or student numbers (anonymously) on the questionnaire in order to ensure confidentiality and appropriate response. The completed forms were then retrieved immediately after completion.

Variables of the study

Dependent variables

Contraceptive Knowledge Assessment, Contraceptive Attitude Scale and Contraceptive Behavior Scale.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants.

Data analysis

The data obtained from this study were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was performed to test the data for normal distribution; the results indicated the data were not normally distributed. Hence, non-parametric inferential statistics were used to analyse the data in this study. Descriptive statistics such as frequency (number), percentage, mean, standard deviation (SD), and median were used to evaluate participants’ characteristic variables and scale scores. Correlations between scale scores were examined using Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses were performed with 95% confidence intervals, and p values ​​< 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethics committee approval for this study was received in writing from the Near East University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (1891/2024/127). Permission was also obtained from the Nursing Faculty. All procedures were carried out in compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. Verbal consent was obtained from the participants. Verbal consent was obtained during data collection to facilitate brief, face-to-face interviews with participants and to facilitate the rapid and efficient completion of the surveys. Participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and confidentiality rights; in addition, they were informed about their rights to voluntarily participate in the study and withdraw at any time without any consequences.

The response of the participants on the correct response varied between 12.5% and 62.3% indicating a low to moderate knowledge of contraception. The question with highest (62.3%) correct response is (which of the following choices is true about pregnancy?). The question that received the least accurate responses was “If you forget to take one birth control pill and remember the next day, what should you do?” with merely 12.5% of participants answering correctly. The findings showed knowledge deficiencies among respondents especially on hormonal contraceptives, emergency contraception and the appropriate guidelines for use.

Discussion

This study investigated contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among international nursing students, utilizing the Contraceptive Knowledge Assessment (CKA), Contraceptive Attitude Scale (CAS), and Contraceptive Behavior Scale (CBS).

According the findings in this study, the overall CKA score for the respondents was 8.3 (SD = 4.63). These results show the students had poor understanding of contraceptives. The study result is in line with studies [32], which found sexually active women had a mean summary knowledge score of 4.6. In addition, Hylton-Kong et al. [27] found poor contraceptive knowledge among their respondents (young women). However, the finding is not in agreement with Haynes et al. [24]; the respondents had a mean score of 19.4, representing 77.6%. The disagreement between this study finding and that of Hayes is surprising due to the fact that the respondents in this study are nursing students who are expected to have more knowledge of contraceptives than the respondents in the studies [24, 27, 32].

In this study, the CAS mean score of the respondents is 106.11 (SD = 16.86), representing 66.3% of the total CAS score. The findings showed that students predominantly expressed positive attitudes regarding contraceptive use; this finding agrees with the findings of [26, 33, 34]; in all the mentioned studies they were found to have positive attitudes towards contraception. The scores on the CBS in this study indicate a mean of 7.56 (SD = 6.92), which represents 37.8% of the total contraceptive behaviour scale scores. This result implies that the respondents were found to have poor contraceptive behaviour. The finding is not in agreement with findings of Wang et al. [35]; the researchers reported the global mean CBS demonstrates participants on average have a general tendency towards positive practices, though not at the maximum level.

The results further indicate a statistically significant weak positive correlation between age and contraceptive knowledge score (rs = 0.154, p =.008); the finding is in agreement with [23]; the researchers found a significant association between age and CKA score (p <.001). The study did not find statistically significant relationships between age and contraceptive attitudes or between age and contraceptive behaviour in this sample of nursing students. Therefore, the findings contradict the results [26], who found a significant difference when taking age (p <.05) and CAS scores.

Limitations and Strengths.

In this study, respondents were recruited via convenience sampling at one university; this may limit the generalisation of our findings to students at other universities. Although we measured CKA, CAS, and CBS using a valid and reliable scale, our results could be influenced by response bias because of using self-reported questionnaires to collect data. Even though our study identified the knowledge, attitude, and behaviour of the respondents towards contraception among nursing students, we also identified research gaps that need further investigation. In addition, our findings lead to the development of practical recommendations that would provide beneficial information for nursing education, research, practice and policy.

Conclusion

From the study findings, we can conclude that the respondents in the study had poor knowledge of contraceptives, poor contraceptive behaviour and positive attitude towards contraceptives use. A significant weak positive correlation between age and contraceptive knowledge was found among the respondents. The findings showed the need for strengthening contraceptive education and targeted interventions in the sampled nursing students.

Recommendations for nursing education, policy and research

Based on the findings of the study, we recommend comprehensive education and training on contraception for the respondents using periodic evaluations and review sessions that are integrated to enhance learning retention. Although attitudes towards contraception were moderately favourable, there is a need for further promotion through conversations, value-clarifying sessions, and role-playing situations that underscore responsible contraceptive decision-making. The low behaviour scores indicate a need for behaviour change communication strategies through the application of behaviour change models (the transtheoretical model, the Health Belief Model, or the Theory of Planned Behaviour), which may assist in improving actual contraceptive practices. The poor behaviour score, as observed in this study, calls for the need to improve access to youth-friendly contraceptive services through campus health facilities and community collaboration.

We also recommended incorporating comprehensive sexuality and contraceptive training in the nursing curriculum and organising workshops, seminars and peer-education sessions from time to time. Compulsory, evidence-based teaching of contraception should be provided to the nursing students. Based on the association between age and knowledge in this sample of respondents, we recommend customising contraceptive instructional materials to enhance understanding among the various age groups. Further studies should be conducted using qualitative methods to explore why positive attitudes among the sampled respondents did not improve their contraceptive knowledge or behavioural scores. Future studies should be conducted with nursing students from other universities to enhance their comprehensive understanding and generalisation of the findings. We also recommend implementing an intervention study using a randomised control study to improve contraceptive knowledge and behaviour among the sampled respondents.
