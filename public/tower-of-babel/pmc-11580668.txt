Strategy for early selection for grain yield in soybean using BLUPIS
Authors: Andreia Schuster, Felipe Lopes da Silva, João Amaro Ferreira Vieira Netto, Emanuel Ferrari do Nascimento, Paulo Eduardo Teodoro, Leonardo Lopes Bhering
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In soybean breeding programs, a great deal of time is devoted to the use of methods that perform selection of individual plants during the initial generations. Our hypothesis is that BLUPIS (simulated individual BLUP) can be efficient when applied in the initial stages of soybean breeding programs. This study aimed to explore the potential of BLUPIS in the early generations of a soybean breeding program, as well as to assess the viability of the strategy of dividing the useful area of experimental plots for estimating genotypic effects and plant selection. The experiment involved 84 segregating populations and 15 soybean parents in the F2 and F3 generations. Yield data was collected from the 2019/2020 and 2020/2021 cropping seasons. In the F2 generation, different data exploration methods were applied to determine the most suitable adaptation to be used in the F3 generation. The individual BLUP (BLUPI) was compared with BLUPIS using information from different replications and/or equal to the information used in BLUPI. The selection conducted by BLUPIS and BLUPI showed high concordance regarding the selected plants. In the F3 generation, segregating populations were selected based on positive genotypic effects, and individual plants within these populations were further selected according to the number of plants determined by BLUPIS. The division of the plot area was an efficient strategy for selecting segregating populations and individual plants within superior populations in the F3 generation, resulting in genetic gains of approximately 1.56 g per plant. When combined with the strategy of advancing generations in the off-season, the BLUPIS approach reduces the time required to achieve a high level of homozygosity. Therefore, BLUPIS proved to be a powerful statistical tool for early selection based on grain yield in soybeans.

Introduction

Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merrill] is one of the main sources of protein in the world, with Brazil and the United States being the largest producers in the world [1–5]. Genetic breeding was responsible for around 40 to 50% of the increase in soybean productivity over the last 50 years [3, 6–8]. Changes in soybean breeding strategies have influenced the time required for developing new cultivars. Traditional methods typically demand around 10 years to release a new cultivar [9, 10]. The conduct of the breeding program is favored by the initial selection of the most promising populations and discarding inferior populations in the F3 generation, concentrating efforts on evaluating a greater number of plants in superior segregating populations.

The application of more accurate statistical methodologies in selection also contributes to genetic gain and results in more accurate grain productivity estimates [11]. Best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) is a powerful tool for estimating genotypic value in breeding experiments. Individual BLUP (BLUPI) is the ideal method for plant selection, but it requires a private evaluation of all plants, because it uses simultaneously information on family and individual for selection [12, 13]. The evaluation of individual plants in soybean breeding programs is a costly and time-consuming practice. Therefore, it is interesting to use a strategy that allows the selection of segregating populations and plants within superior populations in order to maximize gains without the need for individual plant evaluation. Proposed by [14], the simulated individual BLUP (BLUPIS) aims to reduce costs by eliminating the individual evaluation of plants. In this case, the selection of individual plants is conducted based on the genotypic effects information of the evaluated populations considering only the average information of a portion of the populations, without the need for individual assessment. BLUPIS indicates the number of families to be selected, the ideal number of plants to be selected per family or population and the total number of plants to be selected, in addition to indicating the families that must be discarded due to their negative genotypic effect [14].

In soybean breeding programs, a great deal of time is devoted to the use of methods that perform selection of individual plants during the initial generations. To the best of our knowledge, there is no research demonstrating the application of BLUPIS in the initial generations of soybean. The objectives of this work were: a) to study the feasibility of applying BLUPIS in the initial generations of the genetic breeding program in soybean; b) verify the need to include data from the selection plot in the estimation of genotypic effects for the application of BLUPIS in the initial generations of the soybean breeding program; c) study the feasibility of the strategy of dividing the useful area of the plots to estimate the genotypic effects and plant selection in the F3 generation of soybeans; and d) estimate the genetic gains with the application of BLUPIS in the F3 generation of soybean.

Materials and methods

Genotypes

Two distinct diallel crosses were conducted to obtain segregating soybean populations for the development and selection of lines with high yield performance in soybean region 102 of Rio Grande do Sul state. The first diallel involved eight commercial soybean cultivars, representing a complete set of crosses, while the second diallel was a partial one, consisting of seven lines from the Active Germplasm Bank of the Soybean Breeding Program at the Federal University of Viçosa (Table 1). Building on this, 84 segregating populations were obtained. All parents carry the transgenic event INTACTA RR2 PRO® (IPRO). The F1 hybrids were cultivated in a greenhouse at the Diogo Alves de Mello Experimental Field, located at the Federal University of Viçosa. The location is positioned at coordinates 20°46′00′′ S and 42°52′09ʹʹ W, at an altitude of 662 m above sea level. The F1 hybrids were cultivated from June to October 2019, speeding up the breeding process and enabling the evaluation of the segregating F2 populations during the subsequent crop season (2019/2020). Plants from each F1 hybrid combination were harvested together, and from them, the F2 seeds were obtained for the 84 segregating populations.

Experimental design

The F2 and F3 segregating populations, along with the parent plants, were evaluated in experiments during the 2019/2020 and 2020/2021 cropping seasons in Derrubadas, RS (27°14′37.5″ S, 53°55′15.5″ W, and 402 m above sea level), which is part of soybean region 102 and macro-region 1.

Fertilization of the experiments was conducted based on soil analysis and soybean crop recommendations according to the “Liming and fertilization manual for the states of Rio Grande do Sul and Santa Catarina” [15]. The experiments in the F2 and F3 generations started on November 20, 2019, and November 12, 2020, respectively. Cultural practices, as well as pest, disease, and weed management were conducted following technical recommendations for soybean growth [16].

During the 2019/2020 cropping season, a total of 99 treatments were evaluated, consisting of 84 F2 segregating populations and 15 parents, using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with four plots. Each plot consisted of a single 2.0 m long row spaced 0.45 m apart. 30 seeds were distributed per plot, and after emergence, manual random thinning was performed, leaving 24 plants per plot.

In the F2 generation experiment, out of the four replications, plants from replications 1 and 2 were harvested and threshed together to obtain the grain yield for each plot (g plot−1). For replications 3 and 4, 12 plants were individually harvested and threshed to determine the grain yield for each plant (g plant−1) totaling 4032 plants evaluated, and the sum of the yield of the plants within the plot resulted in the grain yield for each plot (g plot−1). The F3 seeds were grouped after harvest according to each segregating population.

In the 2020/2021 cropping season, an experiment was conducted with 84 F3 segregating populations and 15 parents, using a randomized complete block design with three plots. Each plot consisted of a single 3.0 m long row spaced 0.45 m apart. Sowing was conducted with 60 seeds per plot, and after emergence, manual random thinning was performed, leaving 36 plants per plot. In the F3 generation, half of all plots were evaluated, totaling 6048 plants evaluated individually.

Statistical analysis

F2 segregating populations analysis

Data from both plot and individual plant measurements were collected and subjected to statistical analyses using the REML/BLUP mixed model methodology with the Selegen software [17]. Based on information from the replications, three analysis arrangements were obtained to estimate genotypic effects and determine the most suitable strategy to be used in the F3 generation.

The three adopted analysis strategies were as follows:I.Analysis based on yield data (g plot−1) from replications 1 and 2 for genotypic effects estimation and selection using individual plant data from replications 3 and 4 (4032 plants), referred to as Estimation F2.II.Analysis based on yield data (g plot−1) from replications 3 and 4 for genotypic effects estimation and selection using 4032 individual plants data from replications 3 and 4, referred to as Selection F2.III.Analysis using yield data (g plot−1) from all four replications and selection of plants using 4032 individual plants data from replications 3 and 4, referred to as F2.

The total grain yield data per plot (g plot−1) from the four replications (F2), along with grain yield data from Estimation F2 and Selection F2, were analyzed using the mixed model REML/BLUP methodology.

Additionally, the BLUPIS approach was applied, based on the estimates of the genotypic effect of the F2 segregating populations obtained from each of the strategies. Individual plant data were analyzed using the mixed model methodology to obtain the individual additive effect of the evaluated plants (BLUPI). Subsequently, individual plant selection and genetic gains were simulated based on the application of BLUPIS for the three analyzed strategies (F2, Estimation F2, and Selection F2). The results of the genetic gains from these strategies were compared with the individual BLUP, which is the ideal method for selecting individual plants.

Individual BLUP (BLUPI)

The individual BLUP was obtained for plants harvested and individually evaluated in generation F2 in two replicates (Selection F2). To obtain the individual additive effect of plants, two models were used in the Selegen software [17]: model 187 to obtain the average heritability within populations and model 24 to obtain the additive effect of each individual.

Model 187 simultaneously fits a model for the crossbreeding data and another model for the parentage data. The estimates of additive effects for each plant obtained through individual BLUP were used to obtain the genetic gain of BLUPIS with the selection of superior segregating populations, and within these, the selection of superior individuals. Out of the total of 4032 individually evaluated plants, 638 plants were selected in each of the studied strategies (F2, Estimation F2, and Selection F2). The new mean of the selected individuals was obtained by calculating the average of the genetic values of the selected individuals. Pearson correlation estimates were also obtained between the genotypic effects, and Spearman correlation was used for ranking the segregating populations to compare the three BLUPIS strategies with BLUPI. Additionally, the Pearson correlation was used to analyze the number of individuals selected per population. All analyses were conducted using R software [20] and Rbio [21].

F3 segregating populations analysis

The F3 generation plots were divided in two. One half (0.675 m2) of each plot was used to measure grain yield (g plot−1) aiming the estimating the genotypic effects of the F3 segregating populations, referred to as Estimation F3 (Fig. 1). In the other half of the plot (0.675 m2), plants were harvested and stored for subsequent individual plant selection within the best F3 segregating populations, referred to as Selection F3. The plants from the area designated as Estimation F3 were harvested and threshed together to obtain grain yield (g plot−1). Meanwhile, the plants from the Selection F3 area were harvested and stored in labeled bags until the selection process. After obtaining the grain yield data (g plot−1) from the Estimation F3 area, statistical analyses were conducted using REML/BLUP, and the genotypic effects of the segregating population were obtained. The data from the F3 segregating populations were also analyzed using the above-mentioned model, considering both the total plot information (F3) and the data from the Selection F3 area, to obtain the genotypic effects, and genotypic values and rank the segregating populations. These analyses were conducted to compare the genotypic effects obtained based on part of the plot (Estimation F3) with the total plot information (F3) and to assess if the information extracted from part of the plot can represent the grain yield information of the entire plot. Additionally, the correlation between the Selection F3 and Estimation F3 information was evaluated by comparing the estimates of the genotypic effect obtained from these analyses.

This allowed for individual plant selection based on the information from the respective plots, considering that the data from half of the plot (Estimation F3) represented and had a high correlation with the total plot information of F3. The genotypic effect of the populations was obtained for the three sets of information: Estimation F3, Selection F3, and F3, and BLUPIS was applied to the Estimation F3 data. BLUPIS enables the dynamic selection of individuals, considering the genotypic effects of the populations. The number of individuals selected in the best population (nj = 46) was determined by the number of plants harvested in the Selection F3 area of the best population. The number of individuals to be selected in the other populations (nk) was obtained by dividing the genotypic effect of each population (ĝk) by the effect of the best population (TMG7063/28–1) (ĝj), multiplied by the number of individuals selected in the best population (nj = 46), as proposed by [14] in Eq. 5.

Populations with a negative genotypic effect were discarded. For the F3 segregating populations with a positive effect, the number of plants to be selected was estimated as proposed by BLUPIS. The number of plants selected in each replication was equal to nk/3. Visual selection of the plants was conducted to choose individuals with higher grain yield and desirable agronomic traits, such as a greater number of branches and pods, fewer pod defects, and appropriate first pod insertion height.

After the plant selection process, the remaining plants in the Selection F3 area that were not chosen were threshed together for each plot to obtain the total grain yield per plot. In the Selection F3 area, the final yield was determined by summing the yield of the non-selected plants that were threshed together with the yield of the selected plants. In the F3 generation, the total number of individuals was determined based on the number of candidate plants for selection in the best segregating population, which had 46 plants (nj = 46). Thus, a total of 571 F3 plants were selected. These will constitute the F3:4 progenies to continue the breeding program for the development of new soybean lines. The estimated genetic gain with the selection of the best plants within superior F3 populations was calculated based on the average of the selected individuals and the broad-sense heritability of individual plots.

Pearson and Spearman correlation estimates were also performed to compare the results obtained from the F3 segregating populations considering the genetic effects estimates from Estimation F3, Selection F3, and F3 data. Additionally, these results were compared with those obtained in the F2 generation to assess the feasibility of conducting selection in the F2 generation to discard the worst populations. The R software with the package "agricolae" [22] was used for the analysis. Cohen's kappa reliability index [23] was obtained using the R software with the assistance of the "irr" package [24]. This index was used to assess the concordance between the selected populations considering the information from Estimation F3, Selection F3, F3 data, and the F2 generation. The interpretation of the Kappa index followed [25] criteria. Additionally, the percentage of concordance in the selection or discard of F3 segregating populations was calculated.

Results and discussion

Results of F2 populations

The effect of populations, plots, and parents was statistically significant (p < 0.01) according to the likelihood ratio test (LRT) and deviance analysis for the grain yield of F2 segregating populations based on individual plant data (Table 2). Deviance analysis for the three BLUPIS application strategies for grain yield (g plot−1) also showed statistical significance, with p < 0.01 for Estimation F2 and F2, and p < 0.05 for Selection F2. These results indicate the existence of genetic variability among the studied F2 segregating populations, enabling selection within and between these populations with possibilities for genetic gain. The Selection F2 strategy proved to be superior even when using information from a smaller number of replications, highlighting that the information considered in estimating genotypic effects should be related to the plants where the selection will take place. The obtained results demonstrate that the information on the candidate plants for selection is essential in estimating the genotypic effect for the BLUPIS application. Therefore, BLUPIS is an efficient tool to be employed in the early stages of the soybean breeding program, providing the advantage of selecting the most promising plants and eliminating the need to evaluate individual plants, which saves resources in the breeding program.

Based on the results obtained in the F2 generation, a new experiment was conducted, considering the necessary conditions for the application of BLUPIS. In this experiment, the information for estimating genotypic effects was extracted from half of the useful area of the plots, which corresponded to approximately 15 plants. The other half of the area was dedicated to selecting superior plants based on BLUPIS, with approximately 15 plants. To demonstrate the viability and efficiency of this approach, the results of the selection based on Estimation F3 with the total information of the F3 plot were compared with the additional information from the Selection F3 plot.

Experiments conducted in early generations of breeding programs may exhibit a high rate of experimental errors due to the large number of genotypes evaluated and a reduced number of replications, contributing to low magnitude heritability estimates [28–31]. The accuracy estimate was high for estimation F3, which indicates that selection among populations is recommended under these conditions [11, 19, 32]. The coefficient of experimental variation showed moderate estimates for grain yield evaluated in annual grain crops according to [33].

The genetic parameters obtained with Estimation F3 and Selection F3 data were similar, indicating that the grain yield data from the two halves of the plots are correlated, with a moderate correlation estimate of 0.65. On the other hand, the correlation between replications was very weak, with an average of 0.25 and 0.22 for Estimation F3 and Selection F3 data, respectively. When comparing information from different plots for the same segregating population, variations are observed due to the genotypic constitution of the individuals as well as environmental variations between and within plots. However, when comparing information from segregating populations from the same plot, the variations that occur are only due to genetic and environmental factors within the plot. Thus, it is possible to observe that in the strategy of dividing the plots, the use of different replications is reasonable for the adaptation of BLUPIS.

Therefore, the source of extraction of genetic effect estimates has a stronger relationship with the candidate plants to be selected in the strategy adopted in generation F3. Thus, even though the grain yield of the plants reserved for later selection was not directly considered, the information from the rest of the plot (Estimation F3) proved to be efficient in identifying superior populations. The concordance in selection between Estimation F3 and Selection F3 occurred for 69 out of 84 populations. In addition, only one of the superior populations in Selection F3 was not selected by Estimation F3, while six selected populations showed a negative genotypic effect in Selection F3 (Table 6 and Fig. 2). Among the 15 worst-ranked populations by Selection F3, none were among those selected by Estimation F3. The Kappa reliability index showed a strong concordance in the selection of populations. The strong correlation between the genotypic effects and the ranking of segregating populations F3 further supports the existence of a correlation between Estimation F3 and Selection F3 data. Considering that the genetic constitution among plants within the plot is variable, we can still consider that the developed strategy allows for the selection of superior populations and the discarding of inferior populations, with a low risk of losing superior genotypes. The significant advantage consists of selecting individual plants with a higher probability of generating superior lines without the need for individual plant evaluation.

Differences in the number of populations to be selected were observed among the different selection analyses. These differences occur due to fluctuations in the number of populations with positive and negative estimates, as the sum of genotypic effects is null. Therefore, there are slight variations in the number of populations to be selected.

The study observed a very strong correlation between Estimation F3 and F3 data (0.90) and between F3 and Selection F3 data (0.92), indicating a significant relationship between the total plot and its two halves. Among the top 15 ranked populations by F3, 13 of them also appeared in the top 15 ranked populations by Estimation F3 selection. Among the populations selected in Estimation F3, only one showed a negative genotypic effect in the analysis with total F3 data. Furthermore, 13 of the worst-ranked populations by F3 also appeared among the 15 worst-ranked populations based on Estimation F3 data analysis. The concordance between the selected populations was almost perfect according to the Kappa index, and there was a strong correlation between the genotypic effects and the ranking of populations for Estimation F3 and F3.

Implications

Based on these results, the adoption of the strategy of dividing plots for estimation and selection is feasible, and the chosen plot size allows the use of BLUPIS in soybean breeding during the initial generations. The division of plots has the advantage of enabling the selection of promising populations and, within them, the selection of the best individuals in the same generation with a single experiment. It also provides the advantage of conducting the selection based on more accurate statistical information and allows the selection of more promising plants for grain yield from the early generations, such as F3. The comparison between Selection F3 and F3 shows similar results to the comparison between Estimation F3 and F3. The correlation between genotypic effects showed to be very strong, and a high coincidence in the selection of families was found. The Kappa index indicates a high reliability of the selections.

The three analyses conducted with the data from generation F3 show discrepancies with the data from generation F2 regarding the ranking of populations. Two of the worst-ranked populations in generation F2 appear among the superior populations in generation F3 (Populations 76 and 24), and three others still appear among the selected ones in F3 (Populations 74, 14, and 40), indicating that populations would have been wrongly discarded if selection had been practiced in generation F2. The estimates of correlations between the genotypic effects of populations in generation F2 and the analyses of generation F3 were weak and very low. Additionally, the coincidence of selection and the Kappa index show low values. These results can be partly explained by the interaction that occurs between populations and cropping seasons, as well as the decline of the heterosis effect, especially in early generations like the ones under study.

The change in ranking from generation F2 to generation F3 can also be explained by the presence of loci in heterozygosity, environmental influence with the occurrence of genotype x environment interaction, and the moderate heritability of grain yield. As generations progress, the rate of heterozygotes decreases, increasing the fixation of alleles with additive effects, which are primarily responsible for controlling grain yield. The discrepancies in population rankings between F2 and F3 generations are significant and could undermine the effectiveness of early generation selection. Therefore, the practice of selection for grain yield is recommended for more advanced generations with less intense selection in the initial generations, such as generation F3, when 75% of the additive genetic variance is available [5, 10, 32].

The estimated gain from population selection was 13.24 g per plot, which corresponds to a gain of 0.88 g per plant or extrapolated to 196 kg per hectare, equivalent to 4.87% (Table 7). The use of BLUPIS allowed for predicting considerable genetic gains with selection in generation F3. Considering that moderate selection intensity is recommended for initial generations and that the genetic base of currently exploited soybean varieties is narrow [37], the factors most impacted by the application of BLUPIS were the increased accuracy in predicting genetic values and the potential reduction in the time required for developing new lines associated with this statistical tool.

The application of BLUPIS in annual self-pollinated plants such as soybean proved to be a suitable tool when combined with the adaptation of plot division, enabling the achievement of genetic gains in selection between and within populations. Based on the results, BLUPIS has proven to be an accurate statistical tool, and when coupled with a moderate selection intensity in generation F3, it brings several benefits to the breeding program. These advantages include reducing the number of populations to be carried forward in subsequent generations, increasing the probability of generating superior lines in the selected genotype, reducing costs and space requirements for advancing generations, as well as facilitating the adoption of breeding strategies such as conducting off-season generations and reducing the time to reach a high level of homozygosity.

Furthermore, BLUPIS can be integrated into the management of segregating populations, as suggested in Table 1, where the evaluation and selection of populations take place in generation F3. This approach allows for conducting two generations during the off-season, saving time to achieve the desired homozygosity level for plant selection and soybean line development. As a result, generations F1 and F2 are not subjected to evaluations and selection, and neither are generations F3:4 and F3:5, which are used solely for advancing generations and increasing seed volume. In generation F3:6, bulk evaluations are conducted, and within the superior F3:6 progenies, the selection of promising plants is performed to generate lines for subsequent evaluations until the release of new soybean cultivars. Although the potential of BLUPIS is evident from this research, it is necessary to investigate how this method can work under different environmental conditions or with different genetic backgrounds.

Conclusion

BLUPIS is a useful statistical procedure to be applied in the initial generations of the soybean breeding program. The information from the plot of candidate plants for selection is essential to achieve good results in plant selection using BLUPIS. The strategy of dividing the plot for estimating genotypic effects and selecting plants is efficient for applying BLUPIS in the F3 generation in soybean breeding. BLUPIS allows genetic gains in the selection of populations and plants within populations in the F3 generation.
