Health-related quality of life and associated factors among older adults attending primary care clinics in Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Anthonia Nkechiyem Alabi, Ifekunle Ebenezer O. Alabi, Silvia Isioma Ezemenahi, Ramat Adebayo Yusuf, Lawrence Adebusoye, Kemji Nformi, Oyeronke Grace Oyeleke, Victor Iyioluwa Alabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation ageing in Nigeria is associated with rising chronic diseases, psychosocial challenges, weakened traditional family support systems, and limited social security, which may negatively affect the health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) of older adults. While depression, anxiety, and chronic illnesses have been extensively studied, the effects of spirituality and spiritual needs on HR-QoL among older adults in Nigerian primary care are still under explored despite the central role of spirituality in Nigeria’s sociocultural context.ObjectiveTo assess the HR-QoL and its associated sociodemographic and clinical factors among older adults attending primary care clinics in Ilorin, Nigeria.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 280 older adults attending primary care clinics. Data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires, including the World Health Organisation Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF), Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ-20), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Descriptive statistics, independent t tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses were performed as appropriate using SPSS version 27. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean overall quality of life (QoL) score was 59.97 ± 11.48. Participants who were married and had higher educational attainment had significantly higher QoL scores. Pearson correlation analysis showed a significant moderate negative correlation between QoL and depression (r = -0.558; p < 0.001) and a weak-to-moderate negative correlation between QoL and anxiety (r = -0.353; p < 0.001). No significant correlation was observed between spiritual needs and QoL (r = 0.058, p = 0.331). However, multiple linear regression analysis identified sex (p = 0.034), education (p = 0.044), depression (p < 0.001), and spiritual needs (p = 0.027) as variables with significant independent associations with QoL. The regression model accounted for 36.4% of the variance in QoL (R² = 0.364), indicating a moderate predictive capacity of the included variables.ConclusionDepression had the strongest association with QoL among the studied older adults, while higher educational attainment and spiritual needs were associated with improved QoL. Integrating psychosocial and spiritual assessments within primary care may enhance the well-being of older adults in Nigeria.

Introduction

Ageing is a complex biological and social process characterised by a progressive decline in physiological integrity and impaired functional capacity [1, 2]. In Nigeria, the elderly population is rising rapidly [3, 4]. The country currently hosts approximately 14.8 million individuals aged 60 and above, a figure projected to double to 30 million by 2050 [3, 4]. This population increase is accompanied by an increasing burden of chronic non-communicable diseases and multimorbidity, which are frequently compounded by other challenges, such as poverty, the deterioration of traditional family support systems, and the absence of reliable social security [5–7]. These social and cultural factors influence older adult Nigerians’ mental health, spiritual well-being, and health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) [6]. Studies have identified sociodemographic factors such as education, income, and marital status as determinants of HR-QoL among older adults in Nigeria [2, 8, 9]. Other significant determinants include physical health status, chronic diseases, psychological status, and mental health conditions, especially in primary care clinics [10].

In Nigeria, the term “primary care setting” refers to the foundational level of the country’s three-tier healthcare system and acts as the “first point of call” for citizens [11, 12]. In secondary and tertiary hospitals, this is typically the General Outpatient Clinic (GOPC) of the Family Medicine Department [13]. These clinics were chosen for this study as they are patients’ first contact with the health system and they manage most older adults from the region who are served by the hospital. Additionally, the GOPC is expected to provide holistic, person-centred care that integrates biomedical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions, aligning with the multidimensional focus of this study [13–15]. A wide range of undifferentiated and chronic conditions are managed here, making these clinics well suited for capturing a heterogeneous, representative older adult population, in contrast to the more selective groups seen in specialist clinics [13, 16]. Additionally, findings from primary care settings are more easily translatable into policy and practice, as interventions at this level can achieve broad population impact, enhance accessibility, and be integrated into routine care pathways [14, 15].

Despite Nigeria’s increasing geriatric population, primary health care still follows a curative, maternal and child health approach, prioritising infectious diseases over the complex needs of older adults [3, 4]. Spirituality is an important sociocultural resource in Africa but is often excluded from clinical practice, especially in primary care [17, 18]. More than 97.0% of outpatients say that their spiritual needs are not being assessed, and fewer than 3.0% of physicians can identify a standard spiritual assessment tool [19, 20]. Neglecting psychological and spiritual aspects may create gaps in geriatric care, since these factors are related to health-related quality of life [2, 21].

There is a dearth of empirical data exploring the internal psychological and spiritual mechanisms that drive resilience in the face of chronic illness in the Nigerian context [17]. Existing studies on religiosity and spirituality among older adults in Nigeria have mainly relied on non-clinical or researcher-developed measures rather than validated clinical instruments [17], while studies examining spirituality in health settings have reported limited use of standardised spiritual assessment tools [19]. This study addresses this gap and is the first to apply the Spiritual Needs Questionnaire, a standardised instrument that assesses spiritual needs in palliative and elderly care, in the Nigerian primary care context [22, 23]. By measuring these unmet needs alongside screened symptoms of anxiety and depression, this study provides a novel, multidimensional view of the factors shaping geriatric HR-QoL in Ilorin, Nigeria.

Justification with theoretical and conceptual frameworks

This study was based on the Biopsychosocial-Spiritual Model, which holds that geriatric HR-QoL is derived from the dynamic interrelation of biological health, psychological distress, and spiritual well-being [24–26]. This theoretical foundation is further supported by Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, which proposes that health outcomes reflect an individual’s subjective appraisal of stressors rather than the stressors themselves [27, 28]. Within this framework, primary appraisal assesses the level of threat from chronic illness (such as chronic diseases, depression or anxiety), whereas secondary appraisal considers available resources, such as social and spiritual support [28, 29]. In the context of older adults in Nigeria, this perspective indicates that spiritual reframing and social support may reduce the impact of chronic illness. Social and spiritual support may enable older adults to maintain a high quality of life despite serious health challenges by facilitating psychological adjustment [1, 2]. Using this comprehensive approach, this study examines the relationship between QoL, anxiety, depression, chronic conditions, and spiritual needs among older adult Nigerians in primary care. The resulting evidence seeks to inform policies that integrate mental and spiritual health into primary care and promote effective, respectful care for older adults in Ilorin, Nigeria, and potentially sub-Saharan Africa [1, 3, 4]. The findings from this study may be relevant for promoting age-friendly, holistic geriatric healthcare [3]. The findings will also be used as a baseline for further similar studies. They will be included in the database of studies in Nigeria and Africa on the psychosocial and spiritual influences on the HR-QoL of older adults in primary care settings.

The conceptual framework for this study is follows the biopsychosocial theoretical framework that has been extended to incorporate the spiritual dimension of health. This conceptual framework posits that HR-QoL among older adults receiving primary care services is determined by the combined and interacting effects of biological, psychological, social, and spiritual factors. These variables are expected to jointly explain variations in HR-QoL outcomes, thereby supporting a more holistic understanding of ageing and informing the development of comprehensive geriatric care interventions within primary healthcare settings in Ilorin, Nigeria.

Method

Study site

The study was conducted at the General Outpatient Clinics of the Family Medicine Department at the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital (UITH), Ilorin. Ilorin is the capital of Kwara State in the north-central geopolitical zone of Nigeria and borders with five other states [30]. The majority of the inhabitants of Ilorin are Yoruba, with other tribes, such as Fulani, Hausa, Baruba, and Igbo, in the minority. The people are mainly farmers, traders, artisans, private employees, and civil servants. Islam is the most commonly practised religion in the state, with a significant number of Christians and traditional worshipers [31]. The GOPC of the Family Medicine Department serves as the primary care clinic of UITH, Ilorin.

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional design, with data collected over 6 months from September 2024 to March 2025.

Study participants

These were older adult patients aged 60 years and above who came to the General Outpatient Clinic of the Department of Family Medicine, UITH, Ilorin, for various health reasons. While the majority of these patients came from the Ilorin metropolis, others came from other parts of the state and from neighbouring states. According to the department’s medical records, approximately 4,080 older adult patients are seen in the clinics annually.

Inclusion criteria

All adult patients aged 60 years and above who gave written informed consent to participate at the GOPC, UITH, during the study period.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded three groups: older adults with serious health conditions requiring emergency attention, patients with obvious memory impairment and other psychiatric conditions preventing correct responses, and those needing referral to specialist clinics to avoid delaying their timely treatment.

Sample size and sampling technique

A sample size of 280 was calculated using Cochran’s formula and adjusted for a finite population of fewer than 10,000. Participants were recruited during weekday clinic hours (08:00–16:00) using systematic random sampling. Approximately 4,080 older adult patients are seen annually according to the departmental records unit, thus providing a six-month sampling frame of 2,040. Therefore, every seventh eligible older adult patient was recruited. The first participant was selected by simple random sampling by balloting from 1 to 7. If an individual was ineligible or declined, then the next eligible patient was selected. To prevent duplicate sampling, patient folders were labelled upon recruitment. This process continued until the target sample size was reached.

Data collection and quality assurance

Four resident doctors assisted in data collection after receiving standardised training on the study protocol, WHO ethical protocols, and interviewing techniques for older adults. Their training included pilot testing to ensure procedural consistency and reduce potential interviewer and response biases. Regular supervision and evaluation of the collected data ensured quality and conformity with the study objectives and ethical standards.

Study instruments and variables

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire that comprised two sections. The questionnaire was pre-tested in the outpatient clinics of the General Hospital of Ilorin (a tertiary hospital), with 10.0% of the study population. Thereafter, any concern raised was adjusted before its use for the study. The questionnaire had two sections. Section A captured sociodemographic data and self-reported clinical histories of chronic conditions, which were cross-verified against participants’ previous clinical records to confirm accuracy. Section B comprised the following three standardised psychometric instruments.

WHOQOL-BREF (World Health Organisation quality of life-BREF)

This 26-item brief version of the WHO-QoL tool was used to measure the HR-QoL. This was the dependent/outcome variable of the study. This tool has demonstrated high dependability in Nigerian populations, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values of approximately 0.86 [9]. It evaluates QoL across four distinct domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and the environmental domain. In this study, QoL scores are presented as the means ± SDs for each domain and overall QoL.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

This 14-item scale was used to screen for anxiety and depression symptoms, which were independent variables in this study. It consists of two sub-scales, A and B, each containing seven items that assess anxiety and depressive symptoms. The respondents rated their feelings over the past week by indicating their level of agreement with each statement on a 4-point scale (0 to 3). The total score for each sub-scale was calculated by summing the responses to the relevant items. The total scores per sub-scale were used to categorise symptom severity as follows: ‘Normal’ = 0–7, ‘Borderline’ = 8–10, and ‘Abnormal/Cases’ = 11–21. The tool was found to have high sensitivity (85–93%) and specificity (86–91%) in previous studies in Nigeria [32–34]. Permission to use the tool was obtained from the tool developers.

Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ-20)

Another independent variable assessed in this study was the spiritual needs of the participants. These were assessed using the 20-item version of the Spiritual Needs Questionnaire, which evaluates four primary dimensions [22, 23].

Religious need

This need refers to older adults’ desire for connection with God or greater power through beliefs and religious practices such as praying and being prayed for, attending religious services, reading religious books, receiving support from religious leaders, and feeling connected to God.

Existential need

This domain focuses on the individual’s search for meaning, purpose, and understanding in life, especially in the face of ageing, illness, or adversity. This entails things such as reflecting on the meaning of life, discussing fears about death or suffering, finding peace with one’s life experiences, and resolving unanswered existential questions.

Inner peace

This domain reflects older adults’ need for emotional calmness, harmony, and relief from distress. It includes desires for inner tranquillity, peaceful surroundings, opportunities for silence or reflection, emotional comfort, and relief from anxiety and worry.

Giving/generativity

This domain assesses older adults’ desire to contribute to others and to leave a meaningful legacy. It reflects the desire for continued relevance, dignity, and social connectedness. Examples include the need to help others, pass on life experiences or wisdom, feel valued and useful, and express love and compassion.

Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (very strong). Higher scores indicate greater severity of spiritual needs [22, 23]. In this study, the mean overall and domain scores were used to interpret the SpNQ-20 scores. The SpNQ-20 has demonstrated good reliability, with the Cronbach’s alpha for its four factors ranging from 0.71 to 0.87 in various samples [22, 35]. In addition, it was face-validated by Esan et al. in a study in Ilorin, Nigeria, thereby enhancing its contextual suitability and credibility for use within a similar population [23]. The tool was used with the developer’s permission.

The sociodemographic section of the questionnaire used was developed for this study. The full questionnaire is therefore uploaded as a supplementary file along with this manuscript as requested.

The authors also declare the use of the Jenni AI tool for conducting a comprehensive literature search and other AI tools for English language editing.

Data analysis

Data were analysed with IBM SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarise the socio-demographic variables, HR-QoL, depression, anxiety, the presence/absence of chronic conditions, and spiritual needs.

For further analysis of the continuous variables, a normality check was performed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, as well as assessments of skewness and kurtosis. Although some variables showed statistically significant deviations from normality on the Shapiro-Wilk test, the skewness and kurtosis indices were within acceptable ranges (approximately − 1 to + 1), indicating approximate normality of the distribution. Parametric statistical tests were then considered appropriate for further analyses given the relatively large sample size (n = 280) and the near-symmetrical distribution of the variables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Pearson’s correlation, and multiple linear regression were then used as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p = 0.05.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was granted by the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital Ethical Review Committee, and permission to conduct the study was obtained from the head of the Department of Family Medicine UITH, Ilorin. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the principles of Good Clinical Practice by ensuring that no harm was caused to the participants. All the participants provided written informed consent, with the explicit understanding that participation was voluntary and that they could opt out at any time without prejudice. Strict confidentiality was maintained, with all study records kept securely and available exclusively to the research team.

Mean differences in quality of life scores according to sociodemographic characteristics

Marital status

Compared with the “Other” group (i.e., widowed/single), married participants reported significantly higher scores in the physical domain (62.3 vs. 55.6), social domain (56.0 vs. 50.0), and overall QoL domain (61.2 vs. 56.7).

Occupation

This was significantly associated with the physical domain (p = 0.050) and overall QoL scores (p = 0.028). Compared with petty traders, small business owners and other occupations, professionals and retired civil servants generally recorded higher QoL scores. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in the psychological, social, or environmental domains.

Educational level

Participants with a tertiary education had the highest scores across the physical (p < 0.001), psychological (p < 0.001), and overall QoL (p < 0.005) domains. Those with no formal education had the lowest scores.

Age, sex, and type of marriage were not significantly associated with QoL in this study.

Independently associated variables of QoL by multiple linear regression

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the independent sociodemographic and clinical variables associated with overall quality of life among the participants. Variables included in the multivariate regression model were selected based on theoretical relevance and results from bivariate analyses. Variables with p values less than 0.2 in the preliminary analyses, as well as clinically important variables such as age, spiritual needs, religion, and other were entered into the regression model. The model demonstrated a strong overall fit and was statistically significant (F = 14.00, p < 0.001), accounting for 36.4% of the variance in the outcome variable (R² = 0.36; Adjusted R² = 0.34). Diagnostic assessments were performed to ensure the validity of the regression assumptions. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.61, which falls within the acceptable range to indicate an absence of significant auto correlation in the residuals. The normality of the residuals was confirmed by the results of the Omnibus (p = 0.151) and Jarque-Bera tests (p = 0.099), which suggested that the error terms were approximately normally distributed. Furthermore, the multicollinearity diagnostic, including tolerance and variance inflation factor values, was within the established thresholds, indicating that the model was not adversely affected by high correlations between the independent variables. These diagnostics collectively confirm the reliability and stability of the regression results.

After adjusting for the other variables in the model, the regression analysis revealed depression, spiritual needs, sex, and education as significant independent associations with QoL (their 95% confidence intervals did not include zero).

Depression (β = -1.608; p = < 0.001; CI: -2.007, -1.210) was the strongest negative independent association. The narrow confidence interval shows high precision with consistently negative effects on HR-QoL.

Spiritual needs (β = 0.643; p = 0.023; CI: 0.009, 1.196): Greater fulfilment of spiritual needs was associated with improved QoL, suggesting that higher spiritual engagement or expressed spiritual needs corresponded with improved QoL among the participants.

Sex (β = 2.787; p = 0.034; CI: 0.215, 5.359) was significantly positively associated. The confidence interval suggests that being a particular gender (i.e., male, based on the positive coefficient) is associated with higher QoL.

Education (β = 0.765; p = 0.049; CI: 0.004, 1.527): Higher educational attainment is a significant positive independent association.

Discussion

This study assessed health-related quality of life and its associated psychosocial and spiritual factors among older adults in a Nigerian primary care setting to find evidence that could support the development and improvement of holistic geriatric services in primary care settings in Ilorin and other comparable settings in Nigeria and Africa.

HRQOL profile of the participants

The results revealed a moderate level of well-being, with mean overall QoL and domain scores clustering between 53.6 and 62.9. The overall mean score obtained in this study is lower than that reported by Faronbi et al. (66.8 ± 6.91) in southwestern Nigeria [8]. Similarly, the domain mean scores were inconsistent with findings from previous studies in the country. There have been no consistent empirical findings on overall HR-QoL and domain patterns among older adults in previous Nigerian studies are due to demographic differences. Regarding trends in QoL, the psychological domain had the highest transformed mean score (62.9), indicating a level of emotional resilience or adaptation among the elderly individuals in Ilorin. In contrast, the social domain had the lowest score (53.6). This lower social QoL is noteworthy, as traditional Nigerian society is known for its strong extended family ties and support [2]. The lower score may be a reflection of the gradual erosion of these traditional family and social ties with the rising rate of migration of younger family members abroad for greater opportunities [1, 2, 8].

Impact of social and economic factors on the HR-QoL of the participants

Marital status, occupation, and educational level were significantly associated with quality of life at the bivariate level, whereas age, sex, and type of marriage did not significantly affect QoL scores. Compared with unmarried individuals, married individuals had higher physical, social, and overall QoL scores, likely because of the emotional and social support that marriage provides later in life [8, 9]. Higher educational attainment was associated with better physical, psychological, and overall QoL scores, suggesting that education enhances health literacy, socioeconomic opportunities, and access to healthcare, thereby promoting well-being among older adults [9]. Additionally, professionals and retired civil servants reported higher QoL scores than petty traders and small-scale business operators, likely because of greater financial stability and better access to healthcare [9]. These findings align with previous research showing that socioeconomic factors are key determinants of healthy ageing and quality of life [8, 9, 36].

After adjusting for other sociodemographic and clinical variables in the multiple linear regression model, sex, which was not significant in the initial t test, was significantly associated with the adjusted model, suggesting that the relationship between sex and QoL is complex and influenced by other variables. Educational status remained significantly independent of the sociodemographic association of overall QoL, whereas marital status and occupation were not. This suggests that some associations observed at the bivariate level, particularly between marital status and occupation, may have been confounded. The continued significance of education in the adjusted model highlights its important role in promoting well-being among older adults.

The burden of chronic illnesses

A substantial proportion of participants (92.9%) presented with at least one chronic condition. Hypertension (75.7%) and musculoskeletal disorders (58.6%) were the most prevalent. This rate surpasses that reported in previous cross-sectional studies conducted in Nigeria [8, 9, 37, 38], although the morbidity pattern is consistent with the morbidity patterns reported in previous studies in primary care clinics, where hypertension remains a leading cause of geriatric consultations [9, 37, 39].

Despite the high clinical burden, chronic conditions were not statistically associated with quality of life. This phenomenon, known as the ‘disability paradox,’ is especially evident among elderly Nigerians, where cultural factors and community support strongly influence subjective well-being [40]. Cultural influences may have a greater effect on well-being than medical diagnoses alone. This observation is consistent with Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, which proposes that health outcomes are shaped more by an individual’s appraisal of a stressor than by the stressor itself [1, 27–29]. In this context, older adults may interpret chronic illnesses through their spiritual or cultural beliefs, perceiving them as ‘divine tests’ or as part of normal ageing [2, 28]. Strong coping resources, such as spiritual support and family-based care, serve as protective factors. These resources enable older adults to adapt psychologically and maintain high life satisfaction despite physical limitations [1, 29]. These outcomes indicate that interventions targeting illness perception and spiritual coping strategies may better enhance health-related quality of life among older Nigerians than traditional disease-centred care. This confirms the importance of incorporating psychosocial and spiritual support into primary care [2, 41].

The impact of mental health and spirituality

The results of this study revealed that depression, anxiety, and spiritual needs are common among older adults seeking care at primary care clinics. In the bivariate analysis, both depression and anxiety were significantly negatively correlated with overall quality of life, indicating that increasing psychological distress was linked to poorer QoL. However, in the multivariate linear regression model, only depression remained significantly independently associated with QoL, while anxiety lost significance after adjusting for other variables. This strong negative association indicates that psychological distress could be a primary driver of diminished QoL, frequently overshadowing physical ailments [3]. This finding is consistent with recent studies in southwestern Nigeria, which also reported that psychological health is frequently neglected in primary care settings that prioritise physical symptoms [5, 6].

Spiritual needs were not significantly associated with QoL at the correlation level. However, regression analysis showed that higher spiritual needs scores were independently associated with a modest improvement in QoL after controlling for Sociodemographic and clinical variables. These findings suggest that spirituality may play a protective or adaptive role in the well-being of older adults when other influencing factors are considered. Within the Nigerian sociocultural context, spirituality often acts as an important coping mechanism during illness, ageing, and psychosocial stress and may lend its self as a good coping resource [17].

These findings support earlier evidence that mental health and spirituality are essential dimensions of geriatric well-being [2, 17]. These factors should be integrated into comprehensive primary care for older adults. Routine screening for depression, along with the incorporation of psychosocial and spiritually sensitive care, may enhance QoL among the elderly population.

Implications for primary care practice in Nigeria

The findings from this study may have meaningful implications for the care of older adults in primary care settings. The pronounced negative impact of depression on HR-QoL underscores the need for routine mental health assessments among older adults attending primary care clinics. Validated screening instruments for depression and anxiety should be incorporated into regular visits to facilitate early identification and intervention.

The positive association between spiritual needs and quality of life highlights the possible importance of spiritually sensitive care in geriatric healthcare. Primary care providers should use a holistic biopsychosocial-spiritual framework. Key strategies include identifying and addressing patients’ spiritual concerns, obtaining spiritual histories, practising compassionate communication, and referring patients for spiritual care where necessary.

The possible effect of education on quality of life highlights the need for clear health education and effective communication. Older adults with lower levels of education need simplified health information and additional support to understand their health status, follow treatment plans, and engage in self-care.

These findings suggest the need for a multidisciplinary approach to geriatric care. Such care should involve coordinated efforts among physicians, mental health professionals, social workers, caregivers, and, when appropriate, spiritual care providers. Implementing such integrated care models may be essential for enhancing quality of life and thus promoting healthy ageing among older adults in Nigeria.

These findings therefore suggest that to improve QoL for older adults in primary care, clinicians may need to look beyond the biological domain (e.g., chronic disease) and address the psychosocial-spiritual domain by employing a holistic approach to geriatric care, especially within primary care settings.

Strengths and limitations

The instruments employed in this study were validated and demonstrated strong reliability, thereby enhancing their capacity to precisely measure the constructs under investigation. The interviewer-administered approach minimised missing responses and ensured comprehensive data collection, resulting in no participant attrition. Furthermore, the use of research assistants trained in geriatric interviewing skills and standardised questionnaires reduced interviewer-related variability and improved data collection consistency.

However, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the variables examined. Furthermore, the study was conducted at only one site, so the findings cannot be generalised to the wider elderly population in Nigeria or beyond. A larger, multi-centre study is recommended for such generalisation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the health-related quality of life of older adults in primary care may be affected by sociodemographic, psychological, and spiritual factors. These findings highlight the possible need for comprehensive holistic geriatric care in primary healthcare settings.

These findings suggest that addressing older adults’ needs in primary care may extend beyond solely managing physical illnesses. Incorporating psychosocial assessment, mental health services, and spiritual needs into routine primary care may enhance well-being and patient-centred care. This approach is especially relevant in Nigeria, where spirituality often supports coping, resilience, and health perceptions. Future longitudinal and multi-centre studies are recommended to clarify causal relationships and identify effective interventions to improve the quality of life for Nigeria’s growing elderly population.
