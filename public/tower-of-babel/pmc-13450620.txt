Ultrasound-Guided Interscalene Brachial Plexus Block: Single Bolus Injection versus Continuous Catheter Placement in Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair with Implications for Postoperative Rehabilitation – A Prospective Randomized Unblinded Trial
Authors: Renata Beck, Pietro Palumbo, Antonella Cotoia, Giuseppe Maccagnano, Domenico Merlicco, Angelo A Carrideo, Lucia Distaso, Mario Pedone, Emanuele Pio Ricci, Michela Rauseo, Lada Zibar, Gilda Cinnella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examined the effectiveness of the interscalene brachial plexus single-shot block (ISBSS) versus continuous block (ISBC) in managing acute and chronic postsurgical pain (PSP) in patients undergoing upper extremity surgery, and differences between the two groups in rehabilitation.Patients and MethodsPatients who underwent shoulder arthroscopy under locoregional anesthesia received either single-shot (ISBSS group) (n = 42) or continuous perineural catheter (ISBC group) anesthesia (n = 38). The primary outcome was the numeric rating scale (NRS) score related to PSP evaluation. Secondary outcomes included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Score (ASES), range of motion (ROM) in degrees, hospital length of stay, and opioid consumption, all measured postoperatively and during the rehabilitation evaluation.ResultsISBC group exhibited lower median [interquartile range, IQR] NRS score than ISBSS group at postoperative hour 20 (2 [2 to 3] vs 4 [2 to 5]), 24 (2 [2 to 2] vs 4 [1 to 5]), 48 (1 [1 to 2] vs 4 [4 to 5]), 72 (1.5 [1 to 2] vs 5 [4 to 5]) (P < 0.001, resp.); at postoperative day 7 (1 [1 to 2] vs 5 [5 to 6], P < 0.001) and postoperative month 1 (1 [1 to 1] vs 3 [3 to 3], P < 0.001). The ISBC group demonstrated lower opioid consumption, a better ASES score, and higher ROM grades at follow-up month 3 for flexion, abduction, and internal and external rotation (P < 0.001).ConclusionContinuous interscalene brachial plexus catheter infusion may effectively control acute and chronic PSP, facilitating better rehabilitation and healing than single shot injection.

Introduction

Arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tears is a minimally invasive surgical technique.1 This orthopedic procedure may contribute to the onset of postsurgical pain (PSP), while pain can influence compliance during rehabilitation, quality of everyday life, and, thus, surgical outcomes.

In shoulder surgery, the interscalene brachial plexus block (ISB) is the most practiced peripheral nerve block.2–4 This block provides good PSP analgesia, but it is time-limited when used as a single-shot technique.5,6 It can last from 12 to 24 hours after surgery when we use long-lasting local anesthetics. After that period, PSP management can be difficult. Many studies introduce other analgesic methods, such as opioids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), Tylenol ® (acetaminophen), intra-articular shoulder injection, stellate ganglion block, suprascapular nerve block, or, as an alternative, Exparel ® (liposomal bupivacaine) that lasts 72 h after a single dose.7–12 In Europe, this drug is not yet available. Another way to extend the duration of analgesia in the postoperative period is the perineuronal catheter placement.13,14

The literature contains only a few data on the long-term follow-up and the effects of early perioperative and postoperative analgesia on patient rehabilitation.15–17

Our study aimed to compare ISBSS followed by standard intravenous (IV) therapy with opioids and NSAIDs versus ISBC with the perineuronal infusion of local anesthetic plus patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) boluses. Our primary hypothesis was that the ISBC would have better pain control lasting longer than ISBSS. The secondary hypothesis was that the ISBC group would have better compliance during rehabilitation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

Ethical approval for this study (Ethical Committee N° 69/CE/2022) was provided by the Ethical Committee of Foggia University Hospitals, Foggia, Italy (Chairperson Dr. A. Villella) on 28 April 2022, was registered on clinicaltrial.gov (NCT06855381) before data analysis, and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, design, and report followed the recommendations established by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: individuals aged 18 years and older, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score of 1 or 2, a body mass index (BMI) of less than 30 kg/m2, and undergoing shoulder arthroscopy for small to medium rotator cuff tears. Patients were excluded from the study if they presented skin infection on the puncture site, coagulopathy, brachial plexus neuropathy, alcohol or drug abuse, diabetes, severe lung disease, or a history of allergy to one or more study drugs.

The study was performed from May 2022 to May 2024 at the Orthopedic and Anesthesia and Intensive Care Department of University Policlinic Hospital, Foggia, Italy.

An independent research team member generated randomization codes for the ISBSS or ISBC group in a 1:1 ratio using a reproducible web-based system that used the pseudo-random number generator of Wichmann and Hill as modified by McLeod (https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/randomize1). The codes were stored in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, and stapled envelopes.

An anesthesiologist (initials RB), considered an expert in performing interscalene blocks, applied ultrasonographic guided blocks for all the enrolled patients.

Anesthetic Methods

Upon arrival in the operating room, patients were monitored with 3-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), pulse oximetry, and non-invasive blood pressure (BP).

At the beginning of the anesthesia procedure, all patients were administered 1 mg of midazolam, 50 mcg of fentanyl, and 4 mg of ondansetron for nausea and vomiting prophylaxis. We associated the regional block with 1000 mg acetaminophen to provide multimodal PSP control.

The ISBs were performed in an aseptic technique under an ultrasonographic guide (EPIQ Elite, Philips Diagnostic Ultrasound System, Philips Ultrasound, Inc., 22100 Bothell-Everett Hwy, Bothell, WA 98021 USA). The skin was disinfected with chlorhexidine digluconate 2%, the target area was covered with a sterile drape, and the ultrasound probe was dressed with a sterile cover for better infection control.18 An ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block puncture was performed from the lateral to the medial side of the neck. For the ISBSS group, a 22-gauge 50 mm needle (Vygon, Italia Srl – Gruppo Vygon, Viale dell’Industria 60, Padova, Italy) was used. For the ISBC group, a 22-gauge 50 mm SonoPlex needle with an indwelling cannula (E-Cath, according to Tsui, Pajunk GmbH, Medizintechnologie, Karl-Hall-Strasse 1, Geisingen, Germany) was utilized. The needle tip was positioned between the C5 and C6 nerve roots. In both groups, 15–20 mL of Ropivacaine 0.75% were injected with refractory boluses of 5 mL. The needle was extracted immediately after the peripheral nerve block in the ISBC group, and the E-catheter was inserted into the indwelling cannula. Next to the exit of the catheter, on the skin, a protective disk 1.9 cm with Chlorhexidine Gluconate (BioPatch, Ethicon™, Johnson & Johnson Surgical Technologies, Inc.) was inserted. The catheter was not fixed with surgical suture but with the specific Grip-lock® patch for central and peripheral venous catheters. After fixation, the catheter was draped with transparent window dressing (Centurion®, SorbaView®2000, Centurion Medical Products Corp., 100 Centurion Way, Williamston, MI, USA) for better preservation. In the ISBC group, pain management was conducted using the Continuous Ambulatory Delivery Device (CADD Solis®), which allows controlled infusion and/or patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). This device was used for perineural administration of local anesthetics (Ropivacaine 0.2% 6–8 mL h−1, plus PCA with boluses of 4 mL) for pain relief throughout the seven-day follow-up period. We connected the catheter to a mechanical pain pump and monitored its function and location twice daily in our ambulatory pain service. We also replaced the drug container as needed.

In the ISBSS group, pain management involved the IV administration of standard pain therapy drugs. In that way, we ensure that all participants receive effective pain management for their discomfort.

Postoperatively, the following data were recorded: pain was measured with an NRS every 4 h during the first 24 h, at 48 h, 72 h, 7 days, and one month after surgery.

After the block execution, adverse effects, total doses of pain drugs used in the postoperative period, and satisfaction were recorded.

Pain Diaries: Patients were provided with a standardized paper diary and instructed by the pain service team on how to record NRS scores every 4 hours during waking hours for the first 7 days. Shoulder movements in grade were measured with ROM, and shoulder function with ASES score, both before the surgery and at three and six months follow-up (in-person or via e-mail). Evaluation Criteria: ROM scores were assessed by two independent orthopedic residents who were standardized on the measurement techniques using a goniometer for ROM, and other orthopedic residents during follow-up period collected the ASES questionnaires and calculated the score.

Rescue Analgesia: We have clarified the stepwise titration protocol: for any NRS >4 (cut-off), patients received 1000 mg IV acetaminophen; if pain persisted after 30 minutes, 30 mg IV ketorolac was administered, followed by 100 mg IV tramadol as a third-line rescue.

ROM is an orthopedic test for evaluating the range of impairment of the shoulder and is expressed in grades. The ASES scale is a specific assessment tool used to evaluate functional limitations and pain in the shoulder. The ASES score consists of two sub scores: the Activity of Daily Living sub score and the NRS, but it is reported as a single overall score. A higher score indicates better shoulder function.19,20

Orthopedic Techniques

The patients were placed in the lateral position to perform shoulder arthroscopy. All procedures were arthroscopically performed using the single-row repair technique by the same surgeon with the patient positioned in the lateral decubitus. The glenohumeral joint was first evaluated, including the torn cuff, the articular capsule, the biceps tendon, and the subscapularis tendon. The subacromial decompression with acromioplasty was a standard procedure executed with a shaver or electrocautery in all the patients. The footprint of the entire supraspinatus tear was visualized, and the mobility of the rotator cuff was evaluated with a grasper. In all the procedures a 5.5 × 16.3 mm Arthrex Suture Anchor, Corkscrew® FT III with two FiberWire® and one TigerWire® (Arthrex GmbH, Erwin-Hielscher-Strasse 9,81249 München, Germany) was used to repair the tendon on the footprint with a 45° angle between the anchor and the humeral tuberosity.21–23 In both groups, the recruited patients had small C1 and C2 tears of the Southern California Orthopedic Institute (SCOI) classification and were repaired with the suture repair technique with one Achilles tendon.24 Patients with partial thickness tears, large or massive tears (Grade C III and C IV SCOI classification), Grade 3 retraction (Patte classification), or Grade 3 or 4 fatty infiltration (Goutallier classification), were excluded from our study.24–26

Rehabilitation Program

Both groups of patients had their operated shoulder immobilized in a fixed sling for five weeks. Postoperative rehabilitation followed an identical protocol for all participants. Beginning in the first postoperative week, both groups engaged in passive ROM exercises, which continued for eight weeks. On the seventh postoperative day, patients commenced passive flexion of the shoulder joint and pendulum exercises. In the first five weeks after the surgery, an abduction sling was used and only removed for passive-assisted ROM exercises guided by a physiotherapist three times a week. The brace was gradually discontinued during the fourth and fifth weeks after the operation. This increased passive and assisted active ROM, which was properly documented. Regular stretching exercises were implemented after 8 weeks. Patients were cleared for work activity after the 4th month and sports activity after the 6th month after surgery.

Follow-Up

The primary outcome was the comparison of pain severity at each time point between group ISBSS and group ISBC, with the main time point being after 8 h. Secondary outcomes included the comparison of adverse effects (postoperative nausea, vomiting, pruritus, dyspnea, Claude-Bernard Horner Syndrome, dysphonia), PSP analgesic requirements, satisfaction, NRS, ASES, and ROM between the two groups.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size of two independent study groups and statistical power were calculated using a sample size calculator online (https://homepage.univie.ac.at/robin.ristl/samplesize.php?test=ttest). Sample size was calculated from the data of pain score at 24 hours after surgery in our pilot not published study. The sample size required for 85% power at ɑ = 0.05 was estimated to be 36 patients each for the experimental and control group. As 36 patients per group were required, we decided on a total of 40 patients per group, considering a 10% dropout rate.

Because of a relatively small sample size, nonparametric statistics were used. Numeric variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were presented by absolute and relative frequencies. The Mann–Whitney U-test was performed for continuous and ordinal variables to test the differences between the two groups. Chi-square test was performed for differences in categorical variables. A P-value <0.05 was considered significant. All tests were performed using Cloud Software Group, Inc. (2023). Data Science Workbench, version 14. https://www.spotfire.com/.

Discussion

The main results of our study are that in patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with perioperative continuous ISB analgesia with the perineuronal catheter was superior when compared with an ISB single injection of local anesthetic in terms of post-surgical pain and compliance in physical therapy, while patient of ISBC group without pain consistently follows the prescribed treatment plan, including attending appointments, performing home exercises, and making lifestyle changes. Overall, in postoperative period, this group has also reduced the opioid requirement, as well has better performance during rehabilitation. Furthermore, functional outcomes and satisfaction in this group evaluated in follow up of six months was better.

In our opinion, the novelty of our study was that arthroscopic rotator cuff tear repair was practiced completely under locoregional anesthesia, instead of general anesthesia described in other studies.1,12,27–35

Cablay KJ et al found that total IV anesthesia improved post-anesthesia care stay and shortened the total time until discharge compared with inhaled general anesthesia. In their study, they did not practice locoregional analgesia.27

In our study, the perineuronal catheter was inserted in the ISBC group between the roots C5 and C6 for anesthesia and PSP control. Catheter insertion is less common because it requires an expert ultrasound operator and is a time-consuming procedure. The perineuronal catheter was inserted not only for perioperative analgesia, but also to remain in situ for the first week after surgery and connected with a mechanical pump for local anaesthetic administration. Thus, an alternative to this technique may be liposomal bupivacaine that lasts 72 h after a single dose.7–12 However, in Europe, this drug has not been available yet.

Arthroscopic cuff repair is considered a very painful orthopedic procedure. In the literature, extensive data show that the release of inflammatory mediators during surgery is responsible for the onset of acute pain.36 To prevent the release of inflammatory mediators, the American Pain Society guidelines and specific guidelines for cuff repair recommend multimodal PSP management.37,38 Effective management of PSP should begin in the preoperative period and must be tailored to meet the individual needs of each patient. Continuous follow-up assessments and adjustments are essential to ensure effective PSP relief throughout the recovery process. Multimodal analgesic drugs and techniques targeting different mechanisms of action in the peripheral and/or central nervous system provide more effective pain relief when compared with single interventions.37

Ilfeld BM’s review suggested that perineuronal catheters could aid in managing PSP and rehabilitation by reducing postoperative joint inflammation.39 This effect may be attributed to reduced proinflammatory markers present in circulation. Our research indicates that continuous ISB significantly decreases the occurrence of acute and chronic PSP. Furthermore, the benefits associated with this intervention persisted even after the removal of the catheter. These findings also support Ilfeld BM’s in his updated review.40

We found the median difference in cumulative IV opioid (tramadol hydrochloride) consumption between groups within 24 hours post-operatively. The ISBC group did not use any opioids but had continuous catheter infusion of local anesthetics. Also, Vorobeichik L et al in their recently reported meta-analysis of 15 trials, which included 793 patients, confirmed the great consumption of opioids after rotator cuff repair.28

In addition, Hofmann-Kiefer et al29 found that patient-controlled intrascalene block compared with IV administration of opioids improved PSP as well as postoperative nausea and vomiting, but not early rehabilitation of the shoulder joint. Instead, we found significant differences in the ROM of the shoulder after three months of surgery and rehabilitation between the two groups (flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation). Perhaps that difference in findings was due to the long-term follow-up of our patients that Hafann-Kiefer did not perform. Furthermore, their rehabilitation protocol differed from ours.

Rhyner P et al,30 in their recent trial, confirmed that continuous brachial plexus block reduced opioid consumption, pain scores, postoperative nausea, vomiting, and PSP chronic pain. This trial did not test shoulder functional outcomes.

Uno T et al1 confirmed that PSP and ROM were better with a continuous interscalene plexus brachialis catheter than with a single-shot drug administration. The main distinction between that study and ours was that their interscalene block was performed after the surgery, and the surgery was performed under general anesthesia with propofol, sevoflurane/desflurane, and remifentanil. Our experience brings us to perform regional blocks as anesthesia for the surgery, and the patients were awake with bland sedation during the surgery. Locoregional anesthesia modality permitted a rapid turnover of patients in the operating room, saved time, avoided adverse effects of general anesthesia, and fast hospital loss, and allowed better PSP control and compliance during rehabilitation.

Huettner D et al,31 in their study on long-term opioid use patients, confirmed that anesthesiologists might consider using nerve blocks for that patient population to facilitate opioid-sparing and minimize the need of opioids and made more comfortable postoperative recovery. They suggested ISBC because of the duration and decrease postoperative pain levels. Alternatively, they proposed liposomal bupivacaine; however, recent research suggested that its clinical advantages might not be significant.41,42

Schoenherr JW et al43 showed that patients receiving either a single-injection interscalene liposomal bupivacaine or an ISBC experienced an improved quality of recovery 48 hours after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. However, in our opinion, a longer follow-up period is essential to properly evaluate long-term pain outcomes, tissue healing, and functional recovery. For this reason, we maintained the ISBC in situ for one week and followed up our patients throughout a six-month rehabilitation period. Furthermore, since long-lasting liposomal bupivacaine is currently unavailable in Europe, we were unable to investigate its specific effects on PSP and long-term rehabilitation in our cohort.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. We could not conduct the study in a blinded mode due to the organization and different procedural techniques.

We agree that the continuous infusion catheter (the “placebo effect” of the device) may have influenced subjective reporting of pain scores. Additionally, pain measurement is inherently subjective, whether assessed by nurses, our residents, or reported by patients at home. The home pain diary relies on patient compliance, and as anticipated, some patients were lost to follow-up.

We also acknowledge the limitation that no prior study used ISB as anesthesia only for surgery, but in combination with general anesthesia, combined with ISB as analgesia before or after surgery. For this reason, we used our pilot study as a reference to calculate the sample size.

Finally, this study is a small, single-center, prospective, randomized, and unblinded trial (n = 80). The lack of blinding during rehabilitation may have introduced performance bias, as patient expectations and therapist awareness of the analgesic technique could have influenced early functional outcomes. Moreover, although early differences favored the ISBC group, the absence of significant differences at six months suggests that these benefits are early rather than permanent, consistent with the expected plateau in rotator cuff healing.

Future larger studies are needed to confirm these findings and to better evaluate the long-term impact of perineuronal catheters on pain, healing, and functional recovery.

Conclusion

ISBC analgesia in the postoperative period with a perineuronal catheter was superior to a single injection of local anesthetic, and in those patients, during the recovery period, the rehabilitation was easier and less painful. Overall, the opioid requirement and hospital length of stay in the ISBC group were reduced. These findings can be interesting in reducing opioid consumption during acute and chronic PSP therapy.

The functional outcomes and ROM evaluated at 3 months were better in the ISBC group. These early advantages were not sustained at the six-month follow-up, when ASES scores and ROM were comparable between groups.

The synergy between the orthopedic surgeon and anesthesiologist remains essential for optimizing perioperative care and supporting functional recovery.

In our experience, the choice of anesthetic technique contributed to improved rehabilitation compliance during the early postoperative period; however, the overall conclusions must be interpreted with caution given the study’s small sample size, single-center design, and lack of blinding.
