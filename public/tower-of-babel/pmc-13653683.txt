The pill bottle on the counter
Authors: Ethan Bell
Published: 2026

I was 10 years old, sitting in English class, writing about my aunt. The story began in the middle, with a hike we took the summer before her accident. She was the kind of person who talked more going uphill: determined, energetic. Halfway through my assignment, I couldn’t see the page anymore. My aunt had died three months earlier from a self-administered overdose involving opioids, originally prescribed after a car accident. I remember, her polytrauma with infections, her surgeons’ advice to amputate, the never-ending pill bottles on our counter no one talked about. They sat there, permanent fixtures beside the coffee maker, where the mail used to land. My grandmother wiped the counter around them. My uncle set his keys down next to them. Toward the end, she’d told me she was tired of fighting, but 10-year-old me hadn’t understood what she meant.

I knew the kitchen light came on in the night. I would hear the floor shift, the kettle boiling, the pill bottle opening, the small sounds of a person trying to be soundless in a house that was already asleep. I didn’t understand it was pain from her accident that kept her up.

When she died, all I knew was that I’d lost someone I’d loved, my pain. I did not yet know what to name what happened. No one else was naming it either. The not-naming was in the air of the house; we breathed it.

My aunt died from an opioid overdose. That sentence is something I can write now. At 10, I could not have. The adults around me had decided, with the silent unanimity families sometimes achieve, that I would not be told what had taken her life.

Now I understand that when she told me she was tired of fighting, she meant she did not want to keep living.

Ten years later, I carry what my aunt said into the clinic where I work as a rehabilitation technician. I now see that the real question in chronic pain is often not what a person’s surgical options are, but what happens to them when their pain has nowhere left to go.

Marcus had been an amputee for 11 months following a motorcycle accident when I met him in the rehabilitation gym. He moved through the parallel bars with the careful attention of someone who has learned to test things before trusting them. His prosthetist had adjusted the socket twice that week. The fit still wasn’t right. He would stop mid-bar, shift his weight, wait for something to settle.

He told me the worst part of life after amputation wasn’t the loss of his leg. It was his phantom pain, spiking at two in the morning, when no change of position would help. When he told me he had stopped sleeping in the bedroom because he didn’t want to wake his wife, I remembered my aunt’s nighttime movements in the kitchen.

I had accessed his chart that morning. A pain management consult had been ordered six weeks earlier and was still unscheduled. Not lost or misrouted, just sitting in the queue behind everything else the clinic was carrying that month.

As a rehabilitation technician, I can hand a patient a gait belt and spot his transfers. I cannot chart, order, or refer. The physical therapist can write a note; she cannot schedule a consult in a clinic that isn’t hers. I told her what Marcus had said, and she added it to her documentation. The referral stayed where it was, as it had for my aunt.

I continued to think about my aunt that afternoon on my drive home. Memories of her often surface when I am least expecting it. Somewhere along the road, the thing sitting still in my chest for so long began to loosen.

I had been carrying a belief about her care for 10 years without examining it. I’d believed doctors should have amputated. I’d never said it out loud. I’m not sure I ever admitted it. Amputation had been the solution my child mind had quietly settled on, because amputation was a thing that could have been done but wasn’t. A 10-year-old needs a hinge on which his grief can swing.

Marcus had undergone amputation, but still, he could not sleep at night. I understood that pain could persist even after amputation: it stayed with Marcus after amputation, just as it had stayed with my aunt without amputation. What they both needed was someone to receive and heal their pain.

I had been angry at my aunt for 10 years. I want to say that again because it took me most of those 10 years to say it once. I had been angry the way a child is with someone who leaves them, when the actual causes are too much for them to understand. You are not supposed to be angry at someone who has died of a disease, but you cannot be angry at a pill bottle. You cannot be angry at a kitchen. You can be angry at the person who leaves.

On the drive home, I realized that my anger was never really meant for her. Anger was the shape my helplessness took, the helplessness that remains with me as a professional. I am 20 years old now, in scrubs, but in many ways, I am still standing in that kitchen, looking at the bottle no one talked about.

Understanding that did not resolve the feeling, or even forgive it, but it helps guide my work. I’m careful what I think of surgical decisions I’m not involved with. Pain management after limb loss is its own discipline, with no outcomes guaranteed. Marcus is evidence of that.

I fear my aunt’s pill bottle was refilled without anyone ever asking what happens when the pain has nowhere left to go.

The page before me had blurred a decade ago as I tried to write about my aunt because language does that. It closes the distance between what you know and what you do not yet understand. I have been trying to close that distance in rooms where someone’s body is the question. The answer depends on who is willing to stay with them in their pain.
