Association between mental health status and patient satisfaction: a cross-sectional study in three Damascus hospitals, Syria
Authors: Rawan Daboul, MHD Ghazi Aboulkher, Jamal Ataya, Mohammad Hamdi, Rustum Mackieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatient satisfaction is an aspect of healthcare systems, impacting both mental and physical health outcomes. People experiencing anxiety and depression significantly contribute to patient satisfaction complexity, affecting evaluations of healthcare services. Understanding these challenges is essential for tailored care. By examining these dynamics, we aim to enhance healthcare services to meet diverse patient needs.MethodsThis study investigated the relationship between chronic illness, mental health and patient satisfaction in hospital environments. The cross-sectional design included 678 patients with chronic illnesses from Damascus hospitals. We used a meticulously designed paper questionnaire, incorporating reliable measures Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) and Satisfaction with the Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS). Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews, ensuring confidentiality and ethical compliance.ResultsIn our investigation of 663 participants, we found diverse age distribution and balanced body mass index. Simple linear regression revealed that depression levels significantly contributed to increased anxiety. PHQ-Anxiety (PHQ-A) explained 41.6% of PHQ-Depression variance. Additionally, heightened depression levels were associated with decreased satisfaction (SAPS). Education level impacted anxiety, with those without a secondary certificate exhibiting higher PHQ-A levels.ConclusionsAnxiety and depression emerged as key factors influencing patient evaluations of healthcare services. By recognising these complexities, healthcare providers can tailor their services to better meet the diverse needs of patients.Keywords: Public Health, Mental Health, Public Health Practice

Introduction

The significance of patients’ views on healthcare systems remains evident for care system managers.1 Addressing patient satisfaction has become crucial due to its intricate connection with the mental and physical health outcomes of patients.2 This interplay between patient experiences and healthcare quality highlights the need for a comprehensive exploration of factors influencing patient satisfaction. Patient satisfaction, a complex concept, is influenced by various factors, with mental health conditions like anxiety and depression being pivotal contributors.3 These conditions add complexity to evaluating healthcare services, often leading to heightened dissatisfaction.4 Understanding the challenges posed by anxiety and depression is crucial for tailoring healthcare services to individuals with these conditions. For instance, anxiety patients often exhibit heightened nervousness, impatience and difficulty concentrating,5 6 amplifying the perceived severity of their condition. Individuals facing depression encounter obstacles in participating in and managing various aspects of their lives, marked by less constructive thinking. The interplay of these factors complicates mental health condition management and has implications for healthcare service evaluation. The impact of anxiety and depression symptoms on patient satisfaction is more pronounced during hospitalisation. Hospitalisation represents a stressful experience that most individuals will encounter during their lifetime. This experience can significantly exacerbate anxiety and depression symptoms, as clinical evidence demonstrates that hospitalisation impairs patients’ coping mechanisms and amplifies emotional distress.7 Therefore, our research examined how anxiety and depression symptoms influence patient satisfaction levels with chronic illnesses during hospitalisation, while accounting for relevant social and demographic factors. Understanding the specific impact of anxiety and depression on patient satisfaction can inform targeted interventions to improve healthcare experiences for this vulnerable group.

Our exploration draws on a diverse range of scholarly works, including the aforementioned sources. These references form a robust foundation for our investigation into the intersection of mental health, demographic factors and patient satisfaction in healthcare services. While a previous study provided initial insights in one city, further evidence across multiple major urban centres is needed to confirm these findings.8 This study aimed to contribute valuable insights guiding efforts to enhance and tailor healthcare services to the diverse and evolving needs of patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design and was conducted over 1 month (June 2024) in eight major hospitals in Damascus, Syria (Al-Mujtahid, Al-Muwasat, Al-Biruni, Heart Surgery Hospital, Mouwasat Al-Biruni, Al-Assad University Hospital, Ibn Al-Nafis, and Children’s University Hospital). The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided as an online supplemental file.

Study population

The study sample consisted of 678 adult patients diagnosed with one or more chronic illnesses, defined as conditions lasting at least 1 year that required ongoing medical attention or limited activities of daily living. Participants were either under long-term follow-up or admitted for related complications. The study did not exclusively recruit patients with a pre-existing diagnosis of anxiety or depression; rather, all eligible chronically ill patients were included to assess the prevalence of these symptoms within this population. Inclusion criteria were a willingness to provide informed consent and the ability to understand and respond to the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included refusal or inability to participate, severe cognitive impairment, active treatment for severe psychiatric conditions and comorbid medical conditions that could affect psychiatric status.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained and specialised interviewers using a meticulously designed, prevalidated paper questionnaire. The questionnaire was structured to present demographic questions (eg, age, sex, education, type and duration of chronic illness) first, followed by the standardised instruments. The primary tools used were:

The Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4): This ultra-brief, composite scale was used to screen for anxiety and depression symptoms. It consists of two subscales: the GAD-2 for anxiety and the PHQ-2 for depression. Participants rated how often they were bothered by four problems over the last 2 weeks on a scale from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’). A score of ≥3 on either subscale was considered a positive screen. The PHQ-4 is a validated tool with strong psychometric properties.9

The Short Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS): The study used a prevalidated Arabic version of the SAPS to measure patient satisfaction. It evaluates fundamental elements of the patient experience (eg, satisfaction with treatment, clinician care, decision-making) on a 5-point response scale. The SAPS is a tool that has demonstrated excellent reliability and validity in various clinical settings.10 11 The questionnaire took approximately 5 min to complete. To ensure confidentiality, all responses were anonymised using unique identification codes, with no personal identifiers recorded on the data forms. Following the interviews, data from the paper forms were transcribed into a secure digital database for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Following data collection, responses were transcribed into a secure database and analysed using SPSS software (V.28.0). The significance level (alpha) was set at p<0.05.

Primary and secondary outcomes: The primary outcomes were the prevalence/severity of anxiety and depression symptoms (PHQ-4) and the level of patient satisfaction (SAPS). A secondary outcome was the statistical association between mental health symptoms and satisfaction levels.

Analytical methods: Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, SDs) were used to summarise participant characteristics and outcome scores. A simple linear regression was performed to investigate the relationship between depression (PHQ-2) and anxiety (GAD-2) scores. An independent samples t-test was used to compare anxiety levels between participants with and without a secondary education certificate. Only fully completed questionnaires were included in the final analysis; any with missing data on the PHQ-4 or SAPS were excluded.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 678 patients were initially approached for the study. However, 15 participants were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete responses on the PHQ-4 or SAPS questionnaires. This resulted in a final sample of 663 participants whose data were included in the statistical analysis. Body mass index was collected as a general objective indicator of physical health status, which is a relevant factor when studying populations with chronic illnesses. It ranged from 12.49 to 45.13, with a mean value of 24.31 and an SD=of 4.73. And as for age, the mean value was 48.64, between 7 and 87 years old and with an SD=17.31. Financial status was determined based on participant’s self-report in response to a single question about their perceived household income relative to their basic needs. The categories were dichotomised into ‘poor’ and ‘moderate or better’ to simplify the analysis and identify potential health disparities based on significant financial strain.

Multiple relationships between satisfaction and health

To investigate the relationship between anxiety and depression subscales of the PHQ-4 (henceforth referred to as PHQ-A and PHQ-D, respectively), we conducted a simple linear regression. The average PHQ-A score within the sample was 3.03 (SD=2.13), and the average PHQ-D score was 2.67 (SD=1.99). We conducted a simple linear regression. The average PHQ-A level within the sample was 3.03 (SD=2.13), and the average PHQ-D level was 2.67 (SD=1.99). The linear regression analysis revealed a statistically significant model (F (1,661)=470.332, p<0.05), with an adjusted R² of 0.416. This finding suggests that PHQ-D accounts for approximately 41.6% of the variance in PHQ-A among the sampled individuals. Notably, the depression level significantly contributed to an increase in the anxiety level in our study, as indicated by β=0.45, t=21.69, p<0.001.

The linear regression analysis revealed a statistically significant model (F (1,661)=9.03, p<0.05), with an adjusted R² of 0.13. Educational level as a prominent factor

In our study, we observed no significant associations between the independent variables and the dependent variables, except for education level. The participants were categorised into two groups based on their educational attainment: those with a secondary education certificate and those without. Consequently, when an independent t-test was applied, we identified a notable disparity in PHQ-A levels between those without a secondary education certificate (M=3.20, SD=2.10) and those with a secondary education certificate (M=2.73, SD=2.15), (t (661)=2.738, p=0.006>0.05). This suggests that individuals without a secondary education certificate exhibit higher levels of anxiety compared with those with such certification.

Discussion

Demographic factors and satisfaction

Understanding the role of demographic characteristics in shaping patient satisfaction has been the subject of extensive investigation, though results across studies remain inconsistent. In our study, we explored the relationship between several demographic factors—including age, gender, education level, socioeconomic status and place of residence—and patient satisfaction, as well as their potential associations with anxiety and depression. The nearly equal gender distribution (51% male, 49% female) in our sample is a notable strength, reducing the risk of gender bias and enhancing the generalisability of our overall findings to the broader population of chronically ill patients. While no significant gender-based differences were analysed as a primary focus of this study, this balance ensures that the results are not disproportionately influenced by the experiences of a single gender.

Despite the diversity of the sample, we did not find significant associations between these demographic variables and overall patient satisfaction. This aligns with previous findings by Bair et al, who reported no significant effect of income, education or marital status on satisfaction outcomes.12 Similarly, Herrera-Espiñeira et al found no association between patient age and dissatisfaction with communication from medical staff,13 though other studies have reported opposite trends. For example, Elgendy et al observed higher satisfaction among patients over 44 years old.14 Some studies interpret this by suggesting that older patients may be more accepting of hospital routines, more compliant with treatment plans and more respectful towards medical staff, while younger patients may exhibit more resistance or dissatisfaction due to perceived lack of autonomy. 15 16Such discrepancies in the literature suggest that demographic effects may be context-dependent and influenced by cultural or systemic variables.

However, one demographic factor stood out in our analysis: education level was significantly associated with anxiety. Patients who had not completed secondary education exhibited the highest levels of anxiety symptoms. This finding may reflect the influence of health literacy—individuals with limited education may struggle to understand medical information, which can lead to fear, uncertainty and heightened emotional distress. This interpretation is consistent with the results of Ataya et al, who reported a strong link between low education and lack of medical awareness in the Syrian population.17

Interestingly, while socioeconomic status did not show a direct relationship with satisfaction in our data, 40% of our sample identified as poor. This suggests that economic hardship may exert an indirect influence on satisfaction, particularly through its interaction with psychological variables such as depression and anxiety. Previous reviews have noted that dissatisfaction often emerges not from general dissatisfaction, but from specific unmet needs—such as financial insecurity, limited access to care or poor communication with healthcare providers.18

The lack of strong associations between most demographic factors and satisfaction in our results may also suggest that mental health symptoms (particularly depression) play a more dominant role in shaping patients’ perceptions than their background characteristics. This supports the growing perspective that while demographic factors provide context, psychological well-being is often the primary determinant of patient satisfaction.

Mental health findings

Mental health emerged as a central factor influencing patient satisfaction in our study, aligning with prior literature. Elgendy et al noted that inpatient satisfaction is significantly associated with lower severity of mental disorders,14 while both Herrera-Espiñeira and Bair confirmed the role of anxiety and depression, respectively, in shaping patients’ perceptions of care.12 13 This convergence of evidence highlights the need to examine not only the presence of psychological symptoms but also their relative contribution to satisfaction.

Our findings revealed a strong inverse relationship between both anxiety and depression and patient satisfaction. Specifically, as anxiety and depression scores increased, satisfaction scores significantly decreased. However, depression demonstrated a greater impact, accounting for 39% of the variance in satisfaction scores (SAPS), compared with 13% explained by anxiety. This suggests that depressive symptoms may influence how patients interpret their care more profoundly than anxious symptoms, potentially due to the cognitive and emotional disengagement often seen in depression.

These findings are consistent with results from Kavalnienė et al,18 who also found that depression had a stronger predictive value for satisfaction than anxiety. Clinically, this underscores the importance of systematically screening for depression in hospital settings, particularly among patients with chronic illnesses—who may silently endure psychological distress that significantly alters their perception of care quality.

Depression–anxiety interaction

In addition to examining their individual effects on satisfaction, our study also found a significant association between depression and anxiety scores. Depression (PHQ-D) explained 41.6% of the variance in anxiety (PHQ-A), indicating a substantial overlap. This finding reflects the shared etiological factors and comorbid nature of the two conditions, a pattern previously noted in several psychological and psychiatric studies.19 20

While some literature suggests anxiety may precede depression, other studies—including one from the US—support the reverse direction, where increased depressive symptoms predict higher anxiety levels.21 Our results support the latter, suggesting that depression may be the driving psychological state in our sample, influencing both satisfaction and secondary emotional responses like anxiety.

Syrian contextual factors

The context of Syria, shaped by over a decade of war and economic hardship, provides important background for interpreting our findings. The protracted conflict has contributed to widespread psychological trauma, economic insecurity and healthcare system strains —all of which may influence mental health and satisfaction outcomes. In our sample, 40% of patients identified as economically disadvantaged, and 63.2% had low or no formal education—factors previously associated with both increased psychological distress and lower satisfaction.

Furthermore, this study focused on patients with chronic illnesses, who are known to have higher vulnerability to mental health conditions due to ongoing physical symptoms, frequent hospital visits and diminished quality of life. The combination of chronic physical illness and unaddressed psychological needs may compound dissatisfaction and perceived inadequacy of care.

We also hypothesise that gaps in health literacy—exacerbated by educational disruptions during the war—may have contributed to patient dissatisfaction. Limited understanding of medical procedures may foster fear or mistrust, especially among patients with low education levels. This aligns with previous research in Syria highlighting the impact of knowledge and education on health-related perceptions and behaviour.22

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality; we can identify strong associations between depression and satisfaction, but we cannot determine whether depression leads to lower satisfaction or if poor healthcare experiences exacerbate depressive symptoms. Second, our findings are based on self-report measures (PHQ-4 and SAPS), which may be subject to social desirability or recall bias. Third, the study’s geographic scope was limited to public and university hospitals within Damascus. While this provides valuable insight into the Syrian public healthcare system, the results may not be generalisable to private hospitals or rural regions outside the capital. Finally, the recruitment of chronically ill patients means the findings may not apply to patients seeking acute or emergency care.

Conclusions

This study highlights the significant role of mental health, particularly depression, in shaping patient satisfaction among chronically ill individuals in Damascus hospitals. Addressing depression through integrated mental health services could be a strategic approach to enhancing patient experiences in postconflict healthcare settings.
