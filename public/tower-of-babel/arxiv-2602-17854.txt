On the Evaluation Protocol of “Gesture Recognition for UAV-based Rescue Operation based on Deep Learning”: A Subject-Independence Perspective
Domonkos Varga

Abstract
This paper presents a methodological analysis of the gesture-recognition approach proposed by Liu and Szirányi, with a particular focus on the validity of their evaluation protocol. We show that the reported near-perfect accuracy metrics result from a frame-level random train–test split that inevitably mixes samples from the same subjects across both sets, causing severe data leakage. By examining the published confusion matrix, learning curves, and dataset construction, we demonstrate that the evaluation does not measure generalization to unseen individuals. Our findings underscore the importance of subject-independent data partitioning in vision-based gesture-recognition research, especially for applications –— such as UAV–human interaction -—- that require reliable recognition of gestures performed by previously unseen people.

In recent years, deep learning–based approaches coupled with

advances have motivated the development of action-recognition
modules tailored specifically for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)
rescue scenarios, where rapid and reliable interpretation of
human motions may be critical.

Despite this growing interest, a consistent methodological
challenge persists across much of the literature: the lack of
subject-independent evaluation protocols. Numerous published
systems report extremely high accuracy figures while relying on
training and testing splits that reuse the same participants.
Although such evaluation approaches may demonstrate that a
classifier can recognize particular performances of gestures,
they do not provide evidence that the system can generalize beyond
the individuals included in the dataset. In action-recognition
research, this issue is well documented. Models trained and
tested on the same subjects often learn idiosyncratic traits
— such as body proportions, habitual motion patterns, or
performer-specific biases –— instead of learning the underlying
gesture dynamics. As a result, reported accuracies substantially
overestimate the true performance that would be obtained in
real-world deployments.

This methodological pitfall is especially problematic in UAV-based
rescue applications. When deployed outdoors, a UAV must interpret
gestures from unknown people under varied lighting, clothing,
body shapes, and motion styles. A model that succeeds only on
the subjects it has previously seen provides limited practical
value. Therefore, subject-independent experimental design is not
an optional refinement but a fundamental prerequisite for
demonstrating the validity and applicability of
gesture-recognition systems intended for public-safety use.

The present work examines this methodological problem in detail.
We analyze a recently published UAV-based gesture
recognition system
[
19
]
whose dataset
is constructed from only six individuals,
and whose training and testing procedures rely on random
splits over the same set of subjects. We argue that such
evaluation cannot support claims of generalizable human action
recognition. By highlighting this issue and illustrating its
implications, our aim is to promote more rigorous experimental
standards and to support future research in developing
gesture-recognition systems that perform reliably on unseen
human subjects in real rescue environments.

2.2
Data leakage in machine learning research

Data leakage is a well-documented methodological pitfall in
machine learning research, arising when information from
outside the training distribution is inadvertently
incorporated into the model during training
[
9
]
.
This typically
leads to overly optimistic performance metrics that do not
reflect the true generalization ability of the system. Prior
studies have identified multiple recurring forms of leakage,
including accidental duplication of samples across splits,
temporal leakage in sequential data, and—most relevant for
human-centered recognition tasks—subject leakage, where the
same individuals appear in both training and test
sets
[
5
]
. Such
issues often remain unnoticed because they do not necessarily
produce visible errors, but instead manifest as unusually
high accuracy, overly smooth learning curves, or a lack of
class confusions.
Kapoor and Narayanan
[
16
]
examined how data
leakage
contributes to widespread reproducibility issues across
scientific fields that apply machine
learning. Based on a review of 22 studies covering 17 disciplines,
the authors found data
leakage in at least 294 papers, often leading to overly optimistic
results. They introduced a
taxonomy of eight types of leakage (i.e., [L1] a lack of a clean
separation of training and
test datasets, [L1.1] no test set, [L1.2] pre-processing on
training and test sets, [L1.3] feature
selection on training and test sets, [L1.4] duplicates in datasets,
[L2] model use features that
are not legitimate, [L3] a test set that is not drawn
from the distribution of scientific interest,
[L3.1] temporal leakage, [L3.2] non-independence between
training and test samples, and
[L3.3] a sampling bias in the test distribution) and propose
model info sheets as a practical
tool for researchers to detect and prevent such issues.

The authors implemented the system using Python programming

4
Detected data leakage in the evaluation
protocol of Liu & Szirányi

The first indication that the evaluation in the target
study
[
19
]
required closer scrutiny was
the unusually high performance reported across all metrics.
The paper claims gesture-recognition accuracies exceeding 99% on
both the training and test sets, accompanied by a confusion
matrix that is almost perfectly diagonal. Such results are
exceptional to the point of being unprecedented: even
state-of-the-art action-recognition models evaluated on large,
diverse benchmarks rarely achieve comparable performance,
especially when relying solely on 2D pose features. The
near-perfect classification consistency therefore suggested
that the model might be benefiting from unintended information
leakage, prompting a detailed investigation into the
dataset structure and evaluation protocol.

A closer inspection of the confusion matrix published in the
original paper
[
19
]
(reproduced in Figure

2
) reveals several highly suspicious
patterns. The matrix is almost perfectly diagonal, with virtually
all samples classified correctly and only negligible
off-diagonal entries. Such uniform near-perfection is
extremely unusual in human gesture-recognition tasks,
particularly when using 2D pose features, which are inherently
noisy and sensitive to viewpoint variation. Even more striking
is the fact that every gesture class appears to achieve accuracy
extremely close to 100%, including dynamic gestures such as
“Attention” and “Cancel,” which normally exhibit greater
intra-class variability and temporal inconsistency. The
dataset contains participants performing gestures with
different body sizes, poses, and motion styles, yet the
confusion matrix shows no signs of class overlap, hesitation
frames, transitional poses, or borderline
misclassifications—phenomena that are universally
observed in real-world action-recognition experiments.
The complete absence of such effects strongly suggests that
the classifier has learned performer-specific cues rather
than gesture dynamics, consistent with a scenario in which the
same individuals appear in both the training and test sets.
This pattern provides further evidence of data leakage and
supports the conclusion that the evaluation protocol does
not measure true gesture-recognition generalization.

The learning curves — presented in Figures 5 and 6 in the original
paper
[
19
]
and reproduced in
Figure

exhibit several
irregularities that strongly suggest a non-independent
evaluation protocol. Both the training and test accuracy
curves rise in near-perfect synchrony, eventually exceeding

pose-based gesture-recognition task with substantial
intra-class variability. Even more suspicious is the fact that

characteristic of genuine generalization, especially in a
small dataset involving only six subjects, where deep models
typically overfit rapidly. Instead, this inverted relationship
between training and testing metrics is commonly observed when
the two sets are not truly independent, most often due to
subject overlap caused by random splitting of the same
individuals’ samples. The overall smoothness, the lack of
noise or divergence between curves, and the counterintuitive
superiority of test performance thus collectively indicate that
the model was evaluated on data statistically indistinguishable
from the training distribution—providing further evidence of
data leakage.
