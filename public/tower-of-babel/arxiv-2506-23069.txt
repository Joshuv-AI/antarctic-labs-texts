Simultaneous Sieve Estimation and Inference for Time-Varying Nonlinear Time Series Regression
Xiucai Ding, Zhou Zhou

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate time-varying nonlinear time series regression for a broad class of locally stationary time series. First, we propose sieve nonparametric estimators for the time-varying regression functions that achieve uniform consistency. Second, we develop a unified simultaneous inferential theory to conduct both structural and exact form tests on these functions. Additionally, we introduce a multiplier bootstrap procedure for practical implementation. Our methodology and theory require only mild assumptions on the regression functions, allow for unbounded domain support, and effectively address the issue of identifiability for practical interpretation. Technically, we establish sieve approximation theory for 2-D functions in unbounded domains, prove two Gaussian approximation results for affine forms of high-dimensional locally stationary time series, and calculate critical values for the maxima of the Gaussian random field arising from locally stationary time series, which may be of independent interest. Numerical simulations and two data analyses support our results, and we have developed an 𝚁 \mathtt{R} package, 𝚂𝙸𝙼𝚕𝚎 \mathtt{SIMle} , to facilitate implementation.

Introduction

Understanding the complex functional form of a relationship between different variables is one of the main aims in modern data sciences. However, achieving this understanding presents multifaceted challenges in the presence of massive data sets. First, variables often interact in nonlinear, intricate ways that might not conform to conventional models and the underlying theory does not narrow down a specific functional or parametric form for the relationship. Second, the observations usually span long time horizons. Consequently, the relationship is unlikely to remain stable so that the non-stationary relationships between variables evolve, fluctuate, or exhibit different patterns over distinct time periods.

To address these issues, flexible and time-varying regression models are needed. In this paper, we
consider the general time-varying nonlinear time series regression model that

\displaystyle Y_{i,n}=m_{0}(t_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})+\epsilon_{i,n},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n,

(1.1)

where
r>0
is some given fixed integer. Here
\{X_{j,i,n}\}
are the covariates which can be locally stationary and depend on each other,
t_{i}=i/n,
and

\{\epsilon_{i,n}\}
is a general locally stationary time series whose covariance depend on both time and the covariates (see Assumption
2.1
) and satisfies that
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i,n}|\{X_{j,i,n}\}_{1\leq j\leq r})=0.
Moreover,
m_{0}
is the time-varying trend, and
m_{j},1\leq j\leq r,
are some smooth functions that map the time and covariates to the conditional mean.

To ensure the identifiability of the components
m_{j}(\cdot,\cdot)
when
r>1
, we follow the tradition from additive models with i.i.d. inputs, as in
[
26
]
, and without loss of generality, assume that

\mathbb{E}\left(m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})\right)=0,\ 1\leq j\leq r,\ 1\leq i\leq n.

(1.2)

The model (
1.1
) provides a more flexible and general framework and a wide range of models for time-varying locally stationary time series regression can fit into it. In fact, it has been used in the literature, particularly when
r=1
, where identifiability is not an issue, for instance, see
[
45
,
64
,
70
,
71
,
80
]
.
We point out that (
1.1
) can be regarded as a discretized version of the Langevin equation
[
17
]

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}(t,X_{j,t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{1,t},\cdots,X_{r,t})\mathrm{d}M(t),

where
M(t)
is some martingale. In what follows, we summarize related results from the literature in Section
1.1
and provide an overview of our findings, highlighting the novel contributions, in Section
1.2
.

1.1
Some related results

In this subsection, we summarize some related results. To the best of our awareness, few results have been established under the general model setup (
1.1
). Most of the existing literature focuses on pointwise results based on kernel estimation, typically assuming
r=1
, which avoids identifiability issues, allowing
m_{0}
to be combined with
m_{1}
.
In
[
70
]
, under classical strong mixing conditions and using locally stationary processes, the author proposed a Nadaraya-Watson (NW) type estimator utilizing a product kernel. Additionally, the author established convergence rates for this estimation on any compact set and demonstrated the pointwise normality of the estimators. These results and ideas were later applied to study structural changes in
[
71
]
. More recently, in
[
80
]
, the authors advanced kernel estimation theory under a more general locally stationary setting using the physical representation. They also applied their findings to model selection problems.

On the other hand, we note that (
1.1
) has also been studied in the literature under certain structural assumptions. The existing research in this area can be roughly divided into three categories.

•

Time-independent additive models, i.e.,
m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})=m_{j}(X_{j,i,n}),
for all
1\leq j\leq r.
In this setting, the model has been extensively studied under the assumption that the samples are either i.i.d. or from a stationary time series. Especially, the convergence rate and pointwise normality of kernel based estimators have been provided in the literature; see
[
26
,
27
,
37
,
39
,
47
,
49
,
50
,
73
,
77
,
82
]
among others, and the convergence of sieve least square estimators and their pointwise asymptotic normality has also been investigated under various assumptions; see
[
1
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
38
]
among others.

•

Time-varying coefficient models, i.e.,
m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})=f_{j}(t_{i})X_{j,i,n},
for all
1\leq j\leq r.
In this setting, the kernel estimators and simultaneous inference have been studied for the time-varying coefficients
f_{j}(\cdot),1\leq j\leq r
, see
[
29
,
43
,
56
,
84
,
86
]
among others. Additionally, we mention a recent work
[
45
]
focusing on conditional heteroscedastic (CH) models, where the authors conducted statistical inference for the time-varying parameters by analyzing the negative conditional Gaussian likelihoods with kernel estimations. Even though CH models are not immediate forms of time-varying linear regression, according to the discussion in
[
24
, Section C.2.5]
, a straightforward transformation can convert them into a time-varying linear regression model.

•

Time-varying nonlinear regression with multiplicative separable structure, i.e.,
m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})

=f_{j}(t_{i})g_{j}(X_{j,i,n}),
for all
1\leq j\leq r.
In this setting, the kernel estimators and pointwise asymptotic normality have been established in
[
11
,
57
]
, the spline estimators and their asymptotic normality have been studied in
[
81
]
for functional data and in
[
41
]
for locally stationary time series, among others.

In summary, most of the relevant literature focuses on providing kernel-based estimators for the smooth functions in (
1.1
). Although kernel estimation is a useful approach, it is known to suffer from boundary issues and lack of automatic adaptability to the smoothness structures of the underlying regression functions. Moreover, existing works have only established convergence rates on a compact domain, which may limit their applicability when the covariates have unbounded supports. Finally, the literature has primarily focused on proving pointwise distributional results for general smooth functions, which may not be useful for conducting functional or simultaneous inference on the regression coefficient functions.

Motivated by the above challenges, in the current paper, we study (
1.1
) in great generality. First, we provide uniformly consistent nonparamteric sieve estimators for the functions
m_{j}^{\prime}
s. We also address the identifiability issue for the components
m_{j}^{\prime}
s when
r>1.
Second, we develop simultaneous inferential theory for these functions. Our results and methodologies only require mild assumptions on
m_{j}
’s and we also allow the supports of these functions to be unbounded. We give an overview of our results in Section
1.2
.

1.2
Overview of our results and novelties

In this subsection, we give a heuristic overview of our main results. Throughout the paper, for the locally stationary time series, we use the general form of physical representation following
[
23
,
74
,
80
,
87
]
(c.f. Assumption
2.1
) which covers a wide range of commonly used locally stationary time series.
The aim of this paper is to provide a systematic and unified estimation and simultaneous inferential theory for the smooth coefficient functions in (
1.1
) without imposing additional structural assumptions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work in the literature to perform simultaneous nonparametric inference for the general time-varying nonlinear regression (
1.1
).

For the estimation part, we propose the sieve nonparamatric estimators for the smooth functions. However, unlike in the standard applications of sieve expansions where the smooth functions are supported on a bounded domain
[
1
,
12
,
14
,
23
,
24
,
38
]
, the covariates in (
1.1
) are usually supported in
\mathbb{R}
and hence the sieve methods and the classic approximation theory cannot be applied directly. To address this issue, we use the mapped sieve basis functions
[
6
,
60
]
which can monotonically and smoothly map the unbounded domain to a compact domain. Consequently, we can construct a hierarchical sieve basis functions as in (
3.8
) and establish the uniform approximation theory for the smooth functions on unbounded domain; see Proposition
3.1
for more details.
To address the issue of identifiability when
r>1
, in the actual implementation, we propose a two-step sieve estimation approach. In the first step, some pilot estimators (c.f. (
3.3
) and (
3.4
)) are constructed using the basis expansions, which are basis-dependent and may not satisfy condition (
1.2
). Consequently, these estimators can introduce a time-and basis-dependent bias. In the second step, we use the method of sieves again to estimate these bias and correct our pilot estimators; see Section
3.2
for further details. The estimation procedure is computationally cheap and the proposed estimators are theoretically consistent. Especially, under mild regularity conditions, for short-range dependent locally stationary time series, our estimators achieve uniform consistency under mild regularity assumptions; see Theorem
3.2
and Remark
3.4
for more details.

Once the estimation theory is established, we can develop the inferential theory. We provide the simultaneous confidence regions (SCRs) based on our sieve estimators (c.f. (
4.7
)). Then we apply the SCRs to conduct structural testings on the smooth functions. For example, we can test whether the functions are time-invariant (c.f. Example
4.2
) or have multiplicative separability structure (c.f. Example
4.3
). The key technical inputs are to establish Gaussian approximation results for high-dimensional locally stationary time series of the affine form. We prove such results in Theorems
L.2
and
L.3
of our supplement
[
22
]
and they can be of independent interest. Especially, when the temporal relation of the locally stationary time series decays fast enough, the approximate rate matches that of
[
30
]
.
Moreover, to analyze the critical values for the SCRs, we establish the asymptotic distribution of the maximum deviation of the sieve estimators over the joint state-time domain using the device of volume of tubes
[
44
,
46
,
62
]
. For practical implementation, we propose a multiplier bootstrap procedure as in
[
16
,
83
]
which is both theoretically sound and empirically accurate and powerful; see Theorem
4.3
. Numerical simulations and real data analysis are provided to support our results and methodologies. An
\mathtt{R}
package
\mathtt{SMIle}
is developed to ease the implementation.

Finally, we point out that even though we focus on the object of the physical form of locally stationary time series, our methodologies and results can be applied to the locally stationary time series considered in
[
19
,
55
,
70
]
or even the general non-stationary time series in
[
24
]
, after some minor modifications. Before concluding this subsection, we summarize the contributions of the current paper as follows.

(1).

We propose the sieve estimators for the time-varying nonlinear time series regression (
1.1
) using mapped sieve basis functions. Our methodology only requires mild assumptions on the smooth functions and we allow the functions to be supported on unbounded domains. Moreover, our estimation procedure effectively addresses the issue of identifiability and our estimator achieves the uniform consistency.

(2).

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to establish a comprehensive and unified simultaneous inferential theory for the smooth functions in (
1.1
) by constructing their SCRs across both
x
and
t
. Our theory can be used to conduct both structural and exact form tests on the smooth functions.

(3).

We provide a multiplier bootstrap procedure (c.f. Algorithm
1
) to implement our methodologies in practice. The bootstrap procedure can asymptotically mimic the distributions of the statistics proposed in the inferential theory and provide accurate SCRs. Moreover, our bootstrap method is robust and adaptive to the structures of the locally stationary time series and the functions
m_{j}
’s.

(4).

We establish two Gaussian approximation results for affine forms of high dimensional locally stationary time series. Furthermore, we establish the asymptotic distribution of the maxima of a Gaussian random field, with randomness arising from a locally stationary time series. This allows us to study the critical values of the SCRs.

The model and assumptions

In this section, we introduce the model, some related notations and assumptions. For notional simplicity, till the end of the paper, we simply write
Y_{i}\equiv Y_{i,n},X_{j,i}\equiv X_{j,i,n}
and
\epsilon_{i}\equiv\epsilon_{i,n}.
In the current paper, we consider the family of locally stationary time series for
{Y_{i},X_{j,i},\epsilon_{i}}
as introduced in
[
19
,
87
]
using the physical representation. We now state the basic setup of our model in Assumption

.

Assumption 2.1
(Model setup).

For the regression (
1.1
), throughout the paper,
we assume that all
\{X_{j,i}\},\{\epsilon_{i}\}
are locally stationary time series such that for all
1\leq j\leq r,

X_{j,i}=G_{j}(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ \epsilon_{i}=D(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ t_{i}=\frac{i}{n}.

(2.1)

Here
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(\cdots,\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i}),
where we assume that
\{\eta_{i}\}
are i.i.d. random elements with
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i}|\{X_{j,i}\}_{1\leq j\leq r})=0.

Moreover, we assume that
G_{j},1\leq j\leq r
, and
D:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are measurable functions such that for any fixed
t\in[0,1],

G_{j}(t,\cdot),1\leq j\leq r
and
D(t,\cdot)
are property defined random variables. Furthermore, we assume all these functions are stochastic Lipschitz in
t
such that for some
q>2,
some constant
C>0
and any
s,t\in[0,1]

\max\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq r}\left\|G_{j}(s,\mathcal{F}_{0})-G_{j}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0})\right\|_{q},\left\|D(s,\mathcal{F}_{0})-D(t,\mathcal{F}_{0})\right\|_{q}\right\}\leq C|s-t|.

(2.2)

Remark 2.1
.

Several remarks are in order. First, (
2.1
) requires that all the time series admit physical representations and the data generating mechanisms change slowly over time. This is a mild assumption in the sense that many commonly used locally stationary time series, linear or non-linear, can be written into such a form. For illustrations, we refer the readers to Examples
J.1
and
J.2
in our supplement. We also point out that, combining (
2.1
) with (
1.1
), we see that
Y_{i}
also admits a physical representation. We also note that equation (
2.1
) implies that our model encompasses the case where
\epsilon_{i}=H(t_{i},X_{1,i},\dots,X_{r,i},\eta_{i})
. Second, (
2.2
) is used to ensure the local stationarity of the time series which is a commonly used assumption in the study of locally stationary time series. Third, (
2.1
) is a flexible framework and has been frequently used in other time series regression models, for example
[
79
,
80
,
88
]
. Together with our regression (
1.1
), we extend the classes of time varying nonlinear regression models. Fourth, we point out that if one sets
X_{j,i}=Y_{i-j},
i.e.,
Y_{i}=G(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})
for some function
G
and
X_{j,i}=G(t_{i-j},\mathcal{F}_{i-j}),
(
1.1
) with (
2.1
) can be used to efficiently approximate the locally stationary nonlinear AR models as in
[
70
]
and the time-varying ARCH and GARCH models in
[
45
]
. Finally, to simplify the discussion, we focus our analysis on locally stationary time series as described in (
2.1
). However, similar to the analysis in Section 2 of
[
24
]
, our discussion—particularly the estimation part—can be easily extended to general nonstationary time series. Specifically, (
2.1
) can be generalized to
X_{j,i}=G_{j,i}(\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ \epsilon_{i}=D_{i}(\mathcal{F}_{i}).
Since this extension is not the primary focus of the current paper, we will explore it in future works.

In what follows,
we employ the
physical dependence measure

[
19
,
74
]
to quantify the temporal dependence of the time series. For simplicity, we state the definition using
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
.

Definition 2.1

(Physical dependence measure)
.

Let
\{\eta_{i}^{\prime}\}
be an i.i.d. copy of
\{\eta_{i}\}.
Assume that for some
q>2,

\|\epsilon_{i}\|_{q}<\infty.
Then for
k\geq 0,
we define the physical dependence measure of
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
as

\delta(k,q)=\sup_{t}\|D(t,\mathcal{F}_{0})-D(t,\mathcal{F}_{0,k})\|_{q},

where
\mathcal{F}_{0,k}=(\mathcal{F}_{-k-1},\eta_{-k}^{\prime},\eta_{-k+1},\cdots,\eta_{0}).
For convenience, we denote
\delta(k,q)=0
when
k<0.
Similarly, we can define the physical dependence measures for
\{X_{j,i}\},1\leq j\leq r.

The physical dependence measure provides a convenient tool to study the temporal dependence of time series. It quantifies the magnitude of change in the system’s output when the input of the system
k
steps ahead is replaced by an i.i.d. copy. Moreover, the strong temporal dependence of the locally stationary time series can be controlled in terms of
\delta(k,q)
and the concentration inequalities (c.f. Lemmas
M.2
and
M.3
of our supplement
[
22
]
) can be established based on them. For more details on physical dependence measure, we refer the readers to
[
51
,
52
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
. In addition, for the purpose of illustration, in Examples
J.1
and
J.2
of our supplement, we explain how the physical dependence measure can be calculated easily for the commonly used locally stationary time series.

Armed with Definition
2.1
, we impose the following assumption to ensure that the temporal dependence decays fast enough so that the time series has short-range dependence.

Assumption 2.2
.

Suppose that there exists some constant
\tau>1
and some constant
C>0
such that

\max_{0\leq j\leq r}\delta_{j}(k,q)\leq Ck^{-\tau},\ \text{for all}\ k\geq 1,

(2.3)

where
\delta_{j}(k,q),1\leq j\leq r,
are the physical dependence measures of
\{X_{j,i}\},1\leq j\leq r,
and
\delta_{0}(k,q)
is that of
\{\epsilon_{i}\}.

Assumption
2.2
is readily satisfied by commonly used locally stationary time series models. In Section
J.1
of our supplement, we present several classes of locally stationary time series utilizing physical representation and explain how these assumptions are easily met.

Sieve estimation for time-varying nonlinear regression

In this section, we propose a nonparametric approach to estimating the nonlinear functions
m_{j}(t,x),\ 1\leq j\leq r
and the time-varying intercept
m_{0}(t)
based on the method of sieves
[
12
]
. To ensure identifiability under assumption (
1.2
), our proposed method involves two steps. In the first step (Section
3.1
), we construct pilot estimators using the expansion of sieves. However, these estimators may not satisfy assumption (
1.2
). In the second step (Section
3.2
), we apply a correction again based on the method of sieves to ensure compliance with assumption (
1.2
). The uniform consistency of our estimators is established in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Pilot estimators based on sieve least squares

In this subsection, we use the sieve least square estimation approach to estimate
m_{0}(t)
and
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r
. Our methodology is nonparametric and utilizes the sieve basis expansion. Before stating our results, we first pause to provide a heuristic overview of the method. The details will be offered in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
.

Since we can only observe one realization of the time series, it is natural to impose some smoothness condition on
m_{0}(t)
and
m_{j}(t,x)
(c.f. Assumption
3.1
). First, under these conditions,
m_{0}(t)
can be well approximated by

m_{0,c}(t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{c_{0}}\beta_{0,\ell}\phi_{\ell}(t),

(3.1)

and
m_{j}(t,x)
can be well approximated by

m_{j,c,d}(t,x)
(c.f. Proposition

m_{j,c,d}(t,x)=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c_{j}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d_{j}}\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x).

(3.2)

Here
\{\phi_{\ell}(t)\}
are commonly used sieve basis functions in
[0,1]
and
\{b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x)\}
are the sieve basis functions on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R},
and
\{\beta_{0,\ell}\}
and
\{\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\}
are the coefficients to be estimated. Here
c_{j}\equiv c_{j}(n),\ d_{j}\equiv d_{j}(n)
depend on the smoothness of
m_{j},0\leq j\leq r.
Second, due to the unboundedness of domain of
x,

\{b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x)\}
needed to be properly constructed. For detailed discussion,
we refer the readers to Section
3.1.1
.

Finally, in view of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), given a set of sieve basis functions, it suffices to estimate the coefficients. We will use the ordinary least square (OLS) to achieve this goal and obtain the estimators
\{\widehat{\beta}_{0,\ell}\}
and
\{\widehat{\beta}_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\}
. This aspect will be discussed in Section

. Based on the above calculation, we can obtain our pilot estimators below

\widehat{m}^{*}_{0,c}(t,x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{c}\widehat{\beta}_{0,\ell}\phi_{\ell}(t),

(3.3)

and

\widehat{m}^{*}_{j,c,d}(t,x)=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d}\widehat{\beta}_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x).

(3.4)

The pilot estimators (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are constructed using the basis expansions (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), which are basis-dependent and may not satisfy condition (
1.2
). Consequently, these estimators can introduce a time- and basis-dependent biases. This issue will be addressed in Section
3.2

3.1.1
Mapped hierarchical sieves for 2-D smooth functions on unbounded domain

In this subsection, we discuss how to use sieve basis functions to approximate a smooth 2-D function. Note that for
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r,
when
x
is defined on a compact domain in
\mathbb{R}
, the results have been established, for example, see
[
12
, Section 2.3.1]
for a comprehensive review. However, in many real applications, the domain of
x
is unbounded. Therefore, we need to modify the commonly used sieves to accommodate for practical applications.

In the literature, there exist three different approaches to deal with the unbounded domain. The first method is to apply a finite interval method, such as Chebyshev polynomials, to an interval
x\in[-L,L]
where
L
is large but
finite. This method is also known as domain truncation. The second way is to use a basis that is intrinsic to the unbounded domain such as Hermite functions. The third approach is to map the infinite interval into a finite domain through a change of coordinate and then apply a finite interval method. We refer the readers to
[
5
,
6
,
12
,
60
]
for a review.

In the current paper, we employ the third method that we will apply some suitable mappings to map the unbounded domain to a compact one. The reasons are twofold. First, the first and second methods typically result in slower approximate rates, requiring more basis functions to achieve a desired level of accuracy. Second, in most statistical applications, the functions have rapidly decaying tails, which ensures the appropriateness of the mapping method. The mapping idea is popular in numerical PDEs for handling the boundary value problems. For a review of the mapping strategy, we refer the readers to
[
6
,
60
]
. In what follows, without loss of generality, we assume that the domain for

\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{R}_{+}.
The mappings will map the domain to a compact interval, say,

We state the definitions of the mappings as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Mappings)
.

For some positive scaling factor
s>0,
consider a family of mappings of the form:

x=g(y;s),\ s>0,\ y\in I:=[-1,1],\ x\in\Lambda:=\mathbb{R}_{+}\ \text{or}\ \mathbb{R},

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}=g^{\prime}(y;s)>0,\ y\in I;

\displaystyle g(-1;s)=\begin{cases}0,&\Lambda=\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
-\infty,&\Lambda=\mathbb{R}\end{cases},\ g(1;s)=\infty.

Since the above mapping is invertible, we denote the inverse mapping by

y=g^{-1}(x;s)=u(x;s),\ x\in\Lambda,y\in I,s>0.

(3.5)

Based on the above definition, it is easy to see that
\frac{1}{2}u(x;s)+\frac{1}{2}
will map
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
[0,1].
Moreover, as recommended in
[
60
]
, it is advisable to use
s=1
for the mappings in practice. In Section
J.2
of the supplement, we provide several examples of commonly used mappings.

Throughout the paper, without loss of generality, we assume that
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r,
takes value on the whole real line
\mathbb{R}
for
x.
Similar arguments apply when
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.
Using the mappings in Definition
3.1
, due to monotonicity, we have linked
m_{j}(t,x):[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to

\widetilde{m}_{j}(t,y):=m_{j}(t,g(2y-1;s)):[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

(3.6)

Based on the above arguments, we can construct a sequence of mapped sieve basis functions following
[
60

. Let

be an orthonormal base of the smooth functions defined on
[0,1].
Recall (

such that for

\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(x)=\phi_{i}\circ\mathsf{y}(x),\ \mathsf{y}(x):=\frac{u(x;s)+1}{2}.

(3.7)

Note that

is a sequence of orthogonal basis of the functional space defined on
\mathbb{R}

[
60

. Denote

as the orthonormal basis functions based on

Armed with

and

(3.8)

For examples of the commonly used sieve and mapped sieve basis functions and their properties, we refer the readers to Section

Assumption 3.1
.

For some constant
\mathsf{m}_{0},
denote
\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{m}_{0}}([0,1])
as the function space on

of continuous functions that have continuous first
\mathsf{m}_{0}

Moreover, for some sequence of constants
\mathsf{m}_{kj},k=1,2,1\leq j\leq r,

in (

\frac{\partial\widetilde{m}_{j}}{\partial t}\in\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{m}_{1j}}([0,1]),\ \text{and}\ \frac{\partial\widetilde{m}_{j}}{\partial y}\in\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{m}_{2j}}([0,1]),

and for all

is always uniformly bounded and continuous in both

and

Remark 3.1
.

We provide a few remarks on Assumption

. First, the first condition in (

) requires that
\widetilde{m}_{j}(t,\cdot)
is a smooth function with respect to

on the compact interval

In view of the definition (

is also a smooth function with respect to

of order

Second, the second condition in (

being mapped to

is a smooth function with respect to

In view of the mappings in Definition

, we see that it will be satisfied if the original function

belongs to the generalized Schwartz space of order
\mathsf{m}_{2j},
i.e.,
h_{j}(x)\in\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{m}_{2j}}(\mathbb{R}),
and for all

there exists some positive constant

so that

see Section

of our supplement for more details. For an illustration, we can see the decay properties of the mapped basis functions as in the right panel of Figure

of the supplement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds. Furthermore, let
{\phi_{i}(t)}
be commonly used sieve basis functions and
let

be the corresponding mapped sieve basis functions.
For

Proposition

3.1.2
Pilot estimation for the coefficients

According to the approximation result in Proposition

and the definitions of

in (

) and

and

and then construct the pilot estimator as in (

) and

In what follows, we will estimate these coefficients simultaneously using one OLS. Under some mild conditions, inserting the results of Proposition

into (

) will result in

Y_{i}=m_{0,c}(t_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j,c,d}(t_{i},X_{j,i})+\epsilon_{i}+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Since

where for

(3.10)

To ensure identifiability, it is necessary to estimate both

and its mean

as in (

) without addressing the identifiability issue, and then account for it by estimating

in the next section. Note that for the basis functions

constructed according to (
3.8
) and using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we can write

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=m_{0,c}(t_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j,c,d}(t_{i},X_{j,i})+\epsilon_{i}+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle{=\sum_{\ell=1}^{c_{0}}\beta_{0,\ell}\phi_{\ell}(t_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c_{j}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\mathsf{g}}^{d_{j}}\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t_{i},X_{j,i})+\epsilon_{i}+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)},

(3.11)

where for the first mapped orthonormal basis fucntion

as in (
3.8
),

if

and otherwise

to avoid the possible issue of multicollinearity. For simplicity, in what follows, we assume

Throughout the paper, we will use the following short-hand notation

(3.12)

Let the vector

in the order of the indices

(c.f. (

Remark 3.3
.

In the literature (see Chapter 8.5.2 of the monograph
[

]

[

3.2
Bias correction: towards satisfying the assumption (
1.2
)

The previous section provides the pilot estimators (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), which are basis-dependent and may not satisfy condition (
1.2
). Recall (
3.2
) and (
3.10
). To ensure (
1.2
) and correct the bias part, the time-varying mean function
\chi_{j}(t),\ 1\leq j\leq r
, should be estimated and removed from its pilot estimators. We will address this issue via a bias correction procedure in this section.
Under some regularity conditions, we can write that

\chi_{j}(t_{i})=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c_{j}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d_{j}}\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\phi_{\ell_{1}}(t_{i})\mathbb{E}\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(X_{j,i}).

(3.16)

Since the coefficients
\{\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\}
has been estimated in (
3.13
), it suffices to estimate

For notional convenience, we now introduce a sequence of functions
\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t_{i}),\ 1\leq j\leq r,\ 1\leq\ell_{2}\leq d_{j},
defined as
\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t_{i})=\mathbb{E}\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(X_{j,i}).

Moreover, under Assumption
2.1
, we have that for all
t\in[0,1]

\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)=\mathbb{E}\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(G_{j}(t,\cdot)).

(3.17)

Note that
\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(x)
is a mapped basis function. If it is smooth and
G_{j}(t,\cdot)
satisfies certain regularity conditions, then
\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}
inherits the smoothness. Consequently, we can use the method of sieves again to estimate them. We summarize these assumptions as follows.

Assumption 3.2
.

For
1\leq j\leq r
and
1\leq\ell_{2}\leq d_{j},
suppose there exist a sequence of constants constant
\{\mathsf{n}_{j,\ell_{2}}\},
so that the sequence of functions
\{\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)\}
defined in (
3.17
) are
\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{n}_{j,\ell_{2}}}([0,1]).

Under the above assumptions, it is sufficient to estimate the sequence of functions
\{\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)\}
using the method of sieves again, as discussed in Section
3.1
. More concretely, by denoting
\epsilon_{j,\ell_{2},i}=\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(X_{j,i})-\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t_{i}),
we have the following regression equations

\varphi_{\ell_{2}}(X_{j,i})=\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t_{i})+\epsilon_{j,\ell_{2},i},\ i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(3.18)

Similar to (
3.1
), for some constant
c_{j,\ell_{2}},

\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)
can be approximated by

\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2},c}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{c_{j,\ell_{2}}}\gamma_{k}\phi_{k}(t),\ \text{where}\ \gamma_{k}\equiv\gamma_{k}(j,\ell_{2}),

(3.19)

where we recall that
\{\phi_{k}(t)\}
are the basis functions defined on
[0,1]
. Together with (
3.18
), we can estimate the coefficients
\{\gamma_{k}\}
in the same ways as in Section
3.1.2
. As a consequence, we can obtain the estimators for
\{\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)\},
denoted as
\{\widehat{\vartheta}_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)\}.

With the above preparation, we can estimate
\chi_{j}(t_{i})
using

\widehat{\chi}_{j}(t)=\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\widehat{\beta}_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\phi_{\ell_{1}}(t)\widehat{\vartheta}_{j,\ell_{2}}(t).

(3.20)

Applying the above corrections to the pilot estimators (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we can finally obtain our estimators for
m_{0}(t)
and
m_{j}(t,x)
using

\widehat{m}_{0}(t)=\widehat{m}^{*}_{0,c}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\widehat{\chi}_{j}(t),

(3.21)

and for
1\leq j\leq r

\widehat{m}_{j}(t,x)=\widehat{m}^{*}_{j,c,d}(t,x)-\widehat{\chi}_{j}(t).

(3.22)

Theorem 3.2

Suppose Assumptions

Remark 3.4

Theorem

4
Simultaneous inference for the nonlinear regression

In this section, we conduct simultaneous inference for our model (
1.1
). To be concise, we only focus on the nonlinear functions
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r,
based on our proposed sieve least square estimator (
3.22
). Similar arguments apply to the time-varying intercept; see Remark
J.2
of our supplement for more details. In what follows, we assume the error part in Theorem
3.2
satisfies the following mild assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

Till the end of the paper, we assume that for some constant
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{2}

c_{0}^{-\mathsf{m}_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(c_{j}^{-\mathsf{m}_{1j}}+d_{j}^{-\mathsf{m}_{2j}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{j}}c_{j,\ell}^{-\mathsf{n}_{j,\ell}}\right)=\mathrm{O}(n^{-\varepsilon}).

(4.1)

Moreover, we assume that minor error parts in (
3.35
) and (
3.36
) (i.e., the last terms on their right-hand sides) are much smaller than the dominate parts (i.e., the first terms on their right-hand side).

Remark 4.1
.

First, (
4.1
) is the commonly used under-smoothing condition which ensures the deterministic approximation error, or the bias part, in Proposition
3.1
is negligible, allowing the focus to be on the analysis of
m_{j,c,d},
for
1\leq j\leq r.
This approach is commonly used in the literature on the applications of the sieve method to time series; see, for example,
[
12
,
14
,
23
,
24
]
. Moreover, (
4.1
) is a mild assumption when reasonably large values of
c_{j}
and
d_{j}
are chosen. For example, if
\mathsf{m}_{0}=\mathsf{m}_{1j}=\mathsf{m}_{2j}=\infty,
it can be easily satisfied by choosing
c_{j},d_{j}\asymp\log n.
In general, it will be satisfied if we choose

c_{0}\asymp n^{C/\mathsf{m}_{0}},\ c_{j}\asymp n^{C/\mathsf{m}_{j1}},\ d_{j}\asymp n^{C/\mathsf{m}_{j2}},

(4.2)

for some constant
C>\frac{1}{2}.
This shows that we only need a relatively small amount of basis functions once
\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{jk},k=1,2,
are reasonably large.

Second, the second assumption ensures that the estimation error of each auxiliary mean shift in (
3.17
) is negligible compared to the estimator of the entire function
m_{j}(t,x)
. Theoretically, this is a natural assumption since, compared to the 1-D mean shift functions
\vartheta_{j,\ell_{2}}(t)
, the function
m_{j}(t,x)
is a 2-D function, which inherently results in an asymptotically dominating estimation error. Practically, it can be readily addressed by employing relatively smooth basis functions. We illustrate this using (
3.35
) as an example. For instance, if we choose orthogonal polynomials as the basis functions, it is straightforward to see that all
\mathsf{n}_{j,\ell_{2}}=\infty
in Assumption
3.2
. This implies that one only needs
c_{j,\ell}=\mathrm{O}(\log n)
to ensure a negligible error. Consequently, the last term can be bounded by
\mathrm{O}(\gamma(\log n)^{3/2}\varsigma\sqrt{d_{j}/n})
, which is much smaller than the first term.

Finally, we note that under Assumption
4.1
, as demonstrated in our technical proof, a useful expression can be derived to quantify the error term for
\widehat{m}_{j}-m_{j}
. Specifically, when
r=1
, by recalling (
3.29
) for
\bm{f}
and (
3.15
) for
\bm{b}
, we have

\widehat{m}_{1}-m_{1}=(\bm{b}^{\top}-\bm{f}^{\top})(\bm{\beta}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})(1+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)).

The adjustment of
\bm{f}
from the basis functions
\bm{b}
ensures that our proposed estimator satisfies the identifiability assumption.

4.1
Problems setup and simultaneous confidence region

For
1\leq j\leq r
and a coverage probability
1-\alpha,
our goal in this section is to construct a
(1-\alpha)
simultaneous confidence region (SCR) of
m_{j}(t,x)
based on the estimator (
3.22
), denoted as
\{\Upsilon_{\alpha}(t,x),0\leq t\leq 1,x\in\mathbb{R}\}.
More concretely, the SCR aims to have the following property:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{m_{j}(t,x)\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(t,x),\ \forall\ 0\leq t\leq 1,\ \forall\ x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=1-\alpha.

(4.3)

Before introducing various concerned hypothesis testing problems, we first explain how to construct the SCR using the point estimator
\widehat{m}_{j}(t,x)
in (
3.22
). In light of Assumption
4.1
and Proposition
3.1
, for each
1\leq j\leq r
and fixed
0\leq t\leq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R},
we write

\mathsf{T}_{j}(t,x)=\widehat{m}_{j}(t,x)-m_{j}(t,x).

(4.4)

We now introduce some important quantities. Till the end of the paper, for notional simplicity, by recalling (
3.29
) for
\bm{f}
and (
3.15
) for
\bm{b}
, we denote

\bm{r}\equiv\bm{r}(t,x):=\bm{b}-\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \text{and}\ \ \overline{\bm{r}}=(\mathbf{0}^{\top},\bm{r}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{p}}.

(4.5)

Recall (
3.30
) and
\mathsf{I}_{j}
in (
3.15
). Denote
\overline{\mathsf{I}}_{j}=(\bm{0}^{\top},\mathsf{I}_{j}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{p}}
and

h_{j}(t,x)=\sqrt{\bm{l}_{j}^{\top}\overline{\Omega}\bm{l}_{j}},\ 1\leq j\leq r,

(4.6)

where
\bm{l}_{j}\equiv\bm{l}_{j}(t,x):=\overline{\Pi}^{-1}\overline{\bm{r}}\overline{\mathsf{I}}_{j}.
We refer to Remark
J.3
of our supplement for more discussions on the properties of
h_{j}(t,x).
As noted in Remark
L.1
of our supplement, for each fixed pair
(t,x)
,
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{T}_{j}(t,x)
can be well approximated by a quadratic form of a high-dimensional locally stationary time series. Furthermore, as shown in Theorem
4.1
below, it is asymptotically Gaussian with mean zero and variance
h_{j}(t,x)^{2}
. Given
\alpha,
we can utilize

\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\pm}(t,x):=\widehat{m}_{j}(t,x)\pm\mathsf{c}_{\alpha}\frac{h_{j}(t,x)}{\sqrt{n}},\quad t\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.7)

where
\mathsf{c}_{\alpha}\equiv\mathsf{c}_{\alpha}(j,n)
is the critical value that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t,x}\left|\frac{\sqrt{n}\mathsf{T}_{j}}{h_{j}(t,x)}\right|\leq\mathsf{c}_{\alpha})=1-\alpha.

(4.8)

Consequently, for each
1\leq j\leq r,
the SCR can be constructed as

\left\{\left[\mathcal{R}_{j}^{-}(t,x),\ \mathcal{R}_{j}^{+}(t,x)\right],0\leq t\leq 1,x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(4.9)

Armed with the SCRs, we proceed to demonstrate a few applications on how to use them to infer the regression functions
m_{j}(t,x).
For the first application as in Example
4.1
, we are interested in testing whether the underlying function
m_{j}(t,x)
is identical to some pre-given function
\mathfrak{m}_{j,0}(t,x).
Under the null hypothesis, the pre-given function
\mathfrak{m}_{j,0}(t,x)
should be embedded into the SCR in (
4.9
). We summarize this in the following example.

Example 4.1

(Testing exact form)
.

We consider the hypothesis that

\mathbf{H}_{0}:\ m_{j}(t,x)\equiv\mathfrak{m}_{j,0}(t,x),

(4.10)

where
\mathfrak{m}_{j,0}(t,x)
is some pre-given function. For the test, we can calculate the SCR as in (
4.9
). If
\mathfrak{m}_{j,0}(t,x)
can be embedded into the SCR, we shall accept
\mathbf{H}_{0}
in (
4.10
). Otherwise, we need to reject it at level
\alpha
.

For the second application, we are interested in testing some special structural assumptions of
m_{j}(t,x)
. In this paper, we present two important examples as in Examples
4.2
and
4.3
below. One is to test whether
m_{j}(t,x)
is independent of the time (c.f. (
4.11
)) and the other is to test whether
m_{j}(t,x)
has a multiplicative (i.e., separable) structure in
t
and
x
(c.f. (
4.12
)). In these applications, under the specific structural assumptions,

can be estimated with faster convergence rates than those using the general approach (
3.15
). For example, in Example

, under the assumption that

is stationary (i.e., independent of time), instead of using

hierarchical basis functions as in (
3.8
), we only need
\mathrm{O}(d_{j})
mapped sieve basis functions so that we will have a better rate compared to using all the basis functions as illustrated in (
3.35
). Similarly, assuming that
m_{j}(t,x)
is separable in
t
and
x
, as in Example
4.3
, we can estimate the functions solely in
t
and
x
separately. This allows us to use only
\mathrm{O}(c_{j}+d_{j})
basis functions.

Based on the above observations, if the functions
m_{j}(t,x)
under the null hypothesis can be estimated with faster convergence rates than those estimated using the general approach (
3.15
), it suffices to check whether the estimated functions under the null hypothesis (with faster convergence rates) can be embedded into the SCR constructed generally using (
3.15
). We summarize these in the following examples.

Example 4.2

(Testing time-homogeneity)
.

We consider the structural assumption that

\mathbf{H}_{0}:\ m_{j}(t,x)\equiv m_{j}(x),\ \ \forall t\in[0,1],

(4.11)

for some function
m_{j}(\cdot).
Under the null assumption of (
4.11
), we can estimate the function only using the basis
\{\varphi_{j}(x)\}
instead of (
3.8
). Under the assumption of (
4.1
), by an argument similar to Theorem
3.2
, we can obtain a convergence rate
\iota\xi\zeta(d_{j}/n)^{1/2}
which is faster than the rate in (
3.35
).

Example 4.3

(Testing separability)
.

We consider the structural assumption that

\mathbf{H}_{0}:m_{j}(t,x)=C_{j}f_{j}(t)g_{j}(x),\ \forall\ t\in[0,1]\ \text{and}\ x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.12)

for some constant
C_{j}
and some functions
f_{j}(\cdot)
and
g_{j}(\cdot).
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\int f_{j}(t)\mathrm{d}t=1
and
\int g_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1,
so that
C_{j}=\int\int m_{j}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.
Moreover, we shall have that
g_{j}(x)=C_{j}^{-1}\int m_{j}(t,x)\mathrm{d}t,\ f_{j}(t)=C_{j}^{-1}\int m_{j}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x.
Therefore, one can obtain the estimates of
f_{j}(t)
and
g_{j}(x)
by integrating the estimator (
3.22
). Under the null assumption of (

), by a discussion similar to Theorem
3.2
,
we can obtain a convergence rate
\varsigma\gamma(c_{j}/n)^{1/2}+\varsigma\zeta(d_{j}/n)^{1/2}
which is faster than the rate in (
3.35
).

For illustrations of how to use SCR to conduct the tests presented in Examples

In this section, we give a brief overview on the sieve approximation theory

Example G.1

(Trigonometric and mapped trigonometric polynomials)
.

For

we consider the following trigonometric polynomials

\left\{1,\sqrt{2}\cos(2k\pi x),\sqrt{2}\sin(2k\pi x),\cdots\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

The above basis functions form an orthonormal basis
of
L_{2}([0,1]).
Note that the classic trigonometric basis function is well suited for approximating periodic functions on
[0,1].

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be constructed following Example
J.3
. Since
u(x)
in Example
J.3
satisfies that
u^{\prime}(x)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the following mapped trigonometric polynomials form an orthonomal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)},\ \sqrt{2u^{\prime}(x)}\cos(2k\pi u(x)),\ \sqrt{2u^{\prime}(x)}\sin(2k\pi u(x)),\cdots\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N},

where we used inverse function theorem.

Example G.2

(Jacobi and mapped Jacobi polynomials)
.

For
I=(-1,1)
and some constants
\alpha,\beta>-1,
denote the Jacobi weight function as

\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(y)=(1-y)^{\alpha}(1+y)^{\beta},

and associated weighted
\mathcal{L}^{2}
space as
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I).
With the above notations, the Jacobi polynomials are the orthogonal polynomials
\{J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\}
such that

\int_{I}J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)J_{m}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\gamma_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\delta_{n,m},

where
\delta_{n,m}
is the Kronecker function, and

\gamma_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

More explicitly, the Jacobi polynomials can be characterized using the Rodrigues’ formula
[
65
]

J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\left((1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}(1-x^{2})^{n}\right).

Then for
x\in[0,1],
the following sequence forms an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}([0,1])

\left\{\mathsf{J}^{\alpha,\beta}_{k}(x):=\sqrt{\frac{2\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(2x-1)}{\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}}}J_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}(2x-1)\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

We mention that when
\alpha=\beta=0,
the Jacobi polynomial reduces to the Legendre polynomial and when
\alpha=\beta=-0.5,
the Jacobi polynomial reduces to the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind.

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x)
be the mapping as in Example
G.1
. Then the following sequence provides an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
60
, Section 2.3]
)

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)}\mathsf{J}_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}(u(x))\right\}.

Example G.3

(Wavelet and mapped Wavelet basis)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N},
a Daubechies (mother) wavelet of class
D-N
is a function
\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\psi(x):=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2N-1}(-1)^{k}h_{2N-1-k}\varphi(2x-k),

where
h_{0},h_{1},\cdots,h_{2N-1}\in\mathbb{R}
are the constant (high pass) filter coefficients satisfying the conditions

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{2k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{2k+1},

as well as, for
l=0,1,\cdots,N-1

\sum_{k=2l}^{2N-1+2l}h_{k}h_{k-2l}=\begin{cases}1,&l=0,\\
0,&l\neq 0.\end{cases}

And
\varphi(x)
is the scaling (father) wavelet function is supported on
[0,2N-1)
and satisfies the recursion equation

\varphi(x)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{2N-1}h_{k}\varphi(2x-k),
as well as the normalization
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)dx=1
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(2x-k)\varphi(2x-l)dx=0,\ k\neq l.

Note that the filter coefficients can be efficiently computed as listed in
[
21
]
. The order
N
, on the one hand, decides the support of our wavelet; on the other hand, provides the regularity condition in the sense that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{j}\psi(x)dx=0,\ j=0,\cdots,N,\ \text{where}\ N\geq d.

We will employ Daubechies wavelet with a sufficiently high order when forecasting in our simulations and data analysis. In the present paper, to construct a sequence of orthogonal wavelet, we will follow the dyadic construction of
[
20
]
. For a given
J_{n}
and
J_{0},
we will consider the following periodized wavelets on
[0,1]

\Big\{\varphi_{J_{0}k}(x),\ 0\leq k\leq 2^{J_{0}}-1;\psi_{jk}(x),\ J_{0}\leq j\leq J_{n}-1,0\leq k\leq 2^{j}-1\Big\},\ \mbox{ where}

(G.1)

\varphi_{J_{0}k}(x)=2^{J_{0}/2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi(2^{J_{0}}x+2^{J_{0}}l-k),\ \psi_{jk}(x)=2^{j/2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(2^{j}x+2^{j}l-k),

or, equivalently
[
54
]

\Big\{\varphi_{J_{n}k}(x),\ 0\leq k\leq 2^{J_{n}-1}\Big\}.

(G.2)

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x)
be the mapping as in Example
G.1
. Then using (
G.2
), the following sequence provides an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)}\varphi_{J_{n}k}(u(x))\right\}.

Similarly, we can construct the mapped wavelet functions using (
G.1
).

I
Parameter selection

In this subsection, we discuss how to choose the important parameters in practice using the data driven approach as in
[
3
]
. For simplicity, we assume
m_{0}(t)\equiv 0
and
r=1.
In this setting, we only need to choose one pair of
(c,d).
The general case can be handled similarly.

We first discuss how to choose
c
and
d
using cross validation. For a given integer
l,
say
l=\lfloor 3\log_{2}n\rfloor,
we divide the time series into two parts: the training part
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-l}
and the validation part
\{X_{i}\}_{i=n-l+1}^{n}.
With some preliminary initial pair
(c,d)
, we propose a sequence of candidate pairs
(c_{i},d_{j}),\ i=1,2,\cdots,u,\ j=1,2,\cdots,v,
in an appropriate neighborhood of
(c,d)
where
u,v
are some given large integers. For each pair of the choices
(c_{i},d_{j}),
we estimate
\widehat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)
as in (
3.15
). Then using the fitted model, we forecast the time series in the validation part of the time series. Let
\widehat{X}_{n-l+1,ij},\cdots,\widehat{X}_{n,ij}
be the forecast of
X_{n-l+1},...,X_{n},
respectively using the parameter pair
(c_{i},d_{j})
. Then we choose the pair
(c_{i_{0}},d_{j_{0}})
with the minimum sample MSE of forecast, i.e.,

({i_{0}},{j_{0}}):=\mathop{\mbox{argmin}}_{((i,j):1\leq i\leq u,1\leq j\leq v)}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{k=n-l+1}^{n}(X_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k,ij})^{2}.

Then we discuss how to choose
m
for practical implementation. In
[
83
]
, the author used the minimum volatility (MV) method to choose the window size
m
for the scalar covariance function. The MV method does not depend on the specific form of the underlying time series dependence structure and hence is robust to misspecification of
the latter structure
[
58
]
. The MV method utilizes the fact that the covariance structure of
\widehat{\Omega}
becomes stable when the
block size
m
is in an appropriate range, where
\widehat{\Omega}=E[\Phi\Phi^{*}|(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})]=
is defined as

\widehat{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{(n-m-r+1)m}\sum_{i=b+1}^{n-m}\Big[\Big(\sum_{j=i}^{i+m}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{i}\Big)\otimes\Big(\bm{\phi}(\frac{i}{n})\Big)\Big]\times\Big[\Big(\sum_{j=i}^{i+m}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{i}\Big)\otimes\Big(\bm{\phi}(\frac{i}{n})\Big)\Big]^{\top}.

(I.1)

Therefore, it desires to minimize the standard errors of the latter covariance structure in a suitable range of candidate
m
’s.

In detail, for a give large value
m_{n_{0}}
and a neighbourhood control parameter
h_{0}>0,
we can choose a sequence of window sizes
m_{-h_{0}+1}<\cdots<m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{n_{0}}<\cdots<m_{n_{0}+h_{0}}
and obtain
\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}}
by replacing
m
with
m_{j}
in (
I.1
),
j=-h_{0}+1,2,\cdots,n_{0}+h_{0}.
For each
m_{j},j=1,2,\cdots,m_{n_{0}},
we calculate the matrix norm error of
\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}}
in the
h_{0}
-neighborhood, i.e.,

\mathsf{se}(m_{j}):=\mathsf{se}(\{\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j+k}}\}_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}})=\left[\frac{1}{2h_{0}}\sum_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}}\|\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}_{m_{j}}-\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}+k}\|^{2}\right]^{1/2},

where
\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}_{m_{j}}=\sum_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}}\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}+k}/(2h_{0}+1).

Therefore, we choose the estimate of
m
using

\widehat{m}:=\mathop{\mbox{argmin}}_{m_{1}\leq m\leq m_{n_{0}}}\mathsf{se}(m).

Note that in
[
83
]
the author used
h_{0}=3
and we also use this choice in the current paper.

In this section, we provide some additional examples and remarks.

J.1
Examples of locally stationary time series

In this subsection, we present two classes of locally stationary time series that satisfy the conditions outlined in Section
2
.

Example J.1

(Locally stationary linear time series)
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
be i.i.d. random variables and
\{a_{j}(t)\}
be continuously differentiable functions on
[0,1].
Consider the locally stationary linear process such that

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(t)\epsilon_{i-k}.

It is easy to see that the physical dependence measure of the above linear process, denoted as
\delta(k,q)
satisfies that

\delta(k,q)=\mathrm{O}(\sup_{t}|a_{k}(t)|).

Consequently, according to
[
23
, Example 2.4]
, (
2.2
) and Assumption
2.2
are satisfied if the following conditions hold

\sup_{t}|a_{k}(t)|\leq Ck^{-\tau},\ \ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|a_{k}^{\prime}(t)|^{\min\{2,q\}}<\infty.

Example J.2

(Locally stationary nonlinear time series)
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
be i.i.d. random variables. Consider a locally stationary process as follows

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=R(t,G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}),\epsilon_{i}),

where
R
is some measurable function. The above expression is quite general such that many locally stationary time series can be written in the above form. For example, the threshold autoregressive model, exponential autoregressive model and bilinear autoregressive models. According to
[
87
, Theorem 6]
, suppose that for some
x_{0},

\sup_{t}\|R(t,x_{0},\epsilon_{i})\|_{q}<\infty.
Denote

\chi:=\sup_{t}L(t),\ \text{where}\ L(t):=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\|R(t,x,\epsilon_{0})-R(t,y,\epsilon_{0})\|_{q}}{|x-y|}.

Note that
\chi<1
and
\delta(k,q)=\mathrm{O}(\chi^{k}).
Therefore, Assumption
2.2
is satisfied. Moreover, by
[
87
, Proposition 4]
, (
2.2
) holds if

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|M(G(t,\mathcal{F}_{0}))\|_{q}<\infty,\ \text{where}\ M(x):=\sup_{0\leq t<s\leq 1}\frac{\|R(t,x,\epsilon_{0})-R(s,x,\epsilon_{0})\|_{q}}{|t-s|}.

As a concrete example, we consider the time varying threshold autoregressive (TVTAR) model denoted as

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=a(t)[G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1})]^{+}+b(t)[-G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1})]^{+}.

In this case, Assumption
2.2
and (
2.2
) will be satisfied if

a(t),b(t)\in\mathsf{C}^{1}([0,1]),\ \text{and}\ \sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left(|a(t)|+|b(t)|\right)<1.

J.3
The Schwartz space and its generalization

In this subsection, we provide a general class of functional space so that the conditions of Assumption
3.1
are satisfied. We first define the Schwartz space
[
48
, Chapter 8]
.

Definition J.1

(Schwartz space)
.

The Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the topological vector space of Schwartz functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying

1.

f\in\mathtt{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d});

2.

For all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta,
there exists some positive constant
C_{\alpha,\beta}<\infty
so that

|x^{\alpha}\partial_{x}^{\beta}f(x)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}.

Based on the above definition, we can see that
f
and all of its derivatives are rapidly decreasing. Moreover, one can also check that for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
for all multi-indices
\alpha
and any integer
N,
there exists some positive constant
C_{N,\alpha}
so that

|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}f(x)|\leq C_{N,\alpha}(1+|x|)^{-N}.

It is easy to check from the chain rules for higher derivatives
[
42
]
that the second condition in (
3.9
) will be satisfied with
\mathsf{m}_{2}=\infty
if
m_{j}(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).
For general
\mathsf{m}_{2},
we can extend the Schwartz space as follows.

Definition J.2

(Generalized Schwartz space)
.

The generalized Schwartz space of order
\mathsf{m}

\mathcal{S}_{g}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{m})
is the topological vector space of generalized Schwartz functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying

1.

f\in\mathtt{C}^{\mathsf{m}}(\mathbb{R}^{d});

2.

For all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
that
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq\mathsf{m},
there exists some positive constant
C_{\alpha,\beta}<\infty
so that

|x^{\alpha}\partial_{x}^{\beta}f(x)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}.

It is easy to check from the chain rules for higher derivatives
[
42
]
that the second condition in (
3.9
) will be satisfied if
m_{j}(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{g}(\mathbb{R},\mathsf{m}_{2}).

Remark J.2
.

In this remark, we briefly explain how to construct the SCR for the time-varying intercept
m_{0}(t).
Similar to the discussions in Section
4.1
, we shall use the statistic

\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)=\widehat{m}_{0}(t)-m_{0}(t).

Remark J.3
.

h_{j}^{2}(t,x)\geq C_{1}.

Second, using Assumption
3.3
, we can see that uniformly for
(t,x),
we have that

\frac{|\bm{l}_{j}|}{h_{j}(t,x)}\asymp 1.

K.5
Additional real data analysis

In this subsection, we apply our method to study the shape of the monthly risk premium for S
\&
P 500 index as in
[
11
, Section 6.2]
. Let
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
be the conditional mean and conditional volatility of the excess return of the market portfolio, respectively. Our goal is to uncover how
\mu_{t}
relates to
\sigma_{t}.
Especially, we want to understand the functional relation that
\mu_{t}=m(t,\sigma_{t}^{2})
for some unknown function
m.
Such a problem has been studied extensively in the literature of financial economics. In the seminal work
[
53
]
, Merton modeled that
\mu_{t}=\gamma\sigma_{t}^{2}
for some constant
\gamma
representing the risk aversion of the agent. Recently, it has been argued in
[
4
,
7
,
10
,
33
,
36
]
that a general nonlinear and time-varying function will facilitate the modeling and interpretation of the shape of the market risk premium.

Very recently, in
[
11
]
, the authors modeled the relationship between
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
using
\mu_{t}=\rho(t)g(\sigma_{t})
for some unknown functions
\rho
and
g.
They estimated these functions using kernel methods. They found their estimation were consistent with real observations empirically and statistically analysis theoretically. Moreover, they provided some insights for the shape of the monthly risk premium for S
\&
P 500 Index based on their analysis. However, they did not justify why a separable structure was valid for modeling the risk premium. In what follows, we use our Algorithm
1
to justify the separability assumption, i.e., testing (
4.12
).

We follow the setting of
[
11
, Section 6.2]
. In the notation of our model (
1.1
),
m_{0}(t)=0
and
r=1
so that we do not have the issue of identifiability. We set
Y_{t}
to be
\mu_{t}=r_{mt}-r_{ft}
which is the excess return on the S
\&
P 500 Index calculated as the difference between the monthly continuously compounded cumulative return on the index minus the monthly return on 30-day Treasury Bills. For the conditional volatility
\sigma_{t},
we used the realized volatility (RV) measure from Oxford Man Realized Library (
https://realized.oxford-man.ox.ac.uk/
). To be more precise, let
RV_{t}
denote the daily annualized RV during the
t
th month. Then we obtain one-month-ahead RV forecast
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(RV_{t})
using the HAR-RV model as in
[
18
]
, which is our
X_{t}.
Our data covers the period between 31 January 2001 and 31 December 2018. Under the nominal level
0.05,
using the Daubechies-9 basis functions, we find that the
p
-value is
0.38
so we can conclude that the separable structural assumption is reasonable for modeling the risk premium. This supports the analysis of
[
11
]
.

Finally, we estimate the functions
\rho(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
using our sieve estimators. Due to separability, as discussed in Example
4.3
, we can estimate them separability instead of using the hierarchical sieve basis functions. We also provide the simultaneous confidence bands for these functions. In Figure
J
blow, we report the results. We find that when the RV is fixed, the risk premium changes in a nonlinear way of time which suggests the existence of a time-varying risk aversion. Moreover, for the
g(\cdot)
function, it is more flat and seems to have a monotone pattern. Our findings are consistent with those in
[
11
]
; see Figure 7 and the discussion therein.

\rho
and
g
and the blue lines the corresponding simultaneous 95

confidence bands constructed using the multiplier bootstrap.

L
Technical proofs

In this section, we provide the main technical proofs.

L.1
Uniform consistency of the proposed estimators: proof of Theorem
3.2

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
3.2
. Till the end of the paper, for a positive definite
H,
we denote
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}
as the operator norm of
H,
i.e., the largest eigenvalue of
H.

Since
r
is finite, without loss of generality, we assume
r=1.
For general
r,
the proof can be modified verbatim with only additional notional complicatedness. Consequently, we can omit the subscript
j,
so that
m\equiv m_{1},X_{i}\equiv X_{1,i},c\equiv c_{1},d\equiv d_{1},\chi(t)\equiv\chi_{1}(t)
and
\widehat{m}\equiv\widehat{m}_{1}.
In light of the identifiability assumption (
1.2
) and the treatment above (
3.10
), the starting point of our proof is the following decomposition

\displaystyle|\widehat{m}(t,x)-m(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\widehat{m}^{*}_{c,d}(t,x)-m_{c,d}(t,x)|+|\chi(t)-\widehat{\chi}(t)|

(L.1)

\displaystyle+|m_{c,d}(t,x)-m(t,x)|.

Since the last term of the right-hand side of (
L.1
) can be bounded using Proposition
3.1
, it suffices to control the first two terms. We start with the first one.

Using the definitions of (
3.2
), (
3.4
) and (
3.15
), by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have that

|\widehat{m}^{*}_{c,d}(t,x)-m_{c,d}(t,x)|\leq|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{1}|\zeta,

(L.2)

where
\zeta
is defined in (
3.33
).
Since
|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{1}|\leq|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}|,
the rest of the proof leaves to control
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}.
Note that we denote
\bm{Y}=(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Recall the notations around (
H.2
) and (
3.13
) and that

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}=(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}.

(L.3)

Therefore, we need to control
|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}|
which satisfies

|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}|\leq\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathsf{p}}((n^{-1}W^{\top}W))}\right||n^{-1}W^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}|,

(L.4)

where we recall the notation (
3.12
).

First, we establish the convergence results for the matrix
W^{\top}W.
Recall that
W
contains two parts
W_{0}
and
W_{1}.
Denote
W_{0(1)}(i)
as the
i
th column of
W_{0(1)}.
It suffices to analyze the terms
W_{0}(i)^{\top}W_{0}(j),W_{0}(i)^{\top}W_{1}(j)
and
W_{1}(i)^{\top}W_{1}(j).
Without loss of generality, we focus our discussion on the terms

W_{1}(1)^{\top}W_{1}(1),W_{1}(1)^{\top}W_{2}(1),W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(1),W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(2),W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{1}(1).

The general cases of
i,j
can be handled similarly.

We start with the term
W_{1}^{\top}(1)W_{1}(1).
Let
W_{1}(1)=(\mathsf{w}_{12},\cdots,\mathsf{w}_{1n})^{\top}.
Recall (
H.2
). We have

\mathsf{w}_{1k}=\phi_{1}(t_{k})\varphi_{1}(X_{k}),\ 1\leq k\leq n.

Consequently, we have that

\frac{1}{n}W_{1}(1)^{\top}W_{1}(1)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\varphi_{1}(X_{k})^{2}.

Note that by a discussion similar to Lemma
L.8
, we can show that
\mathfrak{h}_{k}:=\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\varphi_{1}(X_{k})^{2},1\leq k\leq n,
is a sequence of locally stationary time series whose physical dependence measure satisfies that

\delta_{\mathfrak{h}}(j,q)\leq C\xi^{2}\varsigma^{2}j^{-\tau},\ \text{for some constant}\ C>0,

where we again recall the notations in (
3.33
).

Together with (1) of Lemma
M.2
, we obtain that

\left\|\frac{1}{n}W_{1}^{\top}(1)W_{1}(1)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{1}(X_{k})^{2}\right\|_{q}\leq\frac{C\xi^{2}\varsigma^{2}}{\sqrt{n}},

(L.5)

where we used the assumption that
q>2.

Moreover, by (2) of Lemma
M.2
, we have that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{1}(X_{k})^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{11}(t_{k})\right|\leq C\xi^{2}\varsigma^{2}n^{-1+\frac{2}{\tau+1}},

(L.6)

where
\Pi_{11}(t)
is the first entry of
\Pi(t)
defined in (
3.27
).
Further, by Lemma
M.4
, we
have that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{11}(t_{k})-\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{11}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|=\mathrm{O}(n^{-2}).

(L.7)

We point out that
\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{11}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\Pi_{11}
which is the first entry of
\Pi
as in (
3.28
). Combining with the above arguments, we conclude that

\left\|\frac{1}{n}W_{1}^{\top}(1)W_{1}(1)-\Pi_{11}\right\|_{q}\leq C\left(\frac{\varsigma^{2}\xi^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\varsigma^{2}\xi^{2}n^{\frac{2}{\tau+1}}}{n}\right).

Similarly, we can show that

\left\|\frac{1}{n}W_{1}^{\top}(1)W_{2}(1)-\Pi_{12}\right\|_{q}\leq C\left(\frac{\varsigma^{2}\xi^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\varsigma^{2}\xi^{2}n^{\frac{2}{\tau+1}}}{n}\right).

Moreover, for
W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(1),
we recall that
W_{0}(1)=(\phi_{1}(t_{1}),\cdots,\phi_{1}(t_{n})).
Consequently, we have that

\frac{1}{n}W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(1)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{1}^{2}(t_{k}).

By the orthonormality of the basis functions and Lemma
M.4
, we obtain that

\left|\frac{1}{n}W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(1)-1\right|=\mathrm{O}(n^{-2}).

Similarly, we can prove that

\left|\frac{1}{n}W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{0}(2)\right|=\mathrm{O}(n^{-2}).

Finally, we discuss
W_{0}(1)^{\top}W_{1}(1).
By definition, we have that
