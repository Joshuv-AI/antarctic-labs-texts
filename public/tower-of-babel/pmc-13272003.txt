Insulinoma in pregnancy: a case report and literature review of diagnostic challenges, multidisciplinary management, and therapeutic dilemmas
Authors: Abdullah Faiz Zaihan, Shairyzah Ahmad Hisham, Srinivas Siwalinggam, Noor Hayatul Al Akmal Noralam, Kaeshaelya Thiruchelvam, Chia Siang Kow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulinoma during pregnancy is exceedingly rare, with fewer than 30 cases reported worldwide. Diagnosis is often delayed due to overlapping physiological and gestational symptoms, posing significant risks to both mother and fetus.CaseWe report a case of a 36-year-old gravida 4 para 2 + 1 woman presenting at 7 weeks’ gestation with recurrent symptomatic hypoglycaemia. She re-presented at 12 weeks’ gestation with persistent neuroglycopenic symptoms and weight loss. Biochemical evaluation demonstrated inappropriately non-suppressed insulin and C-peptide levels during hypoglycaemia, raising suspicion for endogenous hyperinsulinaemia. Ultrasound was non-diagnostic while magnetic resonance imaging identified a 2.3 x 2.5 cm pancreatic lesion consistent with insulinoma. A multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach involving Endocrinology, Obstetrics & Gynecology (Maternal-Fetal Medicine), Hepatobiliary Surgery, and Radiology guided the management. Due to the tumor size and procedural risks during pregnancy, definitive intervention was deferred while medical therapy with octreotide was initiated, achieving partial glycaemic control. The pregnancy was complicated by fetal growth restriction, necessitating delivery at 34 weeks. Postpartum, she underwent endoscopic ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation (EUS-RFA), requiring two sessions before achieving glycaemic stabilization.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges of insulinoma in pregnancy and underscores the importance of MDT-guided individualized management. It also illustrates the limitations of EUS-RFA and medical therapy, particularly in larger tumors, and raises important considerations regarding fetal outcomes.

Introduction

Insulinomas are the most common functional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, yet they remain rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1–4 cases per million person-years (1–3). The diagnosis of insulinoma during pregnancy is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature when excluding postpartum presentations (4, 5).

Diagnosis in pregnancy is particularly challenging. Classical features such as Whipple’s triad may be obscured by overlapping gestational symptoms including nausea, vomiting, fatigue, and dizziness, which are commonly attributed to early pregnancy or hyperemesis gravidarum. In addition, physiological changes in glucose metabolism during early pregnancy, characterized by increased insulin sensitivity and lower fasting glucose levels may further mask or exacerbate hypoglycaemia, resulting in delayed recognition (4–7).

Management of insulinoma during pregnancy presents a significant clinical dilemma. Treatment strategies must carefully balance the risks of recurrent maternal hypoglycaemia against potential fetal harm. Medical therapies such as diazoxide and somatostatin analogues carry concerns regarding teratogenicity and fetal growth restriction (8, 9), while surgical intervention introduces risks of miscarriage, preterm labor, and maternal morbidity (4). Furthermore, evidence guiding the role of minimally invasive approaches, such as endoscopic radiofrequency ablation, remains limited in this special population (10).

Given the rarity of this condition and the absence of standardized management guidelines, we report a case of insulinoma diagnosed in the first trimester, highlighting diagnostic pitfalls, multidisciplinary decision-making, and therapeutic complexities. To further contextualize our findings, we also performed a focused review of the literature to summarize reported presentations, management strategies, and maternal and fetal outcomes.

The focused literature review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus from database inception to April 2026. Search terms included combinations of “insulinoma,” “pregnancy,” “pregnant,” “gestation,” “hypoglycaemia,” “hypoglycemia,” “hyperinsulinaemia,” and “pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor.” Additional articles were identified through reference list screening of relevant reports and reviews. Eligible reports included cases of insulinoma diagnosed during pregnancy or presenting with symptoms during pregnancy. Postpartum-only diagnoses and cases diagnosed before conception were excluded unless there was clear evidence of symptom onset or diagnostic evaluation during pregnancy. Extracted data included maternal age, gestational timing, biochemical profile, tumor localization, imaging modality, treatment approach, and maternal and fetal outcomes.

Case

A 36-year-old woman (gravida 4, para 2, with one prior miscarriage) with no known medical history presented to the Emergency Department at 7 weeks’ gestation with a one-week history of dizziness, presyncope, lethargy, and vomiting. On arrival, her capillary blood glucose (CBG) was 2.5 mmol/L. She was treated with intravenous dextrose and oral glucose, resulting in prompt symptom resolution. Urine analysis demonstrated leukocyturia, and she was treated for a urinary tract infection. She was subsequently discharged with a diagnosis of hypoglycaemia secondary to poor oral intake.

At 12 weeks and 1 day of gestation, she re-presented with recurrent lethargy, severe vomiting, diaphoresis, and shivering. Her CBG was 3.0 mmol/L. She had experienced significant weight loss, from 67.5 kg at 9 weeks’ gestation to 61 kg. Further history revealed recurrent early-morning episodes of diaphoresis and giddiness predating pregnancy, occurring approximately twice monthly and associated with difficulty awakening. These episodes had worsened following conception. Although hypoglycaemic episodes had previously been documented in a primary care setting, no further evaluation had been undertaken.

She was admitted for investigation of recurrent hypoglycaemia. Biochemical evaluation during a hypoglycaemic episode demonstrated findings consistent with endogenous hyperinsulinaemia. Despite a low plasma glucose level, she exhibited inappropriately non-suppressed insulin (82.9 pmol/L; Normal: 17.8 – 173.0 pmol/L) and markedly elevated C-peptide levels (2389 pmol/L; Normal: 366.6 – 1466.6 pmol/L). This strongly supports an endogenous source of hyperinsulinaemia, effectively excluding exogenous insulin administration (11–13). Alternative causes of hypoglycaemia such as adrenal insufficiency were unlikely given preserved cortisol levels (451 nmol/L; Normal: 145.4 - 619.4 nmol/L), while the low Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (56.7 ng/mL; Normal: 73.1 – 243.0 ng/mL) argues against non-islet cell tumor hypoglycaemia.

Ultrasound of the hepatobiliary system did not identify a pancreatic lesion, consistent with the limited sensitivity of ultrasound for insulinoma detection (9%–64%) (3). This limitation is related to the retroperitoneal location of the pancreas and interference from bowel gas and adipose tissue, which may be further exacerbated during pregnancy (14, 15). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) subsequently demonstrated a well-defined lesion measuring 2.3 × 2.5 cm located between the head and tail of the pancreas (Figure 1), with features suggestive of insulinoma.

Selected axial MRI images of the upper abdomen demonstrate a well-defined, lobulated lesion in the junction of body-tail of pancreas. The lesion appears markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging [(A), yellow arrow] and remains hyperintense on T2-weighted fat-saturated sequences (B), indicating lack of signal suppression and suggesting a non-fat-containing component. Diffusion-weighted imaging (C) reveals focal areas of hyperintensity at the posterior aspect of the lesion, with corresponding low signal on the ADC map (D) (white arrow), consistent with true diffusion restriction. These findings are suggestive of increased cellularity within the lesion.

A comprehensive multidisciplinary team comprising endocrinology, maternal-fetal medicine, hepatobiliary surgery, and radiology convened to determine the optimal management strategy. Surgical resection and endoscopic ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation (EUS-RFA) were considered. However, given the tumor size (>2 cm) and potential high risks of intervention during pregnancy, a decision was made to pursue conservative management. Diazoxide was avoided due to potential fetal risks. Octreotide therapy was initiated instead, with the aim for a late-preterm delivery (≥34 weeks) to facilitate post-partum surgical or endoscopic intervention.

Following initiation of octreotide, glycaemic control improved, with CBG levels stabilizing between 4.0 – 7.0 mmol/L. She required prolonged inpatient monitoring and was discharged at 19 weeks’ gestation after a 48-day hospitalization. She remained under close outpatient follow-up.

At 34 weeks’ gestation, an elective cesarean delivery was performed. A female infant weighing 1.21 kg was delivered, consistent with stage 1 fetal growth restriction. During this peripartum period, she experienced recurrent severe hypoglycaemia (CBG 2.0 – 3.5 mmol/L) despite escalation of therapy with the addition of diazoxide.

Definitive management was undertaken with Hepatobiliary and Gastroenterology team proceeded with EUS-RFA. EUS confirmed a 2.5 cm hypoechoic lesion in the pancreatic body-tail region, and RFA was performed at 50 watts. Despite the initial RFA, she continued to experience frequent hypoglycaemia, requiring maximal optimization of octreotide and diazoxide. A repeat EUS-RFA was performed two weeks later. A 3.0 cm hypoechoic area was visualized, and RFA was performed utilizing 5 passes, successfully ablating the lesion.

Following the second intervention, she achieved complete clinical and biochemical resolution. Her CBG stabilized between 4.0 – 7.0 mmol/L after discontinuation of medical therapy, and she was discharged in a stable condition.

The timing and nature of symptom onset in our patient are consistent with prior reports. Most reported cases present during the first trimester or early second trimester, although postpartum presentations are also well described. In several reported cases, patients described intermittent hypoglycaemic symptoms prior to conception that were either mild or misattributed, followed by worsening during early gestation. A similar trajectory was observed in our patient, whose recurrent early-morning hypoglycaemic symptoms predated pregnancy but were not investigated. This highlights a common diagnostic pitfall, where early symptoms are overlooked or attributed to benign causes.

Diagnostic delay is a recurring feature across reported cases. As summarized in Table 1, initial misdiagnosis is frequent, with symptoms often attributed to hyperemesis gravidarum, poor oral intake, or other common gestational conditions (4, 6). Our patient was initially managed for hypoglycaemia secondary to poor oral intake, reflecting this pattern. Despite this, biochemical confirmation remains a cornerstone of diagnosis. Where reported, biochemical findings consistently demonstrate inappropriately elevated insulin and C-peptide levels during hypoglycaemia. The biochemical findings in our case are therefore in keeping with previously reported cases, underscoring the importance of early laboratory evaluation in patients with recurrent or unexplained hypoglycaemia.

β-hydroxybutyrate was not available in the clinical records. Although suppressed ketogenesis during hypoglycaemia would have provided additional supportive evidence for endogenous hyperinsulinaemia, the diagnosis was strongly supported by documented hypoglycaemia, inappropriately non-suppressed insulin, markedly elevated C-peptide, MRI localization of a pancreatic lesion, and subsequent biochemical resolution after tumor-directed therapy. There was no known family history of endocrine neoplasia or inherited metabolic disease. Alternative inherited or hormonal causes were considered unlikely given the adult onset, absence of childhood hypoglycaemia, biochemical evidence of endogenous hyperinsulinaemia, radiological localization of a pancreatic lesion, and resolution after tumor-directed therapy.

Imaging findings in our case also parallel those described in the literature. Ultrasound, although commonly used as an initial modality due to its safety profile, frequently fails to detect insulinomas because of its limited sensitivity (3, 14, 15). MRI has emerged as the preferred modality during pregnancy due to its higher diagnostic yield and acceptable safety profile. In our patient, ultrasound was non-diagnostic, whereas MRI successfully localized a pancreatic lesion, consistent with patterns observed in prior reports.

The most important point of comparison lies in management strategies. As outlined in Table 1, three principal approaches have been described: medical therapy during pregnancy, surgical resection during the second trimester, and delayed definitive treatment postpartum. The choice of strategy is individualized and depends on tumor characteristics, gestational age, and severity of hypoglycaemia.

Medical therapy is frequently employed as a temporizing measure, particularly in early pregnancy. Octreotide has been the most commonly reported agent, with variable efficacy. In several reported cases, partial glycaemic control was achieved, although breakthrough hypoglycaemia remained a concern. Our case is consistent with this pattern, as octreotide improved glycaemic stability but did not completely prevent recurrent hypoglycaemia, particularly during the peripartum period. In addition, fetal growth restriction has been reported in some cases managed conservatively or with prolonged medical therapy. However, fetal growth restriction in the present case should be interpreted as multifactorial rather than attributed directly to octreotide or conservative management. Potential contributors include significant maternal weight loss, reduced nutritional intake during early pregnancy, recurrent hypoglycaemia, and possible placental insufficiency. Therefore, the fetal growth restriction observed in our patient is best interpreted as an important clinical association that is aligned with previously reported outcomes, rather than as evidence of a direct treatment-related effect.

Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for insulinoma and has been successfully performed during the second trimester in selected cases, with favorable maternal and fetal outcomes. However, as demonstrated in Table 1, surgery is often undertaken in patients with well-localized tumors or in those with severe or refractory hypoglycaemia. In contrast, our patient had a relatively large lesion (>2 cm) in the pancreatic body-tail region, which increased the complexity and risk of surgical intervention. This likely contributed to the decision to defer surgery, consistent with cases in which operative risk was considered high.

Postpartum definitive treatment options include surgical resection and minimally invasive endoscopic ablation. Although surgery remains the standard when complete tumor removal and histological confirmation are required, EUS-RFA was selected in this case after multidisciplinary discussion because the lesion was localized, endoscopically accessible, and suitable for a less invasive approach. This avoided major pancreatic surgery and potentially reduced perioperative morbidity. However, the need for two ablation sessions highlights a limitation of EUS-RFA, particularly for larger lesions.

Minimally invasive approaches, such as EUS-RFA, are increasingly used in non-pregnant populations but remain poorly described in pregnancy. Our case contributes to this limited body of evidence. Notably, while most reported cases undergoing definitive treatment achieve resolution following a single intervention, our patient required two sessions of radiofrequency ablation to achieve biochemical remission. This suggests that larger lesions may be less amenable to single-session ablation, an observation that has not been consistently reported and may have implications for procedural planning.

Maternal outcomes across reported cases are generally favorable with appropriate management. However, fetal outcomes are more variable. As summarized in Table 1, fetal growth restriction and preterm delivery are among the reported complications, particularly in cases managed conservatively or with prolonged medical therapy. Our case is consistent with these findings, with the development of fetal growth restriction necessitating delivery at 34 weeks’ gestation.

An important concept emerging from comparison with the literature is the timing of definitive treatment. Two broad management pathways can be identified: immediate intervention during pregnancy and delayed treatment postpartum. Our case supports a staged approach, whereby maternal glycaemic control is achieved during pregnancy using medical therapy, followed by definitive intervention after delivery. This strategy may be particularly appropriate in patients with stable disease or when procedural risks are considered high.

Importantly, our case demonstrates several features that distinguish it from previously reported cases. First, the patient presented early in the first trimester with symptoms that clearly predated pregnancy, yet remained uninvestigated until gestation, further emphasizing the extent of diagnostic delay that can occur. Second, although octreotide is commonly used, our case highlights its limitations in achieving sustained glycaemic control, particularly during the peripartum period despite escalation of therapy. Third, definitive treatment required two sessions of endoscopic ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation, suggesting that larger tumors may require staged or repeated interventions. Finally, this case illustrates the successful application of a staged management strategy, supporting delayed postpartum intervention in selected patients.

Given the rarity of insulinoma in pregnancy and the absence of standardized management guidelines, a multidisciplinary approach is essential. Close collaboration between endocrinology, maternal-fetal medicine, surgery, and radiology allows for individualized decision-making and optimization of maternal and fetal outcomes.

This report has several limitations. The available evidence is limited to case reports and small case series, resulting in heterogeneity in reporting and management strategies. In addition, comparisons across cases are descriptive and not based on standardized outcome measures. Histological confirmation, Ki-67 index, and tumor grading were not available because definitive treatment was performed using EUS-guided radiofrequency ablation rather than surgical resection. This limits pathological characterization of the lesion. Biochemical profile, imaging findings, and clinical response to tumor-directed therapy strongly supported the diagnosis of insulinoma. Nevertheless, by integrating our case with a focused synthesis of previously reported cases, we provide clinically relevant insights into the diagnosis and management of insulinoma in pregnancy.

In conclusion, insulinoma in pregnancy is a rare but often under-recognized cause of hypoglycaemia, with diagnosis frequently delayed by nonspecific symptoms and limited imaging sensitivity. Biochemical confirmation remains essential, while MRI and EUS improve tumor localization. Management should be individualized, balancing maternal stability and fetal safety. Our case supports a staged approach, with medical stabilization during pregnancy followed by definitive postpartum intervention, and highlights that larger tumors may require repeated treatment. Early recognition and multidisciplinary care are critical to optimizing maternal and fetal outcomes.
