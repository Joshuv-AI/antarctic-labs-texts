Structural inequalities and dietary diversity in Odisha: evidence from NSSO 68th and 79th rounds
Authors: Mayadhar Sethy, Sandhya R. Mahapatro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study attempts to measure the caste-based dietary gap and relative contribution of different socioeconomic factors in Odisha, India, through the lens of the Capability Approach. We used National Sample Survey 68th (2011–12) and 79th (2022–23) rounds data that includes 13,113 sample. Dietary diversity was assessed using a 13-food group score, categorized into low, medium, and high based on established FAO/FANTA thresholds. The caste gap in dietary disparities is assessed using Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition for non-linear models. The decomposition analysis reveals the magnitude of caste-based dietary gap and the contribution of observed and unobserved factors to such gap. A gradual improvement in dietary diversity from low to medium category has been observed among SC/ST between 2011 and 12 and 2022-23 in Odisha. The marginal effects show socioeconomic status, household composition, cooking fuel &location are the increases the probability of being in the medium and higher dietary diversity. About 76% and 57% of caste-based inequalities in high and medium dietary diversity (with reference to low dietary diversity) explained by observable differences in endowments. While education and wealth (endowment component) are the key drivers of dietary diversity, the large unexplained component for shift from low to medium dietary status underscores the structural barriers such as social exclusion, cultural norms and differential market access that reinforces caste inequalities. Addressing the caste-based dietary gap therefore requires equity-focused, multidimensional nutrition strategies that combine economic development with targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32743-y.

Introduction

Dietary diversity is widely recognized as a critical indicator of diet quality and nutritional adequacy1,2. It is typically measured by estimating the number of different food items or food groups included in a household’s food basket over a specified period1. Traditionally, dietary diversity was viewed primarily as a reflection of individual knowledge or dietary behavior. However, recent evidence underscores its deep interlinkages with socioeconomic and demographic structures2,3. Thus, dietary diversity represents not only the range of nutrients consumed but also broader aspects of food access, cultural preferences, and the social determinants shaping food choices.

In India, and particularly in economically disadvantaged regions such as Odisha, dietary patterns are influenced by intersecting factors including income, caste, education, occupation, landholding size, and geographic location4,5. Historically, Indian food system have been embedded within social hierarchies and agrarian structures. Lower-income and tribal households, especially in rural areas, have relied on subsistence farming, consuming simple yet nutritionally adequate diets centered on locally grown cereals and pulses. In contrast, upper-caste and wealthier families with greater access to land and markets have enjoyed more diverse diets that include dairy products, ghee, and festive foods6,7. These enduring disparities in food access mirror broader social stratification and continue to influence dietary diversity outcomes today.

The caste system remains a fundamental basis of social and economic inequality in India. Although development has brought some convergence between caste groups, outcomes remain uneven across caste groups. For instance, the average Dalit woman in India lives 14.6 years less than a higher-caste woman, largely due to poorer access to healthcare, nutrition, sanitation, and safe water8. A wide body of research documents highlighted how caste-based disparities shape outcomes in health, education, and employment with disfavoring the marginalized9–11. Despite welfare programs of government such as the Public Distribution System (PDS), Integrated Child Development Scheme (ICDS), Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), Antyodaya Anna Yojana, and Poshan Abhiyaan, caste-based nutritional inequalities persist. Caste-linked deprivation continues to regulate health outcomes, while the social discrimination endured by lower castes reinforces exclusion from mainstream opportunities, perpetuating poor health and nutritional outcomes7,12.

Although inequalities in nutritional status across caste groups are well documented, relatively few studies explored how caste influences food choices and dietary patterns13,14. Cultural taboos and ritual practices deeply shape these patterns. For instance, upper-caste households often adhere to strict vegetarian diets, avoiding ingredients such as garlic and onion during religious observances. Such practices reflect beliefs surrounding purity and ritual conduct rather than mere personal preferences. A notable cultural example is the Mahaprasad of the Jagannath Temple in Puri symbolically unifying yet inaccessible to many due to economic constraints15. Analyses of NFHS-5 data confirm persistent rural–urban divides and caste-based disparities in dietary practices3. Studies in Odisha and Jharkhand further reveal that tribal populations experience severe nutritional deprivation despite targeted poverty reduction programs and decline in wealth inequalities, highlighting the importance of non-income determinants16. Evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of non-poor Indian households remain nutritionally insecure, implying that economic growth alone cannot resolve nutritional inequity17.

Odisha, one of India’s Empowered Action Group (EAG) states, ranks poorly in health and nutritional indicators, as revealed by various rounds of National Family Health Surveys. With over 40% of its population belonging to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes making it the third-largest state in tribal population18. Food insecurity in Odisha is deeply tied to systemic social and economic disadvantages. Empirical studies in Mayurbhanj district19 show that limited land access, low education, and inadequate income restrict dietary options for marginalized communities. While public distribution systems have improved calorie sufficiency, they have been less effective in ensuring access to nutrient-dense and diverse foods4,6. These nutritional gaps reveal deeper structural inequities that persist despite multiple policy interventions. Lower-income and tribal households, particularly in rural areas, continue to face overlapping economic, social, and cultural barriers that constrain their dietary diversity. Further, Covid-19 pandemic disproportionately affected the dietary diversity among low caste families as most of them were in informal work. These challenges underscore the need to understand how the dietary gap and nutritional inequality among the caste group is evolved overtime in Odisha.

To conceptualize these multifaceted barriers, this study applies the Capability Approach developed by Amartya Sen20 to analyse the caste-based disparities in dietary status. This framework posits that well-being depends on an individual’s “capabilities” the substantive freedom to achieve desired state of being and doing. In nutritional terms, dietary diversity reflects not just the food consumed but the capability to access and utilize a nutritious diet. This capability is shaped by personal, social, and environmental “conversion factors” that determine how resources translate into outcomes. Structural inequalities associated with caste, class, and geography operate as negative conversion factors, restricting marginalized groups from converting available resources such as income, education, and assets into improved dietary outcomes21. Guided by the Capability Approach, this study argues that disparities in dietary diversity arise not merely from material deprivation but from systemic barriers that limit the effective conversion of resources into nutritional well-being. Through this framework, the study aims to deepen the understanding of the structural underpinnings of caste-based dietary inequality in Odisha for equitable nutrition policies in India.

The primary objective of the study is to measure the caste-based dietary gap and relative contribution of different socioeconomic factors to such differences in Odisha. Accordingly, this research addresses two central questions: First, what are the key socioeconomic and demographic determinants of household dietary diversity? Secondly, to what extent are the caste-based disparities in dietary diversity explained by differences in socioeconomic deprivation or due to discrimination and disadvantageous position. The findings based on such analysis will answer why do inequalities in dietary diversity persist between lower and upper caste groups despite various welfare measures aimed at reducing caste disparities?

Materials and methods

Data

This study utilized nationally representative secondary data from the 68th (2011–12) and 79th (2022–23) rounds of the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO), conducted by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), Government of India. Both survey rounds employed a multistage stratified sampling design and provided comprehensive data on household-level consumption, demographic, and socioeconomic indicators. The use of these two rounds enables a temporal comparison of dietary diversity in Odisha. Owing to the NSSO’s rigorous data collection methodology and nationwide coverage, the data serve as a credible and reliable source for analyzing household nutrition dynamics in India22,23.

Both survey rounds collected detailed household-level information on expenditure, consumption of food items, landholding, headship, occupational status, education, social group, religion, household resources, and access to the Public Distribution System (PDS), among other characteristics. The sample size for analysis comprised 13,113 households (4,019 from 2011 to 12 and 9,094 from 2022 to 23) from the state of Odisha. Only households with complete records on food consumption and demographic indicators were included; those with missing information on key variables were excluded. After data cleaning, a total of 9,987 households were retained for statistical analysis and to correct for the complex survey design. Since the population studied in both rounds differs, use of standard weight may lead to errors. Hence, sampling weights provided by the NSSO were applied to each round and then was normalized for the pooled data. Normalization was done by dividing each weight by the sum of weights within its round and multiply by a constant. For our study we have used the constant – 1.

Following the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) guidelines and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) methodology, food items were classified into 13 major food groups: (1) cereals, (2) pulses, legumes, nuts, and seeds, (3) milk and milk products, (4) meat and poultry, (5) eggs, (6) fish and seafood, (7) vegetables, (8) fruits, (9) oils and fats, (10) sugar and sweets, (11) condiments and beverages, (12) tubers and roots, and (13) processed foods. A binary indicator was generated for each food group, assigning a score of one if the food group was consumed during the reference period and zero otherwise. The values were summed across all food groups to yield a Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) ranging from 0 to 13 for each household, in accordance with FAO recommendations.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable for this study was household dietary diversity, categorized into three levels based on established thresholds proposed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) project24,25. These thresholds, validated across various contexts to reflect levels of micronutrient adequacy, define dietary diversity scores (DDS) as low (0–4), medium (5–8), and high (9–13).This categorization aligns with prior research26,27 demonstrating that higher dietary diversity is associated with greater energy and nutrient intake, while lower diversity reflects limited nutrient adequacy1. All food group data were adjusted for household size to standardize dietary intake.

Household dietary diversity (HDD) was assessed using food consumption data from the NSSO, which reports both the frequency and quantity of food consumption over a 30-day or 7-day reference period. While both the rounds adopted a Modified Mixed Reference Period (MMRP) that collected data on a wide range of food and non-food items, there are changes in the recall period for some food items between two periods. For comparability, necessary adjustments made with all food consumption data were standardized to a 24-hour equivalent reference period to maintain consistency across survey years.

Explanatory variables

A comprehensive set of explanatory variables was included to capture socioeconomic, demographic, and infrastructural factors known to influence dietary diversity13,28. The primary exposure variable was household caste status, as reported by the NSSO. The survey classifies households into four major social groups: Scheduled Castes (SCs), Scheduled Tribes (STs), Other Backward Classes (OBCs), and Others. For analytical clarity, the sample households were categorized as SC/ST (marginalized groups) and non-SC/ST (including Other backward class and general caste).

A range of predictors was included to explain the caste-based gap in dietary diversity. Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure (MPCE), used as a proxy for household wealth, was categorized into five quintiles-lowest, lower, middle, richer, and richest. The upper two quintiles (upper 40%) were considered economically “better-off.” The education level of the household head was classified as below secondary or secondary and above. Religion was categorized into Hindu and non-Hindu. Infrastructure and amenities were represented by variables such as type of cooking fuel (LPG/electricity, firewood/coal, others) and ration card ownership. Demographic factors included household size, sex of the household head, and proportion of children and adolescents. Location was dichotomized into rural and urban areas.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were first computed to summarize household characteristics by survey year and dietary diversity category. The primary objective was to measure the caste gap in dietary diversity. Three separate binary logistic regression models were estimated to identify the determinants of dietary diversity transitions and the dependent variables are Low-High DDS (0 = low, 1 = high), low-medium DDS (1 = medium, 0 = low), medium-High DDS (0 = medium, 1 = high).

Before model estimation, a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) test was performed to check for multicollinearity among predictors and model fit was assessed using the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) to confirm the significance of including caste as a key determinant.

To quantify the contribution of various determinants to caste-based inequality in dietary diversity, a non-linear Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition analysis was employed13,29,30. This technique decomposes the observed gap in (DDS) between two groups; SC/ST and non-SC/ST into an “explained” component, reflecting differences in endowments (i.e., socioeconomic and demographic characteristics), and an “unexplained” component, representing differences in coefficients or returns to these characteristics. The unexplained portion is interpreted as indicative of structural discrimination, unmeasured contextual barriers, or caste-based differences in how resources translate into dietary outcomes.

This decomposition followed recent extensions of the Oaxaca–Blinder framework for non-linear models applicable to categorical dependent variables29,31. Results

Household dietary diversity in Odisha, 2011-12 & 2022-23

Figure 1 illustrates the variation in household dietary diversity in Odisha between 2011 and 12 and 2022–23. Based on the categorization described earlier, the proportion of households with low dietary diversity declined markedly from 57.5% in 2011–12 to 41.8% in 2022–23. During the same period, the proportion of households in medium dietary diversity found more than doubled from 22.7% to 49.1% indicating a broad improvement in the diversity of household diets.

Conversely, the proportion of households attaining high dietary diversity decreased from 19.7% to 9.0%, suggesting that while households have moved away from severely restricted diets, they continue to struggle to achieve optimal dietary variety and quality. This pattern reflects a general improvement from a very low nutritional base, yet points to persistent structural and economic barriers preventing many households particularly those from marginalized groups from accessing a wide range of nutrient-rich foods.

The burden of low dietary diversity was disproportionately borne by Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) households in both survey rounds, with 66.9% in 2011–12 and 48.6% in 2022–23. In contrast, the proportion of non-SC/ST households experiencing low dietary diversity was considerably lower (50.5% in 2011–12 and 35.8% in 2022–23). Similarly, the proportion of SC/ST households achieving high dietary diversity in 2022–23 was only 6%, compared with double that proportion among non-SC/ST households.

The patterns over time indicate a shift from low to medium dietary diversity across both social groups, whereas the proportion of households with high dietary diversity declined. For example, SC/ST households in the high dietary diversity category fell from 14.6% in 2011–12 to 6.0% in 2022–23. A similar decline was observed among non-SC/ST households. Statistical analysis confirmed that the relationship between caste and household dietary diversity was significant (p < 0.001).

A clear socioeconomic gradient in dietary diversity was observed (Appendix S1). Households with less educated heads, belonging to the poorest monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) quintile, or relying on firewood/coal for cooking were more likely to exhibit low dietary diversity. Conversely, high dietary diversity, characterized by consumption of a wider range of foods, was concentrated among the most educated, wealthiest, and urban households. Chi-square tests indicated that all covariates, except religion, were significantly associated with dietary diversity status (p < 0.001; p < 0.01; p < 0.05).

Associated factors of dietary diversity

Table 2 presents the determinants of household dietary diversity estimated through logistic regression models, including marginal effects, significance levels, standard errors, and likelihood ratio tests. Marginal effects were estimated over odds ration because they measure the impact of each predictor on the overall probability of achieving a particular dietary diversity outcome, irrespective of the base outcome32.

The analysis did not reveal any significant caste-based differences in medium versus high dietary diversity (Model-III), and therefore, the subsequent decomposition analysis focused on low-to-high and low-to-medium dietary diversity transitions. The likelihood ratio tests for Models I (low-to-high) and II (low-to-medium) indicated that including caste categories significantly improved model fit (p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis that the reduced models (excluding caste) were sufficient. This finding underscores the importance of caste or social identity in shaping dietary outcomes, even after accounting for other demographic, socioeconomic actors.

Multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed that all predictor variables had a variance inflation factor (VIF) below 2, indicating the absence of multicollinearity; hence, all variables were retained in the final models.

The goodness-of-fit for the final models was reasonable, and the estimated parameter signs aligned with theoretical expectations. Some statistically insignificant variables were retained in the models because they were part of a group of conceptually relevant covariates.

Compared with non-SC/ST households, SC/ST households had a 4% lower probability of being in medium or high dietary diversity categories, as indicated by Models I and II. Higher educational attainment of the household head, defined as secondary education or above, was positively associated with dietary diversity, increasing the probability of being in high and medium dietary diversity categories by 9% and 6%, respectively, compared with low dietary diversity. Regarding demographic characteristics, the presence of and adolescents in the household significantly increased the likelihood of medium and high dietary diversity by 9% and 6%, respectively. Household headship was not a significant predictor of dietary diversity, whereas household size slightly increased the probability of medium and high dietary diversity by 3% and 2%, respectively.

Household wealth emerged as a strong determinant of dietary diversity. Households in the upper economic class were 26% more likely to achieve high dietary diversity compared with low dietary diversity. Similarly, the use of clean cooking fuels (LPG/Gas) increased the probability of being in high and medium dietary diversity categories by 13% and 9%, respectively. For transitions from medium to high dietary diversity, the use of clean cooking fuel increased the probability by 6%. The presence of children also contributed positively, increasing the probability of medium and high dietary diversity by 4% and 3%, respectively.

Temporal changes in dietary diversity indicated that, in 2022–23, the probability of being in the medium dietary diversity category increased by 23%, while the probability of high dietary diversity declined by 9% compared with 2011–12. This pattern confirms a general shift from low to medium dietary diversity but indicates limited progress in achieving high-quality diets, consistent with trends shown in Fig. 1. Compared with rural households, the probability of transitioning from low to high dietary diversity was higher by 4%, significant at the 10% level; however, this variable was not significant in Models II and III.

Decomposing the SC/ST versus Non-SC/ST dietary diversity gap

The Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition was employed to examine the average gap in household dietary diversity between socially advantaged households (non-SC/ST) and disadvantaged households (SC/ST). Fig. 2 presents the explained characteristics (endowment) and unexplained component (coefficient and interaction effect combinedly) contributed to total differences. The data shows 3/4th of caste differences in dietary gap for low to high diet is explained by endowment factors and for low to medium dietary status, explained component contributed to 57% of total differences.

Tables 3 and 4 present the relative contributions of independent variables to caste-based inequalities in medium and high dietary diversity compared with low dietary diversity, including endowment, coefficient, and interaction effects.

Table 3 shows that the overall dietary diversity gap between SC/ST and non-SC/ST households was 10%. Approximately 57% of the caste-based gap in medium dietary diversity (compared with low dietary diversity) was explained by differences in household characteristics (endowments). This suggests that caste inequalities in achieving medium dietary diversity could be substantially reduced if SC/ST and non-SC/ST households had comparable access to education, wealth, and clean cooking fuel. For example, equalizing access to education and economic improvement could reduce caste-based disparities in medium dietary diversity by 20% and 35.5%, respectively. The presence of children and adolescents narrowed the gap by 5%.

The coefficient (discrimination) effect, representing structural differences in returns to characteristics, accounted for 27% of the total gap, while the interaction effect contributed 15.7%. Among the coefficient effects, being in the upper economic class (−30.7%) and using LPG/Gas (−137.2%) were particularly influential. The large positive constant (41.29%) reflects baseline disparities not explained by the included covariates, capturing structural advantages enjoyed by socially privileged households.

Table 4 presents the results of the caste-based decomposition of dietary diversity for high versus lower dietary diversity households. A substantial gap was observed between SC/ST and non-SC/ST households, with a 17% difference in high versus low dietary diversity, disfavoring SC/ST households (p < 0.001). The explained component, attributable to differences in household endowments, accounted for 76% of this gap (p < 0.001). This indicates that if SC/ST households had the same socioeconomic and demographic characteristics as non-SC/ST households, their probability of achieving high dietary diversity would increase by 76%.

The unexplained component, reflecting differences in returns to characteristics, contributed 13% (coefficient effect) and 9.5% (interaction effect) of the total gap, with weak significance at the 10% level. These findings suggest that caste inequalities in achieving high dietary diversity could largely be mitigated if both social groups had equal access to higher education, wealth, and clean cooking fuel.

Decomposition of individual determinants revealed that household economic status was the largest contributor, explaining 25.77% of the overall disparity between SC/ST and non-SC/ST households. Higher educational attainment (above secondary) contributed 21.5%, access to clean cooking fuel (LPG/Gas) explained 14.6%, temporal changes (2022–23) contributed 8%, and urban residence contributed 3%. Interestingly, the presence of children and adolescents slightly narrowed the gap, contributing − 1.78% to the overall difference.

In the unexplained portion (coefficient effect) of the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition, certain factors exhibited significant effects. Notably, household economic status (estimate: −0.02 for both poor and upper class) and access to clean cooking fuel (estimate: 0.05) were statistically significant. The explained component in both models was significant at the 1% level, confirming that differences in economic status, household environment, and human capital are the primary drivers of caste-based dietary disparities.

The negative coefficients in the unexplained portion indicate that even when SC/ST households have access to clean fuel or improved economic resources, the impact on improving dietary diversity from low to medium or low to high is smaller for SC/ST households compared to non-SC/ST households. This suggests the presence of structural and behavioral barriers, including limited awareness, unequal utilization of resources, and other contextual constraints that hinder the translation of available resources into improved dietary outcomes.

Discussion

Numerous studies have explored caste-based disparities in nutritional outcomes, yet limited attention has been given to how the caste system shapes dietary diversity a crucial predictor of nutritional status. Given Odisha’s persistently poor nutritional achievements and its heterogeneity across caste groups, this study investigates the caste-based dietary diversity gap in the state. It is the first of its kind to measure dietary diversity across caste groups and to assess the contribution of socioeconomic factors to this gap.

The findings reveal that although household dietary diversity in Odisha improved between 2011 and 12 and 2022–23, with a general shift from low to medium dietary diversity, substantial inequalities remain across caste lines. A higher proportion of SC/ST households continue to experience low dietary diversity compared to non-SC/ST households. Limited access to land, livelihood opportunities, and nutritional knowledge are key barriers to achieving diverse diets. These patterns align with earlier research highlighting deep-rooted socioeconomic and demographic disparities across caste groups4,5,7,12.

The decomposition results indicate that caste differences significantly shape dietary diversity outcomes. Approximately 17% and 10% of the differences in high and medium dietary diversity status, respectively, are attributable to caste inequality. Supporting studies confirm that lower-caste households in Odisha are more deprived of nutritious food6. The Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition shows that 77% and 57% of the caste gap in dietary status for high and medium status is explained by observable characteristics. Economic factors alone account for over 50% and 30% of the inequality in medium and high dietary diversity, respectively, relative to low diversity. Wealthier and better-educated households consume a wider variety of foods, leading to higher dietary diversity3,33. Conversely, financial constraints compel poorer households to prioritize calorie intake over dietary variety, relying heavily on staple foods and resulting in nutrient deficiencies34.

Education and access to clean cooking fuel emerge as positive predictors, consistent with existing evidence17,35. Education enhances awareness and health-seeking behavior, while clean fuels save time, reduce respiratory illnesses, and enable diverse cooking practices. Their contribution to the explained gap highlights marginalized groups’ restricted access to these assets. Rural households in Odisha exhibit lower dietary diversity due to limited market access, poor education, and widespread poverty36. The negative coefficient for the presence of children is particularly concerning; it implies that the nutritional needs of children in disadvantaged households remain unmet, perpetuating intergenerational malnutrition despite targeted programs37.

The large unexplained component (45%) for the low-to-medium diversity transition suggests that even when marginalized households possess enabling assets, their returns in terms of dietary diversity remain low. Contextual barriers; such as poor market integration, unaffordability of diverse foods, and cultural norms, inferior public services in tribal regions, limit the benefits of available resources. Cultural preferences and traditional food practices strongly influence food choices and dietary diversity15.

The key finding underscores that caste-based inequalities in dietary diversity persist in Odisha, even though a gradual improvement from low to medium diversity occurred among the marginalized. Widening economic inequality and technological development has wider ramification on caste inequalities38 indicating benefits of developments may not be reaching to all subsections SC/STs alike. Public policies although narrowing the caste-gap in developmental indicator, social discrimination act as major barrier in upward mobility among marginalized communities39,40. The evidence strongly supports the Capability Approach: resources alone are insufficient for achieving nutritional well-being amid structural barriers. Lower-caste groups remain the most disadvantaged, with over half still consuming low-diversity diets. This aligns with studies documenting persistent under nutrition among tribal populations in Odisha16, reflecting how historical marginalization and geographic isolation constrain capabilities20. The observed “glass ceiling” effect, where improvement occurs up to medium but not high dietary diversity illustrates that structural factors continue to obstruct progress toward optimal diets.

The study has the limitation of confining the analysis in measuring dietary diversity through food group counts; it fails to account the calorie consumption- measure nutrient intake. Secondly, the 7-day and 30-day recall data may be affected by recall bias, and as a household-level measure, DDS obscures individual dietary differences. Both the surveys used modified mixed recall period (MMRP), however, for certain items recall period changes between two rounds. The other contextual factors that contributed to the unexplained components is not adequately captured through the secondary data. Third, in the absence of panel data, we are unable to assess the persistent disadvantages in dietary intake of same household. Despite this, the study has the advantage of measuring household dietary diversity based on a wide range of food items and contribute to the existing literature on caste inequality.

Conclusion

The study results show that, although a marked shift in household dietary diversity from low to medium status exhibits over the past decade in the state, the aspiration of universal, high-quality diets remains distant, and progress is unevenly distributed along social lines. The SC/ST households are deprived in accessing dietary varieties from their counter parts. While economic status and education played a greater role in transition from low to high dietary diversity, the other structural barriers (such as discrimination, lack of awareness, cultural norms) is critical for transition from low to medium dietary diversity status. The analysis highlights that the challenge is not solely a lack of resources among marginalized groups, but also a systemic failure to translate available resources into nutritional well-being.

These findings underscore the need for policy interventions that go beyond resource provision to actively dismantle structural barriers limiting dietary outcomes. Enhancing employment opportunities by addressing the skill-based inequalities will uplift the economic status of these disadvantaged groups that help in accessing diverse foods. Kitchen gardening and community gardening should be promoted to make access the fruits and vegetables at affordable prices. The Nutrition programs such as Shree Anna Abhiyan, Poshan Abhiyan should explicitly address caste and tribal disparities by targeting agricultural extension services, nutrition education, and clean energy initiatives, such as the UJJWALA program, with culturally sensitive messages tailored to local food practices. In addition, improving physical access to diverse food markets in remote areas and enhancing the quality and variety of Public Distribution System (PDS) offerings are critical for dietary diversity. The study findings are potentially generalizable to other Indian states with similar socioeconomic patterns such as having significant tribal populations, high intensity of social disparities in developmental indicators, low economic development and poor nutritional outcomes. The insights gained will help in designing policies in those states to identify the risk group and for implementation of appropriate strategies. Moreover, the findings provide timely and policy relevant information on dietary gaps between social groups that support India’s commitment towards achieving Sustainable Development Goal-10, the goal of reducing inequalities.

Future research should focus on factors contributing to the unexplained components of caste-based dietary gaps, including social discrimination, gender dynamics, and local market failures. Addressing dietary inequality thus, requires a fundamental shift from resource-based approaches to a capability-oriented framework, ensuring that all social groups have genuine freedom to achieve a healthy and diverse diet.
