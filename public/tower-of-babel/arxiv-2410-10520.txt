1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact Hausdorff group, and let
P(\mathcal{G})
denote the set of all regular probability measures on
\mathcal{G}
. It is well-known that
P(\mathcal{G})
is a semigroup under convolution, and it is abelian if and only if
\mathcal{G}
is abelian. Furthermore,
P(\mathcal{G})
is a compact, convex set under the weak topology of measures. This topological semigroup has been intensively studied by many researchers over the years. For example, refer to the classic monographs by Parthasarathy
[
10
]
, Grenander
[
4
]
, and Mukherjea and Tserpes
[
8
]
.

Of particular relevance to this paper, Wendel
[
14
]
established many significant results regarding the algebraic, topological, and geometrical structures of
P(\mathcal{G})
. In particular, he showed that it is a closed, convex semigroup, which is not a group except when
\mathcal{G}=\{e\}
, the trivial group, by demonstrating that the only invertible elements are the point-mass measures. The problem we consider is the algebraic regularity of
P(\mathcal{G})
. A semigroup is algebraically regular if every element has a generalized inverse.
The following theorem was proved in
[
9
]
recently by the author:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a compact Hausdorff topological group such that every element in
G
is of finite order. Let

M(G)=\text{Conv}\{\delta_{g}:g\in G\}
,
where ’Conv’ denotes the convex hull. For an arbitrary finite set
\{g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n}\}\subset G
, then

(1.1)

\mu=\frac{\delta_{e}+\delta_{g_{1}}+\delta_{g_{2}}+...+\delta_{g_{n}}}{n+1},

is regular in
M(G)
.Moreover, the elements of the following form namely,

(1.2)

\{\delta_{g}\star\mu\star\delta_{h}:g,h\in G\quad\&\quad\mu=\frac{\delta_{e}+\delta_{g_{1}}+\delta_{g_{2}}+...+\delta_{g_{n}}}{n+1}\}.

are also regular in
M(G)
.

.
The purpose of this note is to show that the theorem is valid for locally compact ,Hausdorff topological groups in which every element is of finite order.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
G
be a locally compact Hausdorff topological group, and
\mathcal{B}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra of all Borel sets in
G
. A probability measure
\mu
is a nonnegative countably additive function on
\mathcal{B}
such that the total mass
\mu(G)=1
. A point mass measure or Dirac delta measure is a measure
\mu
for which there is an element
x\in G
such that
\mu(A)=1
if
x\in A
and zero otherwise,
A\in\mathcal{B}
. Such a measure is usually denoted by
\delta_{x}
. One of the interesting results of Wendel is that the only invertible elements in
P(G)
are Dirac delta measures.

The product in
P(G)
is the convolution
\star
, defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

(
Convolution
)
Let
\mu,\nu\in P(G)
. Then
\mu\star\nu
is the probability measure defined as
\mu\star\nu(A)=\int\mu(Ax^{-1})\,d\nu(x)

for every
A\in\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 2.2
.

(
Generalized Inverse
)
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a semigroup, and let
s\in\mathcal{S}
. An element
s^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{S}
is called a generalized inverse of
s
if
ss^{\dagger}s=s
.

Remark 2.3
.

In a general semigroup
\Omega
, if
\omega\in\Omega
has a generalized inverse, then it has a Moore-Penrose inverse: to be explicit, if
\omega g\omega=g
for some
g\in\Omega
, then

(2.1)

\omega\omega^{\dagger}\omega=\omega\quad\&,

(2.2)

\omega^{\dagger}\omega\omega^{\dagger}=\omega^{\dagger},

where
\omega^{\dagger}=g\omega g
. So to characterize generalized invertibility, it will be enough to characterize Moore-Penrose invertibility.

The following lemma is proved in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
9
]

Let
G
be a locally compact, Hausdorff group and
\mu
be a regular element in
P(G)
with Moore-Penrose inverse
\mu^{\dagger}
. Suppose
e\in S(\mu)
and every element of
S(\mu)
is of finite order. Then
S(\mu)
is a subgroup, and
S(\mu)=S(\mu)^{\dagger}
.

3.
Geometric Characterization of Generalised Invertible Elements in
P(G)

In the subsequent portion of this article, it is assumed that the supports of the measures, and consequently their products, are finite. Therefore, the closures taken will remain unchanged. This simplifies the arguments in the subsequent proofs. The following theorem is locally copact version of theorem 4.1,
[
9
]
. The proof of it exactly the same as that of the above mentioned theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a locally compact, Hausdorff topological group such that every element in
G
is of finite order. Let

(3.1)

M(G)=\text{Conv}\{\delta_{g}:g\in G\},

where ’Conv’ denotes the convex hull. For an arbitrary finite set
\{g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n}\}\subset G
, then

(3.2)

\mu=\frac{\delta_{e}+\delta_{g_{1}}+\delta_{g_{2}}+...+\delta_{g_{n}}}{n+1},

is regular in
M(G)
.Moreover, the elements of the following form namely,

(3.3)

\{\delta_{g}\star\mu\star\delta_{h}:g,h\in G\quad\&\quad\mu=\frac{\delta_{e}+\delta_{g_{1}}+\delta_{g_{2}}+...+\delta_{g_{n}}}{n+1}\}.

are also regular in
M(G)
.

Proof.

Of course, one can directly prove that
\mu
is regular by brutal computation. However, our main interest being the characterization of regular elements, we give a systematic way of arriving at regular elements,
\mu
being one of them. Also , it is to be noted that the proof is exactly same word by word as that of Theorem 4.1,
[
9
]
. However, the details are repeated for the sake of completeness.To start with, we assume that

(3.4)

\mu=\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}\delta_{g_{k}},\quad\alpha_{k}>0,\quad\&\quad\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}=1.

First, we show that if
\mu^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\delta_{h_{j}}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse, it implies that
\{h_{j},j=1,2,...m.\}=\{g_{j},j=1,2,...n\}
.

If
\gamma
is a generalized inverse of
\mu
, then we will have

(3.5)

\mu\star\mu^{\dagger}\star\mu=\mu,\quad\&

(3.6)

\mu^{\dagger}\star\mu\star\mu^{\dagger}=\mu^{\dagger}.

Now by Lemma
2.4
, we find that

(3.7)

S(\mu)=S(\mu^{\dagger})=\{g_{k}:k=0,1,2,...,n\},

and
S(\mu)
is a group. Therefore, we may assume that
\mu^{\dagger}
is the generalized inverse of
\mu
implies that

(3.8)

\mu^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}\delta_{g_{k}},

where
\beta_{k}>0\quad\&\quad\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}=1
.

It is easy to see that

(3.9)

\mu\star\mu^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{j=0}^{n}\left(\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\alpha_{k}\beta_{l}\right)\delta_{g_{j}},

and

(3.10)

\mu\star\mu^{\dagger}\star\mu=[\Sigma_{j=0}^{n}\sigma_{j}\delta_{g_{j}}]\star\mu=\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}\delta_{g_{k}},

where
\sigma_{j}=\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\alpha_{k}\beta_{l}
for each
j
.

Therefore, we find that

(3.11)

\Sigma_{j=0}^{n}\left(\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\sigma_{k}\alpha_{l}\right)\delta_{g_{j}}=\Sigma_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}\delta_{g_{j}}.

Therefore, we have that

(3.12)

\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\sigma_{k}\alpha_{l}=\alpha_{j},

for every
j
. Therefore, by substituting terms, we get

(3.13)

\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\left(\Sigma_{g_{i}g_{l}=g_{k}}\alpha_{i}\beta_{l}\right)\alpha_{l}=\alpha_{j},

for
j=0,1,2,...n.
Now Lemma
2.4
above implies that
S(\mu)=S(\mu^{\dagger})
is a group. Therefore, equation
3.12
can be written as

(3.14)

\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\sigma_{k}\alpha_{S_{j}(k)}=\alpha_{j},

and similarly, equation

(3.15)

\sigma_{j}=\Sigma_{g_{k}g_{l}=g_{j}}\alpha_{k}\beta_{l}\quad for\quad each\quad j,

can be written as

(3.16)

\Sigma_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}\beta_{S_{j}(k)}=\alpha_{j},

where
S_{j}
is the permutation on
\{0,1,2,...n.\}
given by
g_{j}g_{k}\rightarrow g_{S_{j}(k)}
,
j,k\in\{0,1,2,...,n\}
. This can again be written as a matrix equation as follows:

(3.17)

\textbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{S_{(0)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(0)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(0)}(n)}\\
\alpha_{S_{(1)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(1)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(1)}(n)}\\
.\\
\quad.\\
.\quad.,.\quad.\quad.\par\\
\alpha_{S_{(n)}(0)},\alpha_{S_{(n)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{()}(n)}\end{bmatrix},

and the corresponding equation is as follows:

(3.18)

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{S_{(0)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(0)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(0)}(n)}\\
\alpha_{S_{(1)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(1)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(1)}(n)}\\
.\\
\quad.\\
.\quad.,.\quad.\quad.\par\\
\alpha_{S_{(n)}(0)},\alpha_{S_{(n)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(n)}(n)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{0}\\
\sigma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Now equation
3.13
can be explicitly written as follows:

(3.19)

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{S_{(0)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(0)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(0)}(n)}\\
\alpha_{S_{(1)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(1)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(1)}(n)}\\
.\\
\quad.\\
.\quad.,.\quad.\quad.\par\\
\alpha_{S_{(n)}(0)},\alpha_{S_{(n)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(n)}(n)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{0}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{0}\\
\sigma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Combining equations
3.16\&3.17
, we find that the determining equation is as follows:

(3.20)

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{S_{(0)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(0)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(0)}(n)}\\
\alpha_{S_{(1)}(0)}\,\alpha_{S_{(1)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(1)}(n)}\\
.\\
\quad.\\
.\quad.,.\quad.\quad.\par\\
\alpha_{S_{(n)}(0)},\alpha_{S_{(n)}(1)},\quad\ldots\alpha_{S_{(n)}(n)}\end{bmatrix}^{2}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{0}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{k}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Now the proof follows immediately from equation
3.20
above.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

We may apply the generalized doubly stochastic invertibility due to Plemons and Clines, Theorem 2
[
11
]
, to solve for (probabilistic vector) stochastic solutions of the above matrix equation 3.20; this will provide generalized invertible probability measures we are looking for.

Remark 3.3
.

There are important examples of such groups; for instance, the
Grigorchuk group
. This group has growth that is faster than polynomial but slower than exponential. Grigorchuk constructed this group in a 1980 paper and proved that it has intermediate growth in 1984
[
12
]
. In fact, the above-cited group is a locally compact topological metric group under the word metric, and every element has finite order. However, one must investigate the effect of the property mentioned above and the structure of
P(G)
.
