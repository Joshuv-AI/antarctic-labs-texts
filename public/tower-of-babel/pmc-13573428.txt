Comparative effectiveness and safety of robotic versus laparoscopic liver resection for benign liver diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Guo-Wang Li, Jing Li, Qiang Kang, Chen Yang, Wei Wang, Xin Lu, Chun-Hang Qian, Qin Geng, Yang Ke, Yue-Hua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic liver resection (RLR) is increasingly used as an alternative to laparoscopic liver resection (LLR), but its comparative effectiveness and safety for benign liver diseases remain uncertain. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare perioperative outcomes between RLR and LLR.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, WanFang Data, VIP Database (CQVIP), and CNKI were searched from inception to November 2025. Studies comparing RLR with LLR for benign liver diseases were included. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) and relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for continuous and dichotomous outcomes, respectively. Subgroup analyses were performed according to disease type.ResultsNine retrospective studies involving 790 patients (RLR, n = 268; LLR, n = 522) were included. Compared with LLR, RLR was associated with shorter setup-excluded operative time (WMD = -36.24 min, 95% CI = -52.88 to -19.60 min), lower intraoperative blood loss (WMD = -55.66 mL, 95% CI = -69.58 to -41.75 mL), a lower conversion rate to open surgery (RR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.11 to 0.69), and shorter postoperative hospital stay (WMD = -0.68 days, 95% CI = -1.35 to -0.01 days). RLR was also associated with higher albumin levels on postoperative day (POD) 1 (WMD = 1.65 g/L, 95% CI = 0.41 to 2.88 g/L) and lower ALT levels on POD 1 (WMD = − 27.24 U/L, 95% CI = − 48.42 to − 6.05 U/L). Total operative time and postoperative complication rates were comparable between the two approaches. In subgroup analyses, RLR was associated with reduced intraoperative blood loss in both hepatolithiasis and hepatic hemangioma, with a numerically larger reduction in the latter subgroup; additionally, RLR was associated with lower conversion rates and shorter postoperative hospital stay in hepatolithiasis.ConclusionsRLR was associated with advantages in several specific perioperative outcomes compared with LLR for benign liver diseases, without an increase in postoperative complications. However, all available evidence was retrospective, and the clinical significance of modest postoperative biochemical differences remains uncertain. Large prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420251062434.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05244-z.

Introduction

Among benign hepatobiliary conditions requiring liver resection, hepatolithiasis and hepatic hemangioma represent two clinically important indications [1]. Hepatolithiasis is a highly prevalent disease in Asia and its development is attributed to socioeconomic factors, dietary habits, and biliary parasitic infections. With population migration, its global incidence has also increased [2]. As the predominant benign lesion of the liver, hepatic hemangioma has an estimated prevalence of 1.5%. The disease shows a slight female bias and is most often diagnosed during the fifth to sixth decades of life [3, 4]. Surgical intervention may be indicated when these conditions are symptomatic, complicated, or difficult to distinguish from malignancy.

Minimally invasive hepatectomy has been increasingly applied to benign liver diseases, driven by its advantages in reducing surgical trauma and accelerating postoperative recovery [5]. Laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) has become an established minimally invasive approach for appropriately selected patients, with favorable perioperative outcomes compared with open surgery [6]. Furthermore, since the first report of robotic liver resection (RLR) by Giulianotti et al. in 2003 [7], RLR was developed to address several limitations of conventional laparoscopy owing to its three-dimensional high-definition visualization, platform stability, and high degree of instrument dexterity, providing a potentially safer and more efficient surgical approach for complex benign liver resections [8].

Despite the increasing use of RLR, studies comparing RLR with LLR for benign liver diseases have reported inconsistent findings regarding intraoperative outcomes and postoperative recovery. Some studies have suggested that RLR may reduce intraoperative blood loss, shorten operative time, and speed up postoperative recovery compared to LLR [9–12], whereas others have reported no significant differences in postoperative outcomes such as complications or hospital stay duration [13–15].

Furthermore, all available studies are retrospective, and no high-quality randomized controlled trials have been conducted, resulting in a low level of evidence [9–17]. The studies generally had small sample sizes (maximum n = 229 [9]) and, except for the international 33-center study by Kwak et al. [14] and the two-center study by Kim et al. [17], the remaining studies were single-center investigations. Taken together, these limitations weaken the available evidence and preclude definitive conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness and safety of RLR and LLR.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the safety and clinical effectiveness of RLR versus LLR for benign liver diseases and to provide more robust evidence for clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Literature search

From database inception until November 2025, a thorough search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, WanFang Data, VIP Database (CQVIP), and CNKI using both controlled vocabulary and free-text phrases. The study retrieval and selection process was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [18–21]. The systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251062434) before study screening and data extraction. The terms utilized in the search strategy were tailored to meet the specific requirements of each database. Comprehensive search strategies are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following eligibility criteria were used to determine which studies were included: (1) studies comparing the effectiveness and safety of RLR and LLR for benign liver diseases, including hepatic hemangiomas, hepatolithiasis and focal nodular hyperplasia. (2) studies reporting at least one eligible perioperative outcome, including total operative time, setup-excluded operative time, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative transfusion, use or duration of hepatic inflow occlusion, conversion to open surgery, postoperative hospital stay, residual-stone rate, postoperative complications, or postoperative TBIL, ALB, ALT, and AST levels on postoperative days (PODs) 1, 3, 5, or 7. (3) randomized controlled trials or cohort studies. (4) In instances of duplicated patient cohorts across studies, only the most up-to-date study was included.

Any study satisfying at least one of the predefined exclusion parameters was omitted from the pooled analysis: (1) case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies or basic experimental research studies; (2) studies with unavailable full text or that had been retracted; and (3) studies in which outcomes for benign liver disease could not be extracted separately from those for malignant disease. Studies without disease-specific outcome data were included only in the overall analysis and excluded from disease-specific subgroup analyses.

Data extraction

Two investigators (Guo-Wang Li and Qiang Kang) independently reviewed the full texts of all included studies and extracted the relevant data, such as the first author, country, study design, publication year, sample size, and outcome measures. The extracted outcomes included intraoperative variables such as blood loss, conversion to open surgery rate, transfusion rate, total operative time, setup-excluded operative time, use and duration of hepatic inflow occlusion, as well as the extent of liver resection and lesion locations; postoperative variables including hospital stay duration, residual-stone rate, laboratory parameters, and complications. When numerical discrepancies existed between the main text and tables of an original study, values reported in the primary results table were preferentially extracted. To ensure data accuracy and completeness, we contacted the corresponding authors for additional data when data were incomplete or unclear.

Total operative time was defined as the entire operative duration, including robotic setup or docking time when applicable. Studies reporting only operative time after exclusion of robotic setup or docking time were not included in the primary analysis of total operative time and were analyzed separately.

When both unmatched and propensity score-matched data were available, data from the largest non-overlapping matched cohort were preferentially extracted.

Quality assessment

Three reviewers independently assessed the eligible studies' methodological quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), focusing on selection of cohorts, comparability between groups, and outcome evaluation [22–25]. NOS scores ranged from 0 to 9 points, with studies scoring 7–9 points considered relatively high quality, 4–6 points considered moderate quality, and ≤ 3 points considered low quality. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Publication bias assessment

To explore publication bias, funnel plot analysis was employed, which was further quantified by the use of Egger’s and Begg’s tests [26, 27]. A P value < 0.05 was interpreted as indicating potential publication bias.

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were conducted employing Stata version 18.0. For studies reporting medians with interquartile ranges or ranges, means and standard deviations were estimated using established methods proposed by Wan et al. [28] and Luo et al. [29]. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for continuous variables, whereas relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs were used for dichotomous variables [30, 31]. Studies with zero events in both groups were retained descriptively but did not contribute to the pooled RR estimation. Inter-study heterogeneity was quantified using the χ2 test and I2 statistic, with I2 values > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. A random-effects analytical framework was implemented in instances of considerable heterogeneity, while a fixed-effects approach was employed when variance proved minimal. To investigate heterogeneity and assess the robustness of treatment effects, subgroup analyses were carried out according to disease type. The significance level was predetermined at P < 0.05.

Results

Study selection

The study retrieval and screening workflow is graphically summarized in Fig. 1. Initially, 2,448 records were identified. After removal of 1,241 duplicates, 1,207 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following title and abstract screening, 1,164 studies were excluded as they were deemed irrelevant. Eligibility assessment was performed on the 43 remaining studies according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Thirty-four full-text articles were excluded because the benign or malignant nature of the liver disease was not specified. The meta-analysis included nine studies [9–17]: four on hepatolithiasis [11, 14, 15, 17], four on hepatic hemangioma [9, 10, 12, 13], and one on unspecified benign diseases [16].Fig. 1Flow diagram of the study selection process according to the PRISMA guidelines

Characteristics and quality assessment of the included studies

A comprehensive overview of clinical variables across the 9 eligible studies is provided in Table 1. Outcome data are presented in Tables 2 and 3. A total of 790 patients were included across these studies, with 268 undergoing RLR and 522 undergoing LLR. The studies were published between 2016 and 2025. All nine studies had a retrospective design. Seven studies [9–13, 15, 16] were single-center studies conducted in China. The remaining two were multicenter studies [14, 17]: one was an international study involving 33 centers [14], and the other was a two-center study conducted in South Korea [17]. The RLR group had 100 males and 168 females, while the LLR group included 174 males and 348 females. The age distribution showed mean or median values of 45.8 to 62.3 years in the RLR group and 45.4 to 63.0 years in the LLR group. All nine studies reported the extent of liver resection; the reported procedures included major and minor hepatectomy and, in some studies, enucleation [9–17]. Five studies described the lesion locations, including the right lobe, left lobe, bilobar involvement, and caudate lobe [9, 10, 12, 13, 15]. Baseline characteristics, including sex, age, resection extent, and lesion location, were generally comparable between the RLR and LLR groups across the included studies [9–17], except that Lin et al. [16] reported a significantly larger tumor diameter in the RLR group than in the LLR group (4.76 ± 0.76 vs. 3.93 ± 0.57 cm, P = 0.041). Quality appraisal using the NOS indicated that all nine studies were of relatively high quality: two studies scored 7 points [16, 17], four scored 8 points [9–11, 15], and three scored 9 points [12–14]. Total operative time

Eight studies involving 687 patients reported total operative time [9–14, 16, 17]. One additional study reported setup-excluded operative time only and was therefore not included in the primary analysis of total operative time [15]. Marked inter-study heterogeneity (P for heterogeneity < 0.001, I2 = 87.2%) necessitated the adoption of a random-effects model. Total operative time was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (WMD = −7.09 min, 95% CI = −39.51 to 25.33 min, P = 0.668, Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Forest plots of total operative time comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

Forest plots of total operative time comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

An analysis of 163 hepatolithiasis patients across three studies found no significant heterogeneity in total operative time (P for heterogeneity = 0.214, I2 = 35.2%), allowing for adoption of a fixed-effects model [11, 14, 17]. No significant difference in total operative time was observed between groups (WMD = −12.14 min, 95% CI = −34.66 to 10.37 min, P = 0.290, Fig. 2B).

An analysis of 462 hepatic hemangioma patients across four studies revealed significant heterogeneity in total operative time (P for heterogeneity < 0.001, I2 = 88.9%), and accordingly a random-effects model was adopted [9, 10, 12, 13]. No statistically meaningful disparity in total operative time was detected between groups (WMD = −23.51 min, 95% CI = −68.38 to 21.36 min, P = 0.305, Fig. 2C).

Setup-excluded operative time

Three studies involving 178 patients reported setup-excluded operative time [10, 11, 15]. A fixed-effects framework was adopted, as statistical heterogeneity proved minimal (P for heterogeneity = 0.267, I2 = 24.3%). The RLR group demonstrated a notably reduced setup-excluded operative time relative to the LLR group (WMD = −36.24 min, 95% CI = −52.88 to −19.60 min, P < 0.001, Fig. 3).Fig. 3Forest plots of setup-excluded operative time comparing RLR and LLR

Forest plots of setup-excluded operative time comparing RLR and LLR

Two studies involving 146 patients with hepatolithiasis reported the setup-excluded operative time [11, 15]. Both Tu et al. [15] (P = 0.001) and Wang et al. [11] (P < 0.05) reported significantly shorter setup-excluded operative time in the RLR group compared with the LLR group.

One study involving 32 patients with hepatic hemangioma reported a significantly shorter setup-excluded operative time in the RLR group than in the LLR group [10].

Intraoperative blood loss

Nine studies involving a total of 790 patients reported intraoperative blood loss [9–17]. A fixed-effects framework was adopted, as statistical heterogeneity proved minimal (P for heterogeneity = 0.707, I2 = 0%). Substantially reduced intraoperative blood loss was observed in the RLR group relative to the LLR group (WMD = −55.66 mL, 95% CI = −69.58 to −41.75 mL, P < 0.001, Fig. 4A).Fig. 4Forest plots of intraoperative blood loss comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

Forest plots of intraoperative blood loss comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

A stratification analysis of four studies involving 266 hepatolithiasis patients evaluated intraoperative blood loss [11, 14, 15, 17]. No significant heterogeneity was observed among these studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.215, I2 = 34.9%); therefore, a fixed-effects model was used. Patients undergoing RLR exhibited markedly reduced intraoperative blood loss relative to those undergoing LLR (WMD = −50.93 mL, 95% CI = −73.80 to −28.07 mL, P < 0.001, Fig. 4B).

An analysis of four additional studies involving 462 patients with hepatic hemangioma showed no evident heterogeneity (P for heterogeneity = 0.566, I2 = 0%), resulting in employment of a fixed-effects model [9, 10, 12, 13]. A significantly lower intraoperative blood loss was observed in the RLR group relative to the LLR group (WMD = −60.31 mL, 95% CI = −81.19 to −39.43 mL, P < 0.001, Fig. 4C).

The rate of intraoperative transfusion

Six studies involving 490 patients reported intraoperative transfusion rates [10–15]. The analysis utilized a fixed-effects model because there was no appreciable heterogeneity between studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.809, I2 = 0%). The intraoperative transfusion rate was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups, with no statistically significant difference (RR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.34 to 1.06, P = 0.076, see Supplementary Figure S1A).

A stratification analysis of three studies involving 257 hepatolithiasis patients evaluated the rate of intraoperative transfusion [11, 14, 15]. No significant heterogeneity was observed among these studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.470, I2 = 0%); therefore, a fixed-effects model was used. The RLR and LLR groups showed no statistically significant difference in intraoperative transfusion rates (RR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.26 to 1.44, P = 0.259, see Supplementary Figure S1B).

As no significant heterogeneity was observed among the three studies involving 233 patients with hepatic hemangioma (P for heterogeneity = 0.695, I2 = 0%) [10, 12, 13], a fixed-effects model was employed. The RLR and LLR groups showed no statistically significant difference in intraoperative transfusion rates (RR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.27 to 1.26, P = 0.172, see Supplementary Figure S1C).

Use of hepatic inflow occlusion

Three studies reported the use of hepatic inflow occlusion; available-case data were available for 263 patients [11, 12, 14]. Given the moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 52.4%), a random-effects model was applied, despite the heterogeneity test not reaching conventional statistical significance (P = 0.122). The use of hepatic inflow occlusion was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (RR = 1.00, 95% CI = 0.72 to 1.41, P = 0.981, Supplementary Figure S2).

Two studies of 134 patients with hepatolithiasis reported the use of hepatic inflow occlusion [11, 14]. Wang et al. [11] (P = 0.348) and Kwak et al. [14] (P = 0.690) both found no significant intergroup difference in the requirement for hepatic inflow occlusion between the RLR and LLR groups.

One study of patients with hepatic hemangioma found no significant difference in the use of hepatic inflow occlusion between the two groups (P = 0.104) [12].

Duration of hepatic inflow occlusion

Four studies involving 415 patients documented the duration of hepatic inflow occlusion [12–15]. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.001, I2 = 82.3%); therefore, a random-effects model was used. The hepatic inflow occlusion time was comparable between the two groups, lacking detectable statistical significance (WMD = −6.53 min, 95% CI = −16.35 to 3.29 min, P = 0.193, Supplementary Figure S3).

Two studies involving 214 hepatolithiasis patients reported the duration of hepatic inflow occlusion [14, 15]. One study indicated a shorter hepatic inflow occlusion time in patients undergoing RLR than in those undergoing LLR (t = −2.28, P = 0.025) [15], whereas another study documented statistically indistinguishable outcomes (P = 0.426) [14].

Two studies involving 201 hepatic hemangioma patients reported duration of hepatic inflow occlusion [12, 13]. One study found that duration of hepatic inflow occlusion was shorter in the RLR group than in the LLR group (Z = −3.472, P < 0.001) [13], whereas another study showed comparable results between the groups (P = 0.339) [12].

The conversion rate to open surgery

Eight studies involving 758 patients documented the conversion rate to open surgery [9, 11–17]. As inter-study heterogeneity was not evident (P for heterogeneity = 0.941, I2 = 0%), the fixed-effects model was chosen for the analysis. The RLR group had a significantly lower conversion rate to open surgery than the LLR group (RR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.11 to 0.69, P = 0.006, Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Forest plots of the conversion rate to open surgery comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

Forest plots of the conversion rate to open surgery comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis; (C) hepatic hemangioma

Four studies of 266 patients with hepatolithiasis reported the conversion rate to open surgery [11, 14, 15, 17]. Due to no significant heterogeneity (P for heterogeneity = 0.794, I2 = 0%), pooled estimates were derived under a fixed-effects model. The RLR group had a significantly lower conversion rate to open surgery than the LLR group (RR = 0.17, 95% CI = 0.04 to 0.73, P = 0.017, Fig. 5B).

An analysis of three studies involving 430 patients with hepatic hemangioma exhibited no significant heterogeneity regarding the conversion rate to open surgery (P for heterogeneity = 0.997, I2 = 0%), therefore, a fixed-effects model was used [9, 12, 13]. The conversion rate to open surgery was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (RR = 0.44, 95% CI = 0.13 to 1.47, P = 0.180, Fig. 5C).

Postoperative hospital stay

Seven studies of 552 patients reported postoperative length of stay [10–16]. The remaining two studies only reported total length of hospital stay without providing separate data for the postoperative period and were therefore excluded from this specific pooled analysis [9, 17]. As inter-study heterogeneity was evident (P for heterogeneity = 0.012, I2 = 63.1%), a random-effects model was used for the analysis. The RLR group exhibited a significantly shorter postoperative hospital stay than the LLR group (WMD = −0.68 days, 95% CI = −1.35 to −0.01 days, P = 0.046, Fig. 6A).Fig. 6Forest plots of postoperative hospital stay comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis, (C) hepatic hemangioma

Forest plots of postoperative hospital stay comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatolithiasis, (C) hepatic hemangioma

Three studies of 257 patients with hepatolithiasis reported postoperative length of stay [11, 14, 15]. As heterogeneity was high (P for heterogeneity = 0.042, I2 = 68.4%), a random-effects model was used. The RLR group exhibited a significantly shorter postoperative hospital stay than the LLR group (WMD = −1.17 days, 95% CI = −2.34 to −0.00 days, P = 0.049, Fig. 6B).

An analysis of three studies involving 233 patients with hepatic hemangioma exhibited considerable heterogeneity regarding postoperative hospitalization length (P for heterogeneity = 0.071, I2 = 62.2%), warranting a random-effects model [10, 12, 13]. Postoperative hospital stay duration was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (WMD = −0.33 days, 95% CI = −1.34 to 0.67 days, P = 0.515, Fig. 6C).

The residual-stone rate

In the hepatolithiasis subgroup, three studies involving a total of 155 patients reported the residual-stone rate [11, 15, 17]. As inter-study heterogeneity was found to be negligible (P for heterogeneity = 0.881, I2 = 0%), the application of a fixed-effects model was undertaken. Residual-stone rates were comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (RR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.18 to 2.86, P = 0.647, Supplementary Figure S4).

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complication rates were reported in seven studies, encompassing a total of 644 patients [9, 10, 12–14, 16, 17]. One study only reported complications of Clavien-Dindo grade Ⅱ or higher, without providing separate data for grade Ⅰ events [15]. Owing to the lack of significant inter-study heterogeneity (P for heterogeneity = 0.577, I2 = 0%), a fixed-effects model was applied for the analysis. The rate of postoperative complications was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups (RR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.67 to 1.46, P = 0.951, Fig. 7A).Fig. 7Forest plots of postoperative complications comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatic hemangioma

Forest plots of postoperative complications comparing RLR and LLR: (A) benign liver diseases; (B) hepatic hemangioma

Two studies involving 120 hepatolithiasis patients reported the rate of postoperative complications [14, 17]. One study indicated no statistically significant difference in the incidence of postoperative complications between the RLR and LLR groups (P = 0.232) [14], and another study documented only one postoperative complication in the LLR group [17].

Similarly, four studies involving 462 patients with hepatic hemangioma reported postoperative complication rates [9, 10, 12, 13]. Since inter-study heterogeneity was not substantial (P for heterogeneity = 0.554, I2 = 0%), the analytical process relied upon a fixed effects model. Similar rates of postoperative complications were demonstrated for the two groups (RR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.47 to 1.34, P = 0.385, Fig. 7B).

TBIL levels on PODs 1, 3, 5, and 7

TBIL levels on POD 1 were reported across five studies that included a total of 379 patients [10–13, 15]. One study was excluded because the timing of TBIL levels measurement was not specified [9]. In light of marked heterogeneity across studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.023, I2 = 64.6%), pooled estimates were derived under a random-effects model. A lack of marked disparity in POD 1 TBIL values was found when comparing the two groups (WMD = −0.20 μmol/L, 95% CI = −3.90 to 3.50 μmol/L, P = 0.916, Supplementary Figure S5A). Two studies reported TBIL levels on POD 3, involving 175 patients [13, 15]. On POD 3, TBIL levels exhibited no considerable variation between the two groups (P = 0.712 [15]; P = 0.097 [13]). One study with 103 patients showed similar results between groups in TBIL on POD 5 (P = 0.503) [15]. Another study involving 129 patients reported comparable between groups in TBIL on POD 7 (P = 0.498) [12].

A stratified analysis of two studies involving 146 patients with hepatolithiasis demonstrated no significant intergroup difference in TBIL levels on POD 1 [11] and PODs 1, 3, and 5 (POD 1: P = 0.552; POD 3: P = 0.712; POD 5: P = 0.503) [15], respectively.

Similarly, three studies with 233 patients with hepatic hemangioma reported TBIL on POD 1 [10, 12, 13]. Significant heterogeneity among the included studies (P for heterogeneity = 0.006, I2 = 80.5%) necessitated the application of a random-effects model. No meaningful variation in TBIL levels on POD 1 was demonstrated between the two groups (WMD = 0.88 μmol/L, 95% CI = − 5.64 to 7.41 μmol/L, P = 0.791, Supplementary Figure S5B). Similarly, two studies showed similar results in TBIL levels between the two groups of patients with hepatic hemangioma on POD 3 (P = 0.097) [13], and POD 7 (P = 0.498) [12], respectively.

ALB levels on PODs 1, 3, 5, and 7

Four studies involving 250 patients reported ALB levels on POD 1 [10, 11, 13, 15]. One study was excluded because the timing of ALB levels measurement was not specified [9]. A fixed-effects model was deemed appropriate given the evidence of minimal study heterogeneity (P for heterogeneity = 0.880, I2 = 0%). The RLR group demonstrated significantly higher ALB levels on POD 1 than the LLR group (WMD = 1.65 g/L, 95% CI = 0.41 to 2.88 g/L, P = 0.009, Fig. 8). Two studies with 175 patients reported ALB levels on POD 3 [13, 15]. One study found significantly higher ALB levels on POD 3 in the RLR group than the LLR group (t = −4.497, P < 0.001) [13], whereas another study reported no significant difference (t = 0.20, P = 0.843) [15]. One study involving 103 patients reported comparable ALB levels between the two groups on POD 5 (t = −1.40, P = 0.163) [15]. No included study reported ALB levels on POD 7.Fig. 8Forest plots of ALB levels on POD 1 comparing RLR and LLR

Forest plots of ALB levels on POD 1 comparing RLR and LLR

Two studies with 146 patients with hepatolithiasis reported ALB levels [11, 15]. Similar ALB levels were demonstrated for the two groups on POD 1 [11], or on PODs 1, 3, and 5 (POD 1 P = 0.054; POD 3 P = 0.843; POD 5 P = 0.163) [15].

Two studies with 104 patients with hepatic hemangioma reported ALB levels [10, 13]. Hu et al. reported comparable numerical ALB levels on POD 1 between the RLR and LLR groups, but did not provide a pairwise P value. [10].

A stratified analysis of two studies involving 146 patients with hepatolithiasis showed comparable ALT levels across the two surgical approaches on POD 1 [11], as well as on PODs 1, 3, and 5 (POD 1 P = 0.452; POD 3 P = 0.856; POD 5 P = 0.462) [15].

Hospitalization costs

Two studies with 301 patients reported hospitalization costs [9, 13]. Both Jiang et al. [13] and Zhou et al. [9] reported significantly higher hospitalization costs in the RLR group than in the LLR group (Jiang et al.: 74,746 ± 13,939 vs. 49,644 ± 16,149 CNY, P < 0.001; Zhou et al.: 80,674.0 [IQR 75,393.0–86,222.0] vs. 70,648.9 [IQR 61,129.9–80,032.9] CNY, P < 0.001).

Publication bias

Egger’s and Begg’s tests did not detect statistically significant evidence of publication bias for postoperative complications (Egger’s P = 0.377, Begg’s P = 0.548, Supplementary Figure S6). However, given the small number of studies, these tests were underpowered and the results should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

LLR is an established minimally invasive approach for appropriately selected patients with benign liver diseases. RLR has increasingly been applied to technically complex liver resections [32], but evidence specific to benign diseases remains limited. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of RLR versus LLR for benign liver diseases by synthesizing the available evidence, thereby informing clinical decision-making regarding the adoption of RLR.

The findings indicated that, compared with LLR, RLR for benign liver diseases was associated with favorable outcomes in several specific perioperative measures, including shorter setup-excluded operative time, lower intraoperative blood loss, lower conversion rates, shorter postoperative hospital stay, higher POD 1 ALB levels, and lower POD 1 ALT levels. Recently, Li et al. reported that RLR for hepatocellular carcinoma was associated with a lower conversion-to-open-surgery rate than LLR [33]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis undertaken by Wang et al., which included patients with both benign hepatic lesions and malignant liver tumors, demonstrated that RLR was associated with lower blood loss and reducing open conversion rates [34]. These studies support our findings. Therefore, our findings suggest that RLR may offer specific perioperative advantages—namely shorter setup-excluded operative time, reduced blood loss, lower conversion rates and shorter postoperative hospital stay—over LLR in selected patients with benign liver disease, without compromising safety, rather than demonstrating overall superiority across all outcomes.

These benefits may be related to the technical features of the robot-assisted surgical platform. The robotic platform offers a high-resolution, magnified 3D visualization field. It offers 10-fold magnification, which is considerably higher than the 2–4-fold magnification offered by conventional laparoscopy [35]. Therefore, surgeons can identify intrahepatic vascular structures and anatomical variations with greater precision [36]. In addition, it has a highly stable operating system that effectively eliminates minor hand tremors, enabling surgeons to perform more precisely and smoothly [37]. Moreover, wristed instruments with multiple degrees of freedom overcome the restricted range of motion inherent to conventional laparoscopic instruments, allowing precise operation even in narrow or deep liver segments. These technical features may facilitate more precise hemostasis and could partly account for the lower intraoperative blood loss observed with RLR. The precise anatomical visualization and stable operating conditions minimize the risk of injuring critical structures, thereby reducing the likelihood of converting to open surgery. These technical advantages may contribute to lower intraoperative blood loss and a more favorable early postoperative biochemical profile. However, POD 1 ALB levels may also be influenced by perioperative fluid management and albumin supplementation. Moreover, the observed differences in ALB (WMD = 1.65 g/L) and ALT (WMD = − 27.24 U/L) were modest and should therefore not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinically meaningful hepatic protection. Routine liver enzymes may incompletely reflect the multidimensional biological response to surgery. In a prospective multicenter study of patients with cancer, Kudriavtsev et al. observed marked perioperative changes in circulating nuclear DNA and neutrophil extracellular trap-related markers, with the magnitude and pattern of these changes varying according to cancer type and surgical procedure [38]. These findings underscore the need for future studies of RLR to incorporate multidimensional measures of surgical stress and patient-centered recovery, rather than relying solely on modest differences in postoperative transaminase levels.

Subgroup analyses suggested that the pattern of perioperative benefits differed by disease type. RLR was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss in both hepatolithiasis and hepatic hemangioma, with a numerically larger reduction in the hemangioma subgroup. In hepatolithiasis, RLR was additionally associated with a lower conversion-to-open rate and shorter postoperative hospital stay, whereas in hepatic hemangioma, RLR was associated with lower ALT levels on POD 1. This pattern likely reflects the differing surgical complexities and bleeding mechanisms associated with each disease. Hepatolithiasis is often associated with cholangitis and structural changes in the hepatic hilum and liver [2], creating a more intricate anatomical setting in which the robotic system's advantages in visual exposure, instrument maneuverability, and precise dissection can be maximally leveraged to shorten setup-excluded operative time [39–41]. Specifically, the improved depth perception and wristed instrument articulation of robotic systems enable more efficient liver dissection and targeted stone removal, which may explain the shorter setup-excluded operative time observed in hepatolithiasis [11, 15]. In contrast, hepatic hemangiomas—particularly giant hemangiomas—are prone to substantial intraoperative bleeding due to their vascular nature and mass effect; in this setting, the enhanced hemostatic precision and platform stability of the robotic system may confer a particularly pronounced benefit in reducing blood loss. Taken together, these findings suggest that the specific perioperative benefit conferred by RLR depends on the dominant technical challenge posed by each disease—anatomical complexity in hepatolithiasis versus bleeding risk in hepatic hemangioma—rather than an overall, uniform superiority of RLR in one disease over the other.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of both learning curve bias and era bias, which we address separately below. Regarding learning curve bias: as RLR is a newer surgical technique, surgeons performing RLR were theoretically more likely to be at an earlier stage of their learning curve compared with their accumulated experience in LLR [42]. This disparity may be particularly evident when highly experienced laparoscopic surgeons are compared, within the same study period, against surgeons who are still relatively early in their robotic learning curve. Such a disparity should have disadvantaged RLR outcomes relative to LLR. However, our meta-analysis showed the opposite—better perioperative outcomes for RLR. These findings are compatible with potential technical advantages of the robotic platform; however, such platform effects cannot be disentangled from residual confounding related to surgeon selection, institutional experience, and unmeasured differences in case complexity.

Regarding era bias, this form of bias may arise when RLR outcomes, typically reflecting a center’s more recent or maturing practice, are compared with LLR data derived from an earlier historical period. As detailed in Table 1, the RLR and LLR cohorts within each included study were generally drawn from a common overall enrollment period, reducing the likelihood of a direct comparison between contemporary RLR and historical LLR cohorts. However, modality-specific accrual dates were not consistently reported. Therefore, residual era bias cannot be excluded, particularly because robotic surgery may have been introduced later during the study period at some centers.

We wish to emphasize that, in the absence of RCT-level evidence in this field, the present meta-analysis is intended to systematically synthesize existing observational data and identify consistent trends, rather than to serve as a substitute for prospective randomized comparisons. These findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and should inform the design of future RCTs rather than change clinical practice directly. Moreover, pooled group-level effects should not be assumed to predict the benefit for every individual patient, because such extrapolation is vulnerable to ecological fallacy [43]. Accordingly, the present estimates should support shared decision-making and the design of stratified prospective studies rather than be interpreted as universal treatment rules.

Limitations

While this study offers novel data on the clinical safety and effectiveness of RLR and LLR in benign liver diseases, the findings should be approached with prudent interpretation. Selection and information bias may have arisen from the retrospective nature of all included studies. The sample sizes were relatively small, limiting statistical power, and most studies were conducted at single centers, so the results may be influenced by variations in surgical experience and postoperative management. Additionally, disease type (hepatolithiasis vs. hepatic hemangioma) served as a surrogate for surgical complexity in our subgroup analysis; however, considerable heterogeneity exists within each disease category itself (e.g., hemangioma size, stone distribution and laterality, presence of biliary stricture), which could not be further stratified due to insufficient lesion-level granularity in the primary studies. Furthermore, surgical difficulty in liver resection is influenced not only by disease type but also by the extent of resection (major vs. minor hepatectomy) and background liver parenchymal condition (e.g., fatty liver, cirrhosis); although baseline resection extent was comparable between the RLR and LLR groups across the included studies, most primary studies did not report perioperative outcomes stratified by resection extent or liver parenchymal status, precluding further adjustment for these factors in our analysis. Future studies with standardized reporting of lesion characteristics, resection extent, and background liver condition are needed to enable more refined subgroup comparisons. Furthermore, most patients were treated at Asian centers; although one international multicenter study included Western centers, the evidence remained predominantly Asian, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings. Furthermore, only two studies reported quantitative hospitalization-cost data. Because of the limited number of studies, differences in reporting formats, and variation in study periods, these data were summarized descriptively rather than pooled. This limits our ability to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of RLR, a critical factor in its clinical adoption for benign liver diseases [9, 13], and future cost-effectiveness analyses are urgently needed before firm recommendations regarding the economic viability of RLR can be made. Future research should involve large, multicenter, prospective studies to validate these findings.

Future comparative studies could incorporate quantitative imaging-based measures of lesion size, vascular proximity, stone distribution, biliary stricture, and background liver condition to improve adjustment for surgical complexity and case mix [44, 45].

Conclusions

RLR was associated with favorable outcomes in several specific perioperative measures compared with LLR for benign liver diseases, including shorter setup-excluded operative time, lower intraoperative blood loss, a lower conversion rate to open surgery, and shorter postoperative hospital stay, without an increase in postoperative complication rates. RLR was also associated with higher ALB levels and lower ALT levels on POD 1; however, the clinical relevance of these modest biochemical differences remains uncertain. The observed benefits were outcome-specific rather than indicative of overall superiority of RLR over LLR, and different outcome patterns were observed across disease subgroups. Given that all included studies were retrospective and residual confounding, learning-curve effects, and era bias cannot be fully excluded, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Large, prospective, multicenter studies with standardized assessment of surgical complexity are needed to confirm these findings and better define which patients may benefit most from RLR.
