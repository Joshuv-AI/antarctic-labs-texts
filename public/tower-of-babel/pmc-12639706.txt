Pathways to improving the awareness, diagnosis and management of lipodystrophy in Brazil: an expert panel discussion
Authors: Josivan Gomes Lima, Cynthia Melissa Valerio, Amélio F Godoy-Matos, Gerson Carvalho, Joana R Dantas, João Eduardo Nunes Salles, Fernando Gerchman, Renan Montenegro Jr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLipodystrophy syndromes are a group of rare, clinically complex disorders characterized by a partial or generalized lack of adipose tissue. Affected individuals develop severe metabolic comorbidities and frequently display absolute or relative reductions in leptin levels. Congenital generalized lipodystrophy (CGL) was first described in two cases from Brazil in 1954, with subsequent studies highlighting the burden of lipodystrophy syndromes in patients from Brazil and elsewhere. However, despite this earlier research, awareness of lipodystrophy syndromes among the general Brazilian medical community remains limited. In November 2022, a panel of Brazilian specialists with experience in lipodystrophy syndromes, met at a full-day face-to-face meeting to discuss the importance of these rare metabolic diseases in Brazil. This manuscript is an output from this expert panel discussion.MethodsA structured agenda was prepared and involved a series of presentations followed by expert discussion sessions. Discussion points and insights collected during the meeting were developed into a report that was reviewed and approved by all authors. This report served as a basis for the current manuscript. Where applicable, the content presented in this manuscript is supported by published data in lipodystrophy syndromes from Brazil and other international studies.ResultsThe key themes discussed at the meeting and presented here include: (1) the epidemiology, prevalence and clinical presentation of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil, (2) the diagnostic journey of Brazilian patients with lipodystrophy syndromes including diagnostic challenges, and (3) the clinical approach to lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil. Potential knowledge gaps in lipodystrophy syndromes among the general Brazilian medical community are reviewed. Country-level initiatives that can increase disease awareness and help non-specialist healthcare professionals in recognizing patients with lipodystrophy syndromes are also explored.ConclusionThis manuscript provides an overview of the current knowledge base of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil and suggests areas where this may be improved. The information presented here could help the development of a national guideline for lipodystrophy syndromes that supports clinical care pathways for Brazilian patients.Keywords: Diagnosis, Disease management, Fat loss, Generalized lipodystrophy, Lipodystrophy syndromes, Partial lipodystrophy

Background

Lipodystrophy syndromes are a group of rare, clinically heterogeneous disorders characterized by a paucity of adipose tissue [1, 2]. Adipose tissue loss may be genetic or acquired and can affect either the whole body (i.e., generalized lipodystrophy, GL) or specific areas (i.e., partial lipodystrophy, PL) [1]. Accordingly, four main lipodystrophy types are described in the literature — congenital generalized lipodystrophy (CGL), acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL), familial partial lipodystrophy (FPLD), and acquired partial lipodystrophy (APL) [1–9]. In lipodystrophy syndromes, the absence of adipose tissue leads to ectopic fat accumulation and predisposes patients to the development of severe metabolic comorbidities (e.g., insulin resistance, diabetes mellitus, hypertriglyceridemia) [1, 10–12]. Ensuing end-organ complications, including liver and cardiovascular disease, renal failure, and acute pancreatitis, are among the major causes of mortality associated with lipodystrophy syndromes [1, 13–15]. Reduced adiposity can also result in leptin deficiency causing impaired hunger-satiety signaling and hyperphagia, which, in turn, may exacerbate comorbidities [10, 16–18].

A multi-society practice guideline for diagnosing and managing lipodystrophy syndromes was published in 2016 [1]. Subsequent publications from leading clinical teams further describe clinical approaches to suspected lipodystrophy cases [19, 20]. Collectively, these publications have increased awareness of lipodystrophy syndromes among the general medical community; however, knowledge gaps still exist, leading to a widespread underdiagnosis of these rare diseases [10, 19, 20].

On 19 November 2022, the authors of this manuscript, who represent a panel of Brazilian specialists from different regions and with experience in lipodystrophy syndromes, attended a full-day, face-to-face meeting in São Paulo to discuss the importance of these rare diseases. Key themes and insights discussed at this meeting are presented in this manuscript.

Materials and methods

This manuscript was developed from discussions at the panel meeting attended by all authors (organized by Amryt Pharmaceuticals, a wholly owned subsidiary of Chiesi Farmaceutici S.p.A.). A total of six representatives from the regional Amryt Pharmaceuticals Medical Affairs team also attended the meeting for educational purposes only. These participants did not contribute to the final meeting report nor to the content of this manuscript.

Meeting agenda and structure

An agenda was set and agreed by all authors prior to the meeting. The meeting agenda consisted of eight 20- to 30-minute presentations by six of the authors (JGL, CMV, AG-M, GC, JRD, RMJ) and a clinical expert from Turkey (Baris Akinci, Izmir Biomedicine and Genome Center & DEPARK, Dokuz Eylul University Health Campus, Izmir, Turkey) who presented on the real-world management of lipodystrophy syndromes outside Brazil. Questions were permitted during each presentation. Discussion sessions (lasting between 45 and 60 min, chaired by JGL and RMJ) followed the presentations with contributions from each author. A panel of questions submitted by the authors prior to the meeting was used by the session chairs to aid discussions where necessary.

Key themes discussed at the expert panel meeting

Overall, four broad themes related to lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil were presented and discussed during the meeting: (1) the epidemiology, prevalence and clinical presentation of lipodystrophy syndromes in the Brazilian population; (2) the diagnostic journey of Brazilian patients with lipodystrophy syndromes including diagnostic challenges and the establishment of a minimum panel of diagnostic criteria for inclusion in a national practice guidelines for lipodystrophy syndromes; (3) the current management of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil with learnings from real-world data and clinical practices from other countries; and (4) how local medical education initiatives may increase disease awareness among the broader medical community in Brazil. Knowledge gaps in these four areas and possible strategies to improve these in Brazil were reviewed and debated.

Development and finalization of a meeting report

Data and information from the presentations and discussion sessions were collected by a local medical writer (who attended the meeting in a passive capacity only) in the form of notes and video files. Following the meeting, these notes were used to develop a meeting report that summarized the key points discussed for each of the four main themes. A first draft of the meeting report was sent to all authors for review. Based on the feedback received, revisions were made, and a consensus report was developed and approved by all authors. The final meeting report served the basis for this manuscript. All discussion points presented in this manuscript are supported by published data from Brazil (where available) and other international studies.

Results

Expert review of the epidemiology, prevalence and clinical presentation of lipodystrophy syndromes in the Brazilian population

The prevalence of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil: a review of the clinical data

The global prevalence of lipodystrophy syndromes is estimated at 1.3–4.7 cases per million, and at 0.23 and 2.84 cases per million for GL and PL, respectively [21]. Using a combination of genetic modeling together with data from two international registries, the estimated prevalence of FPLD was recently refined to 19.0–30.0 cases per million; however, this study acknowledged that many FPLD cases remain undiagnosed [22].

In Brazil, data are available from a small number of regional epidemiological studies. A study involving 103 patients with CGL types 1 and 2 from the Northeastern state of Rio Grande do Norte, yielded an estimated prevalence of 3.23 per 100,000 [23]. Further interrogation estimated the prevalence of CGL in Central Potiguar mesoregion, the Seridó territory, and the Carnaúba dos Dantas and Timbaúba dos Batistas municipalities was 20.56, 20.66, 498.05, and 217.85 per 100,000 people, respectively [23]. The elevated prevalence of CGL in some areas of Brazil (relative to the global estimate) has been attributed to an increased frequency of CGL-associated pathogenic variants due to a possible genetic founder effect and the presence of parental consanguinity (estimated at 72.4% in a cohort of 44 patients from Rio Grande do Norte) [23–26]. Data from the CGL population in the neighboring Northeastern state of Ceará show that CGL1 predominates, affecting ~ 74% of individuals based on the most recent estimate [27–29].

More recently, a multi-center investigation evaluated the etiology of 424 Brazilian patients with a clinical diagnosis of FPLD, 106 of whom had a confirmed genetic diagnosis. Analysis of these 106 individuals revealed a predominance of pathogenic variants in the LMNA gene (86% of patients), followed by PPARG (10% of patients), PLIN1 (3% of patients), and MFN2 (1% of patients) [30]. Two novel PPARG variants have also been recently identified in three Brazilian patients with FPLD3 [31].

The clinical presentation of lipodystrophy syndromes in the Brazilian patient population: a review of the clinical data

The clinical presentation of lipodystrophy syndromes in the Brazilian population has been examined using data from leptin replacement-naïve subjects. An international chart review study showed that, on average, the first symptoms of lipodystrophy were identified earlier in patients from Brazil (n = 52, 12.8 years) compared with those from Turkey (n = 80, 18.3 years) and the United States (n = 98, 23.4 years) [13]. Variation in the distribution of lipodystrophy types reported for each country may have contributed to these differences [13]. Comorbidities reported for overall the Brazilian cohort in this study (comprising 23 patients with CGL, 25 patients with FPLD, and two patients each with AGL and APL) included diabetes and/or insulin resistance (29% of patients), triglycerides >500 mg/dL (>5.65 mmol/L; 50% of patients), and liver, kidney, and heart abnormalities (71%, 48%, and 23% of patients, respectively) [13]. These results are broadly consistent with the findings from an analysis of a large Brazilian CGL cohort (n = 44) in which 68% of patients had diabetes mellitus and 96% had hypertriglyceridemia (i.e., triglycerides >150 mg/dL [>1.69 mmol/L]) [24]. Similarly, analysis of the Ceará CGL population (N = 19) has revealed a high prevalence of diabetes mellitus (79% of patients) and triglycerides >150 mg/dL (63% of patients) while osteolytic lesions were present in 74% of patients [29]. Diabetes mellitus, severe hypertriglyceridemia (i.e., triglycerides ≥ 500 mg/dL), and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) were also identified in 58%, 35%, and 57% of patients in the cohort of 106 patients with a positive genetic test for FPLD [30].

Mortality events in the Brazilian lipodystrophy population: a review of the clinical data

Patients with lipodystrophy syndromes, especially those with GL, have a lower mean life expectancy versus the general population [13, 15, 32]. Assessment of death certificates and medical records from 20 Brazilian patients with CGL estimated the mean age at the time of death at 27 years. The primary causes of death were liver disease (35% of patients), infection (35% of patients), kidney failure (15% of patients), myocardial infarction/sudden death (10% of patients), and pancreatitis (5% of patients) [15]. In contrast, data from 72 Turkish patients with GL showed that 11 patients died during follow-up (mean age at time of death, 35 years) with sepsis, renal disease, cardiovascular disease, and liver disease among the leading causes [33]. These data from Brazil and Turkey suggest that risk factors for mortality may vary between populations.

Mortality data was limited to four patients in the cohort of 106 Brazilian patients with a genetic diagnosis of FPLD (median age at the time of death, 70 years) and was primarily attributed to cardiovascular events [30]. Notably, all four deceased patients carried a variant at codon position 482 in the LMNA gene, suggesting a potential association between this variant and cardiac complications [30].

Expert panel discussion on the epidemiological of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil

A comprehensive country-wide characterization of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil is needed to better understand the local epidemiology. This research may be hindered by Brazil’s size and geography and varying standards in healthcare. Despite this, outputs from this research could have important implications for disease management resources, especially in prevalence hot spots. For CGL and FPLD, this research may uncover genetic, clinical, and social factors that drive increased disease prevalence in some Brazilian regions [23, 30].

The diagnostic journey of Brazilian patients with lipodystrophy syndromes

In Brazil, as elsewhere, diagnostic delays in lipodystrophy syndromes are common [20, 34]. A paucity of structured patient referral pathways to specialist medical centers. In general, Brazilian patients initially present to a wide range of clinicians but only receive a definitive diagnosis of lipodystrophy following referral to specialist centers, of which there are few in Brazil. For many patients, metabolic disease may have already developed prior to diagnosis of lipodystrophy; for example, Brazilian data from the international chart review study show that diagnosis occurred, on average, 3.4 years after first symptom development [13]. In the Ceará GL population, the mean age of diagnosis was 3.6 years; however, some patients were not diagnosed until at least the fifth decade of life. Expert panel discussion on the disease-related factors contributing to diagnostic delays in lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil

Phenotypic variability and overlap with other conditions. There is a broad phenotypic heterogeneity among patients with lipodystrophy, especially those with PL (Figs. 2 and 3). This, in turn, can confound diagnosis [1, 10, 19]. For example, patients with FPLD1 often present with fat accumulation in the trunk and severe insulin resistance which can resemble truncal obesity complicated with type 2 diabetes [22]. Male patients with FPLD typically have milder metabolic sequelae than females and can remain undetected until comorbidities worsen [10, 35, 36]. Furthermore, as many non-lipodystrophic males have a muscular habitus, recognizing males with PL can be challenging [1]. Malnutrition due to poor economic status may also mask the phenotypic presentation of GL and PL.

Expert panel discussion on the establishment of a minimal panel of diagnostic criteria for inclusion in National practice guideline for lipodystrophy syndromes

Although definitive diagnostic criteria for lipodystrophy syndromes have not been established, clinical features that increase the suspicion of lipodystrophy have been described [1, 20]. However, some of this information may not be generalizable to the Brazilian population. In this regard, at the face-to-face expert panel meeting, the development of a Brazilian practice guideline for lipodystrophy syndromes that could assist non-specialist healthcare professionals (HCPs) was discussed. The expert panel agreed that a national guideline for lipodystrophy syndromes should consider: (1) the organization of the Brazilian healthcare system (including the medical staff involved in the care of patients); and (2) the establishment of structured referral pathways for patients to specialized medical centers [34].

Among the diagnostic parameters for lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil that were considered at the face-to-face expert meeting included:

Holistic evaluation of patient medical history. Guidance for general practitioners on how to perform a holistic, detailed clinical evaluation of suspected cases (including a review of a patient’s family history) could lead to improved recognition of patients and help reduce diagnostic delays [1, 20].

Anthropometric analysis. The use of skinfold thickness measurements, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), and whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can aid diagnosis of lipodystrophy syndromes [1, 11, 20]. Examples of previous studies conducted in Brazil and elsewhere, are given below. Of note, analysis of UK Biobank study showed that females with an FMR >1.2 and males with an FMR >1.7 had an increased cardiometabolic risk, suggesting that FMR may help screening for patients with lipodystrophy-like body habitus and its associated comorbidities [44].Body adiposity index (BAI; defined as [(hip circumference in cm)/((height in m)1.5)–18]) has been proposed as an alternative to DEXA in females with FPLD2. A study involving 13 Brazilian patients with FPLD2 showed that mean BAI was significantly lower versus the control group (n = 13; 24.6 vs. 30.4; P < 0.001) and was correlated with leptin levels [45].A recent study compared skinfold and DEXA measurements and whole-body MRI from eight Asian females with FPLD1, and four female controls matched for age, ethnicity and BMI [46]. The FPLD group had a significantly lower mean thigh skinfold thickness versus the control group (19.5 mm vs. 48.2 mm, P = 0.008), lower mean leg fat percentage (33.3% vs. 47.6%, P = 0.004) and increased mean ratio of trunk-to-leg fat percentage (1.43 vs. 0.95, P = 0.004) versus the control group. Additionally, MRI analysis showed lower mean thigh subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) volume (2350 cc vs. 4921 cc, P = 0.004) and mean calf SAT volume (442 cc vs. 927 cc, P = 0.008) in the FPLD group versus controls [46].

Management of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil

The current management of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil follows the multi-society practice guidelines [1]. Accordingly, the standard clinical approach to lipodystrophy syndromes involves lifestyle modification (i.e., diet and exercise) and the use of standard medical therapies (e.g., glucose- and lipid lowering agents, cardiovascular medications) to treat the specific metabolic comorbidities of the presenting patient [1].

Expert panel discussion on diet and exercise in the management of lipodystrophy syndromes

The key outcomes from the expert panel discussion (with support from the scientific literature) on the use of diet and exercise in the holistic management of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil are summarized below.

Diet is central to disease management. A balanced macronutrient composition (e.g., 50–60% carbohydrates, 20–30% fat, and 20% protein) has been recommended [1]. Diets should be individualized, and the regional and economic status of each patient should be considered. Dietary restrictions may be challenging in patients with hyperphagia [1, 11].

The Brazilian guideline for the pharmacological management of type 2 diabetes provides information on the use of dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP-4) inhibitors (gliptins), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1-RA), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors [54]. While these agents are approved for the treatment of diabetes—not for lipodystrophy—recent retrospective studies have reported the use of GLP1-RA and SGLT2 inhibitors in adult patients with PL showing improved glycemic control relative to baseline [55–57]. Reductions in body weight relative to baseline were also noted [56, 57]. Additionally, a retrospective analysis reported the use of tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1 RA/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist, for diabetes in patients with lipodystrophy syndromes [58]. Among the FPLD cohort (n = 14; 9-month follow-up), median reductions in BMI, HbA1c, triglycerides, and total insulin usage from baseline were observed [58]. The most commonly reported adverse events reported for GLP1-RAs and tirzepatide in these studies were gastrointestinal in nature [56, 58, 59]. Although these early data suggest that SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1-RAs and tirzepatide may be considered in some patients with lipodystrophy syndromes presenting with diabetes, they have not been systematically evaluated in clinical trials in patients with lipodystrophy syndromes nor are they indicated for routine use in the lipodystrophy population.

Metreleptin for the treatment of lipodystrophy syndromes

Metreleptin, a recombinant analog of human leptin, was approved in Brazil in 2023 as an adjunct to diet as a replacement therapy to treat the complications of leptin deficiency in patients with confirmed GL aged ≥ 2 years, and in patients aged ≥ 12 years with confirmed PL in whom standard treatments have not achieved adequate metabolic control [63]. The multi-society guideline for lipodystrophy syndromes lists metreleptin (with diet) as a first-line treatment for the metabolic and endocrine complications of GL including pediatric patients [1]. In Brazil, access to metreleptin is controlled at a government level and is provided on an individual patient basis.

Clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of metreleptin in lipodystrophy [64–67]. In patients with GL (n = 66), metreleptin resulted in significant mean reductions in HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides that were sustained over 36 months [65]. Metreleptin also improved metabolic outcomes (i.e., HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides) in patients with PL, especially those who had baseline HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or triglycerides ≥ 5.65mmol/L (≥ 500 mg/dL; n = 31) [67].

Data regarding the real-world use of metreleptin in Brazil is limited. However, evidence from other regions supports the effectiveness of metreleptin in GL and in select patients with PL [68, 69]. Other effects of metreleptin in patients with lipodystrophy syndromes include improvements in cardiac, hepatic and renal outcomes, female reproductive function, hyperphagia, and quality of life [70–75].

Increasing the awareness of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil

As the familiarity with lipodystrophy syndromes can vary among HCPs, educational initiatives for non-specialists could help improve disease awareness and support patient referral pathways to specialized centers [1, 34].

Discussion

In this manuscript, we discuss the current knowledgebase of lipodystrophy syndromes in Brazil. Importantly, we highlight the main knowledge gaps among the medical community regarding lipodystrophy syndrome and discuss how these may be improved. As a group of rare diseases, lipodystrophy syndromes present a diagnostic challenge for healthcare professionals who encounter them, leading to an underrepresentation of the true prevalence and delayed medical intervention [19, 20, 22]. In this regard, a Brazilian-wide epidemiological study would be an important step support the optimization of medical care of lipodystrophy syndromes. The recent multi-center Brazilian study in FPLD provides a potential framework for a country-wide initiative.

The authors of the current manuscript have recently published an expert consensus document for FPLD in Brazil [42]. Similarly, a national guideline for GL is planned which aims to improve diagnosis and optimize and disease management strategies for this main form of lipodystrophy syndromes. We envisage that some information presented in the current manuscript may help support this endeavor.
