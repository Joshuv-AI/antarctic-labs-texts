A review on toxoplasma gondii research in Armenia: Gaps and opportunities
Authors: Ahmad Daryani, Gohar Grigoryan, Hasmik Gevorgyan, Oleg Shcherbakov, Ruzanna Gevorgyan, Sargis Aghayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Toxoplasma gondii is a zoonotic parasite that can infect humans and animals, through various transmission routes, causing toxoplasmosis. The infection can pose severe complications in immunocompromised individuals and pregnant women, as well as may lead to ocular and neurological disorders in some cases. The global prevalence of T. gondii infection varies depending on geographical, environmental, and socio-cultural factors. In Armenia, our understanding of T. gondii infection epidemiology remains limited, with scarce data on the seroprevalence and risk factors associated with T. gondii infection in both human and animal populations. Therefore, this review aims to summarize the available literature on T. gondii infection/toxoplasmosis research in Armenia identifying the knowledge gaps and outlining research priorities for future studies. We conducted a comprehensive search in different international databases and local libraries for articles published up to the end of 2023 using relevant keywords and inclusion criteria. We extracted the data on the serological methods, sample size and type, demographic and clinical characteristics, and main findings of each study. By synthesizing current research findings and identifying areas requiring further investigation, this review aims to contribute to the advancement of our understanding and management of T. gondii infection/toxoplasmosis in Armenia.

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) is a ubiquitous protozoan parasite that infects a wide range of warm-blooded animals, including humans (Tenter et al., 2000; Ferra et al., 2020). The parasite has a complex life cycle that involves sexual reproduction in felids, the definitive hosts, and asexual multiplication in various intermediate hosts (VanWormer et al., 2013). Humans can acquire the infection through ingestion of contaminated food or water, contact with the infected cat feces, congenital transmission, or organ transplantation (McAuley, 2014; Jones and Dubey, 2012).

In the United States and the United Kingdom, it is estimated that 16–40% of the population are infected, whereas, in Central and South America and Continental Europe, estimates of infection range from 50 to 80% (Dubey and Beattie, 1988).

The clinical manifestations of toxoplasmosis vary depending on the immune status, genetic background, and route of infection of the host (Weiss and Dubey, 2009). In immunocompetent individuals, the infection is usually asymptomatic or causes mild flu-like symptoms (Gelaye et al., 2015). However, in immunocompromised patients, such as those with AIDS, cancer, or organ transplants, the infection can reactivate and cause severe complications, such as encephalitis, pneumonia, or chorioretinitis (Negussie et al., 2017). Congenital toxoplasmosis occurs when a pregnant woman acquires the infection up to 3 months before conception or for the first-time during pregnancy and transmits it to the fetus (Gavinet et al., 1997; Vogel et al., 1996). This can result in miscarriage, stillbirth, or a range of congenital anomalies, such as hydrocephalus, microcephaly, intracranial calcifications, or ocular lesions (Negussie et al., 2017; Abamecha and Awel, 2016; Dubey, 2016).

Toxoplasmosis has a major public health and economic impacts, as it causes significant morbidity, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (Furtado et al., n.d.). Moreover, toxoplasmosis poses a threat to animal health and welfare, as it can cause abortion, neonatal mortality, and neurological disorders in livestock and pets (Buxton et al., 2007). Toxoplasmosis also affects wildlife conservation, as it can cause population declines or extinctions of endangered species, such as sea otters, Eurasian freshwater otters, kangaroos, or Hawaiian monk seals (Hill and Dubey, 2014; Chadwick et al., 2013). The epidemiology of T. gondii infection is influenced by various factors, such as climate, geography, host diversity, and food habits (Dabritz and Conrad, 2010). Although researchers from Yerevan State University, Armenia in collaboration with Toxoplasmosis Research Center (TRC) in Iran, have published articles on various aspects of T. gondii worldwide (Gohardehi et al., 2018; Chegeni et al., 2019a; Chegeni et al., 2019b), there is still a lack of sufficient data on the prevalence, risk factors, and genotypes of T. gondii in different parts of Armenia, a landlocked country in the South Caucasus region. This country boasts a diverse landscape, encompassing arid to alpine zones, and a rich fauna, including livestock and wildlife. Therefore, epidemiological studies of T. gondii infection in Armenia are necessary to assess the burden of disease, identify the sources and routes of infection, and implement appropriate prevention and control measures. These studies help us understand where T. gondii is found. They also reveal its genetic diversity. New strains or genotypes may emerge. The findings support health education and sanitation efforts. They improve food safety measures. They aid vaccine development and drug discovery.

Geographical situations and history of T. Gondii research in Armenia

Armenia lies in the northwest part of the Armenian Highland. It covers an area of 29,743 km2 (General information about Republic of Armenia – Armenia, 2024), situated between 38°50′-41°18′ north latitude and 43°27′-46°37′ east latitude (National Action Programme to Combat Desertificat Ion in Armenia, 2002). The republic shares 1479 km of borders (National Action Programme to Combat Desertificat Ion in Armenia, 2002) with neighboring countries. Specifically, it has a northern border with Georgia, an eastern border with Azerbaijan, a western and southwestern border with Turkey, and a southern border with Iran. According to the results of Republic of Armenia Census 2022 by the Statistical Committee of the Republic of Armenia (ARMSTAT) the permanent population of the country stands at 2,932,731 (ARMSTAT, 2022).

The lowest point in Armenia lies 375 m above sea level, situated along the banks of the Debet River. Conversely, the highest point is the northern peak of Mount Aragats, towering at 4095 m above sea level. Approximately 76.5% of the country's territory lies within the elevation range of 1000–2500 m above sea level, with an average altitude of 1850 m above sea level. The remaining 36.4% of the land consists of mountains and plateaus (General information about Republic of Armenia – Armenia, 2024).

In the Republic of Armenia, there are four distinct regions that vary in terms of their relief origin. Those are the section of the plicate and fission-fragmented mountains and intermountain valleys, the volcanic covering sections, the Arax streamside plicate and fission-fragmented mountains system and the Ararat Valley. The complex relief of Armenia leads to noticeable changes in the natural landscape throughout the country. Each valley, waterbody, and mountain peak possess its own unique scenery. This diversity is influenced by the strategic location of the Armenian Highland, situated at the convergence of several geographical regions (National Action Programme to Combat Desertificat Ion in Armenia, 2002).

The mean temperature in Armenia varies significantly across the seasons. In January, it averages −2.3 °C, while in June, it rises to +16.3 °C. The extreme temperature range in the country spans from a high of 42 °C down to −46 °C. These temperature fluctuations can be quite dramatic, making Armenia a land of diverse climates (National Action Programme to Combat Desertificat Ion in Armenia, 2002).

The average yearly relative moisture varies between 56% and 75%. Unlike other climate factors, it lacks a distinct regular pattern that correlates with altitude. Instead, it is influenced by the micro and meso-climatic conditions specific to the area (National Action Programme to Combat Desertificat Ion in Armenia, 2002).

Given its diverse climate and ecology, the country harbors a variety of domestic and wild animals, which can serve as intermediate or definitive hosts for T. gondii. However, there is limited information available on the prevalence, risk factors, clinical manifestations, and genetic diversity of T. gondii both in humans and animals in Armenia.

Search strategy and selection criteria

To find relevant studies on T. gondii infection in Armenia, we searched the databases (ScienceDirect, Scopus, PubMed, Google Scholar) and Armenian libraries (The Republican Scientific Medical Library of Armenia: https://medlib.am/, The National Library of Armenia: https://nla.am/, Fundamental Scientific Library of the National Academy of Sciences of Armenia: https://www.flib.sci.am/, and Pan-Armenian Digital Library: https://arar.sci.am/dlibra) for studies published up to December 31, 2023. We used the keywords: “Toxoplasma gondii” OR “T. gondii” OR “toxoplasmosis”, “Armenia” OR “Republic of Armenia” OR “Armenian”, “prevalence”, “seroprevalence”, “human”, and “animals”. We also checked the reference lists of the retrieved articles and contacted the authors of relevant studies for additional information.

We applied the following inclusion criteria:a.All studies conducted in Armenia up to December 31, 2023;b.The studies were written in English, Russian or Armenian languages;c.The studies provided information on various research aspects of T. gondii, including prevalence, risk factors, treatment, and diagnosis, both in humans and animals;

Results and discussion

T. Gondii infection in humans

Understanding the prevalence and distribution of T. gondii infections in humans is crucial for public health planning, allocating resources effectively, and targeting interventions. By identifying these patterns, we can develop preventive strategies and educate the public about safe practices. Moreover, studying epidemiology helps identify high-risk groups, enhance diagnostic precision, and ensure timely treatment. In conclusion, investigating the epidemiology of T. gondii yields valuable insights for preventing, diagnosing, and managing infections, thereby safeguarding public health and well-being (Aguirre et al., 2019; Dubey and Jones, 2008; Mead et al., 1999; Dubey, 2008).

T. Gondii infection in healthy people and various patients referring to hospitals

In the group of healthy people, the following people were examined for T. gondii infection, including: pregnant women, women of reproductive age, blood donors, general population and workers (agro-industrial complex, meat processing plant, nutria fur farm, animal breeding farm, stockbreeders, etc.). The first report of T. gondii infection in Armenia dates back to 1961 published by Isahakyan (Isahakyan, 1961). A serological study of patients from children's hospitals and eye clinics in Armenia suspected of toxoplasmosis was conducted at the Department of Biology and Parasitology of the Yerevan Medical Institute. Among the 148 patients examined there were 17 children and 131 adults. Their blood serum was analyzed using the complement fixation test (CFT) method resulting in positive in 11 cases (4 children with inborn toxoplasmosis symptoms and 7 adults) (Isahakyan, 1961). The first studies on the general population including blood donors and pregnant women using the complement fixation test were conducted by Filina et al. in 1964 and 1968. Surprisingly, the prevalence of the parasite in both groups was found to be 0% (Filina and Poghosyan, 1964; Filina and Strelnikova, 1968). Prevalence of T. gondii in different groups of healthy people was 0–78.9% (see Table 1) (Filina and Strelnikova, 1968; Janibekyan et al., 2002).

Molan et al. (2019) in a study on global status of T. gondii infection indicated an average global seroprevalence rate of 25.7% (95% CI: 25.6–25.8%). The overall range of seroprevalence was determined to be 0.5–87.7%. African countries had the highest average seroprevalence rate of 61.4%, followed by Oceania with 38.5%, South America with 31.2%, Europe with 29.6%, USA/Canada with 17.5%, and Asia with 16.4%. Numerous environmental and human factors affect the differences in T. gondii seroprevalence rates observed between the various countries and continents.

Overall, based on the above mentioned prevalence rates (up to 64.2% in different women and 49% in various patients referring to hospital) suggest that monitoring the source of and transmission may assist public health authorities to clarify the risk factors involved, as well as focus on implementing optimal state-specific health policies targeting T. gondii transmission control (Molan et al., 2019).

T. Gondii infection in immunocompromised patients

The data on the epidemiology of T. gondii infection in some countries, especially in low- and middle-income countries, are scarce or outdated.

Armenia, similar to other countries in this category, has limited data on the prevalence of T. gondii infection among immunocompromised patients, based on findings from two studies.

The initial study involved 830 patients with HIV/AIDS receiving dispensary care, investigating various pathologies such as toxoplasmosis through serological and ophthalmological examinations. Of these patients, 160 (19%) passed away, comprising 134 men, 23 women, and 3 children (Table 3) (Hakobyan, 2013).

Another study aimed to assess the frequency and survival rates of CNS events among HIV/AIDS patients. All 104 cases of CNS damage were evaluated for various pathologies, including toxoplasmosis. Notably, there were 7 cases of CNS toxoplasmosis considered among CNS AIDS events. The study revealed that survival rates were higher among HIV/AIDS patients with CNS toxoplasmosis (87.5%) compared to those with cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection and HIV encephalopathy (Hakobyan and Davidyan, 2013) (Table 3).

The estimated global pooled prevalence of T. gondii infection in immunocompromised patients was 35.9% (32.0% for HIV/AIDS patients and 12.1% for cancer patients and 34.5% for transplant recipients (Wang et al., 2017).

Patients with a positive result are at risk of reactivation of the infection, while patients with a negative result should be informed to prevent primary infection. Health education, particularly toward avoiding eating raw and undercooked meat, and avoiding contact with cats' feces should also be considered.

T. Gondii infection in human psychiatric and behavioral disorders and physical diseases

Toxoplasma gondii infection has been associated with various psychiatric and behavioral disorder, such as schizophrenia (Tyebji et al., 2019), bipolar disorder (de Barros et al., 2017), personality changes, risk of having traffic accidents (Gohardehi et al., 2018), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (Nayeri et al., 2020a), epilepsy (Sadeghi et al., 2019), autism (Nayeri et al., 2020b), suicide (Amouei et al., 2020a), headache (Nayeri et al., 2020c), depression (Chegeni et al., 2019a), Parkinson's disease (Bayani et al., 2019) Alzheimer's disease (Nayeri Chegeni et al., 2019), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (Chegeni et al., 2019b; Yin et al., 2022).

The mechanisms underlying these associations are not fully understood, but may involve alterations in neurotransmitter systems, immune responses, and epigenetic regulation (Yin et al., 2022; Nayeri et al., 2022).

Toxoplasma gondii infection has the remarkable ability to manipulate the behavior of rodents. Infected rodents become less fearful of cats, which makes them more susceptible to predation by felines. This altered behavior ultimately facilitates the transmission of the parasite from the intermediate host (rodent) to the definitive host (cat) (Flegr, 2013; Ingram et al., 2013).

On the other hand, T. gondii infection has been associated with various physical diseases, such as diabetes (Ashraf et al., 2023), inflammatory bowel disease (Shapira et al., 2012), hepatitis (El-Nahas et al., 2014), thyroiditis (Valizadeh et al., 2022), chronic liver disease (El-Sayed et al., 2016), hypertension (Erickson et al., 2022), osteoporosis (Zhu et al., 2022), infertility (Hegazy et al., 2023), obesity (Molan and Ismail, 2021), systemic lupus erythematosus (Bassett et al., 2022) and thalassemia (El-Tantawy et al., 2019). The mechanisms underlying these associations are not fully understood, but may involve chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, hormonal imbalance, and metabolic disturbance induced by the parasite.

Numerous studies related to this topic have predominantly taken place in Europe and North America. However, there is limited data available regarding the prevalence and consequences of T. gondii infection in other regions, including the Republic of Armenia. In a study conducted by Sarkisyan et al. in 1975, researchers investigated the prevalence of toxoplasmosis among patients at Ijevan Mental Hospital. The study included patients who had been in the clinic for at least 5 years and had diagnoses such as schizophrenia, intellectual disabilities, and epilepsy. Among the 296 patients examined, 35 (11.8%) tested positive for toxoplasmosis using CFT (Table 3) (Sarkisyan et al., 1975).

The global pooled seroprevalence of T. gondii IgG antibody was 38.27% among neuropsychiatric patients. The highest pooled prevalence of T. gondii IgG antibody was in Europe (57%) followed by Africa (45.25%) and Asia (43%) (Bisetegn et al., 2023).

Clinicians are encouraged to recognize the possibility of T. gondii infection in these patients, and initiate routine diagnostic testing to confirm previous or on going infection.

T. Gondii infection in different animals

Sarkisyan et al. (1963) for the first time studied on prevalence of T. gondii in different animals. They reported that infection rates in cattle, small ruminants and different rodents were 0.77%, 1.6%, and 0–1.64%, respectively (Sarkisyan and Vartanyan, 1963).

The prevalence of T. gondii infection in cats and various livestock such as cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, camels, and poultry can differ based on factors such as geographical location, animal species, farming practices, and diagnostic techniques (Weiss and Dubey, 2009; Dubey, n.d.; Dubey, 2009a; Dubey, 2009b; Elmore et al., 2010; Montoya and Liesenfeld, n.d.; Saadatnia and Golkar, 2012). Toxoplasmosis in food animals can cause significant economic losses due to abortion, reduced productivity, reproductive failure, and condemnation of carcasses (Stelzer et al., 2019; Basso et al., 2017; Halos et al., 2010).

Toxoplasma gondii infection can impact wildlife populations, as demonstrated by a recent study revealing evidence of infection in several wild animals across the southeastern United States. These animals include feral hogs, white-tailed deer, raccoons, coyotes, opossums, woodchucks, elk, gray foxes, otters, and minks (Chadwick et al., 2013; Gerhold et al., 2017).

The authors of the present review have conducted search on T. gondii infection in wild birds in Armenia and found prevalence 12% (unpublished data).

Comparing this to global data, the researchers in several systematic review and meta-analysis studies reported the global pooled prevalence of T. gondii infection in different animals: Montazeri et al. (2020) investigated the seroprevalence of T. gondii, reporting estimates ranging from 0 to 97% in domestic cats and 15–100% in wild felids. Global seroprevalence was 35% in domestic cats and 59% in wild felids (Montazeri et al., n.d.). Another meta-analysis focusing on sheep and goats estimated pooled T. gondii seroprevalence rates of 33.86% in sheep and 31.78% in goats [in Europe (sheep: 41.01%, goats: 38.88%) and Africa (sheep: 38.48%, goats: 37.89%)]. Higher seroprevalence in sheep and goats is linked to greater exposure to cats, their definitive hosts (Ahaduzzaman and Hasan, 2022). The global seroprevalence of T. gondii infection in bovines and pigs was 17.91 and 19%, respectively (Shariatzadeh et al., 2021; Foroutan et al., 2019). Other meta-analysis studies focusing on rodents and birds estimated pooled T. gondii seroprevalence rates of 6 and 4%, respectively. Domestic birds had a higher prevalence at 28% compared to wild birds (Galeh et al., 2020; Zaki et al., 2024). Monitoring T. gondii infection in food-producing animals and wildlife is essential for public health and environmental protection. Livestock can serve as a direct source of human infection through undercooked meat, while wildlife acts as a sentinel for environmental contamination by T. gondii oocysts. These resilient oocysts persist in soil and water, leading to transmission in various ecosystems and food chains. Surveillance enables the identification of high-risk areas, supports food safety regulations, and informs strategies to minimize exposure, especially for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals. Advances in molecular detection methods enhance the accuracy of monitoring, allowing for timely interventions and public awareness campaigns. Through effective monitoring, the spread of T. gondii infection can be controlled, benefiting both human health and ecological stability (Dubey et al., 2020; Robert-Gangneux and Dardé, 2012).

Research gaps

Genetic variability in T. Gondii

Toxoplasma gondii has a complex life cycle and a wide host range, and exhibits a high genetic diversity worldwide. The clinical manifestations and outcomes of toxoplasmosis vary depending on the nutrition, host immune status, host genotype, infecting dose and parasite virulence (Sibley et al., n.d.; Blader and Saeij, 2009).

On the other hand, humans become infected with T. gondii mostly by consumption of uncooked meat or the oocysts. Therefore, information on genotypes of isolates from animals, especially the knowledge of the genotyping of strains prevailing in domestic and wild felids is important with respect to public health because these hosts serve as an important source for the transmission of the parasite to human infections (Amouei et al., 2020b).

The picture of the T. gondii population structure, especially in Northern America and Europe, presented them in three main clonal lineages (Types I, II and III) related to mouse virulence (Ajzenberg et al., 2002; Howe and Sibley, 1995; de Melo Ferreira et al., 2006).

There are different clonal lineages and recombinant strains of T. gondii, with varying geographic distribution and prevalence. However, in certain regions of Europe, including Armenia, there is a notable absence of genotyping data for T. gondii. Taking this into consideration, authors have recently addressed this gap by initiating studies to investigate the circulation of T. gondii genotypes in Armenia. This ongoing work aims to collect and analyze genetic data from various animal species to better understand the diversity and distribution of T. gondii strains within the country.

Disease burden of toxoplasmosis

Based on a comprehensive systematic review, it was determined that the worldwide yearly occurrence of congenital toxoplasmosis stands at approximately 190,100 cases. Additionally, the overall impact of toxoplasmosis on global health is estimated to result in 1.20 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Notably, the most significant disease burden is observed in South America and certain Middle Eastern and low-income countries (Torgerson and Mastroiacovo, 2013).

In the United States, toxoplasmosis imposes an annual burden estimated at 10,964 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), with an associated cost of illness exceeding $2.9 billion (Batz et al., 2012). This parasitic infection is recognized as a prominent cause of death linked to foodborne illnesses in the country (Jones et al., 2014). However, data on toxoplasmosis epidemiology and impact remain limited and varied, particularly in regions where the infection is both endemic and underdiagnosed or underreported.

Toxoplasmosis data in Armenia are notably scarce. This country, with a population of approximately 3 million people, boasts a life expectancy of 76 years. Additionally, Armenia grapples with a significant prevalence of non-communicable diseases, including cardiovascular diseases and cancers, which contribute to the majority of deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) within the country (World Health Organization, 2024).

The epidemiology and burden of human and animal toxoplasmosis in Armenia are largely unknown, as there is no routine surveillance or reporting system for the infection. The only available data are from a few studies that have investigated the seroprevalence of T. gondii infection in different population groups (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3) and animals (Table 4). However, these studies are limited by their small scale, lack of representativeness, and methodological heterogeneity, and do not provide information on the clinical outcomes, risk factors, or economic impact of toxoplasmosis in Armenia.

The risk factors of toxoplasmosis

The risk factors of toxoplasmosis in humans are mainly related to exposure to the T. gondii, which can be found in contaminated food, water, soil, or cat feces. Consuming undercooked or contaminated meat, particularly pork, lamb, or venison, as well as meat products or shellfish like oysters and mussels, can pose health risks. Drinking untreated water or unpasteurized goat's milk may also be hazardous. Handling cat litter or soil that might contain cat feces increases exposure to potential infections. Failing to wash fruits and vegetables that could have been in contact with contaminated soil is another concern. Health risks can vary depending on whether someone lives in a rural or urban area and their level of education. Being pregnant or having a weakened immune system further elevates susceptibility to these dangers (People at Increased Risk for Toxoplasmosis, 2024).

There is an issue of insufficient education level about T. gondii in Armenia which is compounded by the limited dissemination of information through different platforms, so individuals may not be taking necessary precautions to avoid exposure to the parasite.

Environmental contamination with T. Gondii

The occurrence, transmission, and distribution of T. gondii in the environment are shaped by several factors. These include climate change, human activities, host diversity, and genetic diversity of the parasite. For instance, warmer and wetter conditions can enhance the survival and dissemination of oocysts (the infective stage of the parasite) in soil and water. Additionally, human activities such as deforestation, urbanization, agriculture, and pet ownership may modify the habitat and contact patterns of hosts, thereby increasing the risk of exposure to this important zoonotic pathogen (Patz et al., 2000; Meerburg and Kijlstra, 2009; Laaksonen et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2016).

Environmental contamination with T. gondii primarily occurs through the release of oocysts, which represent the resilient and infectious stage of the parasite. These oocysts are shed by felids, the definitive hosts of T. gondii (Bowie et al., 1997). Remarkably durable, oocysts can persist in soil and water for extended periods. They can also be disseminated to distant areas via runoff, wind, or vectors. Furthermore, oocysts have the potential to contaminate crops, vegetables, and fruits that are subsequently consumed by both humans and animals (Yilmaz and Hopkins, 1972; Bettiol et al., 2000; Winiecka-Krusnell et al., 2009).

There are no specific data on the contamination of the soil and the environment with T. gondii oocysts in Armenia. However, it has been shown that T. gondii infection in cats might be an indication of contaminated environment, cats typically shed oocysts for 7 to 21 days after their first infection (Ohiolei and Isaac, 2016). Although oocysts of T. gondii are shed for a period of time during the life of the cat, millions of shed oocysts survive for months or even up to a year in the soil (Dubey, 2010). The high T. gondii infection rates observed in various domestic animals and cats may imply contamination of the rural environment through oocysts excreted by cats.

Research on the environmental contamination with T. gondii would provide valuable information to determine the prevalence, distribution, genotypes, and transmission dynamics of parasite in the country, and to identify the main sources and routes of infection for humans and animals.

Filling the gaps in T. gondii studies is crucial because this parasite affects nearly all warm-blooded animals, including humans. Understanding its genetic variability, transmission routes, and impact on different populations helps improve diagnosis, treatment, and prevention strategies. By addressing these gaps, scientists can develop better vaccines, targeted treatments, and improved surveillance methods to reduce the burden of toxoplasmosis worldwide.

Conclusion

Since the initial identification and detection of T. gondii in Armenia back in 1961, Armenian researchers have dedicated six decades to studying this parasite.

Toxoplasmosis, particularly concerning pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals, poses significant public health risks. Its impact extends beyond medical implications, affecting social and economic aspects as well. Understanding the epidemiology of this infection in humans in different regions of country is crucial for effective parasite control.

Despite toxoplasmosis typically being associated with causing abortions in pregnant women, there remains limited knowledge regarding the incidence rate of congenital toxoplasmosis. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a thorough and well-designed study to investigate congenital toxoplasmosis in Armenia.

Currently, many hospitals in Armenia rely on a single IgM and IgG test to detect toxoplasmosis in pregnant individuals. However, this approach is not entirely reliable and can yield false positive results, potentially leading to unnecessary treatment and even termination of pregnancy. Unfortunately, there is no centralized facility available for consultation and confirmation of diagnosis. To tackle this concern, it is vital to set up reference laboratories capable of delivering more precise outcomes through IgG avidity testing and molecular techniques for assessing human samples. By taking these steps, local clinics and hospitals can enhance the reliability of diagnoses and effectively manage toxoplasmosis during pregnancy.

On the other hand, there is a need to develop and implement research programs directed to the association of T. gondii infection with mental and neurodegenerative disorders.

Despite the numerous domestic cats, there remains inadequate data concerning their T. gondii infection. Therefore, it is essential to conduct thorough nationwide research to determine the prevalence of T. gondii infection in both household pets and free-roaming felines. This understanding will facilitate the development of effective preventive and control strategies against this widespread zoonotic parasite.

Investigating diverse areas of interest, it becomes essential to conduct studies on wild carnivores, such as wild cats. This undertaking seeks to reveal potential sources of infection that could impact environmental contamination.

Furthermore, Armenia is a producer of livestock meat for both local consumption and export. However, at present, there is insufficient data available regarding the existence of viable T. gondii in edible meats. To enhance our comprehension of potential risks, it becomes imperative to isolate T. gondii from meat samples and conduct further characterization, especially in the context of sheep infection and its implications for human health.

Since there is currently no available information regarding the contamination of soil, water, fruits, vegetables, etc., with T. gondii in Armenia, it is crucial to conduct research on the extent of environmental contamination caused by oocysts and even viable T. gondii.

Limited information exists regarding the risk factors and infection sources related to T. gondii in both humans and animals in Armenia. Consequently, there is an urgent need to establish and execute research initiatives aimed at addressing these gaps. The insights gained from such studies will contribute to the formulation of more effective preventive measures for individuals at elevated risk, including pregnant women, women of childbearing age, and HIV patients. These measures aim to reduce the incidence of congenital and cerebral toxoplasmosis. This parasite is known to cause abortions in livestock such as sheep and goats, leading to significant financial implications for farmers and producers. Understanding and mitigating these losses require thorough research into the prevalence and impact of T. gondii infection in livestock populations.

The clinical manifestations and outcomes of toxoplasmosis are influenced by various factors, including the genotypes of T. gondii. Currently, researchers in Armenia are actively engaged in genotyping T. gondii in livestock. However, despite these efforts, there remains a dearth of genetic characterization to pinpoint the specific T. gondii strain prevalent among HIV patients, pregnant women, and domestic cats. Therefore, conducting additional studies in this field is crucial.

Armenian researchers are actively investigating various aspects of T. gondii control. Their efforts include developing immunization strategies using in-silico design for excreted-secreted antigens and exploring treatment options involving herbal extracts. However, to enhance our understanding and combat toxoplasmosis effectively, it is essential to broaden these research activities to encompass additional antigens/genes for immunization and designing of serology and molecular commercial kits, various plant extracts, and synthetic compounds in our future studies.

Finally, to effectively manage and prevent toxoplasmosis, it is crucial to raise awareness about the risk factors associated with this infection in both humans and animals. Implementing educational initiatives is essential, particularly for specific groups such as pregnant women, women of childbearing age (including high school and university female students), individuals seeking premarital counseling, and HIV patients. These educational efforts can be disseminated through health education programs, local newspapers, television broadcasts, radio shows, and more recently, via social media platforms.
