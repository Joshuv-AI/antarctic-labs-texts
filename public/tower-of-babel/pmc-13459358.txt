ZNF662 inhibits oncogenesis through NUPR1/p53 signaling pathway in epithelial ovarian cancer and is regulated by hsa-miR-429
Authors: Zhen Huang, Hongxue Fu, Ling Li, Wenjing Liao, Jing Zhang, Qin Tang, Mao Huang, Tingxiu Xiang, Liangdan Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) represents the most lethal malignancy of the female reproductive system, though its etiology and pathogenesis remain incompletely characterized. The Krüppel-associated box domain zinc finger protein (KRAB-ZNF) family, the largest transcription factor family in mammals, plays critical roles in malignant tumor development and progression. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance and molecular mechanisms of ZNF662 in EOC pathogenesis.MethodsZNF662 expression patterns and their association with clinicopathological features and prognosis were analyzed using integrated bioinformatics (The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), CSIOVDB, Kaplan–Meier Plotter) and clinical tissue specimens. Functional impacts on proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression were assessed then. Mechanistic studies delineating upstream regulators and downstream effectors employed transcriptome sequencing, dual-luciferase reporter assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), and functional validation experiments.ResultsZNF662 expression was downregulated in ovarian cancer tissues, correlating with advanced tumor stage, ascites presence, and poor overall survival. Functional assays demonstrated that ZNF662 suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion of ovarian cancer cells while promoting apoptosis and inducing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest. Bioinformatics analysis and dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that hsa-miR-429 directly binds to the 3ʹ-untranslated region (UTR) of ZNF662, downregulating its expression and promoting malignant behaviors. Transcriptomic and mechanistic validation revealed that ZNF662 transcriptionally represses NUPR1 and activates the p53 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the malignant phenotype of EOC.ConclusionsThe hsa-miR-429/ZNF662/NUPR1/p53 pathway axis critically regulates EOC pathogenesis. ZNF662 represents a promising diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for EOC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00958-0.Keywords: ZNF662, NUPR1, p53, Hsa-miR-429, Epithelial ovarian cancer

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) represents the most lethal malignancy of the female reproductive system, with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) comprising 95% of cases [1]. Globally, it causes approximately 314,000 new cases and 207,000 deaths annually [2]. Most patients with EOC were first diagnosed at advanced stages with metastatic disease, missing the optimal therapeutic window [3]. Although recent advances including Poly ADP-ribose Polymerase inhibitors (PARPi), antiangiogenic agents, and immunotherapy have improved outcomes, the prognosis for advanced-stage disease requires substantial improvement [4–6]. Consequently, deeper understanding of EOC molecular mechanisms and discovery of biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and targeted therapies are critical priorities in precision oncology.

Krüppel-associated box zinc finger proteins (KRAB-ZNFs) represent the largest family of transcription factors in mammalian genomes [7]. These proteins feature two conserved domains: an N-terminal KRAB domain that mediates transcriptional repression and a C-terminal C2H2 zinc finger motif that facilitates sequence-specific DNA recognition and binding [8, 9]. Despite extensive structural homology across the family, the precise molecular functions of most KRAB-ZNFs remain undefined [10]. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence implicates several KRAB-ZNFs members in carcinogenesis, functioning as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors [11]. For instance, epigenetic silencing of ZNF471 drives renal cell carcinoma progression [12], while ZBRK1 (a KRAB-ZNF member) suppresses cervical cancer by directly repressing MMP9 transcription [13].

ZNF662 is a member of the KRAB-ZNF family. It appears in various omics datasets related to specific biological phenomena. Previous reports indicate that ZNF662 downregulation due to epigenetic DNA methylation serves as a prognostic marker in oral squamous cell carcinoma [14, 15]. Reduced ZNF662 expression also correlates with shorter survival in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [15]. Bioinformatics analyses reveal consistent downregulation of ZNF662 in multiple malignancies, with low expression linked to poor prognosis in patients with OC. However, its functional significance in OC progression remains uncharacterized. This study elucidates the role of ZNF662 in epithelial ovarian cancer, defining its expression patterns, biological functions, and molecular mechanisms.

Methods

Tissue samples

A total of 62 EOC tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical treatment at the Department of Gynecology, First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (August–December 2023). All cases were pathologically confirmed as epithelial ovarian cancer. A total of 20 control tissues (bilateral fallopian tubes or ovaries) were obtained from patients undergoing total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy or salpingectomy for uterine leiomyoma. Clinicopathological data (including age, preoperative serum CA125 level, pathological type, international federation of gynecology and obstetrics (FIGO) stage, lymph node metastasis, ascites, and tumor size) were extracted from electronic medical records. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (2022-256). All patients provided written informed consent preoperatively.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV3 (cat. CL-0215), CAOV3 (cat. CL-0055), and ES-2 (cat. CL-0079) were acquired from Procell Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The OVCAR3 cell line was generously provided by Professor Linghu Hua (Chongqing Medical University), and 293 T cells by Professor He Weiyang (Chongqing Medical University). All cell lines used in this study were used to perform STR analysis, and no cross‑contamination with other human cell lines was detected. The reports are provided in the Supplementary Material. Cells were cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Procell, Wuhan, China). Specifically: CAOV3 in McCoy’s 5A, 293 T in Dulbeco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM), and other lines (SKOV3, ES-2, OVCAR3) in RPMI 1640. (All media from Procell, Wuhan, China.)

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression analysis of ZNF662 and NUPR1 was performed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data via Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) [16]. ZNF662 expression was further validated in ovarian cancer cohorts (GSE18520, GSE29540, and GSE27651) sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Prognostic significance was evaluated via the Kaplan–Meier Plotter. Clinicopathological and survival analyses were performed using CSIOVDB [17]. Putative miRNAs targeting the ZNF662 3’UTR were predicted using TargetScan and miRDB, with miRNA expression profiles in ovarian cancer examined using GSE47841 (GEO). Promoter regions of target genes were retrieved from NCBI Gene, and binding sites for ZNF662 were predicted using JASPAR [18].

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from fresh-frozen tissues and ovarian cancer cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Shiga, Japan). RNA concentration and purity were quantified by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara), followed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Reactions (10 µL total volume) contained 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 1 µL cDNA template, 5 µL TB Green Premix, and 3 µL nuclease-free water. Amplification conditions comprised: 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. GAPDH and U6 served as endogenous controls. Relative mRNA expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method1, with primer sequences detailed in Supplementary Supplementary Table S1.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis (WB)

Fresh-frozen tissues and cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (MCE, NJ, USA) supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (MCE). Protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay kit (Beyotime). A quarter volume of loading buffer (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China) was added, and samples were denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Protein samples (30 μg per lane) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and developed with reagents from Zenbio (Chengdu, China). Relative protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Primary and secondary antibodies used are detailed in Supplementary Supplementary Table S2.

Immunohistochemistry

Human and nude mouse tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned (5 µm), deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Supplementary Table S2), followed by 50-min incubation at room temperature with secondary antibodies. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Histological images were captured at 200 × and 400 × magnification using light microscopy.

Lentivirus infection and RNA interference

SKOV3 and ES-2 ovarian cancer cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding Flag-tagged ZNF662 (Hanbio, Shanghai, China). Fluorescence microscopy at 48 h post-transduction confirmed transduction efficiency. Upon achieving > 80% fluorescence positivity, cells underwent 14-day selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to establish stable ZNF662-expressing lines. ZNF662-targeting siRNA, miRNA mimics, and miRNA inhibitors (Tsingke, Beijing, China) were transiently transfected using CALNP RNAi in vitro Transfection Reagent (D-Nano, Beijing, China) per manufacturer’s protocol. Sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S3. Overexpression and knockdown efficiencies were validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blotting.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates at 3000 cells/well (five replicates/group), with peripheral wells containing 100 μL PBS to minimize evaporation. Following 4-h adhesion, culture medium was replaced with 100 μL CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO, TX, USA) at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96-h time points. After 2-h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland).

Colony formation experiment

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 400 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C. Culture medium was replenished every 48 h. After 10–14 days, when colonies were visible by naked eyes, they (> 50 cells) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; 15 min), and quantified by imaging analysis.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Biofil, Guangzhou, China). For migration assays, chambers remained uncoated. For invasion assays, Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) was diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and coated (80 μL/well) in upper chambers. Complete medium with 20% FBS (750 μL/well) served as chemoattractant in lower chambers. Cells (2 × 104/well) in 5% FBS medium were seeded in upper chambers. Following 24 h–48 h incubation at 37 °C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; 12 min), and nonmigrated cells removed by cotton swab. Migrated/invaded cells were imaged (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA) software.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis and cell cycle detection

Cells were washed twice with PBS and processed for flow cytometry. Apoptosis was assessed by dual staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) (both Elabscience, Wuhan, China), incubating samples at room temperature for 20 min in the dark before analysis. For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 70% ethanol, followed by PI staining (30 min, 4 °C, dark). Data were acquired using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

Nude mice xenograft model

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2023-0398). Ten 4-week-old female Balb/c nude mice from Charles River (Beijing, China) were acclimatized for 1 week in individually ventilated cages (IVC) at the institutional animal facility. ES-2/SKOV3 cells stably overexpressing ZNF662 or vector controls (4 × 106 cells in 100 µl PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla (n = 5 per group). Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days using calipers, with volume calculated as 0.5 × length × width2. Body weight, activity, feeding behavior, and tumor growth were monitored daily. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 1.5 cm in diameter or upon signs of cachexia. Excised tumors were photographed, weighed, and fixed in 4% formalin for paraffin embedding and sectioning.

To establish a peritoneal metastasis model, 6-week-old female nude Balb/c mice (n = 6 per group) were intraperitoneally inoculated with 5 × 106 ES-2 ZNF662 overexpressing and vector control ovarian cancer cells suspended in 150 μL of PBS. At 3 weeks postinoculation, the mice were euthanized, and peritoneal metastases were assessed.

Luciferase activity assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed to validate regulatory interactions. For miRNA targeting analysis, wild-type or mutant ZNF662-3’UTR sequences (containing predicted hsa-miR-429 binding sites) were cloned into the pmirGLO vector (GeneCreate Biotech, Wuhan, China). 293 T cells were cotransfected with these constructs and hsa-miR-429 mimics using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). For promoter activity assessment, the NUPR1 promoter region was cloned into pGL3-Basic (Genepharma, Shanghai, China) and cotransfected with pRL-TK normalization vector (Promega, WI, USA) into ovarian cancer cells. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), with Firefly luciferase activity normalized to Renilla.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from ZNF662-overexpressing and control ES-2 cells (three biological replicates per condition) using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Shiga, Japan). Transcriptome sequencing was performed by APExBIO (Shanghai, China), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined by |log2(fold change)|> 1 and p < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on significantly differentially expressed genes.

ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) was performed using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 6 × 107 SKOV3 and ES-2 cells stably expressing ZNF662 were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction was quenched with 0.125 M glycine. Chromatin was enzymatically digested with micrococcal nuclease at 37 °C for 20 min to generate fragments of 150–900 bp. A 10 μL sample was reserved as an input control. For immunoprecipitation, chromatin was incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies: anti-FLAG M2, anti-IgG (negative control), and antihistone H3 (positive control) (catalog numbers in Supplementary Table S2). Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing, the complexes were washout and crosslinks were reversed by incubation with NaCl and Proteinase K overnight at 65 °C. DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR using a Bio-Rad system (California, USA). Amplification products were validated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data from three independent experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Student’s t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied as appropriate, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (two-sided). Significance levels were denoted as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001.

Results

ZNF662 is expressed at low levels in EOC and is associated with poor overall survival

Analysis of TCGA pan-cancer data via GEPIA2 revealed lower ZNF662 expression in most malignancies compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1A), suggesting a tumor-suppressive role across cancers. This pattern was conserved in ovarian cancer (Fig. 1B). Validation using GEO datasets (GSE18520, GSE27651, and GSE29540) confirmed reduced ZNF662 expression in EOC tissues versus normal Fallopian tube (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). Kaplan–Meier Plotter analysis demonstrated that high ZNF662 expression associates with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) (HR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.07–1.55, P = 0.0082) but prolonged overall survival (OS) (HR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–0.99, P = 0.025) (Fig. 1C–D).

Analysis of the CSIOVDB ovarian cancer cohort revealed reduced ZNF662 expression in malignant tumors compared with ovarian surface epithelium (P < 0.001) and ovarian stroma (P = 0.019) (Supplementary Fig. S1D). In serous carcinomas (the predominant epithelial ovarian cancer subtype), low-grade tumors exhibited higher ZNF662 expression than high-grade counterparts (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Platinum-sensitive recurrent cases demonstrated higher ZNF662 levels than platinum-resistant cases (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S1F). Survival analysis within the Epi-A molecular subtype showed reduced ZNF662 expression associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) (HR = 1.979, 95% CI 1.07–3.66; P = 0.0298) and OS (HR = 2.035, 95% CI 1.06–3.91; P = 0.0325) (Supplementary Fig. S1G–H).

Postoperative EOC tissues were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, with Fallopian tube and ovarian tissues from benign disease surgeries serving as normal controls. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analysis confirmed reduced ZNF662 expression in EOC tissues at both mRNA and protein levels compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1E–F). Immunohistochemical analysis of 62 EOC specimens and 20 normal controls revealed high ZNF662 staining intensity in 38.7% (24/62) of carcinomas versus 65.0% (13/20) of normal tissues (χ2 = 4.221, P = 0.039; 95% CI 1.02–5.87), with representative images shown in Fig. 1E.

ZNF662 expression levels were correlated with clinicopathological characteristics in patients with EOC (Table 1). Low ZNF662 expression associated with advanced FIGO stage (P = 0.026) and ascites presence (P = 0.029) but demonstrated no significant correlation with age (P = 0.058), pretreatment cancer antigen 125 (CA125) levels (P = 0.373), histologic subtype (P = 0.534), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.400), or tumor size (P = 0.306). These findings indicate that reduced ZNF662 expression correlates with aggressive clinicopathological features in EOC.

ZNF662 inhibits the proliferation of epithelial ovarian cancer cells

Basal ZNF662 mRNA expression was analyzed in four epithelial ovarian cancer cell lines (Fig. 2A). ZNF662 expression was highest in CAOV3 cells but negligible in SKOV3, ES-2, and OVCAR3 lines. Lentiviral-mediated ZNF662 overexpression was therefore established in SKOV3 and ES-2 cells, with stable transfectants selected via puromycin treatment and validated at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2B–E). Conversely, CAOV3 cells, which exhibit high endogenous ZNF662 expression, were transfected with three distinct ZNF662-targeting siRNAs. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR at 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Western blot analysis of the two most effective siRNAs (si-ZNF662#1 and #2) confirmed reduced ZNF662 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S2B). CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated reduced proliferative capacity in ZNF662-overexpressing cells compared with controls (Fig. 2F–G). Conversely, ZNF662 knockdown enhanced proliferation in CAOV3 (Supplementary Fig. S3C).

ZNF662 induces apoptosis and G0/G1 cell cycle arrest in epithelial ovarian cancer cells

Flow cytometry analysis revealed increased apoptosis in ZNF662-overexpressing SKOV3 and ES-2 cells compared with controls (Fig. 2H) whereas decreased ZNF662 expression had reverse effects in CAOV3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2D). To determine whether ZNF662-mediated proliferation inhibition involves cell cycle modulation, we assessed phase distribution. ZNF662 overexpression increased the proportion of cells in G0/G1 phase while decreasing those in S phase (Fig. 2I). In contrast, ZNF662 knockdown decreased the G0/G1 population and increased the S-phase population (Supplementary Fig. S2F). These results indicated that ZNF662 functioned as a tumor-suppressor by inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in EOC cells.

ZNF662 inhibits migration and invasion by reversing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in epithelial ovarian cancer cells

Results showed that ZNF662 overexpression inhibited wound healing in SKOV3 and ES-2 cells (Fig. 3A). At the same time, ZNF662 knockdown enhanced the migration of CAOV3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2G). Transwell assays demonstrated that ZNF662 overexpression significantly reduced cellular migration through uncoated membranes (Fig. 3B) and invasion through Matrigel-coated chambers (Fig. 3C) compared with controls. Conversely, knockdown of ZNF662 enhanced migration (Supplementary Fig. S2H) and invasion (Supplementary Fig. S2I) in CAOV3. Given EMT’s established role in metastatic progression, we examined EMT marker expression via western blotting. ZNF662-overexpressing cells exhibited downregulated mesenchymal markers (Vimentin, N-cadherin) and upregulated epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 3D-E). In CAOV3 cells, ZNF662 knockdown decreased E-cadherin expression and increased N-cadherin and Vimentin expression compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. S2D). These findings indicate that ZNF662 suppresses metastatic behavior by reversing EMT in ovarian cancer cells.

ZNF662 inhibits tumor growth and metastasis in vivo

To evaluate ZNF662’s effect on ovarian cancer proliferation in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenografts in BALB/c nude mice. SKOV3/ES-2 cells stably overexpressing ZNF662 or vector controls were inoculated into the axillary flank. Tumor growth was monitored until reaching 1.5 cm diameter, at which point mice were euthanized and tumors excised. ZNF662-overexpressing xenografts exhibited significantly slower growth kinetics, reduced tumor volume, and lower mass compared with controls (Figs. 4A–D and Supplementary Fig. S3A–D). Histopathological analysis of excised tumors revealed increased nuclear atypia and Ki67 proliferation index in controls versus ZNF662-overexpressing tumors by H&E and IHC. Transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining confirmed elevated apoptosis in ZNF662-overexpressing tumors (Figs. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S3E).

To investigate the role of ZNF662 in metastasis in vivo, ES-2 cells (vector control or ZNF662-overexpressing) were intraperitoneally injected into nude mice (n = 6 per group). In the control group, 5/6 mice developed peritoneal metastasis with a greater volume of ascites. In contrast, only 2/6 mice in the ZNF662-overexpressing group developed metastasis, and they exhibited a smaller volume of ascites (Fig. 4H–I). As shown in Fig. 4F, the peritoneal cavities and intestinal surfaces of control mice were extensively covered with metastatic nodules, whereas only two nodules were observed in the ZNF662-overexpressing group. Additionally, in mice with liver metastases, the ZNF662-overexpressing group had fewer and smaller liver lesions than the control group (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ZNF662 suppresses ovarian cancer growth and metastasis in vivo.

ZNF662 expression is negatively regulated by hsa-miR-429

Prior studies established ZNF662’s tumor-suppressive function and reduced expression in ovarian cancer. To identify upstream regulatory mechanisms, we focused on micro ribonucleic acids (miRNAs) given their role in post-transcriptional regulation of > 60% of human genes [19]. Bioinformatic screening using miRDB and TargetScan databases predicted 125 miRNAs potentially binding ZNF662 3’UTR (Supplementary Table 4). Intersection with 55 miRNAs upregulated in ovarian cancer (GSE47841 dataset; Supplementary Table 4) identified three candidates: hsa-miR-200b-3p, hsa-miR-200c-3p, and hsa-miR-429 (Fig. 5A).

Transfection of miRNA mimics (hsa-miR-200b-3p, hsa-miR-200c-3p, hsa-miR-429) into ZNF662-overexpressing SKOV3 and ES-2 cells revealed that only hsa-miR-429 downregulated ZNF662 mRNA expression in both lines (Fig. 5B). Subsequent transfection of hsa-miR-429 inhibitors into wild-type cells increased ZNF662 mRNA levels (Fig. 5C). CAOV3 cells were also transfected with hsa-miR-429 mimics and inhibitors. Mimic transfection reduced ZNF662 mRNA expression, whereas inhibitor transfection increased it (Fig. 5D–E). Bioinformatics prediction identified a binding site for hsa-miR-429 within the ZNF662 3’UTR (Fig. 5F). Luciferase reporter assays with wild-type versus mutant ZNF662 3’UTR constructs demonstrated that hsa-miR-429 overexpression reduced wild-type luciferase activity (p < 0.05) but not mutant activity (binding site disrupted), confirming direct binding of hsa-miR-429 and ZNF662 3’UTR (Fig. 5G). Analysis of hsa-miR-429 expression in the same clinical cohort revealed a moderate correlation with ZNF662 expression (r = 0.4559), which was not statistically significant (P = 0.12). This lack of significance may be attributed to the small sample size (n = 16).

The role of ZNF662 in ovarian cancer is regulated by hsa-miR-429

To determine whether hsa-miR-429 mediates the functional role of ZNF662 in ovarian cancer, rescue experiments were performed. SKOV3 and ES-2 cells were cotransfected with hsa-miR-429 mimics (or control mimics) and ZNF662-overexpressing lentivirus (or control vector). CCK-8 assays revealed that hsa-miR-429 mimics transfection increased proliferation rates in both cell lines, while ZNF662 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 5I-J). Similarly, Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrated enhanced migratory and invasive capacities following hsa-miR-429 mimics transfection, which were likewise attenuated by ZNF662 overexpression (Fig. 5K–L). These results confirm that hsa-miR-429 regulates ZNF662-dependent oncogenic phenotypes in ovarian cancer.

NUPR1 is a key downstream target gene of ZNF662

To delineate downstream mechanisms underlying ZNF662’s regulation of malignant phenotypes in ovarian cancer, we performed RNA-sequence on ZNF662-overexpressing and control ES-2 cells. Differential expression analysis identified 809 upregulated and 972 downregulated genes (|log2(fold change)|> 1, P < 0.05; Fig. 6A–B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis demonstrated significant pathway alterations, including PI3K-AKT signaling, TNF signaling, p53 signaling, cell cycle regulation, and apoptotic pathways (Fig. 6C).

Considering KRAB zinc finger proteins as transcriptional repressors, JASPAR—a public repository of transcription factor binding motifs—was employed to analyze the top 50 downregulated DEGs from our RNA-seq data (Fig. 6D, Supplementary Table 5). The three highest-scoring genes (NUPR1, TPM1, and KRT18) were validated by qRT-PCR in ZNF662-overexpressing SKOV3 and ES-2 cells. Nuclear protein 1 (NUPR1) exhibited the most significant mRNA downregulation in both lines (Fig. 6E), with concordant protein reduction confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 6F). Conversely, ZNF662 knockdown in CAOV3 cells increased NUPR1 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S4). Consequently, NUPR1 was prioritized as a ZNF662 downstream target. The results showed that, compared with the negative control IgG, the Flag antibody could enrich the binding site fragments predicted by Jaspar (Fig. I–J). Dual-luciferase assays then also confirmed direct binding of ZNF662 and NUPR1 promoter (Fig. 6H). TCGA-GTEx analysis revealed decreased NUPR1 expression in ovarian carcinomas versus normal epithelium (Supplementary Fig. S4B), while Kaplan–Meier analysis associated high NUPR1 expression with poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Furthermore, we analyzed NUPR1 mRNA levels in same tissues mentioned before. The results showed that high expression of ZNF662 was accompanied by low expression of NUPR1 (r = −0.5676, P = 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Representative immunohistochemical images of ZNF662 and NUPR1 expression in EOC tissues are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4E.

NUPR1 can reverse the tumor-suppressive effect of ZNF662 on ovarian cancer cells

To determine whether ZNF662’s tumor-suppressive function depends on NUPR1, we infected ZNF662-overexpressing SKOV3 and ES-2 cells with NUPR1-overexpressing lentivirus. The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that NUPR1 reversed ZNF662-mediated inhibition of ovarian cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 7A). Transwell assays revealed that NUPR1 partly restored migratory and invasive capacities suppressed by ZNF662 (Fig. 7B–C). Flow cytometry confirmed NUPR1 counteracted ZNF662-induced apoptosis (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, NUPR1 reversed ZNF662-imposed cell cycle arrest in ES-2 cells but showed no significant effect on arrest in SKOV3 cells (Fig. 7E). It should be noted that in SKOV3 cells, NUPR1 overexpression also decreased the G0/G1 population and increased the S-phase population, and while it appeared to reverse ZNF662-induced arrest, although the effect was not statistically significant. This may be owing to lower NUPR1 overexpression efficiency in SKOV3 cells compared with ES-2 cells (Supplementary Fig. S5), and the experiment was only performed in triplicate; additional replicates might yield significant results. Finally, NUPR1 knockdown in CAOV3 cells reversed the effects of ZNF662 knockdown on proliferation, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression (Supplementary Fig. S4F–H). These results indicated that NUPR1 can reverse the tumor-suppressive effect of ZNF662 on ovarian cancer cells.

ZNF662 activates p53 signaling through NUPR1 inhibition

Previous studies reported NUPR1’s role in regulating p53 signaling [20, 21]. Consistent with this, KEGG pathway analysis of transcriptome sequencing data in our study demonstrated significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the p53 pathway following ZNF662 overexpression. Functional assays indicated NUPR1 contributes to cell cycle arrest rescue and apoptosis promotion. We therefore hypothesized ZNF662 activates p53 signaling by suppressing NUPR1 transcription. First, we confirmed the presence of p53 in these cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S6). Western blot analysis revealed ZNF662 overexpression elevated p53, p21, and BAX expression while reducing BCL-2 and cyclin D1 levels in SKOV3 (Fig. 8A) and ES-2 cells (Fig. 8B). At the same time, the opposite trend was observed in CAOV3 in which ZNF662 expression was inhibited (Fig. 8C). Rescue experiments using NUPR1-overexpressing lentivirus reversed ZNF662-mediated effects on all proteins except cyclin D1 in SKOV3 (Fig. 8D–E). Collectively, these results above demonstrate that in epithelial ovarian cancer, ZNF662 is inhibited by hsa-miR-429. ZNF662 binds the NUPR1 promoter to repress transcription, thereby activating p53 signaling and inhibiting malignant phenotypes (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Studies demonstrate that KRAB-ZNF proteins contribute to tumor pathogenesis [22]. ZNF662, a recently identified KRAB-ZNF family member, has been implicated in poor prognosis in select malignancies. However, its role in ovarian cancer remains uncharacterized. Here, we report that ZNF662 is downregulated in epithelial ovarian cancer tissues, and its low expression correlates with advanced clinical stage, ascites presence, and reduced OS. These findings suggest ZNF662 may serve as a prognostic biomarker for EOC. Notably, Kaplan–Meier Plotter analysis revealed a paradoxical association between high ZNF662 expression and survival outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer, indicating a correlation with both shorter PFS and prolonged OS. PFS reflects the effectiveness of treatment in controlling tumors at an earlier stage and can be influenced by different treatment modalities. While OS, which is considered a robust end point as it is unaffected by subsequent treatments, represents the total duration of survival. However, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis did not account for potential confounding variables, suggesting that PFS may be influenced by treatment-related factors. In the CSIOVDB cohort, we observed that patients with high ZNF662 expression had longer PFS and OS. This discrepancy may be attributable to the heterogeneity of ovarian cancer. Owing to the relatively small sample size and short follow-up period in this study (the longest follow-up was less than 3 years), future work will involve expanding the cohort and performing multivariate analysis to control for confounding factors.

To investigate ZNF662 functional impact, we established ZNF662-overexpressing and knockdown EOC cell lines and performed comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assays. We demonstrate that ZNF662 suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion; induces G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, and promotes apoptosis. Collectively, these results indicate ZNF662 functions as a tumor suppressor in ovarian cancer.

MicroRNAs are endogenous 18–22 nucleotide noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression in eukaryotes [23]. In ovarian cancer, approximately 40% of miRNAs exhibit altered DNA copy numbers, while aberrant expression of miRNA-processing enzymes Dicer and Drosha correlates with patient prognosis, implicating miRNA dysregulation in disease progression [24, 25]. Our luciferase reporter assays demonstrate that hsa-miR-429 directly targets ZNF662 by binding its 3’UTR. This aligns with established oncogenic roles: miR-429 promotes proliferation and metastasis in EOC cells, shows elevated serum levels in patients that correlate with disease stage and CA125 levels, and associates with poorer overall survival [26]. Guan et al. further reported elevated miR-429 expression in OVCAR3 and A2780 cell lines versus nononcogenic T80 cells, enhancing proliferation and subcutaneous tumor formation in nude mice [27]. Additionally, SKOV3-derived exosomal miR-429 increases drug resistance in recipient A2780 cells [28]. Collectively, these findings establish miR-429 as an oncomiR that directly suppresses the tumor suppressor ZNF662 in ovarian cancer.

ZNF662 was investigated further for its regulatory functions in ovarian cancer. Transcriptome sequencing combined with JASPAR database analysis, identified NUPR1 as a direct downstream target. The NUPR1 gene (also designated p8 or com1) resides at chromosome 16p11.2, a locus frequently amplified in breast cancer [29, 30]. NUPR1 drives progression in multiple malignancies and functions as an established oncogene in ovarian cancer [31]. Its knockdown suppresses ovarian cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting PI3K–Akt pathway activation [31].

Over 50% of human tumors have p53 mutations, reflecting intolerance to functional p53 during tumorigenesis [32]. However, there are still some tumors whose p53 is wild-type, but in low or no activity, making p53 reactivation a promising therapeutic strategy [33]. Transcriptome sequencing revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the p53 pathway following ZNF662 overexpression. Given established NUPR1–p53 pathway interactions, we hypothesized that ZNF662 regulates p53 signaling through NUPR1 targeting [20, 21]. Functional validation confirmed ZNF662-mediated p53 activation, establishing a ZNF662–NUPR1–p53 signaling axis. This mirrors KRAB-ZNF family patterns: ZNF420 modulates p53 acetylation and downstream targets (PUMA, NOXA, and BAX) in colon cancer, while ZNF545 activates p53 in multiple myeloma [34, 35]. Our findings demonstrate ZNF662-mediated p53 pathway upregulation, providing a molecular foundation for targeted ovarian cancer therapies.

Despite these insights, several limitations must be noted. First, the modest cohort size and limited follow-up period (maximum less than 3 years) preclude analysis of ZNF662 expression–prognosis correlations in our dataset. Future studies will expand the cohort and extend surveillance. Second, conditional knockout murine models would provide stronger mechanistic evidence for ZNF662’s role in EOC. Third, while we identified the hsa-miR-429/ZNF662/p53 axis, additional ZNF662-regulated pathways likely contribute to EOC tumorigenesis beyond our present data.

Conclusions

This study establishes that ZNF662 may function as a tumor suppressor in ovarian cancer. ZNF662 inhibits proliferation, migration, invasion, and cell cycle progression while promoting apoptosis through NUPR1/p53 signaling. Furthermore, hsa-miR-429 post-transcriptionally regulates ZNF662 expression. These findings may provide a molecular foundation for developing ZNF662-targeted therapies in ovarian cancer precision medicine.
