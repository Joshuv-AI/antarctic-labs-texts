An Ultra-Fast MLE for Low SNR Multi-Reference Alignment
Shay Kreymer, Amnon Balanov, Tamir Bendory

Abstract
Motivated by single-particle cryo-electron microscopy, multi-reference alignment (MRA) models the task of recovering an unknown signal from multiple noisy observations corrupted by random rotations. The standard approach, expectation-maximization (EM), often becomes computationally prohibitive, particularly in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) settings. We introduce an alternative, ultra-fast algorithm for MRA over the special orthogonal group SO ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SO}(2) . By performing a Taylor expansion of the log-likelihood in the low-SNR regime, we estimate the signal by sequentially computing data-driven averages of observations. Our method requires only one pass over the data, dramatically reducing computational cost compared to EM. Numerical experiments show that the proposed approach achieves high accuracy in low-SNR environments and provides an excellent initialization for subsequent EM refinement.

II
MLE algorithm for low SNR MRA

In this section, we present the mathematical formulation and derivation of our fast MLE algorithm. We begin by defining the MRA model over
\mathsf{SO}(2)
and the likelihood function. We then derive an approximation of the likelihood using a first-order Taylor expansion, justified by the low-SNR assumption. Finally, based on this approximation, we introduce a frequency-marching algorithm, which recovers the Fourier coefficients sequentially in a closed form.

II-A

Statistical model

We consider a real bandlimited signal on the unit circle:

f(t)=\sum_{k=-L}^{L}\hat{f}[k]e^{ikt},\quad t\in[0,2\pi),

(1)

where
L
is the bandlimit of the signal. Since
f
is real, its Fourier transform is conjugate symmetric, i.e.,
\hat{f}[-k]=\overline{\hat{f}[k]}
. For an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, the rotation operator
R_{\theta}\in\mathsf{SO}(2)
is defined by

(R_{\theta}f)(t)=f(t-\theta)=\sum_{k=-L}^{L}\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}e^{ikt}.

(2)

We consider a set of
n
observations,

y_{j}=R_{\theta_{j}}f+\varepsilon_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots n,

(3)

where
\varepsilon_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
, and
\theta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}\left[0,2\pi\right)
. The assumption that the distribution over the angles is uniform is unnecessary, and we take it only for simplicity. In the Fourier domain, the observations satisfy

\hat{y}_{j}[k]=\hat{f}[k]\,e^{-ik\theta_{j}}+\hat{\varepsilon}_{j}[k],

(4)

where
\hat{\varepsilon}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
. We note that the signal can be estimated only up to a global rotation.

II-B

Preliminary estimates

The algorithm begins with the estimation of the zero-frequency component
\hat{f}[0]
(requires
n=\omega(\sigma^{2})
observations)

\widehat{\hat{f}[0]}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{y}_{j}[0],

(5)

and the Fourier magnitudes
|\hat{f}[k]|

(requires
n=\omega(\sigma^{4})
observations)

\widehat{|\hat{f}[k]|}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\hat{y}_{j}[k]|^{2}-\sigma^{2}\right)}.

(6)

Using these estimates, and due to the conjugate symmetry, it is enough to consider frequencies
k=1,\ldots,L.

II-C

Conditional likelihood

To break the inherent rotational symmetry of the MRA problem, we arbitrarily fix the phase of the
k=1
coefficient by setting
\hat{f}[1]=\widehat{|\hat{f}[1]|}
. With this anchor established, we aim to maximize the log-likelihood of the
k
-th Fourier coefficient phase, conditioned on the estimates of the other frequencies
\{k^{\prime}\in[0,L]:k^{\prime}\neq k\}
:

\begin{split}\ell&(\hat{f}[k];\{\hat{f}[k^{\prime}]\}_{k^{\prime}=0,k^{\prime}\neq k}^{L}]):=\ell(\hat{f}[k])\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{L/2}}e^{\frac{-1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{L}\left|\hat{y}_{j}[m]-\hat{f}[m]e^{-im\theta}\right|^{2}}d\theta.\end{split}

(7)

To simplify the expression, we expand the least squares term in the exponent:

\begin{split}&-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{L}\left|\hat{y}_{j}[m]-\hat{f}[m]e^{-im\theta}\right|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\hat{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\hat{f}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k^{\prime}]\hat{f}[k^{\prime}]e^{-ik^{\prime}\theta}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta},\end{split}

(8)

and rewrite

\begin{split}&\ell(\hat{f}[k])=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{L/2}}e^{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}\\
&e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\hat{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\hat{f}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k^{\prime}]\hat{f}[k^{\prime}]e^{-ik^{\prime}\theta}}d\theta\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log C_{1}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}e^{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}d\theta,\end{split}

(9)

where

C_{j,k}(\theta):=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k^{\prime}]\hat{f}[k^{\prime}]e^{-ik^{\prime}\theta},

(10)

and
C_{1}
is a constant.
The first exponent admits the second-order Taylor expansion in powers of
\|\hat{f}\|/\sigma
:

e^{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}=1+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}{\sigma^{2}}+R_{2}\left(\frac{\hat{f}[k]}{\sigma}\right),

where
R_{2}\left(\cdot\right)

is the Lagrange remainder.
Thus, we can proceed to write the log-likelihood as

\begin{split}\ell(\hat{f}[k])&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log C_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}\left(1+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}{\sigma^{2}}\right.\\
&\left.+R_{2}\left(\frac{\hat{f}[k]}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right)d\theta\\
&\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log C_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}\left(1+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)d\theta\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log C_{1}\left(\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]\right),\end{split}

(11)

where we defined the Fourier transform of
e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}
as:

\hat{C}_{j,k}[m]:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}e^{-im\theta}d\theta.

(12)

We note that
\hat{C}_{j,k}[m]
can be computed efficiently with FFT.

Lemma II.1
.

For
\sigma\to\infty
, the term inside the
\log
in (
11
) is strictly positive, i.e.,
\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\sigma^{2}}>0
.

Proof.

For sufficiently large
\sigma
,
\hat{y}_{j}[k]
scales as
\sigma
and thus
|\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]|<\sigma^{2}
.
Thus,
1+\frac{\Re\left(\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]e^{-ik\theta}\right)}{\sigma^{2}}
is strictly positive. The argument follows since
e^{C_{j,k}(\theta)}
is also strictly positive, and an integral over a strictly positive function is strictly positive.
∎

Next, we use the fact that
\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]\gg\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]
for
\sigma\to\infty
, and further expand the log-likelihood using the second-order Taylor expansion

\log\left(\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=\log\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\sigma^{2}\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]}+\tilde{R_{2}}\left(\frac{\hat{f}[k]}{\sigma}\right).

Therefore,

\begin{split}\ell(\hat{f}[k])&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\biggl(\log\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]+\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{f}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\sigma^{2}\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]}\\
&+\tilde{R_{2}}\left(\frac{\hat{f}[k]}{\sigma}\right)+\log C_{1}\biggr).\end{split}

(13)

The term
\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]
does not depend on the coefficient
\hat{f}[k]
. Thus, denoting
\hat{f}[k]:=|\hat{f}[k]|\phi[k]
, with
|\phi[k]|=1
, the maximization of the log-likelihood (
13
) is equivalent (up to high-order terms in the Taylor expansion) to maximizing

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Re\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]|\hat{f}[k]|\phi[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]}=|\hat{f}[k]|\Re\phi[k]\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{\hat{y}}_{j}[k]\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}{\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]}.

(14)

The optimal phase is explicitly given by:

\phi^{*}[k]=\frac{Z}{|Z|},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{where }Z=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\hat{y}_{j}[k]\overline{\hat{C}_{j,k}[k]}}{\hat{C}_{j,k}[0]}.

(15)

This closed-form solution provides the optimal phase for frequency
k
, conditioned on the current estimates of the remaining frequencies.

II-E

Expectation-Maximization (EM)

EM is the current leading approach for computing the MLE for the MRA problem, particularly in applications like cryo-EM
[
20
,
19
]
. We introduce it here for comparison to our fast MLE algorithm, as it serves as the state-of-the-art benchmark for this task.
The MLE problem is formulated by maximizing the likelihood function over the group of rotations
\mathsf{SO}(2)
. The marginalization over the latent variable—the unknown rotation angles—is done by discretizing the domain of possible rotation angles
[0,2\pi)
into a uniform grid of
R
points,
\Theta_{R}=\{\theta_{r}=\frac{2\pi r}{R}\}_{r=0}^{R-1}
. Let
\hat{f}^{(t)}
denote the estimate of the signal at iteration
t
. The algorithm alternates between two steps: the expectation step (E-step) and the maximization step (M-step). In the E-step, we estimate the posterior probability distribution of the rotations. For the
j
-th observation and grid point
\theta_{r}
, the weight is calculated as:

w_{j}^{(t)}(\theta_{r})=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|\hat{y}_{j}-\sum_{k=0}^{L}\hat{f}^{(t)}[k]e^{-ik\theta_{r}}\right\|^{2}}}{\sum_{l=0}^{R-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|\hat{y}_{j}-\sum_{k=0}^{L}\hat{f}^{(t)}[k]e^{-ik\theta_{l}}\right\|^{2}}},

(16)

such that
\sum_{r}w_{j}^{(t)}(\theta_{r})=1
. In the M-step, we update the signal estimate by maximizing the expected log-likelihood. This results in a weighted average of the aligned observations:

\hat{f}^{(t+1)}[k]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{y}_{j}[k]\left(\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}w_{j}^{(t)}(\theta_{r})e^{ik\theta_{r}}\right).

(17)

III
Computational Complexity

Let
n
be the number of measurements and
L
be the bandlimit. To account for the different discretization requirements, we define
R_{\text{EM}}
- the search grid size for the EM algorithm, and
R_{\text{MLE}}
- the integration grid size for the proposed fast MLE algorithm.
Our fast MLE algorithm estimates the coefficient with a total complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot L^{2}\cdot R_{\text{MLE}})
.
The magnitudes estimation involves averaging over the set of measurements for each frequency, for a total of
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot L)
operations. The algorithm then proceeds to estimate the coefficients sequentially. For each frequency
k\in[2,L]
, we sum the contributions of the
k
previously estimated frequencies, involving an operation of size
(n\cdot k\cdot R_{\text{MLE}})
. Integration involves an FFT over the integration grid
R_{\text{MLE}}
. The dominant cost is the cumulative summation
\sum_{k=2}^{L}(n\cdot k\cdot R_{\text{MLE}})\approx\frac{1}{2}nL^{2}R_{\text{MLE}}
.
The standard EM algorithm performs iterative refinement. The E-Step calculates weights by (
16
) for
n
observations over
R_{\text{EM}}
rotations with cost
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot L\cdot R_{\text{EM}})
. The M-Step updates the estimate by averaging the aligned observations with cost:
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot L\cdot R_{\text{EM}})
. The total complexity for
T_{\text{EM}}
iterations is
\mathcal{O}(T_{\text{EM}}\cdot n\cdot L\cdot R_{\text{EM}})
.

A comparison of the computational complexities reveals a trade-off: our fast MLE algorithm scales quadratically with the bandlimit, whereas EM scales linearly per iteration. However, the critical differentiator is the number of iterations,
T_{\text{EM}}
. While our algorithm is a single-pass, closed-form solution with a fixed runtime independent of the noise level, standard EM requires iterative convergence. Crucially,
T_{\text{EM}}
is not constant; it grows very rapidly as the SNR decreases, as can be seen in our numerical experiments in Section
IV
, and in
[
16
,
2
]
, for instance. In the low-SNR regime, where
T_{\text{EM}}\gg L
, our non-iterative approach offers a substantial computational advantage.

IV
Numerical Experiments

We evaluate the performance of the proposed fast MLE algorithm against the standard EM algorithm, initialized from a random initialization point or initialized from the estimation of our algorithm.

L=5

[0,2\pi)

\sigma^{2}

R_{\text{EM}}=1000
points, and set a convergence tolerance of
10^{-6}
. For the proposed fast MLE algorithm, we used an integration grid of
R_{\text{MLE}}=500
points. All computations were performed on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M3 Pro chip and 36 GB of RAM. The code to reproduce all numerical experiments is publicly available

V
A roadmap to fast maximum-likelihood estimation for cryo-EM

Our goal is to extend the principles developed in this paper to the full cryo-EM model, which involves 3-D rotations (elements of the group
\mathsf{SO}(3)
) and tomographic projections
[
6
]
.
In brief, the key idea is to formulate the likelihood in terms of orthogonal matrices (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
), which play the
\mathsf{SO}(3)
analog of the Fourier phases in the
\mathsf{SO}(2)
setting.
Using similar Taylor expansions, the resulting MLE reduces to a Procrustes problem, whose solution is obtained via an SVD: mirroring the phase-recovery step used in (
15
).
We leave a complete derivation and an efficient implementation to future work.
If realized, this approach could provide an extremely fast
ab initio
estimator, yielding a robust initialization for standard cryo-EM pipelines.
