Postoperative complications of breast risk-reduction surgery in very high risk breast cancer patients: A series of 253 cases
Authors: Juliette Fraisse, Charline Berthier, Charlotte Vaysse, Gabrielle Selmes, Eva Jouve, Marc Soule-Tholy, Thomas Meresse, Dimitri Gangloff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study assesses surgical practices and complications related to risk-reducing mastectomy in patients at very high risk for breast cancer.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted among women over 18 years of age who had undergone risk-reducing mastectomy from January 2015 to April 2022 at the Toulouse-Oncopole University Cancer Institute.ResultsFor the 253 patients having undergone risk-reducing mastectomy between 2015 and 2022, mean age was 43 years, with a majority of patients carrying BRCA1 (48.6%) or BRCA2 mutations (38.3%). Three-quarters of patients (67.6%) underwent prosthetic reconstruction, 17% free-flap reconstruction, and 7% latissimus dorsi flap reconstruction. At least one postoperative complication was experienced by 43% of patients, mostly wound healing disorders (34.9%). A complication requiring no further intervention was recorded in 32% of patients. A further 11.1% required reoperation. Implant loss occurred in 3.5% of patients undergoing prosthetic reconstruction. The rate of free flap loss was 5.9%.Discussion and conclusionThe overall complication rate of 43% aligns with findings from other studies on risk-reducing mastectomy, with notable variations between prosthetic and autologous reconstructions. This study highlights the high complication rates associated with risk-reducing mastectomies, especially in patients undergoing free-flap reconstruction. Despite these challenges, the rates of reconstruction failure and severe complications remain relatively low. Understanding patient-specific risk factors and surgical modalities can help in counseling patients and managing their expectations about potential postoperative issues.Keywords: Breast cancer, BRCA, Risk-reducing mastectomy, Breast reconstruction, Complications, Free flaps, Breast implants

Background

Breast cancer is currently the most common and deadliest cancer in women, with 61,000 new cases in 2023 in France, and a five-year survival rate of 88%. It is estimated that 5–10% of these cancers are of genetic origin, with around 120,000 women genetically predisposed to breast cancer in France.1

Currently, women at very high risk for breast cancer are categorized based on confirmed genetic predisposition through diagnosed mutation or suspicion of same based on family history. The main mutations currently known are BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2. French guidelines allow affected patients to choose between a close surveillance strategy or risk-reducing surgery, generally involving bilateral mastectomy.2

Risk-reducing mastectomy is currently the most effective strategy for limiting the risk for breast cancer in patients genetically predisposed, with an estimated risk reduction of 90–95%, resulting in an annual residual risk of <1%.3

A case series of patients undergoing surgery at the Toulouse-Oncopole University Cancer Institute (IUCT-O) between 2015 and 2022 is presented. The aim was to assess practices for risk-reducing breast surgery in patients at very high risk for breast cancer, focusing specifically on surgical techniques and associated complications.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective, descriptive, single-center study was carried out at the Toulouse-Oncopole University Cancer Institute (IUCT-O) including all female patients over 18 years of age, considered at very high risk for breast cancer following a dedicated oncogenetic consultation, who underwent risk-reducing breast surgery performed by the IUCT-O's breast surgery team, between January 2015 and April 2022.

The study objectives were to evaluate the surgical techniques used and the postoperative complications observed.

Data collection

Patients who were likely to meet our inclusion criteria were recruited through the local genetic cancer surveillance network (GENEPY), supplemented by specific coding data for risk-reducing breast surgery from the Medical Information Department (MID). Data were then collected from patients’ medical records.

Statistical analysis

Population characteristics were presented via descriptive statistics. Quantitative variables were described by median, minimum, and maximum values, as well as by the number of missing data points. Qualitative variables were described by frequency, percentages, and the number of missing data for each category. Given the retrospective nature of data collection, some data were missing; these cases were excluded from statistical analyses where necessary.

Surgical team and techniques

The breast unit of our institution comprises four plastic surgeons and eight breast/gynecological oncology surgeons. Risk-reducing breast surgery is preferentially performed by the plastic surgery team, who operated on 65% of patients in this series. Microsurgical free-flap procedures were exclusively performed by plastic surgeons.

Risk-reducing mastectomy was performed based on the technical feasibility assessed by the surgeon and in accordance with the patient’s preference, and could involve simple mastectomy (removal of the skin envelope and the nipple-areola complex [NAC]), skin-sparing mastectomy (preservation of the skin envelope with removal of the NAC), or nipple-sparing mastectomy (preservation of both the skin envelope and the NAC).

When performed, breast reconstruction consisted of implant-based reconstruction (using a complete retropectoral pocket combined with a serratus anterior fascia flap), a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap, free deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap4 or free gracilis flap5 (the latter two having been grouped as “free flaps” in some analyses).

Complications rates and severity

Among the 253 patients included, 109 patients (43%) experienced at least one postoperative complication, while 144 patients (56.9%) had no complications.

Complications were classified according to their severity, corresponding to Clavien-Dindo grading (Table 1): 79 patients (31.2%) had a Grade I complication; 2 patients (0.8%) had Grade II complications; 28 patients (11.1%) experienced Grade IIIb complication. No grade IIIa, IV or V complications occurred in this series.

Complication rates varied according to mastectomy type, ranging from 9.1% after simple mastectomy to 41.7% for skin-sparing mastectomy, and 46.9% after nipple-sparing mastectomy (Table 5).

No notable difference in complication rate was observed between patients with a history of radiotherapy (42.3%) and those without (43.2%). However, for patients with at least one complication and a history of unilateral radiotherapy, the former was localized on the side of the radiotherapy in 68.8% of patients, on the contralateral breast in 15.6%, and bilaterally in 15.6%.

Prosthetic reconstruction

Among the 171 patients who underwent prosthetic reconstruction, the overall complication rate was 39.2%, most often a complication requiring no further intervention (29.8% grade I and 1.2% grade II). Reoperation without change of reconstruction was required in 4.7% of patients, while complications led to conversion of reconstruction in 1.2% and to reconstruction failure in 2.3% of patients (Table 6). Overall, prosthesis loss occurred in 6 patients (3.5%).

Free flaps

Concerning free flaps, 24 patients underwent a double DIEP flap after bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy, 14 patients underwent a DIEP flap following unilateral risk-reducing mastectomy (among whom 12 had a double DIEP flap performed either as part of secondary breast reconstruction or after curative mastectomy on the contralateral side), 2 patients underwent a double gracilis flap after bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy, and 3 patients underwent a gracilis flap after unilateral risk-reducing mastectomy (among whom 2 had a double gracilis flap performed under the same circumstances). In total, 62 DIEP flaps and 7 gracilis flaps were planned for immediate breast reconstruction following risk-reducing mastectomy, for a total of 69 free flaps planned.

Among these 69 planned free flaps, 2 (2.9%) were converted to another reconstructive technique intraoperatively, owing to the impossibility of harvesting or revascularizing a viable flap; these patients did not require any further surgical intervention. This left 67 free flaps that were actually transferred.

Among these 67 flaps, we observed 4 total flap losses (5.9%) requiring secondary surgical removal of the flap.

When intraoperative conversions (2,9%) and partial flap losses (4.5%) are also taken into account the overall rate of alteration of the reconstruction plan reaches 13%.

Discussion

Complication rate compared with the literature

The complication rate in our population is relatively high, with 43% of patients experiencing at least one complication. These observations are consistent with the literature on risk-reducing mastectomies, where complication rates range from 20% to 55%.7,8 However, the study population included all patients treated by risk-reducing mastectomy at our center, resulting in a very heterogeneous cohort in terms of age, previous breast treatments, clinical characteristics, and surgical techniques used. Comparing our results with more specific studies is thus difficult.

Among observed complications, the majority were grade I complications of Clavien-Dindo classification (31.2%). Only 3.2% of complications led to reconstruction-type conversion, and 2.4% to reconstruction failure. These figures concur with the literature, with reconstruction failure rates close to 5% regardless of surgical technique.9,10

Close attention was paid to documenting all complications, including those classified as grade I, to provide complete information to patients. For example, a delayed wound healing requiring an additional week of local care may seem negligible to healthcare professionals accustomed to such situations, but not, we believe, to individual patients.

Complications by type of mastectomy

Consistent with the literature,7,8,11 we observed higher complication rates for mastectomies with NAC preservation (47%) compared to skin-sparing mastectomies (41.7%) and those with no skin preservation (13.6%).

Complications in prosthetic reconstructions

Prosthetic reconstruction accounted for the majority of reconstructions performed in this series (67.6% of patients) and was associated with an overall complication rate of 39.2%.

Most of these complications were grade I (29.8%) or grade II (1.2%) of Clavien-Dindo classification.

Implant loss (including conversions and reconstruction failures) occurred in 3.5% of patients. This relatively low rate compares to the literature, with rates ranging from 3.6% to 15%.8,12, 13, 14

Aside from a given surgeon's experience, this low complication rate may be linked to the use of complete retropectoral prosthetic pockets combined with a serratus fascia flap, corresponding to the vast majority of implant-based reconstructions in this series. This technique makes it possible to avoid using dermal matrices, as well as providing full coverage, even in cases of skin insufficiency.

Complications in free-flap reconstructions

In free-flap autologous reconstructions, a high rate of flap failure was observed (5.9%) compared with rates typically reported for DIEP and gracilis flap reconstruction, generally cited around 1–3% and up to 5%.15, 16, 17, 18, 19

Several factors specific to our center and to the study period may help explain this higher-than-expected failure rate. Our institution is a comprehensive cancer center with a wide and varied surgical activity, encompassing cutaneous oncologic surgery, curative breast cancer surgery, sarcoma surgery, and reconstructive surgery performed in partnership with the center’s other surgical teams. Our team has substantial experience in free flaps and microsurgery; however, the study period corresponds to the early phase of our team’s experience with bilateral DIEP flap reconstruction. This learning curve may have contributed to technical errors or suboptimal patient selection during part of the study period.

These results tend to confirm that this activity is complex and must be carried out by trained teams of plastic surgeons.

We also sought to evaluate the rate of changes to the initial reconstructive plan, including both reconstructive failures and reconstruction conversions. This rate was particularly high in the free-flap reconstruction group (13%).

Although these situations do not all correspond to free-flap failure as defined in the literature, they are often perceived as a failure by patients. Therefore, this possibility should be discussed during preoperative counseling.

Risk factors for complications

Increased complications were observed among smokers, obese or underweight patients. These are well established risk factors in plastic surgery and breast reconstruction.20,21

Depending on the situation, smoking cessation and weight management can be discussed with the patient before surgery, emphasizing their potential benefits. These risk factors may also influence the choice of surgical techniques, particularly the type of reconstruction.

Conclusion

Risk-reducing breast surgery is currently the most effective strategy to reduce the risk of cancer for women at very high risk for breast cancer. This mutilating surgery is often performed in young and healthy patients with high expectations regarding esthetic outcome. As such, it requires specialized care and proper management.

This study describes our practices in terms of surgical techniques and specific complications. Our complication rate was relatively high: 43% of patients had at least one complication, with most complications requiring no further intervention (32%). We noted a low proportion of implant losses (3.5%) in contrast to a high free-flap failure (5.9%). Due to progress in oncogenetics and surgical techniques, risk-reducing surgeries are likely to become more widespread. Understanding and managing care pathways for these patients is more than ever essential, particularly in providing objective and up-to-date preoperative information.
