Effects of neuroinflammation and neurotrophic factors on mental health of people with axial spondyloarthritis
Authors: Markéta Hušáková, Karel Pavelka, Karolína Kuchaříková, Jan Kotrs, Veronika Balajková, Markéta Kuklová, Patrik Škubica, Hana Hulejová, Ladislav Šenolt, Pavel Mohr, Marta Olejárová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMood and anxiety disturbances can aggravate axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA). This study investigated the relationship between depression, anxiety, disease activity, systemic inflammation, and neurotrophins, specifically brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its precursor proBDNF.MethodsSeventy-four patients with axSpA and 79 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) completed the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). Clinical parameters included Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) with C-reactive protein (-CRP) and patient-reported outcomes (PROs). Serum concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), BDNF, proBDNF, and sortilin were determined by ELISA.ResultsDepression and anxiety were more prevalent in axSpA patients (13.5% and 25.7%) than in HCs (2.1%, p < 0.05 and 8.9%, p < 0.01, respectively). Both BDI-II and BAI scores correlated with ASDAS-CRP and PROs. The severity of depression symptoms did not differ by sex, whereas anxiety was higher in female than in male axSpA patients and healthy controls. Patients with depression showed a lower BDNF/proBDNF ratio and elevated CRP levels (p < 0.05). Serum BDNF levels were reduced in axSpA compared with HCs (median 160.90, IQR 127.90–236.50 vs. 195.00, IQR 150.90–279.90, p < 0.05), with significant sex-related variability (p < 0.05). In a model adjusted for age and sex, a doubling of CRP levels was associated with a 10% decrease in BDNF. ProBDNF levels were positively correlated with IL-6 and sortilin levels.ConclusionThese findings indicate that sex and systemic inflammation contribute to the psychological burden of axSpA. Effective control of inflammation may reduce mental health comorbidities, such as depression and anxiety, which are linked to worse outcomes.Keywords: Axial spondyloarthritis, Depression, Anxiety, Inflammation, Neurobiology, CRP, BDNF, proBDNF

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is an inflammatory disease affecting the sacroiliac joints (SIJs) and spine and/or peripheral joints, leading to progressive structural damage (Navarro-Compán et al., 2025). The global prevalence of axSpA ranges from 0.3% to 1.4%, with higher rates in North America and Europe than in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (Stolwijk et al., 2016), mirroring the distribution of the HLA-B27 allele, the strongest genetic predisposition marker (Chen et al., 2017). Beyond interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, including mechanical stress, dysregulation of interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α and the IL-17/23 axis plays a key role in axSpA pathogenesis (Zhu et al., 2025). According to the severity of sacroiliitis, axSpA is classified into radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA), also known as ankylosing spondylitis (AS), and non-radiographic axSpA (nr-axSpA) (Bosch et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2020). Despite reduced spinal mobility in r-axSpA, both forms show comparable pain, extra-musculoskeletal manifestations (EMMs), disease activity, disability, and comorbidities, including mental health disorders (MHDs) (Ramiro et al., 2023; Garrido-Cumbrera et al., 2019).

Depression and anxiety are the most common MHDs in axSpA. A meta-analysis by Park et al. reported a pooled relative risk (RR) for incident depression of 1.51 (95% CI 1.28–1.79) in r-axSpA (Park et al., 2020), while depression prevalence ranges from 11% to 64% in axSpA (Zhao et al., 2018). Anxiety has been less extensively studied, with prevalence estimates ranging from 8% to 38% depending on the assessment scale (Callhoff et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2022). Registry data from Taiwan further indicate an increased risk of anxiety disorders (adjusted HR 1.84; 95% CI 1.37–2.49)(Shen et al., 2016), with standardized incidence ratios exceeding those of mood disorders in both men and women with r-axSpA [ (Sundquist et al., 2008). The burden of depression and anxiety is higher in axSpA patients with active disease (Zou et al., 2016; Peiyu et al., 2025), but does not differ between radiographic forms (Zhao et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2016).

Depression and anxiety are characterized by persistent sadness, loss of interest, sleep, motor and appetite disturbances, reduced energy and suicidal ideation(Marx et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024). Their biological background is multifactorial and includes genetic susceptibility, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, monoamine alterations, impaired neuroplasticity and neuroinflammation. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α affect microglia, astrocytes, the HPA axis and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signalling (Poletti et al., 2024). BDNF and its precursor proBDNF act through distinct receptors (TrkB, p75NTR and sortilin), regulating neuronal survival, apoptosis and neuroplasticity, with important consequences for emotional regulation, learning and memory (Komori et al., 2024).

Interactions between immune mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α and C-reactive protein), neuropeptides and blood–brain barrier (BBB) function have been implicated in major depressive disorder (MDD) (Banks, 2015; Haapakoski et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2017). However, the mechanisms linking systemic inflammation with depression and anxiety in axSpA remain unclear. Therefore, this study examined whether inflammatory biomarkers (IL-6 and CRP) and neurotrophins (BDNF, proBDNF and sortilin) reflect the burden of mental health disturbances and their relationship with axSpA disease activity.

Methods

Study population

Individuals with established axSpA and healthy controls (HCs; without any known inflammatory rheumatic or psychiatric disorders) were invited to participate in this study. All participants had previously been enrolled in the RHEU-THYMIA cohort, a cross-sectional study of mental health in rheumatic diseases (submitted). Written informed consent (approved by the Ethical Committee of the Institute of Rheumatology, reference no. 5704/2021) was obtained from all the participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligibility criteria

participants were aged 18–65 years. The exclusion criteria included fibromyalgia (FM), other autoimmune diseases (AD), chronic decompensated diseases (e.g., type 2 diabetes, chronic pulmonary, renal, or cardiac disease), malignancy, or current/recent infection. These conditions were excluded because of a potential overlap with nr-axSpA or known associations with mood disturbances. Further exclusions included recent injury or surgery (≤6 weeks), pregnancy, breastfeeding, history of substance abuse, treatment with corticosteroids, opioid analgesics, hormonal therapies (including contraceptives or hormone replacement), antiepileptic or anti-Parkinsonian drugs, or biological therapies for indications other than axSpA. Patients who received experimental therapy were excluded.

Clinical assessment

Demographic data, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, pharmacotherapy, alcohol use, physical activity (per the World Health Organization recommendations), and quality of life were recorded. The latter was assessed using the EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D) life (Rabin et al., 2001). AxSpA patients were classified according to the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) criteria (Rudwaleit et al., 2009). Clinical characteristics included disease duration, inflammatory back pain (IBP), and peripheral manifestations (arthritis in any of the 28 joints; enthesitis according to the Maastricht Ankylosing Spondylitis Enthesitis Score criteria (Heuft-Dorenbosch et al., 2003), or dactylitis). Radiographic evaluation of the sacroiliac joints (SIJs) was used to distinguish radiographic (r-axSpA) from non-radiographic (nr-axSpA) disease.

Patient-reported outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) included the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI), Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Metrology Index (BASMI), and Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score with C-reactive protein (ASDAS-CRP) (Rudwaleit et al., 2009). Current and prior axSpA treatments were recorded systematically.

Blood sampling and analyses

Blood samples were obtained in the morning after a fasting period of at least 2 h. Serum CRP levels were analyzed on the same day. All samples were centrifuged immediately, and serum aliquots were stored at −70 °C until further analysis. All samples were collected in accordance with the ethical standards in the Biobank of the Institute of Rheumatology.

Analysis of BDNF, proBDNF, IL-6 and sortilin

The serum levels of cytokines were analyzed using commercially available ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer's protocol (BDNF, IL-6, Sortilin-Ray Biotech, Norcross, GA, United States; proBDNF - R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The detection limits of the assays were as follows: BDNF 80 pg/ml, proBDNF, IL-6 3 pg/ml, Sortilin 0,81 ng/ml. Absorbance at a primary wavelength of 450 nm was detected using a Sunrise ELISA reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland), and the data were quantified using Kim version 5.43.01 software (Daniel Kittrich (Software Production, Prague, Czech Republic).

Mental health assessement

Symptoms of depression and anxiety were assessed using the validated Czech versions of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) (Ptáček et al., 2016; Kamaradova et al., 2015; Beck et al., 1988; Beck and Carbin, 1988). Each questionnaire consisted of 21 items scored on a four-point Likert scale (0–3). For the BDI-II, scores were classified as no depression (0–13), mild (14–19), moderate (20–28), or severe (29–63). For the BAI, the corresponding categories were no anxiety (0–9), mild (10–18), moderate (19–29), and severe (30–63) anxiety (Beck and Carbin, 1988; Julian, 2011).

Statistical analysis

A sample size calculation indicated that at least 64 participants per group were required to detect a moderate effect size (d = 0.5) at a significance level of 0.05 with 80% statistical power. The final study sample included 74 patients with axSpA and 79 age- and sex-matched controls, which was considered sufficient for the planned group comparisons, correlation and regression analyses. For descriptive statistical analysis, continuous variables were presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CI) or medians with upper and lower quartiles, while discrete variables as frequencies and percentages. The t-test was used for univariate subgroup comparisons, whereas the Wilcoxon exact test or Mann-Whitney test was used for non-parametric data. Correlations were calculated using Spearman's correlation coefficient. Lastly, all multivariable linear regression analyses were modeled with weighted residuals (WLS) and biomarker log2 transformed to account for nonlinearity. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. To control for multiple comparisons in BDI-II and BAI, Bonferroni correction was applied where appropriate. Due to the exploratory nature of biomarker analyses, p-values were not adjusted for multiple testing.

Depression and anxiety in axSpA are associated with worse disease outcomes

Table 3 demonstrated the significant differences in depression and anxiety between axSpA and HC. Depression was more frequent in axSpA patients than in healthy controls (13.5% vs 2.5%), corresponding to an approximately six-fold higher odds of depression (OR ≈ 6.0). Similarly, anxiety symptoms were more prevalent in axSpA (25.7% vs 8.9%), with an estimated odds ratio of approximately 3.5. Furthermore, HC only ever indicated mild depression or anxiety, whereas moderate to severe depression or anxiety was observed in 4% and 8% of axSpA patients, respectively. No significant sex differences in depression were found among patients with axSpA. However, females with axSpA experienced considerably higher levels of anxiety than males with axSpA or healthy females (demonstrated in the Supplementary Material, Table II).

Patients with axSpA and depression or anxiety had higher disease activity than those without: ASDAS-CRP (median, IQR): 3.39 (2.95–3.80) vs. 1.49 (1.02–2.16) and 2.84 (2.07–3.48) vs. 1.39 (0.98–2.04), respectively; all p < 0.001; BASDAI: 5.35 (4.08–6.60) vs. 2.40 (1.18–3.82), p < 0.01, and 4.90 (3.50–6.50) vs. 1.80 (1.10–3.45), p < 0.0001, respectively (referred in the Supplementary Material, Table III). Additionally, individuals with axSpA and depression exhibited a higher functional deficit (BASFI median: 4.20 (IQR: 3.15–6.73) vs. 0.60 (0.10–2.35)) and a lower quality of life (median 5D-EQ: 0.52 (IQR: 0.52–0.61) vs. 0.80 (0.72–1.00); all p < 0.01; see Supplementary Material, Table III). Conversely, axSpA patients with anxiety burden were older (median, IQR: age 46.00 (41.50–53.00) vs. 41.00 (33.00-47.00); p < 0,01) and had poorer outcomes in terms of quality of life, function, and work status (Supplementary Table III). Although no variation in disease duration or radiographic findings was found, 69% axSpA patients with anxiety, compared to 25% of those without, described IBP manifestations (see Supplementary Table III).

In addition, the BDI-II and BAI scores were strongly correlated with ASDAS-CRP (r = 0.590 and 0.660, respectively), BASDAI (r = 0.580 and r = 0.730, respectively), BASFI (r = 0.510 and 0.660, respectively), and 5D-EQ (r = −0.660 and −0.670, respectively), all p < 0.001 (P significant after Bonferroni correction, p = 0.00625; data not shown).

Patients with axSpA have lower serum levels of BDNF, and a lower ratio of BDNF to pro-BDNF is linked to symptoms of depression

Of all the biomarkers tested, only BDNF levels differed significantly between patients with axSpA and healthy controls (Table 4). BDNF levels were lower in axSpA females than in healthy females (median 134.00 pg/ml, IQR 19.01–329.40 vs 216.25 pg/ml, IQR 153.82–275.11; p < 0.01) and also lower than in axSpA males (median 180.86 pg/ml, IQR 133.31–261.29; p < 0.01). Conversely, axSpA males had higher IL-6 levels than axSpA females (median 1052.43 pg/ml, IQR 261.00–1117.69 vs 347.42 pg/ml, IQR 112.34–1066.87; p < 0.05), although IL-6 levels in axSpA did not differ significantly from those observed in HC.

The clinical variables of axSpA and their relationship to BDNF, proBDNF and inflammatory biomarkers

In all axSpAs, a strong positive correlation was found between the serum levels of proBDNF and sortilin (r = 0.580; p < 0.001) and IL-6 (r = 0.470; p < 0.001), as well as between IL-6 and sortilin (r = 0.630; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, there was no substantial correlation between the serum levels of CRP or BDNF and any of these biomarkers (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

Correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations between CRP levels and disease activity indices, including ASDAS (r = 0.75, p < 0.001), BASDAI (r = 0.53, p < 0.001) and BASFI (r = 0.52, p < 0.001). CRP levels were also positively correlated with depression (BDI-II; r = 0.44, p < 0.001) and anxiety scores (BAI; r = 0.61, p < 0.001), and negatively correlated with quality of life (EQ-5D; r = −0.46, p < 0.001), see Table 6.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between mental health disturbances, inflammatory biomarkers and disease activity in patients with axial spondyloarthritis. Symptoms of depression and anxiety were associated with higher disease activity, worse functional status and reduced quality of life. Importantly, depressive symptoms were accompanied by alterations in neurotrophic signaling, reflected by lower BDNF/proBDNF ratios, and by higher CRP levels. These findings suggest that mental health disturbances in axSpA may be linked not only to the clinical burden of the disease but also to underlying biological mechanisms involving systemic inflammation and neurotrophic dysregulation. Together, these results support the concept that psychological symptoms in axSpA may arise from a complex interaction between inflammatory activity and neurobiological pathways.

Consistent with previous epidemiological and registry studies, our cross-sectional analysis supports an increased risk of depression and anxiety in patients with axSpA (Park et al., 2020; Callhoff et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2022; Sundquist et al., 2008; Omar et al., 2023). Several factors have been associated with poorer mental health outcomes in this population, including higher disease activity, female sex, unemployment or sick leave, and insufficient physical activity (Zou et al., 2016; Peiyu et al., 2025; Garrido-Cumbrera et al., 2024). Although the burden of axSpA and limitations in daily activities may impair coping and resilience, Chung et al. reported that depression and anxiety, rather than resilience itself, were associated with poorer health outcomes in axSpA (Chung et al., 2023). In our cohort, females showed worse outcomes, particularly with regard to anxiety symptoms. This observation is consistent with findings by Ristić et al., who reported that women with axSpA exhibited more dysfunctional coping strategies, higher perceived stress and fatigue, and greater anxiety than men (Ristic et al., 2025). These findings highlight the importance of paying particular attention to mental health symptoms in female patients with axSpA. As women with axSpA are often diagnosed later than men (Navarro-Compán et al., 2025; López-Medina et al., 2019), under-recognition of their somatic symptoms may contribute to increased psychological distress and potentially poorer treatment outcomes.

In the present study, we also explored the relationship between inflammatory and neurotrophic biomarkers and mental health disturbances in axSpA. Patients with depressive symptoms showed lower BDNF/proBDNF ratios together with higher CRP levels, suggesting a potential link between systemic inflammation and neurotrophic dysregulation. Previous studies have indicated that inflammatory cytokines may influence neuroplasticity through pathways involving BDNF signaling. Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, these findings support the hypothesis that inflammatory activity may contribute to mental health disturbances in axSpA not only through clinical disease burden but also through biological pathways affecting brain function.

Meta-analyses in MDD have reported elevated circulating CRP levels, including in drug-naïve patients experiencing a first depressive episode (Haapakoski et al., 2015; Çakici et al., 2020). Peripheral CRP has also been associated with somatic depressive symptoms such as fatigue, reduced energy and sleep disturbances (Frank et al., 2021). However, associations between CRP levels and depression severity remain inconsistent. For example, Webers et al. did not observe a direct association between CRP-mediated inflammation and depressive symptoms in patients with axSpA (Webers et al., 2023). Sex may represent an additional moderating factor. A meta-analysis by Jarkas et al. demonstrated that CRP and IL-6 levels were significantly higher in females with depression, but not in males (Jarkas et al., 2024). In our cohort, both sexes exhibited depressive and anxiety symptoms, with women being particularly prone to anxiety. Interestingly, CRP emerged as a key determinant of BDI-II and BAI scores in both sexes, although correlations between CRP and symptom severity were stronger in males. This observation is partly consistent with findings from NHANES III, which reported higher CRP levels in men with recurrent MDD compared with men without depression (Ford et al., 2004). Altered BDNF signaling may also affect pain perception and immune responses, as BDNF contributes to central sensitization and may modulate inflammatory pathways.

Peripheral CRP levels may reflect inflammatory processes that influence central nervous system function despite the limited permeability of the blood–brain barrier. Peripheral inflammation can affect brain signaling through several pathways, including endothelial activation, cytokine signaling, and microglial activation, which may ultimately alter neuroplasticity and reduce neurotrophin production such as BDNF (Karege et al., 2002). Although circulating BDNF may partially reflect central nervous system concentrations, it is also produced by peripheral tissues and stored predominantly in platelets (Forsgren et al., 2011; Castrén et al., 2021).

In our study, patients with depressive symptoms showed a lower BDNF/proBDNF ratio, although this association was only weakly related to disease activity. After adjusting for age and sex, BDNF levels were inversely associated with CRP and were lower in females. Reduced BDNF/proBDNF ratios have previously been linked to depressive symptoms and improvement in depression following therapy (Zhou et al., 2013, 2017; Castrén et al., 2021). In our cohort, BMI and comorbidities did not differ between groups, suggesting that lower BDNF levels in females may reflect disease-related or sex-specific mechanisms.

BDNF signaling is influenced by multiple biological and environmental factors, including neuronal plasticity, stress, and physical activity (Brigadski et al., 2020). Altered BDNF signaling may also affect pain perception and immune responses, as BDNF contributes to central sensitization and may modulate inflammatory pathways (Wang et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2022). Although proBDNF levels in our cohort were not strongly associated with disease activity or mood symptoms, their correlation with IL-6 and sortilin suggests a potential link between chronic inflammation and proBDNF signaling. Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that inflammatory processes may disrupt BDNF/proBDNF homeostasis and contribute to mood disturbances in axSpA.

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, depression and anxiety were assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Although the BDI-II and BAI are widely used instruments for evaluating symptom severity, they do not establish a clinical diagnosis and may be subject to reporting bias. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow determination of whether mental health disturbances fluctuate with disease activity or persist independently of axSpA activity. Moreover, most patients in our cohort exhibited low-to-moderate disease activity with relatively low CRP levels, which may have limited the ability to fully capture the relationship between systemic inflammation and psychological burden.

Another limitation is the absence of neuroimaging techniques or assessment of additional inflammatory cytokines, which might have provided deeper insight into the mechanisms linking inflammation and mental health alterations in axSpA. Recent studies using magnetic resonance imaging have suggested that systemic inflammation may influence brain functional connectivity in axSpA, highlighting the importance of integrating neurobiological approaches in future research.

Despite these limitations, our study provides novel evidence linking inflammatory markers, neurotrophin signaling (BDNF/proBDNF), and mental health disturbances in axSpA. These findings suggest that depression and anxiety may not be solely psychosocial consequences of chronic illness but may also involve biological pathways related to systemic inflammation and neurotrophic regulation. Further research focusing on sex-related differences in mental health burden in axSpA is warranted to support more personalized approaches to disease management.
