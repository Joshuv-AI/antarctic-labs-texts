Postponing the Choice: Advantage of deferred measurements in quantum information processing
Claudio Carmeli, Teiko Heinosaari, Alessandro Toigo

Abstract
. Simultaneously implementing two arbitrary quantum measurements on the same system is impossible. The consequence of this limitation is that selecting one measurement actively excludes other possibilities. Two incompatible choices can then be forced together only at the cost of adding enough noise to the measurements. An intriguing alternative is to postpone the choice, or part of it, until a later stage. We explore the advantages of this deferred decision-making and discover that the benefits critically depends on the assumptions about the forthcoming choice. In certain scenarios postponing the choice introduces no additional cost, while in others partial postponement can be effectively the same as full postponement.



1.
Introduction

The domain of quantum information revolves around the utilization of quantum systems to encode and convey information.
The necessary step in reading out the information written in the state of a quantum system is to carry out a measurement, which in the usual case is a sharp measurement corresponding to a Hilbert space orthonormal basis.
Quantum theory forbids simultaneous measurements of more than one basis, and for this reason one is forced to select a particular basis for readout.
Nonetheless, it is possible to measure more than one basis simultaneously by allowing noise in the measurement outcome distribution, which means that the uncertainty in measurement outcomes increases
[
1
]
.
These kind of approximate joint measurements have been extensively studied and the trade-off in noise levels has been characterized in several important cases, including the case of two mutually unbiased bases
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

When performing a joint measurement, one has to decide beforehand which measurements will be done simultaneously, and then devise the joint measurement setup accordingly.
An alternative approach is to duplicate the initial state of the system and perform measurements on the duplicates.
Since quantum theory forbids perfect cloning of an unknown state, one is left with two approximate copies
[
7
]
.
The resulting noise in measurements is more than in optimal approximate joint measurements
[
8
,
9
]
.
That is not a surprise, as approximate cloning allows any choice of measurements, whereas a joint measurement is tailored for a specific pair of measurements only.
The advantage of approximate cloning is that the target measurements (e.g. two bases) can be decided later.

There is a scenario between these two approaches in which one of the measurements is fixed beforehand but the choice of the other measurement is postponed.
The first measurement necessarily disturbs the state of the system in some way, nevertheless, it can be aimed to perform in a manner that is not totally destructive and that is neutral with respect to all possible choices of the second measurement.
This approach gives an advantage over fixed joint measurements, as the other measurement can be decided later, for instance, after one has obtained information by means of the first measurement.
However, it is not as universal as the approach based on approximate cloning, since one of the measurements is fixed and only the second measurement can be chosen freely.
In this way, the third scenario lies between the two earlier approaches, and one expects that there is a trade-off between freedom of choice and optimal noise levels.
The scenario has natural variations, as we can limit the choice of the second measurement from all possible choices to some more restricted class of choices.
The question is then to find the optimal way to perform the first measurement so that the noise trade-off is as favorable as possible.

Figure 1.
The three different scenarios compared in the present investigation: (a) the target meters are fixed in advance and their approximate joint meter is tailored accordingly; (b) neither of the target meters is fixed in advance, and each of them is measured in a different approximate clone of the original state; (c) only the first target meter is fixed in advance, while the second one is decided after a measurement of the first has been performed.

The comparison of the mentioned three scenarios is not completely straightforward, as we need to decide what quantities we are comparing.
In the current investigation we concentrate on measurements related to orthonormal bases and we assume that the noise is standard uniform noise, i.e., simple coin tossing noise. Within our framework this is equivalent to depolarizing noise.
When it comes to the third scenario of one fixed and one free measurement, we pay particular attention to two alternative cases. In both cases the fixed measurement is related to the same basis. Its optimization however differs according to our knowledge or assumption on the free measurement. Namely, in the first case the free measurement is related to a completely arbitrary basis, while in the second case it is related to a basis that is unbiased with respect to the fixed one.
As one would expect, the noise trade-off is worse in the case with one fixed and one completely arbitrary basis.
We prove that in this case it is exactly as bad as in the approximate cloning scenario, where both bases – and not only the second one – are not fixed in advance. Thus, there is no advantage in knowing the first basis and then arranging the measurement setup by enforcing this additional information. The result is surprising, as one would expect that postponing the choice of both bases is in fact more penalizing than postponing the choice of only one of them. No less surprisingly, we further prove that the case where the free basis is unbiased with respect to the fixed basis actually generates the same noise trade-off as the joint measurement scenario. Therefore, knowing that the two bases are mutually unbiased is enough to achieve the best possible noise trade-off even when the choice of one basis is postponed after the measurement of the other one. This means that the unbiasedness constraint allows to extract as much amount of information on the first basis as if both bases were fixed in advance, while it still enables to defer the choice of the second basis after the measurement of the first one. In conclusion, the two cases in which the second basis is free or it is unbiased with respect to the first basis are just the opposite extremes in the scenario where one of the bases is fixed in advance and the other basis is decided later.

An interesting additional viewpoint can be obtained by looking at overnoisy measurements, i.e., measurements in which the intensity of the noise is allowed to exceed unity. Indeed, mixing a quantum measurement with uniform noise formally makes sense even if the noise intensity is slightly greater than
1
. When the noise intensity takes its largest possible value, one obtains a measurement – called the reverse version of the original measurement in
[
10
]
– which corresponds to discard the outcome of the original measurement and then output any other outcome chosen at random with the same probability. Remarkably, when overnoisy measurements are allowed, the scenario with one fixed and one arbitrary basis does actually improve the noise trade-off with respect to the approximate cloning scenario. Thus, one of the two results proved for the standard noise model fails for overnoisy measurements, namely, postponing the choice of both bases turns out to be more penalizing than postponing the choice of only one of them. The result holding for the standard noise model is then bolstered by the fact that it does not extend to overnoisy measurements, because as we already remarked its failure is just what one would expect in general. This should be compared with the other result proved for the standard noise model, which in fact is true also if we consider overnoisy measurements. Indeed, if the second basis is known to be unbiased with respect to the first one, we show that even in the overnoisy case postponing the choice of one basis still yields the same noise trade-off as in the joint measurement scenario. As an interesting byproduct of our analysis, we also find that, when the noise intensity exceeds unity, a striking difference emerges between joint measurement setups for qubit systems and systems having dimension higher than
2
.

Notations

In this paper, we work in qudit systems, i.e., quantum systems whose associated Hilbert space is
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, where
d
is the dimension of the system. We denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
the
C^{*}
-algebra of all linear endomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and we write
\mathds{1}
for the identity element and
{\rm tr}
for the linear trace of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. In a
d
-dimensional qudit system, a
state
is thus described by an element
\varrho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\right]=1
and
\varrho\geq 0
, i.e.,
\varrho
is positive semidefinite.
For any finite set
X
, a
meter
with outcomes in
X
is a positive operator valued measure (POVM), i.e. a map
\mathsf{M}:X\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
\sum_{x}\mathsf{M}(x)=\mathds{1}
and
\mathsf{M}(x)\geq 0
for all
x
.
The probability of obtaining the outcome
x
by measuring the meter
\mathsf{M}
in the state
\varrho
is given by the Born rule
{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\mathsf{M}(x)\right]
.
We also consider transformations of one qudit system into another one, with possibly different dimensions
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
of the input and output systems. A transformation of this kind is described by a
channel
, i.e., a linear and completely positive (CP) map
\Phi:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})
such that
{\rm tr}\left[\,\Phi(\varrho)\right]=1
for all states
\varrho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})
.
Finally, by combining the notions of meter and channel, we obtain the definition of an
instrument
, that is, a map
\mathcal{J}:X\times\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})
such that
\mathcal{J}(x,\cdot)
is linear and CP for all
x\in X
and
\sum_{x}\mathcal{J}(x,\cdot)
is a channel. We remark that this is the Schrödinger picture for channels and instruments.
The Heisenberg picture just amounts to switch from
\Phi
and
\mathcal{J}
to the adjoint channel
\Phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})
and the adjoint instrument
\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}:X\times\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})
, which are defined by
{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\Phi^{\dagger}(A)\right]={\rm tr}\left[\Phi(\varrho)A\right]
and
{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(x,A)\right]={\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{J}(x,\varrho)A\right]
for all
\varrho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})
,
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})
and
x\in X
.

2.
Approximate joint meters and cloners

Suppose
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
are two meters on a qudit system, with the respective outcome sets
X
and
Y
. Then,
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
can be measured in a single experimental run exactly when there exists a third meter
\mathsf{G}
that has the Cartesian product
X\times Y
as its outcome set and satisfies the relations

\mathsf{M}(x)=\sum_{y}\mathsf{G}(x,y)\,,\qquad\mathsf{N}(y)=\sum_{x}\mathsf{G}(x,y)

(1)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. If this is the case, we say that the meters
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
are
compatible
, and
\mathsf{G}
is a
joint meter
for them.
On the other hand, if
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
are incompatible (i.e., not compatible), we can add noise to both and observe that at a certain threshold value the so obtained noisy meters become compatible. The threshold value depends on
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
and also on the type of noise
[
11
]
.
In our following investigation we focus on the simplest type of noise, that is, the uniform noise.

To provide a concrete example, we fix the target meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
associated with two different orthonormal bases
\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{d-1}\}
and
\{\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{d-1}\}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{Q}(x)=|\,\varphi_{x}\,\rangle\langle\,\varphi_{x}\,|\,,\qquad\mathsf{P}(y)=|\,\psi_{y}\,\rangle\langle\,\psi_{y}\,|

(2)

for all
x,y
belonging to the outcome sets
X=Y=\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
.
Since
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
are
sharp meters
(i.e., consist of rank-
1
projections) and they do not commute, they are incompatible.
However, we can add uniform noise to both and thus obtain the
noisy versions

\mathsf{Q}_{s}(x)=s\,|\,\varphi_{x}\,\rangle\langle\,\varphi_{x}\,|+(1-s)\tfrac{1}{d}\mathds{1}\,,\qquad\mathsf{P}_{t}(y)=t\,|\,\psi_{y}\,\rangle\langle\,\psi_{y}\,|+(1-t)\tfrac{1}{d}\mathds{1}\,,

(3)

where the amount of noise is characterized by parameters
s,t\in [0,1]
. For sufficiently small values of
s
and
t
, the meters
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}
are indeed compatible, and any joint meter for them is therefore an
approximate joint meter
of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
.
The obvious question is how large
s
an
t
can be for a joint meter to exist.
To answer this question, the essential point is that the choice of the two bases is made at once in the present scenario, and the approximate joint meter can then be optimized depending on both bases.

In our previous example the noise parameters
s
and
t
can be different, thus accounting for the trade-off between the accuracies in the approximation of
\mathsf{Q}
and the approximation of
\mathsf{P}
that are achievable by means of any approximate joint meter. This trade-off is always weaker than the relation
s+t\leq 1
, and thus the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
is always contained in the
compatibility region
of the pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
, i.e., the subset

\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})=\{(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]\mid\mathsf{Q}_{s}\text{ and }\mathsf{P}_{t}\text{ are compatible}\}

of the unit square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
(see
[
12
]
).

For the most part of this paper, we consider the case in which the sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
are
mutually unbiased
, that is, the equality
{\rm tr}\left[\mathsf{Q}(x)\mathsf{P}(y)\right]=\frac{1}{d}
is satisfied for all
x,y
. This amounts to the requirement that the two orthonormal bases
\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{d-1}\}
and
\{\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{d-1}\}
of (
2
) are mutually unbiased in the usual sense
[
13
]
. Under the unbiasedness assumption, the compatibility region of the pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
was determined in
[
2
,
4
,
5
]
(see
[
5
, Eq. (5) and Fig. 1]
). Theorem
1
below and the subsequent discussion summarize this earlier result.

An obvious method to measure two incompatible meters on the same qudit system is performing it by means of cloning. Actually, if perfect cloning of quantum states would be possible, then clearly all meters would be compatible, as we could make copies and perform measurements on them
[
14
]
.
Even if perfect cloning is impossible, we may try to use an imperfect but realizable cloning device as a way to jointly measure approximations of incompatible meters
[
15
]
.
Namely, we use a channel
\Gamma:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})
to produce two approximate clones
\varrho_{1}={\rm tr}_{\bar{1}}\Gamma(\varrho)
and
\varrho_{2}={\rm tr}_{\bar{2}}\Gamma(\varrho)
of the unknown initial state
\varrho
, where
\bar{1}=2
,
\bar{2}=1
and
{\rm tr}_{i}
is the partial trace over the
i

\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Then, we measure two meters
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
separately on these approximate clones. The resulting meter
\mathsf{G}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{M},\mathsf{N}}
is thus given by

\mathsf{G}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{M},\mathsf{N}}(x,y)=\Gamma^{\dagger}(\mathsf{M}(x)\otimes\mathsf{N}(y))\,.

(4)

The essential point is that
\mathsf{G}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{M},\mathsf{N}}
is not a joint meter for
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
, but for two meters
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
that are close to
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
, in some sense.

If we apply the procedure described above to the sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
defined in (
2

\Gamma
, the resulting meter
\mathsf{G}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P}}
turns out to be an approximate joint meter of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
.
It is then easy to compare the original pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
with the pair
(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime},\mathsf{P}^{\prime})=(\mathsf{Q}_{s},\mathsf{P}_{t})
obtained by means of approximate cloning, as it is simply a matter of evaluating the noise parameters
s
and

. In particular, we can optimize
\Gamma
over all possible choices of the sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
, thus obtaining a universal device to make two arbitrary sharp meters noisy enough to be compatible.

If we require that
\mathsf{G}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P}}

\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
for any possible choice of the sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}

Equivalently, the approximate cloning device

{\rm tr}_{\bar{1}}\circ\Gamma=sI+(1-s)\tfrac{1}{d}\mathds{1}{\rm tr}\,,\qquad{\rm tr}_{\bar{2}}\circ\Gamma=tI+(1-t)\tfrac{1}{d}\mathds{1}{\rm tr}\,,

The right-hand sides of the last two expressions are instances of the (partially)

3.
Sequential measurements

The third scenario between the earlier two is the one where first of the target meters, say
\mathsf{M}
, is fixed from the beginning, but the second target meter
\mathsf{N}
is decided only later.
In this scenario we first perform a measurement of an approximation
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{M}
, then in the resulting state we subsequently measure
\mathsf{N}
.
A measurement of
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
necessarily disturbs the state of the qudit system in some way, unless
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
is totally noninformative.
However, measuring the approximation
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
can be made less destructive than a direct measurement of
\mathsf{M}

[
20
]
.

The sequential measurement scheme gives an advantage over fixed joint measurements of two meters, because the second meter
\mathsf{N}
may as well be decided only after one has obtained information on the system based on the outcome of
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
.
However, it is not as universal as the approximate cloning setup, since one of the meters is now fixed and the first measurement can be tailored for that specific meter.

The statistics of a meter
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
as well as the statistics of any meter
\mathsf{N}
measured on the qudit system after
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
are both described by an instrument
\mathcal{J}:X\times\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. Namely, for any prior state
\varrho
of the system, the outcome
x
of
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
is obtained with probability
{\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{J}(x,\varrho)\right]
, and the posterior state conditioned to
x
is
\mathcal{J}(x,\varrho)/{\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{J}(x,\varrho)\right]
. Thus, if after
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
we subsequently perform a measurement of
\mathsf{N}
, the probability of obtaining the outcomes
x
and
y
in succession is
{\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{J}(x,\varrho)\mathsf{N}(y)\right]={\rm tr}\left[\varrho\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{N}}(x,y)\right]
, where
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{N}}(x,y)=\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(x,\mathsf{N}(y))
. The overall resulting meter
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{N}}
is then a joint meter for
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
and a disturbed version
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{N}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{N}^{\prime}(y)=\sum_{x}\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(x,\mathsf{N}(y))
.

3.1.
The second choice is arbitrary

If in the sequential measurement scheme described above the target meters are the sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
of (
2
), we can require that the resulting meter
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{P}}
is an approximate joint meter of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
. Indeed, as it happens with approximate cloning, it is then easy to compare the original pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
with its approximation
(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime},\mathsf{P}^{\prime})=(\mathsf{Q}_{s},\mathsf{P}_{t})
.
The essential difference from the cloning scenario is that in the present setting
\mathsf{Q}
is fixed, and only
\mathsf{P}
is allowed to vary among all sharp meters. Requiring that
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{P}}
is an approximate joint meter of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
for any possible choice of the second meter
\mathsf{P}
then amounts to impose that the instrument
\mathcal{J}
satisfies the two conditions

\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(\cdot,\mathds{1})=\mathsf{Q}_{s}\,,\qquad\sum_{x}\mathcal{J}(x,\cdot)=I_{t}

(19)

for some
s,t\in[0,1]
(see Appendix
A
).
An instrument
\mathcal{J}
which satisfies (
19
) is a
joint instrument
for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
, and the meter
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and the channel
I_{t}
are
compatible
whenever they admit a joint instrument
[
20
,
21
]
.

As we did for the pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
and
(I,I)
, we introduce the compatibility region of the pair
(\mathsf{Q},I)
, i.e., the set

\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)=\{(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]\mid\mathsf{Q}_{s}\text{ and }I_{t}\text{ are compatible}\}\,.

We then observe that the relation
\textrm{CR}(I,I)\subseteq\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
can be refined to the chain of inclusions

\textrm{CR}(I,I)\subseteq\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)\subseteq\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})\,.

(20)

Indeed, the second inclusion is implied by the sequential measurement scheme, since the resulting meter
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathsf{P}}
is a joint meter for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}
whenever
\mathcal{J}
is a joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
. On the other hand, to prove the first inclusion, we consider a further measurement scheme in which we produce two approximate clones of the initial state
\varrho
and we subsequently perform a measurement of
\mathsf{Q}
only on the first clone. Thus, there are two outputs, i.e., the outcome
x
of
\mathsf{Q}
and the second clone conditioned to
x
. The scheme is then described by an instrument
\mathcal{J}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{Q}}
, where
\Gamma
denotes the cloning device and
\mathcal{J}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{Q}}(x,\varrho)={\rm tr}_{\bar{2}}\left[(\mathsf{Q}(x)\otimes\mathds{1})\Gamma(\varrho)\right]
. In particular, if
\Gamma
is a joint channel for
I_{s}
and
I_{t}
, the resulting instrument
\mathcal{J}^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{Q}}
is a joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
, hence
\textrm{CR}(I,I)\subseteq\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)
as claimed.

Cloning
\varrho
and then performing a measurement of
\mathsf{Q}
on the first clone is only one out of the many ways to construct a joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
. For this reason, one would expect the first inclusion of (
20
) to be strict. Surprisingly, the next theorem shows that this is not the case. The
proof relies on some basic applications of finite Fourier transform and Weyl-Heisenberg group, and we defer it to Section
5
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
. For any sharp meter
\mathsf{Q}
, the noisy version
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and the depolarizing channel
I_{t}
are compatible if and only if

s+t-1\leq\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{(1-s)(1-t)}\,.

(21)

The comparison of Theorems
1
and
2
yields a further refinement of the chain of inclusions (
20
), namely,

\textrm{CR}(I,I)=\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)\subset\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})\,.

(22)

In particular, the equality
\textrm{CR}(I,I)=\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)
leads to the first main result of the paper.

Result 1
.

Suppose the target pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
are chosen among all possible pairs of sharp meters. Then, the two strategies consisting in

(i)

optimally cloning the qudit state for the purpose of jointly measuring any pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
, and

(ii)

performing a sequential measurement that is optimized for those pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
in which
\mathsf{Q}
is fixed

actually gives the same trade-off between the achievable approximations of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
. When
\mathsf{P}
is arbitrary, there is hence no advantage in knowing
\mathsf{Q}
in advance, and the cloning strategy is still optimal even in the scenario where the choice of
\mathsf{P}
is postponed until an approximate measurement of
\mathsf{Q}
has been performed.

In order to describe the joint instruments which correspond to the extreme points of the set
\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)
, we implement the measurement scheme in which we first apply the optimal quantum cloning device
\Gamma^{(s)}
of (
16
) and we subsequently perform a measurement of
\mathsf{Q}
on the first approximate clone. The resulting instrument
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}\equiv\mathcal{J}^{\Gamma^{(s)}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(s)}(x,\varrho)={\rm tr}_{\bar{2}}\left[(\mathsf{Q}(x)\otimes\mathds{1})\Gamma^{(s)}(\varrho)\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\big\{a_{2}(s)^{2}{\rm tr}\left[(\varrho\mathsf{Q}(x))\right]\mathds{1}+a_{2}(s)b(s)(\varrho\mathsf{Q}(x)+\mathsf{Q}(x)\varrho)+b(s)^{2}\varrho\big\}\,.

It satisfies the relations

\mathcal{J}^{(s)\,{\dagger}}(\cdot,\mathds{1})=\mathsf{Q}_{1-b(s)^{2}}\,,\qquad\sum_{x}\mathcal{J}^{(s)}(x,\cdot)=I_{1-a_{2}(s)^{2}}\,.

(24)

Therefore, if the equality is attained in (
21
), then
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
is a joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
by an argument similar to those following (
15
) and (
18
).

3.2.
The second choice is unbiased with respect to the first choice

In the sequential measurement scheme considered before, the target meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
were arbitrary sharp meters. However, if the second meter
\mathsf{P}
is not completely arbitrary, but it is rather chosen among a restricted set of possibilities, we may be able to optimize the measurement of
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
by enforcing any available additional constraint on
\mathsf{P}
.
Obviously, the condition
\sum_{x}\mathcal{J}(x,\cdot)=I_{t}
does no longer apply in this case, as the choice of
\mathsf{P}
privileges some orthonormal bases. For example, if we know in advance that
\mathsf{P}
is unbiased with respect to
\mathsf{Q}
, we can employ the following
Lüders instrument

\mathcal{J}_{L}^{(s)}(x,\varrho)=\mathsf{Q}_{s}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varrho\mathsf{Q}_{s}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(25)

to perform a measurement of
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
. In this way, if we subsequently measure the unbiased meter
\mathsf{P}
, the resulting joint meter is

\mathcal{J}_{L}^{(s)\,{\dagger}}(x,\mathsf{P}(y))=\mathsf{G}^{(s)}(x,y)

(26)

for all
x,y
, i.e., the optimal approximate joint meter (
13
) of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
.
Therefore, when the unbiasedness condition is satisfied, the optimal approximate joint meter of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
can be implemented in a sequential measurement scheme, which is then the best possible sequential measurement scheme for the target meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}

[
5
]
. Due to the strict inclusion
\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},I)\subset\textrm{CR}(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
, this scheme allows a better approximation of the pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
compared with the case in which
\mathsf{P}
is arbitrary, even in the scenario where the choice of
\mathsf{P}
is postponed after the measurement of
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
. We thus achieve the second main result of the paper.

Result 2
.

Suppose the target pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
are chosen among all pairs of mutually unbiased sharp meters. Then, the two strategies consisting in

(i)

measuring an optimal approximate joint meter of a specific pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
, and

(ii)

performing a sequential measurement that is optimized for those pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
in which only
\mathsf{Q}
is fixed

yield the same trade-off between the achievable approximations of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
. When
\mathsf{P}
is unbiased with respect to
\mathsf{Q}
, there is hence an advantage in knowing
\mathsf{Q}
in advance, and the cloning strategy is no longer optimal in the scenario where the choice of
\mathsf{P}
is postponed until an approximate measurement of
\mathsf{Q}
has been performed.

Remarkably, if
(s,t)
attains the equality in (
21
), the Lüders instrument (
25
) is a joint instrument for the meter
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and the channel
I^{\prime}_{t}
given by

I^{\prime}_{t}(\varrho)=t\varrho+(1-t)\sum_{x}{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\mathsf{Q}(x)\right]\mathsf{Q}(x)\,.

(27)

This channel differs from a depolarizing channel
I_{t}
in that the depolarizing noise
\tfrac{1}{d}\mathds{1}{\rm tr}
of (
6
) is replaced by the
measure and prepare channel

\Phi_{\mathsf{Q}}
defined as

\Phi_{\mathsf{Q}}(\varrho)=\sum_{x}{\rm tr}\left[\varrho\mathsf{Q}(x)\right]\mathsf{Q}(x)\,.

(28)

If the second meter
\mathsf{P}
is mutually unbiased with respect to
\mathsf{Q}
, then the measure-and-prepare channel
\Phi_{\mathsf{Q}}
has the same effect on it as the depolaraizing channel.

4.
Overnoisy devices and extended compatibility regions

There are intriguing differences when we go out of the previously discussed noise model and we allow the noise parameters
s,t
to take also negative values.
Indeed, for any two sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
as in (
2

\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}
defined in (

, but also for sufficiently small negative values of
s
and

In fact,
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}
are valid meters also for
s,t
in the half-open interval

, where
m_{1}=-1/(d-1)
is the minimal value of

and

such that
\mathsf{Q}_{s}(x)\geq 0
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}(y)\geq 0
for all
x,y

s=t=m_{1}

\mathsf{Q}_{m_{1}}(x)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\mathds{1}-\mathsf{Q}(x)\right)\,,\qquad\mathsf{P}_{m_{1}}(y)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\mathds{1}-\mathsf{P}(y)\right)\,.

These are called the
reverse versions
of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
, respectively
[
10
]

in the open interval

(\mathsf{Q}_{s},\mathsf{P}_{t})
is then a convex mixture of the original pair

and the reverse pair

.

Similarly as with meters, the depolarizing channel

defined in (
6
) not only makes sense for

, but it is still a valid channel also for

, where

. Indeed,
I_{r}
is trace-preserving for all real
r
’s, and
r=m_{2}
is the minimal value such that
I_{r}
is a CP map
[
22
]
. The channel
I_{m_{2}}
is known to be the
structural physical approximation
of the
reduction map
of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, i.e., it is the closest channel to the positive trace preserving but not CP map
I_{m_{1}}
that sends any pure state
|\,\xi\,\rangle\langle\,\xi\,|
into the orthogonal state
[1/(d-1)](\mathds{1}-|\,\xi\,\rangle\langle\,\xi\,|)
(see
[
23
]
and references therein). In dimension
d=2
the channel
I_{m_{2}}
is therefore the best realizable approximation of the (hypothetical) universal quantum NOT gate, as described in
[
24
]
.

As we are mainly interested in pairs of quantum devices, we say that the product sets
[m_{1},1]\times[m_{1},1]
and
[m_{2},1]\times[m_{2},1]
constitute the squares of

for the pair

and the pair

, respectively (see Figure

).

– the same measurement for all

\mathsf{P}

by means of a channel
\Pi(\cdot\,|\,x):\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, thus obtaining the overall instrument
\mathcal{J}_{L,\Phi}^{(s)}
given by

(47)

(48)

for all states
\varrho
and using the identity (

\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
, a straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{L,\Phi}^{(s)\,{\dagger}}(x,\mathsf{P}(y))={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\bigg(a_{1}(s)+\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}b(s)\bigg)^{2}\mathsf{Q}(x)+\frac{1}{d}b(s)^{2}\mathsf{P}(y)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}b(s)\bigg(a_{1}(s)+\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}b(s)\bigg)\big(\mathsf{Q}(x)\mathsf{P}(y)+\mathsf{P}(y)\mathsf{Q}(x)\big)

for all
s\in[m_{1},1]
. Given any

, by picking
s\geq 0
such that the point
(s,t)
attains the equality in (

\mathcal{J}_{L,\Phi}^{(s)\,{\dagger}}(x,\mathsf{P}(y))=\tilde{\mathsf{G}}^{(t)}(x,y)

(49)

whenever

. On the other hand, we already observed that
\mathsf{G}^{(s)}=\tilde{\mathsf{G}}^{(t)}
for
t\geq 0
. In both cases the meter
\tilde{\mathsf{G}}^{(t)}
is obtained in a sequential measurement scheme in which we first perform a fixed measurement of
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and subsequently a measurement of
\mathsf{P}
, as claimed.

In dimension

the further relation

(\mathds{1}-2\mathsf{Q}(x))\mathsf{P}(y)(\mathds{1}-2\mathsf{Q}(x))=\mathsf{P}_{-1}(y)

holds for all

\mathcal{J}_{L,\Phi}^{(s)\,{\dagger}}(x,\mathsf{P}(y))=\mathsf{G}^{(s)}_{-}(x,y)

for all

\mathsf{G}^{(s)}_{-}

To finally show that, when

, we need to modify the definition of the conditional channel

and set

\Pi(\varrho\,|\,x)=\frac{1}{d-2}\sum_{z}(1-\delta_{x,z})\bigg(\frac{1}{d-1}\mathds{1}-\mathsf{Q}(z)\bigg)\varrho\bigg(\frac{1}{d-1}\mathds{1}-\mathsf{Q}(z)\bigg)\,,

where

and

yields

\mathcal{J}_{L,\Phi}^{(m_{1})\,{\dagger}}(x,\mathsf{P}(y))=\mathsf{G}_{\mathchoice{\mathrel{\makebox[7.0pt][c]{\rule{0.4pt}{7.5pt}\rule{5.0pt}{0.4pt}}}}{\mathrel{\makebox[7.0pt][c]{\rule{0.4pt}{7.5pt}\rule{5.0pt}{0.4pt}}}}{\mathrel{\makebox[5.5pt][c]{\rule{0.4pt}{5.25pt}\rule{3.5pt}{0.4pt}}}}{\mathrel{\makebox[4.0pt][c]{\rule{0.4pt}{3.75pt}\rule{2.5pt}{0.4pt}}}}}(x,y)\,.

(52)

This relation completes the proof of the final main result of the paper.

Result 4
.

Result
2
still holds true also if negative values of the noise parameters are allowed. Namely, when
\mathsf{P}
is known to be unbiased with respect to
\mathsf{Q}
, the strategy consisting in performing a sequential measurement that is optimized for those pairs
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
in which only
\mathsf{Q}
is fixed overcomes the cloning strategy and yields the same trade-off as measuring an optimal approximate joint meter of a specific pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})
.

5.
Proof of the main results

In this section we prove our main Theorems
2
and
4
by means of a simple symmetrization trick and few elementary facts from the theory of Weyl-Heisenberg groups and Fourier analysis. Before doing it, we need to introduce some further material about finite dimensional Weyl operators and the related covariance properties, see e.g.
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
for more details on the topic.

We start by observing that one can define a natural group structure on the outcome sets
X
and
Y
of the two sharp meters
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{P}
defined in (
2
). Indeed, both sets can be regarded as the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, i.e., the group of integer numbers
\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
in which the composition law is given by addition
{\rm mod}\,d
. If
\omega
denotes any fixed complex
d
th root of unity and
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the map
z\mapsto\omega^{xz}
is then a group homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
into the multiplicative group of unimodular complex numbers. For all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the
Weyl operator

W(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is defined as

W(x,y)\varphi_{z}=\omega^{yz}\varphi_{x+z}

(53)

for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. In this expression
\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{d-1}\}
is the orthonormal basis associated with
\mathsf{Q}
. The Weyl operators are unitary and satisfy the composition rule

W(x_{1},y_{1})W(x_{2},y_{2})=\omega^{x_{2}y_{1}}W(x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2})\,.

Therefore, the map
W:\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is a projective unitary representation of the direct product group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. Moreover, we have the commutation relation

W(x_{1},y_{1})W(x_{2},y_{2})=\omega^{x_{2}y_{1}-x_{1}y_{2}}W(x_{2},y_{2})W(x_{1},y_{1})\,.

For all
s\in[m_{1},1]
and
t\in[m_{2},1]
, the interplay between the Weyl operators and the meters
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and the channels
I_{t}
defined in the previous sections is given by the following covariance and invariance relations

\displaystyle W(x,y)\mathsf{Q}_{s}(z)W(x,y)^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{s}(x+z)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle W(x,y)I_{t}(\varrho)W(x,y)^{*}

\displaystyle=I_{t}(W(x,y)\varrho W(x,y)^{*})\,,

(55)

which hold for all
x,y,z
and
\rho
.

Lemma 1

(Symmetrization trick)
.

Suppose
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
are compatible. Then, there exists a joint instrument
\mathcal{J}
for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
such that

W(x,y)\mathcal{J}(z,\varrho)W(x,y)^{*}=\mathcal{J}(x+z,W(x,y)\varrho W(x,y)^{*})

(56)

for all
x,y,z
and
\varrho
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
be any joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
. For all
z
and
\rho
, we define

\mathcal{J}(z,\rho)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(z-x,W(x,y)^{*}\varrho W(x,y))W(x,y)^{*}\,.

It is easy to check that
\mathcal{J}
is an instrument which satisfies (
56
). Moreover,
\mathcal{J}
is a joint instrument for
\mathsf{Q}_{s}
and
I_{t}
, since

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(z,\mathds{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)\mathcal{J}^{\prime\,{\dagger}}(z-x,W(x,y)^{*}\mathds{1}W(x,y))W(x,y)^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)\mathsf{Q}_{s}(z-x)W(x,y)^{*}=\mathsf{Q}_{s}(z)\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{z}\mathcal{J}(z,\varrho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)\sum_{z}\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(z-x,W(x,y)^{*}\varrho W(x,y))W(x,y)^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)I_{t}(W(x,y)^{*}\varrho W(x,y))W(x,y)^{*}=I_{t}(\varrho)\,,

where the second equalities follow from (
54
) and (
55
).
∎

Instruments satisfying (
56
) are called
W
-covariant
and they were extensively studied in
[
30
]
. In particular,
[
30
, Theorem 1]
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between
W
-covariant instruments
\mathcal{J}
and
positive
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
-valued vector measures on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, i.e., maps
S:\mathbb{Z}_{d}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
\sum_{x}{\rm tr}\left[S(x)\right]=1
and
S(x)\geq 0
for all
x
. This correspondence is given by

\mathcal{J}(z,\rho)=\sum_{x}W(x,0)^{*}{\rm tr}_{\bar{1}}[(\mathds{1}\otimes\mathsf{Q}(z))L(\varrho\otimes S(x))L^{*}]W(x,0)\,,

(57)

where
L:\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
is the unitary operator

L=\sum_{u}\mathsf{Q}(u)\otimes W(u,0)\,.

(58)

By inserting (
58
) into (
57
) and using the cyclicity of
{\rm tr}_{\bar{1}}
with respect to the second factor in the tensor product, we find the equivalent expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(z,\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,u,v}W(x,0)^{*}\mathsf{Q}(u)\varrho\mathsf{Q}(v)W(x,0)

(59)

\displaystyle\times{\rm tr}\left[W(v,0)^{*}\mathsf{Q}(z)W(u,0)S(x)\right]\,.

From this equation, it is easy to show that
\mathcal{J}
is a joint instrument for the meter
\mathsf{M}
and the channel

which are given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u}{\rm tr}\left[\vphantom{\bigg[}\mathsf{Q}(z-u)\smash{\sum_{x}}S(x)\right]\mathsf{Q}(u)\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\Phi(\varrho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,u,v}W(x,0)^{*}\mathsf{Q}(u)\varrho\mathsf{Q}(v)W(x,0){\rm tr}\left[W(u-v,0)S(x)\right]

(61)

for all

and
\varrho
(actually, the derivation of

is already contained in
[
30

). By (

\mathsf{M}
is a

(or

of the sharp meter

q(z)={\rm tr}\left[\vphantom{\bigg[}\mathsf{Q}(z)\smash{\sum_{x}}S(x)\right]

On the other hand, formula (

for all

\sum_{u}\omega^{uv}=d\delta_{v,0}\,,

\displaystyle W(0,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\omega^{yz}\mathsf{Q}(z)\,,\qquad\mathsf{Q}(z)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{y}\omega^{-yz}W(0,y)\,,

\displaystyle W(x,0)

\mathsf{P}(z)=|\,\psi_{z}\,\rangle\langle\,\psi_{z}\,|=\mathcal{F}^{*}\mathsf{Q}(z)\mathcal{F}\,.

\Phi(\varrho)=\sum_{x,z}W(x,z)^{*}\varrho W(x,z){\rm tr}\left[\mathsf{P}(z)S(x)\right]\,.

In this expression we recognize the Kraus form of a

. In particular,

p(x,z)={\rm tr}\left[\mathsf{P}(z)S(x)\right]

(68)

for all
x,z
(see
[
32
]
).

6.
Concluding remarks

We have compared three different ways to perform an approximate joint measurement of two incompatible meters: by directly measuring an approximate joint meter, by implementing a sequential measurement scheme, and by making use of approximate cloning. In this chain of scenarios it is allowed to postpone the choice of an increasing number meters: none, one, and two, respectively. One would quite naturally expect that the larger is the level of choice, the worse becomes the quality of the approximation. However, we have shown that the question is more intricate. Indeed, we proved that the first two scenarios are equivalent if we restrict to target meters that are sharp and mutually unbiased, while the last two scenarios are equivalent if we only assume that the target meters are sharp. Remarkably, of these equivalences, only the first one extends to the case of overnoisy measurements, i.e., noisy measurements in which the intensity of the noise exceeds unity. All our results rely on a detailed analysis of the compatibility regions involved in the three scenarios, whose structure reveals a striking similarity for devices of different types (pairs of meters, pairs of channels or mixed meter-channel pairs). We have found that this similarity is broken only by overnoisy devices. Moreover, the qubit system emerges as a very special case when the noise intensity can exceed unity.

The target meters that we considered throughout this paper were always sharp and the noise model always consisted of the uniform noise. It is natural to ask whether our results extend also to unsharp meters, or, if not, for which class of unsharp meters they still hold true. Moreover, when dealing with unsharp meters, other noise models are available besides the uniform one
[
11
]
. It would be interesting to see whether the equivalences of the above scenarios depend or not on the considered noise model. Finally, further perspective may emerge by considering our results within the framework of compatibility witnesses. Indeed, the compatibility region of two mutually unbiased sharp meters was derived in
[
4
]
by constructing a suitable family of incompatibility witnesses and characterizing the incompatible pair of meters that are detected by each witness in the family. For the compatibility region of two copies of the identity channel, a similar approach was used in
[
33
]
only in the particular case of equally noisy devices. On the other hand, no connection between compatibility regions and incompatibility witnesses has still been explored in the mixed meter-channel case. Establishing such connection should most likely give more insight on the uniqueness of the optimal joint devices described in Section
4.2
, with implications for the possibility of determining them only from their action on (quantum or classical) subsystems.
