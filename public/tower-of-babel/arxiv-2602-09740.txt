Robust Vision Systems for Connected and Autonomous Vehicles: Security Challenges and Attack Vectors
Sandeep Gupta 1 and Roberto Passerone 2

Abstract
This article investigates the robustness of vision systems in Connected and Autonomous Vehicles (CAVs), which is critical for developing Level-5 autonomous driving capabilities. Safe and reliable CAV navigation undeniably depends on robust vision systems that enable accurate detection of objects, lane markings, and traffic signage. We analyze the key sensors and vision components essential for CAV navigation to derive a reference architecture for CAV vision system (CAVVS). This reference architecture provides a basis for identifying potential attack surfaces of CAVVS. Subsequently, we elaborate on identified attack vectors targeting each attack surface, rigorously evaluating their implications for confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA). Our study provides a comprehensive understanding of attack vector dynamics in vision systems, which is crucial for formulating robust security measures that can uphold the principles of the CIA triad.

Introduction

Connected and Autonomous Vehicles (CAVs) are set to redefine mobility by enhancing accessibility, convenience, efficiency, and safety through full driving automation, with projections estimating a global market capitalization of $7 trillion by 2050
[
6
,
63

. According to the SAE J3016 standards, automation levels range from 0 (no automation) to 5 (full automation)
[

. Automotive industries and technology firms are heavily investing in research and development of CAVs to reach Level-5 autonomy. The standard defines Level-5 as a fully autonomous driving system capable of performing dynamic driving tasks under all roadways and in all weather conditions, without the need for human intervention. Level-5 autonomy requires a reliable and secure vision system that can perceive both static and dynamic objects along their path serving as the eyes and ears of CAVs
[
70
,
4

.

In CAVs, vision systems are typically equipped with sensors such as cameras, LiDAR, RADAR, and ultrasonic sensors
[
65
,
40
]
. These sensors detect and interpret information about nearby objects, road conditions, traffic signs, pedestrians, and other vehicles, thereby generating a comprehensive view of the surroundings. Furthermore, CAVs may encounter road incidents or situations that are spontaneous, discrete, and self-contained, collectively referred to as
atomic road events
. Such incidents or situations can arise due to the approach of an emergency vehicle, pedestrian crossing or jaywalking, vehicle turning or lane changing, unplanned construction zone, vehicle breakdown or accident, and natural disaster or unexpected weather change. Consequently, a CAV requires a robust vision system for instantaneous situation understanding and response along with an alert broadcast to Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) that can ensure safety and prevent a disaster.

To attain Level-5 autonomy, a vision system must seamlessly integrate visual perception with cognitive processes, facilitating precise environmental interpretation, informed decision-making, and reliable autonomous operation without human intervention
[

. However, studies
[

3.1
Threat model

It is evident that the vision system plays a pivotal role in the precise estimation of short-term future trajectories by exploiting sensory data. However, studies
[
39
,
40
,
47
]
have reported that vision systems are vulnerable to various attacks, including
physical tampering
,
spoofing
, and
adversarial perturbations
, as well as being susceptible to
environmental factors
. Physical-world attacks can be executed by altering traffic signs with stickers, posters, or graffiti, or by disrupting LiDAR sensors with laser interference
[
4
]
. Spoofing attacks can be carried out by presenting falsified or manipulated images or videos, or by interfering with radar or LiDAR signals to compromise a vision system
[
49
]
.

Adversarial perturbations can be created by geometric transformations, like rotations or translations, and by noise generated using generative models, sensitivity analysis, or optimization algorithms
[
55
]
. Real-world examples, such as manipulated traffic signs, fabricated obstacles, and pedestrians wearing clothing with adversarial patterns, have demonstrated that adversarial attacks can significantly compromise the robustness of vision systems used in CAVs
[
76
]
. The weather (e.g., rain, snow, or fog) and lighting conditions can also degrade sensor performance
[
79
]
.

With machine learning at the core of vision system design, we delve into the challenges involved in leveraging ML for vision systems, focusing particularly on how attacks can affect the robustness of data-processing, classification, and prediction capabilities. Studies have reported that the threat model for ML primarily encompasses
causative attacks
that involve controlling training data, and
exploratory attacks
that aim to learn about the system, potentially to launch further attacks
[
71
]
. Additionally, there can be
untargeted attacks
, where the system predicts incorrectly for any input, and
targeted attacks
forcing a model to predict incorrectly for a particular label. We therefore consider the entire lifecycle of a typical vision system, including the design, development, and deployment phases, for investigating attack surfaces.

3.2.2
Data processing and computation layer

In this layer, data is first processed and fused for objects classification, and objects localization and mapping. The diverse data sources can provide a more accurate and comprehensive view of the CAV surroundings. Data fusion can be performed at three levels: low-level (raw data), mid-level (feature extraction), and high-level (decision-making)
[
65

. The object classification module determines the relevance of objects for tracking and the identification of the state of traffic signals. Machine learning (ML) is applied to transform, detect, and segment visual data to extract information for CAV navigation. Subsequently, the object tracking module initiates the measurement of distance and velocity of objects of interest using 3D point clouds provided by the object localization and mapping module
[
2
]
.

3.2.3
Motion estimation layer

In the motion estimation layer, the sensor data processed by the previous layer is exploited to determine a safe trajectory for CAV navigation
[
64
]
. The safe trajectory can be defined as a sequence of spatiotemporal waypoints in free space, represented by state variables such as position, orientation, and their corresponding rates of change: linear velocity, linear acceleration, angular velocity, and angular acceleration. The short-term future trajectory (STFT) is calculated based on the free space, i.e.,
spatial geometric zones
, and the corresponding sequence of space-related states, i.e.,
geometric waypoints
. Based on the STFT, the actuators decide whether to steer, accelerate, or brake the CAV. Table

Membership inference attacks can compromise confidentiality by potentially exposing sensitive information from the training data. These attacks aim to determine whether a specific data point was included in the training of a model
[
5
]
. The key idea is to build a machine learning attack model that uses the target model output (confidence values) to infer the membership status of the target model input
[
59
]
. Shadow models, for which the training and non-training samples are known, are used to generate the membership inference dataset. Successful attacks rely on two key assumptions, i.e., the structural similarity between the shadow and target models, and the comparability of their training data distributions.

Rezaei and Liu
[
51
]
investigate membership inference attacks on naturally trained models and datasets by exploiting additional information such as intermediate layer activations, input and weight gradients, and decision boundary distances. Song et al.
[
60
]
describe that membership inference attacks can result in sensitive information leakage. The authors determine that adversarially trained models are particularly vulnerable to such attacks, and information leakage correlates with model robustness and generalization. Furthermore, existing provable defenses that are effective against other attacks enable to address membership inference.

Logic corruption refers to the deliberate alteration of model parameters or structural components to induce incorrect or malicious behavior in a CAVVS
[
67
]
. For instance, the presence of adversarial traffic signs in the training data can compromise a CAVVS ability to accurately classify signs during real-time driving
[
32
]
. Data poisoning, backdoor, concept drift and model decay are potential threats for logic corruption compromising system integrity. Data poisoning attacks primarily fall into three categories: availability, targeted, and subpopulation attacks
[
15
]
.
As discussed in Section
5.1
, data poisoning involves injecting malicious data associated with a specific label or all classes into the training set to introduce biases or vulnerabilities into the model, which can later be exploited during inference. Adversaries aim to maximize the average test loss, thereby degrading model performance through an availability attack. In contrast, targeted attacks focus on causing misclassification in specific inputs, typically limited to one or a small number of test samples. Additionally, two attack types clean label and label flipping have been examined based on label control. A subpopulation attack targets specific subgroups within large, diverse datasets, aiming to degrade a model performance on those subgroups while maintaining overall performance.

Backdoor attacks
[
26
]
, which exploit training data with malicious triggers, can cause CAVVS to function normally on benign data but significantly degrade when activated by the same trigger during testing. Some backdoor attacks can be a specific type of data poisoning attack
[
32
]
. Concept drift can be described as unexpected changes in data distribution and relationships between features and the target variable over time
[
62
]
. This can be particularly problematic for CAVVS, as the system relies on multiple sensors, each of which can be susceptible to drift, potentially leading to degraded performance. On the contrary, model decay can be described as a degradation in the performance of a CAVVS, irrespective of changes in the data distribution
[
42
]
.

In general, side-channel attacks (SCA) exploit non-functional program behavior (e.g., memory, timing, power) to extract secret information
[
48
]
. SCA can compromise CAVVS confidentiality by stealing network structure and parameters (i.e., weights)
[
30
]
. SCA can compromise the integrity of CAVVS by altering the models weight. Xiang et al.
[
75
]
estimate DNN parameter sparsity via a side-channel attack, identifying DNN structures and deriving pre-trained parameter values in embedded devices. Wang et al.
[
72
]
simulate the dynamic power trace of mixed-signal Resistive Random-Access Memory (RRAM) in-memory computing (IMC) macros to perform side-channel analysis (SCA). The authors successfully extract the complete network architecture of DNN models stored within isolated memory blocks in an IMC system from the power trace of each tile, without any prior knowledge of the model.

These types of attacks, also referred to as decision-time attacks, are executed by adversaries who subtly modify inputs to deceive models and induce incorrect predictions

These attacks involve replacing original traffic signs or billboards with adversarial versions and drawing black strips or patterns to impair the object detection and traffic sign recognition capabilities of CAVVS. White-box methods primarily use gradient-based, optimization-based, and generative model-based techniques, exploiting the target model architecture and parameters to evade the system. In contrast, black-box methods rely on querying the model repeatedly to infer its behavior and generate adversarial inputs. Transfer-based methods, i.e., train adversarial examples on a surrogate model and then transfer them to the target model, score-based methods, i.e., query the target model multiple times to estimate the target model confidence scores for different inputs, and decision-based methods, i.e., manipulate input data to alter the model final decision, are common strategies for black-box evasion attacks

6.3
Atomic road events

We outline several atomic road events that present challenges to safe autonomous driving. CAVs can encounter these events due to sensor limitations, obstructed line-of-sight, vulnerabilities in deep learning models, and the lack of up-to-date high-definition maps, which hinder their ability to adapt to atomic road changes.
We consider in particular events that occur in object detection, during normal
situations and for unexpected situations.
Finally, we present experimental results validating these difficulties.

6.3.1
Object detection

•

Pedestrian Crosswalk Activation
: Detecting when pedestrians activate a crosswalk signal, indicating their intention to cross the road and requiring the attention of autonomous vehicles.

•

Pedestrian Jaywalking
: Detecting cases where pedestrians cross the road outside designated crosswalks or against traffic signals, requiring cautious navigation and potential intervention.

•

Emergency Vehicle Presence
: Identifying the presence of emergency vehicles, such as ambulances or fire trucks, with their corresponding lights and sirens activated, necessitating appropriate response and yielding.

6.3.2
Normal and spontaneous situations

•

Lane Change
: Detecting instances where a vehicle changes its lane, either to the left or right, indicating a potential change in trajectory and behavior.

•

Lane Departure
: Detecting cases where a vehicle unintentionally or abruptly leaves its designated lane, potentially indicating driver distraction or impairment.

•

Traffic Congestion
: Identifying instances of heavy traffic congestion, which can affect the speed and flow of vehicles and require appropriate navigation and planning strategies.

•

Vehicle Turn Signals
: Recognizing instances where a vehicle activates its turn signals, indicating an intention to turn left or right, and providing valuable information for predicting its future trajectory.

•

Weather Conditions
: Adverse weather conditions, such as snowstorms, sandstorms, or similar phenomena, can result in reduced visibility.

Experimental simulations

To evaluate these effects, we simulate some of these events to assess the
robustness of a pre-trained Inception-V3 model
[
50
]

that was trained on the ImageNet dataset.
We select the Inception model for its key architectural innovations, including
label smoothing to regularize the learning process, factorized
7\times 7

injects gradient information into earlier layers, enhancing both learning
efficiency and feature extraction capabilities.

Figure
14
presents the prediction results of the Inception-V3 model for an arbitrary input under simulated conditions. Initially classifying the input image as a racer with only 12.03% confidence, the model confidence increases to 72.57% in darker surroundings. However, in brighter conditions, the classification shifts to sports car, and sun flares cause misidentification as a planetarium, demonstrating the model sensitivity to lighting. The model exhibits significant sensitivity to simulated weather conditions as well. For example, under simulated snow, the vehicle is misidentified as a geyser, while simulated rain results in a misclassification as a worm fence. Foggy conditions too degrade detection accuracy, leading to the incorrect categorization of the racer as a trailer truck. Moreover, the model prediction accuracy is significantly affected by various environmental factors, including random shadows, extraneous objects or patches, and poor road conditions.

In summary, the observed sensitivity of advanced models to conditions beyond their normal range of operation emphasizes the need for further research to enhance the robustness of CAVVS.

7
Conclusions

CAVVS robustness is crucial for achieving Level-5 autonomy and accelerating the commercialization of CAVs. This article introduced a CAVVS reference architecture to provide a comprehensive understanding of perception, data processing, and motion estimation layers. A concise threat model is presented, identifying critical assets, vulnerabilities, attack surfaces, and potential exploitation techniques to comprehensively assess and analyze security risks within CAVVS design.

A simplified abstraction of the CAVVS system exposes three primary attack surfaces: data, models, and inputs. We delve into attack vectors targeting these surfaces, including data poisoning, exfiltration, extraction, logic corruption, inference attacks, side-channel attacks, evasion techniques, and man-in-the-middle attacks, that can compromise the CIA triad of CAVVS. Additionally, we examine atomic road events that can exacerbate security challenges and impact the object detection, classification, and prediction capabilities of CAVVS.

This research provides a valuable resource for security practitioners and researchers, offering insights into future research directions for the design of robust CAVVS systems essential for achieving Level-5 autonomy.
