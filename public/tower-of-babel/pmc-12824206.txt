Quantum avalanches in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain
Authors: Lv Zhang, Kai Xu, Heng Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recent numerical works have revealed the instability of many-body localized (MBL) phase in disordered quantum many-body systems with finite system sizes and over finite timescales. This instability originates from the rare Griffiths regions in the thermodynamic limit, which rapidly thermalize and induce an avalanche mechanism that destabilizes surrounding MBL regions. Here, we consider the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain, whose phase diagram is enriched by the presence of an intermediate ergodic phase separating two distinct MBL phases characterized by different long-range orders. We investigate the dynamic characteristics of the model when coupled to an infinite bath under perturbation, and through the scaling behavior of the slowest thermalization rate, we find how the critical disorder strength in finite-size systems is affected by the avalanche mechanism. We also implement the embedded inclusion model and use the time evolution of mutual information between each spin and the artificial Griffiths region to probe the diffusion of the thermal bubble. We observe that in finite-sized systems, the critical disorder strength gradually drifts away from the center. Our results show that both the MBL paramagnetic phase and MBL spin-glass phase are unstable at finite sizes.

Introduction

The many-body localized (MBL) phase in disordered, interacting quantum many-body systems1–6 has attracted widespread attention in recent decades, as it violates the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH)7–9. Anderson formulated the question of the possible absence of thermalization in interacting systems, while explicitly solving the non-interacting limit10. Subsequent works have suggested that systems in the MBL phase can be phenomenologically described by emergent quasi-integrability, where the information of the initial state is retained within local integrals of motion (LIOM’s)5,11–16, also known as “l-bits”. Consequently, the system does not thermalize under its own dynamics. Recent studies have revealed that the violation of the ETH can also occur in disorder-free systems, such as Stark MBL17–22. However, later work has pointed out that the localization phenomenon in Stark MBL stems from the Hilbert space fragmentation23–26, which differs from the mechanism underlying the disordered MBL systems27. Furthermore, a series of works have obtained the MBL phase on quantum simulation platforms28–35.

It has now been recognized that for finite-size disordered systems and over finite timescales, there may not exist a stable MBL phase, rather a MBL regime36,37. The results obtained from previous works, derived through exact diagonalization, do not accurately reflect the critical points at which the ergodic-MBL phase transition occurs. Although the disorder strength of a system exceeds the threshold determined by earlier researches, for sufficiently large system sizes and long time, the system may ultimately reaches its thermal equilibrium37,38. Ref39. has suggested that the instability of the MBL phase originates from local, weakly disordered regions, termed Griffiths regions40–43, which emerge in the thermodynamic limit due to statistic theory. These Griffiths regions are in the thermal phase, after rapidly thermalizing within a short period of time, gradually thermalize the surrounding areas, acting as the bath, expanding the range of the thermalization regions and introducing the avalanche mechanism37,44–49. At the same time, another work has discussed an alternative scenario in which localization is destabilized by the resonance proliferation near the transition50. A subsequent work revealed a connection between many-body resonances and the avalanche instability, showing that the avalanche primarily propagates through strong, rare near-resonant eigenstates of the closed system51. Recent numerical works have focused on discussing the propagation of avalanches in the typical MBL phase. One approach to probing the avalanche instability is to treat the remainder of the chain, excluding the Griffiths regions, as an open system, revealing that the critical point of the disordered Heisenberg chain drifts from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W^* \approx 8$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W^*>20$$\end{document} with increasing system sizes37,46. Another approach involves modeling the bath using the same structure as the rest of the chain but with different disorder strength, placing it in the ergodic phase47,49.

The one-dimensional non-interacting Ising Majorana chain is an important model in condensed matter physics, often used to study the Majorana edge states, as an effective model of p-wave superconductors, introduced by Kitaev52. The transition between ordered and disordered localized phases in the noninteracting Ising Majorana model was first proposed by Huse et al53. Over the following two decades, various forms of interaction were introduced into this model54–58. Recently, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain revealed a rich MBL phase diagram58–63. It has been highlighted that this model has two completely different MBL phases, including trivial MBL paramagnetic (PM) phase and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document} symmetry-breaking MBL spin-glass (SG) phase62. A few numerical works have shown that there is an emergent ergodic phase between two MBL phases with different long-range order properties, by calculating conventional landmarks like mean-level spacing ratio and half chain von-Neuman entropy. As the interaction strength increases, the extent of the ergodic phase continues to expand61–63.

Previous works have shown that the thermal phase in various MBL systems expands as the system size increases. For the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain, would the Markov method (applying dissipation at one end of the chain) and the non-Markov method (connecting an artificial Griffith region at one end of the chain) yield the same results? Do the drifts of the critical disorder strengths between the thermal phase and the two topologically distinct MBL phases in the model coincide, as expected from the duality? Moreover, in the non-Markov method, can the dynamics of the mutual information between the artificial Griffiths region and an individual spin reveal the destabilization of the MBL phase? In this paper, to address these questions, we calculated the slowest thermalization rate of the system, coupling with an infinite bath, in the dissipationless limit, and obtained the ergodic-MBL phase transition points (for both PM MBL phase and SG MBL phase) with different system sizes37,46. Meanwhile, we artificially set one end of the interacting Ising Majorana chain to be in ergodic phase47, equivalent to a Griffiths region in the thermodynamic limit, and calculated the dynamic evolution of mutual information between each spin in the rest of the chain and the effective bath.

Methods

Avalanche instability theory

In this subsection, we briefly review the avalanche instability theory37,39 in disordered quantum MBL systems. The theory postulates that the MBL systems can be destabilized by a few emergent thermal inclusions, so called Griffiths regions, in the thermodynamic limit. These Griffiths regions thermalize rapidly, regarded as thermal bubbles, and destabilize the surrounding typical MBL chains by serving as heat reservoirs. The fate of a thermal bubble is corresponded to its thermalization rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document}39,68. Consider a Griffiths region of length N within a sufficiently long one-dimensional spin chain, when this thermal bubble extends over L spins in each direction, the typical level spacing of the enlarged thermal inclusion is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{-(N+2L)}$$\end{document} (as it has thermalized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N+2L$$\end{document} spins in total). If the typical level spacing is smaller than the thermalization rate, the spin would not recognize the discreteness of the spectrum, i.e., the thermal bubble serves as a bath effectively. So the avalanche will not stop until the thermalization rate and the typical level spacing have similar energies, indicating that the scaling behavior of the thermalization rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma \sim g^{-L}$$\end{document} is important. According to the typical level spacing we have discussed before, the point where the transition occurs is at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g=4$$\end{document}.

The probability of obtaining a Griffiths region decreases exponetially with its length N:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(N)\sim \mathscr {O}(e^{-N}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}leading us to a reasonable assumption that there is no direct interaction between any two Griffiths regions. Despite the fact that the entire long chain constitutes an isolated system, after the rapid thermalization of the Griffiths region, we can focus solely on the typical MBL region and regard the Griffiths region as an infinite bath which connects at one end of the MBL chain. In this case, the dynamic features of the MBL chain are governed by the Master equation. The non-steady eigenmodes of the Master equation can reflect the effect of the Griffiths region on the MBL chain, and the thermalization rate can be estimated from the slowest relaxation mode (the eigenvalue with the second-largest real part). In addition, we directly simulate the time evolution of the full chain using the embedded thermal inclusion model47, in which a short spin chain in the thermal phase is artificially attached to one end of a finite MBL chain. The diffusion of the thermal bubble is then probed through the mutual information between each spin in the MBL chain and the effective bath (the artificial Griffiths region).

Markov process

To study avalanche instability, we assume that the system couples with an infinite bath. However, the completely different long-range order properties of its eigenstates in different MBL phases will result in distinct dynamic behaviors when the chain is connected to an infinite bath. When the chain is deeply in PM MBL phase, the eigenstates are product states of the z-direction basis at each spin, which do not exhibit long-range correlations. Therefore, the thermalization of neighboring spins by the bath in the early stage does not affect the spins at the far end. However, when the chain is in deep SG MBL phase, the eigenstates are “Cat-state” along x-direction, which possess a strong long-range correlation. In this case, the thermalization of nearby spins by the bath in the early stage also affects the spins at the far end. This suggests that as we increase the size of the chain, the SG MBL phase requires a larger amount of disorder, compared to the PM MBL phase, to protect this long-range structure from being disrupted, indicating that its critical disorder is more sensitive to the size of system.

Discussion

In this paper, we have investigated the impact of the avalanche instability, originating from Griffiths regions in the thermodynamic limit, on the one-dimensional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain using two complementary approaches.

We first treat the Griffiths region as an effective infinite bath after a short time evolution, thus the rest of the chain is an open system and its dynamics is governed by the Master equation. In the dissipationless limit, we have extracted the slowest thermalization rate, which is related to the thermalization rate that stems from the Griffiths regions. Our results are shown in Fig. 3, where for each system size, the rescaled thermalization rate decreases as the system penetrates deeper into the MBL phase. The crossing points between the curves of adjacent system sizes serve as estimators of the critical points. The data were fitted with a quadratic form, and the crossing points of different MBL phases and different system sizes are shown in Fig. 4. We performed a linear fit between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{lg}L$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2|\textrm{lg}W_{JC}|$$\end{document}. What we can determine is that, in the range of system sizes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$7\le L\le 12$$\end{document}, the critical disorder strength between the thermal phase and the two MBL phases drifts away from the center (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_J=W_h=1$$\end{document}) and the localization of the SG MBL phase is more unstable compared to that of the PM MBL phase, as its slope \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} is larger. However, it remains unclear whether this drift continues to follow the same scaling behavior at larger system sizes, and thus we cannot determine whether a stable MBL phase persists in the thermodynamic limit. Although our data are more consistent with the scenario in which the critical disorder strength ultimately does not converge, we cannot exclude the possibility that stable MBL phases may still exist at larger sizes.

We have also directly studied the dynamical properties of the original model, which is additionally connected to an artificial Griffiths region. The mutual information equation (10) between each spin outside the Griffiths region and the effective bath is used to probe the entanglement and the spread of avalanches. The results shown in Fig. 5 reveal that in a low-disorder system, thermalization gradually propagates from the end connected to the artificial Griffiths region to the opposite end. However, in a highly disordered system (i.e., away from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_J=1$$\end{document}), the spread of thermalization halts at a specific location. These phenomena clearly indicate the existence of an ergodic-to-MBL phase transition. We can roughly estimate the time at which the mutual information between the spin closest to the Griffiths region and the effective bath begins to decline as a signature when thermalization propagates through at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_A+c$$\end{document} spins. Fig. 6, showing the time evolution of mutual information for different system sizes at a fixed disorder strength, illustrates that the MBL PM phase is destabilized by avalanches, and that larger disorder is required to preserve localization as the system size increases. These conclusions are in agreement with our previous analysis of the scaling behavior of the slowest thermlization rate when the system is coupled to an infinite bath.

Our results indicate that the critical points of ergodic-MBL (both PM MBL phase and SG MBL phase) phase transition are not stable and these boundaries would drift away from the center for small system sizes, where the system is in ergodic phase, showing the similar behavior to different models from previous works37,46. In previous work63, the critical disorder was estimated by computing the typical localization length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi _b$$\end{document} from the end-to-end correlations of the system’s eigenstates and comparing it with the avalanche criterion39 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi ^* = (\ln 2)^{-1}$$\end{document}. In addition, other ED-based indicators, such as the area to volume-law entanglement transition and the Poisson–GOE change in the parity-resolved spectral statistics, yield a consistent critical value. While the typical localization length analysis incorporates avalanche-based reasoning, all these diagnostics are nevertheless based on static spectral properties of a closed system. By contrast, our approach, whether through a boundary Lindblad bath or via coupling to an artificial Griffiths region, is intrinsically dynamical. An apparent discrepancy is observed between our results and the previously reported ED results63 for closed systems. We also note that a similar discrepancy between static ED diagnostics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W\simeq <span class='crossLinkCiteEqu'>3</span>.1$$\end{document}) and Lindblad-based dynamical signatures (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W>7$$\end{document}) has been reported previously in standard random-field Heisenberg model37. These observations lead us to reasonably conjecture that the discrepancy originates from the intrinsic difference between static ED probes and open-system dynamical approaches. However, recent work73 has shown that in non-interacting Anderson and Aubry-André-Harper (AAH) models, the critical disorder strength obtained via coupling to a thermal bath exhibits non-monotonic behavior with respect to system size. This suggests that for the interacting many-body system studied here, although the critical disorder exhibits monotonic behavior within computationally accessible system sizes, the employed Lindblad bath method may lead to an overestimation of the critical disorder strength in the thermodynamic limit. Consequently, our work can deepen the understanding of the effects of avalanche in one-dimensional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_2$$\end{document}-preserving interacting Ising Majorana chain. Additionally, the study of the dynamic features can facilitate the design of the experiments to directly obtain the avalanches and thermal bubbles that spread in disordered quantum many-body systems.
