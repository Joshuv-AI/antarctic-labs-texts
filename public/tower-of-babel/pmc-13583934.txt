Venous thromboembolism in women on estrogen therapy: presentation, management, and outcomes in the RIETE registry
Authors: Andreas Verstraete, Thomas Vanassche, Angeles Blanco-Molina, Juan Criado-Garcia, Quentin Van Thillo, Montserrat Pérez-Pinar, Judith Catella, Saskia Middeldorp, Peter Verhamme, Manuel Monreal, RIETE Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Women with estrogen therapy–associated VTE are younger and have a low risk for recurrence in the first year after stopping anticoagulation.•Bleeding during anticoagulation is more frequent among women than among men.

Introduction

The use of combined oral contraceptives (COCs) is a well-established risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE), and the risk is influenced by both the type and dose of estrogen and progestin.1, 2, 3, 4 Among women of reproductive age, the baseline incidence of VTE without hormonal contraception is approximately 2 per 10 000 person-years. COCs increase this risk nearly fivefold (odds ratio [OR], 4.6; 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.2-5.0).3 Although the absolute incidence remains relatively low at about 10 per 10 000 person-years, the widespread use of COCs translates into a substantial number of VTE cases in otherwise healthy young women. COCs contribute to up to 50% of VTE cases in this population.5, 6, 7

The pathophysiology of COC-associated VTE is incompletely understood. Inherited thrombophilias significantly increase the VTE risk in COC users. Heterozygous carriers of factor V Leiden or prothrombin G20210A variants face a three- to sevenfold increased risk for a first VTE, which rises markedly with concurrent COC use (OR, 19.3 and 24.0, respectively).8 However, only about 25% of COC-associated VTE cases have an identified inherited thrombophilia.9 Estrogens exert procoagulant effects by increasing the levels of coagulation factors II, VII, VIII, IX, X, fibrinogen, and von Willebrand factor and reducing the levels of antithrombin and protein S.10, 11, 12, 13 These effects are dose-dependent and are attenuated at lower estrogen doses. COCs also induce acquired activated protein C resistance.14,15 In contrast, progestins have a limited effect on coagulation but may modulate the prothrombotic effects of estrogens.15

The optimal management of COC-associated VTE remains uncertain. Questions persist regarding both the acute and long-term management, including whether COCs can be continued during anticoagulation after VTE diagnosis. In practice, many clinicians discontinue COCs because of concerns for thrombus progression or early recurrence.16 However, abrupt discontinuation may severely exacerbate menstrual bleeding during anticoagulation, thereby negatively affecting quality of life.

The risk for recurrence after COC-associated VTE is also debated. A meta-analysis of 14 studies involving 3112 women reported a pooled recurrence rate of 1.57 per 100 patient-years after stopping anticoagulation.17 In studies with follow-up periods beyond 5 years, the recurrence rate was 1.42 per 100 patient-years, but these included only 1119 women, and only 1 study provided a 10-year follow-up in 318 women. Thus, the long-term recurrence risk beyond 10 years remains uncertain, particularly because new risk factors, such as pregnancy, may arise later in life.

This study aimed to provide real-world data on the clinical presentation, management, and outcomes of women younger than 50 years of age with VTE during estrogen therapy in comparison with men and with women who had VTE not associated with estrogen therapy.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Registro Informatizado Enfermedad TromboEmbólica (RIETE) registry is an ongoing, international, prospective, cohort study that is enrolling patients with objectively confirmed acute VTE across more than 200 hospitals worldwide. As a noninterventional registry, RIETE reflects routine clinical practice at each center. The registry was approved by the reference ethics committee at the Hospital Universitari Germans Trias i Pujol (RIETE, Version 3, 17 March 2017; Ref. CEI PI-17-053), and participation by each center complied with local ethics committee and institutional requirements. All patients provided informed consent and were followed for a minimum of 3 months. A detailed description of the rationale, design, and methodology has been described elsewhere.18

The RIETE registry was initiated in 2001, but for this study, we included patients who were enrolled between 2011 and 2024, because information on the type of hormonal therapy was available only from 2011 onward. We included patients between 10 and 49 years without active cancer and with acute VTE (defined as lower extremity deep vein thrombosis [DVT], pulmonary embolism [PE], cerebral vein thrombosis, or splanchnic vein thrombosis). Active cancer was defined as a cancer diagnosis within the 3 months preceding VTE or ongoing cancer management at the time of VTE diagnosis, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy, surgery, supportive care, or watchful waiting. We excluded patients with isolated superficial thrombophlebitis, upper extremity DVT, catheter-related VTE, renal vein thrombosis, ovarian vein thrombosis, and retinal vein thrombosis. Women who used progestin-only contraceptives (eg, progestin-only pills, progestin-releasing intrauterine devices, implants) were also excluded from this study because of the small sample size (n = 186). Women with pregnancy- or postpartum-associated VTE were not excluded. We compared clinical presentation, treatment, and outcomes among the following 3 groups: (1) women with estrogen-associated VTE, defined as those who actively used any estrogen-containing therapy (see variable definitions) at the time of VTE diagnosis; (2) men with VTE; and (3) women with VTE unrelated to estrogen therapy.

Data source and variable definitions

The data were entered locally via a standardized electronic case report form and were transmitted to the RIETE coordinating center where data management and quality control are centralized. Systematic data checks ensured consistency and accuracy, and queries were resolved through direct communication with the participating sites.

The baseline characteristics, VTE risk factors, laboratory values, treatments, and outcomes were collected prospectively. Immobility was defined as ≥4 consecutive days of bed rest within 2 months before a VTE diagnosis. Surgeries that were performed within 2 months before the VTE event were classified as either minor or major (ie, orthopedic, vascular, cardiac, or neurosurgical procedures). Prolonged travel was defined as ≥6 hours within the 3 weeks preceding VTE diagnosis. Pregnancy was considered a risk factor if the patient was pregnant at the time of diagnosis or had given birth within the preceding 2 months. Estrogen therapy included all estrogen-containing formulations, including COCs, vaginal rings, transdermal patches, and other preparations.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the incidence of recurrent VTE, bleeding events, and mortality during short- and long-term follow-up. All outcomes were adjudicated and confirmed by the local physician. Self-reported events required physician confirmation for inclusion. Recurrent VTE was defined as any new symptomatic VTE event that occurred after the initial diagnosis. Bleeding events were classified as major if they were overt and required the transfusion of 2 or more units of packed red blood cells, involved a critical site (such as retroperitoneal, spinal, or intracranial locations), or were fatal.

We evaluated outcomes based on the 2 phases of VTE management as defined by the 2020 American Society of Hematology (ASH) guidelines, namely the primary treatment phase (up to 6 months after the index event) and the secondary prevention phase thereafter.19 During the primary treatment phase, events were counted from the index event through 6 months or until discontinuation of anticoagulation, whichever occurred first. Events that occurred during extended anticoagulation beyond 6 months after the index event were not included in the primary treatment phase analysis. In the secondary prevention phase, the outcomes were evaluated only after discontinuation of anticoagulation.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the potential impact of pregnancy-associated VTE on outcomes in women with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE, we repeated the primary outcome analyses after stratifying this group by the presence or absence of a recent pregnancy.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis in a larger cohort of RIETE participants who were enrolled from 2001 onward and compared (1) women with hormonal therapy–associated VTE, (2) men with VTE, and (3) women with non–hormonal therapy–associated VTE, acknowledging that the type of hormonal therapy was not specified before 2011.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical data relied on χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. For continuous variables, we used the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. The incidence rates of the outcomes, specifically VTE recurrence, bleeding, and mortality, are reported as events per 100 patient-years with 95% CIs and were compared using the Exact mid-p test. P values are shown without adjustment for multiple testing, and P values of ≤.05 were considered statistically significant. Additional analyses included stratification by treatment phase and anticoagulant type, and subgroup analyses were stratified by relevant clinical variables (eg, presence of additional risk factors). A multivariable Cox regression model with death as a competing risk was used to identify predictors of recurrent VTE after estrogen-associated VTE. The covariates were preselected based on biologic plausibility (age, weight, initial VTE presentation, additional risk factors, personal and family history of VTE, smoking status, thrombophilia testing, and duration of anticoagulation). Univariate comparative analyses (eg, baseline characteristics and incidence rates) were performed using available-case data only. In the multivariable Cox regression model, missing binary variables were imputed as negative, and weight was handled with multiple imputation.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 91 636 patients enrolled in the RIETE registry between 2011 and 2024, we identified 13 418 patients between 10 and 49 years without cancer and with acute VTE, including 7069 men and 6349 women (Figure 1). Among the women, 2786 (44%) developed VTE while using estrogen-containing hormonal therapy. Most patients were recruited in Europe (87%) with 68% of those enrolled in Spain. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Women with VTE during estrogen therapy were younger (mean age, 32 years ± 9) than both the men (39 years ± 8; P < .001) and women with non–estrogen-associated VTE (38 ± 8; P < .001) with a peak incidence between 25 and 29 years (supplemental Figure 1). PE was more common in this group than in women with non–estrogen-associated VTE (59% vs 53%, respectively; P < .001) and occurred at a similar frequency than that in men (57%). Among patients with PE, high-risk presentation (defined by a systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg) occurred in 3.6% of women with estrogen-associated VTE as compared with 1.8% of men and 2.6% of women with non–estrogen-associated VTE. An unusual-site VTE occurred in 2.6% of women with a VTE during estrogen therapy, most often cerebral vein thrombosis (1.8%), which occurred more frequently in this group than in men (0.6%; P < .001) but at a similar frequency as in women with non–estrogen-associated VTE (1.7%). Estrogen-associated VTE was less often accompanied by additional transient risk factors, such as recent immobilization, surgery, pregnancy, or prolonged travel, when compared with the other groups (26% vs 38% vs 50%; P < .001), although the prevalence of additional transient risk factors increased with a longer duration of estrogen use (20% in users of <1 year vs 32% in users of >3 years). A personal history of VTE was reported in 2.2% of these women, and a family history was reported in 17%.

Combined contraceptives were the predominant estrogen-containing therapy and were used by 97% of women with estrogen-associated VTE (Table 2). The median interval from the initiation of estrogen therapy to a VTE diagnosis was 13 months (IQR, 4-48 months). Nearly half (47%) of these events occurred within the first year of estrogen use. The risk persisted over time; 9% of cases occurred between 2 and 3 years after starting therapy, and 30% occurred after more than 3 years of estrogen therapy. The maximum observed interval was 36 years.

Thrombophilia testing

Thrombophilia testing was performed more frequently in women with VTE during estrogen therapy (56%) than in men (36%; P < .001) or women with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE (39%; P < .001; Table 1). However, the results were more often negative in this group than in the other 2 groups (61% vs 49% vs 50%; P < .001). Approximately one-quarter (24%) of women with estrogen-associated VTE carried factor V Leiden or the prothrombin G20210A variant, which was similar in frequency to that observed in men (25%) and women (24%) with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE. Antithrombin, protein C, and protein S deficiencies were found in 1.0%, 1.4%, and 4.4% of women with estrogen-associated VTE, respectively.

Anticoagulant treatment and the management of estrogen therapy

Most patients (60%) with estrogen-associated VTE were treated with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), whereas approximately 25% received direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), with or without initial heparin derivative (supplemental Table 2). Rivaroxaban was the most commonly used DOAC. Invasive interventions (mainly thrombolysis for PE or DVT) were used in 6.2% of these women as opposed to in 3.8% of men and 3.8% of women with non–estrogen-associated VTE (P < .001).

Most women with a VTE during estrogen therapy received short-term anticoagulation (median duration, 6 months; IQR, 4-9 months), and 17.7% received extended anticoagulation beyond 1 year, which was lower than that in men (24.3%) and women with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE (21.9%; P < .001). Supplemental Table 3 summarizes the factors associated with extended anticoagulation beyond 6 months.

Estrogen therapy was abruptly discontinued at the time of VTE diagnosis in 92% of women, and this rate remained stable throughout the study period (Table 2; supplemental Table 4).

Outcomes during primary anticoagulant treatment

Among 2783 women with estrogen-associated VTE, 14 DVT and 7 PE recurrences occurred during primary anticoagulant treatment (≤6 months), corresponding to 1.88 events per 100 patient-years (95% CI, 1.19-2.82; Table 3; Figure 2A). The median time to on-treatment recurrence was 0.9 months (IQR, 0.3-1.5). The on-treatment recurrence rate was lower among women with estrogen-associated VTE than among men (4.08 per 100 patient-years; 95% CI, 3.38-4.89; P < .001) and among women with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE (3.05 per 100 patient-years; 95% CI, 2.22-4.08; P = .067).

The major and nonmajor bleeding rates in women with estrogen-associated VTEs were 2.06 (95% CI, 1.33-3.04) and 6.85 (95% CI, 5.44-8.53) per 100 patient-years, respectively (Table 3). Overall, women had higher bleeding rates than men, mostly owing to uterine bleeding (Figure 2B). The bleeding rates differed between those treated with DOACs and those treated with VKAs, and the greatest difference was observed among women with estrogen-associated VTE. In this group, any bleeding occurred at a rate of 17.6 per 100 patient-years (95% CI, 13.0-23.3) among DOAC-treated patients as opposed to a rate of 5.00 per 100 patient-years (95% CI, 3.54-6.87) among those treated with VKAs, a difference that was larger than that observed in men (5.33 vs 4.31 per 100 patient-years) and women with non–estrogen-associated VTE (13.6 vs 7.58 per 100 patient-years; supplemental Table 5).

All-cause mortality was lowest in women with estrogen-associated VTE (0.53 per 100 patient-years), although the rates of fatal initial PE were similar across the groups (0.36 events per 100 patient-years in women with estrogen-associated VTE vs 0.25 in men with VTE vs 0.36 in women with non-estrogen-associated VTE).

Outcomes after discontinuation of anticoagulation

Follow-up data after stopping anticoagulation were available for 1410 of 2786 (51%) women with an estrogen-associated VTE, 2671 of 7069 (38%) men, and 1537 of 3563 (43%) women with non–estrogen-associated VTE. The median follow-up time after stopping anticoagulation ranged from 9.1 to 10.0 months.

Recurrence after discontinuation occurred at a rate of 2.07 events per 100 patient-years (95% CI, 1.57-2.68) among women with a VTE during estrogen therapy (median time to recurrence, 23 months; Table 4; Figure 2C). Most recurrences were DVT (37/54; 69%). Supplemental Table 6 provides the multivariable model for recurrence after an estrogen-associated VTE. Only a family history of VTE was significantly associated with an increased risk for recurrence (hazard ratio, 3.31; 95% CI, 1.07-10.2). There was no significant difference in the recurrence rates between women with an estrogen-associated VTE with additional transient risk factors and those without additional transient risk factors (1.81 vs 2.16 events per 100 patient-years; rate ratio, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.64-2.36).

Sensitivity analyses

Stratification of the non–estrogen therapy-associated female group by pregnancy status showed that the recurrence rates after stopping anticoagulation were lower among women with pregnancy-associated VTE (2.55 per 100 patient-years; 95% CI, 1.45-4.17) than among those without a recent pregnancy (5.92 per 100 patient-years; 95% CI, 4.81-7.21) (supplemental Table 7). After pregnancy-associated VTE, the bleeding rates during primary anticoagulation were comparable with those in women with estrogen therapy–associated VTE (any bleeding: 8.13 vs 8.70 per 100 patient-years). The primary conclusions regarding recurrence and bleeding outcomes were unchanged after exclusion of women with pregnancy-associated VTE from the non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE group.

In a larger analysis of hormonal therapy–associated VTE, including all patients without cancer under 50 years recorded in RIETE from 2001 to 2024, the findings were consistent; women with hormonal therapy–associated VTEs had the lowest recurrence rate when compared with men and with women with non–hormonal therapy–associated VTE (supplemental Table 8).

Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort study of patients without cancer and with acute VTE under 50 years of age, women with VTE during estrogen therapy were younger and less likely to have additional transient risk factors than men and women with non–estrogen-associated VTE. Most women discontinued estrogen therapy at VTE diagnosis. During anticoagulation, women experienced a higher bleeding risk than men, largely driven by uterine bleeding. After stopping anticoagulation, women with estrogen-associated VTE had the lowest recurrence rate.

The recurrence rate after stopping anticoagulation in women with estrogen-associated VTE was 2.07 per 100 patient-years, which was lower than that observed in men and women with non–estrogen-associated VTE and consistent with earlier, smaller cohorts.17,20, 21, 22 The median time to recurrence was nearly 2 years, underscoring that early recurrences are uncommon. The younger age of this group and lower prevalence of persistent risk factors likely contributed to this low risk for recurrence, although data beyond 10 years remain limited. Nevertheless, the low recurrence rate within the first year, when risk is highest, is consistent with the ASH recommendations for short-term anticoagulation (3-6 months) after a first estrogen-associated VTE event.19 Real-world data from this registry reinforce this practice, showing a median treatment duration of 6 months with only 18% of women continuing anticoagulation beyond 1 year.

Nearly half of the estrogen-associated VTE cases occurred within the first year after starting estrogen-containing therapy with a median time to event of 13 months. This pattern is consistent with earlier studies that described a front-loaded risk for VTE after estrogen initiation.23, 24, 25 However, more than half of the VTE events occurred after the first year, indicating a persistent risk over time.

The underlying predisposition remains unknown in most women with VTE during estrogen therapy. The 2023 ASH guidelines suggest thrombophilia testing in this setting and extended anticoagulation for patients with identified thrombophilia.26 Our study predominantly reflects thrombophilia practices before this guideline implementation. Conventional thrombophilia testing was performed in 56% of women with a VTE during estrogen therapy, which led to the identification of an inherited risk factor in only 39% of cases. Emerging approaches, such as multigene panel testing, may improve the detection of occult thrombophilia and provide further mechanistic insights into estrogen-associated VTE.27

Women experienced higher bleeding rates than men during anticoagulation, which was driven primarily by uterine bleeding. Although abrupt discontinuation of estrogen therapy at VTE diagnosis might be expected to worsen menstrual bleeding in the estrogen-associated group, the bleeding rates were higher in women with non–estrogen-associated VTE. This difference persisted after the exclusion of women with pregnancy-associated VTE, arguing against pregnancy-related bleeding as an explanation. Potential contributors to this excess bleeding risk include older age, a greater comorbidity burden, and more frequent use of DOACs.

In our cohort, the bleeding rates in women were substantially higher with DOACs than with VKAs, and the greatest difference was observed among women with estrogen-associated VTEs. These findings support the concern that abrupt discontinuation of estrogen therapy may exacerbate uterine bleeding during anticoagulation in premenopausal women, particularly among those who are receiving DOACs.28, 29, 30 This differential bleeding risk warrants cautious interpretation given the potential for confounding by indication and temporal bias in this observational data set and should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, continuation of estrogen therapy during anticoagulant treatment may mitigate this bleeding risk. Although the evidence is limited, this approach is generally considered safe. A subgroup analysis from the EINSTEIN DVT and PE trials showed similar recurrence rates among women with and those without concomitant hormonal therapy (3.7 vs 4.7 events per 100 patient-years, respectively).28 The rates of abnormal uterine bleeding were not substantially different between estrogen users (21.4 events per 100 patient-years) and those who did not use estrogens (28.9 events per 100 patient-years), but this comparison may be confounded, because estrogens are often prescribed to prevent or treat heavy menstrual bleeding and menstrual bleeding is often underreported.

Despite their younger age and lower burden of comorbidities, women with estrogen-associated VTE had similar rates of fatal initial PE as other groups, showing that the disease severity in this population should not be underestimated. Preventive strategies include improving VTE risk stratification, prescribing safer contraceptives, such as progestin-only formulations for at-risk women, evaluating the use of progestin-only products in women without VTE risk, and educating women about VTE symptoms to enable early diagnosis and treatment. In addition, lower estrogen doses and natural estrogens may reduce the thrombotic risk.2 Notably, in our cohort, 2% of women with estrogen-associated VTE had a personal history of VTE, which is a contraindication to estrogen use.31

Our study extends the existing evidence in several ways. First, to our knowledge, this was the largest study on VTE during estrogen therapy and included 2786 women with follow-up data after anticoagulation discontinuation available for 1410. Second, we provided a direct comparison with both men and women with non–estrogen therapy-associated VTE, thereby enabling a more clinically relevant characterization than previously available. Third, we provided detailed real-world insights into the bleeding patterns, anticoagulation duration, and management of estrogen therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, it is subject to the inherent constraints of observational research, including potential selection and reporting biases and missing data. Follow-up after anticoagulation discontinuation was available for only about half of the women with estrogen-associated VTE. However, the baseline characteristics were similar between women with and those without follow-up data available with the exception of a slightly higher rate of previous VTE among those without follow-up data (2.8% vs 1.6%). In the multivariable model, missing binary variables were imputed as negative, which may have led to the attenuation of associations if missingness did not reflect true absence. Second, about 20% of women with non–estrogen therapy–associated VTE had pregnancy-associated events, which share hormonal and pathophysiologic features with VTE during estrogen therapy. Sensitivity analyses indicated that inclusion of these cases attenuated the between-group differences, because recurrence and bleeding patterns after pregnancy-associated VTE more closely resembled those observed in estrogen therapy–associated VTE. Third, many patients, especially those with estrogen-associated VTE, were treated with VKAs, reflecting the high proportion of patients enrolled in Spain where DOACs are not routinely reimbursed and the inclusion of patients from the pre-DOAC era. Fourth, very few women continued estrogen therapy during anticoagulation, thereby preventing the analysis on the recurrence and bleeding outcomes in this subgroup. Data on the estrogen indication, type, dosage, and the resumption of (alternative) contraceptives were also unavailable. Fifth, the median follow-up time after stopping anticoagulation in women with estrogen-associated VTE was 10 months. Because most women on estrogen-containing contraceptives do not develop VTE, those who do develop VTE most likely have an underlying prothrombotic predisposition and remain at risk for recurrence in the presence of future risk factors, such as pregnancy. Large-scale studies with follow-up data beyond 10 years are warranted to accurately assess the long-term recurrence risk.

In conclusion, women who developed VTE during estrogen therapy were younger and frequently discontinued estrogen therapy at the time of the VTE diagnosis. Bleeding during anticoagulation was more frequent in women than in men. Women with estrogen-associated VTE had a low risk for recurrence in the first year after discontinuation of anticoagulation if estrogen therapy was stopped. Future studies are needed to assess the long-term risk for recurrence beyond the first decade after VTE and to evaluate strategies for the acute and long-term management of contraceptives.
