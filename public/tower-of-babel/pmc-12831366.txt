ZDHHC9 and spermine metabolism: a palmitoylation-driven pathway to prostate carcinogenesis
Authors: Chunli Chen, Yiqun Zhang, Gaofa Wang, Yang Liu, Bin Ye, Shengqiang Zhou, Kelong Wang, Haojie Zheng, Xiaoxia Dong, Chao Luo, Junshuo Zhou, Kangkang Zeng, Caiyun Fang, Li Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpermine is a natural metabolite that has been shown to hinder the progression of prostate cancer. Protein palmitoylation, a recently identified post-translational lipid modification, is catalyzed by a family of 23 enzymes known as zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyltransferases (ZDHHCs). This study primarily investigates the role of ZDHHC9 in spermine metabolism and its potential association with prostate cancer.MethodsWe employed immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, and RT-qPCR techniques to assess ZDHHC9 expression in prostate cancer tissues and cell lines. The connection between ZDHHC9 and prostate cancer growth was analyzed using xenograft models, CCK-8 assays, and colony formation experiments. Additionally, we utilized mass spectrometry and RNA sequencing to elucidate how ZDHHC9 modulates spermine metabolism.ResultsZDHHC9 was found to be upregulated and appeared to function as an oncogene in prostate cancer. Both in vitro and in vivo functional experiments confirmed that ZDHHC9 facilitated prostate cancer proliferation and enhanced spermine catabolism in a palmitoylation-dependent manner. Mechanistically, ZDHHC9 interacted with hnRNPU and increased its protein stability through S-palmitoylation at the Cys497 and Cys607 residues. Moreover, the S-palmitoylation of hnRNPU mediated by ZDHHC9 could elevate the expression of SAT1, an enzyme responsible for spermine catabolism, by promoting its transcription.ConclusionsOur findings establish a new correlation between palmitoylation, spermine metabolism, and prostate carcinogenesis. Targeting the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU-SAT1 signaling axis might provide a novel therapeutic strategy for prostate cancer.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07589-7.

Background

Prostate cancer refers to an epithelial malignancy that occurs in the prostate gland. With approximately 1.5 million new cases worldwide, it ranks as the second most frequently diagnosed cancer among men [1]. Despite the availability of several treatment options, including radical prostatectomy, hormonal therapy, and radiotherapy, prostate cancer continues to be a significant public health issue. Therefore, new therapeutic targets for prostate cancer are urgently needed.

A key feature of malignancy is metabolic reprogramming [2]. Cancer cells often alter their metabolism to meet increased energy and nutrient demands. Polyamine metabolism produces small cationic molecules such as putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, all of which are necessary for cell growth and survival [3]. Dysregulated polyamine metabolism in tumors can manifest as increased biosynthesis, decreased catabolism, enhanced uptake of extracellular polyamines, or a combination of these factors [4]. However, unlike the accumulation of polyamines observed in most tumors, prostate cancer exhibits a notable reduction in spermine levels [5, 6]. Previous studies have indicated that spermine is inversely related to the malignant transformation of prostate cancer [7, 8]. As a result, it is vital to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms that govern spermine metabolism in prostate cancer.

Palmitoylation is a crucial post-translational lipid modification of proteins [9]. This process involves the attachment of a saturated 16-carbon palmitate to specific cysteine residues via a thioester bond [10]. Palmitoylation can affect protein stability, function, membrane trafficking, and subcellular localization [11]. The most common form of palmitoylation is S-palmitoylation, which is facilitated by zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyltransferases (ZDHHCs) [12]. Recent studies have revealed a strong association between ZDHHC-driven palmitoylation and malignant tumors [13–15]. Notably, elevated expression of ZDHHC7 and ZDHHC14 could suppress the proliferation of prostate cancer cells [16, 17]. However, the potential substrates that are palmitoylated by ZDHHCs in prostate cancer remain largely unexplored. Furthermore, whether high expression of ZDHHCs contributes to prostate tumorigenesis has not been reported.

This study began with an extensive examination of the ZDHHC family gene expression using public databases, identifying ZDHHC9 as a tumor-promoting gene in prostate cancer. Our findings confirmed that ZDHHC9 modulated the S-palmitoylation of hnRNPU(Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein U), which enhanced the stability of SAT1(Spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1) mRNA, ultimately activating spermine catabolism and promoting prostate cancer growth. The ZDHHC9- hnRNPU-SAT1 axis could be useful in preventing, diagnosing, and treating prostate cancer.

Methods

Data retrieval

Public databases, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), were used for gene expression analysis. The status of gene mutations was assessed through the cBioPortal platform.

Tissue microarray, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence

Prostate cancer tissue microarrays (HProA150PG03) were purchased from Outdo BioTech (China). The microarray comprised 94 tumor samples and 56 normal samples. Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to a previously established protocol [18]. Staining scores were assigned on a scale from 0 to 4 for low expression and from 5 to 12 for high expression. Immunohistochemistry images were captured using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). For immunofluorescence, tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA) and subsequently blocked using 5% BSA (Sigma, USA). Primary and fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were added, followed by staining with DAPI (Sigma, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

The following antibodies were used: Rabbit anti-ZDHHC9 (Cat# PA5-56868, Invitrogen, USA) and Cy5-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG(Cat# ab6564, Abcam, USA).

Cell culture, transfection, and lentivirus infection

The human prostate cancer cell lines (DU145, PC-3, 22RV1, and LNCaP), the immortalized prostate epithelial cell line (PZ-HPV-7), and the human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293T) were obtained from Procell BioTech (China). All cells underwent STR identification. PC-3, 22Rv1, and LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA). DU145 and HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM medium(Gibco, USA). PZ-HPV-7 cells were grown in Keratinocyte-SFM medium (Invitrogen, USA). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were kept under standard culture conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

For cell transfection, pcDNA-ZDHHC9, pcDNA-SAT1, pcDNA3.1, hnRNPU-WT, hnRNPU-S497, hnRNPU-S607, ZDHHC9-WT, and ZDHHC9-C169A plasmids were purchased from Vigene BioTech (China).The aforementioned plasmids were introduced into prostate cancer cells with the assistance of the jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus, France). PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 0.5 × 106 cells per well. Each well received a combination of 2 μg plasmid DNA, 200 μL jetPRIME buffer, and 4 μL jetPRIME transfection reagent. For RNA interference, short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) oligonucleotides targeting ZDHHC9 (sh-ZDHHC9), hnRNPU (sh-hnRNPU), or scrambled control shRNA (sh-NC) were cloned into the lentivirus vector pLKO0.1-puro (Vigene BioTech, China). Lentiviral particles were packaged in HEK293T cells. PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells (2 × 105) were infected with the lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Stable cell lines were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma, USA).

RT-qPCR, RIP-qPCR, and Western blotting

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China) and a SYBR Green PCR Kit (Vazyme, China). RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was conducted using a Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, Germany). The RIPA lysis buffer, BCA protein assay kit, SDS-PAGE gels, PVDF membranes, and ECL reagent used for Western blotting were obtained from Beyotime (China). The experimental procedures for RT-qPCR, RIP-qPCR, and Western blotting are detailed in previous reports from our group [18, 19]. Primers are shown in Table S1.

Primary and secondary antibodies were as follows: ZDHHC9 (Cat# PA5-56868, Invitrogen, USA); hnRNPU (Cat# 14,599-1-AP, Proteintech, China); SAT1 (Cat# 10,708-1-AP, Proteintech, China); HA-Tag (Cat# 51,064-2-AP, Proteintech, China); FLAG-Tag (Cat# 66,008-4-AP, Proteintech, China); Rabbit IgG (Cat# 30,000-0-AP, Proteintech, China); Mouse IgG (Cat#B900620, Proteintech, China); GAPDH (Cat#10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China); HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (Cat# SA00001-2, Proteintech, China); and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Cat# SA00001-1, Proteintech, China).

CCK-8 and colony formation assays

Approximately 1,000 cells were placed in each well of 96-well plates. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 (Cell Counting Kit-8) kit (Dojindo, Japan). The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Bio-Tek Instruments, USA). For colony formation, cells were grown in 6-well plates (1,000 cells per well) and stained with 1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China) after two weeks. Quantification was achieved by counting the number of cell colonies.

Mice xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice, aged 4 to 6 weeks, were sourced from Xishan Biotechnology in Suzhou, China. All mice were kept in a pathogen-free environment at the Experimental Animal Center of Hubei University of Medicine (Shiyan, China). PC-3 cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of nude mice. Tumor size was assessed weekly. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: tumor volume = 0.5 ×length × width2. After 4 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and tumor tissues were excised, measured, and weighed.

LC-MS/MS, RNA-seq, and RIP-seq

The proteins isolated by immunoprecipitation were separated using SDS-PAGE and stained with silver. RNA samples that showed no degradation were selected for subsequent sequencing. According to the conditions mentioned in an earlier study, we conducted the LC-MS/MS(liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry), RNA-seq (RNA sequencing), and RIP-seq (RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing) experiments [19]. Quality control, sequencing, and data processing were carried out by Novogene in Beijing, China. We set a 1.5-fold change as the differential expression threshold and p < 0.05 as the significance threshold.

Spermine detection

Spermine was analyzed using a commercial ELISA kit (FineTest, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions for each step. The enzyme-substrate reaction was terminated by the addition of an acid solution. The color change was measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 450 nm. The concentration of spermine was then determined by comparing the optical density of the samples to the standard curve.

Co-IP and Co-IF

Co-IP (Co-immunoprecipitation) was executed using the Co-IP kit from Pierce (USA). Briefly, equal quantities of proteins were incubated with either anti-ZDHHC9 or anti-hnRNPU antibodies. The immunoprecipitated complexes were treated with protein A/G agarose beads. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. In the process of Co-IF (Co-immunofluorescence), paraformaldehyde was employed to fix the cell slides or tissue sections, followed by permeabilization using Triton X-100.

The following antibodies were used:Rabbit anti-ZDHHC9(Cat# PA5-56868, Invitrogen, USA); Mouse anti-hnRNPU (Cat# ab10297, Abcam, USA); Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG(Cat# ab150113, Abcam, USA); and Cy5-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG(Cat# ab6564, Abcam, USA).

RNA and protein decay assays

Following established methods, 10 µg/ml Actinomycin D (Act-D, Sigma, USA) was used to study RNA stability, while 100 µg/ml cycloheximide (CHX, Sigma, USA) was used to evaluate protein stability [19]. After pretreatment with Act-D or CHX, relative RNA and protein expression levels in prostate cancer cells were measured using RT-qPCR or Western blotting, respectively.

ABE assay

Protein palmitoylation was detected using the acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) method with the IP-ABE Palmitoylation Kit for Western Blotting (AIMSMASS, China). In brief, cells were lysed in a lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 50 mM N-ethylmaleimide, immunoprecipitated with an anti-hnRNPU antibody (Cat#14599-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and then mixed with agarose beads. After being washed three times, the precipitates were divided into two equal portions, with one half designated as the -HAM (hydroxylamine) control and the other half exposed to +HAM. The precipitates underwent SDS-PAGE analysis, and palmitoylated hnRNPU was identified using HRP-conjugated streptavidin (Beyotime, China).

Statistical analysis

Using GraphPad Prism software, all statistical analyses were conducted. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant, as determined by either a Student’s t-test or a one-way ANOVA.

Results

ZDHHC9 is significantly overexpressed in prostate cancer

To examine whether ZDHHC9 is potentially relevant to prostate cancer, we downloaded the expression profiles of ZDHHC genes from the TCGA and GTEx databases (Figure S1). We observed that ZDHHC9 was the only member of the ZDHHC family exhibiting significant expression (p < 0.01 and fold change > 2) in prostate cancer tissues, which suggests a close link to prostate cancer (Fig. 1a, Figure S2). Further investigation into ZDHHC9 expression across various tissues and organs revealed elevated levels in multiple tumors, implying its potential role as an oncogene (Figure S3). The GEO database also provided evidence supporting the overexpression of ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer (Fig. 1b). Through the cBioPortal database, we discovered that gene amplification was the most common alteration of ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer samples (Fig. 1c). In prostate cancer single-cell sequencing data (GSE176031), the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm depicted nine cell subsets, with ZDHHC9 showing high expression in the malignant cells (Fig. 1d–f). We then employed immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1g) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 1h) to confirm the increased expression of ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer specimens. We further assessed ZDHHC9 expression in cells derived from human prostate cancer. Compared to the normal cell line, the prostate cancer cell lines exhibited a marked elevation in both ZDHHC9 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1i and j). ZDHHC9 promotes prostate cancer growth in a palmitoylation-dependent manner

To investigate whether ZDHHC9 accounts for prostate cancer development, the plasmids sh-NC, sh-ZDHHC9#1, and sh-ZDHHC9#2 were constructed. RT-qPCR (Fig. 2a) and Western blotting (Fig. 2b) revealed that the shRNA plasmids targeting ZDHHC9 were able to suppress ZDHHC9 expression in PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells, with sh-ZDHHC9#1 demonstrating the highest knockdown efficiency. Thus, this plasmid was chosen for further experiments and designated as sh-ZDHHC9. It was noted that reducing ZDHHC9 expression impeded the proliferation of prostate cancer cells, as evidenced by the CCK-8 (Fig. 2c) and colony formation (Fig. 2d) assays. Moreover, subcutaneous tumors were established in BALB/c-nude mice using the PC-3 cell line with a stable ZDHHC9 knockdown. The size of the tumors was recorded weekly following the injection (Fig. 2e). After four weeks, the tumors were removed and weighed (Fig. 2f). It was observed that knocking down ZDHHC9 could repress the growth of prostate cancer in vivo (Fig. 2g). Immunohistochemical analysis of xenograft tumors showed that Ki67, a marker of cell proliferation, was positively associated with ZDHHC9 expression (Fig. 2h and i). We also achieved ZDHHC9 overexpression by utilizing the pcDNA-ZDHHC9 plasmid (Figure S4a-b). Functional experiments indicated that ZDHHC9 overexpression resulted in enhanced growth of PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells (Figure S4c-d). Given that ZDHHC9 is the enzyme responsible for palmitoylation, we explored whether the oncogenic function of ZDHHC9 depends on its palmitoylation activity. Notably, the application of 2-bromopalmitate (2-BP), a general inhibitor of palmitoylation, inhibited the proliferation of prostate cancer cells overexpressing ZDHHC9 (Fig. 2j and k). It has been reported that the Cys169 residue is essential for the catalytic activity of ZDHHC9 [20]. We generated the mutant plasmid ZDHHC9–C169A and introduced it into PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells. We discovered that the C169A mutant also caused a decline in cell growth (Fig. 2l and m). ZDHHC9-driven palmitoylation enhances spermine catabolism in prostate cancer

RNA-seq was conducted on PC-3 cells with ZDHHC9 knockdown to explore the mechanisms behind ZDHHC9-induced prostate carcinogenesis. Volcano plots illustrated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that had a fold change greater than 1.5 and a P-value less than 0.05 (Fig. 3a). Significant enrichment of the identified genes was observed in the categories of ‘metabolic process’ (GO:0008152), ‘cellular metabolic process’ (GO:0044237), ‘cell proliferation’ (GO:0008283), and ‘regulation of cell growth’ (GO:0001558) (Fig. 3b). Further analysis through KEGG demonstrated that these DEGs were involved in the ‘arginine and proline metabolism’ (hsa00330) pathway as well as the proliferation- related ‘PI3K-AKT signaling pathway’ (hsa04151)(Fig. 3c). Moreover, we acquired RNA-seq data for prostate cancer from the TCGA database and analyzed it using the GSVA package in R software.The Spearman correlation test suggested possible links between ZDHHC9 expression and both the ‘tumor proliferation signature’ and ‘arginine and proline metabolism’ (Figure S5a-c). Polyamine metabolites, such as putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, are important components of arginine metabolism (Fig. 3d). Since spermine acts as a tumor suppressor in prostate cancer, we decided to concentrate our research exclusively on spermine. To elucidate how ZDHHC9 affects spermine metabolism, we transfected PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells with sh-ZDHHC9 vectors. We discovered that suppressing ZDHHC9 expression resulted in elevated levels of spermine compared to control cells (Fig. 3e). Pretreatment with 2-BP also led to increased spermine levels (Fig. 3f). Conversely, DENSpm, a compound involved in spermine catabolism, mitigated the increase in spermine caused by the knockdown of ZDHHC9 (Fig. 3g) and the addition of 2-BP (Fig. 3h). Meanwhile, DENSpm alleviated the inhibitory effects on cell colony formation due to the silencing of ZDHHC9 (Fig. 3i). Intriguingly, the enhanced colony-forming abilities resulting from ZDHHC9 upregulation were reversed following exogenous spermine treatment (Fig. 3j). ZDHHC9 interacts with and upregulates hnRNPU expression

To detect proteins that might interact with ZDHHC9, immunoprecipitates from PC-3 cells were separated using SDS-PAGE and then silver-stained (Fig. 4a). The stained bands were excised and subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry. We identified hnRNPU, an RNA-binding protein, as a potential interacting partner of ZDHHC9 (Fig. 4b). Previous studies have demonstrated that hnRNPU is overexpressed in numerous tumors; however, the association between hnRNPU and prostate cancer has yet to be explored [21–23]. Our analysis of the TCGA and GTEx databases indicated that hnRNPU levels were elevated in prostate cancer (Figure S6). In HEK293T cells, Flag-hnRNPU was able to pull down HA-ZDHHC9, indicating an interaction between these two exogenously expressed proteins (Fig. 4c). Using co-IP experiments, we confirmed that endogenous ZDHHC9 and hnRNPU could bind to each other in both PC-3 and 22Rv1 prostate cancer cells (Fig. 4d). By performing Co-IF assays on prostate cancer tissue sections and cell lines, we noticed that ZDHHC9 and hnRNPU co-localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4e and f). We therefore speculated that there was a strong correlation between hnRNPU and ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer. To test this, we evaluated the impact of ZDHHC9 on hnRNPU expression. A reduction in the hnRNPU protein levels was observed when ZDHHC9 was downregulated, while its overexpression resulted in increased levels of the hnRNPU protein (Fig. 4g, Figure S7). Nevertheless, ZDHHC9 did not change the expression of hnRNPU mRNA (Figure S8). To determine whether ZDHHC9 confers prostate carcinogenesis by modulating hnRNPU, sh-hnRNPU and control plasmids were introduced into PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 4h). We proved that knocking down hnRNPU reversed the tumor-promoting effects of ZDHHC9 overexpression on prostate cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 4i). Furthermore, decreasing hnRNPU mitigated the decline in spermine levels caused by the upregulation of ZDHHC9 (Fig. 4j). ZDHHC9 palmitoylates hnRNPU at cysteines 497 and 607 to increase its protein stability

Since ZDHHC9 is a palmitoyltransferase, we propose that hnRNPU could be a key target modified by ZDHHC9. To verify this hypothesis, we applied the ABE method to measure the palmitoylation of the hnRNPU protein. We found that the endogenous hnRNPU protein exhibited strong palmitoylation signals in both PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 5a). After exposure to 2-BP, the palmitoylation levels of hnRNPU markedly decreased, indicating that hnRNPU undergoes S-palmitoylation through thioester bonds (Fig. 5b). Meanwhile, altering ZDHHC9 expression had a notable impact on hnRNPU palmitoylation, with knockdown reducing and overexpression enhancing the palmitoylation signals (Fig. 5c).We then used SwissPalm, a tool for studying palmitoylation, along with AlphaFold, a protein structure prediction tool, to investigate possible palmitoylation sites on hnRNPU. We discovered that cysteines 497 and 607 in the human hnRNPU protein were palmitoylated (Fig. 5d). Subsequently, we mutated these sites to serine residues (Fig. 5e). We noticed that the mutant plasmids hnRNPU-C497S and hnRNPU-C607S were capable of blocking the S-palmitoylation of the hnRNPU protein in prostate cancer cell lines (Fig. 5f). Notably, the ZDHHC9 mutant lacking catalytic activity (C169A) was unable to facilitate hnRNPU palmitoylation as effectively as the wild-type ZDHHC9 (Fig. 5g). These results support that hnRNPU is a substrate for ZDHHC9-mediated S-palmitoylation, with cysteines 497 and 607 being important sites. We explored whether the stability of the hnRNPU protein is affected by ZDHHC9-mediated palmitoylation, which may alter its expression. As expected, CHX experiments showed that knocking down ZDHHC9 reduced the stability of the hnRNPU protein (Fig. 5h, Figure S9). Similarly, the stability of hnRNPU was diminished by the hnRNPU mutants (C497S and C607S) (Fig. 5i). Given the positive impact of the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU axis on prostate carcinogenesis, we investigated whether hnRNPU palmitoylation is involved in this process. Cellular (Fig. 5j) and animal (Figure S10) experiments revealed a significant reduction in tumor growth following mutations at the palmitoylation sites of hnRNPU. Mutating the palmitoylation sites of hnRNPU also led to a marked increase in spermine levels (Fig. 5k). The ZDHHC9-hnRNPU axis facilitates spermine catabolism by increasing SAT1 expression

To clarify how the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU axis controls spermine catabolism, we conducted RNA-seq on PC-3 cells following the knockdown of hnRNPU (Fig. 6a). GO enrichment analysis indicated that numerous DEGs (p < 0.05, fold change > 1.5) were linked to ‘regulation of cell growth’ (GO:0001558) and ‘metabolic process’ (GO:0008152) (Fig. 6b). Because hnRNPU is a well-known RNA-binding protein, RIP-seq was carried out in PC-3 cells to determine which mRNAs could directly bind to hnRNPU (Fig. 6c). We observed that hnRNPU-bound transcripts were enriched in the GO terms of ‘regulation of cellular metabolic process’(GO:0031323), ‘regulation of metabolic process’ (GO:0019222) and ‘regulation of cell proliferation’ (GO:0042127) (Fig. 6d). By intersecting hnRNPU-enriched mRNAs, hnRNPU-related mRNAs, and ZDHHC9-associated mRNAs, only one overlapping gene, SAT1, was identified (Fig. 6e). Notably, SAT1 is a key enzyme catalyzing spermine catabolism [24]. Thus, we selected SAT1 for additional research. Examination of TCGA data uncovered a link between SAT1 and either hnRNPU or ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer (Figure S11). RT-qPCR and Western blotting analyses demonstrated that SAT1 was positively regulated by ZDHHC9 and hnRNPU in PC-3 and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6f). Simultaneously, the combination of hnRNPU with the SAT1 transcript was validated using RIP-qPCR assays (Fig. 6g) and Integrative genomics viewer (IGV) plots (Fig. 6h). Subsequently, we employed Act-D and discovered that reducing hnRNPU decreased the half-life of SAT1 mRNA (Fig. 6i). We also observed that increasing SAT1 expression could reduce the elevated spermine levels resulting from the knockdown of hnRNPU (Fig. 6j) or ZDHHC9 (Figure S12). In addition, enhancing SAT1 expression reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of hnRNPU knockdown on prostate cancer cell growth (Fig. 6k). Discussion

In this investigation, a considerable enhancement of ZDHHC9 expression was noted in prostate cancer. As a member of the palmitoyltransferase family, ZDHHC9 facilitated the S-palmitoylation of hnRNPU at Cys497 and Cys607, leading to increased expression of the downstream gene SAT1. Through a network of protein-protein and protein-RNA interactions, ZDHHC9 was shown to enhance spermine catabolism and stimulate prostate cancer growth. This study reveals a novel mechanism underlying prostate carcinogenesis linked to S-palmitoylation and spermine metabolism.

Increasing evidence indicates that ZDHHC9 is crucial for numerous biological processes, particularly in cancer development and progression. ZDHHC9 is overexpressed and functions as an oncogene in many types of human malignant tumors, including bladder cancer [25], glioblastoma [20], pancreatic cancer [26], and breast cancer [27]. High ZDHHC9 expression facilitates tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion, marking it as an important therapeutic target. This study systematically examined the expression patterns of ZDHHC family members and identified ZDHHC9 as a possible contributor to prostate cancer. By using immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, Western blotting, and RT-qPCR analyses, it was confirmed that ZDHHC9 was highly expressed in prostate cancer tissues and cell lines. Gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments demonstrated that ZDHHC9 enhanced prostate cancer cell proliferation in vitro and promoted tumor growth in vivo. These findings strongly support the tumor-promoting role of ZDHHC9 and enrich our understanding of the importance of this gene. The application of a palmitoylation inhibitor and the mutation of the enzyme’s active site revealed that ZDHHC9 could drive prostate cancer growth in a palmitoylation-dependent manner. This report is the first to emphasize the correlation between ZDHHC9-mediated palmitoylation and prostate cancer.

RNA-binding proteins represent a broad class of proteins that regulate multiple biological processes and cellular functions. It is well documented that tumorigenesis and tumor progression are significantly affected by these proteins [28, 29]. The stability, localization, and activity of RNA-binding proteins are controlled by post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, ubiquitination, arginine methylation, and acetylation [30, 31]. However, the relationship between palmitoylation and RNA-binding proteins remains inadequately explored. Through mass spectrometry, Co-IP, and double immunofluorescence analyses, we identified hnRNPU as an interacting partner of ZDHHC9 in prostate cancer cells. By performing ABE and CHX assays, we confirmed that ZDHHC9 could modulate the palmitoylation of hnRNPU at the specific sites Cys497 and Cys607, which are crucial for maintaining the protein stability of hnRNPU. Evidence from numerous studies points to hnRNPU as a key biomarker for cancer diagnosis and treatment [21–23, 32]. Our findings demonstrated that ZDHHC9 accelerated prostate cancer growth by interacting with and increasing the expression of hnRNPU.In this context, the function of hnRNPU deserves further in-depth investigation. Consequently, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms behind hnRNPU palmitoylation will deepen our insight into how this protein governs malignant behaviors in tumors. Meanwhile, further exploration of RNA-binding proteins that can be palmitoylated by ZDHHCs is required.

The RNA-binding protein hnRNPU is involved in controlling transcriptional expression, RNA splicing, RNA stability, and chromatin structure in a tissue-dependent manner [33]. Our study revealed that SAT1 was a direct target gene of hnRNPU in prostate cancer. According to RIP-seq and RIP-qPCR assays, hnRNPU exhibited a specific interaction with SAT1 mRNA. Meanwhile, hnRNPU positively regulated SAT1 expression by influencing the stability of SAT1 mRNA. We thus ascertained that SAT1 was an important downstream effector of hnRNPU in the process of prostate tumorigenesis. Current studies indicate that SAT1 is the primary rate-limiting enzyme responsible for the acetylation of spermidine and spermine during polyamine catabolism [34, 35]. Elevated SAT1 expression leads to the production of N-acetylspermidine and N-acetylspermine, which are subsequently secreted from the cells. Abnormal SAT1 expression has been found in many malignancies and is linked to the modulation of tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion [36]. Here, we verified that the knockdown of ZDHHC9 or hnRNPU resulted in the intracellular accumulation of spermine by inhibiting the expression of SAT1. This research uncovered a previously unknown function of the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU-SAT1 pathway in maintaining spermine metabolism. Undoubtedly, additional in vivo and in vitro experiments are needed to validate these findings. Simultaneously, more clinical samples are required to assess the potential of the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU-SAT1 axis as a diagnostic marker and a therapeutic target for prostate cancer.

Conclusions

In summary, ZDHHC9 is overexpressed in prostate cancer, indicating its potential role in this disease. ZDHHC9 and its interacting protein, hnRNPU, collaborate to influence the proliferation and spermine metabolism of prostate cancer cells. Mechanistically, hnRNPU is modified by ZDHHC9 through S-palmitoylation at specific cysteine residues (positions 497 and 607). Additionally, hnRNPU contributes to the stabilization of SAT1 mRNA, which enhances the catabolism of spermine. This study reveals a new connection between palmitoylation and spermine metabolism, highlighting the importance of the ZDHHC9-hnRNPU-SAT1 axis in prostate carcinogenesis.
