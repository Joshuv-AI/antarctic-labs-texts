Control strategy for offshore wind power HVDC transmission system based on series-connected DRU-MMC topology
Authors: Huichen Gan, Huangqing Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper investigates control strategies for large-scale offshore wind power high-voltage direct current (OWP-HVDC) transmission systems employing a series-connected diode rectifier unit and modular multilevel converter (DRU-MMC) topology. First, a comprehensive onshore-offshore coordinated reduced-voltage startup strategy is proposed. By leveraging the bidirectional conduction capability of the MMC, this method enables smooth system energization and safe grid connection of WTGs. Furthermore, to enhance steady-state performance under wind power variations, an enhanced DC voltage control strategy is developed for the offshore MMC, supported by a detailed small-signal stability analysis and rigorous validations under DC and AC grid disturbances. A comparative analysis is then conducted among three distinct grid-forming control schemes: fixed AC voltage control, active power control, and the proposed DC voltage control. Simulation studies on the large-scale OWP-HVDC system based on the hybrid converter reveal that fixed AC voltage control fail to properly regulate DC voltages of the hybrid converter, resulting in severe MMC DC voltage ripples of up to 14.4 %. In contrast, the proposed DC voltage control strategy precisely stabilizes the MMC DC voltage, limiting the ripple to a mere 2.9 % while maintaining bus voltage fluctuations within acceptable 1.3 %. These results indicate that DC voltage control is the most robust grid-forming strategy for series-connected DRU-MMC systems, offering superior power allocation and enhanced protection against over-modulation.Keywords: Offshore wind power, Hybrid converter, HVDC transmission, Diode rectifier unit, Modular multilevel converterSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Offshore wind power (OWP) has emerged as a critical pillar of the global energy transition due to its abundant resources and vast spatial availability1,2. Currently reaching capacities of up to 26 MW, these massive units significantly reduce the levelized cost of electricity by maximizing energy capture and effectively distributing life-cycle costs3. As the exploitation of large-scale deep sea wind farms accelerates, the development of efficient and high-capacity transmission systems becomes imperative to reliably deliver renewable energy to the onshore power grid4,5.

Diode rectifier units (DRUs) offer substantial advantages, including low power losses, compact footprints, light weight, high cost-effectiveness, and simplified control architectures6. When applied to large-scale offshore wind high-voltage direct current (HVDC) transmission, DRU-based systems can achieve an 80% reduction in platform volume, a 65% reduction in weight, and a 30% saving in capital expenditure compared to traditional modular multilevel converter (MMC)-HVDC systems7. However, the unidirectional conduction and uncontrollable nature of DRUs preclude them from establishing the offshore AC voltage. Moreover, offshore wind farms (OWFs) cannot absorb power from the onshore grid to facilitate startup8. These substantial requirements prevent the direct application of standalone DRUs in conventional offshore wind HVDC systems9.

To enable DRU-based OWP-HVDC transmission, two primary technical routes have been investigated. The first approach relies on advanced control of the wind turbine generators (WTGs) to achieve self-startup and grid-forming capabilities10,11. The second approach utilizes auxiliary equipment to establish the offshore AC grid, facilitating WTG startup, synchronization, and essential reactive power/ harmonic compensation12. A prominent example of this second route is the DRU-MMC hybrid HVDC system13. Compared to the first approach, the hybrid configuration allows for the utilization of conventional grid-following WTG control strategies, offering a high degree of technological readiness.

Based on the topology, DRU-MMC hybrid HVDC systems are generally categorized into parallel configurations14–16 and series configurations17–22, each presenting distinct hardware and operational trade-offs. In parallel configurations, the MMC must match the full system DC voltage, imposing severe insulation requirements that hinder footprint and weight reductions for offshore platforms. Operationally, they require independent DC fault ride-through (FRT) for each converter, often necessitating expensive circuit breakers for the uncontrollable DRU. However, parallel topologies offer considerable flexibility during power flow reversal, enabling dynamic reversal with only a brief, partial DRU station shutdown23.

Conversely, series configurations allow the MMC to operate at a lower DC voltage, significantly reducing submodule (SM) capacitance requirements and power device voltage ratings. They also provide a centralized FRT advantage, utilizing the full-bridge MMC’s reverse voltage to clear DC faults for the entire DC link without DRU-side breakers. Despite these hardware benefits, series topologies are severely limited during power reversal, as the shared DC current path mandates a complete system shutdown and restart to reverse power flow. Furthermore, they face inherent challenges in system startup complexity and active power allocation.24.

Due to the unequal DC voltage ratings of the offshore and onshore MMCs, the DC system must operate at a reduced voltage during the system’s startup phase. To achieve this, existing literature proposes incorporating auxiliary equipment such as diesel generators25, energy storage systems26, or parallel AC cables27; however, these solutions substantially increase capital investment costs. While researchers proposed a startup strategy eliminating the need for auxiliary equipment20,22, it failed to address critical transient issues, such as the severe inrush currents that occur at the instant of DRU conduction. Therefore, a detailed control strategy and an electromagnetic transient analysis are needed to ensure the reliability of the startup approach.

Furthermore, according to the reasonable allocation of active power between the DRU and MMC, reference19 proposed an active power-voltage (P-V) droop coordinated control strategy based on traditional V/f control, enabling the regulation of the MMC’s transmitted power under varying wind conditions. Building upon this, reference21 introduced an adaptive droop control that reduces the required capacity of the offshore MMC and mitigates offshore AC voltage fluctuations during wind power variations. While the primary active power control objective is to ensure the DRU transmission power does not exceed its limits.Generally, the theoretical active power ratio between the MMC and DRU in a series configuration should equal their DC voltage ratio. However, due to non-ideal factors such as converter valve losses and the transient charging effects of DC-side grounding capacitances, the actual power ratio deviates slightly from the rated DC voltage ratio. Consequently, during severe OWF power fluctuations, even slight inaccuracies in active power control can induce excessive DC voltage fluctuations in the MMC. Such deviations pose severe risks, including the breakdown of semiconductor devices, accelerated degradation of insulation materials, and a significant reduction in capacitor lifespan.

The generated power is collected by 66 kV AC submarine cables to the offshore converter platform, and then transmitted via ±500 kV DC submarine cables to the onshore converter station for grid integration. For onshore station, a full-bridge MMC is utilized to control the system’s DC voltage and enable reduced-voltage startup for DRU-MMC hybrid system, and a DC chopper is connected on the DC side.

As shown in Fig. 1, within the offshore AC system, AC breaker B1 links the PCC bus with the MMC, while AC breaker B2 links the PCC bus with the DRU. And switch K1 is connected across the positive and negative poles of the DRU. To protect the offshore converter station, a protection thyristor valve S1 is configured in parallel with the MMC on the DC side19.

Startup strategy

Due to the uncontrollable characteristic of the DRU, the OWP-HVDC transmission system based on the series-connected DRU-MMC converter requires an auxiliary startup strategy. This section proposes a coordinated startup strategy for the onshore MMC, the offshore DRU-MMC hybrid converter, and the WTGs, in which the MMC within the hybrid converter delivers power back to the WFs to achieve effective system startup. The startup procedure can be divided into the following seven steps, as shown in Fig. 2. In the initial state, switch K1 is closed, while circuit breaker B1 and B2 are open.

Procedure of the proposed startup strategy for the DRU-MMC series hybrid converter system.

Stage 1: Establishment of system DC voltage. The onshore MMC station starts up, charging its capacitors using the onshore AC grid. The onshore MMC then establishes the system DC voltage, which is controlled to the MMC rated DC voltage (set to 0.25 p.u. in this paper).

Stage 2: Establishment of offshore system AC bus voltage. After the DC voltage stabilizes, the switch B1 is closed, and the offshore MMC is de-blocked and operates in grid-forming mode, establishing the AC voltage at the PCC of the offshore AC system (with an initial value of 1.0 p.u.) and supplying power to the OWFs.

Stage 3: Partial WTGs grid connection and operation. Due to the limited capacity of the offshore MMC converter station, a portion of the WTGs are started first (with a startup power of 0.25 p.u.). Subsequently, the AC WFs begin to generate power.

Stage 4: WTG low voltage ride-through (LVRT). The offshore MMC induces an AC voltage sag to 0.8 p.u., triggering the WTGs to transition into LVRT mode. Concurrently, the turbines’ internal energy dissipation devices activate to absorb the surplus active power.

Stage 5: System DC voltage restoration. Following the opening of the DRU’s DC-side bypass switch K1, the onshore HMMC regulates the system DC voltage up to its 1.0 p.u. nominal level.

Stage 6: Grid connection of the DRU converter station. By closing the AC-side breaker B2, the DRU and its associated filters seamlessly connect to the grid. Meanwhile, as the offshore MMC operates in a passive state throughout the WTG LVRT phase, a slight active power injection from the turbines is required to support the MMC’s SM voltages.

Stage 7: Steady-state operation and black start completion. The offshore MMC restores AC bus voltage at PCC to its rated value, shifting its control strategy from constant AC voltage to other steady grid-forming control. Concurrently, the energy dissipation devices within the AC WTGs are deactivated, allowing the turbines to exit LVRT mode. Finally, the remaining WTGs are synchronized with the grid, ramping the active power of the OWFs to its nominal capacity and completing the startup process.

Two measures are employed to assist the LVRT of the PMSG-based WTG: a DC-link chopper and grid-side converter (GSC) active support control, as shown in Fig. 3. The GSC provides active voltage support to stabilize the MMC DC voltage, while the chopper dissipates surplus power and limits DC-link overvoltage. During the startup phase, the GSC initially operates with zero d-axis active current reference ( = 0). Upon detecting a decline in the MMC DC voltage, an auxiliary active current command generated from the voltage deviation is superimposed onto , forcing the WTGs to inject active power and support the MMC DC voltage.

Control diagram of GSC active support control during LVRT.

Notably, while intentionally triggering LVRT results potential transient stresses that may reduce the WTG life span, the proposed strategy mitigates this impact by limiting the voltage threshold to 0.8 p.u. for only 0.5 s. Modern grid codes mandate WTGs to withstand a 0.2 p.u. sag for 1.3 s 28,29. With current hardware protections, WTGs could easily bear this mild transient. Thus, although inducing a fault-like mode warrants caution, the applied threshold remains safely within operational limits.

Active power control

While fixed AC voltage control accurately maintains the PCC bus voltage at its nominal value, it fails to stabilize the DRU’s transmitted power at 0.75 p.u., thereby necessitating a greater operating margin for the power devices and increasing the power transmission losses. To avoid device overcurrent in the converters caused by substantial surges in OWP generation, active power-AC voltage control can be implemented to the offshore MMC, thereby realizing an exact power allocation between the DRU and the MMC.

The control concept is as follows: when the DRU’s active power is within its nominal limits, the wind power is shared between the DRU and the MMC based on their allocation ratio. Once the DRU’s active power surpasses its rated value, it is clamped at its rated power, leaving the remaining power to be handled by the MMC. When the wind power fluctuates, to avoid reverse power feeding through the MMC, upward fluctuations are compensated solely by the MMC, while downward fluctuations are jointly managed by both the MMC and the DRU. At t = 0.5 s (time point b), the offshore MMC is deblocked to establish an offshore AC system voltage of 1.0 p.u., thereby energizing the offshore wind network. Simultaneously, the GSC outer-loop control switches to LVRT mode and outputs , enabling the WTGs to provide active power support. Concurrently, the WTGs exit LVRT mode, and the GSC outer-loop control reverts to normal mode, outputting . Transient response to system disturbances

DC-side short-circuit fault protection

A permanent inter-pole DC short-circuit fault was applied at the receiving end of the DC cable at t = 5 s, as shown in Fig. 14. Upon fault inception, the DC-link voltage collapses to near zero (Fig. 14a). The offshore MMC is immediately blocked, and S1 is triggered after a 200 μs delay. This action swiftly suppresses the DC and arm currents of offshore MMC to zero (Fig. 14b, d), effectively bypassing the converter and shielding the SMs from additional thermal stress (Fig. 14c). Consequently, the fault current from the AC grid via the DRU is diverted entirely through S1, peaking at approximately 7.6 kA. The maximum transient arm overvoltage is clamped to about 1.5 p.u., which prevents SM capacitor overcharging (Fig. 14f). After 60 ms, the DRU-side AC circuit breaker opens to fully isolate the fault.

Receiving-end AC fault ride-through

Two types of AC faults at the onshore PCC were considered: a single-phase-to-ground fault and a three-phase-to-ground fault, occurring at t = 5 with a duration of 0.1 s. During the faults, the onshore MMC operates in current-limiting mode, and the resulting power surplus causes a DC voltage rise at the receiving end. Once the DC voltage exceeds 1.05 p.u., the DC chopper at the DC-side of onshore MMC activates to dissipate excess energy and clamp the voltage. Waveforms for the single-phase fault and the three-phase fault are shown in Figs. 15 and 16, respectively. In both cases, the offshore WTGs remain connected, and the system returns to steady-state rapidly after fault clearance, demonstrating great FRT and recovery characteristics under asymmetrical and symmetrical AC faults.

Under fixed AC voltage control (as shown in Fig. 17), the PCC bus voltage is precisely regulated at 1.0 p.u. (66 kV), exhibiting minimal fluctuations during wind power variations. However, the active power transmitted by the DRU cannot be stabilized at its 1.0 p.u. reference; the simulation indicates that it exceeds 1.0 p.u. to 1.03 p.u. Because the AC bus voltage is fixed, the wind power fluctuations are entirely absorbed by the MMC, leading to significant ripples in the MMC DC voltage.

Under active power control (as shown in Fig. 18), the active power transferred by the DRU is accurately maintained at 1.0 p.u. During wind power fluctuations, any excess power beyond the DRU’s rated capacity is handled by the MMC; otherwise, the power variations are shared between the DRU and the MMC in proportion to their respective DC voltages. However, this mode fails to tightly regulate the PCC bus voltage at its nominal value. Consequently, substantial bus voltage fluctuations occur to force the DRU power to track its reference.

Under DC voltage control (as shown in Fig. 19), the MMC DC voltage is precisely regulated at 1.0 p.u. (250 kV). As a result, the steady-state active power of the DRU is maintained at approximately 1500 MW, and any transient variations in wind power are exclusively compensated by the DRU. Similar to the active power control mode, the PCC bus voltage cannot be strictly regulated to its rated value, which also results in severe voltage fluctuations.

As illustrated in Fig. 20a, d, under fixed AC bus voltage control, the maximum and minimum values of the system PCC bus voltage during wind speed variations are 66.4 kV and 66.2 kV, respectively, exhibiting a fluctuation rate of 0.6 %. The system DC voltage fluctuates between 979.2 kV and 1020.0 kV, with a fluctuation rate of 1.0 %. However, the MMC DC voltage varies from 213.9 kV to 248.7 kV, resulting in a severe voltage ripple of 14.4 %, which far exceeds the 5 % permissible limit for DC voltage ripple.

According to Fig. 20b, e, under active power control, the system PCC bus voltage fluctuates between 64.7 and 65.0 kV during wind speed variations, corresponding to a 2.0 % fluctuation rate. The system DC voltage ranges from 973.7 to 1020.4 kV (a 1.3 % fluctuation rate). Meanwhile, the MMC DC voltage varies between 239.0 kV and 260.2 kV, maintaining a maximum fluctuation rate of 4.4 %, which closely approaches the DC voltage ripple limit.

As shown in Fig. 20c, f, under DC voltage control, the system PCC bus voltage fluctuates between 65.1 kV and 66.6 kV during wind speed variations, with a fluctuation rate of 1.3 %. The system DC voltage ranges from 980 kV to 1021.12 kV (a 1.0 % fluctuation rate). The MMC DC voltage fluctuates between 239.0 and 260.2 kV, yielding a maximum fluctuation rate of only 2.9 %, which is well within the permissible DC voltage ripple limit.

In conclusion, considering device safety margin, insulation requirements, and control complexity, DC voltage control emerges as the optimal strategy among the three grid-forming control schemes. This control method effectively mitigates MMC DC voltage fluctuations and eliminates the risk of under-modulation.

Conclusion

This paper investigates control strategies for the startup and steady-state operation of large-scale OWP transmission systems based on the series-connected DRU-MMC converter. To address the inherent uncontrollability of DRU, a comprehensive reduced-voltage startup strategy is proposed. Simulation results verify that this strategy smoothly manages WTG grid connections through offshore MMC within the hybrid converter. Furthermore, to optimize steady-state operation performance of the system, three distinct grid-forming control strategies for the offshore MMC are proposed and comparatively analyzed under disturbance conditions. The comparative simulation analysis shows that:

Fixed AC voltage control successfully stabilizes the PCC bus voltage but fails to regulate the power allocation between DRU and MMC. The MMC absorbs the entirety of the wind power fluctuations, resulting in a severe DC voltage ripple of 14.4 %, which far exceeds standard safety limits.

Balancing safety and insulation requirements, the proposed DC voltage control emerges as the optimal grid-forming strategy, as it effectively suppresses fluctuations, prevents over-modulation, and ensures robust operation for DRU-MMC systems. However, it must be acknowledged that a small-capacity offshore MMC inherently restricts voltage regulation and reactive power margins, posing risks of over-modulation and instability under extreme conditions.

Moving forward, several critical areas remain for future investigation. First, with the trend toward large-scale offshore wind cluster development, extending the proposed scheme to multi-wind-farm configurations is essential. Future researches mainly focus on optimizing dynamic power dispatch and resolving the critical broadband oscillation issues inherent in power electronic interconnected systems. Second, DC-side fault protection and transient control systems in such hybrid architectures require deeper exploration. Since key equipment such as high-voltage DC circuit breakers and transient protection frameworks are not yet fully mature Finally, as the penetration of renewable energy increases, it is important to investigate both the offshore wind farms and the MMC-HVDC system grid-forming capabilities to provide robust voltage and frequency support to the onshore power system.
