Why Do Cancer Patients Decide to Withdraw Their Treatment? A Systematic Review and Meta‐Synthesis
Authors: Faezeh Abadi, Esmat Nouhi, Mostafa Bijani, Camelia Rohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDiscontinuation of treatment by cancer patients is a complex decision driven by many factors that are not yet fully understood. Using a qualitative approach is the best way to explore this complexity in greater depth. Therefore, this study aimed to explore and synthesize the factors that influence the decision‐making of cancer patients regarding withdrawal from treatment.MethodsThis is a qualitative systematic review and meta‐synthesis, conducted using the six‐step meta‐synthesis method developed by Sandelowski and Barroso in 2007. Initially, a comprehensive systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and CINAHL to identify relevant studies published between 2000 and 2024 according to our inclusion criteria. Subsequently, conventional content analysis was used to analyze the data extracted from qualitative studies. Initially, 348 studies were retrieved, and after screening, 18 studies were included in the meta‐synthesis.ResultsFrom 177 primary codes, one main theme, two categories (endogenous and exogenous factors), and eight sub‐categories were extracted through the meta‐synthesis process. The factors affecting withdrawal from treatment were: “financial and insurance challenges,” “the communication role of the healthcare team,” “cultural and social factors,” “spiritual and religious beliefs,” “family functioning,” “personal attributes,” “the role of emotions,” and “medication side effects.”ConclusionThe decision to withdraw from treatment is a multifaceted process influenced by personal values, clinical considerations, and social dynamics. A deeper understanding of these factors enables healthcare providers, especially nurses, to design better support programs.Keywords: cancer, cancer patient, decision making, meta‐synthesis, withdrawing treatment

Introduction

Global cancer incidence and mortality rates are increasing rapidly. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, there were approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1]. Although substantial advances have been achieved in cancer treatment, some patients nonetheless choose to forgo disease‐specific interventions, thereby accepting death [2]. Among patients with a low probability of survival, decisions regarding whether to discontinue or continue treatment are often shaped by individual perceptions of the relative value of life quantity versus life quality [3]. In such circumstances, the presence of inconsistent or contradictory judgments poses significant challenges for all parties involved in the decision‐making process, including members of the healthcare team, the patient, and the patient's family [4].

Determining the optimal time to withdraw treatment in cancer patients remains a significant clinical challenge. Current evidence has not established a definitive threshold between the cessation of curative or life‐prolonging therapies and the initiation of palliative care. Furthermore, international guidelines describe the discontinuation of stabilizing treatments in qualitative terms, lacking standardized criteria [5, 6]. Given these uncertainties, an in‐depth examination of this socio‐medical phenomenon is essential [7].

One of the central ethical principles in patient care is respect for patient autonomy [8]. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) recognizes patient autonomy as a fundamental right for patients. This encompasses the patient's authority to accept or decline medical interventions and extends to multiple dimensions, including the right to confidentiality, refusal of treatment, respectful care, informed consent, freedom from degrading treatment, and preservation of dignity [8]. From a clinical perspective, upholding patient autonomy is particularly critical in decisions regarding the discontinuation of cancer treatments, especially when therapeutic benefits are outweighed by adverse effects and curative outcomes are no longer attainable. In such cases, treatment cessation may significantly influence the patient's quality of life, underscoring the ethical imperative to prioritize autonomy [9].

Evidence shows that several factors influence the decision‐making process regarding treatment discontinuation in patients. These factors can be categorized into contextual and unmodifiable determinants. Contextual factors include the patient's educational level, socioeconomic status, and cultural background. In contrast, unmodifiable factors are inherent characteristics such as age, gender, and disease‐related complications [10, 11, 12]. Additionally, patient values, cultural beliefs, and personal convictions significantly shape decision‐making, exerting a substantial and well‐documented influence on treatment‐related behaviors [13]. Another study identified four key variables influencing decision‐making: effective communication and shared decision‐making, expert clinical care, respectful and compassionate treatment, and trust and confidence in physicians [14]. Consequently, awareness of these principles among healthcare providers, particularly physicians and nurses who engage in direct patient care, is crucial. Given their close interaction with patients, healthcare providers' values and beliefs directly shape patients' decision‐making processes regarding treatment [15].

However, many aspects of the decision to withdraw treatment remain poorly understood and inherently complex, necessitating comprehensive review studies to systematically analyze and interpret this phenomenon. Qualitative systematic review and meta‐synthesis are particularly valuable in this regard, as they enable researchers to explore the intricate and multidimensional nature of experiences, systems, and practices [16]. By synthesizing findings from individual qualitative studies, meta‐synthesis can address “why” and “how” questions, thereby providing deeper insights into previously unexplored social phenomena [17]. Thus, the objective of this study was to synthesize knowledge derived from earlier qualitative studies exploring factors that influence the decision‐making of cancer patients to withdraw from treatment.

Methods

Study Design

This is a qualitative systematic review and meta‐synthesis. The authors adhered to the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement. The study was conducted based on the six‐step meta‐synthesis method developed by Sandelowski and Barroso in 2007. This method is widely recognized as a highly effective method for synthesizing qualitative data [18]. The main question of the study was: What factors can influence the decision to withdraw treatment in cancer patients?

Data Collection

Data collection was carried out through the six‐step process: (1) conceiving the qualitative synthesis study, (2) searching and retrieving literature, (3) study inclusion and exclusion criteria, (4) classifying the findings in qualitative reports, (5) study quality assessment, and (6) data synthesis and presentation.

Step 1: Conceiving the Qualitative Synthesis Study

In the first step, the research team was formed with four members. Subsequently, a clear research question was formulated to guide the study and ensure alignment with the research aim. The research question was: “What factors influence the decision‐making of cancer patients to withdraw from treatment?”. To answer this question, the perspectives and experiences of cancer patients, their families, and healthcare providers (physicians and nurses) were evaluated through the literature.

Step 2: Searching and Retrieving Literature

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and CINAHL to identify relevant studies published between January 1, 2000 and January 1, 2024. The last search was performed on March 11, 2025. English keywords and MeSH terms were used to optimize search results, and a university librarian facilitated the identification of additional terminology. Some differences existed in the search methods across the platforms and databases: for instance, in Web of Science, the search was performed within abstracts; in Scopus, it was conducted in titles, abstracts, and keywords; and in PubMed and CINAHL, the search was conducted across all fields. Search terms and search strategies on electronic platforms and databases are shown in Table 1.

Step 3: Study Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Based on the PICO framework, studies were eligible for inclusion if they focused on [Population: Patients diagnosed with cancer, their family members, and/or healthcare providers and studies conducted in ICU/critical care settings were not excluded. Cancer patients may be admitted to ICUs, particularly during advanced stages of disease or end‐of‐life care.], examined [Phenomenon: Perspectives, experiences, and decision‐making to withdraw treatments], and reported [Outcomes: Factors influencing decision‐making to withdraw treatments]. No comparison group was required. Only English‐language qualitative studies, regardless of the country where the research was conducted or the qualitative part of mixed‐method studies with available full text in peer‐reviewed journals were selected. Studies were limited to those published between January 1, 2000, and January 1, 2024. Conference abstracts, editorials, letters to the editor, and non‐primary research reports were excluded. We included primary qualitative research reports focusing on the perspectives/experiences of cancer patients, family members, physicians, and nurses regarding patients' decisions to discontinue treatments. All articles were managed using EndNote 20 software.

Step 4: Classifying Findings in Qualitative Reports

In this step, a data extraction form was prepared by the research team based on the objective of the study. Two authors extracted the necessary information independently. For each included study, the following information was extracted: author(s) and year of publication, country where the study was conducted, title of the articles, study design, sample size, characteristics of the participants, and key findings.

Step 5: Study Quality Assessment

The quality of the included studies was evaluated. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) checklist for qualitative studies [19]. It comprises 10 questions that evaluate aspects such as clarity of research aims, appropriateness of methodology, study design, recruitment strategy, data collection methods, reflexivity, ethical considerations, rigor of data analysis, clarity of findings, and relevance of the results. Responses to each item of the checklist were recorded as “yes,” “no,” or “unclear”, without assigning numerical scores. Two reviewers independently conducted the quality assessment of studies, and the agreement rate between the two reviewers during title/abstract screening was 92%, and disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

For full‐text screening, the inter‐rater agreement was 89%, and disagreements were resolved through consensus. The final selection of 18 studies was confirmed by all reviewers.

Step 6: Data Synthesis and Presentation

The extracted data from primary studies were coded and categorized. Synthesized results offer a coherent perspective that remains faithful to the original studies while also providing a broader interpretation than the descriptions found in the individual reports. We used the conventional content analysis method described by Graneheim and Lundman [20] to perform the analysis and synthesis in three steps: selecting meaning units, condensing and coding, and creating categories on various levels [20]. Coding was performed line‐by‐line by two independent reviewers. Each reviewer extracted meaning units from the findings and discussion sections of the included studies. Initial codes were compared in biweekly team meetings. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. The final codebook, containing 177 unique codes, was reviewed by all team members for consistency. These codes were subsequently organized into categories and subcategories. As these categories and subcategories were refined, the first author (F.A.) reassessed each category and verified it based on the original research reports to ensure reliability.

The final categorizations emphasized factors influencing the decision‐making of cancer patients to withdraw from their treatment. In addition, these results are designed in the form of a conceptual map to show the relationships between the categories.

In the sixth step, the trustworthiness of the findings was assessed. All research team members assessed and discussed the coding, classification, and categorization of the data. A peer evaluation method was used to ensure the preservation of internal homogeneity in data analysis [21]. To establish dependability, the findings were reviewed by external evaluators [22]. The research team included four members with backgrounds in nursing, and all authors have clinical and research experience in oncology and palliative care. Two external evaluators, both with doctoral degrees in nursing and expertise in qualitative studies, audited the results. Additionally, the research team thoroughly examined the entire study, including the meta‐synthesis process, to verify that all research steps adhered to scientific procedures. This review process was conducted to minimize potential biases and ensure the integrity of the research. Furthermore, collaborative work and group reflexivity were employed to enhance the quality and precision of the results.

The inter‐rater agreement for coding was 86% in the initial round; discrepancies were discussed and resolved in team meetings, and the final codebook was approved by all members. Any unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

Main Theme

Clinical decision‐making is not a linear process but a complex phenomenon shaped by a dynamic network of influential factors. This network integrates diverse elements, including the patient's personal values, beliefs, socioeconomic context, and family, with the clinical evidence and professional guidance provided by the healthcare team.

Category 1: Exogenous Factors

It refers to the factors that have an external origin from outside, are uncontrollable, and have a direct or indirect impact, so they can create opportunities or threats. This category includes the following subcategories: “Financial and insurance challenges,” “Communication role of the healthcare team,” “Cultural and social factors,” “Spiritual and religious beliefs,” and “Family functioning.”

Financial and Insurance Challenges

In 38% of the studies, it was explicitly stated that the lack of a supportive financial system and a lack of a supportive insurance system from the government may contribute to the discontinuation of treatment in patients. This problem is especially exacerbated when the disease enters the phase of physical and mental exhaustion, and the family's financial resources are running out. The findings indicated that the lack of a supportive insurance system was an important factor in patients' decision‐making [2, 4, 7, 13, 23, 24].

Communication Role of the Healthcare Team

The therapeutic alliance between a patient and their treatment team is fundamental to the decision‐making process, wherein the team serves in an advisory and guiding capacity. Physicians often attribute patients' treatment discontinuation to a lack of understanding regarding disease severity or insufficient information [25, 26]. However, approximately 88% of studies suggest that, from the patients' perspective, ineffective communication with healthcare providers results in inadequate disease‐related knowledge, contributing to decisional uncertainty [2, 9, 24, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Patients and families emphasize the critical need for clear, empathetic communication about the patient's condition, particularly when conflicting treatment strategies or ambiguous clinical guidelines further complicate decision‐making [29].

Additionally, inconsistencies in medical recommendations among physicians can erode patient trust and exacerbate decision‐making difficulties [24]. Conversely, a collaborative patient‐provider relationship characterized by transparent, honest information exchange significantly enhances patients' ability to make informed choices [4, 7, 25, 33, 34, 35]. Notably, patients report that trust in physicians, particularly within a paternalistic yet benevolent framework, facilitates more confident treatment decisions [9, 25, 32].

A direct quote: “I changed two doctors. My first doctor (quake) seemed like he did not know anything. He never used to say much and used to give medicines in a packet. Then I consulted another doctor, and he was always used to being angry. Whenever I used to ask something, he didn't even care to reply. I left him as well. Now, there is no money also to go and give to such people” [24].

Cultural and Social Factors

Cultural and social factors were identified as influential factors in the decision to discontinue cancer treatment in 61% of the reviewed studies. A prevalent theme was the concealment of illness by patients and their families due to fear of social rejection, as cancer was often perceived as a stigmatizing condition that should remain undisclosed [13, 24, 26]. Additionally, some patients viewed cancer as an invariably fatal disease, leading to the belief that seeking treatment was futile [9, 24, 26, 30, 31, 32]. Skepticism toward conventional medical interventions was also observed, with some individuals opting for traditional or alternative therapies instead [2, 9, 13, 25, 32]. Notably, some studies reported a belief among participants in a so‐called “drug mafia,” wherein cancer medications were perceived as part of a fraudulent scheme designed to exploit patients financially through the sale of chemotherapy drugs [7, 9]. Furthermore, prior unsuccessful treatment experiences within patients' immediate social networks, including family and friends, were found to significantly influence their decisions [2, 13, 32]. Overall, sociocultural misconceptions [9, 24], societal taboos surrounding death, and collectivist values [4] emerged as key determinants in patients' decisions to forgo treatment.

Direct quotes: “Tell anyone and ask me to apply homemade ointment, but when I started my treatment, people in the village started strangely staring at me as if I belonged to some low caste. I am of a higher caste” [24].

Spiritual and Religious Beliefs

A patient's religious beliefs typically originate with their family, while society often influences their specific religious choices. Spiritual and religious beliefs were reported to influence treatment decision‐making in 44% of the reviewed studies. Several patients described transcendent experiences or spiritually significant dreams that fostered an acceptance of death, thereby facilitating their decision to discontinue treatment [2, 34]. Additionally, faith in a divine or supernatural power, often perceived as capable of healing, reduced patients' willingness to pursue medical interventions, as they viewed life and death as divinely ordained and believed their condition might improve through spiritual means alone [9, 32, 33, 34].

In some cases, a perceived abandonment by God or a higher power, coupled with existential hopelessness, also shaped patients' choices [24]. Religious doctrines, convictions, and authoritative rulings were further identified as critical determinants in this process [9, 25]. For instance, a study conducted in Kenya highlighted how supernatural beliefs and the existence of an external magical power, including curses, malevolent spiritual forces, or magical influences originating from a powerful external agent, were attributed to causes of illness. Such perspectives predisposed patients to favor spiritual healing practices over conventional medical treatments [13].

A direct quote: “They say it is the end of line and you must accept it. I trust in God. If God wants, he will stay alive, and if God does not want, he won't” [34].

Family Functioning

Family involvement was identified as a highly influential factor in patients' treatment decisions in 77% of the reviewed studies. Families appear to assume two distinct roles: supportive or indifferent [2, 4]. A supportive family may act as a barrier to patients' decisions to discontinue treatment, as patients often consider familial expectations when making such choices [23, 26, 27, 29, 32, 33]. Conversely, prolonged and ineffective treatment may contribute to familial exhaustion [23, 35], resulting in diminished support and emotional withdrawal [2]. Under these circumstances, as patients' care needs escalate, they may develop greater acceptance of death and consequently opt to cease treatment [2, 4, 26].

The presence of dependent children or family members can create decisional ambivalence, with patients torn between the desire to prolong survival (to continue providing support) and the wish to avoid further treatment‐related suffering [4, 7, 27, 30]. Notably, the maternal role emerged as a complicating factor in decision‐making, with concerns about dependents' welfare creating significant ambivalence [27, 30]. Some patients reported employing adaptive coping strategies such as mindfulness and distraction techniques to enhance psychological resilience post‐decision [23]. However, from the family members' perspective, patient autonomy remains paramount, with an emphasis on preserving dignity and personal values [25, 27, 29, 33, 34, 35]. Family reports indicate that decisional clarity improves when healthcare providers explicitly communicate the futility of continued treatment and the potential benefits of reducing unnecessary suffering [4, 29]. Furthermore, collaborative decision‐making involving both family members and the treatment team facilitates greater acceptance of end‐of‐life choices among stakeholders [2, 4, 29].

Direct quotes: “I felt like I was betraying my family by stopping treatment, but my body couldn't take it anymore. Was I selfish? I still wonder” and “My husband begged me to keep trying. He couldn't accept that I was ready to let go. It hurt more than the cancer” [27].

Category 2: Endogenous Factors

It refers to the factors that have an internal origin, are changeable, and have a direct impact on the behavior and decisions of the decision‐maker. This category includes the following sub‐categories: “Personal attributes,” “the role of emotions”, and “medication side effects”.

Personal Attributes

This factor pertains to an individual's internal cognitive framework, operating independently of social influence. In some cases, it is associated with demographic factors, such as age. It is defined by an individual's subjective calculus regarding the trade‐offs between quality of life (freedom from suffering) and life quantity (survival duration), particularly the assessment of whether prolonged survival justifies the potential hardships of treatment. Patient‐related personal factors were identified in 55% of the reviewed studies as influential in treatment discontinuation decisions. Clinicians reported that cessation of non‐beneficial treatments is more readily considered for older patients, noting that younger individuals tend to prioritize life extension while older patients emphasize quality of life. However, researchers observed that chronological age alone does not consistently predict these preferences, as perceptions of “old age” vary significantly among families [7, 25, 26, 30, 33].

Both patients and family members consistently prioritized quality of life over longevity. Most participants expressed willingness to discontinue ineffective treatments, particularly following disease recurrence or treatment failure [7, 32]. The preference for shorter but more meaningful survival over prolonged life with treatment‐related morbidity was frequently reported [25, 26, 27, 30, 32, 33, 35].

Interestingly, one study noted that some patients refused to accept treatment due to denial of their illness [2]. From the perspective of these individuals, accepting treatment meant accepting the existence of the illness, which they refused to accept for psychological reasons due to psychological defense mechanisms [7].

Direct quotes: “The doctors kept saying ‘fight,’ but no one told me how to live with the pain. I chose to stop because I wanted to feel alive again, even if it meant less time” [27].

The Role of Emotions

Emotional responses to similar situations continue to vary significantly among individuals, as they are determined less by the situation itself and more by the patient's psychological framework and belief systems. Emotional factors play a pivotal role in treatment discontinuation decisions, with 77% of included studies directly referencing their influence. The most frequently reported emotional states, including psychological distress, fear, anxiety, depressive symptoms, diminished resilience, and hopelessness, were observed across all stakeholder groups: treatment teams, family members, and patients [2, 4, 7, 9, 13, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 29, 33, 34]. Among patients, these affective responses primarily stemmed from fears of lost autonomy and concerns about bereaving family members [4, 7, 23, 24, 30, 34]. Family members, conversely, commonly experienced decision‐related guilt and ambivalence, particularly when compelled to make surrogate decisions for cognitively impaired patients without clear knowledge of patient preferences [4, 13, 24, 35].

Clinicians reported experiencing considerable emotional burden when patients discontinued treatment due to socioeconomic constraints (limited education, financial hardship, or disease misperception) rather than clinical rationale [26]. Despite these challenges, most studies emphasized physicians' fundamental commitment to respecting patient autonomy [9, 26, 30]. Notably, protective psychological factors, including existential purpose, intrinsic motivation, and robust social support, emerged as significant deterrents to treatment discontinuation, with evidence suggesting their association with treatment adherence [25].

Direct quotes: “They say there is no hope. They say that we have reached the end stage, and we have to start palliative medicine. It's too early for him to go. I have to let his care turn into palliative care so that he dies without pain. Well, I do not want him to die. I do not want to lose him. A dying loved one” [34].

Medication Side Effects

The experience of pain during ambulatory chemotherapy varies among patients and is influenced by their psychological tolerance and physical resilience. In 72% of the reviewed studies, severe chemotherapy‐related side effects were reported to undermine patients' confidence in treatment efficacy. This skepticism was particularly pronounced when side effects altered physical appearance, such as hair loss, weight loss, or impaired sexual function, negatively impacting body image and self‐perception [2, 4, 9, 23, 24, 26, 27, 30, 31, 32, 35]. Many patients prioritized maintaining their quality of life over enduring debilitating side effects, especially when treatment‐induced fatigue or functional decline compromised their independence in daily activities [7, 23, 26]. This preference was further reinforced when physicians indicated limited therapeutic benefits from continued treatment [9, 31, 33, 34] or when patients acknowledged the incurable nature of their disease [4, 23, 35]. Additionally, polypharmacy was perceived to exacerbate side effects, contributing to treatment‐related burnout and fatigue [7].

Direct quotes: “I got chemotherapy done, and it shattered my whole body. Now, even walking is difficult. I do not remember when was the last time I slept sex with my wife. Sometimes, I pity my wife; she also suffers because of me. She never says, but I am always scared she will leave me” [24].

The conceptual map presented in Figure 2 was generated through a systematic, inductive process grounded in the synthesized data. First, all initial codes extracted from the 18 included studies were compiled and compared. Through thematic analysis and repeated team discussions, these codes were consolidated into eight core categories (subcategories) that consistently appeared across multiple studies. Second, the hierarchical relationship with “cultural and social factors” placed as the outermost/largest circle, emerged inductively from the finding that the included studies consistently described cultural and social context as an overarching determinant that shapes, moderates, or contextualizes all other factors (family functioning, communication, financial challenges, religious beliefs). Specifically, in 61% of the reviewed studies, cultural factors were reported as the foundational framework. Third, the bidirectional arrows indicated reciprocal influence between factors (family influences patient decisions, and patients influence family dynamics; communication flows bidirectionally between healthcare teams and patients/families). Fourth, the relative circle sizes (patient slightly larger than family) are based on patient autonomy as the primary decision‐maker, while some studies explicitly noted that in cases of cognitive impairment, the family assumes greater responsibility, a nuance we have preserved in the model.

A conceptual map of factors affecting the decision‐making to withdraw treatments in cancer patients.

The second most prominent circle pertains to communication between the treatment team, patient, and family, highlighted in 88% of the reviewed studies as a pivotal factor. Six additional factors are situated within these two primary circles, interconnected through bidirectional arrows, emphasizing their dynamic interactions.

At the center of the model lies the final decision, shaped by all eight factors while preserving the autonomy of the decision‐maker. The patient's circle is slightly larger than that of the family, underscoring their primary role in decision‐making. The model also highlights the treatment team's consultative and guiding role, depicted through bidirectional arrows, signifying collaborative dialog among clinicians, patients, and families. Ultimately, the decision is reached through discussion, with respect for the patient's autonomous choice.

We acknowledge that the visual representation involves researcher interpretation; however, this interpretation is explicitly grounded in the synthesized data and was reviewed by all co‐authors for consistency with the source studies.

Discussion

This is a qualitative systematic review and meta‐synthesis that aimed to explore factors influencing the decision‐making of cancer patients to withdraw from treatment. After extracting and analyzing qualitative data from 18 studies, one main theme, two categories of exogenous and endogenous factors, and eight subcategories were identified.

Exogenous factors: Exogenous factors are those that originate externally from the individual and their immediate environment, often arising from broader societal, cultural, and belief systems. Although these factors are largely beyond the patient's control, they exert a significant influence on the decision to discontinue treatment. For instance, a patient's cultural and religious background is frequently considered a stable variable, as it comprises an integrated system of social beliefs developed over time that fundamentally shapes decision‐making processes [4, 11, 36]. Consequently, the healthcare team's awareness of patients' cultural and religious attitudes, coupled with their cultural competence, is critical for facilitating more informed and appropriate patient decisions regarding treatment withdrawal [36].

Family dynamics constitute a critical factor influencing patient decision‐making. The process often involves a collaborative approach, where decisions are made through consultation between the patient, their family members, and the treatment team, while the principle of individual autonomy is maintained [24, 37]. However, the absence of a familial support system can lead patients to make this decision more autonomously and, at times, more rapidly [24, 38]. This underscores the necessity for structured psychological support and the establishment of governmental support mechanisms to assist patients who lack a family network in critical situations [35].

Furthermore, financial constraints and inadequate insurance coverage are significant determinants in the decision to discontinue treatment. This is particularly significant when familial financial resources are depleted or when other family members face substantial economic pressure from the cost of care [39]. Such conditions can precipitate the choice to halt treatment [7]. Consequently, governmental provision of comprehensive insurance support and the development of robust financing mechanisms such as subsidies for high‐cost treatments like chemotherapy are essential to alleviate economic burdens and enable patients and their families to make decisions based on clinical rather than financial considerations [40].

Endogenous factors: Endogenous factors are characterized by their intrinsic and internal origin, remaining largely unaffected by external environmental conditions. These factors are generally within the patient's control and can be potentially modified. A prominent example includes the side effects of chemotherapy, which are experienced by a majority of cancer patients and vary widely in their nature and severity [2]. The tolerance for these side effects exhibits significant individual variation. Particularly when side effects alter physical appearance, they can serve as a primary motivator for the decision to discontinue treatment [24]. Consequently, the treatment team's attention to individual differences in patients' experiences and perceptions of side effects, supported by robust counseling systems, is critical for developing a deeper understanding of patient decision‐making [41].

Furthermore, patients' emotional states constitute a key endogenous factor, encompassing a wide spectrum of internal feelings ranging from fear and social rejection to disappointment with treatment outcomes. For instance, in patients experiencing profound hopelessness, altering the decision to discontinue treatment is unlikely without direct psychological intervention to address this belief [42]. Therefore, psychotherapeutic interventions that attend to these emotional dimensions are of paramount importance. The provision of specialized counseling and psychological support by experienced clinicians during this critical period can significantly aid patients in making more informed and rational decisions [24, 43, 44].

Comparison With Existing Theoretical Frameworks

Our conceptual model aligns with the shared decision‐making (SDM) framework [37, 45]. SDM emphasizes the active involvement of patients, families, and clinicians in treatment decisions. Similarly, our model highlights bidirectional communication between the healthcare team and patients/families (pivotal in 88% of studies) and preserves patient autonomy at the center. However, our model extends SDM by explicitly identifying cultural and social factors as an overarching determinant that shapes the entire decision‐making environment, a dimension less emphasized in classic SDM models.

However, our model extends the SDM framework in several novel ways. First, SDM typically focuses on the interaction between patient and clinician, with family involvement acknowledged but not systematically integrated. In contrast, our model explicitly positions family functioning as a distinct and influential factor (reported in 77% of studies), with bidirectional arrows showing reciprocal influence between the patient and family. Second, our model introduces a hierarchical structure in which cultural and social factors serve as the outermost, overarching layer that shapes and contextualizes all other factors, including communication and decision‐making processes. This cultural embedding is less emphasized in classic SDM models. Third, our model preserves conditional pathways (in cases of cognitive impairment, the family assumes greater responsibility), adding nuance to the SDM framework's assumption of consistent patient autonomy. Fourth, our model incorporates structural barriers (financial challenges, insurance systems) and physical experiences (medication side effects) as direct influences on the decision to withdraw treatment, factors that are not explicitly theorized in SDM.

Clinical Application of the Model

The conceptual model can be operationalized in clinical practice as a reflective and assessment tool. Oncology teams may use the model to systematically explore which factors are influencing a specific patient's decision to withdraw treatment. The following implications are offered as tentative recommendations based on the available qualitative evidence, not as prescriptive conclusions. For instance, if financial challenges are identified as a primary driver, the team could involve social workers or financial counselors. If family functioning emerges as a key factor, family meetings or mediation could be considered. If medication side effects are prominent, palliative care or symptom management services should be prioritized. Future research should develop and validate a structured questionnaire or checklist based on this model to support clinical assessment and shared decision‐making.

While our synthesis identified common factors across all settings, we also observed differences based on country income level. In studies from lower‐middle‐income countries (Iran, Kenya, India), financial constraints, inadequate insurance coverage, and strong spiritual/religious beliefs were more prominently reported as drivers of treatment withdrawal. In contrast, studies from high‐income countries (USA, European nations) emphasized patient autonomy, communication quality, and medication side effects more strongly. These differences likely reflect variations in healthcare financing systems, social safety nets, and cultural norms regarding patient‐provider relationships. Future meta‐syntheses should consider conducting separate subgroup syntheses for low‐ and high‐income country contexts to explore these differences further.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study provided a valuable opportunity to identify the factors influencing treatment refusal among cancer patients, with no restrictions regarding cancer type or geographical region. A strength of this meta‐synthesis is its 360‐degree, multistakeholder perspective; by examining the perspectives and experiences of three key target groups: cancer patients, their families, and physicians and nurses, this study provides comprehensive insights into this issue. Using the six‐step meta‐synthesis method developed by Sandelowski and Barroso as a structured framework ensured the uniformity of raw data and allowed for a more precise synthesis. Although there is a narrow border between the recognition of endogenous and exogenous factors, the authors tried to categorize them with great accuracy and discussed them within the research team. Furthermore, the conceptual framework proposed in this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the factors affecting treatment refusal in cancer patients, and it can be a good basis for designing future studies.

This study only included studies published in English, regardless of the country where the research was conducted, so there may be some relevant studies in other languages that were not included in this study. Although the researchers were proficient in English, their native language was Persian, which may have introduced constraints in accurately translating non‐native quotations. However, to mitigate this issue, direct quotes from the original studies were used in the results section without modification to enhance clarity and accuracy. Another limitation of this meta‐synthesis is that the Norton et al. [28] study, while retained for its relevant data on life‐sustaining treatment withdrawal and communication, does not explicitly specify whether all patients in its sample had cancer. However, the research team determined that the findings on communication needs were conceptually transferable to the oncology context. Our findings are not globally generalizable in a statistical sense, but they offer transferability to similar clinical and cultural contexts. Readers should assess the fit between our sample (18 studies) and their own context.

Conclusions

The findings of this study reveal that the decision to withdraw treatment is influenced by a combination of sociofamilial and personal intellectual factors. This choice is made following consultation with both family and the treatment team, while ultimately preserving the patient's autonomy. The conceptual map developed in this study may support patients, their families, and the treatment team in making decisions about discontinuing treatment with reduced psychological burden. By identifying the multiple dimensions of this decision, it addresses the crucial role of emotions in decision‐making, as patients report a spectrum of experiences ranging from profound hopelessness to faith in a higher power for healing. Therefore, attention to the role of emotions is crucial in this situation. Furthermore, given the significant influence of the treatment team's communication, the study's findings can enhance the team's understanding of this process, enabling them to guide patients' navigation of this critical stage through more constructive and dynamic interactions.
