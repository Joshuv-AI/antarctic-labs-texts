Preconditioning and Linearly Implicit Time Integration for the Serre-Green-Naghdi Equations
Linwan Feng, David Shirokoff, Wooyoung Choi

Abstract
The treatment of the differential PDE constraint poses a key challenge in computing the numerical solution of the Serre-Green-Naghdi (SGN) equations. In this work, we introduce a constant coefficient preconditioner for the SGN constraint operator and prove rigorous bounds on the preconditioned conditioning number. The conditioning bounds incorporate the effects of bathymetry in two dimensions, are quasi-optimal within a class of constant coefficient operators, highlight fundamental scalings for a loss of conditioning, and ensure mesh independent performance for iterative Krylov methods. Utilizing the conditioning bounds, we devise and test two time integration strategies for solving the full SGN equations. The first class combines classical explicit time integration schemes (4th order Runge-Kutta and 2nd–4th order Adams-Bashforth) with the new preconditioner. The second is a linearly implicit scheme where the differential constraint is split into a constant coefficient implicit part and remaining (stiff) explicit part. The linearly implicit methods require a single linear solve of a constant coefficient operator at each time step. We provide a host of computational experiments that validate the robustness of the preconditioners, as well as full solutions of the SGN equations including solitary waves traveling over an underwater shelf (in 1d) and a circular bump (in 2d).

1
Introduction

The shallow water equations (SWEs) provide an effective model for simulating coastal waves and large-scale fluid circulation in ocean basins. This includes important physical phenomena such as tides, storm surges, and the effect of extreme weather, such as hurricanes, on wave generation.

From a modeling standpoint, the SWEs arise from an asymptotic expansion of the (depth-averaged) Euler equations truncated to the leading
O(1)
term in
\beta\equiv\bar{h}/\lambda\ll 1
. Here
\beta
is a small parameter measuring the ratio of the mean water depth
\bar{h}
to the characteristic wavelength
\lambda
. Carrying out the asymptotic expansion (in
\beta
) to the next order results in dispersive corrections to the SWEs. Physically, the incorporation of dispersion to the SWEs plays an important role in the propagation of long waves at larger (but still small)
\beta
. In particular, solitary waves may form through the balancing of dispersion and (steepening effects of) nonlinearity, while dispersion may also regularize the formation of shocks.

The simplest models that incorporate dispersion arise under a weakly nonlinear assumption of small amplitude waves, i.e.,
\alpha\equiv a/\bar{h}\ll 1
with
a
the amplitude of the wave. These include models for long waves such as the Koreteweg de-Vries (KdV) equation
[
19
]
and the Boussinesq equations
[
4
]
for uni- and bi-directional waves. Several important phenomena such as extreme events observed in coastal regions, however, do not satisfy the weakly non-linear assumption, thus motivating the need for extending the models to include high-order nonlinear effects. To this end, Serre
[
42
]
, Su and Gardner
[
43
]
and Green and Naghdi
[
12
]
derived a new class of long wave models with the leading-order dispersive terms that appear at
O(\beta^{2})
without any assumption on
\alpha
. Asymptotically, the Serre/SG/GN equations (often referred to as the SGN equations) modify the shallow water equations by incorporating dispersive terms truncated at an order of
\beta^{2}
. Therefore, the resulting SGN equations can also be considered as the
dispersive shallow water equations
(DSWEs).

There are numerous asymptotic models closely related to the SGN equations. For instance, the SGN equations for the depth-averaged velocity can be formally extended to any arbitrary order in
\beta^{2}
, e.g., see the work by Wu and others
[
46
,
26
,
27
,
29
,
6
]
. However, it has been shown that the models truncated to even-orders in
\beta^{2}
(i.e., the even-order models) are unstable when the wavenumber
k
is greater than some critical values, at which the growth rate becomes infinity
[
30
,
6
]
. Therefore, the even-order models must be avoided while the odd-order models (including the SGN equations) are useful for numerical studies. In fact, the dispersion relation for the depth-averaged DSWEs shows that the wave frequency is bounded as
k\to\infty
so that there is a less severe constraint on the step size for any conditionally-stable time integration scheme.

Related to the SGN equations, Nwogu
[
33
]
introduced an alternative weakly nonlinear long wave model aimed at more closely approximating the linearized Euler equations dispersion relation (cf. the model solved in
[
3
]
). Following Nwogu, Wei
et al.

[
45
]
proposed the first-order DSWEs written in terms of the horizontal velocity at a vertical level (as opposed to a depth-averaged velocity).
In particular, it was shown that the higher order DSWEs for the horizontal velocity at the bottom are also well-posed at any order of approximation
[
6
]
.

There are, however, numerical advantages to solving the depth averaged SGN equations over the DSWE equations in terms of the horizontal bottom velocity. For the depth-averaged SGN equations, the mass conservation equation contains a first order spatial derivative (see the detailed discussion in §
2
). On the other-hand, for other DSWEs, high-order spatial derivatives need to be evaluated accurately to avoid numerical instability
[
6
]
.

For work on the existence of solutions to the three-dimensional water wave equations we refer to
[
1
]
; a general summary of the derivation for different equivalent SGN models can be found in
[
20
,
6
]
. The physics of water waves and their derivation of their models have been extensively studied, e.g.,
[
24
,
8
,
25
,
4
,
6
]
.

1.1
Prior Numerical Work on the SGN Equations

The dispersive terms in the SGN equation contain mixed space-time derivatives resulting in a differential constraint. This differential constraint poses a key computational challenge that can dominate the computational cost of an SGN solver. In one dimension, the constraint can be handled in a straightforward fashion without significant cost, either via classical iterative methods,
direct solvers, or integral equation approaches, for example, in
[
8
,
24
,
17
]
.
In two dimensions the differential constraint results in a couple system for the velocity components with off diagonal blocks introduced by a varying topography.

One approach to time-discretize the SGN equations is to split the equation into a hyperbolic step (which can be treated with hyperbolic solvers) and a step for the elliptic (differential) constraint. These approaches include Strang splitting
[
3
,
21
,
39
]
, and predictor-corrector type approaches
[
22
,
34
,
10
,
32
]
. A correct treatment of the boundary conditions
[
32
]
is then important when splitting methods are used in non-periodic geometries. Most of these aforementioned works focus on practical details of spatial discretizations (e.g., with finite element methods) and structure preservation (e.g., well-balanced and positive preserving schemes).

Approaches that address the SGN elliptic constraint include the development of quadtree multigrid methods
[
35
]
. An alternative approach,
[
21
,
7
]
instead modifies the SGN equation—to an asymptotically consistent one in
\beta
—by replacing the elliptic constraint with a block diagonal constant coefficient operator. The constraint can then be solved via two time-independent decoupled scalar elliptic systems.

In contrast to splitting methods or predictor-corrector methods, an alternative approach is to introduce a hyperbolic relaxation of the SGN
[
9
]
where the differential constraint is replaced by a hyperbolic system. The relaxation approach can then be solved using explicit structure preserving methods
[
13
]
that leverage the extensive body of existing hyperbolic PDE solution techniques. The original work of Favrie and Gavrilyuk has since been extended to incorporate the effects of topography
[
5
,
14
]
; numerical solutions include high order Galerkin spatial discretizations
[
5
]
and up to second order in space and time structure preserving methods (e.g., well-balanced, positivity preserving) properties
[
14
]
. Practical details regarding convex limiters
[
15
]
and schemes that preserve mass and energy
[
36
]
have also recently been devised. Structure preservation can improve accuracy over long times, and provide a pathway for ensuring numerical stability.

1.2
Contribution of this Work and Organization of Paper

The differential constraint in the SGN requires solving linear systems involving an operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
with variable coefficients in both space and time. The focus of this paper is on introducing formulas for constant coefficients
(\sigma,\alpha)
that define an operator
\mathcal{A}=\sigma\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-\alpha\nabla(\nabla\cdot)
, together with quasi-optimal bounds on the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
. The bounds then enable time-stepping discretizations for the SGN that avoid fully constructing and inverting
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
, namely:

1.

For time integration methods that require solving systems involving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
, the operator
\mathcal{A}
may be used as a preconditioner in a conjugate gradient method. Linear systems involving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
arise in (standard explicit) time-stepping discretizations of the SGN equations, and must be solved in large numbers, making fast solvers desirable.

2.

The operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
may be treated in a linearly implicit multi-step method where only
\mathcal{A}
is treated implicitly. Such linearly implicit multistep methods are efficient as they require only one solves of
\mathcal{A}
per time-step.

This paper is organized as follows. We first introduced the SGN equations (§
2
) and background for the paper (§
3
). We then present (§
4
) new formulas for the constant coefficient operator
\mathcal{A}
along with rigorous bounds on the conditioning number and generalized eigenvalues. Utilizing the bounds, §
5
devises zero-stable linearly implicit multistep time integration schemes. Numerical tests are then performed in (§
6
) showcasing the effectiveness of the preconditioner
\mathcal{A}
in a suite of progressively challenging test cases. The full SGN equations are then solved in dimensions one and two, comparing the performance and accuracy of different time integration approaches. The Appendices provide proofs of the main theoretical results.

2.2
an alternative
constraint
form of the equations (equivalent for smooth solutions). Spatial discretizations of the constraint form will naturally lead to a differential algebraic equations (DAE) which will play a role in the development and stability analysis of the numerical schemes.

2.1
SGN Equations as Asymptotic Models of the Euler Equations

As with the shallow water equations (SWE), the SGN equations arise as asymptotic expansions of the depth averaged incompressible Euler equations. In a domain
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
, where
D=1
or
D=2
, the incompressible Euler equations for a homogeneous fluid layer of constant density
\rho
take the form:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{t}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\cdot\nabla_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_{3}p-\mbox{\boldmath$g$}\;,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{3}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$w$}

\displaystyle=0\;.

(2.1b)

For notational purposes, we separate out the horizontal spatial coordinates
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
from the vertical coordinate
z\in\mathbb{R}
. In (
2.1
),
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$u$},w)\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
is the fluid velocity with
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
being the horizontal component,
w
the vertical component;
p(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},z,t)
is the pressure;
g
is the gravitational force. Meanwhile
\nabla_{3}=(\nabla,\partial_{z})
is the (full) gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
, where
\nabla
is the gradient in the variables
x
.

As shown in Su and Gardner
[
43
]
, the SGN (or DSWEs) arise from: (I) integrating the Euler equations over the vertical column of fluid (i.e.,
z
coordinate); and (II) imposing boundary conditions at the free surface
\zeta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)
and the bottom bathymetry
h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. The derivation results in evolution equations for the new dependent variables which are the surface elevation
\zeta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)
, and the depth-averaged horizontal velocity
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)
defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)=\frac{1}{\zeta+h}\int_{-h}^{\zeta}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},z,t)\;\,\mathrm{d}z\;.

(2.2)

It is worth noting that the process of depth-averaging the Euler equations results in expressions of the form
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
arising from the (depth-averaged) nonlinear terms. To obtain a closed system of PDEs for
\zeta,\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
, the nonlinear term
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
is approximated in terms of
\zeta
and
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
under the following assumptions

\displaystyle\beta\equiv\frac{\bar{h}}{\lambda}\ll 1\;,\quad\quad\zeta/\bar{h}=O(a/\bar{h})=O(1)\;,\quad\quad|\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}|/(g\bar{h})^{1/2}=O(1)\;,\quad\quad\nabla h=O(\bar{h}/\lambda)=O(\beta)\;.

Here
\bar{h}
is the average water depth, while
a
and
\lambda
denote the characteristic amplitude and wavelength of the wave. Typical values for
a
and
\lambda
may be estimated in the initial data
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{0}
(see Figure
1
). The assumption
\beta\ll 1
is referred to as the shallow water, or long wave assumption. Note that the assumptions allow for a “large amplitude”
a
to be comparable in magnitude to the mean water depth
\bar{h}
. Likewise, the associate fluid motion
|\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}|
relative to
g\bar{h}
does not need to be asymptotically small in
\beta
and may be
O(1)
.

With the asymptotic assumptions, one can then obtain a closed system for
\zeta
and
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
correct to
O(\beta^{2})
. This yields the (dimensional form) of the Serre-Green-Naghdi (SGN) equations

\displaystyle\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)=h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\zeta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)\;,\phantom{\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\right)=0\;,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{t}+\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}+g\nabla\zeta=\frac{1}{\eta}\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}G+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}F\right)-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\eta G+F\right)\nabla h\;,

(2.3c)

where
G
and
F
are defined as

\displaystyle G=\nabla\cdot\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{t}+\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\cdot\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\right)-\left(\nabla\cdot\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\right)^{2}\;,\qquad\qquad F=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{t}\cdot\nabla h+\left(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}h\;.

(2.4)

Note that when the right-hand side of the SGN velocity equation is neglected, one recovers the (non-dispersive) shallow water equations. We therefore, also refer to (
2.3
) as the
dispersive shallow water equations
. If an additional small-amplitude assumption of
\alpha=O(a/\bar{h})\ll 1
is adopted, the system (
2.3
) can be reduced to the weakly nonlinear Boussinesq equations
[
4
]
.

For the remainder of the paper, we non-dimensionalize all variables with respect to
g
and
\bar{h}
, or, equivalently, set
g=1
,
\bar{h}=O(1)
, and drop the bar on
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}
and use
u
for the depth-averaged velocity.

2.2
Constraint Form of the SGN Equations

We now recast (
2.3
) into two evolution equations plus a PDE constraint. For classical solutions with sufficient regularity, the resulting constraint form is equivalent to (
2.3
).

First, multiply the velocity equation in (
2.3
) by
\eta
and group terms involving
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{t}
on the left to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{t}+\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$u$}+\eta\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle=\nabla\left\{\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)-\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)^{2}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}h\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\eta\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\eta\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)-\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)^{2}\right)+\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}h\right\}\nabla h\,,

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}

\displaystyle\equiv\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}\;\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)-\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\nabla h\;(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$})+\eta\nabla h\;\left(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\,.

(2.6)

Note that the last three terms in
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
encapsulate the bathymetry effect and vanish when
\nabla h=0
.

Next, commute the time derivative
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{t})=(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$})_{t}-\mathcal{G}_{\eta_{t},h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
in (
2.5
), and use the
\eta
equation in (
2.3
) to express
(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})_{t}
in terms of
\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$u$},h
. After introducing the new variable
\mbox{\boldmath$U$}=\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
, one obtains the SGN equations (or DSWEs) in
constraint form

\displaystyle\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)=h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\zeta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)\,,\phantom{\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)=0\,,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{t}+\eta\nabla\zeta+\nabla(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$})=\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$u$},h)\,,\phantom{\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$U$}-\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0\,,

(2.7d)

where the forcing
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$u$},h)
is

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$u$},h)

\displaystyle\equiv\nabla\left\{\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)-\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)^{2}\right)\right.+\eta^{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}h+\eta\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right\}

\displaystyle-\nabla h\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)-\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)^{2}\right)\right.+\eta\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.+\eta\left(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}h+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\left(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)\vphantom{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\,.

(2.8)

In equation (
2.7
), the term involving
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
has components

\displaystyle\nabla(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$})=\begin{pmatrix}(\eta uu)_{x}+(\eta uv)_{y}\\
(\eta uv)_{x}+(\eta vv)_{y}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=(u,v)\,.

The presence of the constraint in (
2.7
) highlights that spatial discretizations (e.g., via method of lines) will lead to an index-1 differential algebraic equation (DAE).

For the numerical computations (§
6
) of the SGN equations, we adopt periodic (
d=1
) or doubly periodic (
d=2
) boundary conditions for
\eta
and
u
. However, the main results for the operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
in §
4
will be formulated for both periodic and homogeneous Neumann (i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0
on
\partial\Omega
) boundary conditions.

Remark 1
.

(Conservation equation when
\nabla h=0
)
When the bathymetry is flat, i.e.,
\nabla h=0
, equations (
2.7
) yield a conservative system. In this case,
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}=\nabla W
reduces to a gradient of a scalar
W(\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
, and
\nabla\eta=\nabla\zeta
. The time-evolution equations become

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{t}+\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}+\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-W\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$U$}-\mathcal{G}_{\eta}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(2.9c)

The system (
2.9
) conserves the mass
m
, horizontal momentum
M
, and total energy
E
defined by

\displaystyle m=\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,,\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$M$}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\boldmath$U$}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,,\qquad E=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left[\zeta^{2}+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$})+\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,.

3
Mathematical Background for the Constraint Equation

This section collects the mathematical notation and background relevant for the linear operators in the paper. Specifically, we introduce (§
3.1
) the bilinear (weak) form of the operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
, and the operator
\mathcal{A}
. Subsection
3.2
provides background on conditioning numbers relevant for the use in preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) methods.

3.1
Linear Operators and Bilinear Forms

Throughout,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is either an open connected domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
, or
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}
is the (doubly-periodic) torus with side lengths
L_{x},L_{y}
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\sigma I-\alpha\nabla\cdot\nabla\,

where
\sigma,\alpha>0
are real constants (§
4
will provide formulas for
(\sigma,\alpha)
).

The bilinear forms associated to the differential operators
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
and
\mathcal{A}
will be defined on the Hilbert space
H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
given by

\displaystyle H_{\rm div}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{2}\;|\;\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}\,,

which is endowed with the norm

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\|_{\rm div}^{2}\equiv\int_{\Omega}\left[|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,.

When
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, the outward boundary unit normal,
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, is defined for almost every
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\partial\Omega
, and the normal trace of functions
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
is understood to be
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0
in
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, e.g., see
[
31
, Chapter 3.5.2]
. When
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the boundary conditions in
H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
(i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega
) are replaced by periodic boundary conditions on
u
.

Although the SGN equation solution
\eta=\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)
is a function of
t
, for the purposes of preconditioning,
t
enters only as a parameter in
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
. When discussing the weak forms we therefore consider
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
as a function of
x
only. Furthermore, we assume that
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and that
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\geq\eta_{\rm min}>0
is bounded strictly away from zero. This assumption excludes some physically relevant scenarios such as the modeling of “dry” states where
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},t)=0
in regions of the domain. Throughout we keep
\sigma>0,\alpha>0
as arbitrary constant values and use
\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star}
to refer to our concrete formulas used in the operator
\mathcal{A}
.

The bilinear forms
A:H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\times H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and
B:H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\times H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
corresponding to the weak forms of the operators
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
respectively are:

\displaystyle A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Omega}\left[\sigma\,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}+\alpha\,\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\big)\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\big)\right]\,\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\quad\quad\forall\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\,,

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Omega}\Big[\eta\,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}+\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}\,\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\big)\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\,\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\big)\big(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\phantom{\int}+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\,\big(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\big)\big(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\big)+\eta\,\big(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\big)\big(\nabla h\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\big)\Big]\,\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\quad\quad\forall\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\,.

(3.2)

The bilinear forms arise in the usual fashion from the strong form of the PDE through integration against a test function, e.g.,
A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]=(\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$v$})
and
B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]=(\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$v$})
, for
\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
(where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the
L^{2}
inner product) being twice continuously differentiable with vanishing normal component (cf.
[
40
, Chapter 12]
for additional details on operator extensions via bilinear forms).

It is clear from the definitions that both
A[\cdot,\cdot]
and
B[\cdot,\cdot]
are symmetric, i.e.,
A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]=A[\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]
and
B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]=B[\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]
. Moreover,
A[\cdot,\cdot]
is positive definite (
A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]>0
for
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\neq 0
), and it is the case
B[\cdot,\cdot]
is positive definite as well.

We will often write
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
etc. These expressions are understood via the following definitions

1.

(The operator
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
) For
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
, the function
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
is defined by:
Find
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle A[\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]

\displaystyle=B[\mbox{\boldmath$f$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]\;\quad\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\,.

(3.3)

2.

(The operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
) For
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
, the function
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}^{-1}\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
is defined by: Find
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle B[\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]

\displaystyle=A[\mbox{\boldmath$f$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]\;\quad\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\,.

(3.4)

3.

(Generalized eigenvalues) The generalized eigenvalue problem
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\lambda\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
is: Find
(\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\lambda)\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}
(
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\neq 0
) such that

\displaystyle\lambda A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]

\displaystyle=B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}]\;\quad\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)\,.

(3.5)

The fact that both
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
u
can be restricted to a real-valued function in
H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
in (
3.5
) follows from the symmetry of the bilinear forms.

3.2
Conjugate Gradient and Conditioning Numbers

Conjugate gradient (CG) is an iterative Krylov method for solving linear matrix systems of the form
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
where
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is symmetric positive definite (in exact arithmetic, CG converges after a finite number of iterations). A straight-forward, but not necessarily sharp
[
44
, Chapter 38]
, bound on the convergence of CG is given in terms of the conditioning number
\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$A$})\equiv\lambda_{\rm max}/\lambda_{\rm min}
where
\lambda_{\rm max}
,
\lambda_{\rm min}
are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
A
:

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{k}\|_{A}\leq 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{k}\|\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{0}\|_{A}\,,\qquad\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{A}\equiv\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,.

(3.6)

In (
3.6
)
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{k}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}
is the error after
k
iterations of CG,
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
is the exact solution.

The convergence of CG may be improved (such as in settings where
A
has a large conditioning numbers) by solving a preconditioned system with a symmetric positive definite
P
. Applying CG to the following system, which shares the same solution as
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}\quad\quad\textrm{with}\quad\quad\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\,,

(3.7)

results in a preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method. It is worth emphasizing that practical implementations of PCG avoid computations of matrix square roots and only require linear system solves involving
P
. The conditioning number of the PCG matrix in (
3.7
) is then modified from that of
A
to be the ratio of maximum to minimum eigenvalues of
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}
. Namely, the conditioning number, and upper bound on the convergence rate may be (significantly) reduced if
P
is “close” to
A
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is used as a preconditioner for
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
, the operator analogue of
\kappa
for symmetric operators is

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})\equiv\frac{\mu_{\rm\max}}{\mu_{\rm\min}}\,,

(3.8)

where
\mu_{\min}
and
\mu_{\max}
are the (generalized) Rayleigh quotients

\displaystyle\mu_{\min}=\inf_{0\neq\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)}\frac{B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]}{A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]}\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\mu_{\max}=\sup_{0\neq\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega)}\frac{B[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]}{A[\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}]}\,.

(3.9)

The quantity
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
measures the ratio of the largest to smallest values in the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
. Not only does
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
play the operator analogue of the PCG matrix conditioning number, but it also appears as a bound in the convergence of operator/Hilbert space versions of preconditioned gradient descent (see
[
18
]
) and PCG (e.g., variable coefficient Laplacian
[
11
]
). A smaller value of
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
will yield a tighter bound on the convergence of PCG.

Bounds on the continuum operators also carry over to the discrete setting as well.

Remark 2

(Conditioning number bound for spatially discrete problems)
.

When the operator bilinear forms
A[\cdot,\cdot]
and
B[\cdot,\cdot]
are discretized via finite element methods, the quantity
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
is a mesh-independent upper bound on the discrete matrix preconditioned conditioning number (see also
[
18
]
).

Namely, let
V\subset H_{\rm div}(\Omega)
be any finite dimensional subspace with basis
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in V
. The discrete matrices that arise from the weak form (
3.5
) are symmetric and positive definite

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$A$}_{ij}=A[v_{i},v_{j}]\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$G$}_{ij}=B[v_{i},v_{j}]\qquad\textrm{for}\;i,j=1,\ldots,n\,.

Then
\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$G$})\leq\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
. In particular, the discrete conditioning number
\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$G$})
is bounded independent of the mesh. This ensures that the convergence rate of PCG is bounded independent of the mesh.
\clubsuit

4
Preconditioner for the Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}

In this section we introduce the constant coefficient preconditioner

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\sigma^{\star}\,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\alpha^{\star}\,\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$u$})\,,

(4.1)

and establish bounds on the generalized eigenvalues and conditioning number of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
. These bounds will play an important role in ensuring convergence for preconditioned conjugate gradient methods or linear stability in semi-implicit time-stepping.

The coefficients in (
4.1
) for
\mathcal{A}
are defined in terms of pointwise estimates of
\eta
and
h

\displaystyle|\nabla h|_{\rm max}\equiv\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\,,\quad\quad\eta_{\rm\min}\equiv\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,,\quad\quad\eta_{\rm\max}\equiv\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,.

(4.2)

•

For flat bathymetry (
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}=0
) we set

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}\equiv\eta_{\rm max}\,,\qquad\qquad\alpha^{\star}\equiv\tfrac{1}{3}\eta_{\rm max}^{3}\,,\qquad\qquad\kappa_{\rm ub}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\,.

(4.3)

•

For variable bathymetry (
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}\neq 0
) we take

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}

\displaystyle\equiv\,\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\tfrac{1}{6}(4+\sqrt{13})\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\,,\quad\quad\alpha^{\star}\equiv\tfrac{1}{6}(4+\sqrt{13})\,\eta_{\rm max}^{3}\,,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm ub}

\displaystyle\equiv\,\max\left\{\frac{\sigma^{\star}}{\eta_{\rm min}},\left(\frac{4+\sqrt{13}}{4-\sqrt{13}}\right)\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\right\}\,.

(4.5)

Note that
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}
quantifies the maximum slope of the bathymetry with
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}=0
corresponding to a flat bottom. When solving the SGN equations,
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}
can be determined as a one-time computation since
h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
is prescribed as part of the problem data.

The significance of the coefficients is highlighted by the next theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
\eta,\nabla h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
\eta_{\rm min}>0
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
be defined as above and
\mathcal{A}
defined with the coefficients (
4.3
)–(
4.4
). Then the condition number
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})\leq\kappa_{\rm ub}\,.

(4.6)

Moreover, the generalized eigenvalues
\lambda
of
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\lambda\,\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
are real and lie in the interval

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\rm ub})^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq 1\,.

(4.7)

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is deferred to Appendix
A
.

The inequalities (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are important for several reasons. Firstly, smaller values of
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
are desirable as they provide stronger performance bounds for preconditioned conjugate gradient when solving systems involving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
.

Without the use of a preconditioner
\mathcal{A}
, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
would be unbounded and bounds such as (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) would not exist. This would render methods such as conjugate gradient far less effective (or fail entirely) for solving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}
. While the bounds (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are formulated in terms of PDE operators, they provide mesh-independent PCG convergence rates for the spatially discrete problem as well (see Remark
2
)

Secondly, when semi-implicit time-stepping approaches are used to solve the SGN equations, the clustering of eigenvalues
\lambda
near
1
is important to ensure linear stability.

The formula (
4.5
) highlights two ways in which the upper bound on
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
may diverge and lead to a loss in practical effectiveness of
\mathcal{A}

•

(Large contrast ratio) If
\eta_{\max}/\eta_{\min}\gg 1
, and the second term in (
4.5
) dominates then
\kappa_{\rm ub}\sim(\eta_{\max}/\eta_{\min})^{3}
.

•

(Large variations in bathymetry) If
\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\gg 1
so that the first term in (
4.5
) dominates, then at worst
\kappa_{\rm ub}\sim\eta_{\max}|\nabla h|_{\max}^{2}
.

We conclude this section with several remarks.

Remark 3
.

(Robustness of the coefficients
\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star}
) One may ask if alternative formulas may be used for
(\sigma,\alpha)
and still yield an upper bound on
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
? It turns out that
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
is bounded for any positive coefficients
\sigma,\alpha>0
in
\mathcal{A}
. Alternative choices of
\sigma,\alpha
may however yield larger values of
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h})
and decrease the efficiency of the preconditioner
\mathcal{A}
or increase the error constant in semi-implicit time integration. In practice, this robustness allows for some flexibility in under or overestimating the formulas for
\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star}
. It turns out, see Appendix
B
, that the choice of
\mathcal{A}
is
quasi-optimal
within a class of constant coefficient operators.

\clubsuit

Remark 4
.

(Replacing
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}
with
\eta_{\rm max}|\nabla h|_{\rm max}^{2}
in
\mathcal{A}
) The coefficient
\sigma^{\star}
relies on the maximum pointwise bound for
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}
. In some practical settings, it may be simpler to individually estimate
|\nabla h|_{\rm max}
and
\eta_{\rm max}
separately. Instead of (
4.4
), one can take:

\mathcal{A}=\sigma^{\star\star}I-\alpha^{\star\star}\nabla\nabla\cdot
where

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star\star}=\eta_{\rm max}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{6}(4+\sqrt{13})|\nabla h|_{\rm max}^{2}\right)\,,\qquad\alpha^{\star\star}=\tfrac{1}{6}(4+\sqrt{13})\,\eta_{\rm max}^{3}\,.

(4.8)

These coefficients still ensure the eigenvalue bound (
4.7
) and conditioning number bound (
4.6
) where
\kappa_{\rm ub}
is replaced with a (less optimal) bound

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm ub}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{6}(4+\sqrt{13})|\nabla h|_{\rm max}^{2}\right),\left(\frac{4+\sqrt{13}}{4-\sqrt{13}}\right)\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\right\}\,.

(4.9)

Unlike (
4.4
), these coefficients are not
quasi-optimal
in the sense discussed in Appendix
B
, and can result in a drop in PCG performance (see §
6.2
). However, they are still effective coefficients for both PCG and to define the implicit
\mathcal{A}
in semi-implicit time-stepping.
\clubsuit

Remark 5

(Riesz Preconditioner)
.

The choice of
A[\cdot,\cdot]
can be viewed as designing a Riesz preconditioner for the operator
B[\cdot,\cdot]
; for a discussion of Riesz preconditioners in the context of bilinear forms see
[
18
,
28
,
11
]
.

5
Time-stepping for the SGN Equations

This section provides details on the time integration schemes we use to solve (
2.7
). Subsections
5.1
and
5.2
present explicit Runge-Kutta (ERK) and multistep methods that handle the constraint through preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) methods. Subsection
5.3
presents linearly implicit multistep (ImEx-type) schemes that treat
\mathcal{A}
implicitly and
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
explicitly.

The linearly implicit multistep schemes admit several advantages—they require only one linear solve involving
\mathcal{A}
per time step and they do not suffer from order reduction (cf. Remark
7
). However, linear implicit schemes do require a precise choice of
\mathcal{A}
to ensure zero-stability, which will be discussed in subsection
5.4
.

In this section, it is useful to view the SGN system (
2.7
) as an evolution equation with a constraint

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\mbox{\boldmath$U$}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla\cdot(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$})\\
-\eta\nabla\zeta-\nabla\left(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)+\mbox{\boldmath$F$}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$U$}\,,

(5.1b)

where
F
is defined in (
2.8
). Spatial discretizations of (
5.1
) via a method-of-lines give rise to a differential algebraic equation (DAE)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\,,

(5.2a)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$g$}(\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})

\displaystyle=0\,,

(5.2b)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$g$}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Specifically, the ODE variables are spatial approximations given by
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\approx(\eta,\mbox{\boldmath$U$})^{T}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}\approx\mbox{\boldmath$u$}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}\approx(-\nabla\cdot(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}),\;-\eta\nabla\zeta-\nabla(\eta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$})+\mbox{\boldmath$F$})^{T}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$g$}\approx\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\mbox{\boldmath$U$}
.

In general, (
5.2
) will be an
index
-1 DAE since the constraint equation (
5.2b
) can be solved to implicitly define
p
as a function of
w
. This is because
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
is an invertible operator (§
4
), and matrix approximations to
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
constructed by testing
B[\cdot,\cdot]
with Galerkin or finite element subspaces of
H_{\rm div}
are also invertible.

For simplicity we adopt constant time steps
\Delta t>0
where
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, however the results for Runge-Kutta methods naturally extend to variable step sizes. The fluid variables at time
t_{n}
are written as
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}\approx\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(t_{n})
,
\eta_{n}\approx\eta(t_{n})
,
\zeta_{n}\approx\zeta(t_{n})
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{n}\approx\mbox{\boldmath$U$}(t_{n})
.

Remark 6
.

While the focus here is on time discretizations of (
2.7
), the same discretization approaches can be applied to the non-constraint form of the equations (
2.3
).

5.1
Explicit Runge-Kutta with Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient

We discretize the SGN equations (
2.7
) via the classical explicit RK4 scheme defined by the Butcher coefficients

\tfrac{1}{2}&&&\\

This yields the following equations for the stages

(5.3b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\eta_{n}^{(i)},h}\;\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{n}^{(j)}\,,\phantom{\sum_{i=1}^{i-1}}

(5.3c)

where

. The final update is

\displaystyle\eta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\left(-\nabla\cdot\left(\eta_{n}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}^{(j)}\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\left(-\eta_{n}^{(j)}\nabla\zeta_{n}^{(j)}-\nabla\left(\eta_{n}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{n}^{(j)}\right)+\mbox{\boldmath$F$}_{n}^{(j)}\right)\,.

While RK4 is an explicit method, the algebraic constraint in (

) still requires a linear solve for
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
at each stage. To solve (

), we apply PCG with the preconditioner
\mathcal{A}
defined by the coefficients (

). Since each solve is independent, one can change the coefficients of
\mathcal{A}
as needed for each stage or step.

Remark 7

(Order reduction in RK schemes)
.

Runge-Kutta methods may exhibit a reduction in order when applied to initial boundary value problems. The reduction often stems from the presence of boundary conditions or a lack of regularity in the solution. Due to the periodic domain, the test cases we employ do not exhibit order reduction. However, the RK schemes presented in this section could exhibit order reduction when applied to problems with time-dependent boundary conditions (multistep methods do not exhibit order reduction).
\clubsuit

5.4
Zero-stability for Linear Index-1 Algebraic Equations

In this subsection we establish, in the spirit of absolute stability, conditions for zero-stability of (
5.9
) applied to linear time-independent problems. Although we focus on constant time-independent problems, the result provides a guide for ensuring zero-stability of the equations (
5.10
).
We say that the scheme is
zero-stable
if the dynamics (
5.9
) with
\Delta t=0
yield bounded iterates; zero-stability is a necessary condition for convergence.

Herein, assume a linear constraint
\mbox{\boldmath$g$}(\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}-\mbox{\boldmath$w$}
, with invertible matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. The scheme (
5.9
) with
\Delta t=0
reads:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{s}a_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{n+j}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(5.12a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{s}c_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}b_{j}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}-\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{n+j}\big)\,,

(5.12b)

where
(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,s
are given as initial data. Next, introduce the following polynomials defined in terms of the time-stepping coefficients

\displaystyle a(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{s}a_{j}z^{j}\,,\qquad b(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}z^{j}\,,\qquad c(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{s}c_{j}z^{j}\,.

(5.13)

Equation (
5.12a
) defines a stable linear recurrence for
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{n}
provided
a(z)
satisfies the
root condition
: the roots of
a(z)
lie inside the unit disk with only simple roots on the boundary. Every practical linear multistep method (including both AB2, AB3, AB4 and SBDF2) has an
a(z)
satisfying the root condition.

With
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{n}
completely determined by (
5.12a
), equation (
5.12b
) defines a forced linear recurrence for
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n}
. The stability of
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n}
then depends on the homogeneous equation (
5.12b
) (i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{n}\equiv 0
) which takes the form

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{s}c_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}b_{j}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}-\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n+j}\big)\,.

(5.14)

A family of eigenvector solutions to (
5.14
) then follow as: let
(\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{p}$},\lambda)
satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{p}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{p}$}\,\qquad(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{p}$}\neq 0)\,.

(5.15)

Substituting the ansatz
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{n}=z^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{p}$}
into (
5.14
) yields

\displaystyle c(z)-b(z)+\lambda b(z)=0\,.

(5.16)

A sufficient condition for stability of (
5.14
) is then ensured if each solution of (
5.16
) has
|z|<1
. Following in the spirit of absolute stability, introduce the region of zero stability as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\equiv\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\;\big|\;|z|<1\textrm{ holds for each $z$ satisfying }\eqref{Eq:CharEq}\big\}\,.

(5.17)

Note that
\mathcal{D}
is a property of the time-stepping coefficients only. One then has the following.

Theorem 5.1

(Sufficient conditions for zero-stability of DAEs with linear constraints)
.

If
a(z)
satisfies the root condition, and
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}
for each
\lambda
satisfying (
5.15
), then the dynamics (
5.12
) are stable.

Figure
2
presents the stability regions
\mathcal{D}
for SBDF2. For reference, the stability regions for SBDF1 and SBDF3, which are not used in Section
6
, are shown. Up to a reflection in the imaginary axis and shift, the sets
\mathcal{D}
are identical to those characterized in
[

]
. The figures show that SBDF1 and SBDF2 always contains the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
. In contrast, depending on the variables
\eta
and
h
, it is possible for the largest eigenvalue to lie outside the SBDF3 stability region—rendering SBDF3 unstable. Note that by modifying the SBDF coefficients for orders
3,4
and
5
as in
[
41
]
, it is possible to always devise time-stepping schemes where the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
lie in the stability regions
\mathcal{D}
. We do not explore these approaches here.

6.1
Spatial Discretizations

Throughout this section we adopt a domain with periodic (in
d=1
) or doubly-periodic (
d=2
) boundary conditions, e.g.,

\displaystyle\eta(x,y+L_{y})=\eta(x+L_{x},y)=\eta(x,y)\;,\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(x,y+L_{y})=\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(x+L_{x},y)=\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(x,y)\;,

(6.4)

where
\Omega=(0,L_{x})\times(0,L_{y})
with
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
being the periods in the
x
- and
y
-directions, respectively. We then discretize space with an equispaced grid of
n
points in each direction, e.g., in dimension
d=1
, the grid spacing
\Delta x
and mesh points are

\displaystyle\Delta x=\frac{L}{n}\qquad x_{j}=j\,\Delta x\qquad 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n-1}=L-\Delta x\,.

Functions
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(x)
are discretized as
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}(x_{j})\approx u_{j}
with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n-1})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

Function derivatives are obtained spectrally as

\displaystyle u_{x}(x_{j})\approx(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u})_{j}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{F}^{-1}(\imath\mathbf{K})\mathbf{F}\,,

where
\mathbf{F}
is the discrete Fourier transform matrix and
\mathbf{K}
is a diagonal matrix
consisting of discrete wavenumbers of the form

\displaystyle k_{j}=\frac{2\pi}{L}j\qquad j=-\frac{n}{2}+1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1\,.

We adopt the convention that the
n/2
wavenumber is
0
. The operators
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
and
\mathcal{A}
are then discretized with spectrally accurate approximations (we suppress the parameter dependence of
\eta
and
h
on
\mathbf{G}
)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}\mbox{\boldmath$u$})(x_{j})\approx(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u})_{j}\qquad(\mathcal{A}\mbox{\boldmath$u$})(x_{j})\approx(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u})_{j}\qquad(j=0,\ldots,n-1)\,.

(6.5)

For example, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
has the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\sigma^{\star}\mathbf{I}+\alpha^{\star}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}\mathbf{D}\,,

where
\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}=\overline{\mathbf{D}}^{T}
is the conjugate transpose of the derivative matrix
\mathbf{D}
. By construction, both
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{G}
are real symmetric and positive definite.

In presenting numerical results, we make use of both the (discrete)
2-
and
\infty-
norms

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,\Delta x}^{2}=\Delta x\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\qquad\qquad\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq i\leq n-1}|u_{i}|\,.

We also use the
2-
norm induced by a matrix
\mathbf{M}\succ 0
(which will generally be set to
\mathbf{G}
)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,\Delta x,\mathbf{M}}^{2}\equiv\left(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}\right)\,\Delta x\qquad(\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n})\,.

With these notations, note that
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,\Delta x}^{2}=\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,\Delta x,\mathbf{I}}^{2}
. For the equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\,,

(6.6)

let
\mathbf{u}^{*}\equiv\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
be the exact solution. In convergence studies where preconditioned conjugate gradient is used to solve (
6.6
), we track the error

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})\equiv\frac{\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{*}\|_{2,\Delta x,\mathbf{G}}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2,\Delta x}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2,\Delta x,\mathbf{G}^{-1}}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2,\Delta x}}\,.

(6.7)

Here
\varepsilon
measures the discrete error of
\mathbf{u}
(in the
\mathbf{G}
norm), or equivalently the discrete residual
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}
(in the
\mathbf{G}^{-1}
norm). Note that we use
\varepsilon
as a primary measure of the PCG error, as opposed to the
2
-, or
\infty
-norm, since PCG minimizes
\varepsilon
over progressively larger Krylov subspaces. This ensures that
\varepsilon
is non-increasing with each iteration of PCG.

The generalized eigenvalues of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{A}
are then

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{k}\qquad(\mathbf{u}_{k}\neq 0)\,

(6.8)

and, without loss of generality, can be ordered as

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}\,.

For dimension
d=2
, we extend all the definitions in the section in the natural way via tensor products.

7
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work we have introduced a constant coefficient operator
\mathcal{A}
as a means to devise time integration approaches for the SGN equations that require only matrix-vector products involving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
, i.e., are
matrix-free
. The coefficients are rooted in theory, are shown to be quasi-optimal (within a class of constant coefficient operators) and enable mesh-independent performance of preconditioned conjugate gradient methods. Furthermore, the operator
\mathcal{A}
enables the use of linearly implicit time integration that reduces the total number of linear solves involving
\mathcal{A}
to one per time step. The computational theory is further supported by a suite of test cases.

Solving linear systems involving the constant coefficient operator
\mathcal{A}
is advantageous over direct solves involving
\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
since the latter is variable coefficient in both space and time. The formulas for
\mathcal{A}
and bounds provided here may also provide avenues for other efficient computational approaches. These potentially include successive convolution where the analytic and fast application of the Green’s function for
\mathcal{A}
can be applied; exponential integrators since
\exp(\mathcal{A})
may be readily evaluated using the Fourier transform, or boundary integral methods. Such approaches could be particularly useful in the context of large scale computations (e.g., modeling the ocean) as the Green’s function for
\mathcal{A}
can be analytically approximated and locally applied thereby avoiding the need to solve large linear systems. This would help to enable the use of physical models that incorporate dispersion for the simulation of waves in oceanography.

Appendix B
Quasi-optimality of the Conditioning Number Bound

The proof of the main result relies on
(\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star},\kappa_{\rm ub})
satisfying (MI). One might then wonder if there is a set of coefficients satisfying (MI) that yield a (fundamentally) tighter conditioning number bound on
\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{\eta,h}
? To answer this question, consider the optimal conditioning number bound obtained by coefficients satisfying (MI)

\displaystyle(P)\qquad

Minimize:

\displaystyle\quad\gamma

Subject to:

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{\boldmath$M$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\preceq\gamma\,\mbox{\boldmath$M$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,\quad\textrm{holds for}\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\,,

Note that (P) is a convex problem since the objective function
\gamma
is linear and the constraints on
\sigma
and
\alpha
are an intersection of a family of linear matrix inequalities. Denote the solution to (P) as
\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
.

The answer turns out to be that no other set of coefficients satisfying (MI) can avoid the scalings of
\nabla h
and
\eta
in
\kappa_{\rm ub}
.

Theorem B.1
.

For any functions
\eta,\nabla h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\eta_{\rm min}>0
, the bound
\kappa_{\rm ub}
satisfies

\displaystyle\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}\leq\kappa_{\rm ub}\leq\left(\tfrac{4+\sqrt{13}}{4-\sqrt{13}}\right)\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}\,.

(B.1)

In the case of a flat bathymetry (
\nabla h=0
) then
\kappa_{\rm ub}=\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
.

The inequality (
B.1
) shows that
\kappa_{\rm ub}
is always within a constant factor of
\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
. Thus the fundamental scaling of
\eta
and
\nabla h
in
\kappa_{\rm ub}
cannot be avoided via other coefficients satisfying (MI).

To establish the result, consider a fixed pair
\eta
and
\nabla h
and introduce the feasible constraint set in (P) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\{(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\Big|\;\mbox{\boldmath$M$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\preceq\gamma\,\mbox{\boldmath$M$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right\}\,,

(B.2)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\Big|\;\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\quad\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\preceq\gamma\,\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\right\}\,.

(B.3)

Here
\widetilde{M}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
is defined in (
A.6
) and the second line follows from the same Householder reflection in Appendix
A
. The set
\mathcal{S}
is also non-empty since
\eta_{\rm max},|\nabla h|_{\rm max}
are assumed to be finite.

Case 1: A flat bathymetry
(\nabla h=0)
When
\nabla h=0
the coefficients
(\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star},\kappa_{\rm ub})
solve (P).

In this case the matrix inequality in
\mathcal{S}
reduces to two linear inequalities defined by the diagonals of
\mbox{\boldmath$M$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\big|\;

\displaystyle\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\;\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\leq\alpha\leq\gamma\;\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\,\quad\forall x\in\Omega\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\big|\;

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm max}\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\eta_{\rm\min}\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}_{\rm max}\leq\alpha\leq\gamma\,\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}_{\rm min}\Big\}\,.

(B.4)

The second line follows from taking the (essential) supremum and infimum over
x
. Thus,
\mathcal{S}
is polyhedral—it consists of two pairs of linear inequalities.

The coefficients
(\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star},\kappa_{\rm ub})
for
\mathcal{A}
then exactly solve (P), since

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}=\eta_{\rm max}\,,\quad\alpha^{\star}=\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}_{\rm max}\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}=\max\left\{\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}},\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\right\}=\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}=\kappa_{\rm ub}\,.

While the choice of
\alpha^{\star}
is unique, any value
\sigma\in[\eta_{\rm max},\kappa_{\rm ub}\eta_{\rm min}]
yields the optimal
\kappa_{\rm ub}
value.

Case 2: A variable bathymetry
(\nabla h\neq 0)

Here the set
\mathcal{S}
is no longer polyhedral, and the coefficients
(\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star},\kappa_{\rm ub})
for
\mathcal{A}
will be quasi-optimal solutions of (P).

We first bound
\mathcal{S}
from the inside and outside with polyhedral sets.

Proposition B.2

(Polyhedral bounds on
\mathcal{S}
)
.

For a fixed
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
and
\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, we have:

(a)

(Outer polyhedral bound of
\mathcal{S}
) Any
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
necessarily satisfies

\left.\begin{aligned} \max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)&\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\eta_{\rm min}\\
\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}_{\rm max}&\leq\alpha\leq\gamma\,\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}_{\rm min}\end{aligned}\;\right\}\,.

(B.5)

(b)

(Inner polyhedral bound of
\mathcal{S}
) If
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)
satisfies

\left.\begin{aligned} \max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{+}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)&\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\eta_{\rm min}\\
\lambda_{+}\eta_{\rm max}^{3}&\leq\alpha\leq\gamma\,\lambda_{-}\,\eta_{\rm min}^{3}\end{aligned}\;\right\}\,,

(B.6)

then
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
.
Here
\lambda_{\pm}
are eigenvalues defined in (
A.7
).

The main idea of the proof is to bound
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
in (
A.6
) with diagonal matrices.

Proof.

(Of Proposition
B.2
)

(a) Let
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
. Using the fact that semidefinite matrices have non-negative diagonal entries, comparing the diagonals in the matrix inequality for
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
in (
A.6
), shows that
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)
satisfy:

\left.\begin{aligned} \eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\leq\sigma&\leq\gamma\,\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\\
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\leq\sigma&\leq\gamma\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\\
\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\leq\alpha&\leq\gamma\,\tfrac{1}{3}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{aligned}\right\}\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\,,

(B.7)

Note that the first inequality in (
B.7
) imposes a tighter upper bound on
\sigma
while the second inequality in (
B.7
) imposes a tighter lower bound on
\sigma
. Maximizing and minimizing (
B.7
) over
x
, and combining the two
\sigma
inequalities together yields (
B.5
).

(b) Substituting the matrix inequality (
A.7
) into the definition of
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
shows that
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
(for almost every
x
) satisfies the following bound

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{-}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}&0\\
0&\lambda_{-}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{pmatrix}\,

\displaystyle\preceq\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{+}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}&0\\
0&\lambda_{+}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{pmatrix}\,.

Thus, the matrix linear inequality in
\mathcal{S}
for
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
can be ensured if
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)
satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\preceq\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{-}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}&0\\
0&\lambda_{-}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{pmatrix}\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\,,

and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{+}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}&0\\
0&\lambda_{+}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{pmatrix}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\,.

We thus observe that if
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)
satisfy

\left.\begin{aligned} \eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})&\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\\
\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{+}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}&\leq\sigma\leq\gamma\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{-}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\\
\lambda_{+}\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})&\leq\alpha\leq\gamma\,\lambda_{-}\,\eta^{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\end{aligned}\;\right\}\quad\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega\,,

(B.8)

then
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
. Just as with, (
B.7
), the first inequality in (
B.8
) imposes a tighter upper bound on
\sigma
while the second imposes a tighter lower bound. Maximizing and minimizing (
B.8
) over
x
, and combining the two
\sigma
inequalities together yields (
B.6
).
∎

The coefficients for
\mathcal{A}
are in fact not only feasible, but yield the tightest conditioning bounds provided by the inner approximation

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}\equiv\,\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\lambda_{+}\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\,,\quad\quad\alpha^{\star}\equiv\lambda_{+}\,\eta_{\rm max}^{3}\,,\quad

(B.9)

along with

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm ub}\equiv\,\max\left\{\frac{\sigma^{\star}}{\eta_{\rm min}},\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\right\}\,.

(B.10)

Of Theorem
B.1
.

The lower bound in (
B.1
) follows immediately since
(\sigma^{\star},\alpha^{\star},\kappa_{\rm ub})
is a feasible point of (P) and
\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
is the global optimum of (P).

The upper bound in (
B.1
) is a consequence of the polyhedral bound (
B.5
) in Proposition
B.2
(a). Namely, every set of points in
\mathcal{S}
, including the optimal value
\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
satisfies

\displaystyle\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}\geq\Gamma\equiv\max\left\{\frac{1}{{\eta_{\rm min}}}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right),\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm max}}{\eta_{\rm min}}\right)^{3}\right\}\,,

(B.11)

Here
\Gamma
is the larger of the two lower bounds on
\gamma
provided by (
B.5
).

Next, note that we can bound
\kappa_{\rm ub}
from above by a constant factor of
\Gamma
. Since
\lambda_{+}>1>\lambda_{-}>0
and
\eta
is non-negative, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}\leq\lambda_{+}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right)\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm ub}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}\eta_{\rm min}}\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega}\,\left(\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\eta(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|\nabla h(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})|^{2}\right),\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\frac{\eta_{\rm max}^{3}}{\eta_{\rm min}^{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\lambda_{-}}\Gamma\,.

Combining this with (
B.11
) yields the upper bound in (
B.1
).
∎

Remark 10

(Alternatives to
\lambda_{\pm}
)
.

The choice of eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
for upper and lower bounds in (
A.7
) is not unique. In fact, the following diagonal matrix bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(2+\sqrt{3})}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tfrac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{1}{2}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}&\tfrac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix}\preceq\tfrac{1}{2}(2+\sqrt{3})\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tfrac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix}

(B.12)

can be used to obtain (slightly) tighter inner and outer polyhedral bounds on
\mathcal{S}
. The bound (
B.12
) can also be used to obtain a set of
(\sigma,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
with a (slightly) improved guarantee
\gamma\leq(2+\sqrt{3})^{2}\gamma^{\star}_{\rm P}
. However in practice, we found that there was no practical improvement using these coefficients.
