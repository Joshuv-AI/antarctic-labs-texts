The adverse effect of mood swings on the risk of cardiovascular diseases: Evidence from Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Yuanhui Dai, Yuqi Hao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recent studies have explored the impact of personality traits, including mood swings, on physical health. However, it remains unclear whether there is a direct cause-and-effect link between mood swings and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). A STROBE-compliant cross-sectional observational study was conducted and analyzed using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to examine the potential causal relationship between mood swings and a range of CVDs, such as arrhythmia, artery aneurysm, coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure, hypertension, stroke, ischemic stroke, and peripheral artery disease. We sourced genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary data for mood swings from the UK Biobank, and for CVDs from the GWAS Catalog and FinnGen databases. We excluded single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to potential confounders such as obesity, smoking, sex, diabetes, as well as SNPs suspected of horizontal pleiotropy, as identified by MR-PRESSO and the MR-pleiotropy method, prior to the final analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the MR-Egger, inverse variance weighted, and leave-one-out methods. After screening, 57 SNPs were identified as instrumental variables for mood swings, and 9 SNPs related to confounding factors were excluded. An increase in mood swing frequency is correlated with a significant increase in the likelihood of various conditions. Notably, arrhythmia in the FinnGen dataset showed an odds ratio (OR: 2.28, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.44–3.61, P < .001), and atrial fibrillation had an OR (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.23–4.11, P = .01). CHD risk was elevated in both the IEU OpenGWAS project (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.30–3.21, P < .001) and GWAS Catalog (OR: 4.45, 95% CI: 1.75–11.33, P < .001). Increased risks were also noted for heart failure (GWAS Catalog: OR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.09–2.83, P = .02) and hypertension (FinnGen: OR, 2.17; 95% CI: 1.47–3.19, P < .001). However, no significant associations were found for conditions such as arterial aneurysms or ischemic stroke. In combined analyses, mood swings were associated with a higher risk of CHD (OR: 2.21, 95% CI: 1.64–2.97, P < .01), heart failure (OR: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.21–2.50, P < .01), and other CVDs. This study revealed a causal link between mood swings and various CVDs, highlighting intriguing findings. This suggests that implementing proper psychological interventions to stabilize mood may be beneficial for preventing negative cardiovascular events.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a major global health challenge, leading to a significant number of deaths and disabilities around the world.[1] These conditions affect the heart, brain, and blood vessels in the limbs,[2] with varying incidence rates influenced by genetics, lifestyle, environmental factors, and socioeconomic status.[3,4] Common types of CVDs include coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, heart failure, arrhythmias, peripheral arterial disease, and aortic aneurysms, all of which pose serious health risks. The World Health Organization identifies CVDs as the primary cause of death globally, with approximately 17.9 million people dying from these diseases every year. This accounts for approximately 31% of all deaths worldwide, with the highest toll observed in low-income countries.[5]

Mood swings have a profound effect on both mental and physical aspects of human health.[6] These swings involve rapid and intense changes in emotional state, significantly influencing an individual’s feelings. Research has shown that mood swings are linked to mental health issues such as bipolar disorder, depression, and anxiety, which can weaken the human immune system.[7] A large-scale epidemiological study from the UK Biobank found that 44.4% of its participants (out of 488,105) experienced mood swings in their daily lives.[8] Previous studies have suggested in prior research that mood swings, as a psychiatric condition, may be related to oxidative stress.[9] This condition can reduce the secretion of total bilirubin, a critical antioxidant, during oxidative stress.[10] A decrease in bilirubin levels may lead to elevated blood pressure, thereby increasing the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage.[11] Consequently, it is hypothesized that mood swings can significantly influence the pathological processes of CVDs. Indeed, substantial evidence indicates a link between mental health issues (such as depression, mental instability, and mood swings) and CVDs.[12] The American Heart Association also noted an association between psychological health and CVDs.[13] Additionally, an observational study indicated that variations in physical and mental health significantly affect CVDs among postmenopausal women.[14]

While a connection between mood swings and CVDs has been observed, the precise cause-and-effect relationship remains unclear. Notably, no study has yet applied the Mendelian randomization (MR) technique to explore this potential causal link between mood swings and CVDs. MR analysis, a tool used in epidemiology and genetics, helps identify causal relationships between exposures and outcomes.[15] It employs genetic variants as instrumental variables, providing insights akin to a randomized controlled trial by utilizing the natural randomization of genetic variation. Compared with traditional observational studies, this method is less prone to bias from confounding factors. In our research, we used MR analysis to examine the relationship between mood swings and various CVDs, such as arrhythmia, arterial aneurysm (including abdominal and thoracic types), stroke (including ischemic stroke), coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure, hypertension, and peripheral artery disease. Our goal was to obtain clinical therapeutic insights at the genetic level (Fig. 1).

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data collection and ethics statement

Our research utilized existing data from large genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary databases and did not involve collecting new data from participants. Therefore, ethical approval was not required. MR analysis is guided by 3 core principles: (1) genetic instrumental variables (IVs) must be strongly linked to the factors under investigation. (2) The IVs should not be related to any potential confounding factors. (3) IVs must not be directly associated with the outcome traits of interest. Through a meticulous process of selecting appropriate IVs, excluding any that might introduce confounding effects, and conducting MR and sensitivity analyses, we were able to estimate the relationship between mood swings and various CVDs.

2.2. Data extraction

We gathered data on mood swings and 11 cardiovascular-related diseases from large GWAS summary databases, including FinnGen,[16] UK Biobank,[8] IEU OpenGWAS project,[17,18] and GWAS Catalog.[19] Summary statistics for mood swings were obtained from a large-scale GWAS meta-analysis conducted by the UK Biobank, which involved 204,412 cases and 247,207 controls, totaling 451,619 samples.[8] For specific CVDs, we selected data on arrhythmia (59,182 cases and 204,429 controls), arterial aneurysm (7603 cases and 317,899 controls), abdominal arterial aneurysm (3201 cases and 317,899 controls), and thoracic arterial aneurysm (7321 cases and 317,899 controls) from the FinnGen database.[16] Atrial fibrillation data were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (60,620 cases and 970,216 controls)[20] and the FinnGen database (40,594 cases and 168,000 controls).[16] Data on CHD were sourced from the IEU OpenGWAS project (60,801 cases and 123,504 controls),[18] GWAS Catalog (22,233 cases and 64,762 controls),[21] and the FinnGen database (39,036 cases and 303,463 controls).[16] Heart failure statistics were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (47,309 cases and 930,014 controls)[22] and the FinnGen database (24,008 cases and 299,879 controls).[16] Hypertension data were collected from the FinnGen (98,683 cases and 243,756 controls)[16] and GWAS Catalog (129,909 cases and 354,689 controls)[23] databases. Ischemic stroke information was sourced from the GWAS Catalog (34,217 cases and 406,111 controls)[24] and the IEU OpenGWAS project (10,307 cases and 19,326 controls).[18] Peripheral artery disease data were obtained from FinnGen (7098 cases and 211,694 controls)[16] and GWAS Catalog (11,226 cases and 649,565 controls).[25] Stroke statistics were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (40,585 cases and 406,111 controls)[24] and the FinnGen database (34,560 cases and 249,480 controls)[16] (Table 1).

2.3. Selection of instrumental variables

The process of our study is illustrated in Figure 2. Initially, we identified valid IVs in our dataset. The single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) proposed for the exposure group were selected based on a significance threshold of P < 5E-8 to serve as candidate IVs. Subsequently, we determined the linkage disequilibrium threshold using the 1000 Genome Project criteria, setting it to r2 = 0.001 and kb = 10,000. This helped us filter out and retain only independent IVs. Following this, SNPs that were not present in the outcome data or had no available proxies, as well as those associated with cardiovascular-related diseases with a significance level of P < .05, were excluded. We also eliminated SNPs linked to potential confounding factors such as sex, obesity, diabetes, and smoking, with a P-value of <1E-5, according to the PhenoScanner database.[26] Finally, to avoid the inclusion of weak IVs, we calculated the F-value, excluding IVs with an F-value of <10. 2.4. Statistical and sensitivity analyses

We performed a two-sample MR analysis to assess the causal relationship between mood swings and CVDs. The analytical approach used was the inverse variance weighted method. We then applied the Cochrane Q test, MR-PRESSO, and MR-Egger intercept tests to examine heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. An SNP was marked as an outlier and considered for removal in the analysis if it had a P-value of <.05 in the MR-PRESSO test. Consequently, MR analysis was recalculated after adjusting for these outliers. Moreover, to check the reliability of our findings, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis; a P-value >.05 indicated no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy. Finally, we aggregated results from different datasets for the same cardiovascular-related disease through a meta-analysis. If the heterogeneity measure I2 exceeded 50%, a random-effects model was employed. Conversely, for I2 values < 50%, a fixed-effects model was applied.

3.2. Causal effects of mood swing on cardiovascular-related diseases

Figure 3 demonstrates the link between mood swings and the likelihood of CVDs. As the frequency of mood swings increases, there is a significant increase in the risk of various conditions. Specifically, a higher incidence of arrhythmia was observed in the FinnGen data (odds ratio [OR]: 2.28, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.44–3.61, P < .001), atrial fibrillation in FinnGen (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.23–4.11, P = .01), CHD in the IEU OpenGWAS project (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.30–3.21, P < .001), in the GWAS Catalog (OR: 4.45, 95% CI: 1.75–11.33, P < .001), and in FinnGen (OR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.32–3.16, P < .001). Additionally, an increased risk of heart failure in the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.09–2.83, P = .02) and hypertension in FinnGen (OR: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.47–3.19, P < .001) was noted. No statistically significant association was found between mood swings and cardiovascular conditions. This included arterial aneurysm (OR: 1.95, 95% CI: 0.86–4.22, P = .11), abdominal arterial aneurysm (OR: 2.46, 95% CI: 0.69–8.81, P = .17), thoracic arterial aneurysm in the FinnGen dataset (OR: 2.06, 95% CI: 0.89–4.76, P = .09), and atrial fibrillation in the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.20, 95% CI: 0.89–1.61, P = .23). Similarly, no significant findings were noted for heart failure in FinnGen (OR: 1.72, 95% CI: 0.99–3.00, P = .06), hypertension in the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12, P = .07), ischemic stroke in both the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 0.92–2.26, P = .11) and IEU OpenGWAS project (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 0.89–5.74, P = .09), peripheral artery disease in FinnGen (OR: 2.40, 95% CI: 0.86–6.66, P = .09) and the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.76, 95% CI: 0.84–3.70, P = .14), and stroke in both the GWAS Catalog (OR: 1.54, 95% CI: 0.99–2.36, P = .05) and FinnGen (OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 0.85–2.16, P = .17). In the combined analysis of different datasets for each cardiovascular condition, the findings showed that an increased frequency of mood swings was associated with a higher risk of several CVDs. Specifically, the risk of CHD was higher (OR: 2.21, 95% CI: 1.64–2.97, P < .01), as was the risk of heart failure (OR: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.21–2.50, P < .01), ischemic stroke (OR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.04–2.35, P = .03), peripheral artery disease (OR: 1.96, 95% CI: 1.07–3.57, P = .03), and stroke (OR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.07–2.15, P = .02).

4. Discussion

As the pace of work accelerates and society becomes more diverse, deteriorating mental health is increasingly recognized as a prevalent issue, garnering more attention. Numerous epidemiological studies have highlighted a potential link between mental and physical health. MR analyses have further established the causal impact of poor mental health on various health conditions including autoimmune and CVDs. For instance, Zhang et al[28] discovered that neuroticism can increase the risk of CVDs. Unlike psychiatric disorders, individual personality traits are marked by developmental stability and heritability, reflecting personal attitudes and behaviors toward life and potentially exerting a significant lasting effect on physical health. Mood swings, characterized by frequent, abrupt, and unpredictable emotional changes such as irritability, elation, and anxiety, can occur in both healthy individuals and those with psychiatric conditions. Recently, Zhong et al[29] demonstrated a causal link between mood swings and several autoimmune diseases through MR analysis, underscoring the impact of mood swings on physical health. This study aimed to determine the role of mood swings in influencing CVDs. We employed a two-sample MR approach to explore the causal relationship between mood swings and various cardiovascular conditions including arrhythmia, arterial aneurysm, atrial fibrillation, CHD, heart failure, hypertension, stroke, and peripheral artery disease. The exposure data on mood swings were sourced from the UK Biobank, while the outcome data for CVDs were obtained from FinnGen, the IEU Open GWAS project, and the GWAS Catalog. Choosing exposure and outcome data from different sources helped minimize participant overlap and ensured the reliability of our findings. In our analysis, genetic instruments for mood swings that were not significantly associated with the outcomes were first excluded, followed by the removal of SNPs linked to mood swings and confounding factors related to CVD, such as sex, diabetes, obesity, and smoking. The MR-PRESSO method was then applied to eliminate SNPs showing broad horizontal pleiotropy in the causal relationship.[30] Our two-sample MR analysis indicated that mood swings could increase the risk of arrhythmia, CHD, heart failure, ischemic stroke, stroke, and peripheral artery disease. Sensitivity analysis of the Q test revealed significant heterogeneity in the relationship between mood swings and conditions, such as arrhythmia, heart failure, hypertension, peripheral artery disease, and stroke. This heterogeneity may stem from the diverse sources and methodologies of the exposure and outcome datasets. However, it is noteworthy that the I2 test showed no significant heterogeneity for outcomes identified as heterogeneous in the Q test. Moreover, the leave-one-out analysis confirmed the stability of our results. MR pleiotropy tests and MR-PRESSO analysis were instrumental in excluding SNPs with pleiotropy, helping avoid false positives. Overall, our study’s findings are reliable, although the presence of heterogeneity in some CVDs datasets warrants further attention.

A wealth of epidemiological research has established a link between mood swings and psychopathological conditions in both young people and adults, with a particular emphasis on bipolar disorder.[31] Although the exact causes of mood swings remain unclear, efforts are underway to uncover the neurometabolic underpinnings of emotional fluctuations. Mood swings are thought to be potentially associated with neurometabolic disorders caused by various pathogenic factors.[32] Recent GWAS have identified 46 distinct loci associated with mood instability, situated near genes involved in several brain biochemical pathways.[33] For instance, genes such as phospholipase C-like (PLCL) PLCL1 and PLCL2 have been linked to gamma-aminobutyric acid and melatonin signaling pathways, which are crucial for the onset of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.[34,35] Furthermore, the identification of genes related to mitochondrial energy production suggests that disorders of energy metabolism could significantly contribute to the emergence of mood swings.

As our understanding of personality deepens, there has been a significant push to unravel the molecular underpinnings of personality traits and their impacts on physical health. A growing body of epidemiological and clinical evidence suggests that mood disorders might independently influence the risk of CVDs or related mortality. While direct mechanisms linking mood swings to cardiovascular conditions remain elusive, insights can be gleaned from the relationship between psychopathology and cardiovascular health. This connection is underscored by the fact that many genes associated with mood swings are involved in biochemical pathways relevant to psychopathological conditions, such as depression, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder.[33] Evidence suggests that exposure to unpredictable external stressors can precipitate both mood disorders and CVDs.[36] Epidemiological research has highlighted a link between unpredictable stressors and depression in humans, whereas animal studies have shown increased depression-like behaviors following exposure to such stressors.[37,38] Unpredictable stressors have also been associated with various cardiovascular issues, including hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and arrhythmia.[39,40] Experimental work has demonstrated that external stressors can alter the levels of neuroactive substances, such as serotonin (5-HT), dopamine, norepinephrine, and corticotropin-releasing factor, all crucial for regulating neuroendocrine systems.[37] Disruptions in these systems, such as the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, have been linked to mental health disorders and adverse cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes including diabetes and insulin resistance.[41] This suggests that exposure to unpredictable external stressors and subsequent biological responses could be a key mechanism underlying the connection between mood swings and CVDs. The central neurotransmitter system has also emerged as a critical biological pathway mediating this relationship. For instance, disturbances in monoamine function are pivotal in the onset of depression, and antidepressants that inhibit monoamine oxidase can negatively impact cardiovascular health.[36,42] Neurotransmitters, such as norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, and 5-HT, are vital in regulating the endocrine and autonomic nervous systems and are essential for cardiovascular system homeostasis. In summary, the neuroendocrine and central neurotransmitter systems could be biological processes mediating the link between mood swings and CVDs, warranting further investigation through experimental studies.

4.1. Study strength and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first effort to examine the causal link between mood swings and CVDs using an MR analysis. MR analysis avoids confounding factors and other biases, providing robust evidence. In this study, exposure and outcome data were sourced from different databases, minimizing sample overlap and enhancing the credibility of our findings. Multiple datasets were analyzed for certain CVDs data, and a meta-analysis was employed to combine the results, thereby enlarging the sample size and strengthening the reliability of our conclusions. Additionally, selecting individuals of European ancestry helped to prevent population selection bias. During the analysis, SNPs associated with mood swings and potential confounding factors such as smoking, diabetes, obesity, and sex were excluded. Moreover, the MR-PRESSO method and sensitivity analysis were used to identify SNPs with significant pleiotropy. However, it is important to acknowledge some limitations in the application of our findings. First, while sex was recognized as a confounding factor, the scarcity of data made it challenging to conduct subgroup analyses. Second, the MR analysis was only adjusted for 4 confounding factors, and the omission of other potential confounders might limit the generalizability of our conclusions.

5. Conclusion

This research demonstrated that mood swings could elevate the risk of several cardiovascular conditions such as arrhythmia, CHD, heart failure, ischemic stroke, stroke, and peripheral artery disease. These intriguing results indicate that appropriate psychological interventions to stabilize mood could help prevent negative cardiovascular incidents.
