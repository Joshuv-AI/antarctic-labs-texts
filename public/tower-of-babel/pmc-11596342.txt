Association between gastroesophageal reflux disease and venous thromboembolism: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ting Ao, Yingxiu Huang, Peng Zhen, Ming Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The link between gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and venous thromboembolism (VTE) is not well understood. We performed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study to explore the potential causal effect of GERD on VTE. To explore the causal relationship between genetically predicted GERD and the risk of VTE, including pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Three MR statistical techniques (inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger) were used to evaluate the potential causal relationship in genome-wide association studies. As part of the sensitivity analysis, we assessed heterogeneity and pleiotropy, and used the leave-one-SNP-out method to enhance the robustness of our findings. MR analysis results indicated that genetically predicted GERD was associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (odds ratio [OR] = 1.239, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.146–1.339), PE (OR = 1.307, 95% CI = 1.156–1.477) and DVT (OR = 1.160, 95% CI = 1.012–1.330). Moreover, this study did not detect any heterogeneity or pleiotropy. GERD has a causal effect on venous thromboembolism, PE, and DVT.

1. Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), which includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the third most common cardiovascular condition globally.[1,2] Some studies have reported that VTE is associated with a 30-day mortality rate of 10.6%, with 30% to 50% of survivors developing long-term complications.[3–5] Evidence suggests that VTE and atherosclerotic diseases may share common risk factors, such as smoking, metabolic syndrome, obesity, and dyslipidemia.[6,7]

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is characterized by symptoms or complications resulting from backflow of acidic stomach contents into the esophagus.[8] Several studies have suggested that GERD is associated with a higher risk of coronary artery disease,[9] acute myocardial infarction,[10] and stroke.[11] Given these associations, GERD may also contribute to the risk of VTE.[12] However, existing research presents inconclusive results, potentially influenced by biases, including reverse causation and confounding variables such as demographics, sample size, lifestyle, socio-economic factors, and health status.[13]

To date, no studies have thoroughly investigated the causal relationship between GERD and VTE. Therefore, this study aims to employ a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to examine the effect of GERD on VTE.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

We performed MR analysis to elucidate the causal relationship between GERD and venous thromboembolism, including PE and DVT, using GWAS data from a sizable population cohort. The instrumental variable (IV) model relies on the following assumptions: the instrument must be strongly associated with the exposure; the instrument must be independent of confounding factors that affect the outcome; and the instrument affects the outcome only through exposure. To validate this hypothesis, we selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with robust associations with GERD as IVs to validate this hypothesis. This study followed the principle of genetic inheritance, which posits that alleles are inherited randomly from parents to their offspring and that SNPs are not affected by potential confounders, including environmental and socio-economic conditions. Additionally, we assessed pleiotropy to determine whether the IVs influenced the outcomes through mechanisms unrelated to the exposure. The research design of this study is depicted in Figure 1.

2.2. Data source

Aggregate data on the association of exposure related SNPs with GERD were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) public availability (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The analysis included 129,080 patients with GERD and 473,524 controls of European ancestry from a recent GWAS.[14] Summary statistics of VTE, PE, and DVT were obtained from the FinnGen public availability. The relevant phenotypic codes obtained were “finngen_R10_I9_VTE” (21,021 cases and 391,160 controls), “finngen_R10_I9_PHLETHROMBDVTLOW” (6501 cases and 357,111 controls), and “finngen_R10_I9_PULMEMB” (10,046 cases and 401,128 controls) (Table 1). To validate our results, we used an additional GWAS dataset from the UK Biobank as a replication sample.[15] To reduce the risk of bias owing to demographic stratification, all analyses were conducted using data exclusively from participants of European descent. This study did not require ethical approval or patient consent, as the data were derived from publicly available published studies.

2.3. Instrumental variables selection

The criteria for selecting SNPs as IVs were as follows: SNPs must satisfy genome-wide significance criteria (P < 5 × 10−8); SNPs must not be in genetic linkage disequilibrium, defined as r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kbp window according to the European 1000 Genomes panel; and SNPs must have an F-statistic exceeding 10 to mitigate weak instrument bias.[16] The F-statistic is defined as F=R2(n−k−1)/K(1−R2), where R2 represents the degree of exposure explained by IVs, n denotes the sample size for the exposure, and k indicates the number of IVs.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was employed to obtain the primary causal estimates. The IVW method provides a consistent estimate of the causal effect of exposure on the outcome, assuming that each genetic variant adheres to the IV criteria.[17] The results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was determined using a P value of <.05.

To evaluate the robustness of the MR results, we conducted a range of sensitivity analyses, including the weighted median method, MR-Egger regression, heterogeneity testing, MR-pleiotropy assessment, and leave-one-out analysis. An adaptation of the MR-Egger regression can detect specific violations of standard IV assumptions, offering an adjusted estimate that remains unaffected by these issues. This method also assesses the degree of directional pleiotropy and functions as a robustness check.[18] The weighted median provides consistent results even if up to 50% of the information is derived from invalid IVs.[19] Furthermore, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to ascertain whether the noteworthy results were influenced by a single SNP. Cochran Q statistic was used to assess the heterogeneity among individual SNP-based estimates. For exposures represented by a single SNP, causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio method. This method determines the causal relationship by calculating the ratio of the SNP–outcome association estimate to the SNP–exposure association estimate. To evaluate the strength of each instrument, the F-statistic was used, where an F-statistic above 10 indicated a sufficiently strong instrument.[16]

The analyses were conducted using the R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria, version 4.3.3). MR analyses were carried out with the “Two Sample MR” R package.

3. Results

3.1. Causal effects of GERD on VTE

The association between GERD and VTE was assessed using MR analysis. The number of eligible SNPs associated with GERD is shown in Figure 2. A total of 75 independent SNPs were selected to genetically predict VTE, PE, and DVT. The F-statistics for these SNPs exceeded 10, indicating their robustness as an instrument.

3.2. Sensitivity analysis

The MR-Egger regression intercepts were not significantly different from zero, indicating the absence of horizontal pleiotropy (all intercepts P > .05) (Fig. 2). The weighted median approach further substantiated the consistency of these findings. Figure 3 shows the scatter plots of the genetic associations between GERD and VTE. Cochran Q test demonstrated no evidence of heterogeneity among the genetic IVs for the evaluated outcomes (all P > .05). Furthermore, funnel plots revealed no substantial asymmetry, suggesting negligible publication bias and minimal directional horizontal pleiotropy (Fig. 4). The stability of the causal estimates was additionally confirmed through leave-one-out analysis, which demonstrated that no single IV had an undue impact on the observed causal relationships (Fig. 5).

3.3. Replication sample results

In the replication phase, 77 IVs were selected to assess the causal relationship between GERD and VTE, PE, and DVT. The MR results from the replication sample were consistent with those of the discovery sample. Specifically, GERD was found to have a positive causal relationship with VTE (ORIVW = 1.007; 95% CI = 1.004–1.010; P < .05). Furthermore, GERD was causally linked to an elevated risk of PE (ORIVW = 1.002; 95% CI: 1.001 to 1.005; P < .05) and DVT (ORIVW = 1.005; 95% CI: 1.002–1.007; P < .05) (Table 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we analyzed 2 distinct GWAS datasets to assess the association between GERD and VTE risk. Our results demonstrate that GRED had a causal effect on VTE, PE, and DVT.

Several studies have reported a correlation between GERD and the risk of VTE. A retrospective cohort study involving 135,409 patients reported an increased incidence of PE in patients with GERD (OR = 1.51; 95% CI = 1.11–2.04; P = .008).[20] Sun et al demonstrated a positive association between GERD and 7 cardiovascular disease outcomes, including VTE, with OR of 1.29 (95% CI = 1.16–1.44).[12] However, some studies have reported inconsistent results. Proton pump inhibitors were widely prescribed for GERD. A large prospective cohort study found that the use of proton pump inhibitors was significantly linked to a higher risk of VTE than nonusers (hazard ratio = 1.36; 95% CI = 1.24–1.50).[21] Another study found patients with a preoperative gastrointestinal issue (including “heartburn” or “acid reflux”) had a higher VTE risk score. However, there was no difference in the incidence of VTE compared to patients without preoperative gastrointestinal issues (0.29% vs 0.45%, P = .21).[22]

Several mechanisms may elucidate the association between GERD and VTE. Firstly, inflammation was associated with the risk of VTE.[23] Refluxed gastric juice prompts the epithelial cells to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines.[24] The persistent inflammatory responses observed in patients with GERD may contribute to their association.[25] Additionally, studies have indicated that the activity of nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells(NF-κB) was increased in the mucosa of GERD patients.[26] NF-κB mediates interactions among endothelial cells, platelets, and the inflammatory response, disrupting the delicate balance between coagulation and fibrinolysis, thereby promoting thrombosis.[27] It is essential to clarify the mechanisms through which GERD elevates the risk of VTE. Future research should validate the findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.

Furthermore, most cross-sectional epidemiological studies have struggled to establish causation due to ambiguous temporal relationships. Our study utilized MR methods to control for confounding factors and to determine the causal effects of GERD on VTE, PE, and DVT. Our findings indicate that the presence of GERD is associated with an increased risk of developing VTE, PE, and DVT by 23.9% (ORIVW = 1.239, 95% CI = 1.146–1.339), 30.7% (ORIVW = 1.307, 95% CI = 1.156–1.477), and 16% (ORIVW = 1.160, 95% CI = 1.012–1.330), respectively. GERD on VTE for the first time using summary-level data from multiple large consortia. Our causal inferences were enhanced by the use of MR design, which mitigated biases related to residual confounding and reverse causation. Second, we employed sensitivity analyses such as the weighted median and MR-Egger methods. The consistency in the magnitude and direction of the estimates across these analyses reinforces the overall robustness of our findings. Finally, we performed replication analyses using additional data sources for VTE outcomes to assess the reliability and robustness of our findings. Replication analyses validated the results of the primary analyses, confirming the accuracy and robustness of our conclusions.

5. Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, a significant limitation inherent in MR studies is the potential for pleiotropy, which could undermine the reliability of the findings. Nonetheless, the MR-Egger regression revealed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy in our analyses. Second, to ensure genetic homogeneity, we analyzed and contrasted the causal associations of GERD with VTE only in individuals of European descent. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations of other racial or ethnic backgrounds. Third, our MR analysis used summary-level data, which precludes examination of the impact of GERD on VTE across various subgroups, such as age or sex. The absence of specific demographic data (e.g., sex and age) in the original studies limited the possibility of conducting further subgroup analyses. Additionally, although our results elucidated the causal connection between GERD and VTE, further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms.

6. Conclusions

In summary, our study employed a 2-sample MR approach to assess the causal relationship between GERD and VTE risk. Our findings indicate a potential association between genetically predicted GERD and increased risk of VTE. To further elucidate this relationship, additional research is required, including investigations into molecular mechanisms and therapeutic studies.
