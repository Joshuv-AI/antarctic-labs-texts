Multifunctional scaffold based on Ti3C2Tx/PCL nanofibers composite hydrogel directs myogenic differentiation and microenvironment remodeling for volumetric muscle loss
Authors: Yihui Zhang, Wenkai Zhang, Zeyuan Xie, Wei Yu, Xiangqi Zhang, Yangwen Luo, Muge Gu, Jiayu Wang, Wei-En Yuan, Yebin Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Volumetric muscle loss (VML) regeneration remains clinically challenging, primarily due to compromised myogenic differentiation and an oxidative-inflammatory microenvironment that impedes regeneration. Consequently, timely resolution of inflammation and dynamic remodeling of the immune niche are imperative for functional VML recovery. Ti3C2Tx MXene, a two-dimensional nanomaterial, demonstrates significant potential in biomedical applications owing to its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. However, oxidative degradation and hydrolysis occur upon aqueous exposure, leading to compromised bioactivity. To preserve structural integrity and mitigate oxidative degradation of Ti3C2Tx MXene, it was encapsulated in electrospun fibers to fabricate Ti3C2Tx MXene/PCL membrane (MP). This biomimetic multifunctional scaffold was fabricated via hydrogen bond-driven self-assembly, integrating alternating layers of MP membranes and hyaluronic acid-catechol/4-arm-PEG (HP) hydrogel, and is hereinafter referred to as the MPHP scaffold. In vitro analyses demonstrated enhanced cellular adhesion and myogenic differentiation (evidenced by > 3.9-fold upregulation of myogenic differentiation (MyoD) and >2.9-fold upregulation of myogenin (MyoG)), confirming potent myogenesis-promoting capabilities. In vitro and in vivo analyses consistently demonstrated the scaffold's capacity to reprogram macrophage polarization to regenerative M2 phenotypes, effectively remodeling the regenerative niche. During the remodeling phase, it accelerated myotube fusion and maturation, while concurrently promoting angiogenesis and neuromuscular junction reinnervation. These coordinated mechanisms restored structural and functional muscle integrity, with treated VML models exhibiting a 38% increase in exercise tolerance versus controls. The MPHP scaffold thus represents a multifunctional biomimetic platform for VML regeneration, demonstrating significant translational potential in tissue engineering. Moreover, this encapsulation strategy preserves Ti3C2Tx MXene bioactivity and expands its therapeutic applicability beyond conventional limitations.

Introduction

The musculoskeletal system is essential for movement, support and protection of the human body and accounts for approximately 40% of the total body weight [1,2]. Volumetric muscle loses (VML) refers to traumatic or surgical loss of skeletal muscle with 20% or more of the muscle belly by weight. It's a devastating injury that challenges muscle repair capacity, resulting in severe permanent muscle damage, structural changes, and impaired limb function [3]. Currently, autologous and allogeneic transplantation in clinical practice have the risk of infection, necrosis and the serious risk of immune rejection [4,5]. In addition, some gene and cell therapies are still in clinical trials, but their therapeutic methods and effects still need to be further explored [[6], [7], [8]]. At present, clinical attention has turned to tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, hoping to create multifunctional muscle scaffold capable of mimicking the natural muscles to rebuild its structure and function [9].

Skeletal muscle repair and regeneration is a complex process consisting of several consecutive and overlapping stages, involving the participation of multiple cells, growth factors and cytokines [10]. The muscle regeneration first contains a proinflammatory phase, which is associated with increased inflammatory responses and activation of muscle stem cells (MuSC), followed by a recovery phase, which is characterized by the transmission of anti-inflammatory cues that maintain myofiber formation, angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and ultimately lead to functional recovery of the muscle [[11], [12], [13]]. Notably, macrophages constitute the largest collection of immune cells during muscle regeneration, from initial injury to late remodeling of muscle tissue. During muscle regeneration, they exert various functions on multiple cells while adjusting their phenotype and function throughout regeneration [14]. After muscle injury, macrophages rapidly accumulate and secrete nitric oxide, which promotes the degradation of muscle fibers and induces vasodilation to recruit more inflammatory cells. Pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages engraft necrotic muscle fibers and support the growth and proliferation of satellite cells and inhibit premature myogenic cell fusion [[15], [16], [17]]. Anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages promote the differentiation of myoblasts (myoblasts differentiate into myogenin expressing myocytes) and fusion into multinucleated myotubes [18,19], thereby accelerating the regeneration process. While persistent M1 macrophages, in addition to stimulating macrophage phagocytosis, also inhibit the recruitment or phenotypic differentiation of M2 macrophages [20]. In addition, Latroche et al. found that anti-inflammatory macrophages were able to stimulate the co-formation of capillaries and myotubes in 3D triple cultures in vitro [21].

Two-dimensional Ti3C2Tx MXene has attracted more and more attention in the field of biomedicine due to its unique properties such as high conductivity, layered structure, adjustable surface chemistry, and excellent mechanical properties [22,23]. The structure of Ti3C2Tx exhibits an alternate arrangement of Ti and C layers, and the surface is covered by various functional groups (-OH, -O, -F, etc.) after peeling-off from the MAX phase [24,25]. Some studies have found that the incorporation of Ti3C2Tx MXene into hydrogels confers conductivity and antioxidant properties, which can be applied in the field of tissue regeneration. Wei et al. [26] found that crosslinking MXenes into hydrogels to prepare conductive hydrogels can provide an extracellular matrix-like substrate for the growth of NSCs, which can effectively promote the proliferation and differentiation of NSCs. Zheng et al. [27] prepared hydrogels incorporating Ti3C2Tx MXene@MnO2 nanoparticles and found that they promoted muscle regeneration through electroactivity and microenvironment management. But directly adding Ti3C2Tx MXene to the hydrogel means that the nanosheets will be in constant contact with water. It has been shown that MXene degrades more easily in aqueous solution than in other states, and except to the oxidative degradation caused by oxygen and dissolved oxygen, it also undergoes direct hydrolysis, which results in impaired structure and function [[28], [29], [30]]. As a result, the service life of Ti3C2Tx MXene is reduced, thus limiting its application. It is therefore crucial to find effective strategies to avoid oxidation and preserve its structure and properties.

While various drugs have been explored to mitigate fibrosis or promote myogenesis, pharmacological interventions alone cannot regenerate lost muscle mass or restore contractile function after VML injury. For instance, formoterol treatment increased peak isometric torque by 18% over untreated VML controls, and adding leucine reduced losses in muscle mass, whole-body lipid oxidation, and strength. However, formoterol's efficacy is limited: muscle mass and function remained far below healthy levels, and its benefits depended on concurrent rehabilitation. In contrast, losartan, though effective at reducing fibrosis, paradoxically impaired muscle function. Together, these findings show that single-agent pharmacotherapy cannot compensate for the physical loss of muscle tissue. An ideal VML repair strategy thus requires a combination of tissue engineering scaffolds, cell-based therapies, and/or rehabilitative training.

Muscle regeneration is a long-term and complex process. Therefore, there is a need for a method that can exert the efficacy of Ti3C2Tx MXene while reducing its oxidative or hydration inactivation in the process. Despite its favorable properties, PCL has several intrinsic limitations that restrict its application in VML repair. the slow degradation kinetics of poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL)(typically 2–3 years for complete absorption) do not align with the faster timeline required for functional muscle regeneration, potentially hindering timely tissue remodeling. Besides, PCL is bioinert and lacks the bioactive cues necessary to actively promote myogenesis, angiogenesis, or immunomodulation, often requiring functionalization with bioactive molecules or nanomaterials to enhance its regenerative capacity. Therefore, we mixed Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets into the spinning fibers to prepare Ti3C2Tx MXene/PCL (MP). With the degradation of the scaffold, Ti3C2Tx MXene is gradually exposed to play a role in different stages of regeneration. The intrinsic biomimetics of nanofibers, including high surface area to volume ratio, porous structure and excellent mechanical properties that closely resemble the complex structure of natural ECM, are expected to coordinate cellular behaviors critical in muscle repair and regeneration [31,32]. However, the direct use of spinning membrane for VML still has problems such as poor hydrophilicity and poor tissue adhesion, which will affect its therapeutic effect.

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a major component of the skeletal muscle extracellular matrix and plays a critical role in muscle development and regeneration. HA-based hydrogels have been extensively explored for tissue engineering due to their excellent biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and ability to support cell adhesion and proliferation. Recently, catechol-conjugated HA (HA-CA) hydrogels have emerged as a promising platform for muscle repair. In a 2025 study by Lee et al., an HA-CA patch incorporating muscle tissue-derived extracellular matrix (MEM) effectively stimulated satellite cell activation and supported de novo muscle regeneration with functional restoration in a rat VML model [33]. Moreover, the adhesive properties of HA-CA hydrogels, conferred by the catechol moieties, enable seamless integration with host tissue and sustained retention at the injury site, which is particularly advantageous for VML repair where mechanical stability is critical. Therefore, it would be an efficient strategy to integrate MP membrane into such a composite scaffold by layer-by-layer stacking of membrane with hydrogel, which can mimic natural skeletal muscle tissue characteristics and promote cell adhesion and growth.

Herein, MP nanofiber membranes were prepared by electrospinning. Subsequently, this membrane was layered with a hydrogel comprising HA-CA crosslinked with 4-arm PEG-SH (forming HP hydrogel), resulting in a multifunctional biomimetic scaffold. By enhancing the stability of Ti3C2Tx MXene, the MPHP scaffold preserved its ability to effectively scavenge free radicals, protect cells from oxidative stress damage, and retain functionality across different stages of muscle regeneration. The scaffold is anticipated to promote volumetric muscle loss (VML) regeneration and restore muscle structure and function by modulating the regenerative immune microenvironment and guiding myogenic differentiation and myogenesis.

Materials and methods

Materials

HA (MW:40-80 kDa), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-Hydroxy succinimide (NHS), were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (CHN). 4-arm PEG-SH (MW:2 kDa) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanyang Bio Tech. Inc. (CHN). Dopamine hydrochloride (DA∙HCl) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). PCL (Capa 6400) was obtained from Perstorp (SWE). Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets were obtained from Xi 'an Qiyue Biotechnology Co., LTD (CHN). Hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP, 99.5 %) was procured from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd. 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (CHN). DCFH-DA assay kit, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and IL-4, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay kit and CCK-8 assay kit were purchased from Shanghai Beyotime Biological Co., Ltd. (CHN). Riboflavin, methionine, nitrotetrazolium chloride, salicylic acid, FeSO4, ABTS and potassium persulfate were obtained from Shanghai Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd (CHN). H2O2 was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd (CHN). FBS (FSD500) was obtained from ExCell Biotech Co., Ltd. (CHN). Penicillin-streptomycin and basic medium was purchased from Grand Island Biological Company (Gibco, USA). RAW 264.7 cells and C2C12 cells were ordered from the cell bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, CHN). Isoflurane was obtained from Shenzhen ruiwode Life Technology Co., Ltd (CHN). 4% paraformaldehyde obtained from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd (CHN). All the antibodies were purchased from Abcam (CHN).

Characterization of Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets

The microstructure properties of Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (FEI Talos, F200X S/TEM, ThermoFisher Scientific Co.). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (AXIS Ultra DLD, Shimadzu) was performed to measure Ti3C2Tx.

Preparation and characterization of electrospinning nanofiber membrane

The 15% w/w PCL solution was obtained by dissolving PCL in HFIP. Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets with different contents (Ti3C2Tx: PCL = 0.5%, 1% and 2%; w/w) were dissolved in deionized water and sonicated in an ice bath for 5 min. The two solutions were mixed and stirred in a magnetic stirrer for 0.5 h. The PCL and MP nanofiber membranes were produced using an electrospinning device (SS-2534, Ucalery Technology and Development, Beijing, CHN). The electrospinning experimental conditions were as follows: voltage (−4 kV, +10 kV), flow rate (0.2 mm min−1), needle (18 G), collector distance (15 cm), collector rotation speed (600 rpm) and room temperature. The ambient relative humidity inside the electrospinning device was controlled within the range of 25% to 40%. The prepared nanofiber membranes were vacuum dried in a desiccator for 24 h to remove residual HFIP.

Characterization of the mechanical properties of the membrane

The mechanical properties of nanofiber membranes were evaluated by using a dynamic mechanical analysis (Q850, TA, USA). The DMA analysis of the membranes was performed with reference to the specifications of ISO 6721-9:2019 (R2024) in tensile mode. Specifically, the membranes were cut into rectangles (70 mm × 15 mm, length × width), and the thickness was recorded through a micrometer. The ends of the membrane were clamped and pulled at a rate of 10 mm/min until breaking, and their stress-strain curves were recorded.

Construction of PHP and MPHP scaffolds

HA-CA was prepared according to the method of zhong et al. [34,35]. The chemical structure of HA-CA conjugate was confirmed using FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet 6700, Thermo scientific, USA) and 1H NMR (400 MHz, Bruker AMX-400, USA). HP hydrogel was prepared by dissolving HA-CA in PBS (pH = 5.8) and mixing with 250 mg/mL 4-arm PEG-SH (8:1, v/v). The hydrogel was then formed by adjusting pH to 7.5 with 1 M NaOH. The microstructures of the HP hydrogel were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (VEGA/IAC, SJTU), and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) mapping analysis was performed to determine the distribution of Ti atoms. Water contact angles of PCL and MP membranes were measured using a video-optical contact angle meter (DSA100, KRÜSS Scientific). PCL and MP nanofiber membranes were cut into rectangles (10 mm × 5 mm, length × width). The prepared HP hydrogel was coated onto a custom-fabricated mold, followed by deposition of the nanofiber membrane. The multilayered scaffold was subsequently assembled by sequentially stacking three hydrogel layers alternated with two nanofiber membranes.

Swelling behavior of HP hydrogels

To investigate the structural stability of the scaffolds, 1% and 2% HP hydrogels were prepared. Initial weights (W0) of hydrogels were recorded, after which they were immersed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, 5 mL) at 37 °C. They were removed and weighed (Wt) at predetermined time. The test was repeated three times. The equilibrium swelling ratio was calculated as the following formula:Swellingratio(%)=Wt−W0W0×100%

Rheological measurements

A rotational rheometer (DHR20, TA, USA) was using to determine the rheological characteristics of hydrogels. The characterization of the mechanical properties of the hydrogel was performed in accordance with the guidelines of ASTM F2900, whereas the specific testing protocol was based on ISO 6721-10 with appropriate optimizations. The hydrogels were placed between parallel plates (plate diameter: 20 mm; gap: 500 μm). Oscillatory frequency sweeps were conducted from 0.1 to 100 rad/s at a constant strain of 1% (within the linear viscoelastic region) and a temperature of 25 °C. The storage modulus (G’) were recorded under a frequency sweep mode.

In vitro release of Ti3C2Tx

The prepared 1% MP electrospun membrane was immersed in a vial containing 1 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and placed in a thermostatic shaker (100 rpm, 37 °C). At predetermined time points (1, 4, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days), 200 μL of the release medium was withdrawn and an equal volume of fresh, pre-warmed release medium was replenished. The withdrawn samples were diluted with 2% nitric acid, and the amount of released Ti was quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-MS, i CAP Q, Thermo, USA). The test was repeated three times.

In vitro degration of MPHP scaffold

The prepared MPHP scaffold was implanted subcutaneously into rats - a simple procedure that facilitates observation, sample retrieval, and preliminary assessment of degradation. Briefly, after removing the dorsal hair and disinfecting the skin with iodophor, a small incision was made. The sterilized scaffold was inserted subcutaneously, placed flat, and the incision was closed with sutures. The wound was then disinfected again with iodophor to prevent infection.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays and CCK-8 assays were used to detect the cytotoxicity and proliferation of C2C12 cells exposed to PHP and MPHP scaffolds, respectively. The concentration of C2C12 cell suspension was diluted to 2 × 104 ml−1, and cultured with PHP and MPHP scaffolds. After 24 h of culture with the scaffolds, the test was performed according to the instructions of LDH test kit. Absorbance was read on a microplate reader at a detection/reference wavelength of 490/630 nm (Synergy LX, BioTek, USA). CCK8 was used to evaluate the effect of the scaffolds on cell proliferation after 1, 3 and 5 days of culture. Absorbance was read on a microplate reader at a detection/reference wavelength of 450/630 nm.

Extraction of primary muscle cell

Primary muscle cells were extracted using the tissue mass cell culture method. Newborn BALB/c mice (0-1 days after birth) were euthanised via cervical dislocation, and limb muscles were cut into 1 mm3 size with ophthalmic scissors after removal of fat, connective tissue, and bone. The muscle patches were soaked with medium and evenly coated on the wall of the culture bottle, with the spacing between each patch being 0.2 cm - 0.5 cm. The bottle was placed bottom up, medium was added to the bottle and placed in a 37 °C cell incubator. After incubation for 2 to 4 h, the small pieces of muscle were attached to the wall, and the bottle was slowly flipped and laid flat. Primary muscle cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium containing 20% FBS. After 3-5 days of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium.

Live/dead staining

Membranes were cut with a circular cutter to fit in 24-well cell culture plates and immersed in 75% ethanol for 30 min. The sterilized membranes were placed in 24-well plates containing cell slides and secured with sterile steel rings. Subsequently, C2C12 and muscle primary cells were seeded on membranes at a concentration of 105, respectively. After 24 h of culture, the cells were stained with the live/dead staining kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence images were taken by high resolution laser confocal microscopy (Leica STELLARIS 5, Leica, GER) and inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, JPN).

Free radical scavenging assay

•OH and O2•- scavenging assays were used to assess the ROS scavenging ability of the MPHP scaffolds. The scavenging ability of ·OH was determined by Fenton reaction. The sample was placed in the test solution of 10 mM FeSO4 and 10 mM H2O2. After incubation for 10 min, 10 mM salicylic acid-ethanol was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min [36]. The absorbance of the solution at 510 nm was recorded using a plate reader (infinite M200 Pro, TECAN) and the ·OH scavenging was calculated. For O2•- scavenging assays, assay solutions were prepared in PBS containing riboflavin (20 μM), methionine (12.5 mM), and nitrotetrazolium chloride (75 μM). Samples were mixed with 1 mL of assay solution and exposed to a constant light intensity for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant at 560 nm were recorded using a flatbed scanner (infinite M200 Pro, TECAN). The absorbance of the assay solution under light and without light was recorded as positive and negative controls, respectively. O2•− scavenging ratio were evaluated according to the following formula:O2·−scavengingratio(%)=Asample−AnegativeconbtrolApositivecontrol−Anegativecontrol×100%

The antioxidant capacity of the MP membranes was assessed by DPPH and ABTS experiments. For DPPH scavenging experiments, the samples were homogenized and dispersed in ethanol, followed by addition of DPPH (3.0 mL, 100 μM) and incubation for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance of the supernatant at 517 nm was recorded using a flatbed scanner. The DPPH radical scavenging ability was calculated according to the following formula:DPPHscavenging(%)=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100%Where Acontrol is the absorption of the blank detection solution and Asample is the absorption of the sample solution.

For ABTS assays, 7 mM ABTS and 2.45 mM potassium persulfate were reacted in the dark for 12 h, then the reaction solution was centrifuged and the supernatant was diluted with PBS to prepare the assay solution. The samples were mixed with 1 mL of ABTS assay solution and react for 10 min in the dark. The absorbance of the supernatant at 734 nm was recorded using a flatbed scanner.

Intracellular ROS scavenging determination

The DCFH-DA assay was used to assess intracellular ROS scavenging determination. C2C12 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. The cells were then stimulated with 500 μM H2O2 for 2 h, followed by the addition of PCL or MP membranes and further incubation for 24 h. PBS treatment was used as the control group. The intracellular ROS levels were measured by DCFH-DA probe method according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were taken using ImageXpress Micro Confocal (Molecular Devices, USA).

Quantitative Real-time PCR analysis

The effect of scaffolds on myogenic differentiation of primary muscle cells was investigated by analyzing myogenic genes (MyoG and MyoD), as well as myosin heavy chain (MHC) gene expression. Briefly, primary muscle cells were seeded into 24-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. Then, the medium was exchanged with differentiation medium containing PCL and MP membranes, respectively. Cells incubated with differentiation medium were used as control group. The differentiation medium was refreshed every other day. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (R411, Vazyme) according to the instructions. RNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop detector (Thermo Fisher, USA) and adjusted to 200 ng μL−1. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA by using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, CHN). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green reagent (Vazyme, CHN) on a fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (CFX Opus 96, Bio-Rad). Primer sequences are shown in Table S1. The expression levels of the genes were calculated by threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCt) method. The results were normalized with GAPDH.

To evaluate the effect of scaffolds on macrophage (M0) phenotype, the scaffolds were cultured with RAW 264.7 cells. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. For the investigation of M1 and M2 phenotypic differentiation, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and IL-4 were used for stimulation, respectively. After 2 days of culture, RNA was extracted and the expression of related genes was detected according to the above procedures.

Establishment of VML model and histological examination

We evaluated the effect of scaffolds on skeletal muscle regeneration using a rat model of anterior tibial (TA) VML. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Female SD rats (180 g to 200 g, n = 4) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., LTD. Female Sprague-Dawley rats were chosen for this study to avoid the potential confounding effects of aggressive behavior and fighting-induced injuries commonly observed in male rats when housed in groups, which could independently affect muscle repair and inflammatory responses. Additionally, female rats have been widely used in previous VML studies, allowing direct comparison with the literature [37,38].

Rats were anesthetized using isoflurane in a gas anesthesia machine (Shenzhen ruiwode Life Technology Co., Ltd, CHN). The skin was cut to expose the tibialis anterior muscle, creating a cuboid shaped defect (10 mm × 5 mm, approximately 30% - 40% of the weight of the muscle was removed). The scaffold was transplanted to fill the defect area. The skin was closed by 6-0 nylon suture (Pudong Jinhuan Medical) and sterilized with iodophor. No treatment was performed as the control group. The same surgical procedure was performed (skin incision, fascia incision, and exposure of the tibialis anterior muscle) in sham-operated group, but no muscle tissue was excised (i.e., no VML defect created). The incision was then closed identically to the other groups.

The rats were then sacrificed at 8 weeks after operation and the TA muscles on both sides were photographed and weighed. Muscles were then fixed in 4% (v/v) formaldehyde solution, embedded, and sectioned. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, Masson's trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical staining of CD31 were performed. The sections of the specimens were stained with immunofluorescence, including COL-I, MHC, laminin, CD206, α-SMA and β-III-tubulin. The digitized information was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Evaluation of muscle motor function recovery

8 weeks after operation, the rats were placed on the small animal running platform (XR-PT-10B, Shanghai shxinruan Technology Co. LTD, CHN) for running test. The running postures and running time of rats in each group were recorded. To better evaluate the recovery of motor function in rats, the animals were placed on the CatWalk gait analysis system (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands) at 3, 6, and 8 weeks post-surgery. The rats were trained to walk along a corridor, and their footprints were recorded. Motor function recovery was assessed by analyzing the footprint area and average walking speed.

Statistical analysis

For HE and Masson images, the minimum (Min.) Feret's diameter (the shortest axis across the center of the myofiber) of centrally nucleated myofibers was measured and statistically analyzed to determine myofiber size. For Masson, iNOS, CD68, and CD206 staining, image analysis was performed using Image-J software (NIH), and the results were normalized to the positive area for statistical calculation. For CD31 and α-SMA images, the numbers of CD31+ and SMA+ vessels (only those with a discernible lumen) were counted within the regenerated area to obtain vessel density. The regenerated area was primarily defined as the region containing myofibers with centrally located nuclei. For βIII-tubulin+ images, the regenerated area was similarly used to count positive profiles to determine nerve density. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) unless otherwise stated. GraphPad Prism 8 software and Origin 8.5 software were used for statistical analysis. Image-J software (NIH) was used for image analysis. One-way ANOVA was used for comparison among multiple groups, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Fig. 1C shows the SEM images of PCL and MP nanofiber membranes. The nanofibers have a smooth surface and are evenly distributed. Fig. 1D shows elemental mapping images of 1% MP nanofiber membrane. The Ti elements image shows dense bright spots, indicating that Ti3C2Tx MXene were encapsulated in PCL nanofibers. The effective encapsulation of Ti3C2Tx MXene can protect it from direct contact with oxygen and water, so that its activity is well preserved for long-term function. The elemental analysis of 0.5% MP and 2% MP membrane were shown in Fig. S2, which shows bright spots of different densities, indicating the content and distribution of Ti3C2Tx in the PCL nanofibers. The water contact angle results of PCL and MP nanofiber membranes with different contents showed that they were hydrophobic. The PCL contact angle was 138.96° ± 1.05°, the contact angles of 0.5% MP, 1% MP, and 2% MP were 138.93 ± 3.16°, 138.87 ± 2.03°, and 134.47 ± 3.63°, respectively (Fig. 1E). The addition of Ti3C2Tx did not significantly reduce the water contact angle of the membranes, which also indicated that Ti3C2Tx was successfully spun into the fiber. In addition, a small number of Ti3C2Tx with larger particle sizes were surrounded by the spun fiber bundles. This part of the Ti3C2Tx MXene works first, the Ti3C2Tx embedded in the fiber will gradually be released over time.

The mechanical properties of the membranes were evaluated by tensile testing (Fig. 1F). Both PCL and MP membranes with different contents had good ductility. Among them, 0.5% MP had the highest stress strength of 8.34 MPa and tensile strain at break of 336.8%. PCL had the lowest stress of 2.08 Mpa. The stress-strain curves of 1% MP and 2% MP were similar, the stress was 4.57 MP and 4.55 MPa, and the tensile strain at break was 246.3% and 260.0%, respectively. As the content of Ti3C2Tx further increased to 5%, the stress strength of the membrane increased to 4.99 MPa, while its tensile strain at break decreased to 139.5%. It indicates that the addition of Ti3C2Tx can significantly increase the strength of the membrane, but with the further increase of the content, the mechanical strength of the membrane decreases instead.

The HA-CA structure was confirmed by 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 1G) and FTIR spectrum (Fig. 1H). The peaks at δ 6.75-6.95 ppm correspond to the H in the aromatic ring, and the peaks at δ 2.9 and 3.1 ppm correspond to the catechol methylene-proton peak, all of which indicate the successful binding of dopamine to HA. Compared with the FTIR spectrum of HA, HA-CA showed a peak at 1728 cm−1, which may be the vibrational absorption peak of −COOH. The strong absorption peak of HA-CA at 1559 cm−1 was formed by N-H bending vibration of amide II band. In addition, the intensity of the asymmetric and symmetric tensile vibrations of −COONa at 1601 cm−1 and 1406 cm−1 were significantly reduced. These confirm the successful formation of a chemical coupling between HA and CA via an amide bond.

The microscopic images of HP hydrogels are shown in Fig. 1I. Both 1% HP and 2% HP hydrogels were loose and porous. From the swelling curve of the hydrogel (Fig. 1J), the HP hydrogel underwent rapid swelling in the first 3 h and basically maintained swelling equilibrium after 12 h (1% HP: 18.24 ± 1.70%; 2% HP: 27.48 ± 3.39%). The mechanical properties of the hydrogel were investigated by dynamic rheological analysis (Fig. 1K). The storage modulus (G’) of 1% HP and 2% HP hydrogel was about 165 Pa and 275 Pa, respectively. With the increase of HA-CA content, the crosslinking degree of the hydrogel increased, and its mechanical strength also increased. Considering that pore size is particularly important for the transport of waste and the transport of oxygen, 1% HP was chosen was selected as the scaffold. The cumulative release of Ti3C2Tx from the electrospun membrane at day 60 was 7.4 ng (Fig. 1L), lower than expected. Given the differences in the release medium and the fact that the in vivo microenvironment promotes scaffold degradation, the actual release in vivo is expected to exceed that observed in vitro [39,40]. At day 60, the scaffold had degraded by 58.86% in vivo (Fig. 1M). Examination of the explanted scaffolds at this time point revealed that the hydrogel was almost completely degraded, whereas a small amount of the electrospun membrane remained, suggesting that the membrane can also be fully degraded at later stages [41,42].

Good biocompatibility of scaffolds is a prerequisite for their effect of muscle regeneration in vivo. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the cell viability and cell proliferation rate of the scaffolds and the control group were similar. Similar results were obtained in live or dead staining experiments (Fig. 2C), indicating that MPHP scaffolds had good cytocompatibility. Promote myogenic differentiation and alignment and maturation of muscle fibers

MyoD and MyoG belong to the myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) family. MRFs act at multiple points in the muscle lineage, from coordinating myoblast proliferation, to various stages of skeletal muscle development, such as myoblast fusion and myotube maturation [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. The major component of myosin is myosin heavy chain (MyHC), the expression of which determines the contractile properties of muscle fibers, is an important protein associated with myogenic differentiation and myogenesis [52]. MYH1 is one of the isoforms encoding myosin (contractile). The primary muscle cells were cultured with different Ti3C2Tx content scaffolds in differentiation medium for 4 days, and the changes in the expression of MyoD, MyoG and MYH1 genes were detected (Fig. 4A–C). We found that the expression of above-mentioned muscle regeneration-related genes in the scaffold containing Ti3C2Tx was higher than that in the scaffold without Ti3C2Tx. In particular, the expression levels of MyoD, MyoG and MYH1 genes in 1% MPHP scaffold group were significantly higher than those in control group (>3.9-fold upregulation of MyoD and >2.9-fold upregulation of MyoG). The statistical results showed that the MPHP scaffolds could significantly promote myogenic differentiation.

Type Ⅰ collagen (COL-I) constitutes the outer matrix and provides mechanical support and structural stability to muscle fibers. After muscle fiber injury, COL-I is involved in the generation of new myofibrils and tissue repair by interacting with other cells and proteins [53]. Laminin is widely distributed throughout the body, mainly in the basement membrane (BM), and plays an important role in tissue structure and maintenance, cell signaling, adhesion, and migration. The major role of laminin in muscle is to connect muscle fibers and satellite cells with other BM components such as collagen, glycoproteins, proteoglycan and glycosaminoglycan, which plays an important role in muscle regeneration and repair [54]. More evidence of muscle fiber formation was observed 8 weeks after injury by immunofluorescence staining COL-I, laminin and MHC in de novo formed muscle tissue. From Fig. 4D, we found that the COL-I deposition in the PHP and MPHP groups was more pronounced than that in the control group, and the deposition in the MPHP group was more ordered and complete. While the control and PHP groups showed less laminin, the MPHP scaffold group was more intact. The muscle morphology in MPHP group was also more similar to that of the sham group.

Promote angiogenesis, nerve innervation and recovery of exercise endurance

Revascularization is essential for the structural and functional recovery of muscle regeneration because of the highly metabolic nature of skeletal muscle [56,57]. CD31 is an important marker of vascular endothelial differentiation, indicating that the vascular network of remodeling muscle is gradually established. α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) is normally expressed in vascular smooth muscle. Mature blood vessels are composed of endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells that stabilize vascular structure and tension. The density of CD31+ vessels and the number of α-SMA + vessels were both significantly higher in the MPHP scaffold group than in the control and PHP scaffold group (Fig. 6A–D), indicates that MPHP scaffold promotes angiogenesis and help reconstruct the vascular network. The higher density of neovascularis in the MPHP group is beneficial for gas exchange, diffusion of water and nutrients, and removal of waste products in the nascent muscle, which also explains the larger muscle fiber diameter in the MPHP group. In addition, innervation is essential for the long-term functional recovery of injured muscles [58]. The effect of MPHP scaffolds on the regeneration of muscle innervation was assessed by staining the β III-tubulin (neuronal marker). Quantitative results showed that the density of mature nerves in the regenerated area was significantly larger than that in the control group, suggesting enhanced innervation. Conclusion

Ti3C2Tx MXene has great potential in the field of biomedicine due to its unique surface chemistry, excellent mechanical properties and antioxidant properties, as well as other properties. However, it is susceptible to oxidation and moisture, which greatly reduces its activity. Therefore, we designed a multifunctional MPHP nanofiber-hydrogel scaffold to mimic the native muscle structure and microenvironment by layer-by-layer stacking for VML. In vitro studies found that the scaffold protected the activity of Ti3C2Tx, which enabled it to exert antioxidant effect in vivo, effectively scavenging excessive ROS and protecting muscle from oxidative stress. Cell experiments confirmed that the scaffold could induce myoblast adhesion and growth, and significantly promote myogenic differentiation by up-regulating the relative expression of MHC, MyoD and MyoG gene during myogenesis. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that the scaffold could induce the transformation of macrophages into M2 type, thereby reshaping the microenvironment of muscle regeneration and promoting the regeneration process. We found that the scaffold could promote muscle fiber regeneration and maturation, reduce fibrosis and the risk of scar formation, and effectively promote skeletal muscle regeneration in the rat model of VML in vivo. Moreover, by enhancing the reconstruction of vascular network and restoring re-innervation, the overall structure and function of muscle are restored, and the exercise endurance of the regenerated muscle is enhanced. One limitation of this study is that only female rats were used. Given known sex differences in immune function and skeletal muscle regeneration, the results may not fully translate to male subjects. Future studies should include both sexes to assess the generalizability of the scaffold's therapeutic effects.

To contextualize the performance of the MPHP scaffold, its functional and histological outcomes were compared with representative biomaterial-based strategies for VML repair reported in the literature. The mushroom-derived decellularized scaffold (md-CdM) supported the formation of mature, centronucleated myofibers, significantly reduced fibrosis, improved organized microvasculature, and enhanced locomotor performance and muscle strength [59]. The microporous annealed particle (MAP) hydrogel with cell-scale porosity supported the formation of innervated myofibers and neuromuscular junctions (NMJs), indicating functional neuromuscular recovery, demonstrating potential for functional restoration after VML injury [60]. In this comparison, we note that the MPHP scaffold, without cell seeding or minced muscle supplementation, reached 93.73 ± 3.63% muscle mass restoration, a myofiber diameter of 44.40 ± 15.50 μm (comparable to the sham group's 55.29 ± 12.00 μm), and a remarkably low fibrosis area of 2.66% ± 0.49%. While acknowledging the inherent limitations of cross-study comparisons due to differences in animal models, defect locations, injury volumes, and evaluation time points, the MPHP scaffold exhibits competitive regenerative performance, particularly in fibrosis suppression and myofiber maturation.

Therefore, this scaffold is expected to be used as an ideal scaffold model for skeletal muscle defect regeneration in the field of tissue regeneration, and provides a way to protect the activity of Ti3C2Tx MXene and broaden its application possibilities.
