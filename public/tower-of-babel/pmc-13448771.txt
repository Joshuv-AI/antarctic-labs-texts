Lower social participation and physical activity in patients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor: Their association with apathy
Authors: Misa Komaki, Yoshiki Arakawa, Keita Ueda, Katsutsugu Umeda, Tsukasa Ueno, Masahiro Tanji, Yohei Mineharu, Takeshi Funaki, Takayuki Kikuchi, Toshiya Murai, Ami Tabata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to compare social participation and physical activity in patients with craniopharyngioma or central nervous system germ cell tumor (CNS germ cell tumor) with those of healthy participants and examine their associations with social behavioral disorders.MethodsWe recruited adult patients with either childhood‐ or adult‐onset conditions who received treatment at the Kyoto University Hospital and healthy participants. Social participation and physical activity were assessed using the Community Integration Questionnaire (CIQ) and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire‐Short Form (IPAQ‐SF). Social behavioral disorders, including apathy, were evaluated using the Frontal Systems Behavior Scale (FrSBe). Data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Spearman's rank correlation.ResultsA total of 20 patients (mean age 35.15 ± 14.00 years), comprising 14 with craniopharyngioma and 6 with CNS germ cell tumor, along with 25 healthy participants (mean age 35.52 ± 15.28 years), were recruited. The patients' total scores (CIQ) and total physical activity (IPAQ‐SF) were significantly lower than those of healthy participants (p < 0.001 and p = 0.021, respectively). Significant negative correlations were found between patients' productivity (CIQ) and total scores (FrSBe), and between productivity and apathy (FrSBe) (ρ = −0.449, p = 0.047 and ρ = −0.572, p = 0.008, respectively).ConclusionPatients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor had lower levels of social participation and physical activity than healthy participants, and greater apathy was linked to reduced productivity. These findings suggest that joint activity planning and encouragement from caregivers may promote social participation in patients with apathy.

INTRODUCTION

Craniopharyngioma originates from remnants of the craniopharyngeal duct, and central nervous system germ cell tumor (CNS germ cell tumor) shows a predilection for the diencephalon such as the pituitary and the pineal gland. 1 , 2 According to the Brain Tumor Registry of Japan (2009–2015), the 5‐year survival rates of craniopharyngioma and pure germinoma are 95.8% and 96.7%, respectively. 3 Despite their favorable prognosis and several long‐term survivors, these tumors can significantly impact patients' quality of life. Young adults with craniopharyngioma have reported perceived disadvantages at work and in social integration. 4 Pediatric brain tumor survivors, specifically males, and CNS germ cell tumor survivors have high unemployment rates. 5 Additionally, postoperative childhood and adolescent craniopharyngioma patients may experience significant difficulties in daily functioning and social relationships, even if they show normal intellectual development on neuropsychological examination. 6 Regarding physical activity, several adult patients with primary brain tumors may be inadequately active from the time of diagnosis until post‐treatment. 7 Moreover, over 70% of patients with pituitary adenoma, a brain tumor affecting structures in the diencephalic region, have low overall physical activity levels. 8

Social behavioral disorders, including apathy, disinhibition, and executive dysfunction, are among the higher brain dysfunction that can occur after an acquired brain injury. 9 , 10 Several reports on social behavioral disorders in patients with brain tumors have been published. 11 , 12 , 13 Apathy has been reported in patients with craniopharyngioma. 14 Furthermore, social participation in patients with acquired brain injury is associated with apathy and executive dysfunction. 15 , 16 However, the association between physical activity and apathy in patients with acquired brain injury remains underexplored. 17 , 18 The actual conditions of social participation and physical activity in patients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor, as well as the association between social participation or physical activity and social behavioral disorders, have not been adequately investigated.

To bridge these gaps, this study aimed to (1) compare social participation and physical activity in patients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor; (2) assess the severity of social behavioral disorders in these patients; and (3) examine the association between social participation, physical activity, and social behavioral disorders. Our findings could inform targeted support strategies to promote social participation and physical activity, ultimately aiding patients, families, and caregivers.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study performed a secondary analysis of data obtained from the same survey as the original research article. 19 This prospective single‐center observational study was approved by the Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Approval Number: R2052, dated July 19, 2019). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We provided an explanation of the study to patients as well as each patient's family member who knew them best. Our study excluded minors from the analysis. Patients treated for craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor at the Kyoto University Hospital were surveyed. The recruitment period for patients spanned from July 19, 2019, to June 15, 2022. The sample size was determined based on the number of patients who could be recruited at the Kyoto University Hospital within this period.

The eligibility criteria for patients were as follows: (a) diagnosis of craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor; (b) prior history of treatment for craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor; and (c) provision of written informed consent. Patients were excluded from the study if (a) their tumor lesions were outside the diencephalon; (b) they had a prior history of cognitive dysfunction; (c) they suffered cerebrovascular complications during treatment; (d) new lesions arose after informed consent was provided; (e) their families did not cooperate in the study; and (f) they were deemed unsuitable by the researchers and/or the attending physician. Time since the last treatment was defined as the interval between the completion of the final treatment modality (e.g., the last day of radiotherapy, the final dose of chemotherapy, or the date of surgery for patients who underwent surgery alone) and the date of assessment. Participants' medical records were accessed for research purposes from July 19, 2019, to May 29, 2026. The authors had access to identifiable participant information during and after data collection.

Healthy Japanese participants aged 18–69 years were recruited through the distribution of leaflets and snowball sampling. Individuals who consented to participate in the study were included. The recruitment period for healthy participants spanned from September 15, 2023, to December 27, 2023. Healthy participants were excluded from the study if (a) they had an underlying disease or a history of a disease that would affect cognitive, mental, or motor function; (b) they had hearing or visual impairment; (c) they scored 1.5 standard deviations (SDs) lower than the mean score on the Block Design, Vocabulary, Symbol Search, and Coding of the Japanese version of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale‐4th edition (WAIS‐IV) 20 ; (d) they scored 33 points or more on the Japanese version of the Autism‐Spectrum Quotient (AQ) 21 , 22 ; and (e) they scored 14 points or more on the Japanese version of the Beck Depression Inventory‐2nd Edition (BDI‐II). 23

Data collection methods and tools

For patients, social participation was assessed by family members, while their physical activity and social behavioral disorders were evaluated by the patients themselves. For healthy participants, both social participation and physical activity were self‐assessed.

Participants' characteristics

Data on age, sex, educational background, and body mass index (BMI) were collected for all participants. Additional clinical data of patients were obtained from their medical records, including Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), treatment‐related information, and comorbidities. Among the comorbidities, hormone deficiencies were assessed by evaluating deficiencies in adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH), luteinizing hormone/follicle‐stimulating hormone (LH/FSH), growth hormone (GH), and antidiuretic hormone (ADH), along with the use and details of replacement therapy for each deficiency.

Cognitive function in patients was evaluated using the WAIS‐IV for intelligence, the Wechsler Memory Scale‐Revised (WMS‐R) for memory, and the Behavioural Assessment of the Dysexecutive Syndrome (BADS) for executive function.

Social participation

The Japanese version of the Community Integration Questionnaire (CIQ) assesses social participation and has been examined for reliability and validity. 24 , 25 , 26 It consists of 15 items divided into three subscales: “Home integration” (5 items on a 10‐point scale), “Social integration” (6 items on a 12‐point scale), and “Productivity” (4 items on a 7‐point scale). 24 , 25 , 26 Home integration asks about housework and shopping; social integration about leisure activities and visiting friends; and productivity about going out, working, and attending school. Total scores were evaluated on a scale of 0–29, with higher scores indicating a higher degree of social participation. In this study, patients' CIQ was assessed by their family members, whereas that of the healthy participants was assessed by the participants themselves.

Physical activity

The Japanese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire‐Short Form (IPAQ‐SF) is designed to assess physical activity in daily life, such as leisure time, at home, at work, and during transportation for adults aged 15–69 years. 27 , 28 , 29 Its reliability and validity have been confirmed. 28 , 29 The amount of physical activity in performing these four daily activities was scored as a continuous variable. Participants reported the duration (in minutes) and frequency (days) of walking, moderate‐intensity activities (e.g., carrying light loads), and vigorous‐intensity activities (e.g., carrying heavy loads), which were performed for at least 10 min each time. Physical activity was defined as 3.3 metabolic equivalents (METs) for walking, 4 METs for moderate‐intensity activities, and 8 METs for vigorous‐intensity activities. The total physical activity (METs‐minutes/week) was calculated from the METs, duration, and frequency of walking, moderate‐intensity activities, and vigorous‐intensity activities. Additionally, we classified the level of physical activity of the participants into three categories (high, moderate, and low) according to the criteria described in the guidelines. 27 Patients and healthy participants self‐assessed their IPAQ‐SF scores.

Social behavioral disorders

The Japanese version of the Frontal Systems Behavior Scale (FrSBe) is a questionnaire used to evaluate social behavioral disorders in patients. 30 , 31 This scale has been reviewed and standardized for reliability and validity. 30 , 31 It consists of 46 items, including apathy (14 items), disinhibition (15 items), and executive dysfunction (17 items). Each item is rated on a five‐point scale. The total score for each subscale and the combined total scores are converted into T‐scores according to the participant's sex, age (18–39/40–59/60 or older), and years of education (12 years or less/13 years or more). A T‐score of 65 or higher (1.5 SD or higher) indicates significant social behavioral disorders. Patients were asked to respond to their disease status. “Post‐disease” referred to the most recent disease status (within 6 months of the time of evaluation) and not immediately after treatment.

Data analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the assessment results in relation to participant characteristics, such as age, gender, educational background, and BMI. The level for determining statistical significance was two‐sided at 5%. The following were non‐normally distributed: (1) educational background, home integration (CIQ), productivity (CIQ), and total physical activity (IPAQ‐SF) for patients; (2) age, educational background, BMI, social integration (CIQ), productivity (CIQ), and total physical activity (IPAQ‐SF) for healthy participants. Therefore, we decided to employ nonparametric tests for the subsequent statistical analyses using JMP Pro 17.0 and 19.0, with a two‐sided significance level of 5%.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the results of the CIQ, IPAQ‐SF, FrSBe, WAIS‐IV Full‐Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ), WMS‐R General Memory, and BADS General Score results and to examine participant characteristics. Patients were categorized into groups according to the presence of deficiencies in each of the following hormones: ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH, GH, and ADH. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare CIQ, IPAQ‐SF, and FrSBe apathy scores between groups. Additionally, the proportions of patients with clinically significant apathy, defined as a FrSBe apathy T‐score ≥ 65, were compared between groups.

Patients were compared with healthy participants with respect to some of their characteristics. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare age, education, and BMI, while the chi‐square test was used to compare gender. To compare social participation and physical activity between patients and healthy participants, we compared the CIQ and IPAQ‐SF scores using the Mann–Whitney U test. To examine the correlations between the IPAQ‐SF scores and BMI in patients and healthy participants, respectively, we calculated Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. To analyze the association between social participation or physical activity and social behavioral disorders in patients, we performed a correlation analysis between the CIQ or IPAQ‐SF and FrSBe and calculated Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. We also conducted an analysis to explore the correlation between CIQ and IPAQ‐SF scores and various cognitive function assessments, including WAIS‐IV, WMS‐R, and BADS, by calculating Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

RESULTS

Participants' characteristics

Thirty‐four patients aged 18 years or older with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor who had been treated for at least 6 months at the Kyoto University Hospital provided their consent. However, 14 patients met the exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Thus, this study included 20 patients with craniopharyngioma (n = 14) and CNS germ cell tumor (n = 6). The mean age was 35.15 ± 14.00 years; age at diagnosis was 25.70 ± 15.89 years. The mean interval between the final treatment modality and assessment was 83.11 months (range, 6.0–279.4 months). The final treatments included surgery (n = 7), chemotherapy (n = 5), and radiotherapy (n = 8). All patients were medically stable at the time of assessment, with KPS scores of 90–100 (Tables 1 and S1). None of the patients had motor impairment, whereas 13 patients had visual impairment; among them, 3 patients presented with both bitemporal hemianopsia and unilateral decreased visual acuity. Additionally, a patient had epilepsy, and 15 patients had hormone deficiencies. Among the patients with hormone deficiencies, 12 (60.00%) had ACTH deficiency, 11 (55.00%) had TSH or LH/FSH deficiencies, 8 (40.00%) had GH deficiency, and 13 (65.00%) had ADH deficiency. All patients with ACTH, TSH, or ADH deficiencies were receiving hormone replacement therapy, whereas four patients with LH/FSH deficiency and three with GH deficiency were not (Table S2). The family members of the patients who participated in this study were parents (n = 13), spouses (n = 5), a sister (n = 1), and a daughter (n = 1). The mean total FrSBe score was 58.22 ± 14.59, and the mean apathy score was 60.02 ± 14.38. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the presence of hormone deficiencies (ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH, GH and ADH), and the CIQ, IPAQ‐SF, and FrSBe apathy scores were compared between groups. However, no significant differences were observed in any of the measures (Table S3a–f). The median apathy score and percentage of patients with an apathy T‐score ≥ 65 among patients with hormone deficiencies (n = 15) were 61.40 (interquartile range [IQR] = 21.60) and 40.00%, respectively, compared with 55.00 (IQR = 9.30) and 0.00%, respectively, among patients without hormone deficiencies (n = 5).

Thirty‐four healthy individuals participated in this study. However, nine participants met the exclusion criteria (Figure 1), and this study included 25 healthy participants. The mean age was 35.52 ± 15.28 years (Table 1).

Table 1 shows that there were no significant differences in age and gender. However, educational level was significantly higher in healthy participants, and BMI was significantly higher in patients (p = 0.832, p = 1.000, p = 0.043, and p < 0.001).

Social participation

For patients, the median of total scores (CIQ) was 16.38 (IQR = 8.31), home integration was 3.75 (IQR = 8.44), social integration was 7.00 (IQR = 3.75), and productivity was 5.00 (IQR = 3.75) (Table 1). For healthy participants, the median of total scores (CIQ) was 20.75 (IQR = 5.88), home integration was 6.25 (IQR = 4.25), social integration was 9.00 (IQR = 2.00), and productivity was 6.00 (IQR = 1.00). Regarding CIQ scores, the patients' total, home integration, social integration, and productivity scores were all significantly lower than those of healthy participants (p < 0.001, p = 0.020, p = 0.010, and p < 0.001) (Figure 2). Five patients (25%) scored 0 points for home integration. Among patients, 14 (70%) had gone out almost every day in the month preceding the study, compared to 24 (96%) healthy participants. Regarding education and employment status among patients, 15% were university students, 40% were employed full‐time, 15% were employed part‐time, 10% were receiving continuous support from employment services, 15% were unemployed, and 5% were retired. In contrast, among healthy participants, 32% were university students, 20% were graduate students, 36% were employed full‐time, 8% were employed part‐time, and 4% were retired (Table 1).

Physical activity

The median (IQR) total physical activity (METs‐minutes/week) (IPAQ‐SF) of patients was 912.00 (IQR = 863.38) (Table 1), whereas that among healthy participants was 1398.00 (IQR = 2091.00). Regarding categorical variables, 10% of the patients were classified as high, 45% as moderate, and 45% as low; by contrast, 28% of healthy participants were categorized as high, 48% as moderate, and 24% as low. Patients' total physical activity was significantly lower than that of healthy participants (p = 0.021) (Figure 3). In contrast, no significant association was found between total physical activity and BMI in either patients or healthy participants (Table S4).

Correlations of social participation and physical activity with social behavioral disorders

There were no significant correlations between patients' CIQ total scores and FrSBe total scores, WAIS‐IV FSIQ, WMS‐R General Memory and BADS General Score (ρ = −0.392, p = 0.087; ρ = 0.050, p = 0.835; ρ = −0.181, p = 0.446; and ρ = 0.198, p = 0.403) (Table 2). However, significant negative correlations were found between CIQ productivity and FrSBe total scores and between CIQ productivity and apathy (ρ = −0.449, p = 0.047; ρ = −0.572, p = 0.008). No significant correlations were observed between patients' total physical activity and FrSBe total scores, WAIS‐IV FSIQ, WMS‐R General Memory, and BADS General Score (ρ = 0.353, p = 0.127; ρ = 0.106, p = 0.656; ρ = 0.103, p = 0.665; and ρ = 0.429, p = 0.059).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined social participation and physical activity in patients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor. Both outcomes were significantly lower than those among healthy participants. Social participation, particularly productivity, was negatively associated with apathy, whereas physical activity showed no significant relationship with social behavioral disorders.

Compared with healthy participants, patients demonstrated significantly lower total CIQ scores as well as scores for home integration, social integration, and productivity. Regarding home integration, some patients may not have assumed functional roles within their households. Therefore, rehabilitation should not only assess the skills required for domestic tasks but also consider which activities can be performed independently and which can be shared with family members. Regarding social integration and productivity, patients may have fewer opportunities to leave home. Accordingly, interventions should aim to increase opportunities for community participation, including commuting to school or work, shopping, leisure activities, and other social engagements. Although most participants in both groups were of working age, three patients were unemployed. Supporting unemployed patients and facilitating their return to work may require collaboration with governmental and welfare agencies that provide vocational assistance.

A significant negative correlation was observed between CIQ productivity and apathy. This suggests that greater apathy reduces the frequency with which patients leave homes, the time they spend working or attending school, and their engagement in volunteer activities. These results are consistent with previous findings in patients with acquired brain injury. 15 Interventions that address apathy may therefore be necessary to improve community participation and employment outcomes. Although patients with apathy may be able to complete scheduled tasks with external support, such as structured routines and reminders from caregivers, it is equally important that they find enjoyment in physical activity and planned outings tailored to their individual circumstances. 32 , 33 Previous studies have also shown that adult survivors of childhood‐onset brain tumors with panhypopituitarism present higher levels of apathy and executive dysfunction compared with those without the condition. 34 Similarly, in this study, patients with hormone deficiencies tended to show higher apathy score and a greater prevalence of the symptom, although the statistical analysis yielded no significant differences. As the symptoms of apathy overlap with those of hormone deficiencies, 35 , 36 apathy may be more pronounced in these patients. Specifically, deficiencies in ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH, and GH may affect asthenia, apathy, and vitality. 35 , 36 , 37 In this study, all patients with hormone deficiencies received ACTH and TSH replacement therapy. In contrast, LH/FSH was not replaced in four patients, and GH was not replaced in three patients. Therefore, the possibility that LH/FSH or GH deficiencies may have influenced activity or apathy cannot be excluded. Moreover, the proximity of craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor to both the frontal‐subcortical circuits implicated in apathy 9 , 10 , 30 , 31 and the hypothalamic‐pituitary axis implicated in hormone deficiency makes it difficult to clearly distinguish between the two, and both conditions may coexist. Thus, clinical support should address the potential overlap of apathy and hormone deficiency in these patients.

Our findings on physical activity are consistent with those of earlier studies in patients with acquired brain injury, which reported reduced activity levels in patients compared with healthy participants. 18 This suggests that lower physical activity may occur independently of motor impairment. Patients had a significantly higher BMI than healthy participants. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed between total physical activity and BMI in either patients or healthy participants; however, a decrease in physical activity leading to reduced energy expenditure may contribute to an increase in BMI. 38 Additionally, previous studies have reported that craniopharyngioma increases the risk of hypothalamic obesity, either because of the tumor itself or its treatment. 39 A population‐based study in Spanish adults reported that overweight (25 < BMI < 30) and obese (BMI > 30) individuals were less physically active than those of normal weight (18.5 < BMI < 25). 40 To summarize, physical activity and BMI may influence each other bidirectionally. Therefore, promoting physical activity is important, and the IPAQ guidelines recommend 30 min of moderate‐intensity activity (4 METs) as a baseline for adults. 27 Thus, the 45% of patients with “low” levels of physical activity could aim to engage in 30 min of moderate‐intensity activity at least 5 days a week. If this is not feasible, approximately 37 min of daily walking (3.3 METs) would provide comparable physical activity.

Finally, no significant correlation was found between physical activity and social behavioral disorders. This indicates that other factors, beyond social behavioral disorders, may influence physical activity in these patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Specifically, the limited sample size precluded lesion‐based analysis and the use of multivariable regression to adjust for potential confounding variables. Although significant differences were observed, they should be interpreted with caution because of the increased risk of Type I error. Second, the educational level of patients was significantly lower than that of healthy participants. This difference may have influenced social participation, as craniopharyngioma and CNS germ cell tumor often develop during childhood or adolescence, potentially disrupting patients' educational attainment. Third, the timing of data collection differed between patients and healthy participants. For patients, part of the data collection overlapped with the COVID‐19 pandemic in Japan, beginning in January 2020, when repeated states of emergency (2020–2022) included restrictions on leaving home and recommendations for remote work. These restrictions may have reduced social participation and physical activity. 41 , 42 , 43 In contrast, data from healthy participants were collected after the restrictions had subsided. Thus, simultaneous data collection across both groups may have yielded different results. Fourth, epilepsy or visual disturbances can profoundly affect daily life, particularly because of driving restrictions. In this study, one patient had epilepsy, and three patients had visual impairment that could affect their ability to drive; however, data on the driving abilities of the remaining 16 patients could not be collected. As previous research has reported a significant association between driving and community integration among patients with acquired brain injury, 44 future studies should collect data on driving capability when examining social participation in the future.

Furthermore, future studies should include larger sample sizes to enable lesion‐based analyses and apply more robust statistical methods, including multivariable regression analyses, to identify factors related to physical activity. Moreover, longitudinal research is required to examine whether clinical outcomes, including social participation, can be improved through interventions that address apathy.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that patients with craniopharyngioma or CNS germ cell tumor had significantly lower levels of social participation and physical activity compared with healthy participants. Moreover, greater apathy was associated with reduced productivity. These findings highlight the need to identify factors influencing physical activity, raise awareness among patients, families, and caregivers about the potential impact of social behavioral disorders on social participation, and develop supportive interventions that address them.
