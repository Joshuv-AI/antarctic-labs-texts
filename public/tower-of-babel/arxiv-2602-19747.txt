Symmetry and Exact Solutions of General Spin-Boson Models
Yifan Sun 1,4 , and Lian-Ao Wu 1,2,3

Abstract
Spin–boson models are the canonical benchmark for quantum dissipation. We show the symmetry structure of general spin–boson Hamiltonians and obtain their spectra explicitly by exploiting the symmetry. As an illustration of the general case, we numerically demonstrate the exact solution for the two-mode case.

Symmetry of the models.—
One well-known symmetry of Hamiltonian (
1
) is its conserved parity, arising from the joint parity of the spin and bosons. This discrete symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
plays a central role in organizing the spectrum and constraining the dynamics of the system. The total parity operator is usually defined by
\Pi=\sigma_{z}\mathcal{P}
, where
\sigma_{z}
and
\mathcal{P}=(-1)^{\sum_{l}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}}
are the parity of spin and bosons, respectively. However, a commonly overlooked symmetry is the time-reversal symmetry, usually expressed by the anti-unitary operator
\mathcal{T}
. The function of the operator is specified by

\mathcal{T}a_{j}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=a_{j}
,
\mathcal{T}a_{j}^{\dagger}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=a_{j}^{\dagger}
,
\mathcal{T}\sigma_{x}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\sigma_{x}
, and
\mathcal{T}\sigma_{z}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\sigma_{z}
. Therefore, the total symmetry of Hamiltonian (
1
) can be characterized by
\mathcal{T}
,
\Pi
, and
\mathcal{T}\Pi
.

Examining the symmetry more closely allows us to construct a general procedure for diagonalizing the Hamiltonian in spin space. A rotation about the spin-
X
axis generated by the bosonic number operator yields the basic transformation rules
e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}ae^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}=e^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}}a
, and

e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}a^{\dagger}e^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}=e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}}a^{\dagger}
(see Supplementary I for the proof). These relations allow us to introduce or remove exponential Pauli-
X
factors coupled to the bosonic operators. The normal parity operator can, in fact, be viewed as a special case of such rotations: it rotates the spin and each bosonic mode halfway toward the identity, leaving the interaction term invariant as a whole and thereby preserving the symmetry of the total Hamiltonian. In contrast, our transformations implement arbitrary spin rotations generated by the bosonic number operators, equivalently acting as bosonic rotations conditioned on the spin state. This leads to a richer set of transformation patterns and alters the symmetry structure of the Hamiltonian. In particular, such variations can render the action of the operator
\mathcal{T}
nontrivial.

Applying the transformations with
\theta=\pi/2
and extending the transformation to the multimode case, one obtains the following Hamiltonian

U^{\dagger}H_{M}U=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger}-a_{k})+\Delta\Pi,

(2)

where
U=e^{i(\pi/2)\sigma_{x}\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}}
. It can be seen that the Hamiltonian (
2
) is manifestly diagonal in the spin sector. The symmetry structure of the Hamiltonian is also modified by this transformation. While the spin-parity symmetry is preserved, the bosonic sector retains only the combined parity–time-reversal symmetry. Consequently, the transformed Hamiltonian is characterized by the symmetry operators
\sigma_{z}
,
\mathcal{PT}
, and
\sigma_{z}\mathcal{PT}
. The resulting model describes a qualitatively different spin–boson system. In this system, the crosstalk between the spin and the bosonic modes becomes explicitly parity dependent, as manifested in the third term of right-hand-side of Eq. (
2
). The second term, proportional to the weighted sum of bosonic momenta, induces flips of the bosonic parity and further affects the effective parity of the spin through the coupling. Although no physical system of this form has yet been recognized, the transformed Hamiltonian remains closely connected to the generalized spin–boson model, a fundamental framework for light–matter interactions as presented in the introduction. The two Hamiltonians share the same spectrum, and their solvability properties are also directly linked. This correspondence provides an interesting insight into each model through the other. An equivalent form of Hamiltonian (

U_{B}=e^{-i(\pi/2)\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}}

\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger}+a_{k})
, while the other terms remain unchanged. Importantly, the single-mode case (
N=1

One extension.—
The model described by Hamiltonian (
1
) can be extended to

H_{MS}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\sigma_{x}\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger 2}+a^{2}_{k})+\Delta\sigma_{z},

(3)

which represents a single spin interacting with a multimode squeezed bosonic field. The single-mode case of this model, known as the two-photon Rabi model, has been widely explored in various physical settings
Gerry1988
, including quantum dots embedded in a QED microcavity
Valle2010
and trapped-ion platforms
Felicetti2015
. Its spectral properties have attracted considerable attention
Chen2012
;
Travenec2012
;
Maciejewski
, and several isolated exact solutions have been identified
Zhang2014
;
Zhang2015
. However, the symmetry and solvability of the multi-mode generalization have rarely been explored.

By squaring the transformations in the previous section, the transformation rules
e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}aae^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}=e^{-i2\theta\sigma_{x}}aa
, and
e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}e^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}a^{\dagger}a}=e^{i2\theta\sigma_{x}}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}
can be obtained, which can be further applied to decouple the spin from the squeezed bosonic field. Similarly, applying the squared transformations with
\theta=\pi/4
and extending the transformation to the multi-mode case, the transformed Hamiltonian can be given by

\tilde{U}^{\dagger}H_{MS}\tilde{U}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger 2}-a_{k}^{2})+\Delta\Pi_{q},

(4)

where
U=e^{i(\pi/4)\sigma_{x}\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}}
and

\Pi_{q}=\sigma_{z}\cos{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}\right)}+\sigma_{y}\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}\right)}.

In fact, the operator
\Pi_{q}
encodes the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
parity of the system, representing a natural extension of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry inherent in Hamiltonian (
1
). It plays the same role as the third term in Hamiltonian (
2
), and constitutes a key ingredient underlying the solvability of the model described by
H_{MS}
.

The discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry of
H_{MS}
is specifically generated by
\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}
with
\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}=e^{i(\pi/2)\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}}
. This implies that the Hilbert space decomposes into four invariant subspaces labeled by the eigenvalues of
\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}
. As revealed by Hamiltonian (
4
), the spin–boson interaction decouples, and the spin contribution is transformed into a symmetry-induced operator. This reformulation produces an explicit decoupling structure for the entire Hamiltonian. Because the operators
a_{k}^{\dagger 2}
and
a_{k}^{2}
change the boson number by exactly two, it remains even or odd so that the bosonic parity is strictly preserved. Hence, the third term can be treated independently within the even and odd boson-number sectors, and the full Hilbert space naturally splits into two disjoint subspaces,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{e}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{o}
, corresponding to even and odd total bosonic parity. The effective Hamiltonians that govern the two subspaces are

H^{\pm}_{MS}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger 2}-a_{k}^{2})+\Delta\Pi^{\pm}_{q},

(5)

where
\Pi^{+}_{q}=\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}
for even boson-number states and
\Pi^{-}_{q}=-i\sigma_{y}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}
for odd boson-number states. Within each invariant subspace,
H^{\pm}_{MS}
can be further block-diagonalized in the spin basis, resulting in a four-block decomposition of the full Hamiltonian. This decomposition directly reflects the underlying
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry of the system.

Exact solutions.—
The exact solution of
H_{M}
can be obtained more transparently after diagonalizing it in the spin sector. A convenient and powerful method for achieving this is to work in the Bargmann representation
Bargmann1961
;
Bargmann1962
, an analytic function space that is isomorphic to the Hilbert space of bosonic modes. The key correspondence between bosonic operators and analytic functions in the Bargmann space is
a^{\dagger}\leftrightarrow z
, and
a\leftrightarrow\partial_{z}
. Furthermore, the correspondence between Bargmann-space wavefunctions and Fock states is given by
|n\rangle\leftrightarrow z^{n}/\sqrt{n!}
. With this treatment, the Schrödinger eigenvalue equation for the original bosonic Hamiltonian is transformed into an eigenvalue problem for analytic functions of complex variables. To treat systems with multiple bosonic modes, one introduces the multi-variable Bargmann space
Vukics2018
;
Chabaud2022
, in which each mode is represented by its own complex coordinate and the full Hilbert space is realized as analytic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. For clarity and ease of comparison with known results, we present the solution in the formalism of Ref.
Braak2011
, although other equivalent formulations are equally valid.

Using the operator
U
and
\pi/2
rotation of the bosonic modes, the following Hamiltonian can be given,

H_{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger}+a_{k})+\Delta\Pi.

(6)

In the spin sector, the Hamiltonian
H_{D}
becomes block-diagonal,
H_{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{H}^{+}_{D},\tilde{H}^{-}_{D})
. Using multi-varibale Bargammn representation, these operators take the form

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{D}^{\pm}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}z_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(z_{k}+\partial_{z_{k}})\\
&\pm\Delta(-1)^{\sum_{l=1}^{N}z_{l}\partial_{z_{l}}}\\
=&(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z})\cdot\bm{\partial_{z}}+\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{z}+\bm{\partial_{z}})\pm\Delta T,\end{split}

(7)

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product
Styan1973
.

\bm{\omega}
,
\bm{g}
,
\bm{z}
, and
\bm{\partial_{z}}
denote the corresponding vectors of parameters, variables, and partial derivatives. The operator
T
represents the reflection of all mode variables, defined by
Tf(\bm{z})=f(\bm{-z})
(see Supplementary II for the proof).

By solving the Hamiltonians
H_{D}^{\pm}
, one obtains the corresponding energy spectra of the two sectors. The associated Schrödinger equation reads

(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z}+\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{z}}\psi(\bm{z})+(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{z}-E)\psi(\bm{z})\pm\Delta\psi(\bm{-z})=0.

(8)

The equation can be solved using the
G
-function method
Braak2011
. Specifically, the
G
-function associated with this system is given by

G_{N}^{\pm}(X)=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}A_{\bm{n}}\left(1\mp\frac{\Delta}{X-\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{n}}\right)\bm{g}^{\bm{n}},

(9)

where
\bm{n}
denotes an
N
-component multi-index vecotr of non-negative integers, and the summation over
\bm{n}
indicates that each component runs independently from 0 to
\infty
. The multi-index monomial
\bm{g}^{\bm{n}}
is defined by
g_{1}^{n_{1}}g_{2}^{n_{2}}\ldots g_{N}^{n_{N}}
. The coefficients
A_{\bm{n}}
is given by

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}n_{j}A_{\bm{n-1}+\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{n-1}}A_{\bm{n-1}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}A_{\bm{n-1}-\bm{e}_{j}},\end{split}

(10)

Here,
\bm{e}_{j}
denotes an
N
-component unit vector whose
j
-th entry is 1 and all other entries are 0.
\bm{1}
denotes an
N
-component vector with all entries being 1.
X
is defined by
E+\sum_{j}g_{j}^{2}/\omega_{j}
.
f_{\bm{n}}(X)
is given by

f_{\bm{n}}(X)=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{g_{j}^{2}}{\omega_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}-X+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{X-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\right).

(11)

The spectrum of the Hamiltonian (
1
) is determined by the zeros of the functions
G^{\pm}_{N}(X)
. In particular, the zeros of
G^{+}_{N}(X)
correspond to the spectrum of the positive-parity sector, while those of
G^{-}_{N}(X)
correspond to the negative-parity sector. The initial condition for Eq. (
10
) can be set by
A_{\bm{0}}=1
, where
\bm{0}
is the zero vector. To obtain each
A_{\bm{n}}
, one needs the analytical requirements of
\psi(\bm{z})
and permutation symmetry of the bosons. From Hamiltonian (
1
), one can observe that the spectrum of the system remains invariant under the simultaneous exchanges
g_{j}\leftrightarrow g_{k}
and
\omega_{j}\leftrightarrow\omega_{k}
. This property effectively reduces the number of degrees of freedom, thereby ensuring the solvability of Eq. (
10
) and ultimately yielding an exact solution of the Hamiltonian. Further details are provided in Supplementary III.

reduces to the standard Rabi model

Conclusions.—
In this work, we highlight two fundamental symmetries of generalized spin–boson models that ultimately enable their exact solution. The first is time-reversal symmetry, which lies at the root of the general conventional expressions for diagonalizing the Hamiltonian. The second is the permutation symmetry of the bosonic modes, which underpins the construction of the series expansion underlying the generalized
G
-function.

We further emphasize that the diagonalizations given in Eqs. (
2
) and (
4
) reveal a previously unnoticed connection between generalized spin–boson models and a parity-interaction model. Finally, the theoretical framework developed here opens the way for analyzing the intricate energy spectra of generalized spin–boson systems in high-dimensional Hilbert spaces, which we leave for future investigation.

a
, the left-hand side reads

\begin{split}&(e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}a}ae^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}a})(e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}a}ae^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}a})\\
&=e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}a}aae^{-i\theta\sigma_{x}\otimes a^{\dagger}a},\end{split}

(S10)

and the right-hand side reads

(e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}}\otimes a)(e^{i\theta\sigma_{x}}\otimes a)=e^{i2\theta\sigma_{x}}\otimes(aa).

(S11)

The transformation on the Pauli operators is similar to Eq. (
S9
).

II
II. The reflection operator in Bargmann space.

The mapping from boson operators to the Bargmann space is given by

a_{j}^{\dagger}\rightarrow z_{j},~a_{j}\rightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}.

(S12)

As shown in the main text, the map transforms the last term of the Spin-Boson Hamiltonian into

\Delta\sigma_{z}(-1)^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}+\cdots)}.

(S13)

Next, we use the two-term form to show the case, and the general case is the same. Notice that
e^{i\pi}=-1
. Therefore, in the Bargmann space, the operator should be

(e^{i\pi})^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}=e^{i\pi(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}.

(S14)

Because the Taylor expansion of an exponential function shows good convergence, one can use the expansion for computation. For the factor
z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}/\sqrt{m!n!}
(corresponding to
|m\rangle|n\rangle
in the Fork space), it is given by

\begin{split}&(-1)^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}\frac{z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}\\
=&e^{i\pi(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}\frac{z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}=e^{i\pi z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}}e^{i\pi z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}}}\frac{z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}\\
=&\left[\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a!}(i\pi z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}})^{a}\frac{z_{1}^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}\right]\left[\sum_{b=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b!}(i\pi z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})^{b}\frac{z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\right].\end{split}

(S15)

Using a simple relation, the above analysis can be simplified. Notice that

(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})^{k}z^{n}=n^{k}z^{n}.

(S16)

This is because

\begin{split}&(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\cdots(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})z^{n}\\
&=(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\cdots(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})nz^{n}=(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\cdots n^{2}z^{n}\\
&=\cdots=n^{k}z^{n}.\end{split}

(S17)

Therefore, more generally, for an element in the Bargmann space
f(z)
, one has

\begin{split}&e^{\lambda z\frac{d}{dz}}f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}(z\frac{d}{dz})^{k}(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n})\\
=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}(z\frac{d}{dz})^{k}z^{n}\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda n)^{k}}{k!}z^{n}\right]\\
=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda n)^{k}}{k!}\right]z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}e^{n\lambda}z^{n}\\
=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(e^{\lambda}z)^{n}=f(e^{\lambda}z).\end{split}

(S18)

Let
\lambda=i\pi
, and one can go back to the previous case. Therefore,

\begin{split}(-1)^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}\frac{z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}=(-1)^{m+n}\frac{z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}=\frac{(-z_{1})^{m}(-z_{2})^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}},\end{split}

(S19)

and, moreover,

(-1)^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}f(z_{1},z_{2})=f(-z_{1},-z_{2}).

(S20)

This means that
(-1)^{(z_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}}+z_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{2}})}
actually equals the
\hat{T}
operator in the following part, or Braak’s method. As mentioned, the gneral multi-mode case is the same.

III
III. Derivation of solutions to General Spin-Boson Hamiltonians

We start from Schrödinger the equation,

(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z}+\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{z}}\psi(\bm{z})+(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{z}-E)\psi(\bm{z})\pm\Delta\psi(\bm{-z})=0,

(S21)

where

\begin{split}\bm{\omega}&=\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}),\\
\bm{z}&=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}),\\
\bm{g}&=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}),\\
\partial_{\bm{z}}&=(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{N}}),\\
\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z}&=(\omega_{1}z_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}z_{N})\end{split}

(S22)

We take the positive case as an example. By using the method presented by D. Braak, the solution to the equation is encoded by two coupled equations

\begin{split}(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z}+\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{z}}\psi(\bm{z})+(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{z}-E)\psi(\bm{z})+\Delta\phi(\bm{z})=0,\\
(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{z}-\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{z}}\phi(\bm{z})-(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{z}+E)\phi(\bm{z})+\Delta\psi(\bm{z})=0,\end{split}

(S23)

under the condition that
\psi(-\bm{z})=\phi(\bm{z})
. Notice that the second equation is obtained by performing the reflection operator
T
in the second equation.

Firstly, shift the parameter by
\bm{y}=\bm{z}+\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}
, where
\bm{\omega^{-1}}=(1/\omega_{1},\ldots,1/\omega_{N})
. Then, the equation changes to

\begin{split}(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{y})\cdot\bm{\partial_{y}}\psi(\bm{y})+[\bm{g}\cdot&\bm{y}-(E+\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})]\psi(\bm{y})+\Delta\phi(\bm{y})=0,\\
(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{y}-2\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{y}}\phi(\bm{y})-[&\bm{g}\cdot\bm{y}+(E-\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})]\phi(\bm{y})+\Delta\psi(\bm{y})=0,\end{split}

(S24)

Notice that
\bm{\partial}_{\bm{z}}=\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}
and
\bm{\omega}\circ(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})=\bm{g}
.

Secondly, assume that
\psi
and
\phi
have exponential scale factors, which guarantees the convergence of the solutions at infinite points. Hence, set

\begin{split}\psi(\bm{y})&=e^{-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y}),\\
\phi(\bm{y})&=e^{-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})}\bar{\phi}(\bm{y}).\end{split}

Then, the set of equations changes to

\begin{split}(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{y})\cdot\bm{\partial_{y}}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})&-X\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})+\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{y})=0,\\
(\bm{\omega}\circ\bm{y}-2\bm{g})\cdot\bm{\partial_{y}}\bar{\phi}(\bm{y})-[2\bm{g}\cdot\bm{y}&+X-4\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})]\bar{\phi}(\bm{y})+\Delta\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})=0,\end{split}

(S25)

where
X
is defined by
E+\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})
. This is based on the partial derivative

\begin{split}&\bm{\partial_{y}}[e^{-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})\\
=&-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}[e^{-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})]+[e^{-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})}\bm{\partial_{y}}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})],\end{split}

as well as the one for
\bar{\phi}
. The exponential factors can be canceled out.

Thirdly, consider the polynomial solutions of
\bar{\psi}
and
\bar{\phi}
. Set

\begin{split}\bar{\psi}(\bm{y})&=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}B^{\prime}_{\bm{n}}\bm{y}^{\bm{n}}\coloneq\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}=-\infty}^{\infty}B^{\prime}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}}y_{1}^{n_{1}}y_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots y_{N}^{n_{N}},\\
\bar{\phi}(\bm{y})&=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}B_{\bm{n}}\bm{y}^{\bm{n}}\coloneq\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}=-\infty}^{\infty}B_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}}y_{1}^{n_{1}}y_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots y_{N}^{n_{N}},\end{split}

(S26)

where
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{N})
. Substitute the solutions to the first of Eqs. (
S25
), one has

B^{\prime}_{\bm{n}}=B_{\bm{n}}\frac{\Delta}{X-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}}.

(S27)

Using the above relation and the second of Eqs. (
S25
), one obtains the recurrence relation for
B
,

\begin{split}&g_{1}(n_{1}+1)B_{n_{1}+1,n_{2},\ldots}+g_{2}(n_{2}+1)B_{n_{1},n_{2}+1,\ldots}+\cdots\\
=&f_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}-g_{1}B_{n_{1}-1,n_{2},\ldots}-g_{2}B_{n_{1},n_{2}-1,\ldots}-\cdots.\end{split}

(S28)

In a more compact form, one has

\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}(n_{j}+1)B_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{n}}B_{\bm{n}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}.

(S29)

\bm{e}_{j}
denotes an
N
-component vector whose
j
th component is
1
and all others are zeros.
f_{\bm{n}}
is given by

f_{\bm{n}}(X)=2\bm{g}\cdot(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega}^{-1})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}-X+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{X-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\right).

(S30)

Calculating each
B_{\bm{n}}
from the recurrence, the formal solutions to Eq. (
S25
) can be given by

\begin{split}\psi(\bm{z})&=e^{-(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})\cdot\bm{z}}\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\frac{\Delta}{X-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}}B_{\bm{n}}(\bm{z}+\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega}^{-1})^{\bm{n}},\\
&\phi(\bm{z})=e^{-(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega^{-1}})\cdot\bm{z}}\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}B_{\bm{n}}(\bm{z}+\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega}^{-1})^{\bm{n}}.\end{split}

(S31)

Like what Braak has done in his work, the energy spectrum (or equivalently
X
) can be obtained by using the analytical property of the solutions: (a) in order to avoid singular points at
\bm{z}=\bm{0}
,
B_{\bm{n}\in(\mathbb{Z^{-}})^{N}}=0
; (b) in order to obtain the solutions to the original Eq. (
S21
) (the positive sector),
\psi(\bm{z})
must equal to
\phi(-\bm{z})
. Hence, the spectrum, or the eigenvalues of Eq. (
S21
), can be given by the zeros of the function
G^{\pm}_{N}(X;\bm{z})=\phi(-\bm{z})-\psi(\bm{z})
. Notice that different
\bm{z}
s in the complex-variable space shall not change the spectrum. Therefore, by taking
\bm{z}=\bm{0}
, one can employ

\begin{split}G_{N}^{\pm}(X)\coloneq G_{N}^{\pm}(X;0)=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}B_{\bm{n}}\left(1\mp\frac{\Delta}{X-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\right)(\bm{g}\circ\bm{\omega}^{-1})^{\bm{n}},\end{split}

(S32)

for finding the zeros. Furthermore, the energy spectrum can be obtained by the zeros. Notice that the spectrum for the negative sector is obtained by making the transformation
\Delta\rightarrow-\Delta
.

Next, we discuss the calculation of
B_{\bm{n}}
. Although
B_{\bm{n}\in(\mathbb{Z^{-}})^{N}}=0
is set due to the analytical property of the solutions, Eq. (
S29
) still holds for all
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Then, one can calculate each
B_{\bm{n}}
by substituting different integers into the subscripts. There are many ways to accomplish this, and we give one strategy of them as follows.

Define the total bosonic mode number as
p=\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}
. By evaluating
p
with integers, or equivalently setting
n_{j}
s, one can obtain series of linear equation sets for
B_{\bm{n}}
. Notably, Eq. (
S29
) changes to

\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}n_{j}B_{\bm{n}-\bm{1}+\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{n-1}}B_{\bm{n-1}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{n-1}-\bm{e}_{j}}.

(S33)

It can be seen that the cases when
p<N
are trivial. This is because there are no choices of
\bm{n}
in those cases such that the components of the vector
\bm{n-1}+\bm{e}_{j}
are all non-negative. A simple example is the case when
p=N-1
. In such a case, if
\bm{n}=(N-1,0,0,\ldots,0)
,
\bm{n-1}+\bm{e}_{j}
is
(N-2,-1,-1,\ldots)
, which has a large number of negative components. As we mentioned,
B_{\bm{n}}
with negative subscripts must be zeros. Another example is the case when
\bm{n}=(1,1,\ldots,1,0)
. Substituting it into Eq. (
S33
), one obtains the relation
0\cdot B_{\bm{0}}=0
, which corresponds to a boundary condition. In the main text, we set
B_{\bm{0}}=1
. The other choices of
\bm{n}
under this condition are similar.

Things go differently when
p\geq N
. For example, when
p=N
, there is only one effective set for
\bm{n}
, i.e.,
\bm{n}=\bm{1}
. Then, one has

\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{0}}B_{\bm{0}}=f_{\bm{0}}
. This is an under-determined equation for
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}
. There are multiple choices, and one can obtain a confirmed one by introducing extra symmetry constraints, such as the permutation symmetry of the spin-boson Hamiltonian.

The specific description and the affection of the permutation symmetry are discussed as follows. From the Hamiltonian, it can be observed that

[g_{i}\rightleftharpoons g_{j},~\omega_{i}\rightleftharpoons\omega_{j}]\Leftrightarrow[a_{i}^{\dagger}\rightleftharpoons a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{i}\rightleftharpoons a_{j}].

(S34)

Furthermore, exchanging the
i
th and
j
th bosonic modes (exchanging
a_{i}^{\dagger}
and
a_{j}^{\dagger}
, and simultaneously exchanging
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
) will not affect the spectrum. There are no differences emerging after the transformations, except the name of.the modes. Therefore, the zeros of Eq. (
S32
) must not change. Specifically in Eq. (
S32
), if one makes the exchange of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, along with the exchange of
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, it can be obtained that

\begin{split}&\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}\left[1\mp\frac{\Delta}{X-(n_{1}\omega_{2}+n_{2}\omega_{1}+\ldots)}\right]\left(\frac{g_{2}}{\omega_{2}}\right)^{n_{1}}\left(\frac{g_{1}}{\omega_{1}}\right)^{n_{2}}\ldots\\
=&\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}B_{n_{2},n_{1},\ldots}\left[1\mp\frac{\Delta}{X-(n_{2}\omega_{2}+n_{1}\omega_{1}+\ldots)}\right]\left(\frac{g_{2}}{\omega_{2}}\right)^{n_{2}}\left(\frac{g_{1}}{\omega_{1}}\right)^{n_{1}}\ldots.\end{split}

(S35)

The equation holds because one can change the number of the summation index, for example, rename the one with
\omega_{1}
by
n_{1}
, and the one with
\omega_{2}
by
n_{2}
. Hence, the main factors align with each other, and one has

B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}=B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}(g_{1}\rightleftharpoons g_{2},\omega_{1}\rightleftharpoons\omega_{2})
. It means that
B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}
solved from Eq. (
S33
) equals to
B_{n_{2},n_{1},\ldots}
solved from the equation after exchanging
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
as well as
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. More generally, one has

B_{\ldots,n_{i},\ldots,n_{j},\ldots}=B_{\ldots,n_{j},\ldots,n_{i},\ldots}(g_{i}\rightleftharpoons g_{j},\omega_{i}\rightleftharpoons\omega_{j})

(S36)

Apart from the observation on the series, one can also look into the recurrence. Notice that
B_{\bm{n}}
is given by Eq. (
S33
), and exchanging
g
and
\omega
will affect the value of
B
. Taking the exchange of the first and the second modes as an example, Eq. (
S33
) changes to

\begin{split}&g_{2}n_{1}B_{n_{1},n_{2}-1,\ldots}+g_{1}n_{2}B_{n_{1}-1,n_{2},\ldots}+\cdots\\
=&f_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}-1,\ldots}B_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}-1,\ldots}-g_{2}B_{n_{1}-2,n_{2}-1,\ldots}-g_{1}B_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}-2,\ldots}-\cdots.\end{split}

(S37)

Also, by renaming the subscripts, one has

\begin{split}&g_{1}n_{1}B_{n_{2}-1,n_{1},\ldots}+g_{2}n_{2}B_{n_{2},n_{1}-1,\ldots}+\cdots\\
=&f_{n_{2}-1,n_{1}-1,\ldots}B_{n_{2}-1,n_{1}-1,\ldots}-g_{1}B_{n_{2}-1,n_{1}-2,\ldots}-g_{2}B_{n_{2}-2,n_{1}-1,\ldots}-\cdots.\end{split}

(S38)

From Eq. (
S30
), it can be noticed that
f_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}-1}=f_{n_{2}-1,n_{1}-1}(\omega_{1}\rightleftharpoons\omega_{2})
. Then, compared with the original Eq. (
S33
), the solutions to Eq. (
S38
) have a correspondence relation with those of the Eq. (
S33
),

\begin{split}&B_{n_{2}-1,n_{1},\ldots}\rightarrow B_{n_{1},n_{2}-1,\ldots}\\
&B_{n_{2},n_{1}-1,\ldots}\rightarrow B_{n_{1}-1,n_{2},\ldots}\\
&B_{n_{2},n_{1},\ldots}\rightarrow B_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots}\\
&\cdots.\end{split}

(S39)

Therefore, the solutions to Eq. (
S33
) naturally satisfy the property of Eq. (
S36
). However, as previously obtained, if one of the subscripts in
B_{\bm{n}}
is zero, a boundary-type equation of it, i.e.
0\cdot B_{\bm{n}}=0
, can be found by properly setting the subscript of Eq. (
S33
). This indicates that
B_{\bm{n}}
with at least one zero subscript can be set arbitrarily. Combining the results, one can conclude that the solutions of
B_{\bm{n}}
will satisfy the symmetry no matter what kind of boundary conditions is chosen, but the boundary conditions themselves do not follow the symmetry.

Now, we go back to equation
\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{0}}
. Notice that each
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}
has a boundary-type equation. Therefore,
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}
can be solved by the set of equations composed of the particular boundaries and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{0}}
. For example, one can set
B_{1,0,\ldots}=B_{0,1,\ldots}=\ldots=B_{\ldots,1,0}=1
and solve
B_{\ldots,0,1}
by
\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}B_{\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{0}}
. However, remember that all
B
s have to satisfy the exchange rules due to the symmetry requirements. So, if one would like to obtain the
G
-function which happens to be the one under certain exchange of
g
and
\omega
, the corresponding boundaries must also be exchanged. Otherwise, the symmetry will not remain in the spectrum. A convenient way to avoid the change of boundaries is to introduce tighter conditions for them. Back to the example of
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}
, one can set that all of them are equal, and then
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}=f_{\bm{0}}/\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}
.

More generally, for each
p\geq N
, a set of linear equations can be obtained. As discussed previously, each set of equations is obtained by substituting
\bm{n}
under a certain
p
. Therefore, as a recurrence, the variables are those with larger subscripts (left-hand side of Eq. (
S33
)) for a certain
p
. The total number of them equals the number of
N
-tuples
\bm{n}
under that the total bosonic mode number is
p-(N-1)
. This is due to the form of the subscripts on the left-hand side of Eq. (
S33
),
\bm{n-1}+\bm{e}_{j}
. In particular, the number of variables is

\binom{p}{N-1}.

(S40)

The number of boundary-type equations (
0\cdot B=0
) equals the number of
N
-tuples with at least one zero. Therefore, the number of effective equations equals the number of
N
-tuples without zeros. In such a case, the
N
-tuples can be expressed by
\bm{n}^{\prime}+\bm{1}
, where
\bm{1}
is the all-one
N
-tuple and
\bm{n}^{\prime}
is the
N
-tuples given total bosonic numbers
p-N
. Therefore, the number of effective equations is given by

\binom{p-1}{N-1}.

(S41)

Using the property of binomial coefficients, it can be found that

\binom{p-1}{N-1}<\binom{p}{N-1}.

(S42)

This means that the number of equations obtained under a particular
p
is less than the number of variables, making the equation set underdetermined. However, because of the boundaries, the number of variables can be largely reduced. In fact, some
N
-tuples under total boson number
p
give the same boundary-type equations. Therefore, the number of variables that has at least one zero-subscript (the number of
N
-tuples
\bm{n}
under
p-(N-1)
with at least one zero component) will contribute to the true number of variables. Extending the symmetric boundary conditions of
B_{\bm{e}_{j}}
, for given
p
, one can set the following rules at most

\begin{split}&B_{p-N+1,0,\ldots}=B_{0,p-N+1,\ldots}=\cdots=B_{\ldots,p-N+1},\\
&B_{p-N,1,0,\ldots}=B_{1,p-N,0\ldots}=\cdots=B_{\ldots,1,p-N},\\
&\cdots.\end{split}

(S43)

Therefore, the total number of variables can be reduced to the number of
N
-tuples with no zeros plus the number of the above rules, under
p-N+1
. The number of the above rules equals the total number of ways to divide
p-N+1
into the summation of less than
N-1
positive integers. Hence, the number of variables after introducing the exchange rules is

\binom{p-N-2}{N-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{p-N}{k-1}

(S44)

Similarly, an inequality can be found by the binomial coefficients,

\binom{p-1}{N-1}>\binom{p-N-2}{N-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{p-N}{k-1},

(S45)

for
p>N
(remember that the case when
p<N
has been discussed). The inequity shows that, by introducing the exchanging rules, the number of variables can be reduced to be lower than the number of equations. Therefore, for a given
p
, by introducing a proper number of rules based on permutation symmetry, a linear set of equations for
B_{\bm{n-1}+\bm{e}_{j}}
is always solvable, which ultimately leads to the integrability of the whole model.

IV
IV. The example of the two-mode case

We briefly illustrate the formulas for the two-mode case. In such a case, the Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{split}H_{2}=\omega_{1}a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}+g_{1}\sigma_{x}(a_{1}^{\dagger}+a_{1})+\omega_{2}a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}+g_{2}\sigma_{x}(a_{2}^{\dagger}+a_{2})+\Delta\sigma_{z}.\end{split}

(S46)

Using the method in the previous section, one obtains the
G
-function for the two-mode case,

\begin{split}&G_{2}^{\pm}(X)=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}B_{n_{1},n_{2}}\left[1\mp\frac{\Delta}{X-(n_{1}\omega_{1}+n_{2}\omega_{2})}\right]\left(\frac{g_{1}}{\omega_{1}}\right)^{n_{1}}\left(\frac{g_{2}}{\omega_{2}}\right)^{n_{2}},\end{split}
