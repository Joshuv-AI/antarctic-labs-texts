Deterministic Edge Connectivity and Max Flow using Subquadratic Cut Queries
Aditya Anand, Thatchaphol Saranurak, Yunfan Wang

Abstract
We give the first deterministic algorithm that makes sub-quadratic queries to find the global min-cut of a simple graph in the cut query model. Given an n n -vertex graph G G , our algorithm makes O ~ ​ ( n 5 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{5/3}) queries to compute the global min-cut in G G . As a key ingredient, we also show an algorithm for finding s s - t t max-flows of size O ~ ​ ( n ) \widetilde{O}(n) in O ~ ​ ( n 5 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{5/3}) queries. We also show efficient cut-query implementations of versions of expander decomposition and isolating cuts, which may be of independent interest.

Technical Overview

Maximum
s
-
t
flow:

In this overview, we assume
W=1
. That is, the input graph
G
has unit capacities, and the value of
s
-
t
maximum flow is at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
. Our algorithm is based on the classical Dinitz’s algorithm
[
Din06
]
, which we recall below.

The algorithm maintains a flow
f
and proceeds in iterations. In each iteration, we first construct the (directed) residual graph
G_{f}
for the flow
f
. Next, we obtain the layered graph
G_{L}
from
G_{f}
. The vertex set of
G_{L}
is
V
. Define layers
L_{0},L_{1}\ldots
, where
L_{i}
is the set of vertices at distance
i
from
s
in
G_{f}
. The edge set of the layered graph is the set of edges
\{u,v\}
in
E(G_{f})
, such that
u\in L_{i}
and
v\in L_{i+1}
for some
i
. A
blocking flow

f^{\prime}
is then a flow from
s
to
t
in
G_{L}
, such that on every flow path, some edge is saturated (that is, the flow through the edge equals its capacity). Let
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)
denote the length of the shortest (directed) path from
s
to
t
in
G_{L}
. Finally, we find a blocking flow
f^{\prime}
and augment the flow
f
with
f^{\prime}
, and proceed to the next iteration.
The crucial property of the algorithm is that, after each iteration,
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)
must strictly increase. Once we reach an iteration where a blocking flow of non-zero value does not exist, the obtained flow
f
is maximum.
We will use the standard fact that once
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)\geq n^{2/3}
, the total additional units of flow that can be sent in subsequent iterations is at most
O(n^{2/3})
.

To make only sub-quadratic cut queries, our key contribution is to show how to compute a blocking flow
f^{\prime}
using only
\widetilde{O}(n+\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime})\mathrm{dist}(s,t))
queries where
\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime})
denotes the value of
f^{\prime}
. Our algorithm then keeps augmenting the flow via our blocking flow subroutine until the resulting flow is maximum.

To analyze the query complexity, we divide the algorithm into two phases. The first phase contains all iterations when
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)\leq n^{2/3}
, and the second phase contains all subsequent iterations.
In the first phase, we make
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot n^{2/3}+n\cdot n^{2/3})=\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
queries because (1) there are at most
n^{2/3}
iterations since, after that, we must have
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)>n^{2/3}
, (2) the total value of flow augmented during these iterations at most the maximum flow value, which we assumed to be at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
, and (3)
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)\leq n^{2/3}
.
In the second phase, we again make
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot n^{2/3}+n^{2/3}\cdot n)=\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
queries because (1) there are at most
n^{2/3}
iterations since, after that,
f
must already be maximum, (2) the total value of flow augmented during these iterations is at most
O(n^{2/3})
, and (3)
\mathrm{dist}(s,t)\leq n
.

Global minimum cut:

The starting point of our global min-cut algorithm is the algorithm of
[
LP20
]
. We give a high-level description of this algorithm below.

Given a set of terminals
T
, a Steiner min-cut is a minimum cut that separates some pair of terminals, i.e., a minimum size cut
(C,V\setminus C)
that satisfies
C\cap T
,
T\setminus C\neq\emptyset
. Note that a Steiner min-cut when
T=V(G)
is a global min-cut.
Suppose
(C,V\setminus C)
is the minimum Steiner Cut in the graph
G
. The algorithm of
[
LP20
]
is divided into two cases: one where
\min\{|T\cap C|,|T\setminus C|\}\leq\mathrm{polylog}(n)
, and the other where
\min\{|T\cap C|,|T\setminus C|\}\geq\mathrm{polylog}(n)
.

In the first case, they find the Steiner min-cut using the
isolating cuts
technique and finish.
In the second case, they compute an expander decomposition and replace
T
by a smaller set
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
with
|T^{\prime}|\leq\frac{|T|}{2}
, such that some minimum Steiner cut
C
for
T
still separates
T^{\prime}
. Then they recursively run the same procedure with
T^{\prime}
. Since they do not know which case actually happens, they run the isolating cut subroutine at every recursion, while continuing to sparsify
T
.
There can be only
O(\log|T|)
recursions since the size of
T
decreases by half after each recursion.
Observe that if the initial terminal set
T=V(G)
, the algorithm must find a global mincut at some point.

Several challenges arise when implementing this algorithm in the cut-query model. First of all, one needs to compute isolating cuts and expander decomposition in this model. But, superficially, we can be hopeful because the common primitive for these techniques is a
s
-
t
max-flow algorithm, and we have a new efficient algorithm when the max-flow value is at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
.

Unfortunately, the flow instances when computing isolating cuts and expander decomposition may have max-flow value
\Omega(m)
, where
m
is the number of edges, even when the input graph is simple. For example, the flow instances for computing isolating cuts have the following structure: The super source
s
connects to many terminals
v
, and the capacity of edge
(s,v)
is the degree of the vertex
v
. This is similar for the super sink
t
. When
T=V(G)
, for example, the max-flow value of this flow instance can be as large as
\Omega(m)
.

To overcome the above challenge, our solution is that we choose the initial terminal set
T
to be, instead of the whole vertex set
V(G)
, a dominating set
D
of size
\widetilde{O}(n/\delta)
, where
\delta
is the minimum degree.
The correctness of this is based on our new and simple structural lemma (
Lemma

5.3
): every cut
(C,V\setminus C)
of size at most
\delta-1
must separate
D
, i.e., we must have
D\cap C,D\setminus C\neq\emptyset
.
Therefore, assuming that global min-cut has size at most
\delta-1
(otherwise, it has size
\delta
, which is trivial to compute), we conclude that the minimum Steiner Cut with respect to
D
is the global min-cut. To initialize our terminal set as
D
, we show how to compute
D
using
\widetilde{O}(n)
queries.

Why does starting with
D
help? This is because we will set up all flow instances such that only vertices in
D
are connected to super source
s
or super sink
t
and the capacity of each edge incident to
s
and
t
is at most
\delta+1
.
2
2

2

Strictly speaking, our max-flow algorithm takes
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
queries only if edges have unit capacity.
But, we can simulate an edge from super source
s
to vertex
a
of capacity
\tau
by replacing it with
\tau
paths:
s\to v_{1}\to a,\dots,s\to v_{\tau}\to a
. We do similarly for the super sink
t
. The total number of newly added vertices is
\widetilde{O}(n/\delta)\cdot(\delta+1)=\widetilde{O}(n)
.

So the max-flow value is only
\widetilde{O}(n/\delta)\cdot(\delta+1)=\widetilde{O}(n)
, but we prove that this new flow instance can still be used for computing isolating cuts and expander decomposition (in
Sections

5.3
and
A
respectively).
Therefore, we can employ our max-flow algorithm with
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
queries to compute both isolating cuts and a version of expander decomposition in only
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
queries.

In this section, we will formally define terms and notations which we shall adopt throughout the paper.

Graphs:

Given an undirected simple graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
, we define
\delta_{G}
to be the
minimum degree
of
G
and
\lambda_{G}
to be the
size of the minimum cut
in
G
. For a vertex set
S\subseteq V
, we let
G[S]
denote the subgraph induced on the set of vertices
S
. For a set of vertices
S
, we denote by
\partial_{G}(S)
the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in
S
. For a vertex
u
, we denote by
N_{G}(u)
the set of vertices
v
with
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
. For a set of vertices
U
, we denote by
N_{G}(U)
the set
\bigcup_{u\in U}N_{G}(u)\setminus U
, the set of neighbors of vertices in
U
outside
U
. We denote a cut in
G
using the notation
(S,V(G)\setminus S)
for
S\subseteq V(G)
. For simplicity, we shall refer to
V(G)\setminus S
as
\overline{S}
.
We say that a vertex set
S\subseteq V(G)
is a
dominating set
of
G
if, for any vertex
v\in V(G)
, either
v\in S
or
v
has at least one neighbor in
S
.

Given a directed graph
H
, we use
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(s,t)

to represent the distance (length of the shortest path) between two vertices
s,t\in V(H)
. We drop the subscripts when the graph is clear from the context.

Flows and residual graphs:

For a flow
f
from
s
to
t
, we use
\mathrm{val}(f)
to denote the
size of the flow

f
. We describe the flow by flow values
f(u,v),f(v,u)
for every edge
\{u,v\}\in E
with
f(u,v)=-f(v,u)
.
Given a graph
G
with non-negative edge capacities
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a flow
f
, we define the residual graph as the directed capacitated graph
G_{f}
, whose vertex set is
V(G_{f})=V
. For an edge
\{u,v\}\in E
with flow
0\leq f(u,v)\leq c(u,v)
, the
residual capacity
is defined as
c_{f}(u,v)=c(u,v)-f(u,v)\geq 0
. We also define
c_{f}(v,u)=c(v,u)+f(u,v)
.
Then we add two edges in
G_{f}
: if
c_{f}(u,v)>0
, we add an edge from
u
to
v
with capacity
c_{f}(u,v)
and if
c_{f}(v,u)>0
, we add an edge from
v
to
u
with capacity
c_{f}(v,u)
.

Types of queries:

Next, we formally define cut queries and bipartite independent set queries.

3.1
Cut Query.

For an undirected capacitated graph
G
with capacities
c:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R^{+}}\cup\{0\}
, given a set
S\subseteq V(G)
, the cut query oracle for graph
G
returns the total capacity of cut-edges
\textsc{Cut}(S):=c(S,\overline{S})=\sum_{a\in S,b\in\overline{S}}c(a,b)
.

3.2
Bipartite Independent Set (BIS) Query.

Given a (possibly directed) capacitated graph
H
with capacities
c:E(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R^{+}}\cup\{0\}
, sets
A,B\subseteq V(H)
as input such that
A\cap B=\emptyset
, the bipartite independent set query oracle returns the boolean value which indicates if
c(A,B)=\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}c(a,b)>0
.

BIS queries were introduced by
[
BHPNR+18
]
and have been extensively studied in the context of problems on undirected graphs.
In an undirected graph
H
, a BIS query between
A
and
B
can be simulated with
3
cut queries since

\displaystyle c(A,B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textsc{Cut}(A)+\textsc{Cut}(B)-\textsc{Cut}(A\cup B)\right)

We will require BIS queries on the residual graph for our maximum flow algorithm. The following lemma demonstrates how to simulate BIS queries on the residual graph using cut queries.

3.3
.

Given an undirected capacitated graph
G
and an explicit flow
f
, we can simulate a BIS query on the residual graph
G_{f}
by using
3
cut queries in
G
.

Proof.

Given
A,B\subseteq V
, the BIS query on
G_{f}
will return whether there exists
a\in A
,
b\in B
such that
c_{f}(a,b)>0
. This is equivalent to

\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}c_{f}(a,b)>0

Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}c_{f}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}c(a,b)-f(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}c(a,b)-\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}f(a,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[\textsc{Cut}(A)+\textsc{Cut}(B)-\textsc{Cut}(A\cup B)]-\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}f(a,b)

We can use
3
cut queries to get
\textsc{Cut}(A)
,
\textsc{Cut}(B)
and
\textsc{Cut}(A\cup B)
. Since
f
is explicit, we can obtain
\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}f(a,b)
in zero queries. Therefore, a BIS query in
G_{f}
can be simulated by
3
cut queries in
G
.
∎

The next lemma gives some simple facts about maximum flows which will be useful for the subsequent analysis.

We have the following:

Next, we need the following property of Dinitz’s algorithm (

After augmenting the flow

4.8
.

Given a capacitated graph

where each edge weight is an integer in the range

and two vertices

,
Algorithm

1
finds an

-

Proof.

For the purpose of analysis, consider the

-
t

is the smallest subscript such that

. By

, since the distance between

and

in the residual graph after

. This bounds the number of iterations required.

Since the graph contains
n

, by the pigeonhole principle, there must exist a pair of consecutive layers

and

. Therefore, the total edge weight between these layers is bounded by
O((\frac{n}{d})^{2}W)=O(n^{2/3}W)
.
By the definition of the layered graph, the set of edges

forms a cut between

. Thus, by

Consequently, the remaining flow size in the residual graph

is at most

. Since each iteration of blocking flow increases the flow value by at least

4.3
Implementing Dinitz’s Algorithm via BIS Queries

Now we implement Dinitz’s blocking flow algorithm
[
Din06
]
in the cut query model.

4.9
.

Given a flow
f
such that
\mathrm{dist}_{G_{f}}(s,t)=d^{\prime}
, we can compute a blocking flow
f^{\prime}
, augment the flow
f
with
f^{\prime}
, and recompute the residual graph using
\widetilde{O}(n+d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))
BIS queries on
G_{f}
.

Proof.

We first construct the layered graph
G_{L}
of
G_{f}
by running BFS in
\widetilde{O}(n)
queries using Corollary
4.3
. This gives us
L_{0}=\{s\},...,L_{d^{\prime}}=\{t\}
. The goal is to find an
s
-
t
path in
G_{L}
efficiently using a stack-based method.

Next, we maintain a stack to track our search for an
s
-
t
path. Initially, the stack contains
\{s\}
. At each step, let the top element of the stack be
u
, where
u\in L_{i}
. We then attempt to find
v\in L_{i+1}
such that
c_{f}(u,v)>0
. If such
v
exists, we can find it using
\widetilde{O}(1)
queries by Lemma
4.1
, and we push
v
onto the stack.
If no such
v
exists, we pop
u
from the stack and remove
u
from the layered graph, as
u
cannot send any flow to the next layer.

When the stack reaches a length of
d^{\prime}+1
, this indicates that we have found an
s
-
t
path. We add this flow path to
f^{\prime}
, adjust the residual capacities along the path, and then reset the stack to
\{s\}
to search for another path.
The process terminates when the stack is empty, meaning there is no further flow from
s
to any vertex in the next layer.

Now we analyze the query complexity. In each step, we either push a new vertex onto the stack or pop one off and remove it from the graph. Each push or pop requires
\widetilde{O}(1)
queries. Whenever the stack reaches length
d^{\prime}+1
, we send flow from
s
to
t
, and the stack is reset, reducing its length by
d^{\prime}
.
The total length of the stack can increase by at most
n+O(d^{\prime})\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime})
, as this is the maximum number of vertices and flow pushes in the layered graph. Therefore, the total number of queries is at most
\widetilde{O}(n+d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result,
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

By
4.9
, a single blocking flow iteration requires
\widetilde{O}(n+d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))
queries, where
\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime})
represents the current flow value, and
d^{\prime}
is the distance from
s
to
t
in the layered graph. Therefore, to analyze the algorithm’s query complexity, we should consider three factors: the total number of iterations, the flow value, and the distance. Balancing these factors is essential for optimizing the overall query complexity.

By
Lemma

4.8
, we know that the number of iterations is at most
O(n^{2/3}W)
. When the distance is less than
n^{2}/3
, the flow value is at most
O(nW)
. Conversely, when the distance exceeds
n^{2/3}
, the remaining flow value is at most
O(n^{2/3}W)
.

Thus, the total query complexity can be expressed as:

\sum\widetilde{O}(n+d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))\leq O(n^{\frac{2}{3}}W)\cdot\widetilde{O}(n)+\sum\widetilde{O}(d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))

Breaking it down further:

=\widetilde{O}(n^{\frac{5}{3}}W)+\sum_{d^{\prime}<n^{\frac{2}{3}}}\widetilde{O}(d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))+\sum_{d^{\prime}\geq n^{\frac{2}{3}}}\widetilde{O}(d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))

For the two cases:

1.

For
d^{\prime}<n^{\frac{2}{3}}
, we have

\sum_{d^{\prime}<n^{\frac{2}{3}}}\widetilde{O}(d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))\leq n^{\frac{2}{3}}\cdot O(nW)=\widetilde{O}(n^{\frac{5}{3}}W)

2.

For
d^{\prime}\geq n^{\frac{2}{3}}
, we have

\sum_{d^{\prime}\geq n^{\frac{2}{3}}}\widetilde{O}(d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{val}(f^{\prime}))\leq n\cdot O(n^{\frac{2}{3}}W)=\widetilde{O}(n^{\frac{5}{3}}W)

It now follows that the total query complexity is at most
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3}W)
, which proves the result.
∎

5
Global Min-cut Algorithm

In this section, we present our algorithm for finding global min-cut, proving
Theorem

1.1
. We will focus on obtaining the following threshold version of the result.

5.1
.

Given a graph
G
and a parameter
\tau\leq\delta-1
, there is an algorithm that either

•

returns a cut
(C,V\setminus C)
with size
|\partial C|
at most
\tau
, or

•

certifies that the global min-cut in

must have a size larger than
\tau

in
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
cut queries.

Note that
Theorem

1.1
now follows immediately by binary search. If the global min-cut has size

We fix the parameter

The rest of this section is dedicated to proving

We first give a crucial yet simple observation that any dominating set must be separated by every cut of size at most
\delta-1
in

5.1
, and show how to efficiently compute the dominating set in

.
Then, we show how to efficiently compute minimum isolating cuts of size at most

Given these three subsections, we are ready to implement the global minimum cut algorithm of
[

]
in the cut query model. In high level, the algorithm starts by computing a dominating set

and has two cases. If the minimum cut is unbalanced with respect to

). Otherwise, if the minimum cut is balanced with respect to

5.1
Dominating Sets are Separated by Small Cuts

Below, we show a simple and crucial observation: any dominating set must be separated by a cut of size at most
\delta-1
.

5.2
.

Given a graph
G
, a vertex
R\subseteq V
is
c
-separated if for every cut
(C,V\setminus C)
of size
|\partial C|\leq c
, we have
C\cap R,R\setminus C\neq\emptyset
. That is,
R
hits both sides of the cut
C
.

5.3
.

For any dominating set
R\subseteq V
,
R
is
\delta-1
-separated, where
\delta
is the minimum degree.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that this is not the case. Then there is a cut
(C_{1},C_{2}=V\setminus C_{1})
of size at most
\delta-1
where
R\subseteq C_{1}
. Now we have
\delta-1\geq|E(C_{1},C_{2})|\geq|E(R,C_{2})|\geq|C_{2}|
where the last inequality is because
R
is dominating. But every vertex in
C_{2}
has degree at least
\delta
, therefore we must have
|E(C_{1},C_{2})|\geq|C_{2}|(\delta-(|C_{2}|-1))\geq\delta
for
1\leq|C_{2}|\leq\delta-1
, which leads to a contradiction.
∎

We remark that the above lemma is the crucial place where we exploit that the graph is simple.

5.2
Computing a Dominating Set

In this section, we show how to compute a dominating set efficiently in the cut-query model.

5.4
.

There is a deterministic algorithm that finds a dominating set
R\subseteq V
of size
|R|\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\delta})

using
\widetilde{O}(n)
cut-queries.

Proof.

We will first state our algorithm, then prove its correctness, and finally analyze its complexity under the cut query model.

Algorithm

We construct
R
iteratively. Initially, we set
G^{\prime}=G
,
R=\emptyset
. We first apply the following reduction rule – as long as there is a vertex in
v\in G^{\prime}
whose degree in
G^{\prime}
is larger than
\frac{\delta}{2}
, we add it into
R
and delete
v\cup N_{G^{\prime}}(v)
from
G^{\prime}
.

After exhaustively applying the above reduction rule, if
V(G^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
and there is no vertex in
G^{\prime}
with a degree larger than
\frac{\delta}{2}
, then we do the following. Consider the bipartite graph
H
whose vertex set is the bipartition
W_{1}\cup W_{2}
, where
W_{1}=N_{G}(R)
and
W_{2}=V(G^{\prime})
. The edge set of
H
is the edge set of
G
restricted to the edges between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
.

First, observe that
\forall u\in W_{2}
,
|E(W_{1},u)|=\deg(u)-|E(u,W_{2}\setminus\{u\})|\geq\frac{\delta}{2}
, otherwise, this vertex would have been considered in the previous step. Thus, we have
|E(W_{1},W_{2})|=\sum_{u\in W_{2}}|E(W_{1},u)|\geq\sum_{u\in W_{2}}\frac{\delta}{2}\geq|W_{2}|\frac{\delta}{2}
. Then there exists a
v\in W_{1}
such that
|E(v,W_{2})|\geq\frac{|E(W_{1},W_{2}))|}{|W_{1}|}\geq\frac{|W_{2}|\delta}{2|W_{1}|}\geq\frac{|W_{2}|\delta}{2n}
.
We add
v
into
R
and delete all its neighbors in
G^{\prime}
.
We repeat until
V(G^{\prime})=\emptyset
, hence
R
must be a dominating set.

Correctness

It remains to bound the size of
R
. When there is a vertex in
G^{\prime}
with degree larger than
\frac{\delta}{2}
, we reduce
|V(G^{\prime})|
by at least
\frac{\delta}{2}
each time. So we can add at most
\frac{2n}{\delta}
such vertices into
R
.

When no vertex in
G
has degree larger than
\frac{\delta}{2}
, we remove at least
|V(G^{\prime})|\frac{\delta}{2n}
vertices from
G^{\prime}
. Thus the size of
G^{\prime}
reduces by at least a
1-\frac{\delta}{2n}
fraction. Thus after
{O}(\frac{n}{\delta})
iterations, the size of
G^{\prime}
reduces by a constant factor, and hence this step can happen at most
{O}(\frac{n}{\delta}\log n)=\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\delta})
many times. In total, we have
|R|\leq\frac{2n}{\delta}+O(\frac{n}{\delta}\log(\frac{n}{\delta}))=\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\delta})
.

Complexity

First, we want to find a vertex
v
whose degree in
G^{\prime}
is at least
\frac{\delta}{2}
. Given a vertex
w
, we can find the degree of
w
in
G^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
queries. We enumerate each vertex
w\in V(G^{\prime})
, and find its degree
\deg(w)
. If
\deg(w)<\frac{\delta}{2}
, then we mark
w
as irrelevant and continue. If
\deg(w)>\frac{\delta}{2}
, then we find all its neighbors in
G^{\prime}
using
\widetilde{O}(\deg(w))
queries using
Lemma

4.1
. Note that we delete all these neighbors of
w
from
G^{\prime}
. Let us charge the query cost uniformly to each vertex in
\deg(w)
, and note that each vertex receives a charge of
\widetilde{O}(1)
. Observe that the degree of a vertex in
G^{\prime}
is non-increasing, thus irrelevant vertices remain irrelevant.

Now it remains to analyze the case when no vertex of
G^{\prime}
has degree
>\frac{\delta}{2}
in
G^{\prime}
. Recall that we want to find a vertex
v\in W_{1}
such that
|E(v,W_{2})|\geq\frac{|E(W_{1},W_{2}))|}{|W_{1}|}
.
We will use binary search. Divide
W_{1}
into two (roughly) equal sized groups
W_{11}
and
W_{12}
. By
Lemma

4.1
, using
\widetilde{O}(1)
queries, we can find both
E(W_{11},W_{2})
and
E(W_{12},W_{2})
. By simple averaging, either
{E(W_{11},W_{2})}\geq\frac{|E(W_{1},W_{2}))|}{|W_{1}|}{|W_{11}|}
or
{E(W_{12},W_{2})}\geq\frac{|E(W_{1},W_{2}))|}{|W_{1}|}{|W_{12}|}
. Without loss of generality, assume the former. We now recurse on
W_{11}
, and repeat the same process, till we reach a singleton vertex

, which must satisfy
|E(v,W_{2})|\geq\frac{|E(W_{1},W_{2}))|}{|W_{1}|}
.

The total number of cut queries is at most
\widetilde{O}(1)
, since in each iteration we use
\widetilde{O}(1)
queries and there are at most
\widetilde{O}(1)
iterations of the binary search.
Therefore, the total query complexity of finding the dominating set is dominated by the query complexity of the reduction rule, and hence is at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
.
∎

5.3
Computing Minimum Isolating Cuts

This section shows an efficient algorithm for computing a minimum isolating cut. Let us first recall its definition below.

5.5
Minimum Isolating Cut.

For any set of vertices
R\subseteq V
and
r\in R
, the minimum isolating cut of
r
is an
\{r\}
-
(R\setminus\{r\})
min-cut. The minimum isolating cut (of
R
) is the minimum sized cut among the minimum isolating cuts over all

.

5.6
.

Given an unweighted graph
G
and a set of vertices
R\subseteq V
such that
|R|
is at most
\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\tau})
, there is an algorithm that either

•

outputs a minimum isolating cut of
R
of size at most
\tau
, or

•

certifies that the minimum isolating cut of
R
has a size larger than
\tau

in
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
cut queries.

We remark that the algorithm can be modified easily to output an isolating cut for each
r\in R
whose min-isolating cut has size at most
\tau
.

Proof.

First, we will state the algorithm, which follows the approach of
[
LP20
]
, adapted to our setting. Then we will discuss its correctness, and finally argue about implementation and the query cost. We will use the concept of

. For two sets

and

.

Algorithm:

Encode each vertex in

using a unique

dimensional bit vector. Next, compute

or

\mathcal{C}
be the set of these bi-partitions. Note that each pair of distinct vertices

For each of these

(A,B)\in\mathcal{C}
of

to the vertex set of

. For each vertex

(s_{\text{source}},a)
with capacity
\tau+1
. Similarly, for each
b\in B
, add an edge
(b,s_{\text{sink}})
with capacity
\tau+1

has a capacity of

. Find an

-

Let

be this max-flow and
(C_{A},C_{B}=V(G)\setminus C_{A})
be the restriction of the minimum cut corresponding to this maximum flow to

(we simply remove
s
and
t
from the cut to obtain
C_{A},C_{B}
). Consider the graph
G^{\prime}
after deleting every such cut, that is, we delete the edges
\partial{C_{A}}
for every bi-partition
(A,B)\in\mathcal{C}
. For each

, define

to be the set of vertices reachable from

. Further, let

be the set of

which satisfy the following property – for every bi-partition

, if

, and if

. In other words,

whose edges to

Let
T_{r}^{\prime}=\{r\}\cup(T_{r}\setminus R)

from

Then, we compute the min-cut between

and

in

\lambda_{r}
be the size of this cut (and let

with

has a size larger than

. This concludes the description of the algorithm.

Correctness:

We now show the correctness of our algorithm. Note that our algorithm is similar to
[

]
, but we need to argue its correctness a bit more carefully since whenever we compute an

B
min-cut for a bi-partition, we set the capacities of the edges incident to the source and sink vertices as

(instead of

as in their setting). On a high level, this still works since we are only interested in the isolating cuts of size at most
\tau
; if there is no isolating cut of size
\leq\tau
, we will simply output that this is the case. The next few lemmas formally show that our algorithm is indeed correct.

5.7
.

For any bipartition
(A,B)\in\mathcal{C}
and any

, we say that

is saturated if in the maximum flow

(s_{\text{source}},a)

Proof.

If

, then the edge

is cut by the

∎

5.8
.

For each bi-partition
(A,B)
,
(C_{A},C_{B})
is a minimum cut in

that separates

C_{B}\cap B

Proof.

Since

is a restriction of the

5.12
Unbalanced/Balanced Cut.

For any set of vertices

We need the following tool called

For every positive integer

.

Let

We say that

5.15
.

Suppose that

is
\tau
-separated and

Towards proving this key lemma, we begin by introducing the notion of expanders and expander decomposition that we need.

5.16

\Phi(S)=\frac{|\partial S|}{\min\{|R\cap S|,|R\setminus S|\}}\geq\phi(\tau+1)

5.17
.

Our next and crucial step in this section is to obtain a decomposition into

-almost expanders.
We defer this proof to

since this proof is mostly standard.

At a high level, the algorithm combines an implementation of the cut-matching game
[

]
together with the expander pruning technique
[
SW19
]
. The cut player trivially needs zero queries since the auxiliary graph will be explicitly maintained. The matching player applies our algorithm for
s
-
t

This takes

of size

and each terminal sends at most

and, hence, our flow algorithm from

1.2
uses

queries. The formal statement is summarized below:

5.18
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a terminal set
R\subseteq V
with
|R|\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\tau})
and a parameter
\phi=\mathrm{poly}(\frac{1}{\log n})
, one can find a partition of the vertex set
V
into subsets
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k}
, where

1.

Each

.

2.

The number of crossing edges

.

After obtaining this decomposition, we classify each part as follows.

5.19
.

For each set

,

, we say that

is

1.

empty, if
R_{i}^{\prime}=\emptyset

2.

Now, we construct

as follows:

1.

Include every vertex of

in

, for each

.

2.

For each

,

•

•

to

;

•

else if it is large, add

arbitrary vertices of

to

.

5.20
.

Suppose that
R
is
\tau
-separated and

for each

Proof.

Since
|\partial V_{i}|\geq\tau+1
and
\sum_{i\neq j}E(V_{i},V_{j})=O(\phi|R|(\tau+1)\log^{6}n)
, the number of subsets is bounded by

. Therefore, the total number of vertices in

across all small

is at most
O(\phi|R|\log^{6}n)
.

For each large

to add to
\widetilde{R}
. Additionally, for each

|R_{i}\setminus R_{i}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{|R_{i}|}{\log n}
. Combining these results, we have

|\widetilde{R}|\leq\frac{|R|}{\log n}+O(\phi|R|\log^{6}n)

as desired. Now we show that
\widetilde{R}
must be
\tau
-separated.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
\widetilde{R}
is not
\tau
-separated. This implies the existence of a cut

with cut-size

. We will demonstrate that this cut

Before we proceed further, we will show that, in this case,

must contain the “smaller” side of every large

. The next lemma will clarify this point.

5.21
.

,

, we must have

.

, which is a contradiction.

In total, we have

.

.

6
Conclusion

We show the first subquadratic deterministic algorithms for the
s
-
t
minimum cut and global minimum cut problems in simple graphs, both using
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3})
cut queries.
Nevertheless, there remains a considerable gap between our results and the current lower bound of
\Omega(n)

[
HMT88
]
.
Improving the lower bound to
\omega(n)
is very interesting as it would separate deterministic and randomized cut query complexity for the global min-cut problem.

For the upper bound side, algorithms using
\widetilde{O}(n)
queries would be exciting. This algorithm must be very different from ours because we explicitly compute a maximum
s
-
t
flow and the flow of value
\nu
may have representation size as large as
\Omega(n\sqrt{\nu})=\Omega(n^{1.5})
even on simple unweighted graphs.
In fact, it is known that, given any
simple
unweighted graph
G
where the maximum
s
-
t
flow value is
\nu
, there exists a subgraph
H\subseteq G
with
O(n\sqrt{\nu})
edges such that the maximum
s
-
t
flow in
H
has the same value as the one in
G
and this bound is tight (see, e.g.,
[
KL98
,
RSW18
]
). Thus, it is interesting whether there is a (near-optimal) algorithm for explicitly computing maximum
s
-
t
flow in a simple unweighted graph using
\widetilde{O}(n\sqrt{\nu})
cut queries. Through our framework, this would immediately imply a global min-cut algorithm using
\widetilde{O}(n^{1.5})
cut queries, reaching the barrier of this approach.

It is interesting to generalize our result to weighted graphs or just non-simple unweighted graphs. Our technique does not work because
Lemma

5.3
is specific to simple unweighted graphs.

5.18

Definitions.

Given a graph
H=(V(H),E(H))
, define the
sparsity
of a cut
(S,V(H)\setminus S)
as
\frac{|E(H,V(H)\setminus S)|}{\min\{|S|,|V(H)\setminus S|\}}
. Further, given a terminal set
R
, we define the
sparsity with respect to
R
of a cut
(S,V(H)\setminus S)
as
\frac{|E(H,V(H)\setminus S)|}{\min\{|S\cap R|,|(V(H)\setminus S)\cap R|\}}
. We say that the set
R
is
\phi
-expanding in
H
if there is no cut
(S,V(H)\setminus S)
in
H
which has sparsity at most
\phi
with respect to
R
. Further,
H
is
\phi
-expanding if
V(H)
is
\phi
-expanding in
H
. We say that a cut
(S,V(H)\setminus S)
is
b
-balanced with respect to
R
if both
S\cap R
and
(V(H)\setminus S)\cap R
have size at least
b|R|
. For a set of vertices
S\subseteq V(H)
, we define the volume of
S
,
\text{vol}_{H}(S)
, as the sum of the degrees of the vertices in
S
in
H
. The conductance of a cut
(S,V\setminus S)
in
H
is defined as
\frac{E(S,V\setminus S)}{\min\{\text{vol}_{H}(S),\text{vol}_{H}(V\setminus S)\}}
. The conductance of
H
is the minimum conductance across all such cuts. We drop the subscripts when the graph
H
is clear from the context.

The following is the key subroutine we need for finding a decomposition into
(\phi,R)
almost-expanders.

A.1
One-step of Expander Decomposition.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, terminal set
R\subseteq V
and parameters
\phi=\mathrm{poly}(\frac{1}{\log n})
and
\tau\geq 1
such that
|R|\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{n}{\tau})
, using
\widetilde{O}(n^{\frac{5}{3}})
queries we can either

•

return a cut
(S,\overline{S})
that is
(\frac{1}{\log^{5}n})
-balanced and
(\phi\tau)
-sparse with respect to
R
.

•

return a set
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
of size
|R^{\prime}|\geq(1-\frac{1}{\log n})|R|
such that
R^{\prime}
is
\frac{\phi\tau}{\log^{5}n}
-expanding in
G
.

Before showing this crucial lemma, let us first show why it implies
Lemma

5.18
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.18
.

We start with the input graph
G
and run the algorithm of
Lemma

A.1
. If
Lemma

A.1
returns a cut
(S,\overline{S})
, then we recursively apply
Lemma

A.1
on
G[S]
and
G[\overline{S}]
. On the other hand, if
Lemma

A.1
returns a set
R^{\prime}
, then
G
is a
(\phi,R)
almost expander with core
R^{\prime}
. The recursion depth for the first case is at most
\log^{5}n\log|R|
, and every leaf of the recursion tree, where the second case occurs, together give us the decomposition into
(\phi,R)
almost expanders.

The argument about the number of crossing edges is a standard charging argument: Every time we obtain a cut
(S,\overline{S})
, that is
\frac{1}{\log^{5}n}
balanced and
\phi(\tau+1)
sparse with respect to
R
, we charge the number of cut edges to the terminals on the side of the cut with smaller number of terminals. Since each cut is
\phi(\tau+1)
sparse, each terminal
r\in R
receives a charge of at most
\phi(\tau+1)
. Since every cut we find is
\frac{1}{\log^{5}n}
balanced, the recursion depth is at most
\log^{5}n\log|R|
, and hence a terminal is charged at most
\log^{5}n\log|R|\leq\log^{6}n
times. It follows that the number of cut edges is at most
\mathcal{O}(\phi|R|(\tau+1)\log^{6}n)
.
∎

We now proceed to prove
Lemma

A.1
. The key ingredient is the cut-matching game of
[
KKOV07
]
. Here, we state the cut-matching game where in each round the matching player returns a perfect
b
-matching
instead of a perfect matching. A perfect
b
-matching is a set of edges such that each vertex
v
has exactly
b
edges in the set containing
v
. A perfect
b
-matching between two sets
A
and
B
is a perfect
b
-matching in which every edge is between a vertex of
A
and a vertex of
B
. We now describe the cut-matching framework.

•

Start with an empty graph
X
with
n
vertices.

•

In round
i
, the cut player chooses a bisection
(A,B)
of
V(X)
.

•

Then, the matching player returns an arbitrary perfect
b
-matching
M_{i}
between
A
and
B
, and the edges in
M_{i}
are added to
E(X)
.

The goal of the cut player is to guarantee that, after a few rounds, the graph
X
(whose edge set is the union
\bigcup_{i}M_{i}
) has sparsity at least
b
, i.e., for every
S\subseteq V(X)
, we have

E_{X}(S,V(X)\setminus S)\geq b\cdot\min\{|S|,|V(X)\setminus S|\}.

A.2
[
KKOV07
]
.

In the cut-matching game where the matching player always returns a perfect
b
-matching in each round, there exists an algorithm for the cut player that, after
r=O(\log n)
rounds, guarantees that
X
has sparsity at least
b
.

We remark that the running time of
Theorem

A.2
is exponential, but since we will know the entire graph
X
along with its edge set
E(X)
whenever we invoke this theorem, we spend zero cut queries to run this algorithm, and hence it does not affect the query complexity of our algorithm. However, if we insist on a polynomial time implementation, we can still guarantee that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}M_{i}
has sparsity at least
b/O(\sqrt{\log n})
using
[
ARV09
]
. For the rest of the section, for simplicity, we work with the exponential time cut player as in
Theorem

A.2
.

Before we prove
Lemma

A.1
, we need an implementation for the matching player. Towards this, let us define the notion of an
almost perfect

-matching

A.3
.

Given sets

\beta,b

, a set of edges

\beta
-almost perfect

B

b
-matching, if there exists a set of
\beta b
edges

The matching player, in each round, either finds a sufficiently balanced and sparse cut with respect to the terminal set

, or returns an embedding of an almost perfect

Before stating this theorem, we define the notion of a flow embedding. Given a set of edges

, we say that
M\preceq^{\text{flow}}\frac{1}{\phi}G
to mean that there is a multicommodity flow in

that can simultaneously send one unit of flow across each edge in

.

Matching Player.

Suppose we are given a graph

, integers

, a terminal set
R\subseteq V

and a parameter
\phi=\mathrm{poly}(\frac{1}{\log n})

(A,B)
of

|A|=|B|=\frac{|R|}{2}

(1)

(2)

obtains a flow embedding of a

(\tau+1)
-matching

Add two vertices

with capacity
\tau+1

\frac{1}{\phi}
.

Then since

, the max-flow from

to

is at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
. We can then compute an

-
s_{sink}
max flow in
\widetilde{O}(n^{\frac{5}{3}})
queries by Theorem
1.2
3

3

As in previous instances, we can add parallel edges and subdivide the edges incident to the source and sink to ensure that all edges have unit capacity
. If the maximum flow is of size
\geq(|A|-\beta)(\tau+1)
, then using the set of flow paths from the flow algorithm, we obtain a
\beta
-almost perfect

-

(\tau+1)

Proof of
Lemma

A.1
.

We run the cut player algorithm in
Theorem

A.2
, and the matching player algorithm in
Theorem

A.4
with
\beta=\frac{|R|}{\log^{5}n}
. If in any round the matching player returns a cut that is
\phi(\tau+1)
sparse with respect to
R
with both sides having at least
\beta
terminals, we simply return this cut as this cut satisfies the requirement of
Lemma

A.1
. Otherwise, in every round, the matching player returns an
\beta
-almost perfect
(\tau+1)
-matching
F
, so that there exists a set
F^{\prime}
with
|F^{\prime}|\leq\beta(\tau+1)
such that
F\cup F^{\prime}
is a perfect
b
-matching and we add the edges in
F\cup F^{\prime}
to
X
to continue the cut-matching game. Mark the edges
F^{\prime}
as
fake edges
.

After
r=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
rounds of the cut-matching game, if the matching player did not return a balanced and sparse cut in any round,
Theorem

A.2
guarantees that
X
has sparsity at least
b=\tau+1
. Note that the total number of fake edges is at most
\beta(\tau+1)r
. Also, since each matching flow embeds in
\frac{1}{\phi}G
, and there are at most
O(\log n)
rounds, we must have
X\preceq^{\text{flow}}\frac{\mathcal{O}(\log n)}{\phi}G
.

Consider any cut
(S,V(X)\setminus S)
with
|S|\leq|V(X)\setminus S|
in
X
. Since the sparsity is at least
(\tau+1)
, it follows that
|E(S,V(X)\setminus S)|\geq(\tau+1)|S|
. Note that in
X
, every vertex has degree
(\tau+1)r=\mathcal{O}((\tau+1)\log n)
.

This means that
\text{vol}(S)\leq\mathcal{O}(|S|(\tau+1)\log n)
, and hence the conductance of every cut
(S,V(X)\setminus S)
is at least
\Omega(\frac{1}{\log n})
.

We will now show that there exists a set
R^{\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
,
|R^{\prime}|\geq|R|(1-\frac{1}{\log n})
, and
R^{\prime}
is
\phi(\tau+1)
expanding in
X
. The construction of
R^{\prime}
is simple. On a high level, our goal is to try to certify the expansion of
X
after removing the fake edges
F^{\prime}
. Therefore, a natural step is to apply expander pruning to the graph
X
after deleting the set of fake edges
F^{\prime}
.

A.5
[
SW19
]
, Theorem 1.3.

Given a graph
H
in which every cut has conductance at least
\phi^{\prime}
, and a set of deletion edges
F^{\prime}
, there exists a prune set
P\subseteq V(H)
with
\text{vol}_{H}(P)\leq\frac{8}{\phi^{\prime}}|F^{\prime}|
, such that in the induced subgraph on
V(H)\setminus P
after removing
F^{\prime}
, in
H[V(H)\setminus P]\setminus F^{\prime}
, every cut
(S,(V(H)\setminus P)\setminus S)
has conductance at least
\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{6}
.

Apply expander pruning to graph
X
, with the set of fake edges
F^{\prime}
as the deletion set, and the conductance parameter
\phi_{X}=\Omega(\frac{1}{\log n})
to obtain a prune set
P
. Here we recall that the graph
X
is explicitly known, so we spend zero queries to obtain this set
P
. Note that
\text{vol}(P)\leq\frac{8|F^{\prime}|}{\phi_{X}}\leq\mathcal{O}(\beta(\tau+1)r\log n)=\mathcal{O}(\beta(\tau+1)\log^{2}n)\leq\frac{|R|(\tau+1)}{\log^{2}n}
since
\beta=\frac{|R|}{\log^{5}n}
. But each vertex has degree
r(\tau+1)
in
H
, hence the size of
P
,
|P|\leq\frac{\text{vol}(P)}{r(\tau+1)}\leq\mathcal{O}(\beta\log n)\leq\frac{|R|}{\log^{2}n}
. We define
R^{\prime}
as all the vertices
v\in R\setminus P
, which have a combined at most
\frac{\tau+1}{10}
number of edges incident to
P
and edges incident in
F^{\prime}
. Note that
|F^{\prime}|+\text{vol}(P)\leq 2\text{vol}(P)\leq\frac{2|R|(\tau+1)}{\log^{2}n}
.

By Markov’s inequality, the number of
v\in R
for which there are more than
\frac{\tau+1}{10}
edges in
F
or whose other endpoint is in
P
, must be at most
20\frac{|R|}{\log^{2}n}\leq\frac{|R|}{2\log n}
. But this means
|R^{\prime}|\geq|R|(1-\frac{1}{2\log n})-|P|\geq|R|(1-\frac{1}{\log n})
since
|P|\leq\frac{|R|}{\log^{2}n}
.

Since each vertex
v\in R
has degree
r(\tau+1)
, it follows that each vertex in
R^{\prime}
has degree at least
\frac{r}{10}(\tau+1)
in
X[V(X)\setminus P]\setminus F^{\prime}
. The next lemma proves the desired expansion property of

.

.

R^{\prime}

Consider a cut
