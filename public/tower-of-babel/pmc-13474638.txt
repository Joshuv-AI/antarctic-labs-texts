Using intelligent behavioral monitoring to assess the impact of dietary energy and protein on growth, digestion, metabolism, and behavior of young female yaks
Authors: Duoting Wu, Lushun Huo, Jiajun Wang, Zhisheng Wang, Rui Hu, Liyuan Shi, Yingqi Peng, Huawei Zou, Fali Wu, Yahui Jiang, Quanhui Peng, Jianxin Xiao, Yiping Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of graded dietary energy and protein levels on growth, nutrient utilization, and behavior in housed young female yaks, integrating automated behavior monitoring via a YOLO-based computer vision system. Twenty-four 17-month-old Maiwa yaks (160.43 ± 14.54 kg) were randomly assigned to three dietary treatments (n = 8 per treatment): low energy and protein (LELP: net energy for gain [NEg] = 3.3 MJ/kg, crude protein [CP] = 10.50%, medium energy and protein (MEMP: NEg = 3.9 MJ/kg, CP = 12.51%), and high energy and protein (HEHP: NEg = 4.5 MJ/kg, CP = 14.50%). A 120-d feeding trial was conducted, followed by a 4-d digestion and metabolism trial. Growth performance was enhanced in the MEMP group relative to the LELP group, as indicated by significantly greater average daily gain (ADG) and a reduced feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) (P < 0.05). Nutrient metabolism was enhanced at higher dietary levels, as indicated by elevated serum urea nitrogen (BUN) and ruminal ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N) contents in the MEMP and HEHP groups (P < 0.05). Automated behavioral analysis revealed that the MEMP group spent less time standing and more time lying than the HEHP group (P < 0.05), suggesting improved welfare and rumination opportunity. Furthermore, the moderate diet (MEMP) optimized the utilization and deposition rates of both energy and nitrogen. In conclusion, a moderate energy and protein diet (NEg = 3.9 MJ/kg, CP = 12.51%) optimally supports the growth and metabolism of young female yaks under housed conditions. This study provides a scientific basis for the precise nutritional management and intelligent monitoring of yaks in intensive production systems.

Introduction

The yak (Bos grunniens) is an indispensable species on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, sustaining local livelihoods through meat, milk, and fiber production while playing a pivotal role in alpine ecosystem stability (Joshi et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2016). Traditional yak husbandry relies on seasonal grazing of native pastures—a system constrained by the region's extreme climate, hypoxic conditions, and forage that is chronically low in protein and energy, especially during winter (Long et al., 1999; Niu et al., 2004; Zomer et al., 2016). Consequently, growth rates are slow, productivity remains low, and animals are highly vulnerable to seasonal nutrient deficits.

Growing market demand for consistent, high-quality yak products is driving a transition from pastoralism to intensive, housed production systems (Liu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025). This shift is essential for achieving year-round supply, enhancing feed efficiency, and alleviating pressure on fragile grasslands. Under housed conditions, yaks are typically fed nutritionally balanced total mixed rations (TMR), representing a radical shift from diverse forage grazing to a controlled, formulated diet. However, the specific dietary energy and protein requirements for yaks under such intensive management remain poorly defined (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). While previous studies have elucidated how yaks adapt their rumen microbiota to fibrous native forages (Ren et al., 2025), their physiological and metabolic responses to processed TMR—especially regarding growth performance, nutrient utilization, and rumen function—are largely unknown.

Precision livestock farming technologies offer unprecedented opportunities to monitor individual animal responses in real time. Computer vision systems, for instance, enable automated tracking of key behaviors such as feeding, rumination, and resting, which are robust proxies for health, welfare, and nutritional status (Aikman et al., 2008; Beauchemin et al., 2008; Llonch et al., 2018). Although widely adopted in dairy and beef systems (Salau et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2020), the integration of Precision livestock farming tools—particularly behavior-based monitoring—into yak production systems remains in its infancy (Fuentes et al., 2022). Linking continuous behavioral data with dietary interventions could unlock new insights into nutrient–behavior–performance relationships under controlled conditions.

Therefore, this study hypothesized that graded levels of dietary energy and protein would differentially influence growth, nutrient digestion, rumen fermentation, and metabolic profiles in housed yaks, and that these responses could be captured through automated behavior monitoring. This study was designed with the following objectives: (1) to evaluate the effects of varying the energy and protein levels in TMR on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters, and blood metabolites in young female yaks; and (2) to develop a YOLO-based computer vision system for automated behavior recognition, enabling the analysis of diet-induced changes in daily activity patterns. This work aims to establish an integrated, data-driven framework for the intelligent nutritional management of yaks, thereby supporting the sustainable intensification of a critical high-altitude livestock system.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animals in this study were handled in accordance with the Chinese Animal Welfare Guidelines, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Nutrition, Sichuan Agricultural University (approval No.: SCAUAC202212–18).

Experimental animals and design

The experiment was conducted at the Cattle Farm of the Teaching and Research Base, Sichuan Agricultural University, Ya'an, Sichuan, China (E: 102°59′2″, N: 29°58′59″). The breeding base is located in a subtropical humid monsoon climate zone, characterized by mild weather, abundant rainfall, no extreme heat in summer, and no severe cold in winter. The average annual temperature ranges from 14 to 19 °C, and the altitude is 546.3 m.

Exp.1: twenty-four healthy 17–18-month-old female Maiwa yaks with similar body weights (160.43 ± 14.54 kg) were selected as experimental animals. A single-factor randomized design was adopted, with the yaks randomly divided into three treatment groups (8 replicates per group, 1 yak per replicate) and fed in individual pens. The experimental period was 135 d, including a 15-d pre-feeding period and a 120-d formal experimental period. Each group was fed diets with different energy and protein levels: low energy and low protein (LELP group, net energy for gain [NEg] = 3.3 MJ/kg, crude protein [CP] = 10.5%), medium energy and medium protein (MEMP group, NEg = 3.9 MJ/kg, CP = 12.51%), and high energy and high protein (HEHP group, NEg = 4.5 MJ/kg, CP = 14.5%).

Exp. 2: after Exp. 1, a digestion and metabolism trial were conducted on each yak. The experimental yaks were fed the same diets as in Exp. 1, with consistent feeding methods and rearing environments. The trial lasted 4 d, with yaks housed in individual cages.

Experimental diet

The basal diet was formulated according to the Feeding Standard of Beef Cattle (NY/T 815–2004) (with a NEg of 3.9 MJ/kg and CP of 12.5%) (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2004). The diets were formulated using this energy and protein levels as the median gradient. Three diet groups were formed: the LELP group, MEMP group, and HEHP group. The energy gradients were 84.6%, 100.0%, and 115.4% of the standard, respectively, and the protein gradients were 84%, 100%, and 116% of the standard, with a consistent energy-to-nitrogen ratio. All ingredients were thoroughly mixed according to the formulas to produce a TMR for feeding. Feeding and management

Before the experiment, the experimental yaks were given stomach-strengthening treatments and deworming. They were raised in individual pens, with one yak per pen. The water troughs were cleaned twice a week to maintain a dry and hygienic environment. Feeding was conducted at 08:00 and 16:00 daily, and yaks had free access to feed and water. After each feeding, approximately 5% of the feed was left as residue, and feed intake was recorded accordingly. The feeding and management conditions were consistent across all groups. On d 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 of the experimental period, yaks were weighed on an empty stomach before the morning feeding. On d 0 and 120, their standing posture was adjusted, and body measurements were taken: body height, body length, and chest depth were measured with a measuring rod, while chest circumference was measured with a tape measure.

Sample collection

During the experiment, feed samples were collected once a month. The rations and feed raw materials from each treatment group were collected using the quartering method and stored in a freezer at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. Residual TMR from each yak was collected after feeding. After the experiment, the residual feed from each yak was mixed, dried to a constant weight at 65 °C, crushed, and stored after passing through a 40-mesh sieve.

On the mornings of d 0 and 120 of the experiment, prior to feeding, 10 mL of blood was collected via jugular vein puncture using disposable 5 mL vacuum blood collection tubes. After standing for 10 min, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 1370 × g for 10 min. The serum was divided into 1 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C for the determination of blood biochemical indicators. Additionally, 4 mL of blood was collected using vacuum blood collection tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant, and routine blood tests were performed within 2 h.

On d 0 and 120 of the formal experimental period, rumen fluid was collected before the morning feeding. A gastric juice collection tube (length: 2.6 m; outer diameter: approximately 19 mm; sampler head outer diameter: 30.04 mm; A1141K, Wuhan Kelibo Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to extract a total of 100 mL of rumen fluid. To avoid saliva contamination, the first 10 mL of rumen fluid was discarded. The remaining fluid was filtered through four layers of gauze, and the pH was measured using a pH meter (pH-3, HORIBA, Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). The filtered rumen fluid was then transferred to 10-mL centrifuge tubes and stored at −20 °C for subsequent determination of rumen fermentation parameters.

Daily behavior

Image acquisition and dataset creation

Continuous video data were collected for 10 consecutive days (d 60–70) using recorders with uninterruptible power supplies and local storage. The recorders were placed on the wall behind the yaks in the barn, capturing footage 24 h per day. All recorded videos were extracted frame by frame into images for subsequent model construction and validation. The study consists of two core components, with a dedicated dataset for each. The first was a yak individual identification model dataset, used for training a YOLOv5 identification model. A total of 900 yak images were selected and extracted from the collected videos. Each yak in every image was annotated using LabelMe (v5.2.1, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA, USA). All annotations, containing identity information, were then converted into labels stored in the YOLO format. The second was the behavior recognition model validation dataset. This dataset was specifically designed for evaluating the accuracy of the subsequent behavior recognition algorithm. It contained a total of 2300 images. All images were manually annotated with the true behaviors of feeding, standing, and lying. This allowed for comparison and assessment of the algorithm's identification results.

Model training and performance evaluation

Yak individual identification model: the aforementioned 900 annotated images were used to train the YOLOv5 model. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (630 images, 70%) and a test set (270 images, 30%). The training set was input into the YOLOv5 network for training. The trained model was then validated on the test set. Performance was evaluated using standard object detection metrics: Precision, Recall, and mAP@0.5. The model exhibited favorable performance on the test dataset, with precision reaching 99.96%, recall 100%, and mAP@0.5 99.6%. Yak Behavior Recognition Model: This model first utilized the pre-trained YOLOv5 model to obtain bounding boxes for yaks in the video frames. Subsequently, three predefined horizontal boundary lines (high line 1, middle line 2, and low line 3) were set in the program. The model identified behaviors by judging the relative vertical position of the top edge of the detected bounding box against these three lines: if the top edge was between the high line and the middle line, the behavior was identified as feeding; if the top edge was between the middle line and the low line, the behavior was identified as standing; if the top edge was below the low line, the behavior was identified as lying (Fig. 2). Performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The model was evaluated using the previously mentioned validation dataset of 2300 images with manually annotated true behaviors. Analysis was conducted via a confusion matrix. The model achieved recognition accuracies of 97.0%, 94.3%, and 99.2% for feeding, standing, and lying behaviors, respectively (Fig. 3). This model established via deep learning algorithms automatically identifies yak behaviors and calculates the daily duration and distribution of three behaviors (feeding, standing, and lying) for each yak. The behavior monitoring algorithm in this experiment ran on the Ubuntu 18.04 operating system (Canonical Ltd., London, England, UK). Python 3.9 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used as the programming language, with PyCharm (version, JetBrains, Prague, Czech Republic) as the Python integrated development environment (IDE). The real-time yak behavior monitoring model was trained and run in the PyTorch (2.2.0, Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA) environment, and behavior monitoring results were automatically saved to Excel 2010 spreadsheets. Python code was written to automatically extract and statistically analyze the occurrence time nodes and durations of various behaviors from the Excel 2010 spreadsheets, and scatter plots of the distribution of the three behaviors within 12 h were generated.

Feeding duration was defined as the period from when the yak's head extended into the feeding trough (start point) to when it left the trough (end point), with a minimum duration of 15 s. Lying duration was defined as the entire body lying on the ground without significant movement, marked if lasting more than 15 s (minor movements within 5 s were ignored). Standing duration was defined as the yak standing upright with all four limbs supporting its body, marked if stationary for more than 15 s (minor head or limb movements were ignored).

During the formal experimental period, automatic temperature and humidity recorders (TP500, TOPRIE Electronics, Shenzhen Toprie Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) were placed at the front, middle, and rear of the barn, hung above the fence of individual pens, and positioned away from direct sunlight to avoid interference from the sunshade boards. The sensors were set to automatically record temperature and relative humidity (RH) every 30 min, and data were automatically exported for subsequent analysis.Temperature-humidityindex(THI)=(1.8×Temperature+32)-0.55×(1-RH)×(1.8×Temperature-26).

Additionally, the ear temperature of each yak was recorded daily using an electronic ear tag intelligent monitoring system (DM0138, Sichuan Nanshui Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, Sichuan, China).

Picky eating behavior

After 60 d of the trial, the TMR was collected from each yak for 5 consecutive days. Feed particle size was determined using the Pennsylvania State Particle Separator (Gilson Company, Lewis Center, OH) (Kononoff et al., 2003). The separator has three sieves with aperture sizes of 19, 8, and 1.18 mm (top to bottom), and a bottom collection pan. This separated the feed into four fractions: long (>19 mm), medium (8–19 mm), short (1.18–8 mm), and fine particles (<1.18 mm). A fresh sample of approximately 600 g was placed on the top sieve. The separator was shaken horizontally for 20 cycles, allowed to rest, and then shaken for another 20 cycles in the opposite direction to ensure adequate separation. Each feed sample was analyzed in triplicate. Calculation of physically effective neutral detergent fiber (NDF) (peNDF):

peNDF > 8 mm (based on 2 sieves) = Feed NDF concentration (% DM) × pef > 8 mm.

peNDF >1.18 mm (based on 3 sieves) = Feed NDF concentration (% DM) × pef >1.18 mm.

The formula for calculating the picky eating index (Kargar and Kanani, 2019) was as follows:Degreeofpickyeatingforaspecificparticlefraction(%)=100×(Providedfeed×Particleproportioninprovidedfeed-Remainingfeed×Particleproportioninremainingfeed)/(Feedintake×Particleproportioninprovidedfeed).

A picky eating index of 1 indicates no picky eating behavior; <1 indicates selective rejection; and >1 indicates a preference for that particle fraction.

Digestion and metabolism experiment

After Exp. 1, digestion and metabolism trials were conducted over four consecutive days. Feces and urine were collected daily, with the volume of feces and urine from each yak recorded and weighed. Fecal samples were collected in the morning, at noon, and in the evening daily. A 10% aliquot of fresh feces was taken, with part added to dilute sulfuric acid for nitrogen fixation, and the rest directly mixed and stored. The total weight of feces from the previous day was measured every morning. After the trials, the 4-d fecal samples were combined, dried at 65 °C to produce air-dried samples, crushed, and sieved for subsequent analysis. Urine from the previous day was collected each morning and filtered through gauze. A portion of the urine sample was added to dilute sulfuric acid for nitrogen fixation and the remaining portion was sealed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis.

Index determination

Growth performance

One day before the formal experiment, all experimental yaks were weighed, with their weights recorded as initial body weight (IBW). On the last day of the experiment, they were weighed before the morning feeding, and their weights were recorded as final body weight (FBW). Body length, height, chest circumference, and chest depth of the yaks were measured. The feed offered and residual feed of each yak were recorded daily to calculate the dry matter intake (DMI) at each nutritional level. The average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (F/G) were calculated based on body weight and feed intake data.

Chemical analyses

All feed and fecal samples were dried in a forced-air oven at 65 °C for 48 h, then weighed and ground before filtering through a 1-mm sieve for subsequent analysis. Dry matter (DM) and crude ash (Ash) contents in feed and feces were analyzed using methods 930.15 and 942.05 (AOAC, 2005), respectively. The organic matter (OM) in feed and manure was obtained by subtracting Ash from DM. Calcium (Ca) content was determined by flame atomic absorption spectrometry according to method 985.35 ((AOAC, 2023) . Phosphorus (P) content was determined by spectrophotometry according to method 986.24 (AOAC, 2023). According to the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method (method 984.13), the total nitrogen content in feed, feces and urine was analyzed (AOAC, 2005). The CP content of feces and feed was calculated using total nitrogen × 6.25. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were analyzed using an Ankom A2000i Fiber Analyzer (Ankom Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA) following the method of Van Soest et al. (1991). The ether extract (EE) was analyzed using the Soxhlet extraction method according to method 920.39 (AOAC, 2023). Gross energy (GE) in feed, feces, and urine was determined using an automatic oxygen bomb calorimeter (Parr 6400, Parr Instrument Company, Moline, IL, USA).

Blood indicators

Serum biochemical parameters, including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), glucose (GLU), β-hydroxybutyric acid (β-HB), urea nitrogen (BUN), and globulin (GLB), were determined using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 3100, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Whole blood routine parameters, including white blood cell count (WBC), granulocyte percentage (GRAN), lymphocyte percentage (LYM), monocyte percentage (MON), red blood cell count (RBC), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), eosinophil percentage (EO), and hematocrit (HCT), were measured using an automatic hematology analyzer (BC-5000 Vet, Shenzhen Mindray Animal Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

Rumen fermentation parameters

The pH of the rumen contents was measured immediately after collection using a pH meter (pH-3, HORIBA, Ltd., Osaka, Japan). The rumen fluid samples were thawed at 4 °C and then centrifuged at 2500 × g at room temperature. Next, 1 mL of the supernatant from each sample was transferred to a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube containing 0.2 mL of metaphosphoric acid solution (25%, w/v). The mixture was cooled in a water bath at 4 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of volatile fatty acids (VFA), including acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), butyric acid (BA), isobutyric acid (IBA), valeric acid (VA), and isovaleric acid (IVA), were then determined using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 8890, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N) was measured by the phenol-sodium hypochlorite colorimetric method. Microbial crude protein (MCP) was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (A045-4-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) (Wang et al., 2024).

Calculations and statistical analysis

The calculation formulae for DMI, ADG, and F/G were as follows:DMI(kg/d)=(Totalfeedprovided-Totalresidualfeed)/Numberofdays;ADG(kg/d)=(FBW–IBW)/Numberofdays;F/G=Totalfeedintake/Totalweightgain.

Net energy required for gain, calculated from metabolizable energy (ME), was determined using the equation proposed by NASEM (2016):NEg=1.42ME-0.174ME2+0.0122ME3-1.65.

The calculation formulas for the digestible energy (DE), ME, and methane energy (CH4-E) were as follows:GEI(MJ/d)=GE(MJ/kgDM)×DMI(kgDM/d);FE(MJ/d)=GEoffeces(MJ/kgDM)×Fecaloutput(kgDM/d);UE(MJ/d)=GEofurine(MJ/kg)×Urinevolume(kg/d);DE(MJ/d)=GEI-FE;DEd=DE/GEI;CH4-E(MJ/d)=(6.05+0.02×DEd)×DEd;ME(MJ/d)=DE-UE-CH4-E.

In these formulas, GE is the gross energy of feed, GEI is the gross energy intake, FE is the fecal energy excretion, UE is the urinary energy excretion, and CH4-E is the methane energy excretion. Notably, CH4-E was calculated using the formula proposed by Blaxter et al. (1965).Theapparentdigestibilityofnutrientsinthediet%=A×B-A1×B1/A×B×100,where A is the feed intake (kg/d); B is the nutrient content in the diet (%); A1 is the fecal output (kg/d); B1 is the nutrient content in the feces (%).

The calculation formulas for nitrogen intake (NI), fecal nitrogen (FN), urinary nitrogen (UN), apparent nitrogen digestibility, retained nitrogen (RN), and nitrogen retention rate were as follows:NI(g/d)=DMI(kgDM/d)×Dietarynitrogencontent(g/kgDM);FN(g/d)=Fecalnitrogencontent(g/kgDM)×Fecalexcretion(kgDM/d);UN(g/d)=Urinarynitrogenconcentration(g/L)×Urinevolume(L/d);Apparentnitrogendigestibility(%)=100×(NI-FN)/NI;RN(g/d)=NI-FN-UN;Nitrogenretentionrate(%)=100×RN/NI.

The maintenance and gain requirements for DE, ME, CP, and digestible CP were estimated using multiple linear regression across all experimental yaks (n = 24). The daily requirement (R, g/d or MJ/d) was modeled as:R=a×W0.75+b×ADG,where W0.75 is the metabolic body weight (kg), a is the regression coefficient for maintenance requirement, and b is the regression coefficient for gain requirement.

The original data were collated using Excel 2010. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Different dietary energy-protein level treatments were compared via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test using the following model:Yjk=μ+EPk+Yakj:k+ejk,where Yjk represents the observed value of the jth yak in the kth treatment group; μ represents the overall mean; EPk represents the fixed effect of energy-protein level; Yakj:k represents the individual random effect; and ejk represents the random error term. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were used to evaluate the linear and quadratic effects of different dietary energy-protein level treatments. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were expressed as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Discussion

Postnatal growth in ruminants is governed by genetic potential and dietary nutrition, with energy and protein serving as pivotal determinants (Guo et al., 2025; Schroeder and Titgemeyer, 2008). Over the 120-d trial, both the MEMP and HEHP diets significantly improved growth performance compared with the LELP group, as reflected by greater ADG and lower F/G in both MEMP and HEHP groups, as well as greater body length and chest circumference in the MEMP group compared with LELP. Notably, no significant differences were observed between the MEMP and HEHP groups in ADG and F/G, although the MEMP group achieved numerically superior values in these traits. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the MEMP diet optimally supported growth in young female yaks.

Most hematological parameters remained within physiological ranges across all treatments, indicating that varying dietary energy and protein levels did not induce systemic immune challenge or compromise general health. Notably, at 120 d, yaks fed the MEMP diet exhibited significantly higher MCHC than those in the LELP group. This finding suggests that the MEMP diet optimally supported erythrocyte hemoglobin synthesis or iron utilization (Jo et al., 2024), potentially enhancing oxygen delivery to support the elevated metabolic demands of growth. Although RBC decreased from the start to the end of the trial in all groups—a change potentially associated with acclimation to individual housing—values were still clinically normal. The maintenance of most blood indicators within normal ranges, coupled with the MCHC increase in the MEMP group, underscores that the MEMP-level energy-protein supply best supports physiological homeostasis in growing yaks.

At 120 d, NH3–N concentration was significantly higher in the MEMP and HEHP groups than in the LELP group, whereas ruminal pH, MCP yield, and VFA profile did not differ significantly among dietary treatments. This suggests that the ruminal fermentation environment remained largely stable across the energy-protein gradients tested, with no significant shift in VFA composition or fermentation pattern.

The daily physiological behaviors of yaks are key indicators for assessing their welfare status (Chelotti et al., 2024; Magrin et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024). Among these, feeding behavior reflects individual feed intake and energy conversion efficiency (Chen et al., 2025). This study showed that yaks in the LELP group exhibited obvious sorting behavior. They strongly preferred the thin fraction (105.43%) and the short fraction (105.09%), but clearly avoided the length fraction (85.44%) with higher fiber content. In contrast, the HEHP group showed a length index close to 100 (98.41%), suggesting negligible avoidance of fiber. This difference arises from the contrasting dietary compositions. The LELP diet contained the highest proportion of rice straw (20.00%) and a considerable amount of oat hay (25.00%), resulting in the highest total roughage content (45.00%) among the three groups. This provided ample physical effectiveness factor and peNDF, allowing yaks to selectively consume concentrate-rich fractions first. In contrast, the HEHP diet had lower roughage and peNDF contents, which limited selective feeding opportunities and resulted in minimal sorting behavior. In terms of daily time allocation, the HEHP group exhibited significantly less lying time and more standing time than the MEMP and LELP groups, whereas feeding duration did not differ significantly among groups. Similarly, Leonardi et al. (2003) found that when the proportion of alfalfa hay in the diet doubled, lactating dairy cows increased their avoidance of long particles. In summary, yak sorting behavior is regulated not only by nutritional requirements but also directly affected by dietary composition.

The present study was conducted under individual housing to enable precise monitoring of behavior and intake—a controlled setting essential for mechanistic investigation but one that does not fully replicate group-housed production environments. In such practical settings, social interactions and physical occlusions may alter behavioral expression and challenge the accuracy of the YOLO-based monitoring algorithm employed in this study. Future research should therefore validate these findings in group-housed yaks and explore integrating advanced multi-object tracking frameworks. These systems, by predicting trajectories from appearance and motion features, could mitigate occlusion issues and enhance the robustness of automated behavioral phenotyping for commercial application.

The apparent digestibility of CP, GE, EE, and NDF was significantly higher in the MEMP and HEHP groups than in the LELP group, with no significant differences between the two higher-nutrient groups. The marked improvement in NDF digestibility suggests enhanced fiber degradation under higher energy-protein supply, although the underlying microbial mechanisms were not directly elucidated in this study. The critical observation is that similar digestibility between the MEMP and HEHP groups was accompanied by divergent growth performance, indicating that factors beyond digestion efficiency determined productive outcomes. The MEMP diet likely supported efficient tissue growth through a favorable balance between nutrient retention and behavioral adaptation. Specifically, the MEMP group exhibited significantly more lying time and less standing time than the HEHP group, which may have facilitated rumination and digesta retention, while maintaining comparable RN with the HEHP group. In contrast, the HEHP group, despite high digestibility, showed less favorable behavioral time allocation, potentially offsetting its slight numerical edge in digestibility. These findings underscore that optimal growth requires more than high nutrient digestibility; it demands a balanced integration of digestive efficiency, behavioral adaptation, and post-absorptive utilization.

This interpretation is substantiated by nitrogen and energy metabolism data. Nitrogen intake, FN, UN, and RN were significantly higher in the MEMP and HEHP groups than in the LELP group, with comparable nitrogen retention rates between the two higher-nutrient groups, consistent with findings in other ruminants (Reed et al., 2007). Based on multiple linear regression across all experimental yaks, the maintenance requirements for CP and DCP were estimated at 2.651 and 1.149 g/(kg W0.75 d), respectively, with gain requirements of 137.701 and 60.251 g/kg. Similarly, GEI, DE, ME, and NEg were significantly enhanced in the MEMP and HEHP groups relative to the LELP group. Regression analysis yielded strong model fits for the relationships of DE and MEI with ADG. The maintenance requirements for DE and ME were quantified at 0.291 and 0.247 MJ/(kg W0.75 d), with gain requirements of 15.247 and 12.968 MJ/kg. Notably, the MEMP group achieved superior growth performance despite comparable nitrogen retention and energy availability to the HEHP group, suggesting that post-absorptive nutrient partitioning and behavioral adaptation, rather than absolute metabolic efficiency, determined productive outcomes.

Conclusion

This study establishes that in housed production systems, a moderate energy-protein diet (NEg = 3.90 MJ/kg, CP = 12.51%) optimizes the growth and metabolism of young female yaks. Compared to low-nutrient regimens, this balanced formulation significantly improves ADG, feed efficiency, and nutrient digestibility, supported by a favorable rumen fermentation profile. Automated behavioral analysis further revealed that this optimal diet promotes increased resting time, indicating enhanced rumination opportunity and welfare. In contrast, a high-nutrient diet provided no additional growth benefit, suggesting clear diminishing returns. These findings provide both a nutritional benchmark for precision feeding and a practical demonstration of computer vision as a management tool in modern yak production.
