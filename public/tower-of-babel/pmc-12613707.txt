Analysis of the association between physical health and BMI in single-child and multiple-child families: a study based on the SUREG model and the BMA approach
Authors: Zhiyu Qin, Qianqian Li, Xiaotian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina’s single children policy, implemented from 1979 to 2015, lasted for 35 years, with the result that China is now the country with the highest number of children without siblings in the world. This policy profoundly reshaped China’s family structure and parenting models, significantly impacting adolescents’ physical health and Body Mass Index (BMI).MethodsThis study analyzes data from the Chinese Adolescent Health Database, comprising 7,482 adolescents aged 12–18 years from 17 cities in Shandong Province collected between 2015 and 2020. The database includes indicators such as height, weight, 50-meter sprint, standing long jump, sit-and-reach, and vital capacity, which were assesses these indicators against the revised National Student Physical Health Standards of 2014 (CNSPHS). Using the Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) model and Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA), the study examines the specific effects of being a single child on physical fitness test scores (PFTS) and BMI, and the nonlinear relationship linking the two. Among the participants, 28% (n = 2,089) were only children and 72% (n = 5,393) had siblings. The primary purpose of this study is to quantify the impact of China’s one-child policy on adolescent physical health outcomes through rigorous statistical analysis, providing evidence-based insights for targeted interventions to improve health outcomes in this unique demographic group.ResultsThe SUR regression indicated that the PFTS and BMI scores of single children were lower than those of multiple-child families’ children by 1.412 and 1.213 points, respectively. Further analysis uncovered an inverted U-shaped relationship linking PFTS to BMI, indicating an optimal, moderate BMI range for physical health. These results highlight the importance of monitoring BMI in adolescent health management and underscore the necessity of targeted physical activities and nutritional guidance for single child.ConclusionBeing a single child has a significant negative impact on physical test scores and BMI, indicating potential vulnerabilities in their physical health. This discovery is crucial for policymakers and educators, prompting them to provide more opportunities for physical activity and to strengthen the cultivation of healthy eating habits among only children, thereby enhancing their physical health and overall well-being.Keywords: Single-child family, Physical fitness test, Body mass index, Chinese adolescents

Introduction

The physical health status of children and adolescents has attracted global attention over the past few decades as lifestyles have changed and social structures have evolved [1] Recent studies report a global population of people living with obesity of upwards of one billion people in 2022. Since 1990, the number of obese adults worldwide has doubled, and the number of obese children and adolescents (ages 5–19) has tripled [1]. An average of one hour of moderate-to- vigorous-intensity physical activity per day is very beneficial to the health of adolescents and young people [2], however, very few teens do reach that level of commitment to physical activity [3]. This is especially true in China, where the single-child policy has been in place for many years, resulting in a large number of single-child families there [4] and widespread social concern about the health and development of only children [5]. Recent, rapid economic and technological development adds to the problem of obesity faced by Chinese adolescents [6, 7], as does lack of exercise [8]. This has led to a downward trend in the physical health of Chinese students aged 7–18 years [9, 10]. The National Student Physical Fitness Standards (CNSPFS) is a set of standards established in China with the goal of comprehensively assessing and improving students’ physical fitness [11]. These standards comprehensively assess students’ physical fitness, capabilities, and health status, including their height, weight, lung capacity, 50-meter dash, and sit-and-reach tests aimed at thoroughly evaluating students’ physical health [12]. Schools can conduct extensive physical fitness tests based on these standards, analyzing students’ performance in various sports activities to comprehensively assess their fitness and health conditions. Such evaluations not only help schools and teachers determine the physical health status of students but also guide students in conducting scientific physical activity to improve their fitness [13]. The use of such comprehensive physical assessments helps in the early detection of health issues in students [14], making it possible to provide personalized health guidance and exercise recommendations. Moreover, encouraging student participation in physical activities enhances their fitness and overall health. In this fashion, schools can promote the holistic development of students, focusing not only on their academic achievements but also on their physical health.

PFTS and BMI are important indicators of physical health in children and adolescents [15]. Early studies generally focused on comparing differences in these indicators between only children and multiple-child families’ children, but the results have been inconsistent. Some studies have found that only children are four times more likely to be obese or overweight than those with siblings [5], while other studies have not found a significant association between only-child status and obesity [16, 17]. Furthermore, various studies have reported different obesity prevalence rates depending on the assessment tools and cut-off criteria used. For instance, a national survey in China found that 16.8% of boys and 12.6% of girls were overweight or obese based on the Chinese BMI standards, while WHO criteria showed even higher rates (21.3% and 15.4%, respectively) [18]. The primary difference between these standards lies in the cut-off points; the Chinese criteria use lower BMI thresholds to classify overweight and obesity. This is because substantial evidence indicates that Asian populations often exhibit a higher body fat percentage and an increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases at lower BMI levels compared to Western populations [19, 20].It has also been argued that only children have better academic performance and school performance, while multiple-child families’ children have better physical condition and anaerobic capacity [21]. These discrepancies can partly be attributed to regional differences, age groups studied, and inconsistent definitions of physical health outcomes. Including these variations provides a clearer context for understanding the current study. In conclusion, there is, to date, no uniform conclusion on the effect of only- child status on adolescents’ physical health. Thus, in order to fully clarify the effect of single-child status on the relationship between physical health and BMI, empirical investigative work is needed. This study differs from previous research in the following ways. First, most studies to date have been cross-sectional studies, often using means and t-tests to analyze changes in PFTS and BMI [22] scoring standard, but this does not intuitively capture differences in standardized fitness levels relative to age- and sex-specific norms. Moreover, such approaches may inadvertently introduce bias when evaluating minors’ physical development. In this study, the raw scores for physical fitness test items were converted into standardized scores based on the revised National Physical Fitness Standards for Students in 2014 [12], which enables norm-referenced comparisons across populations. These standardized scores allow for systematic identification of students who may benefit from additional physical activity support, while maintaining an objective, developmental perspective rather than making absolute health judgments. Second, prior studies have found an inverted U-shaped relationship linking students’ physical health to BMI [23–25]. However, these studies did not employ rigorous testing, while the current study ensures the accuracy of its results through relevant tests. Finally, the parameters of prior studies were usually oriented to a single model, primarily a model with optimal fit selected from a set of to-be-selected models. When interpreting the data, one can easily run afoul of estimation bias and precision overestimation. This study not only deals with this issue by model averaging but also provides a preliminary verification of the stability of the regression results.

Data and method

Data

The data for this study were taken from the Adolescent Health Database [26]. This database uses field tests, questionnaires, and data collection methods to gather information on adolescent health resources. It covers multiple rounds of surveys for the 2015/2016, 2016/2017, 2017/2018, and 2020/2021 school years, and aims to elucidate middle and high school students’ health status and health-related behaviors in China. It is the first publicly shared dataset on adolescent health and its related behaviors in China. The methodological framework for this research follows a structured approach consisting of four key components: (1) data source and sample characteristics, (2) measurement procedures and variable definitions, (3) statistical models, and (4) robustness tests. This balanced framework ensures comprehensive examination of the research questions while maintaining analytical rigor.

Policymakers, educational institutions, and other stakeholders all find it beneficial to formulate or adjust existing strategies to improve the well-being of Chinese youth. The data include the physical fitness test data and body composition data of adolescents from 17 cities in Shandong Province from 2015 to 2020 [27]. Outliers were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) data significantly deviating from reality due to measurement and entry errors, and (2) missing data in the database. A total of 9,048 samples were collected, and 1,546 samples were removed due to serious deviations or missing variables, resulting in a final sample size of 7,482. The sample age range was 12–18 years, covering areas of Shandong with varying levels of economic development, thus providing strong representativeness. The surveyed students all expressed informed consent and complied with ethical requirements. Student test data are presented in Table 1.

The dependent variable in this study is PFTS scores, evaluated using the CNSPHS [11], which assesses students’ physical health levels through a comprehensive evaluation of body shape, physical function, and physical quality. Specific tests include a 50-meter dash, standing long jump, lung capacity, sit-and-reach, and BMI. Physical education teachers strictly conduct these tests according to the relevant standards. The Adolescent Health Database includes the scores for these required tests. Importantly, the CNSPHS first converts raw performance metrics to standardized scores based on age-specific and gender-specific standards, which accounts for differences in physical development. This standardization process means that raw physical advantages (such as greater height or weight) do not necessarily translate to better standardized scores if the performance does not meet the expected standards for the individual’s physical characteristics. For this study, the scores are standardized as follows: standardized score = (sum of the individual scores/5), resulting in PFTS values ranging from 12 to 100 points.

Body Mass Index, known as BMI (weight kg/height2 m2) is an internationally recognized method for assessing the degree of obesity in humans [28]. It is widely used to evaluate an individual’s weight status and can indicate whether they are underweight, of average weight, overweight, or obese. This study processes the scores from the database according to the scoring standards to obtain BMI scores ranging from 60 to 100 points.

The core independent variable in this study is sibling status, a dummy variable. The Adolescent Health Database directly includes this variable, assigning a value of 1 to samples that are children without siblings and 0 to those with siblings.

Control variables include age, place of residence, participation in sports interest classes, boarding status, grade, gender, ethnicity, father’s education level, mother’s education level, family economic conditions, internet entertainment, frequency of consuming sugar, frequency of consuming fried foods, frequency of eating noodles, and frequency of eating fast food. (See Table 2) These control variables were selected based on three criteria: (1) established associations with childhood obesity and physical fitness in previous literature, following the socio-ecological model framework [29]; (2) availability in the Adolescent Health Database; and (3) potential confounding effects identified through preliminary correlation analyses. Specifically, demographic variables (age, gender, ethnicity) control for biological and developmental differences; socioeconomic variables (parental education, family economic conditions) account for resource availability and health literacy; lifestyle variables (dietary habits, internet use) capture behavioral patterns known to influence physical activity and BMI; and environmental variables (urban/rural residence, boarding status) reflect differential access to physical activity opportunities and food environments [30, 31].

The magnitude and consistency of these negative associations remain stable even when applying different statistical approaches, with only minor variations in coefficient values. This is because the standardization process adjusts raw performance according to age-specific and developmental norms, allowing for assessment of physical fitness relative to expected standards for each child’s physical characteristics.

Figure 1 shows the comparative influence of key variables on PFTS across different statistical models. The negative effect of single child status remains consistent across models, demonstrating robust evidence for this relationship regardless of statistical approach. While the magnitude varies slightly between OLS (−1.397), SUREG (−1.412), and BMA (−1.358) methods, the direction and significance level remain unchanged. Rather than restating coefficient values visible in the figure, it’s important to note that accounting for inter-variable correlations (SUREG) and model selection uncertainty (BMA) confirms the stability of these relationships. Similarly, age maintains a significant positive effect across all models, while rural residence consistently demonstrates a negative impact.

Figure 2 illustrates how different statistical approaches affect the estimation of variables’ influence on BMI. The relationship between single-child status and BMI remains significantly negative across all models, though with varying magnitude. Rather than simply listing coefficient changes that are already visible in the figure, our analytical focus should be on understanding that methodological choices matter: when accounting for correlations between physical fitness performance and BMI (Model 4), and when incorporating model uncertainty (Model 6), the estimated effects shift but remain significant. This pattern applies not only to our primary variable of interest but also to control variables such as boarding status, whose estimated impact changes substantially between modeling approaches.

Table 4 shows that the PIP values for both PFTS and BMI for single children were 1.00, which indicates that single child status has a significant negative effect on the PFTS and BMI of adolescents. This result can be explained by the fact that single children have advantages in terms of material resources but may lack competition and interaction with their peers [32]. Single children have fewer opportunities to participate in outdoor sports because of their parents’ overprotection, and this affects their sports performance and health status. Thus, even though single children are economically advantaged, this advantage does not translate into better sports performance or health.

The PIP value for age was also 1.00 for both PFTS and BMI, indicating that increasing age has a significantly positive effect on both adolescent fitness test scores and BMI. This is related to the gradual improvement of physiological and motor skills in adolescents as they mature [33]. As they get older, adolescents improve their physical functioning and coordination, leading to better performance on sports tests.

The effect of place of residence on PFTS was more significant (PIP value of 0.69), while its effect on BMI was insignificant (PIP value of only 0.01). This reflects differences in sports facilities and nutritional intake between urban and rural areas: urban adolescents may have easier access to sports training resources and a more balanced diet, while those in rural areas may have more limited access.

The PIP value for participation in sports interest classes was 0.92, indicating that participation in sports interest classes has a significantly positive impact on adolescents’ PFTS. This is related to the fact that sports interest classes provide specialized training and systematic exercise, which helps adolescents improve their sports skills and physical fitness.

The impact of single-child status on adolescents’ PFTS and BMI was assessed by applying Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), seemingly unrelated regressions (SUREG), and Bayesian model averaging (BMA). The results consistently show that all three models yield similar conclusions, both in terms of significance and direction of influence, thus validating the robustness of the study. The application of this multi-model approach is frequently used in the existing literature outside of China, and some scholars have emphasized the importance of using multiple statistical methods in economics research to enhance the robustness of results [34]. This is, therefore, a generally accepted approach.

To test the sensitivity of the sample and make the conclusions somewhat comparable, this analysis is focused on the male sample, making the core explanatory variable be single-child status, thereby reducing the sample size. In the regression results for Sample A, the absolute value of the regression coefficient of single-child status on PFTS slightly increased, while the absolute value of the regression coefficient of the BMI slightly decreased. However, there was no significant change in the direction or the degree of significance of these coefficients, and the change was simply caused by the change in the number of samples. Generally, men are more willing to participate in sports than women [35, 36], but this did not affect the main regression findings. This suggests that the effect of single-child status on PFTS and BMI is not affected by gender differences and provides further evidence for the generalizability of the results.

In addition, two variables — exercise in physical education class and exercise outside of class — were included in Sample B. The corresponding questions in the questionnaire were, “How often did you engage in strenuous exercise (playing, running, jumping, throwing vigorously) in physical education class during the past seven days?” “Which of the following would be a good description of your performance in the past week?“, drawing on the work of Binbin Tang and Linping Liu [37]. These two variables were defined as participation in in-class and out-of-class physical activity, respectively, coded with exercise = 1 and no exercise = 0. The regression results for Sample B are consistent with those of the primary regression. This emphasizes the importance of including crucial control variables in the model to avoid bias caused by omitted variables. In other studies, scholars have similarly confirmed the robustness of their estimation results by including additional control variables in their analyses [38].

Finally, interaction terms for single-child status with rural background and student grade level were introduced. The directions of the regression coefficients remain unchanged by this addition, and the magnitudes of the coefficients are slightly changed. Such a change is essential for the stability of the results of further study and allows us to explore the heterogeneous effects of different socio-economic backgrounds and stages of education. This in-depth analysis reveals the complexity of the effects under examination. It echoes Jonathan’s use of interaction terms in education policy research, where he reveals the different faces of policy effects through interaction term analysis, demonstrating the value of this approach in understanding complex social problems [39].

U-test

This study tested the previously hypothesized inverted U-shaped relationship linking PFTS to BMI using Stata17 software. First, in order to make the results easy to observe, the BMI variable was assigned as a four category variable (BMI4), with low weight = 1, normal = 2, overweight = 3, and obese = 4. After data processing, the software was used to carry out hypothesis testing. The specific process was as follows.

Hierarchical regression analysis was used to test the inverted U-shaped relationship between PFTS and BMI. First, the control variables were added to model 7, then the primary term of BMI4 was added to model 8, and the square term of BMI4 was introduced into model 9. Regression analyses were carried out separately; the results are shown in Table 6. The relationship between the linear effect of physical education class exercise and extracurricular exercise on PFTS in model 7 is insignificant, but the other control variables significantly affect PFTS. Model 8 shows that there is still no significant effect of physical education and extracurricular exercise on PFTS, but there is a significant linear relationship between all other variables, including BMI, on PFTS. In model 9, there is a significant positive correlation between BMI and PFTS (coef = 3.372, p < 0.01) and a significant negative relationship between the squared term of BMI and PFTS (coef=−0.807,p < 0.01); the two coefficients are heteroscedastic, and both are significant at the 99% confidence interval. An inverted U-shape relationship between BMI and PFTS can thus be presumed to exist.

Discussion

After setting control variables such as physical activity inside and outside the classroom and out-of-school sports interest classes, single-child status was found to have a significant negative effect on PFTS. This is consistent with prior studies that have found that only children have lower height and fitness scores compared to children with siblings [41]. While the raw measurements in Table 1 show that SC have advantages in height, weight, 50-meter sprint, standing long jump, and spirometry, these advantages do not translate to better standardized fitness scores. This apparent contradiction can be explained by understanding that standardized scores evaluate performance relative to expected norms based on physical characteristics. Despite having physical advantages that might be attributed to better nutrition and resources, SC do not achieve the expected levels of physical fitness relative to their physical development. This suggests that physical advantages from potentially better nutrition are offset by lifestyle factors such as reduced physical activity and overprotection. This, in turn, supports the claim that single children status has a significant negative effect on PFTS. However, this effect is easily ignored by parents. Some parents spoil their children too much, only focusing on satisfying their children’s requirements and creating a superior living environment and conditions for their children [4] but neglecting physical activity [42].

At the same time, only children have lower Body Mass Index scores than multiple-child families’ children. This is mainly because only children consume more food, show poorer performance in certain dietary behaviors, and have a higher risk of being overweight or obese. These findings can be interpreted through multiple theoretical frameworks. From a socio-ecological perspective, the family environment of single children creates a unique developmental context where resource concentration may paradoxically lead to poorer health outcomes. The absence of sibling interaction reduces opportunities for active play and competitive physical activities that are crucial for motor development [43]. Furthermore, from a socio-educational standpoint, our findings suggest that parental educational investment in single children may inadvertently prioritize academic achievement over physical development, reflecting broader cultural values in post-reform China [44]. Geographic factors also significantly influence these outcomes. Our data from 17 cities in Shandong Province reveal distinct urban-rural disparities, single children in urban settings have lower PFTS than rural children (p < 0.01). This geographical variation reflects differential access to physical activity resources, nutritional options, and educational pressures across regions. These findings align with Tong et al.‘s observation that urbanization levels correlate significantly with decreased physical activity among Chinese youth [45].It is known that single children consume more vegetables, fruits, meat products, and sugary beverages and exhibit poorer eating behaviors [46]. In the long term, such poor eating behaviors can gradually turn into eating habits that are difficult to change, thus increasing the risk of obesity [47], and overeating during adolescence can also lead to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease in the future [48]. Today’s ever-accelerating pace of life has contributed to the popularization of fast food, which is usually high in calories, fat, and sugar but low in nutritional value [49]. Parents of only children often have more resources and inclination to fulfill their children’s personal dietary preferences, which may lead them to provide the children with unhealthy foods. This, in turn, can lead to the formation of long-term poor eating habits [50] that ultimately hurt the child’s BMI. Studies have shown that parents of only children are overprotective and spoil their children because of the relative concentration of resources. This type of upbringing limits children’s opportunities and interest in participating in physical activities, affecting the development of their physical fitness [46]. In addition, with the popularization of electronic technology, single children are more likely to become addicted to video games and the internet and lack sufficient outdoor activities, negatively affecting their physical health and the development of social skills. Often, parents tend to have higher academic expectations for single children, resulting in excessive academic pressure and a lack of sufficient time and energy to participate in physical activity. Coupled with long-term poor dietary habits, such consequences may lead to a simultaneous decline in single children’s physical fitness and their health.

This study revealed an inverted U-shaped relationship linking PFTS and BMI. According to the energy balance theory, weight change is determined by the balance between energy intake and expenditure. When energy intake exceeds expenditure, excess energy is converted to body fat, leading to weight gain; when energy expenditure exceeds intake, weight loss occurs.

Adolescence when a balance between nutritional intake and physical activity is essential for healthy growth. The BMI on the left side of the inverted U-shaped curve is low, meaning that the energy intake is insufficient to support the adolescent’s growth and development and daily activity needs. Low body mass test scores may reflect reduced physical strength and endurance due to insufficient energy. As BMI increases, energy intake begins to meet or slightly exceed energy expended, contributing to increased muscle mass and improved fitness, thereby leading to improved fitness test scores. However, as BMI increases to a certain point, the curve begins to slope downward, suggesting that beyond a certain threshold, excessive energy intake begins to affect the body negatively instead. On the right side of the inverted U-shaped curve, a higher BMI may reflect excessive energy intake or insufficient energy expenditure, resulting in the accumulation of body fat. Excess body fat not only increases the risk of several chronic diseases but also leads to reduced exercise capacity, in that overweight and obese individuals have less endurance and flexibility than individuals of average weight, directly affecting their fitness test scores. The results of this study also make clear that there is a threshold for optimal BMI for fitness test scores beyond which fitness test scores begin to decline. This implies a need to find a balance in adolescent health promotion strategies to ensure that adolescents can achieve and maintain an optimal BMI within this range through proper diet and adequate exercise. This will help them maintain good physical fitness and prevent obesity and other chronic diseases associated with unhealthy diets and lifestyles.

Like all studies, this study has some limitations. (1) The data collected in this study were limited, in that they were confined mainly to Chinese adolescents aged 12–18 years, with no data from other countries or regions included for comparative analysis. (2) The index BMI used to assess obesity in this study involved only height and weight, while other essential obesity indicators such as waist circumference [51] and waist-to-hip ratio [52] were not included in the data. (3) Birth order and number of siblings were not identified in this study because youngest and single children have similar lifestyles [53] and are similarly more likely to be overweight or obese [54, 55]. (4) Due to the limitations of the survey data, the path of the influence of single- child status on PFTS and BMI was not explored in depth, and further in-depth research on this issue will be conducted in the future. (5) The cross-sectional nature of our study limits causal inferences, as we cannot establish temporal relationships between only-child status and physical outcomes. (6) We did not account for parental physical activity patterns and health behaviors, which likely influence children’s physical fitness through both genetic and environmental pathways. (7) Our standardization of physical fitness scores followed the CNSPHS, which may limit international comparability. (8) The study did not capture qualitative aspects of family dynamics that might explain the mechanisms behind the observed associations. (9) The focus on Shandong Province, while providing regional depth, may limit generalizability to other regions of China with different socioeconomic conditions and cultural practices.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides empirical evidence revealing a significant correlation between single- child status and reduced physical fitness levels, alongside elevated Body Mass Index. These findings imply potential long-term health risks for single child adolescents, indicating a crucial area for public health intervention. Consequently, policymakers, educators, and family health practitioners should collaboratively formulate targeted and evidence-based interventions to enhance physical activity opportunities and nutritional education specifically tailored for single child families. Future research should further explore the underlying mechanisms driving these disparities and assess the effectiveness of specific intervention measures.
