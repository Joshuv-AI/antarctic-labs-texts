Wave propagation in dual-porosity media under the combined effects of light, heat, elasticity, and gravity
Authors: Najmeddine Attia, Taoufik Moulahi, Abdelaala Ahmed, E. S. Elidy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the propagation of two-dimensional thermoelastic waves in a semiconductor medium characterized by double porosity and subjected to the influence of gravity. We develop the theoretical framework using the Lord–Shulman (L–S) and Green–Lindsay (G–L) models. Both models account for finite thermal wave speeds and relaxation times. The coupled governing equations describing temperature, carrier density, stress, and displacement fields are analytically solved using the harmonic wave method. The analysis emphasizes the combined influence of gravitational acceleration, temporal variation, and thermoelastic coupling on the dynamic behavior of the medium. Three-dimensional graphical results illustrate the interactions among these parameters and their impact on thermal and mechanical responses. The findings show that gravity significantly modifies the amplitude and spatial distribution of thermoelastic waves, enhancing the coupling between thermal and elastic fields within the porous structure. Comparative evaluation of the CD, L–S, and G–L theories reveals the crucial roles of relaxation times and gravitational effects in controlling thermoelastic wave propagation in double-porosity semiconductor materials.

Introduction

The thermoelasticity theory explains how elastic bodies respond to combined thermal and mechanical effects. Interest in this theory arises from several practical applications. For instance, in aeronautics, metallic structures—typically elastic—are subjected to intense thermal stresses due to high velocities, necessitating analysis of their behavior under such conditions. In nuclear reactors, elevated temperatures and thermal gradients produced by nuclear reactions generate significant thermal stresses, requiring construction using materials with special thermoelastic properties. During the latter half of the twentieth century, Biot1 proposed the coupled thermoelasticity theory (CD theory), derived from Fourier’s heat conduction law, to address inconsistencies in the classical uncoupled thermoelasticity theory (CUCT). The traditional CUCT contained a physical contradiction, as it implied parabolic heat propagation with an unrealistically infinite speed. To overcome this issue, a relaxation time parameter was introduced into the heat equation2. Later, Green and Lindsay (G–L)3 advanced this approach by incorporating two relaxation times, developing what became known as the generalized thermoelasticity theory. Many researchers4–6 have applied this generalized framework to analyze a variety of physical problems. More recently, thermal shock problems have been examined within the scope of the generalized thermoelasticity theory, using the two-temperature model in two-dimensional contexts influenced by multiple external fields7,8.

Semiconductors hold significant importance in modern technology, particularly in electronic and electrical circuit applications. Hence, it is essential to develop models that describe their behavior when exposed to external factors such as light, which induces thermal effects. Internal excitation processes lead to particle displacements, resulting in thermoelastic (TE) deformations. Additionally, photo-excitation triggers optical or photothermal transport mechanisms, causing the transfer of surface electrons and generating carrier density variations associated with plasma waves—this phenomenon is termed electronic deformation (ED). Gordon et al.9 were among the earliest to explore semiconductor properties through the study of acoustic waves produced by electromagnetic radiation. Later, photoacoustic spectroscopy was used to analyze the effect of laser illumination on semiconductor media10. Numerous studies11–13 subsequently employed photothermal theory and photoacoustic methods to assess how laser beams interact with solid and fluid samples. The photothermal diffusion process, together with temperature gradients, has been applied to examine variable thermal conductivity in semiconductors14. Theoretical models based on photothermal principles have also been utilized to estimate various physical characteristics of solid elastic semiconductor materials and to describe interactions between plasma and thermoelastic waves15. Lotfy and Tantawi16 investigated the coupling between thermoelastic and photothermal theories in a non-homogeneous nanocomposite semiconductor, forming what is referred to as the photo-thermoelasticity theory. More recently, several researchers17–19 have analyzed the behavior of plasma–thermal–elastic waves in semiconductors under the framework of generalized magneto-thermoelasticity theory, incorporating new formulations such as the two-temperature and hyperbolic two-temperature models.

The concept of double porosity has emerged as a significant extension of classical poroelastic and thermoelastic theories to describe the behavior of materials possessing two distinct pore systems, each characterized by different pore sizes and interactions. This framework, originally proposed to model geological formations such as fractured rocks and later extended to engineered porous materials, captures the complex coupling between mechanical, thermal, and diffusive fields occurring within media that contain both macro- and micro-porous networks. In double-porosity media, the solid matrix is permeated by a primary pore network and a secondary pore system, allowing for fluid exchange between them, which profoundly influences the material’s response under mechanical loading, thermal gradients, and electromagnetic effects. Ieşan and Quintanilla20 established a comprehensive thermomechanical model for double-porosity structures, incorporating thermal stresses and porosity interactions to analyze stability and wave propagation phenomena. Recent developments by Yahya and Abd-Alla21 have extended this theory to rotating magneto-thermoelastic systems, highlighting the influence of magnetic fields and rotational motion on thermal and mechanical behavior under double-porosity effects. Similarly, Salah et al.22 investigated nonlocal and diffusion effects in rotating thermoelastic media with double porosity, showing how nonlocal elasticity modifies wave characteristics and stress distributions. Their further work on magneto-thermoelastic responses under initial stress emphasized the coupling between magnetic fields, rotation, and porosity parameters in influencing deformation and energy dissipation23. Complementary studies by Kumar and Paswan24 analyzed reflection phenomena of plane waves in fractional thermoelastic double-porosity media, revealing the combined effects of rotation, initial stress, and fractional derivatives on dynamic wave behavior. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the inclusion of double porosity enhances the predictive capability of thermoelastic models, enabling more accurate simulation of heat conduction, fluid movement, and stress evolution in complex materials. The interaction between the two pore systems leads to additional relaxation times and coupled field equations, producing rich dynamic responses that are crucial for understanding natural materials such as rocks, bones, and advanced porous composites. The theory also serves as a foundation for exploring coupled magneto-thermoelastic and diffusion phenomena in modern engineering materials where microstructural heterogeneity plays a key role. Double-porosity models thus provide a powerful theoretical framework to analyze coupled physical processes in multi-porous media subjected to thermal, mechanical, and electromagnetic influences, bridging the gap between classical continuum mechanics and the multiscale behavior observed in real-world materials.

The effect of gravity on thermoelastic materials is essential in understanding how body forces influence deformation, temperature distribution, and stress fields in solids. When a thermoelastic body is subjected to a gravitational field, the equilibrium and heat conduction equations become strongly coupled, altering both the transient and steady-state behaviors of the material. Gravity-induced stresses significantly affect thermoelastic wave propagation, stability, and energy transport, making their inclusion vital in applications such as geophysics, aerospace, and structural analysis. Bayones et al.25 examined a thermoelastic medium exposed to a gravity field and laser pulse within four generalized thermoelastic theories, showing that gravity modifies temperature profiles and displacement responses, especially during transient heating. Ramadan et al.26analyzed temperature-dependent thermoelasticity under gravity within the Lord–Shulman framework, revealing that gravitational forces reduce wave speeds and alter relaxation characteristics due to their effect on the thermal field. Kadian et al.27 studied the combined effects of initial stress and gravity on fiber-reinforced thermoelastic materials using the Moore–Gibson–Thompson model, demonstrating that gravitational forces interact with pre-stress and reinforcement anisotropy to influence dynamic stability. These investigations confirm that gravity cannot be neglected in thermoelastic modeling, as it directly impacts thermal stresses, strain energy distribution, and the evolution of temperature-dependent fields. Understanding thermoelastic behavior under gravity provides critical insight into material performance in real-world environments, from Earth’s crustal layers to high-altitude and space applications. The concept of relaxation time in thermoelasticity represents the finite speed of thermal and mechanical wave propagation by accounting for the time delay between applied thermal disturbances and resulting heat flux or stress responses. It overcomes the unrealistic infinite heat conduction predicted by classical Fourier’s law. Goswami and Sarkar28 analyzed wave propagation in thermoelastic media with relaxation time and showed that relaxation effects lead to attenuation and dispersion of thermoelastic waves. Prasad29 studied the influence of scale length on wave behavior under size-dependent thermoelasticity with one relaxation time, demonstrating that both microstructural and temporal parameters significantly modify phase velocity and energy dissipation. Fernández and Quintanilla30 investigated decay and regularity in second-gradient thermoelastic plates, revealing that relaxation time enhances system stability and smoothness of temperature and displacement fields. Overall, relaxation time introduces realistic transient responses, finite propagation speeds, and improved thermomechanical modeling accuracy for advanced materials.

Recent advances in thermoelasticity and wave propagation in complex media have emphasized the importance of incorporating non-classical heat conduction models and microstructural effects to accurately describe material behavior under extreme conditions.

Rayleigh waves are surface acoustic waves that propagate along the boundary of elastic media, with amplitudes that decay exponentially with depth. In nonlocal piezothermoelastic orthotropic media, their behavior becomes more complex due to the combined influence of anisotropy, electromechanical coupling, and long-range interactions31. Nonlocal theory accounts for size-dependent effects, which are particularly important in micro- and nano-scale structures where classical elasticity fails to provide accurate predictions.

In such media, the coupling between thermal, electrical, and mechanical fields significantly affects the propagation characteristics of Rayleigh waves, including velocity, attenuation, and dispersion32. The presence of phase-lag models further refines the analysis by incorporating finite thermal and diffusive response times. Additionally, orthotropic material properties introduce directional dependence, which alters wave behavior along different axes. These combined effects make the study of Rayleigh waves in nonlocal piezothermoelastic media essential for applications in sensors, actuators, and advanced smart materials33–35.

Recent advances in material science and thermoelastic modeling have focused on improving the understanding and prediction of mechanical and thermal behavior in complex media. In semiconductor materials, precise control of surface integrity and subsurface damage is essential for maintaining performance, especially under manufacturing processes such as precision grinding36. The interaction between thermal and mechanical effects also plays a key role in porous and multiphase systems, where heat transfer mechanisms can be accurately described using multi-energy equation models37. In geophysical and engineering applications, external forces such as tidal loading influence subsurface stiffness and deformation, highlighting the importance of coupled thermoelastic and mechanical analysis38. Moreover, emerging concepts such as mechanical cloaking and material camouflage demonstrate how internal structural design can manipulate stress distribution and elastic wave propagation39. The integration of data-driven approaches with thermoelastic theory has further enabled the identification of uncertain material parameters, enhancing predictive capabilities in complex systems40. These developments collectively contribute to a deeper understanding of thermoelastic behavior in advanced materials and structures under coupled physical effects.

Recent developments in solid mechanics and wave dynamics have highlighted the complex behavior of elastic waves in advanced materials and engineered systems. Elastic-wave propagation is strongly influenced by material heterogeneity, structural design, and external excitations, leading to phenomena such as wave asymmetry and directional propagation control41. In engineered structures, vibration characteristics are highly sensitive to defects, imperfections, and geometric variations, which can significantly alter resonance frequencies and dynamic stability42. At micro- and nano-scales, material properties such as elastic modulus exhibit orientation-dependent fluctuations, which affect local deformation and wave transmission behavior43. In addition, advanced metamaterials have demonstrated the ability to control and attenuate low-frequency elastic and seismic waves through tailored band structures and internal architectures44. The interaction between mechanical vibrations and control systems, such as inverter-driven devices, also plays a role in managing harmonic responses and improving system performance45. These combined effects emphasize the importance of integrated analytical and experimental approaches for understanding and controlling elastic wave behavior in modern engineering applications.

The present work introduces four novel contributions that extend significantly beyond existing literature. First, while previous studies have investigated double-porosity thermoelasticity20, photothermal semiconductors46, and gravitational effects25 separately, no prior work has simultaneously coupled all four effects light, heat, elasticity, and gravity in a unified double-porosity semiconductor model, revealing interactions such as gravity-modulated photothermal conversion and porosity-dependent thermoelastic damping that cannot be captured by partial models. Second, this study provides the first systematic comparative analysis of CD, L–S, and G–L theories under identical double-porosity, photothermal, and gravitational conditions, demonstrating that the choice of theory affects predicted amplitudes by up to 60% and that the G–L theory with two relaxation times produces the most physically realistic results by capturing multi-scale relaxation processes (phonon–phonon scattering at 1–10 ps, electron–phonon scattering at 0.1–1 ps, and thermal diffusion over 10 ns). Third, we provide the first quantitative demonstration that gravity reduces displacement amplitude by 30%, increases stress by 20%, and enhances near-surface temperature by 15% in double-porosity semiconductors through three mechanisms: gravitational compression adding σ_gravity = ρg x3, compressive strain converting to heat via ε₁∇·u, and gravity acting as a restoring force opposing upward wave propagation—findings with direct implications for large-scale semiconductor devices where gravitational forces are typically neglected. Fourth, we quantify how the thermoelastic coupling parameter ε₁ and thermoelectric coupling parameter ε3 control energy conversion, showing that increasing ε₁ from 0.01 to 0.10 raises temperature by 35% but reduces displacement by 25% through a positive feedback loop (thermal expansion → strain → additional heat → further expansion), while increasing ε3 from 0.01 to 0.10 raises carrier density by 50% through the Seebeck effect driving carrier migration from hot to cold regions, providing design guidelines for optoelectronic devices. Collectively, these contributions establish the most comprehensive analytical model to date for wave propagation in porous semiconductors under realistic operating conditions, with direct applications to solar energy harvesting, power electronics, laser diodes, geothermal energy extraction, and MEMS/NEMS devices.

In the context of double-porosity media, Two distinct void networks coexist within the solid matrix:

Pores These are the primary void network, consisting of small, typically spherical or irregular cavities with diameters ranging from nanometers to micrometers. Pores are characterized by low permeability (typically 10-19 to 10-16 m2) but high specific surface area (100–1000 m2/g). They store most of the fluid volume and provide the primary capacity for energy storage. In geological materials, pores correspond to the intergranular spaces between mineral grains. In engineered materials, pores are often intentionally introduced during manufacturing to reduce weight or enhance thermal insulation.

Fissures These are the secondary void network, consisting of larger, crack-like or planar voids with lengths much greater than their apertures (aspect ratio typically > 10:1). Fissures have high permeability (10-12 to 10-9 m2) but low storage capacity. They serve as the primary pathways for fluid and energy transport. In natural materials, fissures often result from thermal stresses, tectonic forces, or drying shrinkage. In semiconductors, fissures can be generated during thermal cycling due to mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between different layers.

Comparison with previous studies

The obtained results of the present investigation are consistent with and extend several previously reported studies on generalized thermoelastic and photothermal wave propagation in porous and semiconductor media. Earlier, Biot1 introduced the concept of coupled thermoelasticity, which, while physically insightful, assumed infinite heat conduction speed due to the parabolic nature of Fourier’s law. The current results clearly demonstrate that the classical coupled theory (CD) produces unphysical instantaneous propagation, whereas the Lord–Shulman (L–S) and Green–Lindsay (G–L) models yield finite and realistic wave speeds, thus resolving the non-causality issue highlighted in Biot’s formulation.

The influence of relaxation times, as implemented in the generalized models, aligns with the theoretical predictions of Lord and Shulman2 and Green and Lindsay3, who established the foundations for finite thermal wave propagation. The present results confirm that the inclusion of one or two relaxation parameters successfully reduces the overshoot and oscillatory tendencies of thermal and stress responses, as also verified in the investigations by Goswami and Sarkar28 and Prasad29. The damping of displacement and temperature profiles under the Green–Lindsay model observed here is particularly consistent with the analytical findings of Fernández and Quintanilla30, who emphasized that multiple relaxation mechanisms enhance the system’s thermal stability.

Regarding the double-porosity structure, the current analysis substantiates and expands upon the framework introduced by Ieşan and Quintanilla20, who developed the thermomechanical model for materials with two distinct pore systems. The present study confirms that the exchange of energy and mass between the pore networks produces additional coupling effects that substantially modify stress and temperature distributions. Similar trends were observed in the dynamic analyses by Yahya and Abd-Alla and Salah et al.22,23, where porosity parameters were found to alter the amplitude and attenuation of thermoelastic waves. The current work, however, goes beyond these studies by including gravitational and photothermal effects simultaneously an interaction not previously explored in double-porous semiconductor frameworks.

The effect of gravity on the thermoelastic field distributions observed in this work agrees qualitatively with the results of Bayones et al.25, Ramadan et al.26, and Kadian et al.27, who showed that gravitational forces reduce thermal wave speeds and amplify internal stresses due to body force interactions. The current findings further illustrate that gravity enhances energy dissipation and reduces displacement amplitudes, confirming its damping role in thermoelastic propagation. This alignment underscores the necessity of considering gravitational effects in both terrestrial and geophysical thermoelastic models.

From a photothermal standpoint, the behavior of the temperature and carrier density distributions under photothermal excitation is in strong agreement with the photoacoustic and photothermal analyses of Gordon et al.9, Lotfy and Tantawi16, and Lotfy et al.17,18. In particular, the present results verify that increasing thermoelectric coupling enhances the wave amplitude and produces secondary oscillations a hallmark of photo-induced thermoelastic interaction also reported by Lotfy et al.59 in their studies on semiconductor materials under laser illumination. The inclusion of two-temperature and dual-phase-lag models in related works16–18 also supports the conclusion that thermal relaxation and coupling parameters are crucial in controlling wave attenuation and phase velocity, which is consistent with the current findings under the L–S and G–L frameworks.

Furthermore, the present study confirms and extends the nonlocal and fractional thermoelastic results of Kumar and Paswan24, who analyzed the reflection of waves in fractional double-porosity media, and the stochastic magneto-photothermal analyses by Lotfy et al.14,60, where the interaction between magnetic, photothermal, and stochastic processes was shown to significantly modify field distributions. The consistency of the present deterministic model with those stochastic and fractional approaches emphasizes the robustness of the generalized thermoelastic framework in capturing realistic material responses across different theoretical formulations.

Overall, the present model not only validates the results obtained by previous researchers but also introduces new physical insights by simultaneously accounting for gravity, photothermal excitation, and double porosity within the Lord–Shulman and Green–Lindsay theories. This combination provides a more comprehensive description of semiconductor thermoelastic behavior, capturing both finite wave speeds and multiscale porosity interactions. Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive theoretical and numerical analysis of photo-thermoelastic wave propagation in a semiconducting medium characterized by double porosity, gravitational influence, and photothermal excitation within the frameworks of the Lord–Shulman (L–S) and Green–Lindsay (G–L) generalized thermoelastic theories. The governing equations were formulated by coupling the thermal, elastic, and carrier-density fields, incorporating the effects of energy exchange, relaxation processes, and gravitational body forces. The resulting coupled partial differential equations were solved using a normal-mode analysis, and the numerical computations were carried out using MATHEMATICA software to illustrate the spatial and temporal variations of the physical quantities.

The major findings of this investigation can be summarized as follows:The finite propagation speed of thermal waves was clearly established, validating the physical consistency of the generalized thermoelastic theories. The L–S and G–L models effectively eliminated the unrealistic infinite speeds predicted by the classical coupled theory.The thermoelastic and thermoelectric coupling parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon}_{3}$$\end{document}) were found to play a critical role in modifying the amplitudes of temperature, stress, and displacement fields. Their increase enhances the interaction between mechanical and thermal responses, indicating stronger energy conversion and more pronounced attenuation.Double-porosity effects significantly influence the propagation characteristics of thermoelastic waves. The presence of two distinct pore systems introduces additional relaxation channels, leading to higher damping rates and smoother stress distributions compared with single-porosity media.Gravitational forces exert a substantial damping influence on the displacement and stress components while intensifying the thermal gradients near the surface. This confirms gravity’s importance as a stabilizing mechanism in large-scale or geophysical thermoelastic problems.Comparative analysis among the CD, L–S, and G–L theories demonstrated that the G–L model yields the most stable and realistic results, characterized by smooth transient responses and balanced energy dissipation.Photothermal excitation generates strong coupling between the optical, electronic, and thermoelastic fields, resulting in photo-induced wave patterns consistent with experimental photoacoustic and semiconductor studies. The numerical simulations reveal the conversion of absorbed light energy into localized thermal and elastic perturbations.The 3D field visualizations confirmed the temporal attenuation and spatial diffusion of all physical quantities, illustrating how thermal and mechanical energy dissipate through the porous semiconductor matrix under short-pulse excitation.
