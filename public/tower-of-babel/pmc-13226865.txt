Asthma Medication Adherence and Exacerbations in Pregnancy: Bridging the Gap Between Evidence and Practice
Authors: Sun Young Paik, Sang-Heon Kim
Published: 2026

Asthma is a lifelong chronic disease, and its impact extends into pregnancy and the peripartum period. The prevalence of asthma among pregnant women is estimated to be 3%–12%, and despite regional variations, it is increasingly recognized as a growing global health concern.1 Pregnancy and asthma are closely intertwined, with each influencing the clinical course of the other. Pregnancy-induced physiological and immunological changes can destabilize asthma control and increase the risk of exacerbations.2 Compounding this issue, concerns over the safety of asthma medications for the fetus often lead to inappropriate discontinuation of maintenance therapy.

In this issue of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology Research, Lim et al.3 report findings from a nationwide analysis of National Health Insurance claims data, providing important insights into the relationship between asthma medication use during pregnancy and the pre- and postpartum periods and the risk of asthma exacerbations. One of the most notable findings is the strikingly high rate of treatment discontinuation during pregnancy (up to 83%), accompanied by reductions in inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use. These observations are consistent with previous reports from Korea and other countries,4,5,6,7,8 which have documented low use of ICS and other controller medications, as well as a progressive decline in asthma medication use across all trimesters of pregnancy. In addition to low adherence to asthma medications, this study also found that pregnant patients are prone to delay visits to clinics and medical evaluations by physicians for asthma. These observations indicate that patients may disengage from healthcare during pregnancy, including reduced clinic visits, ultimately resulting in decreased prescription and use of asthma medications. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether the low prescription rates reported by Lim et al. 3 are attributable to patient nonadherence, clinician prescribing behavior, or a combination of both. Overall, these findings reflect a persistent failure to translate guideline recommendations into clinical practice, with asthma medications frequently discontinued during pregnancy despite clear evidence supporting the safety of ICS and bronchodilators.9,10

Evidence regarding asthma control during pregnancy remains relatively limited. Earlier reports suggested that asthma symptoms may improve during pregnancy in a subset of patients.11 However, more recent studies indicate that improvement is uncommon, with most women experiencing either no change or worsening of asthma control during pregnancy.12,13 These findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of asthma in pregnancy and highlight the need for careful monitoring and individualized management throughout this period. While it is well established that nonadherence increases the risk of asthma exacerbations, a meta-analysis of previous studies has identified several additional risk factors during pregnancy, including increased maternal age, obesity, smoking, black ethnicity, multiparity, depression or anxiety, and severe asthma.14 Building on these known determinants, Lim et al. 3 further demonstrated that moderate-to-severe rhinitis is a key contributor to exacerbation risk, and that a history of severe exacerbations within the preceding 4 years also represents an important predictor during pregnancy. They further report that the crude odds ratio for severe exacerbations over the 4 years before conception was higher than that for the preceding 12 months. However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as the 2 time windows are nested and not independently assessed within a mutually adjusted model. A larger crude odds ratio does not necessarily imply better predictive performance. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that a multi-year exacerbation history may offer additional clinical insight beyond the conventional 12-month window, and may justify extending preconception counselling accordingly.

Another noteworthy finding of this study concerns medication adherence and exacerbations in the postpartum period. Data on asthma medication use and disease control after delivery remain limited.15 In this study, the authors found that adherence to asthma medication did not return to pre-pregnancy levels, even among women who experienced frequent exacerbations during pregnancy. These findings indicate that gaps in asthma management persist beyond pregnancy, extending into the postpartum and lactation periods.

In interpreting and appraising the findings of this study, a few limitations should be acknowledged. First, the claims-based design limits the availability of important clinical variables, such as smoking, body mass index, lung function, and inflammatory biomarkers, and restricts the evaluation of asthma outcomes to healthcare utilization rather than symptom control. Second, because the study included only women who delivered, it does not provide information on the potential impact of uncontrolled asthma on earlier adverse perinatal outcomes, including miscarriage. Finally, as the analysis focused primarily on asthma exacerbations, other important consequences of uncontrolled asthma on maternal and fetal health—such as preeclampsia, preterm birth, gestational diabetes, fetal distress, and low birth weight—were not assessed.

Patient education is critical in the management of asthma during pregnancy and postpartum periods. It has been reported that concerns about the safety of asthma medication during pregnancy is the most important reason for nonadherence or discontinuation of asthma medication.16,17 To prevent inappropriate discontinuation of therapy, patients should be provided with clear, evidence-based information regarding the safety of asthma medications. In addition to well-established asthma medications, including ICS, there is an urgent need to establish robust safety data for newer agents, such as biologics. Recently, a 2025 international Delphi consensus involving 141 experts from 32 countries concluded that asthma biologics may be used during conception, throughout pregnancy, and during breastfeeding when clinically indicated, while emphasizing that international registries are essential to refine guidance.18 A global pharmacovigilance analysis of 15,715 pregnancy-related reports found that overall reporting of adverse events with biologics was not higher than with conventional therapies, but identified drug-class–specific signals that require structured confirmation: omalizumab, mepolizumab, and dupilumab showed increased reporting signals for spontaneous fetal death and spontaneous abortion in pharmacovigilance databases, whereas benralizumab showed the opposite pattern.19

Asthma care for women of reproductive age should span the entire reproductive period, from preconception planning through pregnancy and into the postpartum year, with active physician engagement throughout and risk-stratified follow-up after delivery. Thus, asthma action plans should be individualized and adapted across the 3 trimesters, as well as during the peripartum, postpartum, and lactation periods.20,21 Importantly, achieving optimal asthma care during pregnancy requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach, involving primary care physicians, asthma specialists, obstetricians, pharmacists, and nurses, to ensure continuity of care across the reproductive period.22 There is an urgent need to develop evidence-based recommendations for asthma management during pregnancy and to ensure their effective implementation within diverse healthcare systems. Ultimately, bridging these gaps across the life course will be essential to improving both maternal and fetal outcomes in women with asthma.
