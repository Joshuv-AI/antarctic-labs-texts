Developing a nomogram prediction model to enhance diagnostic accuracy of supplemental ultrasound post-negative mammography
Authors: Cheng Li, Yong Luo, Yan Jiang, Xumiao Wu, Qi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The effectiveness of mammography in women with dense breasts is compromised by a high rate of false-negative results. While supplemental ultrasound increases sensitivity, its low positive predictive value (PPV) leads to more unnecessary biopsies. This study aims to develop a nomogram model to predict the malignancy of breast masses that are additionally identified as suspicious by supplemental ultrasound after an initial negative screening mammography. The goal is to improve the PPV of supplemental ultrasound and potentially reduce unnecessary biopsies. In this study, eligible data were collected retrospectively and then randomized into training and validation sets. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator was used to identify the most important predictive variables in the training set. The maximum Youden index determined the optimal model threshold, and model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration curves, decision curve analyses, and metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value. The study included 425 breast masses, 345 benign and 80 malignant. These were divided into 298 for the training set and 127 for the validation set. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator identified the 5 most important predictive variables for the construction of the model. The model showed strong discrimination with area under the curve values of 0.91 (0.87–0.95) for the training set and 0.88 (0.81–0.96) for the validation set. Hosmer–Lemeshow tests indicated a good model fit, with P-values of 0.78 and 0.12 for the training and validation sets, respectively. In addition, decision curve analyses highlighted the clinical utility of the model. The model also showed commendable diagnostic performance in terms of sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value. The nomogram model significantly increased the PPV of supplemental ultrasound from 0.18 to 0.56 in the training set and from 0.21 to 0.56 in the validation set. This study successfully developed a nomogram model to predict the malignancy of suspicious breast masses additionally identified by supplemental ultrasound. The model shows robust performance and significantly improves the PPV of supplemental ultrasound, suggesting a promising way to reduce unnecessary biopsies in such cases.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women.[1] Mammography, the primary screening modality for breast cancer, has significantly reduced mortality and improved patient outcomes.[2,3] However, its effectiveness is limited in women with dense breast tissue, as evidenced by a high false-negative rate of 12.2% to 17.4%.[4–6] This limitation is attributed to the masking effect of dense tissue,[7,8] which obscures tumor visualization and detection, potentially delaying diagnosis.

In women with dense breasts and having negative screening mammography results, supplemental ultrasound can detect malignancies that may be obscured by dense tissue, increasing breast cancer detection sensitivity to 99% to 100%.[9,10] Ultrasound also offers advantages such as the absence of ionizing radiation, cost-effectiveness, and wide availability.[11,12]

However, the routine adoption of supplemental ultrasound remains controversial,[13] mainly due to its low positive predictive value (PPV). A systematic review indicated that while supplemental ultrasound led to 11.7 to 106.6 additional biopsies per 1000 examinations, it detected only 0.3 to 7.7 additional cancers.[12] This low PPV leads to more unnecessary biopsies, which can increase patient anxiety and healthcare costs. Therefore, there is a need to improve the PPV of supplemental ultrasound to reduce unnecessary biopsies. This study aims to improve the PPV of supplemental ultrasound by developing a model to predict the malignancy risk of breast masses that are additionally identified as suspicious by supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening. We hypothesize that the breast cancers yielding false-negative results in screening mammography have distinct ultrasound characteristics predictors for malignancy, which is different from the conventional ultrasound Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) criteria. By identifying these predictors, we can develop a model tailored to this specific subset of breast masses that can improve the PPV of supplemental ultrasound and potentially reduce unnecessary biopsies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data collection

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital (KY2021PJ255). In our institution, mammography is the primary screening method for breast cancer. For women with dense breasts (defined as mammographic breast density BI-RADS C or D) and negative results (mammographic BI-RADS 1–3), supplemental ultrasound is routinely recommended for further examination to detect potentially missed malignant masses. Biopsy procedures, either surgical or vacuum- assisted, are advised when BI-RADS category 4A or higher masses are detected to obtain definitive pathological diagnoses.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of a dataset of women who underwent opportunistic breast cancer screening at Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital (Eastern District) from January 2016 to January 2021. Inclusion criteria include: (1) screening at the institution between January 2016 and January 2021; (2) mammographic breast density classified as BI-RADS C or D; (3) breast masses classified as BI-RADS 1, 2, or 3 on mammography; (4) underwent supplemental ultrasound within 30 days; and (5) breast masses reclassified as BI-RADS 4 by supplemental ultrasound and biopsied within 60 days with definitive pathological diagnoses. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete or missing imaging data; (2) inconclusive pathology reports; (3) presence of multiple BI-RADS 4 masses, whether ipsilateral or contralateral; and (4) history of breast cancer or previous surgery at the mass site.

Clinical, imaging, and pathological data for breast masses meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were retrospectively reviewed and collected from the institutional medical database. For all reviews, the ACR BI-RADS 5th edition standard[14] was used to classify imaging characteristics of the breasts and masses. In cases of disagreement, a senior sonographer, or radiologist was consulted. The reviewers were blinded to the pathology reports. Pathological classification followed the World Health Organization classification of breast tumors, 4th edition.[15] Ultrasound characteristics of calcifications were categorized as no calcification, microcalcification (<0.5 mm in diameter), or macrocalcification (≥0.5 mm in diameter).[16] Mammographic breast density was categorized as non-dense (categories A and B) and dense (categories C and D). Breast mass size was determined by the largest diameter observed in ultrasound.

2.2. Model construction

The collected data were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set in a 2:1 ratio. The pathological diagnosis of the masses served as the dependent variable. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm[17] was used to identify the most predictive variables within the training set. This method progressively reduces the coefficients of variables toward zero as the tuning parameter lambda (λ) increases, prioritizing variables based on their predictive ability. The goodness of fit of the model was evaluated using binomial deviance, calculated by 10-fold cross-validation over different λ values. The optimal λ was chosen based on the 1 standard error (1-SE) criterion,[18] which selects a λ where the model’s average binomial deviation is within 1-SE of its minimum. This approach strikes a balance between model complexity and accuracy. Variables with nonzero coefficients at this optimal λ were included in a logistic regression model, and a nomogram based on this model was developed[19] for practicality and interpretability. The model’s optimal threshold was determined by the maximum Youden index, calculated as sensitivity + specificity − 1. Breast masses with model-predicted probabilities above this threshold were classified as “high risk” for malignancy, while those below were considered “low risk.”

2.3. Model validation

The discriminative ability of the model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) as measures. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Calibration curves compared the model’s predicted probabilities with observed outcomes, and a P-value > .05 in the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a reliable calibration. The diagnostic performance of the model at the optimal threshold was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value (NPV). The clinical utility of the model at this threshold was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA),[20] which weighs the net benefit of correctly identifying malignant cases against the harms of false positives. DCA curves were plotted against strategies of biopsying all masses (“treat-all” strategy) and biopsying none (“treat-none” strategy). A model that demonstrated a higher net benefit than these strategies was considered clinically useful.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.1.1). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the t test, whereas categorical variables were compared using either the chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher exact test, depending on the data characteristics. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. The LASSO algorithm and logistic regression model were implemented using the “glmnet” package.[21] The nomogram and calibration curves were constructed using the “rms” package.[22] ROC curves and their AUC values were calculated using the “pROC” package.[23] DCA was performed with the “rmda” package.[24] The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was performed with the “ResourceSelection” package,[25] and the comparative analysis was performed with the “compareGroups” package.[26] Descriptive statistics of the logistic regression model were computed using the “jtools” package.[27]

The maximum Youden index of the model was 0.69 in the training set, corresponding to a threshold of 0.25. Breast masses with probabilities above this threshold were classified as “high risk” for malignancy. The diagnostic performance of the model at this threshold is detailed in Table 4. The PPV of supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening was relatively low at 0.18 (53/298) in the training set and 0.21 (27/127) in the validation set. However, our model significantly improved these values to 0.56 (95% CI: 0.48–0.65) in the training set and 0.56 (95% CI: 0.44–0.70) in the validation set. In addition, the NPVs of the model were high at 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98) in the training set and 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88–0.97) in the validation set. The sensitivity and specificity of the model were 0.83 (0.74–0.93) and 0.86 (0.81–0.90), respectively, in the training set and 0.74 (0.56–0.90) and 0.84 (0.76–0.91), respectively, in the validation set. These results further confirm the effectiveness of the model in accurately differentiating malignant from benign masses.

In summary, a nomogram predictive model was developed to predict the malignancy risk of breast masses that are additionally identified as suspicious by supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening. The model showed strong discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and predictive accuracy. In addition, the threshold for distinguishing between high and low risk of malignancy was determined. This threshold significantly increased the PPV of supplemental ultrasound while maintaining high NPV.

4. Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed a nomogram predictive model by integrating 5 significant ultrasound variables to predict the malignancy risk of breast masses identified as suspicious by supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening. The model demonstrated robust discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and predictive accuracy.

Previous studies have shown that the PPV of supplemental ultrasound is relatively low, typically ranging from 0.07 to 0.25.[28–30] In our study, the PPV of supplemental ultrasound was 0.18 in the training set and 0.21 in the validation set, which is consistent with these previous findings. However, by focusing only on biopsying masses identified as high risk by our model, the PPV of supplemental ultrasound significantly improved to 0.56 (95% CI: 0.48–0.65) in the training set and 0.56 (95% CI: 0.44–0.70) in the validation set. Furthermore, our model’s prediction of low risk for certain breast masses yielded an impressively high NPV of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98) in the training set and 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88–0.97) in the validation set. This suggests a small increase in the risk of missing malignant cases in model-predicted low-risk masses while avoiding unnecessary interventions in benign cases. The balance of high PPV and NPV underscores the potential of our model to optimize supplemental ultrasound in breast cancer screening protocols. This advancement offers a path toward more personalized, efficient strategies, better patient outcomes, and a reduction in the burden of unnecessary medical procedures.

To our knowledge, this is the first predictive model for this specific subset of breast masses. Previous studies have explored methods to increase the PPV of supplemental ultrasound in reducing unnecessary biopsies. For example, Kim et al[31] proposed downgrade criteria for supplemental screening ultrasound. In their study, breast masses with certain characteristics were classified as low risk for malignancy to avoid biopsy, thus increasing the PPV of supplemental ultrasound. However, their approach did not result in a predictive model or a clinically practical nomogram. Another study by Yang et al[32] developed a nomogram predictive model for ultrasound BI-RADS 4A breast masses detected by combined mammography and ultrasound screening. However, the screening protocol and target population in their study differed from ours.

Our study identified a set of ultrasound characteristics: mass shape, margin, calcification, vascularity, and size, as significant predictors of malignancy in breast masses additionally detected by supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening. This is consistent with our hypothesis that the breast masses yielding false-negative mammography results have a distinct set of ultrasound characteristics predictive of malignancy that differs from the conventional ultrasound BI-RADS criteria. We speculate that the ultrasound characteristics identified by our model that are more predictive in this specific subset of breast masses are easily obscured by dense breast tissue (such as shape, margin, calcification) or are not visible on mammography (such as vascularity). In addition, mass size has been identified as a significant predictor of false-negative mammography results in previous studies.[33,34] We speculate that the relevance of mass size may be due to the effect of breast density, which can obscure larger masses and is associated with an increased risk of malignancy.[35–37]

However, our study is not without limitations. First, its retrospective nature may introduce selection bias. For example, there is a potential bias from excluding patients who had dense breasts but did not undergo supplemental ultrasound following initial negative mammography screening (211/3248, 6.5%), as well as those who had BI-RADS 4 in supplemental ultrasound but did not undergo biopsy (21/499, 4.2%). Although the number of such cases was relatively small, their exclusion may have affected our results. In addition, not tracking follow-up data for masses classified as BI-RADS categories 1 to 3 by supplemental ultrasound may result in missed malignancies. Second, the relatively small sample size may introduce a risk of overfitting. However, we mitigated this risk by using LASSO to select the most relevant predictive variables and the 1-SE criterion to determine the optimal λ value. Third, the lack of external validation limits the generalizability of our model. Future studies should aim at external validation to improve the applicability of the model. Fourth, the subjective nature of manually classifying breast density and mass characteristics could lead to inter-examiner variability. Future research should investigate more consistent, objective image analysis methods, such as radiomics. Finally, the performance of the model may be affected by the lack of information on patients’ family history of breast cancer and BRCA mutations. The inclusion of these variables in future studies could potentially improve the predictive accuracy of the model.
