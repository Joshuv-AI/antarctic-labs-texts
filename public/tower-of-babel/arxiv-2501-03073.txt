Retrieval-Augmented TLAPS Proof Generation with Large Language Models
Yuhao Zhou

Abstract
We present a novel approach to automated proof generation for the TLA+ Proof System (TLAPS) using Large Language Models (LLMs). Our method combines two key components: a sub-proof obligation generation phase that breaks down complex proof obligations into simpler sub-obligations, and a proof generation phase that leverages Retrieval-Augmented Generation with verified proof examples. We evaluate our approach using proof obligations from varying complexity levels of proof obligations, spanning from fundamental arithmetic properties to the properties of algorithms. Our experiments demonstrate that while the method successfully generates valid proofs for intermediate-complexity obligations, it faces limitations with more complex theorems. These results indicate that our approach can effectively assist in proof development for certain classes of properties, contributing to the broader goal of integrating LLMs into formal verification workflows.

Introduction

Formal verification plays a crucial role in ensuring the correctness of critical systems, particularly distributed systems where subtle errors can have severe consequences. The TLA+ language
[
14
]
has emerged as a powerful framework for specifying such systems, providing mathematical precision in system modeling. Its proof system, TLAPS, enables formal verification of system properties through machine-checkable proofs. However, constructing these proofs demands significant expertise and manual effort, creating a bottleneck in the verification process.

The challenges of proof construction are particularly evident in verifying complex distributed algorithms. Even for experienced users, developing TLAPS proofs requires careful reasoning about system properties, proper structuring of proof steps, and deep understanding of the underlying mathematical concepts. This complexity often leads to proofs becoming a major time investment in the verification workflow.

Recent developments in Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened new possibilities for automating formal reasoning tasks. Notable success has been achieved in generating proofs for tactic-based interactive theorem provers like Coq
[
24
]
and Lean
[
16
]
, where proof construction relies on tactical proof styles
[
28
,
23
]
. However, TLAPS employs a distinct, hierarchical proof style that emphasizes structured decomposition rather than tactic sequences, presenting distinct challenges for proof automation.

We address these challenges through a novel approach to automated TLAPS proof generation. Our method takes as input a given proof obligation and its associated definitions (Figure
1
) and generates as output a complete, TLAPS-verifiable proof (Figure
2
). The key insight of our approach is combining two complementary strategies: systematic decomposition of complex proof obligations into manageable sub-obligations, and retrieval-augmented generation that leverages existing verified proofs to guide the construction of new ones.

To evaluate our approach, we conducted experiments using the Boyer-Moore Majority Vote algorithm
[
4
]
as a case study. Our results demonstrate successful proof generation for intermediate-complexity properties, while also revealing current limitations with more complex theorems. This evaluation provides insights into both the potential and current limitations of LLM-based proof automation in TLAPS.

Our work makes the following contributions:
(1) a novel proof generation method combining obligation decomposition with retrieval-augmented generation,
(2) practical implementation producing TLAPS-verifiable proofs,
(3) experimental evaluation revealing both capabilities and limitations of LLM-based TLAPS proof generation.

Preliminaries and Problem Statement

TLA+ and TLA+ Proof System:

TLA+ (Temporal Logic of Actions)
[
14
]
is a formal specification language designed for modeling concurrent and distributed systems. It is used to specify system properties, which can then be verified against the system model using model checkers such as TLC
[
30
]
.

The TLA+ Proof System (TLAPS)
[
6
]
is a software tool for mechanically checking proofs of TLA+ specifications. A proof that follows TLAPS’s syntactic requirements is referred to as a
TLAPS proof
throughout this paper.
TLAPS integrates with automated theorem provers (e.g., Z3
[
9
]
, and Zenon
[
3
]
) and interactive proof assistants (e.g., Isabelle
[
18
]
) to discharge proof obligations, enabling the mechanical verification of complex properties. Its modular architecture supports a stepwise refinement approach, facilitating the verification of high-level system properties down to low-level implementation details.

While TLAPS offers a powerful framework for formal verification, the proof process often requires substantial manual effort to construct intermediate lemmas and guide proof steps, which can become a significant bottleneck. This is particularly evident in synthesis tasks
[
21
]
, where the manual effort required to construct proofs for synthesized candidates can be prohibitively high.
Our tool aims to automate the generation of TLAPS proofs for given
goal proof obligations
and associated definitions. We consider a TLAPS proof
valid
if and only if TLAPS can mechanically verify the proof.

Large Language Models:

Large Language Models (LLMs) are machine learning models trained on extensive text corpora to perform natural language understanding and generative tasks, achieving state-of-the-art performance in various language processing tasks, including text generation, summarization, and question answering
[
19
]
. Through pre-training on large-scale datasets
[
11
]
, LLMs learn a broad spectrum of language patterns, contextual relationships, and semantics.

A key feature of LLMs is their ability to perform
in-context learning

[
10
]
, where the model generates outputs by conditioning on a set of examples provided directly in the input prompt, without requiring any changes to the model’s internal parameters. A specific application of in-context learning is
few-shot prompting

[
32
]
, where the model is supplied with only a few examples or demonstrations to infer and perform the desired task. Few-shot prompting has demonstrated effective results across various tasks by enabling the model to generalize from limited examples.

Recent research has investigated the use of LLMs to automate aspects of formal verification, such as generating proofs or suggesting lemmas in formal provers
[
28
,
23
]
. LLMs can learn from large datasets of formal proofs and natural language descriptions to generate plausible proof steps, potentially reducing the manual effort involved in constructing formal proofs. However, LLMs have limitations in this context: the syntactical and semantic correctness of generated proofs are not guaranteed, and the reasoning capabilities of LLMs are limited compared to human experts.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation:

Retrieval-Augmented Generation
[
12
]
(RAG) is a framework that enhances the performance of LLMs by incorporating an external retrieval mechanism. RAG dynamically fetches relevant information from an external knowledge base, which provides contextually relevant data to the LLM. This augmented information is then used to condition the model’s output, generating more accurate and contextually grounded proof steps.

In the context of proof generation, RAG helps address some of the challenges faced by LLMs by ensuring that the generated proof steps are informed by external sources, such as a library of proved lemmas
[
28
]
. This approach improves the accuracy of proof steps and aligns them with verified knowledge. Additionally, RAG can dynamically adapt to new information and domain-specific constraints, making it particularly suitable for assisting in the automated generation of complex proofs.

TLAPS Proof Generation Problem:

The problem we consider in this paper is the following:
given a goal proof obligation
{G}
specified in TLA+, together with all associated definitions required for
{G}
, generate a proof that follows TLAPS syntax and can be mechanically verified by TLAPS to establish
{G}
. Figures
1
and
2
illustrate an example input and its corresponding valid proof.

3
Our Approach

LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across various domains, yet their application to formal theorem proving presents challenges. Our experimental results (Section
4
) reveal that LLMs, even when enhanced with advanced prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thoughts (CoT)
[
25
]
, struggle to effectively handle complex TLAPS proof obligations and definitions.

Through analysis of existing TLAPS proofs, we observed that manual proof development typically follows a top-down approach, where complex proof obligations are systematically decomposed into simpler, more manageable sub-obligations. These resulting sub-proofs are generally concise and straightforward to verify. Figure
2
illustrates this decomposition strategy with a simple example. While this particular example could be proved directly using SMT solvers, it serves to demonstrate the hierarchical structure of TLAPS proof development.

Our observations revealed two key insights: First, LLMs demonstrate better reasoning capabilities when handling simpler, focused proof obligations, suggesting that the proof obligation decomposition could enhance the effectiveness of LLM-based proof generation. Second, sufficiently simple sub-obligations are often easier to be proved by automated theorem provers like SMT solvers, providing an efficient path to verification once proper decomposition is achieved.

Based on these observations, we propose a two-phase approach to TLAPS proof generation: (1)
sub-proof obligation generation
, which decomposes complex proof obligations into simpler ones, and (2)
simple proof generation
, which constructs TLAPS proofs for these decomposed obligations. The following subsection opens with an overview of our system architecture and then provides an in-depth discussion of each component.

3.1
System Overview

Our system takes as input a goal proof obligation
{G}
and its associated definitions in TLA+ syntax, and aims to output a valid TLAPS proof to establish
{G}
.
The system consists of two main components designed to leverage LLMs’ capabilities while managing their limitations in complex reasoning tasks. The first component, sub-proof obligation generation, systematically breaks down complex proof obligations into a collection of simpler sub-proof obligations that are more amenable to LLM processing. The second component, simple proof generation, focuses on constructing valid proofs for these sub-proof obligations. Here,
simple
implies that the proof of the input obligation is assumed to be straightforward. Therefore, this component focuses on generating simple and concise proofs.

This decomposition strategy enables effective proof generation by aligning with LLMs’ strengths in handling simple, focused, self-contained reasoning tasks while mitigating their limitations in managing complex, multi-step logical arguments. By breaking down complex proofs into structured sequences of simpler sub-proof obligations, we achieve more reliable automated proof generation.

As illustrated in Figure
3
, our system operates iteratively through two main components, plus integrated verification mechanisms at key points.

Figure 3
:
System Overview

•

\mathit{Obl}
and attempts to generate a set of simpler sub-proof obligations
\{\mathit{Obl}_{1},\mathit{Obl}_{2},\dots\}
that, collectively, are sufficient to prove the original obligation
\mathit{Obl}

for subsequent processing (see Section

).

•

Simple Proof Generation (Step 3):

For each sub-proof obligation
\mathit{Obl}_{i}
in
S_{\mathit{Obl}}

\mathit{Prf}_{i}
. These proofs are collected in set
S_{\mathit{Prf}}
for verification (see Section
3.3
).

•

Verification Mechanisms:

TLAPS performs two critical verification steps:

–

Obligation Decomposition Check (Step 2):
Verifies that the conjunction of all sub-obligations implies the original goal:
\bigwedge_{i}\mathit{Obl}_{i}\implies\mathit{Obl}
.

–

Proof Step Check (Step 4):
Verifies each generated proof
\mathit{Prf}_{i}
against its corresponding obligation
\mathit{Obl}_{i}
.

If the obligation decomposition check fails, the system initiates the generation of new sub-obligations. If TLAPS is unable to verify any
\mathit{Prf}_{i}
for a given
\mathit{Obl}_{i}
, the system deems the sub-obligation
\mathit{Obl}_{i}
too complex and proceeds to further decompose it. In practice, our implementation generates multiple candidate proofs for each sub-obligation in parallel to increase the chance of finding a valid proof before resorting to further decomposition.
This iterative process continues until either a complete TLAPS proof of the goal obligation
{G}
is found, or the number of decomposition attempts exceeds a predefined threshold.
Importantly, our system is designed specifically to generate proofs that can be mechanically verified by TLAPS. It does not attempt to falsify proof obligations or generate counterexamples - tasks that are typically handled by the TLC model checker in practice. The details of each component and their interactions are presented in the following subsections.

Example

Figure
2
illustrates a complete proof generated by our system for the theorem
Even(x+x)
. This example demonstrates the iterative interaction between our system’s components and TLAPS’s verification mechanisms.

Taking advantage of TLAPS’s support for top-down proof development, our system first proposes a high-level proof structure. The Sub-proof Obligation Generation component (Step 1) decomposes the theorem
Even(x+x)
into two initial sub-obligations:
<1>1
to prove
x + x = 2 * x
, and
<1>2
to establish
Even(2 * x)
. TLAPS then verifies (Step 2) that these sub-obligations would be sufficient to prove the original theorem, checking the validity of
QED BY <1>1, <1>2 DEF Even
before the actual proofs of
<1>1
and
<1>2
are generated.

After confirming the validity of this decomposition, the Simple Proof Generation component (Step 3) proceeds to generate proofs for the individual sub-obligations. For
<1>1
, it generates a proof using
OBVIOUS
, which TLAPS successfully verifies. The term
OBVIOUS
indicates that TLAPS can independently prove the obligation without delegating it to provers such as SMT solvers or Isabelle. However, the proof attempt for
<1>2
fails verification (Step 4), triggering another decomposition cycle. The system decomposes
<1>2
into two simpler sub-obligations:
<2>1
establishing that
2 * x \in Nat
, and
<2>2
proving that
(2 * x) % 2 = 0
. Again, TLAPS first verifies that these sub-obligations suffice to prove
<1>2
through
QED BY <2>1, <2>2 DEF Even
.

For these simpler sub-obligations
<2>1
and
<2>2
, the Simple Proof Generation component successfully generates proofs using
OBVIOUS
, which TLAPS verifies. With verified proofs for all sub-obligations, our system constructs the complete hierarchical proof structure shown in the figure.

3.3
Simple Proof Generation

Figure 6
:
Retrieval-Augmented Prompt for Simple Proof Generation

The Simple Proof Generation component focuses on producing valid proofs for the sub-obligations generated in the previous step. While these sub-obligations are simpler than the original proof obligation, generating valid proofs for TLAPS remains challenging. Our initial experiments revealed that LLMs often produce syntactically invalid proofs that fail TLAPS parsing, likely because their training data lacks sufficient TLAPS-specific examples.

To address this challenge, we employ a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approach that utilizes a database of verified TLAPS proofs to guide LLM-based proof generation. As illustrated in Figure
6
, our approach consists of three main steps: retrieving similar proof statements from a proof database, synthesizing a prompt with these examples, and generating candidate proofs using an LLM.

Construction and Organization of Proof Database

Our proof database consists of individual proof statements extracted from verified TLAPS specifications in the TLA+ Examples repository
[
2
]
. A
proof statement
refers to the text between two step numbers in a TLAPS proof script, representing a single logical step or assertion. For instance, the statement
\A x \in Nat : x + x = 2 * x OBVIOUS
, taken from the TLAPS proof in Figure
2
, is a proof statement because it appears between the step numbers
<1>1
and
<1>2
.

This sentence-level granularity allows our retrieval system to match similar proof patterns at the appropriate level of detail. By utilizing the extensive collection of verified proofs from the TLA+ Examples repository, we ensure our database contains a diverse set of proof patterns and techniques commonly used in TLAPS proofs.

Retrieval of Similar Proof Statements

Given a target proof obligation
\mathit{Obl}
, our retrieval process identifies similar proof statements from the database
\mathcal{D}
through semantic similarity matching:

An embedding function
f
maps each proof statement (text) to a vector in an
n
-dimensional space. For the target obligation
\mathit{Obl}
and each statement
{Q}_{i}
in the database
\mathcal{D}
, we compute embeddings
\mathbf{v}_{\mathit{Obl}}=f(\mathit{Obl})
and
\mathbf{v}_{{Q}_{i}}=f({Q}_{i})
. In our implementation, we leverage pre-trained embeddings to generate these vector representations, with detailed information about the specific model provided in Section
4
. Using cosine similarity:

\text{Sim}(\mathbf{v}_{\mathit{Obl}},\mathbf{v}_{{Q}_{i}})=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\mathit{Obl}}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{{Q}_{i}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{\mathit{Obl}}\|\|\mathbf{v}_{{Q}_{i}}\|},

we form our
reference set

\mathcal{R}_{k}
from the
k
proof statements with highest similarity scores.

Proof Generation and Verification

Using the retrieved reference set
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, we construct a prompt (shown in Figure
7
) that guides the LLMs through the proof generation process. Our implementation generates multiple candidate proofs in parallel to increase the likelihood of finding a valid proof. If no generated proof can be verified by TLAPS, we consider the obligation too complex and return it for further decomposition.

Heuristics for Leveraging Automated Provers

Our implementation employs a staged heuristic approach to proof generation, prioritizing TLAPS’s built-in automated proving capabilities before engaging more complex LLM-based generation. For each sub-obligation, the system follows a three-tiered strategy:

First, the system attempts to verify the sub-obligation using
OBVIOUS
, TLAPS’s most basic proof tactic. If successful, this provides the simplest possible proof. When
OBVIOUS
fails, the system escalates to
BY AllProvers
, invoking TLAPS’s full suite of automated provers including SMT solvers and Isabelle automation.

Only when both automated approaches fail does the system proceed with LLM-based proof generation. This hierarchical strategy significantly improves efficiency by reserving LLM-based proof generation for obligations that truly require sophisticated reasoning, while leveraging TLAPS’s native capabilities for simpler cases.

4
Evaluation

We evaluated our approach through experiments with proving the Boyer-Moore Majority Vote algorithm and a basic arithmetic property, focusing on both the system’s overall and individual components’ effectiveness.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Case Study Selection

We evaluated our approach using three proof obligations drawn from the official TLAPS example repository
[
2
]
. The main proof obligation is based on the Boyer-Moore Majority Vote algorithm
[
4
]
, which involves verifying a safety property using the inductive invariant method
[
15
]
. This obligation is particularly suitable for evaluating proof generation capabilities due to its blend of arithmetic reasoning and logical inference. To focus exclusively on the proof generation process, we included the inductive invariant as part of the input definitions. Both the main proof obligation and its definitions are available in the repository
[
2
]
.
In addition, we evaluated a hand-crafted, intermediate-complexity obligation,
Init => cnt <= OccurrencesBefore(cand, i)
, derived from the same algorithm. This obligation, being simpler than the main proof obligation, is specifically designed to assess the effectiveness of individual components of the proposed method.
Finally, as the simplest case, we evaluated an arithmetic property used as the illustrative example shown in Figure
1
. These proof obligations were carefully chosen to span a range of complexity levels, providing a comprehensive assessment of our system’s performance across diverse proof scenarios.

To ensure data integrity and prevent leakage, we ensured that the proof database used for RAG excluded all proofs contained in the evaluation set.

Methods Compared and Implementation

Our evaluation compared three categories of proof generation approaches. First, we established a baseline using TLAPS’s built-in automation, specifically employing SMT solvers (
BY SMT
) and Isabelle automation (
BY Isa
).

Next, we evaluated direct proof generation using state-of-the-art LLMs, including GPT-4o, o1-mini, and o1, with minimal prompting. Additionally, we tested advanced prompting techniques, such as Chain-of-Thoughts (CoT)
[
25
]
and Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT)
[
29
]
, with GPT-4o. Detailed prompts are provided in Appendix
0.A
. The o1 series models were excluded from advanced prompting evaluations as they incorporate such techniques internally.

Finally, we assessed our proposed method, which utilizes GPT-4o in Sub-proof Obligation Generation and Simple Proof Generation components, implementing the RAG by using
nomic-embed-text-v1.5

[
17
]
as embedding model and
chromadb

[
1
]
for similarity search.

All experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X CPU, 96GB of RAM.

Generated Proofs Validity

For the main proof obligation of the majority vote algorithm’s safety property, no method generated a valid proof. The TLAPS built-in automation (both automated theorem provers and Isabelle) failed to prove the goal obligation within seconds, despite being allowed a 10-minute timeout period. All tested LLMs (GPT-4o, o1-mini, and o1) with minimal prompting produced syntactically invalid proofs, exhibiting issues from using unsupported tactics to missing step numbers. Despite ten attempts per model, none generated a valid proof. Advanced prompting techniques with GPT-4o, while yielding more structured outputs, similarly failed to produce syntactically valid proofs across ten attempts. Our proposed method also failed, but in a different way: the sub-proof obligation generation component could not produce valid decompositions of the goal obligation that passed the Obligation Decomposition Check, despite ten attempted decompositions.

For the intermediate-complexity obligation, while TLAPS built-in automation failed and all basic LLM approaches failed to generate valid proofs within ten attempts, our method demonstrated consistent success. In all three independent attempts, our method generated valid proofs verified by TLAPS, with one representative proof shown in Figure
8
. Significantly, every decomposition proposed by the sub-proof obligation generation component passed the Obligation Decomposition Check across all attempts, demonstrating our method’s ability to consistently generate logically sound proof structures.

For the simple arithmetic property (Figure
1
), TLAPS built-in automation proved it directly. Although all LLM approaches provided correct logical reasoning, they failed to generate syntactically valid proofs. Our method successfully generated a valid proof (Figure
2
).

Execution Time

For the main proof obligation, although no method generated a valid proof, their runtime characteristics provide valuable insights. The TLAPS built-in automation terminated within seconds despite the 10-minute timeout allowance. Among the LLM approaches, both with minimal and advanced prompting, GPT-4o completed each attempt in 10-15 seconds, while o1 and o1-mini exhibited more variable performance, requiring between 20-60 seconds per attempt. Our method’s runtime was dominated by two components: 10-15 seconds for each sub-proof obligation generation attempt, followed by 5-10 seconds for the Obligation Decomposition Check, which consistently failed in this experiment.

For the intermediate-complexity obligation, our method required approximately two minutes to generate a valid proof, while other approaches exhibited similar failure patterns and timing characteristics as in the main proof obligation. The arithmetic property demonstrated a wider performance range: TLAPS built-in automation proved it in under one second, LLM approaches maintained their model-specific timing patterns across both prompting strategies while failing to generate valid proofs, and our method successfully generated a valid proof in one minute.

Generated Proofs Quality

We exclude TLAPS’s built-in automation from our quality analysis since it operates fundamentally differently from other proof generation methods. While it can verify theorems automatically, it is designed as an automated theorem prover that determines validity without producing proof scripts. When successful, it confirms that a theorem is true but does not generate the step-by-step proof structure that our analysis aims to evaluate.
For the main proof obligation, while no method succeeded, our approach achieved better syntactic accuracy by focusing on individual sub-obligations rather than complete proofs. This decomposition-based approach particularly helped maintain syntactic correctness for simple proof steps.

For the intermediate-complexity obligation, our method generated a valid but verbose proof with some unnecessary definition expansions compared to human-written proofs. Other LLM approaches produced proofs that remained invalid even after manual syntax corrections. Despite its verbosity, our method’s proof maintained correctness and readability.

For the arithmetic property, while all LLM approaches showed improved syntax compared to their attempts on complex obligations, only our method achieved TLAPS-verified proofs.

Effectiveness of Components in the Proposed Method

For the main proof obligation, the sub-proof obligation generation component failed during the initial decomposition, as it was unable to produce sub-obligations that satisfied the Obligation Decomposition Check. This failure prevented a meaningful evaluation of the subsequent proof generation steps.

The intermediate-complexity obligation better demonstrated our system’s capabilities. The sub-proof obligation generation component successfully produced decompositions up to four levels deep, while the simple proof generation component effectively proved these sub-obligations. While the RAG process provided useful premises selection guidance and syntactical hints, the proof generation relied significantly on the heuristics.

For the arithmetic property, our method demonstrated effective decomposition by breaking down a goal not directly provable by
OBVIOUS
into sub-obligations that were
OBVIOUS
-provable, showcasing the effectiveness of our decomposition strategy.

Discussion

Our experiments highlight both challenges and opportunities in LLM-based TLAPS proof generation. We hypothesize that the primary limitation lies in the lack of TLAPS-specific training data available to LLMs. This likely affects the performance of all LLM-based approaches, from basic prompting to our more sophisticated method, with the impact being particularly noticeable in complex proofs.

However, our method’s success with intermediate-complexity obligations demonstrates the potential for automating portions of proof development in TLAPS. Future work could explore enhanced decomposition strategies and specialized training approaches to handle a broader range of proof obligations.

Related Work

Recent years have seen significant advances in applying LLMs to formal reasoning tasks. In the domain of theorem proving, LeanDojo
[
28

has showed the use of language models and RAG for generating proof tactics and selecting premises in the Lean theorem prover, providing both tactical suggestions and a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating LLMs on formal proof tasks. CORPA
[

extended the use of LLMs to both Coq and Lean provers, demonstrating how in-context learning can improve proof generation by learning from existing examples.

Beyond pure theorem proving, LLMs have shown promise in software verification tasks. Clover

leverages LLMs to generate Dafny code and annotations, while Chakraborty et al.
[

apply them to the challenging task of loop invariant generation. Wen et al.
[

combine LLMs with static analysis tools for program specification synthesis, and the Lemur system
[
27

demonstrates how LLMs can enhance traditional program verification frameworks.

Research has also explored LLMs’ capabilities in general reasoning tasks
[
13
,
31
]
and the crucial role of prompt engineering in formal methods applications
[
7
,
8
]
. Techniques such as in-context learning
[
10
,
20
]
and dynamic prompt adjustment
[
25
,
29
]
have proven particularly effective in improving LLMs’ performance. These advances in prompt engineering have been essential for addressing the inherent challenges of using LLMs in domains requiring precise logical reasoning and formal correctness.

Our work builds upon these foundations while addressing the unique challenges of generating valid TLAPS proofs. Unlike previous work focusing on tactic-based provers like Lean and Coq, we tackle TLAPS’s declarative proof style through a novel combination of proof obligation decomposition and retrieval-augmented generation. This approach bridges the gap between LLMs’ natural language capabilities and the formal requirements of TLAPS proof verification.

6
Conclusions

This paper presented an approach to automated proof generation in TLAPS using LLMs. Our method addresses the challenge of generating valid proofs through two key components: a decomposition-based strategy that breaks down complex proof obligations into simpler ones, and a retrieval-augmented generation approach that leverages existing verified proofs to guide proof construction.
Our experimental evaluation on the Boyer-Moore Majority Vote algorithm revealed both the potential and current limitations of LLM-based proof generation. While our method successfully generated valid proofs for simpler obligations, it struggled with more complex proofs, primarily due to limitations in decomposing complex proof obligations.

Future work could explore more sophisticated decomposition strategies, specialized training on TLAPS syntax, and hybrid approaches combining LLMs with traditional automated theorem provers. We believe this work represents an important step toward understanding how LLMs can be effectively integrated into formal verification workflows.
