The Effect of Multimorbidity and Anticoagulation Use in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation on The One-Year Outcome: Analysis from Jordan Atrial Fibrillation (JoFIB) Study – A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Basil Abdin, Eman Abuqweider, Imad Alhaddad, Remin Shabaneh, Ghayda’ Bader, Taima Bader, Mohammad Abu-Shaban, Qais Salah, Ayman Hammoudeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with atrial fibrillation (AF) frequently have multiple comorbidities that increase the risk of hospitalisation and contribute to higher mortality. However, studies examining the prevalence of comorbidities among Middle Eastern patients with AF and their impact on clinical outcomes are scarce. This study aimed to assess the impact of comorbidities in a Middle Eastern population with AF treated with contemporary anticoagulation.DesignProspective observational cohort study.SettingPatients from 20 hospitals and 30 outpatient cardiology clinics across Jordan were enrolled from May 2019 through October 2020.Participants2020 consecutive patients were enrolled. 117 of them were lost to follow-up, and 1903 had available data for analysis. Of the total, 1096 (54.3%) patients were women, and 924 (45.7%) were men. Eligible patients were 18 years of age or above, had a confirmed AF diagnosis and provided informed consent.Primary and secondary outcomesWe are examining the outcomes of patients with AF, comparing those who have multimorbidities versus oligomorbidities. The primary outcomes were AF-related complications occurring within 1-year follow-up: major bleeding, non-major bleeding, stroke/cerebrovascular accidents, systemic emboli and acute coronary syndrome. Secondary outcomes included causes of death among deceased patients.ResultsAmong the cohort, 1160 (57.4%) had two or less comorbidities (oligomorbidity group) and 860 (42.6%) had three or more comorbidities (multimorbidity group). Compared with the oligomorbidity group, the multimorbidity group had significantly higher rates of hypertension (97.9% vs 57.2%), diabetes mellitus type II (92.4% vs 7.3%), cardiovascular disease (100% vs 79.6%), chronic kidney disease (18.4% vs 1.8%) and chronic lung disease (7% vs 1%, p<0.001 for all comparisons). Over the 1-year follow-up, the multimorbidity group experienced higher rates of hospitalisation (24% vs 16.9%, p<0.001), all-cause death (21.4% vs 9.5%, p<0.001) and cardiovascular death (11.8% vs 4.7%, p=0.002) than the oligomorbidity group. All-cause mortality in the multimorbid group was mostly related to hypertension with an OR of 5.5 (95% CI 2.07 to 14.73), history of stroke (OR 2.7, 95% CI1.85–4.03) and heart failure (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.64 to 3.33). However, the use of warfarin (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.82) and novel oral anticoagulants (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.91) had protective effects from all-cause mortality.ConclusionMiddle Eastern patients with AF appear to exhibit a high burden of comorbidities. The results suggest the more comorbidities in these patients, the higher the rates of hospitalisation and death.Trial registration numberNCT03917992.Keywords: Death, Sudden, Cardiac; Hypertension; Heart failure

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, and its overall burden has been increasing over recent decades, particularly in developing countries, with a prevalence of 1%–2% of the general population (about 60 million cases) at the current time.1 AF rarely occurs alone and is typically accompanied by multiple comorbidities, especially in the elderly. The presence of comorbid disorders, other risk factors, cardiovascular (CV) diseases (CVDs) and improving life expectancy all contribute to AF development.2 3 As a worldwide challenge, AF has become a major medical problem with significant public health implications due to its high prevalence and strong association with morbidity and mortality.2 4

Many clinical studies showed that non-valvular AF (NVAF) is commonly associated with a heterogenous set of comorbidities, including heart failure (HF), diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), obesity and pulmonary diseases.4 These often occur in clusters, giving rise to the concept of multimorbidity, which increases with age and affects AF outcomes, including stroke risk.5 The Middle Eastern population has a high prevalence of risk factors including DM, obesity and HTN—key contributors to AF and its complications. Moreover, the presence of rheumatic valvular and other CVDs increases the risk of hospitalisation and adverse outcomes among patients with AF.6 7

The management of NVAF focuses on three core components: anticoagulation, rate control and rhythm control.8 The use of oral anticoagulants (OACs) is a central part of the ABC pathway for AF management. Currently, novel OACs (NOACs) are the preferred agents over vitamin K-dependent agents (VKAs) due to superior safety, ease of administration and favourable efficacy profiles.9 10 Various studies have shown that early management of AF, particularly in patients with high comorbidity burden, has been shown to reduce CV events, stroke and all-cause mortality.9 11 Furthermore, optimal management of modifiable risk factors and comorbidities has improved the overall morbidity, leading to reduced healthcare costs, lower mortality and enhanced AF prognosis.12

Despite established links between comorbidities and worse AF outcomes, evidence on how combinations of comorbidities influence hospitalisation and mortality remains scarce.13 The Jordan atrial fibrillation (JoFIB) study focuses on Middle Eastern patients with AF to nourish the limited data available about the effect of contemporary management of AF on improving the outcomes of these patients.14 15

This study provides a deep understanding of how the presence of many comorbidities alongside AF affects those patients. Previous studies have focused on how the AF type, medical regimen used in the management of AF and its complications, AF-associated symptoms and other characteristics like echo findings and CHAD-VASc score (a score assessing the stroke risk in AF patients, using the acronym: Congestive heart failure (1 point), Hypertension (1 point), Age ≥ 75 (2 points), Diabetes (1 point), Stroke/TIA (2 points), Vascular disease (1 point), Age 65-74 (1 point), and Sc/Sex female (1 point)) affect the outcomes of patients with AF (15). Our analysis assesses the AF prognosis in the presence of multimorbidity and how early management may affect the clinical outcomes of high-risk patients.16 17

Methods

Design

The study sample and data were retrieved from the JoFIB study, which is a nationwide, multicentric, prospective, observational study registry of patients with AF. The methods and baseline data of the JoFib study have been described previously.18 The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03917992), and institutional review board (IRB) approval (Reference number 10/2021/415) was obtained at the beginning of the study for the whole project from the IRB of the University of Jordan Hospital. Ethical approval was not obtained separately for this analysis, as it is part of the study that was already approved by the same IRB. Sample size calculation was not performed because this is a national study that accepted as larger number of participants as can be enrolled.

Setting and participants

Consecutive adult patients aged 18 years, diagnosed with AF and provided informed consent were selected and enrolled from May 2019 through October 2020, who were evaluated at 20 hospitals and 30 outpatient cardiology clinics in Jordan.

Data collection and variables

The patients were recruited during clinic visits or admission at hospitals for AF. A written consent was signed by each patient, and follow-up was done through phone calls. The response rate cannot be determined, as the data collectors did not mention the number of invitations and acceptance by each one of them. The questionnaire developed in an order from general and demographic data to medical history, pharmacological history and follow-up data. The diagnosis of AF was confirmed by 12-lead ECG, rhythm strip lasting >30 s; >1 episode of AF on ambulatory ECG monitor or a past diagnosis by a treating cardiologist. Valvular AF (VAF) was diagnosed in the presence of moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis, or cardiac prosthetic mechanical valve; otherwise, patients were classified as having NVAF. Data were collected using a standardised clinical data form at the time of enrolment. Then, patients were followed up prospectively for 1 year, collecting their data at 1, 6 and 12 months through hospital admissions, outpatient clinic records or phone calls. Baseline demographic features, laboratory data, transthoracic echocardiographic findings, comorbidities and use of OACs and other medications were recorded. Comorbidities are diseases or medical conditions that are simultaneously present with another, specific defined disease or medical condition in a patient, such as DM, HTN, CAD, HF and CKD. CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED (a score that is used to assess the 1-year risk of major bleeding in patients with AF, with one point given for each of the following: Hypertension, Abnormal liver/renal function, Stroke history, Bleeding history, Labile INR, Elderly (> 65 yrs) and Drug/alcohol use) scores were also recorded.

Endpoints of interest were all-cause death, CV death (if caused by myocardial infarction (MI), cerebrovascular disease, HF, pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrest or death associated with CV procedures), stroke, systemic embolism, non-major bleeding events, major bleeding events (any bleeding requiring blood transfusion, hospitalisation or drop in haemoglobin level of at least 2 g/L, based on the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis and hospital admissions over 1 year.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 20.0, SPSS Inc. Chicago, Illinois). The sample was divided into two groups according to the number of comorbidities present concomitant with AF: the oligomorbidity group, those with two, one or no comorbidities alongside AF, versus the multimorbidity group, who have three or more comorbidities. This dichotomisation was based on the frequency of comorbidities among Jordanian people, where the mode number of comorbidities is two comorbidities. Continuous variables were described using mean (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentages. Pearson χ2 test and independent t-tests were used to compare categorical variables and continuous variables, respectively, between patients with AF and one or no comorbidities and patients with AF and two or more comorbidities. Admissions, reasons for admission, complications, outcomes and cause of in-hospital death were all described using Pearson χ2 tests. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p-values<0.05 considered to be statistically significant. Finally, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate whether the presence of two or more comorbidities was predictive of the outcomes in patients with AF and controlled for possible confounding factors with clinical relevance to each outcome.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Of the total 2020 consecutive patients enrolled in this study, 1160 (57.4%) had AF with oligomorbidity (having two or fewer comorbidities) and 860 (42.6%) had AF with multimorbidity (having three or more comorbidities). At 1-year follow-up, a total of 117 (5.8%) patients were lost to follow-up, and 1903 (94.2%) had available data for analysis. Baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, comorbidities and echocardiographic features of the oligomorbidity and multimorbidity subgroups are shown in table 1. Compared with the oligomorbidity group, the multimorbidity patients were older (71.1 9.2 vs 65.6 14.7, p<0.001), more likely to be overweight (43.8% vs 32.8%, p<0.001) and have dyslipidaemia (59.7% vs 34.1%, p<0.001) and had higher mean CHA2D2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores (4.6 1.5 vs 2.8 1.7, p<0.001, 2.0 1.1 vs 1.4 1.0, p<0.001, respectively). Regarding the AF classification that is mostly dominated in each group, the multimorbidity group tended to have non-paroxysmal AF more than the oligomorbidity group (67.9% vs 61.2%, p<0.002). Moreover, a strong association was found between VAF and the presence of multimorbidity, as 815 (94.8%) patients in the multimorbidity AF group tended to have VAF in comparison with only 125 (10.8%) in the oligomorbidity AF (p<0.001). Regarding the comorbidities and their tendency to present with multimorbidity versus oligomorbidity, most of them tend to present with several other concomitant comorbidities, as 97.9% of the patients in the multimorbidity AF group had HTN, 92.4% had DM, 100% had CVDs, CKD in 18.4% and 7% had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (p-value of each<0.001). As for the echocardiography findings, the multimorbidity AF group tended to have left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 40%, LV hypertrophy (LVH) and pulmonary HTN more than the oligomorbidity group (p<0.05) (table 1).

Discussion

The key findings from this most recent study of AF in the Middle East are as follows: (a) comorbidities are present in the majority of the studied patients, (b) Middle Eastern patients with AF and multimorbidity had worse outcomes, especially those with HTN, HF and a previous history of stroke or Systemic Stroke (SE) and (c) the use of contemporary anticoagulation protected this group from death.

Patients with AF worldwide have a higher prevalence of chronic comorbidities than the general population, which are almost always present later in the disease course.13 Among the most common comorbidities, HTN, HF, coronary artery disease and obesity contribute to almost half of AF cases.13 The higher the prevalence of comorbidities in patients with AF, the higher the rate of hospitalisation and death, especially in patients who have pulmonary disease, diabetes, prior MI, HF or chronic renal disease.4 13 Moreover, Cicek et al demonstrated that the presence of multimorbidity in elderly patients increased the risk of complications and mortality due to myocardial injury.19 This may be attributed to the interaction between the comorbidities coexisting with AF, which has a synergistic harmful effect on patients’ health and may lead to worse outcomes in addition to the AF-related risks alone.

Almost all patients with AF have at least one comorbidity compared with community controls.13 We observed that CVDs and HTN were the most prevalent comorbidities in our cohort. The presence of multimorbidity was mainly related to a higher prevalence of both CVD and HTN. Many cohorts, such as the Framingham and ARIC studies, demonstrated that HTN is a major risk factor for AF development.20 21 This may be related to secondary structural cardiac changes that predispose patients to develop AF. Furthermore, a strong correlation was found between HTN and AF and an increased risk of CVD and mortality.22 The presence of chronic high blood pressure in addition to AF was significantly associated with a higher incidence of both stroke/SE and major bleeding compared with the non-HTN group.22

Many clinical studies showed that NVAF is commonly associated with a heterogeneous set of comorbidities, including HF, DM, HTN, CAD, cerebrovascular disease, CKD, obesity and pulmonary diseases.4 These comorbidities often occur in clusters, leading to the concept of multimorbidity that increases with ageing and impacts the outcomes of AF and its complications, such as stroke.5 The Middle Eastern population has a high prevalence of risk factors, including DM, obesity and HTN, which contribute the most to developing AF and its consequential complications. Moreover, the presence of rheumatic valvular and other CVDs carries a high risk of hospitalisation and the development of serious complications that lead to worse outcomes in patients with AF.6 7

The cornerstone of NVAF management revolves around three key components: anticoagulation, rate control and rhythm control.8 The use of OACs is one of the main primary pillars of the ABC pathway for the management of AF. Currently, NOACs are the preferred agents over VKAs due to their ease of use, efficacy and safety profile.9 10 Many studies have shown that early management of AF, especially in patients with a high comorbidity burden, lowers the risk of worse cardiovascular events, stroke and all-cause death.9 11 Furthermore, the management of modifiable risk factors and comorbidities has improved overall morbidity, reduced the total cost of care, lowered mortality and improved AF prognosis.12

While the presence of risk factors and comorbidities in patients with AF increases the rates of hospitalisation and death, data regarding the impact of certain comorbidity combinations on hospitalisation and death are still scarce.13 The JoFib study focuses on Middle Eastern patients with AF to strengthen the limited data available about the effect of contemporary AF management on improving the outcomes of these patients.14 15

This study provides a deeper understanding of how the presence of multiple comorbidities alongside AF affects these patients. Previous studies have focused on how the AF type, medical regimen used in AF management, AF-associated symptoms and other characteristics such as echocardiographic findings and CHAD-VASc score affect outcomes.15 Our analysis assesses AF prognosis in the presence of multimorbidity and how early management may affect the clinical outcomes of high-risk patients.

The prevalence of CVD in our population was very high, with HF as a common comorbid condition. One study recorded that out of 2000 patients with AF, 227 (11.4%) had CAD. The same study showed that compared with the rest of the sample, those with CAD had a significantly higher prevalence of HTN, hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes and smoking.23 Furthermore, HTN was found to be the most common comorbidity in patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF),24 in addition to CVD and congestive HF.25 Although HTN is a major modifiable risk factor for HF with both preserved and reduced EF,26 it is also a strong and independent predictor of AF, particularly when the EF is <50%.26

Similar to HTN, DM was very common in the multimorbidity group compared with the oligomorbidity group. Many studies have demonstrated evidence of an interconnection between AF and DM, with 25% of patients with AF having concomitant DM.27 Other studies have also shown that DM increases the risk of AF recurrence after ablation.28 CKD was more common in the multimorbidity group, with almost one in every five patients having CKD. This can be partially explained by the higher prevalence of both HTN and DM in CKD.29 Additionally, AF incidence increases as CKD severity progresses, with 12.2% of patients with CKD stages I–II having AF compared with 14.4% in stages III–V versus 7.5% in patients without CKD.30 This study also showed that advanced age increases all-cause mortality in patients with AF. This is explained by the increasing risk of DM, CKD, HF and HTN with age, which means that elderly people often have multiple morbidities.23

This study showed that the multimorbidity AF group had more structural heart disorders, including depressed LV systolic function (LVEF <40%), LVH and pulmonary HTN. The literature shows that patients with a higher number of comorbidities have larger left atrial volumes and reduced function in all atrial strain phases.31 The Framingham and ARIC studies demonstrated that HTN, a major risk factor for AF, causes cardiac remodelling, resulting in LVH and fibrosis, which significantly increases filling pressure and left atrial size.20 21 On the contrary, another study found that patients with 1–2 comorbidities, but not those with ≥3, showed further progression of impaired left and right atrial functions in almost all atrial strain phases at the 14-month follow-up.31

VAF was also more prevalent in the multimorbidity group, as patients tended to have higher CHA2DS2-VASc scores and a higher risk of stroke/thromboembolism events than those without valvular disease,32 which correlates with our findings. The overall risk of mortality depended mainly on LV function. Ruxandra et al demonstrated that 1-year mortality in patients with HF and AF is influenced by different factors depending on LVEF. Patients with AF and HF with reduced EF had a higher risk of 1-year mortality than those with HFpEF.33

Treatment of AF is crucial to reduce the associated morbidity and mortality, so multiple pharmacological agents are often needed to target different pathways through which AF can cause complications. These agents include anticoagulants and rate-control and rhythm-control medications. The clinical benefit from these medications consists of alleviating patients’ symptoms (eg, shortness of breath, palpitations and angina), improving quality of life, preventing cardiac remodelling and decreasing the thromboembolic risk. It may work by decreasing the risk of short-term mortality, especially in patients with pulmonary embolism, who can be predicted using many modalities of short-term mortality prediction.34 Several trials have been carried out to investigate the effectiveness of the long-term use of AF medications and their impact on patients’ quality of life.35 Studies have found that restoration and maintenance of normal sinus rhythm was the main factor to enhance patients’ symptoms and improve their subjective well-being.36

This study showed that anticoagulation with either DOACs or VKAs was associated with decreasing the risk of all-cause mortality in the multimorbidity group. However, this may be confounded with other variables that are not mentioned in this study that need further investigation and lightening, especially in patients who are contraindicated from using warfarin because of medical or surgical contraindication. It was found that the lower the use of OACs, the higher the morbidity burden in patients with AF with a higher prevalence of comorbidities, especially >5 comorbidities. However, the use of antiarrhythmic drugs was associated with an increased burden of comorbidities.37 Furthermore, the risk of developing stroke and SE was higher in patients with AF who were not prescribed OACs than the risk of intracranial bleeding in patients who were started on OACs,38 which in turn relatively increased the overall morbidity and mortality in patients with AF.

Limitation

A few limitations of this study are worthy of discussion. First, potential bias of residual confounding is inevitable in studies with observational design, in addition to the comprehensiveness of data collection and patient recall of events. These were overcome by recruitment of consecutive patients from different sectors of the local healthcare system. Furthermore, the major cardiovascular events examined were hard endpoints, such as death, stroke and major bleeding. All of these endpoints were very unlikely to be affected by recall issues. Unfortunately, survival time analysis could not be performed because the exact date of death was not available. This study had a lower ratio of patients lost to follow-up (5.7%) compared with other studies. However, the discriminatory ability of some analyses and the generalisability of the results are still a point of concern. Finally, all-cause mortality was a secondary outcome that triggered our attention and was mentioned in the discussion. However, it is still a short period for a chronic disease to observe its effect on patients’ death. Despite these limitations, this study represents an important contribution to the contemporary knowledge of baseline features and 1-year outcome in a Middle Eastern population with AF.

Conclusion

Middle Eastern patients with AF appear to have a high prevalence of comorbidities. HTN, CVDs, DM type II, CKD, dyslipidaemia and chronic lung disease appear to be the most common comorbid diseases in these patients. The presence of multiple comorbidities in patients with AF is associated with a higher rate of hospitalisation and death. Commitment to treatment may improve the outcomes and decrease the rate of death in these patients.
