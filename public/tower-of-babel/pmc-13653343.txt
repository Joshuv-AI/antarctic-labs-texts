Preventing Plasma‐Induced Resist Hardening in Molybdenum Disulfide Transistors via Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography
Authors: Dahyeon Kim, Yanfeng Zhao, Sungyeon Kim, Seungchan Lee, Sangmin Eom, Tae Yeon Kim, Hyun Seok Lee, Joonki Suh, Byungjo Kim, Myungsoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAchieving the theoretical performance of 2D molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) electronics is currently bottlenecked by interfacial contamination derived from lithographic processing. A critical, yet often overlooked, mechanism is the interaction between photoresist (PR) and plasma, which chemically alters the interface. In this work, we elucidate the atomistic origin of this degradation: theoretical calculations based on molecular dynamics (MD) and density functional theory (DFT) reveal that plasma exposure functionalizes the PR with oxygen, drastically increasing its adsorption energy on the MoS2 surface from −1.15 to −2.36 eV. This doubling of adsorption energy creates thermodynamically stable, hardened residues that resist conventional removal methods. To overcome this fundamental limitation, we introduce a universal prevention‐first strategy: Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography. By utilizing a sacrificial layer to physically isolate the channel, we shield the MoS2 from reactive plasma species, preventing the formation of hardened residue entirely. This strategy results in a ten‐fold reduction in contact resistance (R C) from 2.59 × 106 Ω·µm to 2.58 × 105 Ω·µm and an order‐of‐magnitude enhancement in on‐current. Crucially, we demonstrate the universality of this method by successfully applying it to top‐gated transistor arrays, electron‐beam lithography (EBL), and atomic layer deposition (ALD)‐synthesized films.Keywords: 2D materials, molybdenum disulfide, photoresist, residue, transistors

Introduction

2D transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are promising candidates for next‐generation field‐effect transistors (FETs) beyond conventional silicon technology [1, 2]. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), in particular, has garnered significant attention due to its suitable bandgap and high‐mobility potential [3, 4]. However, despite advances in wafer‐scale synthesis [5, 6, 7], translating the intrinsic quality of these materials into high‐performance devices remains elusive. The primary bottleneck is interfacial contamination introduced during photolithography, a cornerstone process of semiconductor manufacturing that is currently incompatible with the fragile surface chemistry of 2D materials [8, 9]. The challenge is severely exacerbated by plasma processing, which is essential for defining channel geometries. While polymeric photoresists (PR) are standard in lithography, plasma exposure chemically alters the polymer structure, creating hardened residues. Conventional cleaning strategies often fail to address this issue; ultrasonication can delaminate the atomically thin channel, and thermal annealing is frequently ineffective because the decomposition temperature of the cross‐linked, plasma‐hardened PR often exceeds the thermal budget of the device stack [10, 11, 12, 13]. Consequently, these stubborn residues degrade carrier mobility, induce unintentional doping, and result in severe contact resistance (RC ) penalties [14, 15, 16, 17]. Previous attempts to minimize residues have largely been directed toward large‐area channel transfer techniques [18]. Even though employing a sacrificial layer successfully minimizes residues on the channel, its removal prior to the main fabrication processes means that the specific impact of residues left on the MoS2 surface by lithography and etching has been largely underexplored.

In this work, we combine atomistic theory with experimental validation to solve this contamination bottleneck. First, using Molecular Dynamics (MD) and Density Functional Theory (DFT), we uncover the microscopic origin of residue hardening. We reveal that plasma treatment functionalizes the PR with oxygen, which increases the adsorption energy of the polymer on MoS2 from −1.15 to −2.36 eV. This thermodynamic stabilization explains why post‐fabrication cleaning is futile: the residue is not merely physisorbed but chemically pinned to the lattice. Guided by this insight, we propose a prevention‐first solution: Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography. Expanding beyond conventional metal‐assisted contact lithography [19], we report a significant enhancement in ON‐current by applying this technique to active channel definition—a step highly prone to photoresist residue—complemented by a comprehensive theoretical investigation into plasma‐induced photoresist‐MoS2 adhesion mechanism.

Instead of attempting to clean a contaminated surface, we utilize a sacrificial metal layer to physically shield the MoS2 channel from direct interaction with both organic polymers and reactive plasma species. This approach leverages a critical differential in surface chemistry: while plasma‐hardened PR bonds strongly to MoS2, it does not form such robust bonds with the inert gold mask. Furthermore, the wet etchant (KI/I2) used to remove the mask effectively undercuts and lifts off any overlying residue, leaving the MoS2 surface pristine—a feat unattainable with conventional processing. We validate this strategy by demonstrating a dramatic ten‐fold reduction in R C (down to 2.58 × 105 Ω·µm) and an order‐of‐magnitude increase in on‐current (I ON). To prove the method's readiness for realistic circuit integration, we extend its application beyond standard back‐gated devices to top‐gated transistor arrays, electron‐beam lithography (EBL) processes, and ALD‐grown MoS2 films. This establishes the sacrificial metal mask technique as a universal, non‐destructive integration strategy essential for unlocking the true potential of 2D semiconductor electronics.

Results and Discussion

Photoresist Residue From Conventional Lithography on MoS2

A primary challenge in fabricating high‐performance MoS2 devices is the interfacial contamination introduced during photolithography, a problem that is severely exacerbated by plasma processing. Plasma exposure is known to chemically alter PR, creating stubborn, hardened residues. These residues are extremely difficult to remove and catastrophically degrade device performance by increasing R C, scattering charge carriers, and creating interface traps, ultimately masking the material's intrinsic properties. Figure 1a shows a schematic illustration of a transferred MoS2 film with a clean surface free from polymer residues. Figure 1b presents a schematic image revealing locally remaining PR residues after a conventional photolithography process. Figure 1c displays widespread, hardened PR residues formed after plasma treatment, where patterned PR was used as an etching mask. To quantify this contamination bottleneck, we performed a direct comparison using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). As a clean reference, the as‐transferred MoS2 surface is pristine (Figure 1d). In sharp contrast, while the inert gold surface exhibits minimal contamination after undergoing the identical full lithography and plasma‐etching process (Figure 1e), the MoS2 surface retains high‐density residue (Figure 1f). This direct comparison clearly demonstrates that MoS2 is far more susceptible to this plasma‐induced contamination than metal surfaces, posing a critical obstacle to device fabrication.

Further evidence of this residue is found in Raman spectroscopy, a technique highly sensitive to lattice vibrations. Raman spectroscopy was employed to assess the amount of residue remaining on the MoS2 surface (Figure S1). Raman spectra were collected for pristine MoS2, MoS2 after Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography, and MoS2 after conventional photolithography. The characteristic E2g and A1g peaks, corresponding to the in‐plane and out‐of‐plane vibrations of Mo─S bonds, respectively, were analyzed. A progressive decrease in the intensities of these peaks was observed in the order of pristine > conventional photolithography. This attenuation in Raman signal is primarily attributed to two factors: (1) physical screening of the Raman signal by residual photoresist, and (2) damping of Mo─S vibrational modes due to molecular interactions between sulfur atoms in MoS2 and the photoresist residue [8].

To overcome this contamination bottleneck, we developed and implemented a novel Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography technique, with the fabrication sequence schematically outlined in Figure S2. A metal‐assisted residue‐free transfer process has previously been demonstrated; however, it was confined to the transfer stage, and systematic investigations of metal mask lithography are still lacking [20]. The core principle of this method is to use a sacrificial gold layer to completely shield the sensitive MoS2 channel before any photoresist is applied. Gold was selected as the sacrificial material due to its chemical inertness against the halide‐based plasma used for MoS2 etching, and its distinct wet‐etching selectivity compared to TMDs and contact metals. This strategy ensures that the MoS2 surface is never exposed to either the PR or, more importantly, the damaging plasma during critical patterning steps. However, a critical prerequisite is ensuring that the deposition and removal of the sacrificial metal layer do not introduce secondary damage to the underlying MoS2 lattice. Any damage to the 2D lattice could negate the benefits of a cleaner interface. Therefore, a rigorous characterization of the MoS2 after the gold protection and removal process is essential to validate this technique, as will be discussed in the following sections.

Interface and Residue Analysis Using C‐AFM, AES, and TEM

To investigate the electrical and chemical consequences of the residue identified by AFM, we directly compared samples prepared using conventional lithography vs. our Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography. We first probed the local electrical properties using Conductive Atomic Force Microscopy (C‐AFM). At a 0.1 V bias, the MoS2 surface processed with conventional lithography (Figure 2a) reveals a spatially non‐uniform current distribution, characterized by spatially distributed insulating patches [21]. In stark contrast, the current map from our gold‐protected sample (Figure 2b) shows vastly more uniform conductivity across the entire surface. The conventional sample's profile exhibits multiple high‐resistance area, confirming that insulating PR residues act as local current barriers. Conversely, the gold‐protected sample displays a uniformly low and flat resistance profile, consistent with a pristine, contaminant‐free semiconductor surface.

We then performed chemical characterization to complement these electrical findings. Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES), which is highly surface‐sensitive to carbon (a primary component of PR), was used for comparison. The AES spectrum from the conventionally processed MoS2 (Figure 2c) displays a pronounced carbon signal, confirming the presence of significant carbonaceous residue. In contrast, the spectrum from the gold‐protected surface (Figure 2d) reveals a reduced carbon peak, indicating a substantially cleaner surface. Figure S3 shows the carbon peaks with different fabrication processes including pristine, photolithography, electron beam lithography, Sacrificial Metal Mask photolithography, and Sacrificial Metal Mask electron beam lithography. Together, the C‐AFM and AES analyses provide compelling, mutually reinforcing evidence: the Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography effectively mitigates the formation of electrically insulating, carbon‐based residues.

However, a critical prerequisite for this method is that the protection process itself, the deposition and removal of the sacrificial gold layer, does not damage the underlying MoS2. Any induced lattice damage could negate the benefits of a cleaner interface. We therefore investigated the structural integrity of the MoS2 after the full protection and removal sequence. To prevent damage, which can occur during low‐vacuum metal deposition, we deposited the gold via e‐beam evaporation under high vacuum (1 × 10−6 Torr). Previously, it was reported that evaporating metallic films on MoS2 at pressures lower than 5 × 10−6 Torr is sufficient to avoid damage [22]. The gold was subsequently removed using a standard KI‐I2 wet etchant. The post‐processed flakes were then analyzed by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). High‐Resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Figure 2e,f) and Scanning TEM (STEM) analysis (Figure 2g) reveal a consistently sharp and well‐defined interface between the MoS2 layers. The absence of observable damage, defects, or interfacial reactions confirms that our high‐vacuum evaporation and wet‐etch process fully preserves the structural integrity of the MoS2. This validates our Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography as a non‐destructive technique, ensuring that the performance gains are truly due to a cleaner interface, not an altered material. As shown in Figure S4, the comparison of PL peaks between the pristine and gold‐protected MoS2 samples (following gold wet etching) reveals no significant degradation, demonstrating the structural and optical stability of the material.

Insights From Theoretical Calculations

To gain fundamental atomistic insights into our experimental observations, we performed Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations and Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations. MD simulations were employed to examine the effects of plasma treatment on the molecular structure of the photoresist (PR), while DFT calculations were designed to elucidate two critical mechanisms: the increased adhesion of plasma‐treated PR to the MoS2 surface and the electronic pathway by which this residue degrades device performance. The results provide a clear scientific rationale for Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography.

MD simulations of oxygen radicals, denoted as O(p), bombarding bulk PR slab revealed that N‐O(p) and C‐O(p) bonds readily formed through dynamic interactions between incident atomic oxygen and PR molecules. The number of newly formed bonds was found to be correlated with the incident energy of atomic oxygen as shown in Note S1. Consequently, the stable representative plasma‐treated PR molecular structure possesses four additional oxygen atoms bonded to the original PR backbone. Using DFT, we calculated the adsorption energies of PR molecules on monolayer MoS2 surface. As summarized in Table 1, the pristine PR molecule exhibits an adsorption energy of −1.15 eV. In stark contrast, the plasma‐treated PR molecule displays a significantly stronger binding affinity, with an adsorption energy of −2.36 eV. Our calculations indicate that the oxygen‐functionalized PR molecule exhibits a significantly more negative adsorption energy compared to its pristine counterpart, corresponding to a thermodynamically more stable interface with stronger adhesion. Furthermore, calculations utilizing a larger MoS2 supercell (32 × 32 × 35 Å3) confirmed that the adsorption of two positive plasma‐treated PR molecules is energetically more favorable than that of negative PR variants. This quantitative increase in adsorption energy magnitude clearly demonstrates that plasma treatment induces chemical modifications to the PR, causing it to adhere more tenaciously to the MoS2, substrate.

This stronger interaction is also corroborated by the spatial distribution of the ground‐state electron density, shown together with the corresponding adsorption configurations in Figure 3a,b. The relative higher adsorption favorability of plasma‐treated PR is reflected in the increased ground‐state electron density overlap between the PR molecule and the surface, arising from the extra oxygen atoms attached to the molecule. These results explain why the plasma‐hardened residues are difficult to remove and underscore the necessity of shielding the MoS2 surface.

While the pristine lattice retains a clean 1.60 eV bandgap, PR adsorption introduces defect states within the gap. Notably, the plasma‐treated PR induces a significantly higher density of these defect states near the valence band maximum (VBM); these states act as effective hole traps and scattering centers, providing the electronic origin for the observed device degradation.

Residual PR molecules on the surface can exert a negative impact on device properties. To further understand these influences, Density of States (DOS) calculations were performed based on the derived ground‐state electron density to investigate electronic structure changes due to PR residues. As shown in Figure 3c, pristine monolayer MoS2 exhibits a clean bandgap with no defect states observed between the valence band and conduction band. The calculated bandgap is 1.60 eV, which is consistent with reported theoretical calculation results ranging from 1.55 to 1.68 eV. The valence band maximum (VBM) was aligned to zero; the VBM is composed mainly of Mo 4d and S 3p states, while the conduction band minimum is dominated by Mo 4d states. In contrast, both PR‐adsorbed systems exhibited defect states within the bandgap. Projected DOS (PDOS) analysis reveals that these defect states originate primarily from the O 2p orbitals of the PR on the surface. In the pristine PR adsorbed system, two observed defect states are located close to the valence band, at 0.1 and 0.3 eV above the VBM. However, in the plasma‐treated PR system, while the defect levels remain close to the VBM, there is a significant increase in the density of states. This indicates a higher probability and number of electrons being trapped at these defect states.

This transition from a clean bandgap to a high density of in‐gap states is critically important. Charge carriers are likely to be trapped in these localized defect states near the VBM rather than contributing to conductivity through the bands, thereby severely degrading carrier transport and overall device performance. These simulations thus provide a clear atomistic and electronic mechanism for the severe performance degradation observed in devices fabricated using conventional, plasma‐exposed lithography.

Improved Electrical Characteristics of MoS2 FETs using Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography

To conclusively demonstrate the practical benefits of Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography, we fabricated and systematically characterized local back‐gated field‐effect transistors (FETs) utilizing monolayer MoS2 as the channel material. This architecture, featuring a 10 nm HfO2 high‐k gate dielectric on a local gate, provides enhanced electrostatic control. A schematic representation of this device structure is shown in Figure 4a,b. As depicted, conventional processing risks leaving detrimental PR residues at the critical metal‐MoS2 interface, whereas the Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography is designed to yield a clean interface.

To isolate the effects of lithography residue, we employed a previously reported residue‐free transfer method (Figure S7) [21]. These statistical results confirm the representative data: the gold‐protected process yields a median I ON approximately one order of magnitude higher than the conventional process. This improvement is a direct consequence of cleaner semiconductor interfaces and reduced contact resistance, achieved by preventing PR residue contamination.

To demonstrate the scientific rigor and broad applicability of our method, we conducted two additional studies. First, to prove its versatility beyond photolithography, we applied the technique to a top‐gate structure fabricated using electron‐beam lithography (EBL). This was motivated by the need to test its efficacy against different polymer resists, namely e‐beam resist (ER). The fabrication (Figures S12 and S13), structure (Figure S14), and post‐ALD material quality (Figure S15) are detailed in the Supplementary Information. The electrical comparison (Figure S16) shows a discernible improvement, which is less pronounced than in the photolithography case. This result is consistent with our hypothesis, as EBL processes are known to leave less stubborn residue than plasma‐treated PR. Second, to confirm the technique is not limited to a specific material source, we applied it to MoS2 films grown by Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) (Figure S17). The quality of this bilayer MoS2 was confirmed by Raman and PL (Figure S18). Transfer curves from local back‐gate devices (Figure S19) fabricated on this ALD‐MoS2 again demonstrate a clear and consistent on‐current enhancement, corroborating the primary findings from our MOCVD‐based devices. Collectively, the primary statistical data and the supplementary validation studies strongly demonstrate that the residue‐free Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography technique is a robust and broadly applicable strategy, effective across different lithography types (photolithography, EBL) and material sources (MOCVD, ALD) for improving 2D transistor performance.

Contact Resistance Analysis via the Y‐Function Method

To isolate and quantify the R C, a key parameter directly affected by interfacial contamination, we employed the Y‐function method (YFM). This method relies on analyzing the device's transfer characteristics at a low drain bias. The detailed formulation of the Y‐function method is presented in Note S2 [23]. Figure 5 shows the Y‐function, defined as Y = I DS /g m 0.5 (where g m is the transconductance), plotted vs. V GS for all three fabrication methods, using data from V DS = 0.2 V. From this analysis, the mobility reduction coefficient (θ) was also extracted. This coefficient is related to R C via the expression θ = θ 0 + R C C ox µ 0 W CH /L CH, where θ 0 represents intrinsic mobility reduction factors, Cox is the gate oxide capacitance, µ0 is the low‐field mobility, and W CH /L CH is the channel aspect ratio. By analyzing θ in the strong inversion regime, R C can be determined.

Contact resistance extraction via the Y‐function method. Plots of the Y‐function (I D /g m 0.5 ) and mobility attenuation factor (θ) vs. gate voltage (V GS) extracted in the linear regime (V DS = 0.2 V) for MoS2 transistors fabricated using: (a) Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography, (b) Conventional Lithography, and (c) Plasma‐Induced Hardened conditions. The extracted contact resistance (R C) values reveal a dramatic degradation trend: the metal mask‐protected device achieves the lowest R C of 2.58 × 105 Ω·µm, compared to 2.59 × 106 Ω·µm for the conventional device and 5.25 × 108 Ω·µm for the plasma‐hardened case. This quantitative analysis confirms that shielding the channel from direct polymer‐plasma interaction eliminates the high‐resistance residue layer responsible for severe contact degradation.

Using this YFM approach, we extracted an R C value of approximately 2.58 × 105 Ω·µm for the MoS2 FET fabricated with gold‐protected lithography (Figure 5a). This value is a full order of magnitude lower than the 2.59 × 106 Ω·µm extracted for the device made with conventional lithography (Figure 5b). Underscoring the severity of the contamination, the plasma‐induced PR‐hardened device (Figure 5c) exhibited an R C of 5.25 × 108 Ω·µm, over two orders of magnitude higher than the conventional, non‐hardened device. Crucially, these electrical degradation trends align perfectly with our theoretical predictions. The dispersive mid‐gap states identified in our DFT calculations (Figure 3) act as effective charge trapping centers, serving as the microscopic origin of the high contact resistance and carrier scattering observed in the electrical measurements (Figure 5). This comprehensive analysis confirms that the order‐of‐magnitude improvement in I ON in our gold‐protected devices is a direct consequence of eliminating these high‐resistance, plasma‐hardened PR residues.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have addressed the critical challenge of plasma‐induced photoresist (PR) contamination, a key fabrication bottleneck in MoS2 device manufacturing. Given the impracticality of conventional plasma cleaning or ultrasonication for fragile 2D materials, preventing residue formation is paramount. Crucially, our atomistic calculations identified the oxygen‐functionalization of the photoresist as the primary driver for the dramatically increased adhesion energy, providing a theoretical basis for the experimentally observed residue stubbornness. Based on this key insight, we introduced and validated a Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography technique. This method strategically shields the MoS2 channel, preventing direct contact between the PR and the 2D material during plasma processing. Comprehensive structural, chemical, and electrical analyses (TEM/STEM, AES, C‐AFM) confirmed this strategy yields a substantially cleaner, damage‐free MoS2 interface compared to conventional methods. The impact on device performance is unambiguous. By eliminating this contamination layer, we achieved a ten‐fold reduction in R C, as quantified by the Y‐function method. This directly translated into a statistically‐validated, order‐of‐magnitude enhancement in I ON for our MoS2 FETs. Crucially, we demonstrated the universality of this strategy by successfully applying it to top‐gated transistor arrays, electron‐beam lithography, and ALD‐synthesized films. This broad compatibility proves that Sacrificial Metal Mask Lithography is a scalable, integration‐ready solution capable of supporting high‐density circuit designs. By eliminating the trade‐off between lithographic precision and interfacial cleanliness, this work clears a major hurdle for the realization of high‐performance logic and memory devices based on 2D semiconductors.

Experimental Section

Monolayer MoS2 Film Growth

Monolayer MoS2 films serving as transistor channels were synthesized via metal‐organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD). Molybdenum hexacarbonyl (Mo(CO)6; MHC) and diethyl sulfide ((C2H5)2S; DES) were employed as the molybdenum and sulfur precursors, respectively. During the growth stage, the precursor flow rates were precisely regulated using mass flow controllers (MFCs), with MHC supplied at 1 sccm and DES at 0.4 sccm. The growth was conducted at 750°C for a duration of 7 h. C‐plane sapphire substrates were used to promote the growth of high‐crystallity monolayer MoS2. Ar was utilized as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 320 sccm, while H2 was introduced at 5 sccm as a reducing agent. To facilitate nucleation and accelerate lateral growth, a NaCl block was placed 8 cm upstream of the growth substrate acting as a growth promoter.

Wet Transfer

First, monolayer MoS2 grown by MOCVD was transferred via a wet transfer method. To prevent PMMA‐induced residues, PPC was spin‐coated at 4000 rpm under atmospheric conditions. The PPC/MoS2 stack was then separated from the sacrificial sapphire substrate using a KOH solution. After multiple rinses with DI water, the stack was transferred onto the target SiO2 wafer, followed by annealing on a hot plate at 80 °C for 5 min and subsequent cleaning in acetone.

C‐AFM Measurement

C‐AFM measurements were performed using a Park Systems NX7 system equipped with a Variable‐Enhanced Current Amplifier (VECA). We employed NSC18/Cr‐Au for the scanning. Prior to measurement, a current offset calibration was applied to zero the baseline current. To establish a reliable electrical connection between the sample and the sample disk, MoS2 were transferred onto a Si substrate coated with a 100 nm‐thick Cr layer, followed by the fabrication process. The Cr layer was then connected to the sample disk using silver paste (Figure S18). The preamplifier gain was set to 109 V/A, ensuring stable data acquisition with an extremely low background noise level within ±0.3 pA (Figure S19). All measurements were conducted under a constant sample bias of 0.1 V.

AES Measurement

Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) analysis was performed using a PHI‐710 system (ULVAC‐PHI, INC) equipped with a cylindrical mirror analyzer (CMA). The measurements were conducted with an acceleration voltage and electron beam energy of 5 kV, and a target current of 10 nA. Sputtering for depth profiling was carried out using a 1 kV Ar+ ion beam rastered with a sputtering rate of 35 Å/min calibrated for SiO2.

Local Back Gate Transistor Fabrication

All transistors with a local back‐gate structure were fabricated using photolithography. The gate electrode was fabricated by electron beam evaporation of a 2 nm Ni adhesion layer followed by 80 nm of Au, with a gate length of 2 µm. A 10 nm HfO2 gate dielectric was subsequently deposited via atomic layer deposition (ALD). Monolayer MoS2 was transferred onto the substrate using a wet transfer technique. A 5 nm Au protection layer was deposited by electron beam evaporation under a base pressure below 1 × 10−6 Torr. The MoS2 regions outside the defined active area were selectively removed by Cl2/Ar plasma etching using an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) system. Source and drain regions were defined using photolithography, and the Au protection layer in the contact areas was selectively etched using a KI/I2 solution. The source and drain electrodes, consisting of Ni (2 nm), Au (50 nm) and Ni (10 nm), were then deposited by electron beam evaporation, followed by lift‐off, with a channel length of 6 µm. To remove the Au protection layer in the channel region, an additional photolithography step was performed, and the exposed Au was etched using the same KI/I2 solution.

Top Gate Transistor Fabrication

Large pad regions were first patterned using photolithography, followed by the deposition of Ni (2 nm) and Au (60 nm) by electron beam evaporation. Monolayer MoS2 was transferred via a wet transfer process, then a 5 nm Au protection layer was subsequently deposited by electron beam evaporation under a base pressure below 1 × 10−6 Torr. The regions outside the active area were then removed by etching after patterning with electron beam lithography. Next, for source/drain electrode patterning, electron beam lithography was employed to define a channel length of 500 nm, and Ni (2 nm)/Au (40 nm) was deposited by electron beam evaporation. To remove the Au protection layer in the channel region, electron beam lithography was used to pattern the area, and the Au was etched using a KI/I2 solution. A Si seed layer (2 nm) was deposited by electron beam evaporation, followed by the deposition of a 10 nm HfO2 layer via ALD. For pad opening, photolithography was employed to pattern the desired areas, after which HfO2 was removed using an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) etching process with CF4/Ar gases. Finally, the gate electrode was patterned by electron beam lithography to achieve a 200 nm gate length, and Ni (2 nm)/Au (30 nm) was deposited by electron beam evaporation.

TEM and SEM Imaging

The structure of the device was analyzed using high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM; JEM‐2100F, JEOL). TEM samples were prepared by Ga ion milling using a Helios 450HP FIB system, and a carbon protection layer was deposited during the process. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a cold field‐emission SEM (FE‐SEM; S‐4800, Hitachi High‐Technologies) operated at a beam energy of 5 kV.

DC Measurements

The electrical characteristics of the device were examined using a Keysight B1500A semiconductor parameter analyzer under ambient room temperature conditions.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

MD simulations were carried out using the LAMMPS package [24] with the ReaxFF reactive force field [25]. The potential developed by K. Malgorzat et al. was employed to accurately describe interatomic interactions among C, N, H, and O elements in the organic system. A single resin molecule of AZ 5214‐E was first relaxed under the NVT ensemble at 300 K to remove internal strain. Subsequently, an amorphous bulk structure was generated by randomly packing 100 resin molecules into a simulation box of 50 × 50 × 20 Å3 using the Packmol package [26], as illustrated in Figure S5. The amorphous configuration was equilibrated under the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps, followed by an NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm for an additional 100 ps to achieve the target density. A time step of 0.2 fs was used throughout the simulations with the velocity–Verlet integration algorithm. After equilibration, a vacuum layer of 70 Å was added along the z‐axis to create an exposed surface, and the system was further equilibrated under the NVT ensemble for 50 ps to stabilize the free surface prior to bombardment simulations. The simulation cell consisted of three regions: the bottom 5 Å layer was fixed to prevent unphysical drift and to mimic the bulk constraint, the upper 20 Å layer was defined as the thermostatted region to allow structural relaxation and heat dissipation, and the remaining middle region was treated as dynamically active. To differentiate between oxygen atoms originally present in the photoresist and those introduced from the plasma, the incident oxygen atoms were denoted as O(p). Their initial positions were randomly generated within a region 15–20 Å above the surface to avoid overlap with the existing structure. The bombardment and relaxation processes were conducted iteratively in sequential loops. In each loop, a single O(p) atom was introduced at a random position 20–25 Å above the surface and assigned a vertical velocity corresponding to a predefined kinetic energy of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, or 3.0 eV. These energies represent the low‐energy, radical‐dominated regime, allowing the study of surface chemical reactions without inducing significant morphological damage. After each O(p) injection, the system was propagated under the NVE ensemble for 0.4 ps to capture the ballistic impact dynamics, followed by an NVT ensemble at 300 K for another 0.4 ps to allow structural relaxation. This NVE–NVT sequence was repeated for each bombardment–relaxation cycle, ensuring proper thermal equilibration after every impact. Such a thermostat configuration and looping scheme followed a well‐established protocol described in previous studies [27, 28, 29], which has been widely adopted for simulating repeated plasma‐bombardment processes to achieve realistic energy dissipation and structural response. Sputtered atoms were removed from the simulation box after each cycle, and this bombardment—relaxation loop was repeated until a total of 1,000 O(p) atoms were introduced. Finally, the system was equilibrated under the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 20 ps to ensure complete relaxation. Detailed results of the molecular dynamics simulations are provided in the MD Simulation section (Note S1).

DFT Calculation

The present DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4) [30] which utilizes plane‐wave as the basis set for the expansion of wavefunction of the electrons. The projected augmentation wave (PAW) [31] was used for the valence electrons, and the exchange and correlation functional was described using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA–PBE) [32]. The kinetic energy cut–off for the plane‐wave expansion of wavefunction was 600 and the Brillouin zone was sampled using gamma point only due to the large size of the system in the simulation, 20 × 20 × 30 Å3 for structural relaxations. And k‐mesh of 4 × 4 × 1 was used for single point calculations and 8 × 8 × 1 for density of states calculations. The convergence criteria for calculations of force and energy were 0.02 eV/Å per atom and 10−6 eV. The conjugate gradient method and the blocked–Davidson method were employed for the iterative relaxation of ions and electron density respectively. Dipole correction along the z direction was applied to improve convergence efficiency of energy and the calculation of force. The dispersion term (the Van Der Waals interaction) correction was applied using the DFT—D3 method with zero‐damping function from Grimme [33]. The simulated adsorbate–substrate system consists of a monolayer MoS2 as substrate and PR molecule initially positioned 3 Å above it. The PR molecules and the substrate were fully relaxed separately before joining as adsorbate systems. The bottom atom layer of the MoS2 substrate was kept fixed and the top two atom layers and the PR molecule were fully relaxed.
