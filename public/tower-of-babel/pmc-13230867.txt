Potential role of leptin in colorectal cancer liver metastasis involving lipid metabolic reprogramming and immunosuppressive macrophage polarization
Authors: Yuchen Xie, Hongyu Wang, Xiao Dong, Yueran Chen, Yidong Xia, Xueying Huang, Wenling Zhang, Shipeng Dai, Yichan Zhou, Xiaofeng Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver metastasis, a hallmark of advanced Colorectal cancer (CRC), represents the leading cause of mortality in affected patients. While surgical resection of primary tumors and metastatic lesions remains the gold-standard therapeutic intervention, only 10–20% of patients with hepatic metastases qualify for curative resection, underscoring the critical need for novel therapeutic strategies. Through integrated bioinformatics analysis, we identified Leptin (LEP) as a potential candidate gene associated with colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM). Functional analyses including colony formation, wound healing and cross-well migration indicated that LEP overexpression was associated with enhanced proliferation, invasion and metastasis potential of CRC cells, and vice versa. Mechanistically, Nile Red lipid staining revealed potential lipid metabolic alterations linked to LEP levels in CRC cells. In vivo, a CRLM murine model suggested the potential involvement of LEP in promoting hepatic metastasis and inducing tumor-associated macrophage M2 polarization. Immunofluorescence (IF), quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analyses further supported the correlation between LEP and the upregulation of M2-macrophage markers and associated cytokines. These findings suggest that LEP may function as a candidate modulator of CRLM progression through lipid metabolism rewiring and immunosuppressive macrophage polarization, warranting further investigation into its potential as a diagnostic biomarker or therapeutic target for metastatic CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48506-2.Keywords: Colorectal cancer liver metastasis, Leptin, Lipid metabolism, Macrophage polarizationSubject terms: Cancer metabolism, Cancer microenvironment, Gastrointestinal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors globally, constituting 10% of all cancer cases and ranking as the third most common cancer worldwide1,2. CRC predominantly affects individuals over the age of 50, though the proportion of younger patients has been on the rise in recent years3. Lifestyle factors including high-fat diets, insufficient dietary fiber intake, obesity, lack of physical activity, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption are recognized as primary risk factors for CRC3. Genetic predispositions, such as familial adenomatous polyposis and hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, also elevate the risk of CRC3. Patients with long-term chronic inflammatory bowel diseases, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, are additionally at heightened risk for CRC4.

Colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM) is a frequent complication in advanced cases and a significant cause of mortality among colorectal cancer patients5. Liver resection is an effective therapeutic option for patients with solitary or a few localized liver metastases, provided the primary tumor has been or can be resected6,7. However, due to the absence of specific early screening methods, numerous patients are diagnosed in the middle to late stages. For patients with multiple liver metastases or metastatic involvement in other organs, surgical intervention becomes more challenging, and the prognosis is generally poor8. Although chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy can constrain tumor progression to some extent, the long-term survival rate remains suboptimal. Based on the current clinical challenges and treatment bottlenecks of liver metastasis of CRC, it is of urgent scientific significance and clinical application value to analyze the metastasis mechanism at the genetic level and develop targeted intervention strategies.

To investigate the key genes influencing the metastasis of CRC, in the present study, we retrieved the COAD dataset from the TCGA database. It was found that the expression of LEP varied between different tumors and their corresponding adjacent normal tissues. The survival curve demonstrated that high expression of LEP was associated with a poor prognosis, which imply that LEP might be a key gene influencing the progression and metastasis of CRC. Leptin (LEP) was initially recognized as a hormone secreted primarily by adipose tissue, primarily contributing to the regulation of energy balance and body weight control9. However, LEP is also expressed in various other cell types. Its role extends beyond energy regulation, playing a significant part in lipid metabolism. Recent years have seen a growing body of research highlighting LEP’s significant role in the initiation, progression, and treatment of various cancers. For instance, studies have demonstrated that LEP can stimulate the proliferation and angiogenesis of breast cancer cells by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway10. Similarly, in CRC, high levels of LEP expression have been linked to tumor progression and unfavorable prognoses. Specifically, LEP promotes the survival and proliferation of cancer cells by modulating the cell cycle and inhibiting apoptosis11. However, its functional role and mechanistic underpinnings in CRLM progression remain poorly defined, particularly regarding interactions between metabolic reprogramming and immune microenvironment remodeling.

Herein, we investigate the potential role of LEP in CRLM through its putative dual regulation of tumor-intrinsic lipid metabolism and extrinsic immunosuppression. Using integrated functional genomics and preclinical models, our data suggest that LEP overexpression correlates with CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and metastatic dissemination, while its genetic suppression is associated with the attenuation of these phenotypes. Mechanistically, our observations point toward LEP-linked lipid metabolic rewiring in CRC cells, as supported by Nile Red staining and lipidomic profiling, which may enhance their survival in nutrient-scarce metastatic niches. Concurrently, LEP appears to correlate with the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, potentially creating a permissive microenvironment for hepatic colonization. These findings highlight LEP as a potential candidate linking metabolic adaptability and immune evasion in CRLM, warranting further investigation into its feasibility as a dual-target strategy to disrupt metastatic cascades.

Results

LEP is a key gene that influences CRC metastasis and patient prognosis

To identify key genes influencing CRC metastasis, we obtained the COAD dataset from the TCGA database. This dataset was categorized based on the metastasis stage specified in the clinical information, yielding an M0 group (337 cases) and an M1 group (65 cases). We then performed differential gene expression analysis and generated a volcano plot (Fig. 1A), identifying 394 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 290 upregulated and 104 downregulated genes. Given the crucial role of the tumor immune microenvironment in CRC progression, we selected a dataset containing 1793 immune-related genes. By intersecting these with the DEGs, we identified 63 common genes and constructed a Venn diagram (Fig. 1B). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 1C) and a heatmap (Fig. 1D) for these 63 genes were generated using the STRING database. Cytoscape software was used to analyze and visualize the PPI network, leading to the identification of 20 hub genes (Fig. 1E). To screen for prognosis-related genes, we applied univariate Cox regression and LASSO regression analyses to the 394 DEGs, yielding 21 prognosis-associated genes (Fig. 1F). Taking the intersection of the hub genes and the prognosis genes, we constructed a Venn diagram (Fig. 1G) and identified one key common gene: LEP. Subsequently, a radar chart generated from TCGA data (Fig. 1H) revealed differential LEP expression between various tumors and their adjacent normal tissues. Specifically, LEP expression was lower in CRC tissues than in adjacent normal tissues. Survival analysis using clinical data from the TCGA dataset demonstrated that high LEP expression was associated with poor prognosis (P = 0.00019; Fig. 1I). This finding was consistent with results from the public KM-Plotter database (P = 0.0032; Fig. 1J). These results suggest that LEP may be a key gene influencing CRC progression and metastasis.

Prediction of the downstream pathways of LEP and its impact on the immune microenvironment

To explore potential downstream pathways influenced by LEP, we employed the DAVID database to conduct KEGG and GO enrichment analyses on the 63 immune-related differentially expressed genes. We subsequently generated bar charts (Fig. 2A), dot plots (Fig. 2B), and chord diagrams (Fig. 2C) to visualize the results. KEGG enrichment analysis primarily indicated associations with pathways including Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, Pancreatic secretion, and Steroid hormone biosynthesis. In the GO enrichment analysis, for Biological Process (BP), the genes were mainly enriched in processes such as synapse organization, antimicrobial humoral response, and regulation of hormone levels. For Cellular Component (CC), enrichment was primarily associated with structures including the neuronal cell body, presynapse, and glutamatergic synapse. For Molecular Function (MF), the genes were predominantly linked to functions such as signaling receptor activator activity, receptor ligand activity, and hormone activity.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the 63 immune-related differentially expressed genes revealed significant enrichment of gene sets associated with cell-cycle checkpoints, mitotic G1 phase and G1-S transition, and lipid metabolism. This may imply that these biological processes play key roles under the relevant experimental conditions (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we explored the correlations between LEP and different immune cells. Among these, the correlation between LEP and tumor-associated macrophages was the most prominent (Fig. 2E and F). Analysis using the TIMER database revealed a positive correlation between LEP expression in CRC tissues and M2-type macrophage polarization (Fig. 2G). Findings from these public database analyses suggest that high LEP expression may, in part, contribute to CRC progression and metastasis by modulating lipid metabolism and macrophage polarization.

Paradoxical LEP/LEPR crosstalk in liver metastasis of CRC

Since LEPR serves as the receptor of LEP, a pan-cancer analysis of LEPR was also conducted in public databases. The outcomes revealed that LEPR was expressed in a wide range of tumors. In comparison with adjacent tissues, the expression of LEPR in CRC was relatively low (Fig. 3A). To further investigate the expression of LEP and LEPR in colorectal cancer, primary CRC tissues, adjacent tissues, and liver metastases were collected from six patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases for qRT-PCR analysis. The qRT-PCR results indicated that there was no significant difference in the expression of LEP and LEPR between CRC tissues and adjacent tissues. However, compared with primary tumors and adjacent tissues, LEP was highly expressed, whereas LEPR was significantly down-regulated in CRC liver metastases (Fig. 3B and C). Subsequently, blood samples were collected from six normal individuals, six CRC patients without distant metastases, and six CRC patients with liver metastases for ELISA. The results demonstrated that the expression of LEP in the blood of patients with liver metastasis was notably higher than that of healthy individuals and patients without metastasis. In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in the blood LEP expression between healthy people and CRC patients without metastasis (Fig. 3D). After that, the knockdown and overexpression of LEP were carried out in two human CRC cell lines (HCT116 and SW480). Immunofluorescence results showed that the expression of LEPR was significantly reduced after LEP overexpression, while LEPR expression was upregulated following LEP knockdown (Fig. 3E and F). These results demonstrate a paradoxical decrease in LEPR expression despite elevated LEP levels in CRC liver metastases. The inverse correlation between LEP overexpression and LEPR suppression highlights a potential feedback loop driving CRC aggressiveness and hepatic dissemination.

Over-expression of LEP promotes the proliferation, migration and invasion of CRC cells

To thoroughly investigate the impact of LEP on the malignant phenotype of CRC cells, we first employed small interfering RNA (siRNA) to effectively silence LEP expression in human CRC cells. Simultaneously, we utilized plasmid transfection to achieve overexpression of LEP in CRC cells. The efficiency of LEP knockdown and overexpression was confirmed using qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. 4A and B). To assess the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of CRC cells, we conducted a series of functional assays, including the Wound healing assay, Colony formation assay, and Transwell assay. As anticipated, the pc-LEP group (overexpression group) demonstrated a significantly larger migration area at 24 h compared to the 0-hour time point (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the si-LEP group (knockdown group) exhibited a markedly smaller migration area (Fig. 4C). To more accurately assess the migration capacity independent of cell proliferation, we performed wound healing assay in the presence of mitomycin C (Figure S1). The results showed that LEP overexpression significantly enhanced the migration ability of colorectal cancer cells under mitomycin C treatment, whereas LEP knockdown markedly inhibited this migratory capacity. In the colony formation assay, notable differences were observed. Compared to the control group, CRC cells in the pc-LEP group displayed a significantly higher proliferation rate and formed more colonies (Fig. 4F). Conversely, CRC cells in the si-LEP group showed a significantly reduced proliferation capacity and fewer colony-forming cells (Fig. 4E). The Transwell assay further corroborated these findings, revealing a similar trend. CRC cells in the pc-LEP group exhibited significantly enhanced invasive ability compared to the control group (Fig. 4H). In contrast, the si-LEP group demonstrated significantly diminished invasive capacity (Fig. 4G). Based on these results, we conclude that LEP plays a pivotal role in activating the malignant phenotype of human CRC cells, promoting their proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings suggest that LEP may serve as a critical regulator in the progression of CRC.

Aberrant expression of LEP affects lipid metabolism reprogramming in CRC cells

Under normal physiological circumstances, LEP is capable of inhibiting fat synthesis and facilitating fat breakdown. Nevertheless, the LEP resistance phenomenon existing in CRC cells fosters abnormal lipid accumulation inside tumor cells. Previous research has indicated that when cells are exposed to an excessive amount of lipids, the endoplasmic reticulum continuously transforms these lipids into triglycerides and cholesterol esters, which then form lipid droplets (LDs)12. Among cellular organelles, LDs have a distinct structure. They are composed of a dense hydrophobic core of neutral lipids surrounded by a single phospholipid layer. Cancer cells frequently make use of LDs to guarantee energy production, thus promoting tumor growth13. Nile red staining serves as an indicator for detecting the content of LDs. In this study, we noticed that in human CRC cell lines, the intensity of Nile red staining diminished with the knockdown of LEP, while overexpression of LEP led to an increase in Nile red staining intensity (Fig. 5A and B). These observations strongly back the idea that the abnormal expression of LEP has an impact on the quantity of LDs in CRC cells. To have a more in-depth understanding of how LEP regulates lipid metabolism, we further meticulously investigated the changes in the mRNA levels of several key metabolic enzymes involved in lipid production in CRC cells. These enzymes include ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD). Based on our qRT-PCR data, the knockdown of LEP significantly decreased the mRNA levels of SCD and FASN in CRC cells. On the contrary, the overexpression of LEP had the opposite effect compared to LEP knockdown (Fig. 5C and D). In summary, we hold the view that LEP up-regulation promotes lipid accumulation in intestinal cancer cells.

Overexpression of LEP promotes liver metastasis of CRC and macrophage polarization

To explore the impact of LEP overexpression on the liver metastasis of CRC, we transfected MC38 cells with lentiviruses that overexpress LEP or empty lentiviruses. qRT-PCR was used to detect transfection efficiency (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, a mouse model of CRLM was developed by transfecting MC38 cells with lentiviruses. Three weeks later, liver specimens were harvested, and the quantity of liver metastatic foci and liver weight were documented. The findings demonstrated that, when compared with the control group, the number of metastatic tumors and liver weight in the LEP-OE group increased substantially (Fig. 6B and C). ICH of Ki67 showed that overexpression of LEP promoted the proliferation of CRC (Fig. 6B). IF analysis indicated an elevated abundance of the macrophage marker F4/80 in the liver metastatic tumors of mice in the LEP-OE group. In the LEP-OE group, the expression of CD206, a marker for M2-type macrophage polarization, increased significantly, whereas the expression of iNOS, a marker for M1-type polarization, decreased (Fig. 6D). The detection of macrophage-related cytokines in the liver showed that the expression of cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β associated with M2-type macrophages increased significantly in the LEP-OE group (Fig. 6E). Conversely, in the LEP-OE group, the expression of cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α associated with M1-type macrophages decreased; however, no statistically significant down regulation of IL-1β was observed (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that LEP may facilitate the occurrence and development of CRLM by influencing macrophage polarization. Moreover, we employed CLD to deplete macrophages in mice. IF analysis revealed that after intraperitoneal injection of CLD, the expression of the macrophage marker F4/80 in the livers of CRLM mice was significantly suppressed (Fig. 6G). Compared with the LEP-OE group, the number of metastatic tumors and liver weight in the LEP-OE + CLD group decreased significantly (Fig. 6H). Therefore, overexpression of LEP promotes CRLM by enhancing tumor proliferation and polarizing macrophages toward an M2 phenotype. Macrophage depletion via CLD reverses these effects, confirming the pivotal role of macrophage polarization in LEP-driven CRLM progression.

Overexpression of LEP promotes macrophages to secrete tumor-promoting related cytokines

Subsequently, we isolated mouse BMDM and co-cultivated them with MC38/LEP-OE (sn) and MC38/NC(sn) respectively for a period of three days. IF assays demonstrated that the expression of CD206 in BMDM co-cultured with MC38/LEP-OE(sn) was significantly elevated, whereas the expression of iNOS was notably reduced (Fig. 7A). The outcomes of qRT-PCR indicated that the M2 macrophage markers ARG1 and MRC1 were significantly up regulated in BMDM (Fig. 7B), while CD86 and NOS2 were down regulated (Fig. 7C). The expression levels of cytokines in the cell supernatant after co-culture were measured. We found that the expression of IL-10 in the supernatant of BMDM co-cultured with MC38/LEP-OE (sn) increased significantly (Fig. 7D), while there were no significant disparities in the expression of TNF-α, TGF-β, and IL-1β (Fig. 7E - G). IL-10 is a crucial cytokine secreted by macrophages that fosters tumor progression. Therefore, we administered AS101 (an IL-10 inhibitor) via intraperitoneal injection to CRLM mice, and the inhibitory effect of AS101 on IL-10 was verified by ELISA (Fig. 7H). In comparison with the LEP-OE group, the number of liver metastatic foci and liver weight in the LEP-OE + AS101 group decreased significantly, while there was no statistical difference between the Vector + AS101 group and the Vector group (Fig. 7I-J). These results imply that the overexpression of LEP might influence the occurrence and development of liver metastasis in CRC by affecting the secretion of IL-10 by macrophages.

Discussion

CRC ranks as the third most prevalent malignant tumor worldwide1. Liver metastasis in the advanced stage of CRC is one of the significant factors influencing the mortality rate of CRC patients5. Therefore, it is essential to understand the key molecular mechanisms involved in the progression of CRC liver metastasis. In this study, we employed bioinformatics methods to analyze the mRNA dataset from the TCGA database. Differentially expressed genes that might affect the progression of CRC were screened out. By combining with the immune-related gene set, 63 immune-related differentially expressed genes were selected. The PPI network, univariate Cox regression, and lasso regression were utilized to screen out 20 hub genes and 21 prognosis-related genes. Eventually, we focused on LEP. LEP is a hormone secreted by adipocytes, and its primary function is to regulate energy balance and body weight14. Under normal physiological conditions, LEP assists the body in maintaining an appropriate body weight by influencing appetite and metabolism. It regulates the decomposition and synthesis of fat through various pathways, thereby affecting fat storage15. In obese individuals, the secretion level of LEP is typically high. However, due to the long-term effect of high-level LEP, LEP resistance emerges, preventing LEP from effectively suppressing appetite and increasing metabolism, which may further result in weight gain16.

LEP resistance extends beyond associations with obesity and metabolic diseases; it may also influence tumor initiation and progression. Studies suggest LEP promotes tumor growth through multiple pathways. First, LEP stimulates angiogenesis, providing increased blood supply that facilitates tumor growth and dissemination17,18. Second, LEP regulates the immune system, potentially suppressing immune cell attacks on tumors and enabling immune evasion19. LEP resistance frequently correlates with metabolic syndrome, where dysfunctions like insulin resistance and hyperglycemia are closely linked to cancer development20. Research shows elevated LEP expression in breast cancer correlates with tumor growth and metastasis, potentially exacerbated by LEP resistance21. Furthermore, LEP resistance associates with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)- a significant liver cancer risk factor22. Our study identified abnormally elevated LEP in CRC liver metastases. In vitro experiments demonstrated that LEP overexpression inhibits LEPR expression, driving lipid metabolism reprogramming. This leads to lipid droplet accumulation in CRC cells, promoting tumor progression and immune microenvironment abnormalities. Lipid metabolism dysregulation represents a hallmark metabolic alteration in cancer cells23–25. Substantial evidence indicates enhanced lipid uptake, storage, and lipogenesis fuel tumor growth across cancers26. In CRC patients, lipid metabolism undergoes significant changes: increased lipogenesis, decreased fatty acid oxidation, metabolic rewiring (reduced cholesterol synthesis, increased lipid transfer/storage), and elevated secretion of lipid metabolites (e.g., lactate, free fatty acids, oxidized phospholipids) that remodel the tumor microenvironment23. Free fatty acids promote M2 macrophage polarization via PPARγ pathway activation, which suppresses NF-κB signaling, reduces pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α), and increases anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10)27,28. Abnormal lipid metabolism also aberrantly activates mTOR signaling, further promoting M2 polarization and influencing tumor progression29. Our findings establish LEP as a central orchestrator of CRLM progression through lipid metabolism rewiring, positioning it as a promising diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for metastatic CRC.

Macrophages are key components in the tumor immune microenvironment of CRC30–32. As highly plastic immune cells, macrophages are classified into subtypes-primarily M1 and M2-based on functional states, playing complex roles in tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis33,34. M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effects by presenting tumor antigens, activating T cells, and enhancing adaptive immunity35,36. Conversely, M2 macrophages promote tumor invasion and metastasis. Through secretion of cytokines and growth factors, they sustain cancer stem cell self-renewal and proliferation, driving tumor recurrence and metastasis7,35–37.Our study demonstrates that LEP overexpression promotes M2 macrophage polarization within the tumor immune microenvironment and increases secretion of the pro-tumorigenic cytokine IL-10. Elevated IL-10 expression in CRC correlates with tumor progression and poor prognosis38,39. IL-10 primarily functions by suppressing anti-tumor immunity while promoting angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis. Additionally, IL-10 reinforces M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization, amplifying its pro-tumor effects40. M2 macrophages further secrete anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factors, establishing a tumor-permissive microenvironment. Therapeutic strategies targeting IL-10 may improve patient prognosis and enhance anti-tumor immunity41.Critically, our findings reveal that CLD or AS101 intervention partially inhibits LEP-driven CRC liver metastasis progression in mice. This indicates LEP influences CRC liver metastasis by modulating macrophage polarization and tumor-related cytokine secretion. Targeting macrophages and IL-10 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC liver metastasis.

Our study suggests a potential association between LEP up-regulation and alterations in lipid metabolism and the tumor immune microenvironment of CRC, which may be linked to tumor progression and liver metastasis. These findings point toward LEP as a potential candidate for further evaluation in the management of CRLM. Future analyses involving independent cohorts of serum and tissue samples could potentially complement existing clinical indicators, offering preliminary insights for the early prediction and diagnosis of CRLM. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which LEP modulates lipid metabolism remain to be fully elucidated, and its specific impact on macrophage polarization warrants further investigation. Given the exploratory nature of this study, the clinical significance of these findings requires validation through multi-center, large-scale prospective studies.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings suggest that LEP overexpression may contribute to CRLM by being associated with lipid metabolism reprogramming in tumor cells and the potential facilitation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment involving M2 macrophage polarization and IL-10 secretion (Fig. 8). These results highlight LEP signaling, lipid metabolic pathways, and tumor-associated macrophages as potential candidate areas for further investigation in mitigating CRLM progression.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomic and clinical data for patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Following exclusion of cases with ambiguous metastasis (M) staging, the cohort was stratified into two groups: M0 (n = 337) and M1 (n = 65). Differential gene expression analysis between these groups was performed using the “EdgeR” package in R software, with significance thresholds set at |log2(fold change)| > 1 and p < 0.05. Immune-related gene sets were acquired from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.org/home). Data visualization, including volcano plots, heatmaps, and Venn diagrams, was conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed through the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and visualized using Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/download.html).

Survival analysis was performed using the survival R package, with Kaplan-Meier curves generated to assess prognostic significance. For gene expression-based stratification, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on median expression values using the Kaplan-Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Functional annotation was conducted through the DAVID database (version 6.8, https://David.NcifcRF.Gov/), including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses42–44. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the Gseaplot2 R package. Immune-related gene associations were investigated through the TIMER database (http://timer.comp-genomics.org/).

Specimen collection

Tissue samples, including primary colorectal cancer tumors, liver metastases, and corresponding adjacent normal tissues, were obtained from patients who underwent surgical procedures at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. The study adhered to the following inclusion criteria: (1) postoperative pathological confirmation of adenocarcinoma diagnosis; (2) pathological verification of distant metastasis. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) absence of histological diagnosis; (2) presence of a second primary malignancy; (3) distant metastasis to non-hepatic sites; (4) cases where only metastatic tumor resection was performed without concurrent primary tumor surgery. Cells and cell culture

The human colorectal carcinoma cell lines HCT116 and SW480, along with the murine colon adenocarcinoma cell line MC38, were procured from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). These cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic solution. Cell cultures were incubated under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, with regular monitoring and subculturing to maintain optimal growth conditions.

Cell transfection

In human CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW480, the knockdown and overexpression of the LEP gene were accomplished via small interfering RNAs and overexpression plasmids, respectively, which were provided by Transheep Biotechnology (China). For the initial cell seeding, 1 × 10⁵ cells were plated into each well of a six-well plate. The si-LEP or pc-LEP and liposome reagents were individually diluted in serum free medium. Subsequently, these two diluted components were combined and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to ensure the sufficient binding of si-LEP or pc-LEP to the liposomes, thereby forming complexes. The resulting complexes were then added to the culture plates containing the cells, followed by gentle mixing, and the cell culture was continued. Six hours post transfection, the medium was replaced with serum containing medium.In MC38 cells, overexpression of the LEP gene was achieved through lentiviral transfection using LEP-OE from Transheep Biotechnology (China). Initially, 1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded into each well of a six-well plate and cultured in 2 mL of medium for 24 h. After that, the volume of the medium was decreased to 1 mL, and an adequate amount of virus along with 40 µL of polyethylene glycol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were added. After an incubation period of 12 h, the cells were transferred to normal medium and subjected to screening with puromycin.The transfection efficiency was confirmed using qRT-PCR (Table S1).

Western blot

Treated cells or tissue specimens were collected, and total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio, China). The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE (Epizyme, China) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk solution at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, followed by exposure processing. The following primary antibodies were used in this study: LEP (1:1000, Servicebio, China) and GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech). All antibodies were diluted at appropriate ratios using an antibody diluent (New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co. Ltd).

qRT‑PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and samples with the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by means of the Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, China). To standardize the mRNA expression levels, GAPDH was employed as a reference (Table S1).

Transwell migration assay

700 µL of DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS was introduced into the lower chamber. The Transwell chamber (Corning, USA) was utilized to assess the cell migration ability. A total of 20,000 HCT116 cells and 20,000 SW480 cells were separately seeded into the upper chamber, each suspended in 200 µL of serum free DMEM medium. The experiment was divided into a test group and a control group. The cells were then incubated in a constant-temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide for 24 h. After the 24 h incubation period, the upper chamber was removed. The serum free medium was carefully aspirated, and the cells were gently rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After fixation, they were washed twice more with PBS and then allowed to air-dry. Next, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min and finally washed with PBS. The chamber was then inverted onto a glass slide, and the relevant statistical data were counted and recorded under a microscope.

Wound healing assay

HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into six-well plates for culture. When the cell density reached 90%, a standard 10-µL pipette tip was used to manually create a linear scratch on the confluent cell monolayer. The plate was washed with PBS to remove floating cells, and then complete DMEM medium was added. The plate was subsequently incubated in an incubator at 37 °C. Photographs were taken at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope, and the width of the scratch was recorded. The experiment was repeated using mitomycin C as follows: after culturing until the cell confluency exceeded 90%, the medium was aspirated, and the plate was washed twice with PBS to remove serum residues. Serum-free medium containing mitomycin C (concentration: 5 µg/mL) was added, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h to inhibit cell proliferation. After scratching with a 10-µL pipette tip, the plate was rinsed with PBS, and complete DMEM medium was added. The plate was then incubated in a 37 °C incubator, and photographs were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope to record the scratch width.

Colony formation assay

Cells (1 × 10³) from various intervention groups were seeded into six-well plates and cultured to allow colony formation. After a two-week period, the supernatant was removed by aspiration, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) for 30 min. Following fixation, the cells were washed once more with PBS and then stained with crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

1) Tissue Sample Preparation: Paraffin-embedded sections of mouse liver metastases were deparaffinized by sequential immersion in xylene and graded ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. 2) Blocking: Sections were incubated with 5% goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature to block non-specific binding. 3) Primary Antibody Incubation: Sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent (Servicebio Co., Ltd.) at 4 °C overnight: LEP (1:200, Servicebio, China); LEPR (1:500, Servicebio, China), CD206 (1:500, Servicebio, China), iNOS (1:500, Servicebio, China), F4/80 (1:500, Servicebio, China). 4) Secondary Antibody Incubation: After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488 ) diluted in PBS at room temperature for 60 min in the dark. 5) Nuclear Staining: Sections were stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL) at room temperature for 10 min to visualize cell nuclei. 6) Imaging: Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and representative fields were captured for analysis. Cell Sample Preparation: Cultured cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The subsequent steps for blocking, antibody incubation, and staining were identical to those described for tissue samples. Antibody Information: All primary antibodies used in the experiment were unconjugated antibodies and were purchased from Servicebio Co., Ltd. (China) and diluted according to the manufacturer’s recommendations using the provided antibody diluent.

Nile red staining

Forty-eight hours after transfection, CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW480 were counted and then seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. The cells were then cultured in an incubator for 24 h. Afterward, the cells to be detected were removed from the incubator. They were washed twice with PBS, and the PBS was then aspirated. Next, 4% paraformaldehyde fixative was added, and the cells were fixed at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed 2 times with PBS once more. Staining was conducted using Nile red, a fluorescent dye obtained from Solarbio Life Sciences, China. The staining process was carried out strictly following the manufacturer’s staining instructions to guarantee staining uniformity. Ultimately, photographs of the stained cells were taken using a fluorescence microscope.

Extraction and culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM)

C57BL/6 mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation and then disinfected with 75% alcohol. The femurs and tibias of the mice were dissected and adherent tissues were removed. Bone marrow was then flushed from the bones into a centrifuge tube using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The suspension was centrifuged at 1250 rpm for 5 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was treated with 3 ml of medium containing red blood cell lysate, mixed, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Finally, after removal of the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in DMEM for culture. Subsequently, the cells were treated with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 20 ng/mL) for 5 days for further experiments.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Tissue samples were first homogenized thoroughly in PBS. After complete grinding, they were centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute for 20 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected. For blood samples, they were subjected to centrifugation at a centrifugal force of 1000×g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatant was retrieved. As for the supernatant of the cell culture medium, it was centrifuged at a centrifugal force of 300×g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, while the precipitate was discarded. Subsequently, all the collected supernatants were stored in a refrigerator at a temperature of -20 °C for subsequent experiments. The concentrations of LEP, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β in the serum or the supernatants were assayed with a commercially available ELISA kit (MultiSciences, China) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The sample’s optical density (OD) was gauged at a wavelength of 450 nm and then analyzed by means of a microplate reader.

Establishment of the CRLM model in mice

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2404094). Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were used to develop the CRLM (colorectal cancer liver metastasis) model. Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing Medical University. All animals were housed under the same environmental conditions, including constant temperature, constant humidity, and light/dark cycles, to reduce variability and ensure consistency throughout the study. Before the experiment, the mice were induced into deep anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 50 mg/kg. In addition, buprenorphine was administered at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg to control pain. The body temperature of the mice was continuously monitored using a thermostatic blanket control device equipped with an accurate rectal probe and maintained at approximately 36.5 °C. The sample size was determined based on our previously published research. No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were set, and all animals were included in the final analysis without exception. A random number generator was used to generate the randomization sequence. Humanitarian endpoints were predefined to safeguard animal welfare, including criteria such as significant weight loss (> 30%), observable behavioral changes (such as reduced activity), and signs of pain or distress. All mice were divided into a control group (n = 5) and an LEP-OE group (n = 5). After anesthesia, a transverse incision was created in the left upper quadrant of the mouse abdomen. Using a 1 mL syringe, MC38 cells at a concentration of approximately 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL were slowly injected into the splenic capsule. A cotton swab was used to stop the bleeding at the injection site. Twenty minutes later, after the tumor cells had circulated systemically, the spleen was removed, the incision was sutured, and the mice were returned to their cages for recovery. Three weeks later, the mice were humanely euthanized by inhaling carbon dioxide in a sealed container, and then cervical dislocation was performed to confirm death. Photos of the liver were taken, and the liver was weighed. The number of liver tumors and the liver weight were recorded. Some liver tissues were preserved for further analysis. Regarding the mice treated with clodronate liposomes (CLD), starting from one day before the establishment of the CRLM model, 200 µL of CLD was injected intraperitoneally. Subsequently, additional injections were given every three days until the model was fully established. To minimize observer bias, a blinding procedure was implemented to ensure that the researchers conducting the measurements were unaware of the group assignments of each experimental group. We confirm that all experimental methods involving animals were carried out in strict accordance with relevant international, national, and institutional guidelines and regulations. Throughout the study, we continuously monitored and evaluated the health status of the animals to ensure that the highest ethical standards were maintained. This study was reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) 2.0 guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed and statistically processed using ImageJ software. GraphPad Prism 10.0 software was utilized. The data were analyzed and compared using the t-test or One-Way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The symbols used for significance levels are as follows: ns for p ≥ 0.05, * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001, and **** for p < 0.0001.
