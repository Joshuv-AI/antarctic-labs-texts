Characterizing Small Circuit Classes from 𝐅𝐀𝐂 0 \mathbf{FAC}^{0} to 𝐅𝐀𝐂 1 \mathbf{FAC}^{1} via Discrete Ordinary Differential Equations
Melissa Antonelli, Arnaud Durand, Juha Kontinen

Abstract
In this paper, we provide a uniform framework for investigating small circuit classes and bounds through the lens of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Following an approach recently introduced to capture the class of polynomial-time computable functions via ODE-based recursion schemas and later applied to the context of functions computed by unbounded fan-in circuits of constant depth ( 𝐅𝐀𝐂 0 \mathbf{FAC}^{0} ), we study multiple relevant small circuit classes. In particular, we show that natural restrictions on linearity and derivation along functions with specific growth rate correspond to kinds of functions that can be proved to be in various classes, ranging from 𝐅𝐀𝐂 0 \mathbf{FAC}^{0} to 𝐅𝐀𝐂 1 \mathbf{FAC}^{1} . This reveals an intriguing link between constraints over linear-length ODEs and circuit computation, providing new tools to tackle the complex challenge of establishing bounds for classes in the circuit hierarchies and possibly enhancing our understanding of the role of counters in this setting. Additionally, we establish several completeness results, in particular obtaining the first ODE-based characterizations for the classes of functions computable in constant depth with unbounded fan-in and Mod 2 gates ( 𝐅𝐀𝐂𝐂 ⁡ [ 𝟐 ] \mathbf{FACC[2]} ) and in logarithmic depth with bounded fan-in Boolean gates ( 𝐅𝐍𝐂 1 \mathbf{FNC}^{1} ).



1
Introduction

Implicit computational complexity is a active area of theoretical computer science that aims to provide machine-independent characterizations of relevant complexity classes.
In the context of recursion theory, a foundational work in this field is by Cobham
[
17
]
, who, in the 1960s, provided an implicit characterization of the class of polynomial-time computable functions (
\mathbf{FP}
) through a
limited
recursion schema.
This work not only led to the development of function algebras characterizing multiple classes different from poly-time (see
[
14
]
), but also inspired several alternative recursion-theoretic approaches
[
5
,
27
,
29
]
.
Among them, the one introduced in
[
9
]
, shows that ordinary differential equations (ODEs) provide a natural tool for algorithmic design and offers an original characterization of
\mathbf{FP}
, using
linear
systems of equations and
derivation along a logarithmically growing function
(such as the length function
\ell
).
Informally, the latter condition controls the number of recursion steps, while linearity governs the growth of objects generated during the computation.
This approach has recently been generalized to the continuous setting
[
6
,
7
]
and to the study of two small circuit classes
[
2
]
.
In this paper, we extend the latter far further by presenting a
comprehensive
investigation of small circuit classes through the lens of ODE schemas.

Although small circuit classes have been characterized in different ways, investigating them through the ODE framework has never been attempted (except for
[
2
]
).
This approach is both
interesting
, as for a descriptive method based on ODEs to be meaningful and fruitful, it must be able to adjust with very subtle changes in restricted models of computation, and
challenging
, because even simple and useful mathematical functions may not be computable within the considered classes and, consequently, the tools available may seem drastically restricted and the naturalness of the approach questionable.
However, the results of this paper show that this is not hopeless.
Very simple and natural restrictions on linearity – namely, constraining the values of coefficients and allowing (or not) the call for the sign of the defined function inside the linear term – and on the function along which derivation is performed, allows us to capture the very essence of small circuit computation.
Many classical functions – such as polylog bit sum, parity, majority, iterated sum and product, and binary search – are shown to be naturally encapsulated in very simple linear ODE families.
For this reason, we believe that our approach to this line of research, which is still in its beginning, can shed some new light on circuit computation and offer a simple (and machine-independent) framework for analyzing these notoriously hard problems through very
natural
and
uniform
constraints on linear ODEs.

Concretely, the results of this paper have led to completely original ODE-based characterizations for the classes of functions computable by unbounded fan-in circuits of constant depth and polynomial size that allow
Mod 2
gates (
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
) and by bounded fan-in circuits of logarithmic depth and polynomial size (
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
).
We have also improved our understanding of schemas capturing the classes of functions computable by unbounded fan-in circuits of constant depth and polynomial size (
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
), possibly including
Maj
gates (
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
), by generalizing and making them part of a more general picture.
Finally, we have shown that considering schemas with
derivative along
\ell_{2}=\ell\circ\ell
is definitely worth investigating: in particular, the corresponding linear schema is in
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
, somewhat mirroring what
\ell
-ODE does for
\mathbf{FP}
.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we present the basics of the method developed in
[
9
]
, the necessary notions to formulate our results, and recap definitions of the small circuit classes we will consider throughout the paper.
In Section
3
, we present, in a high-level yet systematic and uniform manner, the main schemas (differently) corresponding to the classes that will be studied.
In Section
4
, we introduce ODE-based schemas obtained by deriving along
\ell
and imposing different constraints on linearity, and we show that they are computable in the respective circuit classes.
We also provide improved completeness results for
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
, along with completely original ODE-based characterizations for
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
and
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.
In Section
5
, we introduce a new class of ODE schemas, the defining feature of which consists of deriving along
\ell_{2}
.
Our main result here is introduction of a very natural linear
\ell_{2}
-ODE the computation of which can be performed in
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.
We conclude in Section
6
pointing to possible directions for future research.
For space reasons, most proofs are omitted or only sketched in the paper, but full details can be found in the Appendix.
To make the exposition shorter, most of our characterizations are proved indirectly, as these proofs tend to be more compact.
However, direct proofs (both in the uniform and non-uniform settings) can be provided (but are not presented here).

2.1
Capturing Complexity via ODEs

One of the main approaches developed in implicit computational complexity is that based on recursion.
It was initiated by Cobham
[
17
]
, who gave the first characterization for
\mathbf{FP}
via a limited recursion schema.
This groundbreaking result has inspired not only similar characterizations for other classes, but also alternative recursion-based approaches to capture poly-time computable functions, such as safe recursion
[
5
]
and ramification
[
27
,
28
]
.
Among them, the proposal by
[
9
]
offers the advantages of not imposing any explicit bound on the recursion schema or assigning specific role to variables.
Instead, it is based on Discrete Ordinary Differential Equations (a.k.a. Difference Equations) and combines two novel features, namely derivation along specific functions, to control the number of computation steps, and a special syntactic form of the equation (linearity), to control the object size.

Recall that the
discrete derivative of
\mathbf{f}(x)
is defined as
\Delta\mathbf{f}(x)=\mathbf{f}(x+1)-\mathbf{f}(x)
, and that ODEs are expressions of the form

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}=\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

where
\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}
stands for the derivative of
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
considered as a function of
x
, for a fixed value for
\mathbf{y}
.
When some initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
is added, this is called Initial Value Problem (IVP).
Then, in order to deal with complexity, some restrictions are needed.
First, we introduce the notion of
derivation along functions
.

Notation 1
.

For
x\neq 0
, let
\ell(x)
denote the length function, returning the length of
x
written in binary, i.e.
\lceil
log
{}_{2}(x+1)\rceil
, and such that
\ell(0)=0
.
We use
\ell_{2}=\ell\circ\ell
.
For
u\geq 0
, let
\alpha(u)=2^{u}-1
denote the greatest integer whose length is
u
; similarly,
\alpha_{2}(u)=2^{2^{u}-1}-1
is the greatest integer
t
such that
\ell_{2}(t)=u
.
Finally, let
\div 2
denote integer division by 2, i.e. for all
x\in\mathbb{Z},x\div 2=\big\lfloor\frac{x}{2}\big\rfloor
.

Notice that the value of
\ell(x)
changes (it increases by 1) when
x
goes from
2^{t}-1
to
2^{t}
(for
t\geq 1
).
Similarly, the value of
\ell_{2}(x)
changes when
x
goes from
2^{2^{1}-1}-1
to
2^{2^{t}-1}
(for
t\geq 1
).

Definition 1

(
\ell
-ODE Schema)
.

Let
\mathbf{f},\lambda:\mathbb{N}^{p}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbf{h}:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}
be functions.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})}=\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

(1)

is a formal synonym of
\mathbf{f}(x+1,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})+\big(\lambda(x+1,\mathbf{y})-\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\times\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)
.
When
\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})=\ell(x)
, we call (1) a
length-ODE
or
\ell
-ODE.

Intuitively, one of the key properties of the
\lambda
-ODE schema is its dependence on the number of distinct values of
\lambda
, i.e., the value of
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
changes only when the value of
\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})
does.
Computation of solutions of
\lambda
-ODEs have been fully described in
[
10
]
.
Here, we focus on the special case of
\lambda=\ell
, which is particularly relevant for our characterizations.
If
\mathbf{f}
is a solution of (1) with
\lambda=\ell
and initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
, then
\mathbf{f}(1,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(0),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(0),\mathbf{y})\big)
.
More generally, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(x-1,\mathbf{y})+\Delta\big(\ell(x-1)\big)\times\mathbf{h}\big(x-1,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x-1,\mathbf{y})\big)=\mathbf{f}\big(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y}\big)+\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y})\big)
, where
\Delta(\ell(t-1))=\ell(t)-\ell(t-1)
.
Starting from
t=x\geq 1
and taking successive decreasing values of
t
, the first difference such that
\Delta(t)\neq 0
is given by the biggest
t-1
such that
\ell(t-1)=\ell(x)-1
, i.e.
t-1=\alpha(\ell(x)-1)
.
Setting
\mathbf{h}(\alpha(-1),\mathbf{y},\cdot)=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})
, it follows by induction that:

\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})\big)

In the present paper, both polynomial expressions (see
[
2
]
) and limited polynomial expressions are considered.
Let
\mathsf{sg}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the sign function over
\mathbb{Z}
, taking value 1 for
x>0
and 0 otherwise, and
\mathsf{cosg}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}

be the cosign function, such that
\mathsf{cosg}(x)=1-\mathsf{sg}(x)
.

Definition 2
.

A
limited
(
\mathsf{sg}
-)
polynomial expression
is an expression over the signature
\{+,-,\div 2\}
(and the
\mathsf{sg}
function, resp.) on the set of variables/terms
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{h}\}
and integer constants; (
\mathsf{sg}
-)
polynomial expressions
are defined analogously over the signature
\{+,-,\div 2,\times\}
.

To define the notion of degree for polynomial expressions, we follow the generalization to sets of variables introduced in
[
2
]
.

Definition 3
.

Given a list of variables
\mathbf{x}=x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{m}}
, for
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, the
degree of a set
\mathbf{x}
in a
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expression
P
,
\deg(\mathbf{x},P)
, is inductively defined as follows:

•

\deg(\mathbf{x},x_{i_{j}})=1
, for
x_{i_{j}}\in\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{m}}\}
, and
\deg(\mathbf{x},x_{i_{j}})=0
, for
x_{i_{j}}\not\in\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{m}}\}

•

\deg(\mathbf{x},\mathsf{sg}(P))=\deg(t\mathbf{x},c)=0
, for
c
being an integer constant

•

\deg(\mathbf{x},P+Q)=\deg(\mathbf{x},P-Q)=\max\{\deg(\mathbf{x},P),\deg(\mathbf{x},Q)\}

•

\deg(\mathbf{x},P\times Q)=\deg(\mathbf{x},P)+\deg(\mathbf{x},Q)
and
\deg(\mathbf{x},P\div 2)=\deg(\mathbf{x},P)
.

A
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expression
P
is said to be
essentially constant in a set of variables
\mathbf{x}
when
\deg(\mathbf{x},P)=0

and
essentially linear in
\mathbf{x}
, when
\deg(\mathbf{x},P)=1
.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
.
The expression
P(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=3x_{1}x_{3}+2x_{2}x_{3}
is essentially linear in
x_{1}
, in
x_{2}
and in
x_{3}
.
It is not essentially linear in
\mathbf{x}
, as
\deg(P,\mathbf{x})=2
.
The expression
P^{\prime}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}\mathsf{sg}((x_{1}-x_{3})\times x_{2})+x_{2}^{3}
is essentially linear in
x_{1}
, essentially constant in
x_{3}
and not linear in
x_{2}
.
Clearly,
P^{\prime}
is not linear in
\mathbf{x}
.

The concept of
linearity
allows us to control the growth of functions defined by ODEs.
In what follows, we will consider functions
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, i.e. vectors of functions
\mathbf{f}=f_{1},\dots,f_{d}
from
\mathbb{N}^{p+1}
to
\mathbb{Z}
, and introduce the linear
\ell
-ODE schema.

Definition 4

(Linear
\lambda
-ODE)
.

Given
\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}^{d},\mathbf{h},\lambda:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
linear
\lambda
-ODE
definable from
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{u}
if it is the solution of the IVP with initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
and such that:

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\lambda}=\mathbf{u}\big(\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{h}(x,\mathbf{y}),x,\mathbf{y}\big)

where
\mathbf{u}
is
essentially linear in the list of terms
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
.
For
\lambda=\ell
, such schema is called
linear length ODE
.

If
\mathbf{u}
is
essentially linear in
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
, there exist matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, whose coefficients are essentially constant in
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
, such that
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
and:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=\mathbf{A}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{h}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\times\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{B}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{h}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big).

Then, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
is

\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\Bigg(\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{t=u+1}\bigg(1+\mathbf{A}\Big(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{h}(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{f}(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})\Big)\bigg)\Bigg)\times\mathbf{B}\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{h}(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{f}(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})\big)

with the convention that
\prod^{x-1}_{x}\kappa(x)=1
and
\mathbf{(}\alpha(-1),\mathbf{y},\cdot,\cdot)=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})
.
Actually, the use of matrices is not necessary for the scope of this paper, but it is consistent with the general presentation of computation through essentially linear ODEs, of which full details can be found in
[
9
,
10
]
.

One of the main results of
[
9
]
is the implicit characterization of
\mathbf{FP}
by the algebra made of basic functions
\mathsf{0},\mathsf{1},\pi^{p}_{i},\ell,+,-,\times,\mathsf{sg}
, where
\pi^{p}_{i}
denotes the projection function, and closed under composition (
\circ
) and
\ell
-ODE.

2.2
On Small Circuit Classes and Function Algebras

Boolean circuits are vertex-labeled directed acyclic graphs whose nodes/gates are either input nodes, output nodes
or are labeled with a Boolean function from the set
\{\wedge,\vee,\neg\}
.
Boolean circuit with modulo gates allows in addition gates labeled
Mod 2
or, more generally,

Mod
p
, that output the sum of the inputs modulo
2
or
p
, respectively.
Finally, Boolean circuit with majority gates includes
Maj
gates, that output 1 when the majority of the inputs are 1’s.
A family of circuits is
\mathbf{Dlogtime}
-uniform if there is a Turing machine (with a random access tape) that decides in deterministic logarithmic time the direct connection language of the circuit, i.e. which, given 1
{}^{n},a,b
and
t\in\{\wedge,\vee,\neg\}
, decides if
a
is of type
t
and
b
is a predecessor of
a
in the circuit (and analogously for input/output nodes).
When dealing with circuits, the resources of interests are
size
, i.e. the number of gates, and
depth
, i.e. the length of the longest path from input to output (see
[
33
]
for further details and related results).

Definition 5

(Classes
\mathbf{FAC}^{i}
and
\mathbf{FNC}^{i}
)
.

For
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{AC}^{i}
(resp.,
\mathbf{NC}^{i}
) is the class of language recognized by a
\mathbf{Dlogtime}
-uniform family of Boolean circuits of unbounded (resp. bounded) fan-in gates of polynomial size and depth
O((
log
n)^{i})
.
We denote by
\mathbf{FAC}^{i}
(resp.,
\mathbf{FNC}^{i}
) the corresponding function class.

It is known that unbounded (poly
(n)
) fan-in can be simulated using a bounded tree of depth
O(
log
n)
, so that
\mathbf{FNC}^{i}\subseteq\mathbf{FAC}^{i}\subseteq\mathbf{FNC}^{i+1}
.
However, the inclusion has been proved to be strict only for
i=0
.
In contrast, when dealing with classes with counting ability, interesting bounds have been established.

Definition 6

(Classes
\mathbf{FACC[p]}
and
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
)
.

The class
\mathbf{ACC[p]}
(resp.,
\mathbf{TC}^{0}
) is the class of languages recognized by a
\mathbf{Dlogtime}
-uniform family of Boolean circuits including
Mod
p
(resp.,
Maj
) gates of polynomial size and constant depth.
We denote by
\mathbf{FACC[p]}
(resp.,
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
) the corresponding function class.

We can even consider other levels in the
\mathbf{FTC}
hierarchy, such that for
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{FTC}^{i}
corresponds to classes of functions computed by families of unbounded fan-in circuits including
Maj
gates, of polynomial size and depth
O
((log
n)^{i}
).
It is known that the inclusion
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}\subset\mathbf{FACC[2]}
is proper, as the parity function cannot be computed in constant depth by standard unbounded fan-in Boolean gates, but it can be if
Mod 2
gates are added.
Moreover, since any
Mod
p
gate can be expressed by

,
\mathbf{FACC[p]}\subset\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
.

As mentioned, Cobham’s work paved the way to several recursion-theoretic characterizations for classes other than
\mathbf{FP}
, including small circuit ones.
In 1988/90, Clote introduced the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{0}
to capture functions in the log-time hierarchy (equivalent to
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
) using so-called

(CRN)
[

,

]
.
The greater classes
\mathbf{FAC}^{i}
and

are captured adding

[

]
, while

\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
has been characterized both in terms of the
k
-bounded recursion on notation
(

-BRN) schema
[

]
and relying on the basic function
tree

[

]
.
In 1990, an alternative algebra (and logic), based on a schema called

[

]
.
In 1991, a new recursion-theoretic characterization (and arithmetic

was presented
[

]
.
In 1992 and 1995, other small circuit classes, including

[

,

]
.
More recently, characterizations for
\mathbf{FNC}^{i}
were developed in
[

]
, this time following the ramification approach.
In
[
2

, the first ODE-based characterizations for

Related approaches to capture small circuit classes have also been provided in the framework of model- and proof-theory, both in the first and second order

3
Towards a Uniform Understanding of Small Circuit Classes

We informally outline here the comprehensive investigation of small circuit classes in terms of ODEs (see Figure
1
for a complete description).
By slightly modifying the restrictions on the linearity allowed in
\ell
-ODE and
\ell_{2}
-ODE, we can perform computation and, in several cases, characterize multiple function classes in the range from
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
to
\mathbf{FAC}^{1}
.

Notation 2
.

An equation or, by extension, an ODE schema is said to be
strict
when it does not include any call to
f(x,\mathbf{y})
in
A
or
B
(not even under the scope of the sign function).
We say that
B
includes
simple
calls to
f(x,\mathbf{y})
if
f(x,\mathbf{y})
occurs in
B
only in expressions of the form
s=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
.

For clarity’s sake, we will use
k(x,\mathbf{y})
for functions with limited range, typically in
\{0,1\}
, and
K(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y}))
for (possibly limited)
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expressions of restricted range.

We use
s\big(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y})\big)
or (when not ambiguous) simply
s(x)
as an abbreviation for signed expressions in which
f(x,\mathbf{y})
can occur, i.e.
s=\mathsf{sg}\big(E(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y}))\big)
, where
E
is any polynomial expression in which
x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y})
and
f(x,\mathbf{y})
may appear.
Finally, for readability, we often use

•

s(t)
as a shorthand for
s\big(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y}),f(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})\big)
,

•

K(t,\mathbf{y},h,f)
, or even
K(t)
, as a shorthand for
K(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y}),f(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y}))
,

•

K_{i}(t)
(or
B_{i}(t)
), with
i\in\{0,1\}
, as a shorthand for

The Class

We start by revising the characterization of

Definition 7

(Schemas
\ell

\ell

Given

\ell

.
As anticipated, this investigation demonstrates a strong link between depth constraints over circuits and the syntactical constraints that define the corresponding linear length ODE.
Particularly interesting in this setting is the IVP defined by linear equations in which

Lemma 1
.

Let
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
be computable in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.
Then,
f(x,\mathbf{y})
defined from
g
and
h
as the solution of the IVP made of
f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=\big(K(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y}))-1\big)\times f(x,\mathbf{y})

where
K\in\{0,1\}
is a limited
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expression and
f(x,\mathbf{y})
occurs in it only under the scope of the sign function in expressions of the form
s=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
, is in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Proof Sketch.

By definition of
\ell
-ODE,
f(x,\mathbf{y})=\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}K\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}),f(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})\big)
, with the convention that
K(\alpha(-1),\cdot)=g(\mathbf{y})
.
Clearly, if there is a
t\in\{0,\dots,x\}
such that
K(t)=0
, then
f(x,\mathbf{y})=0
.
Therefore, we compute
f(x,\mathbf{y})=\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}K_{1}(u)
which is either 0, if
g(\mathbf{y})=0
or there is a
K_{1}(u)=0
, or
g(\mathbf{y})
, otherwise.
By hypothesis, we can compute in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
both
g(\mathbf{y})
and, for any
u\in\{0,\dots,\ell(x)-1\}
,
K_{1}(u)
. Thus, we conclude in one layer defined by an unbounded fan-in
\wedge
-gate.
∎

This result can actually be strengthened by showing that it holds even for generalized expressions of the form
s=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y})-r)
occurring in
K
.

To conclude the presentation of schemas for

, let us consider simple cases of bounded search encoded by the aforementioned ODE schemas.
(For further details, see Appendix
.)

Example 2

(BSearch)
.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{p+1}

Then, for all

(\forall z\leq\ell(x))R(z,\mathbf{y})=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
,
where

and

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=(h_{R}(x,\mathbf{y})-1)\times f(x,\mathbf{y}),

which is clearly an (very limited) instance of the schema presented in Lemma

.

reaches or not a boundary, say

.
This can be expressed in a natural way by an instance of the (generalized) non-strict schema (in

4.2
The Class
\mathbf{FACC[2]}

By weakening the constraints on linearity of the schemas which define
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
, we introduce an
\ell
-ODE the computation of which is provably in
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
and actually characterizes this class.
We consider a non-strict schema such that
A=-1
and
B=K\in\{0,1\}
.
As we will see (Section
4.4
), it is a special case of the schema we will introduce to capture
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.
Together with the functions and schemas defining
\mathbb{ACDL}
, it is also enough to capture
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
, thus providing the very first ODE-based characterization for this small circuit class “with counters”.

Definition 8

(Schema
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
if it is the solution of the IVP with
f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=-f(x,\mathbf{y})+K\big(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

where
K\in\{0,1\}
is a limited
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expression and
f(x,\mathbf{y})
occurs in
K
only under the scope of the sign function.

Notably, this schema allows us to capture the computation performed by
Mod 2
gates and to rewrite the parity function in a very natural way.

Example 3

(The Parity Function)
.

Let
z
be a string of
\ell(z)=n
bits.
To count the number of 1’s in
z
we consider the following system defined by
\mathsf{par}(0,y)=\mathsf{BIT}(0,y)
and:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathsf{par}(x,y)}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{par}(x,y)+\big(\mathsf{sg}(\mathsf{par}(x,y))\times\mathsf{cosg}(\mathsf{BIT}(\ell(x)+1,y))+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\mathsf{cosg}(\mathsf{par}(x,y))\times\mathsf{sg}(\mathsf{BIT}(\ell(x)+1,y))\big)

which is clearly an instance of
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
.
The parity function is now computed by
\mathsf{par}(z,z)
.

Notice that this example shows that
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
really increases the power of
\ell
-ODE
1
, as the parity function is known not to be in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
(see
[
22
]
).
This also indicates that
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
is actually a schema capturing the specific computational power corresponding to
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
.
Moreover, it is shown that the desired closure property holds for it.

Lemma 2
.

If
f(x,\mathbf{y})
is defined by
\ell
-b
0
ODE
from functions in
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
, then
f(x,\mathbf{y})
is in
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
as well.

Proof Sketch.

For simplicity, let us consider the simple case of
f(x,\mathbf{y})
occurring only under the scope of the sign function in expressions of the form
s=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
.
Relying on the fact that
K(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y}))=K_{1}(x)\times\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))+K_{0}(x)\times\mathsf{cosg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
, we can rewrite the equation defining
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=-f(x,\mathbf{y})\times\big(K_{0}(x)=K_{1}(x)\big)+\big(K_{0}(x)=K_{1}(x)\big)\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\Big(\big((K_{0}(x)=1)\times\textsc{Mod 2}\Big(\sum^{\ell(x)}_{i=x}\big(K_{0}(i)=K_{0}(i+1)\big)\Big)=0\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\big((K_{0}(x)=0)\times\textsc{Mod 2}\Big(\sum^{\ell(x)}_{i=x}\big(K_{0}(i)=K_{0}(i+1)\big)\Big)=1\big)\Big).

Intuitively, if
K_{0}(t)\neq K_{1}(t)
, no change occurs.
In contrast, if
K_{0}(t)=K_{1}(t)
we restart the computation (as the preceding value of the input must not be the last one for which
K_{0}=K_{1}
) by removing the previous value of
f(t,\mathbf{y})
, and return 1 if either one of the following holds:

i

K_{0}(t)=K_{1}(t)=1
, i.e.
K(t)
“generates” a 1, and the number of changes of values (temporarily assuming that
t
is the last value in
\{0,\dots,\ell(x)-1\}
such that
K_{0}(t)=K_{1}(t)
) between subsequent values, i.e.
K_{0}(t)
and
K_{0}(t+1)
, is such that 1 (possibly alternating) “propagates” until the end of the computation or

ii

K_{0}(t)=K_{1}(t)=0
, i.e.
K(t)
“generates” a 0, and the subsequent number of changes of values is such that 0 does not propagate to the final value, i.e.
Mod 2
(
\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{i=t}\big(K_{0}(t)=K_{0}(t+1)\big)\Big)=1
.

It is easy to see that the value computed in this way corresponds to the final value of the computation, i.e.
f(x,\mathbf{y})
is given by
f(t,\mathbf{y})
such that for any
t^{\prime}\in\{t,\dots,\ell(x)\},K_{0}(t^{\prime})\neq K_{1}(t^{\prime})
.
Clearly, this is a special case of
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
in which no call to
f(x,\mathbf{y})
occurs in
A
or
B
, namely of the form
\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=-k(x,\mathbf{y})\times f(x,\mathbf{y})+k(x,\mathbf{y})\times k^{\prime}(x,\mathbf{y})
, for
k(x,\mathbf{y}),k^{\prime}(x,\mathbf{y})\in\{0,1\}
.
Computation through this schema can be implemented by circuits of constant depth and polynomial size including
Mod 2
gates:
for any
t\in\{0,\dots,\ell(x)-1\}
, we can compute
k(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})
and
k^{\prime}(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})
in parallel and check both their mutual consistency, i.e. whether
k(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})=k^{\prime}(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})
, and the consistency between subsequent pairs, i.e. whether
k(\alpha(t),\mathbf{y})=k(\alpha(t+1),\mathbf{y})
; then, using
Mod 2
gates, we can compute whether the number of changes of values is even or odd and conclude in constant depth via (unbounded fan-in)
\vee
-gate.
(For further details, see Appendix
).
∎

Since
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
has been shown to capture computations performed by
Mod 2
gates, our characterization for
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
is obtained by simply endowing
\mathbb{ACDL}
with it.

Theorem 2
.

\mathbf{FACC[2]}=[\mathsf{0},\mathsf{1},\ell,\mathsf{sg},+,-,\div 2,\pi^{p}_{i},\#;\circ,\ell\emph{-ODE}_{1},\ell\emph{-ODE}_{3},\ell\emph{-b}_{0}\emph{ODE}]

Proof.

(\supseteq)
Following the proofs
[
2
, Prop. 12, 17]
plus Lemma
2
for
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
.

(\subseteq)
Consequence of
[
2
, Th. 18]
and of the fact that the parity function can be simulated by
\ell\text{-b}_{0}\text{ODE}
(see Example
3
).
∎

4.3
The Class
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}

In this section, we introduce schemas the computation of which is proved to be in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
.
One is defined in terms of strict linear
\ell
-ODE and provides a new, compact characterization for this class.
The second schemas considered are not strict, as they include simple calls to
f(x,\mathbf{y})
, and are defined uniformly with respect to corresponding (i.e., non-strict) schemas for
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
(as well as for
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
and
\mathbf{FAC}^{1}
, see Sections
4.4
and

We start by dealing with the linear ODE schema defined deriving along
\ell
and so that
A(x,\mathbf{y})
and
B(x,\mathbf{y})
are both
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expressions, with no call to
f(x,\mathbf{y})
.

Definition 9

(Schema
\ell\text{-pODE}
)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell\text{-pODE}
from
g
and
h
, if it is the solution of the IVP with initial value
f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and such that

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=A\big(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\times f(x,\mathbf{y})+B\big(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

where
A
and
B
are
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expressions.

This compactly encapsulates not only iterated multiplication and addition but also
\ell
-ODE
1
and
\ell
-ODE
3
computation.

Example 4

(Counting and Iterated Addition in
\ell\text{-pODE}
)
.

Notably,
BCount
(resp. iterated addition,
ItAdd
) can be expressed as a special case of
\ell\text{-pODE}
such that
A=0
and
B=k(x,\mathbf{y})
with
k,g\leq 1
, i.e. by considering an IVP such that
f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=k(x,\mathbf{y})\qquad(\mbox{resp. }\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=B(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y})).

Taking
g(\mathbf{y})=\mathsf{BIT}(0,y)
and
k(x,\mathbf{y})=\mathsf{BIT}(\ell(x),y)
,
f(x,x)=\sharp\{u:\mathsf{BIT}(u,x)=1\}
.

These examples are especially relevant as they reveal that
\ell\text{-pODE}
is really more expressive than all strict schemas introduced so far (
BCount
is not computable in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
, see
[
31
,
32
]
).
The closure property can be shown to hold for
\ell\text{-pODE}
by relying on the following result.

Proposition 1
.

If
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
are computable in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
and
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell\text{-pODE}
from
g
and
h
, then
f
is computable in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
as well.

Proof.

By definition of
\ell
-ODE, such
f
satisfies

f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\Bigg(\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{t=u+1}\bigg(1+A(r,\mathbf{y})\bigg)\Bigg)\times B(u,\mathbf{y})

with the convention that
\prod^{z-1}_{z}\kappa(x)=1

Lemma 3
.

Let

•

by
t

Count

•

In constant depth, for any

\neg K_{0}(t-1)
and
K_{0}(t)
and select the corresponding

For the second equation type, first note that, if

(which is clearly computable in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
). Let

.
Then, for any

,
\mathsf{sg}(f(0,\mathbf{y}))>0
.
Therefore, by definition of
\ell
-ODE,

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\big(1+K_{1}(u)\big),

with
\prod^{t^{\prime}}_{t=-1}\kappa=g(\mathbf{y})
.
This can be computed in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
:
g(\mathbf{y})
and each

, for

can be computed in parallel in the beginning (

and

being computable in
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
for hypothesis and

-polynomial expression by definition) and

The Class

In this section, we consider two restrictions on

Lemma 4

(Bounded Concatenation with Simple Calls)
.

Let

and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
be computable in
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.
Then, computation through the schema defined by the IVP with initial value
f(0,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
and such that:

\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=f(x,\mathbf{y})+K\big(x,\mathbf{y},h(x,\mathbf{y}),f(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

where

is a
\mathsf{sg}
-polynomial expression, and
f(x,\mathbf{y})
appears in it only under the scope of the sign function in expressions of the form
s=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
, is in
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.

Proof Sketch.

By definition of
\ell
-ODE,
f(x,\mathbf{y})=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}2^{\ell(x)-u-1}\times

K(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}),

f(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}))
, with the convention that
K(\alpha(-1))=g(\mathbf{y})
.
Let us define the truncated sum (or concatenation) between
a
and
b-1
bounded by
\ell(x)-1
, as
S(a,b)=\sum^{b-1}_{u=a}K\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}),(u)\big)
, with
S(u)=S(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})=f(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})
.
In particular, we consider two sums in which the sign of the first term is assumed to be either 0 or 1:
S_{0}(a,b)=\sum^{b-1}_{u=a}K(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}),S(u))
, with
S_{0}(a)=0
and
S_{1}(a,b)=\sum^{b-1}_{u=a}2^{\ell(x)-u-1}\times K_{1}(u)
.
Therefore, for any
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

f(x,\mathbf{y})=S(-1,\ell(x)/2)+S(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))=+\begin{cases}S(-1,\ell(x)/2)\\
S_{0}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))\times(1-s(\ell(x)/2-1))\\
S_{1}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))\times s(\ell(x)/2-1).\end{cases}

Clearly,
S(-1,\ell(x)/2),S_{0}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))
and
S_{1}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))
can be computed in parallel:
we know the actual starting value of
S_{0}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))
, which is 0, while to compute
S_{1}(\ell(x)/2,\ell(x))=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=\ell(x)/2}2^{\ell(x)-u-1}K_{1}(u)
it is enough to know that, for any
a>\ell(x)/2
,

.
Once
S(-1,\ell(x)/2)=f(\alpha(\ell(x)/2),\mathbf{y})
is computed and its sign tested, we can select between
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
accordingly.
We conclude by taking the concatenation between
S(-1,\ell(x)/2)
and either
S_{0}
or
S_{1}
.
∎

This schema can be generalized so to allow calls to

in expressions of the form

Remark 3
.

The condition that

We now move to the new characterization of

Definition 10

(Schema

)

Given

Lemma 5

If

6
Conclusion

The objective of this paper was to start a new and uniform approach for studying circuit complexity through the lens of ODEs.
This approach allows to consider bounds on size or depth in circuit computation in terms of constraints on the linearity of the equations defining recursion schemas in two main contexts of derivatives: derivation along
\ell
and along
\ell_{2}
.
Additionally, we have investigated completeness both improving and placing in a coherent setting the results obtained in
[
2
]
for
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
, and providing what, to the best of our knowledge, are the first ODE-based characterizations for
\mathbf{FACC[2]}
and
\mathbf{FNC}^{1}
.
In addition of being a simple and natural way to design algorithms, ODE tools have also enabled us to obtain relatively simple and self-contained membership proofs and characterizations compared to the often convoluted constructions or external theories referenced in previous literature.

Many directions for future investigation remain open.
A natural one would be the study of characterizations for the entire
\mathbf{FAC}
and
\mathbf{FNC}
hierarchies. For this, one has to identify which ODE features or characteristic would help to jump from one depth level, say
(\log n)^{i}
, to the next.
Furthermore, a possible next step of our research involves defining (strict)
\ell

However, this study has just been initiated here, and a systematic investigation is left for future work.

Developing proof-theoretical counterparts to our ODE-style algebras, in the form of natural rule systems inspired by the ODE design to syntactically characterize the corresponding classes, represents another promising avenue for exploration.
