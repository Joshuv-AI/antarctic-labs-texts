Mitochondrial DNA variation reveals shared maternal lineages between Kazakh and Turkish fat-tailed sheep breeds
Authors: Bekzat Nabiyev, Uğur Şen, Alper Koçyi̇ği̇t, Hasan Meydan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocal livestock breeds represent a significant genetic resource shaped by historical human migrations. While morphological similarities between Central Asian and Anatolian sheep breeds have long suggested a shared ancestry, molecular evidence linking these populations to specific historical migrations remains limited. This study investigates the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of Kazakh (Edilbay) and Turkish (Akkaraman, Morkaraman, Awassi) fat-tailed sheep breeds using mtDNA D-loop sequencing to explore the maternal lineages of these historically connected populations.MethodsThe study included Edilbay sheep from Atyrau (Kazakhstan) and Akkaraman (Kırşehir), Morkaraman (Iğdır), and Awassi (Şanlıurfa) sheep from Türkiye. Genomic DNA was extracted using the salt precipitation method. A 531 bp fragment of the mtDNA control region (D-loop) was amplified. Genetic diversity indices were calculated using DnaSP v6, while population structure (AMOVA and pairwise FST) was analyzed in ARLEQUIN v3.5, and phylogenetic relationships and haplogroup networks were reconstructed using an unrooted Neighbor-Joining tree in SplitsTree 4 and a Median-Joining network in Network 4.1, respectively.ResultsOur results reveal high levels of genetic diversity (Hd: 0.990) in the studied populations, indicating a rich maternal genetic diversity in the region. We found a very low genetic differentiation (FST = 0.017) and shared haplogroup structure (predominantly Haplogroup B) between the Kazakh Edilbay and Turkish Morkaraman breeds. This genetic affinity reflects historical connections among sheep populations distributed across regions historically linked by pastoral movements and trade routes. In contrast, the Awassi breed formed a genetically distinct and isolated cluster (FST > 0.30), reflecting distinct maternal lineages rooted in the Fertile Crescent.ConclusionsThese findings clarify the maternal genetic structure of these indigenous breeds and support a historical connection, highlighting the Morkaraman and Edilbay as conserved genetic reservoirs of a shared pastoral heritage.Keywords: Kazakh and Turkish Fat-Tailed Sheep, mtDNA D-loop region, Maternal lineages, Haplotype analyses, Phylogeography

Background

Since their initial domestication, sheep (Ovis aries) have dispersed globally alongside human civilizations, evolving into distinct local breeds shaped by migration routes and environmental adaptation [1–4]. In the steppes of Central Asia and the highlands of Anatolia, fat-tailed breeds such as Edilbay, Akkaraman, Morkaraman, and Awassi have become economically important. These breeds are well-adapted to harsh conditions, thriving in sparse pastures and enduring extreme seasonal variations where other livestock might fail [5, 6].

Understanding the phylogeographic history of these breeds is critical not only for conserving their unique genetic reservoirs but also for tracing the historical movements of the pastoralist communities. While morphological similarities, particularly the fat-tail phenotype adapted to harsh environments, suggest a shared pastoral heritage between Central Asian and Anatolian sheep breeds [3, 7, 8], molecular evidence linking these geographically distant populations remains limited. Earlier studies have focused on phenotypic variation or local genetic characterization [9], thus, the transcontinental maternal lineages remain poorly understood [10].

The Morkaraman and Akkaraman sheep breeds are raised in nearly every region of Türkiye. In fact, they hold the top two positions among the most commonly raised sheep breeds in Türkiye [6]. Both sheep breeds are classified as fat-tailed sheep. They are native Turkish sheep breeds adapted to harsh weather conditions and challenging, limited pasture conditions. The Awassi (Ivesi) sheep breed is also classified as a fat-tailed sheep. In addition to being raised in Türkiye, it is a sheep breed widely raised in Middle Eastern countries. It is widely raised in southeastern Türkiye and the Mediterranean region. The Edilbay sheep breed is a native Kazakh sheep known as a derivative of Kazakhstan’s native sheep breeds. Like the Morkaraman, Akkaraman, and Awassi sheep breeds, it is classified as a fat-tailed sheep. The Edilbay is raised in Kazakhstan’s variable steppe climate, and it survives during the harsh winters of Central Asia by taking advantage of pastures [5].

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), particularly the hypervariable D-loop region, is a widely used marker clarifying these evolutionary relationships due to its high mutation rate, lack of recombination, and maternal inheritance [11, 12]. This region allows for the precise detection of haplotypes and phylogenetic clusters, making it a valuable tool to analyze the domestication history and dissemination routes of livestock species [13]. Recent advances in molecular techniques have further solidified the reliability of mtDNA in distinguishing breed origins and defining conservation priorities [14, 15].

Therefore, this study aims to determine the phylogenetic relationships, genetic diversity, and maternal lineages of the Kazakh Edilbay and Turkish Akkaraman, Morkaraman, and Awassi sheep breeds. By sequencing the mtDNA D-loop region, we seek to investigate the shared maternal lineages between these geographically distant breeds, reflecting potential historical pastoral movements and provide a molecular basis for the conservation of these indigenous genetic resources.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Local Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Ondokuz Mayıs University (Protocol no: 2023-52 and date: 22 June 2023). A total of unrelated 110 sheep were sampled from different populations in Türkiye and Kazakhstan (Table 1). The study included Edilbay sheep sampled from three private farms in Atyrau (Kazakhstan), as well as Akkaraman (Kırşehir), Morkaraman (Iğdır), and Awassi (Şanlıurfa) sheep collected from nine private farms across Türkiye. For each breed, blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of individuals using sterile EDTA tubes and transported via cold chain and stored at − 80 °C until processing. To ensure that the sampled individuals were unrelated, available farm pedigree and mating records were carefully examined for all breeds. Blood samples from the Kazakh Edilbay breed were transported to Türkiye under cold-chain conditions after obtaining all necessary official transport permits.

DNA isolation, PCR Amplification of mtDNA D-loop Region and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the salt precipitation method described by Miller et al. [16]. A 531 bp fragment of the mtDNA control region (D-loop) was amplified to avoid tandem repeats, using primers designed by Chen et al. [17]: Forward 5’-ACT GCT TGA CCG TAC ATA GTAC-3’ and Reverse 5’-AGT ATT GAG GAC GGG GTAA-3’. PCR amplifications were performed in a 50 µL reaction volume containing 50 ng genomic DNA, 10×Dream Taq buffer (with MgCl2), 0.2 mM dNTPs, 10 pM of each primer, and 0.75 U Taq DNA Polymerase. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C (10 s), annealing at 55 °C (15 s), and extension at 72 °C (15 s), with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were visualized on 2% agarose gels, purified, and sequenced by a commercial service using an ABI 3730 capillary sequencer in a single direction.

Molecular data analysis

Raw sequence chromatograms were visually inspected using FinchTV software. Low-quality ends were trimmed, and any ambiguous nucleotide peaks were manually verified and corrected before being aligned with the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MEGA 7 [18]. Genetic diversity indices - including the number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (π) - were calculated using DnaSP v6 [19]. To assess population structure, Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) and pairwise FST values were computed using ARLEQUIN v3.5 [20]. To infer historical demographic dynamics and test for potential population expansion events, Fu and Li’s D and F neutrality statistics were computed. Furthermore, mismatch distribution analysis was performed, and the demographic expansion models were statistically tested by calculating the raggedness index (r) and Ramos-Onsins and Rozas’s R2 statistic using DnaSP v6 [21]. Phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using an unrooted UPGMA tree based on the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) substitution model in SplitsTree 4 [22] for the animals studied and reference haplogroups (Haplogroup A: HM236174; Haplogroup B: HM236176; Haplogroup C: HM236178; Haplogroup D: HM236180; Haplogroup E: HM236182; O. musimon: HM236185; O. ammon: HM236188; O. vignei: HM236186). Finally, a Median-Joining network was constructed using Network 4.1 [23] to visualize haplogroup relationships among the studied breeds.

As a result of haplotype analyses, the number of haplotypes (h) ranged from 13 (Awassi) to 27 (Akkaraman) sheep populations (Table 2). The highest values of haplotype diversity (Hd) was detected in Akkaraman (0.994) whereas the lowest was observed in Awassi (0.951). In terms of nucleotide diversity (π) and average number of nucleotide differences (k), the highest and lowest values were estimated in Akkaraman and Awassi, respectively. When all populations were considered together, the average haplotype diversity and nucleotide diversity were calculated as 0.990 ± 0.007 and 0.017, respectively.

Discussion

Domestic sheep (Ovis aries) is now distributed in nearly every region worldwide and among the most abundant of all farm animals. Native sheep breeds - Edilbay, Morkaraman, Akkaraman, and Awassi - are vital to the livestock sectors of Kazakhstan and Türkiye, having successfully adapted to harsh steppe and Anatolian pasture conditions. Within the framework of conserving animal genetic resources, this study investigates the genetic diversity and origins of these breeds to determine the potential impact of historical Oghuz migrations on their population structure. To this end, haplotype and nucleotide diversity indices were employed as primary metrics to assess the genetic variation and evolutionary relationships among these populations.

Genetic diversity and expansion

In this study, the sequencing of the mtDNA D-loop region revealed a high level of genetic diversity among the studied sheep populations. The overall haplotype diversity and nucleotide diversity were calculated as 0.990 ± 0.007 and 0.017, respectively. These high values suggest that the Edilbay, Akkaraman, Morkaraman, and Awassi breeds have retained a rich genetic pool and have likely not experienced severe genetic bottlenecks during their development. Among the breeds, the Akkaraman population exhibited the highest haplotype diversity (0.994 ± 0.046), which may be attributed to its wide geographic distribution in Türkiye and potential historical admixture with other local breeds. High genetic diversity is a characteristic feature of sheep breeds located near domestication centers, supporting the hypothesis that Anatolia and Central Asia served as key regions for sheep genetic resources. Our findings indicate a rich maternal genetic background, which is consistent with previous studies on Near Eastern sheep breeds [23–26].

Historical population dynamics were further investigated using mismatch distribution and additional neutrality statistics (Table 7). Fu and Li’s D and F statistics were statistically non-significant (P > 0.05) across all individual breeds and the overall population, indicating that the observed nucleotide variations are primarily driven by demographic events rather than background selection. The mismatch distribution analyses revealed very low raggedness indices (r), ranging from 0.0084 in Akkaraman to 0.0374 in Awassi, with an exceptionally low overall value (r = 0.0050). Similarly, Ramos-Onsins and Rozas’s (R2) statistics remained low across all groups (overall R2 = 0.1215). Coupled with the highly negative overall Fu’s Fs value (-76.333; P < 0.01), these demographic indices support a model of recent and sudden historical population expansion, complementing the star-like haplogroup topologies observed in the median-joining network.

Furthermore, the mismatch distribution analysis exhibited a bimodal demographic pattern (Fig. 2), primarily reflecting the presence of distinct maternal lineages (multiple haplogroups) within the studied populations, while the smooth distribution of the curve and the low raggedness index confirm a history of demographic expansion. Specifically, the Edilbay breed exhibited a unique demographic signature with negative Fu and Li’s D and F values (-0.147 and − 0.203, respectively) and the low R2 statistic (0.1248) among the studied groups. This particular combination, along with a significantly negative Fu’s Fs (-5.199, P < 0.05), suggests a very strong and potentially more recent demographic expansion specific to these Kazakh herds. This observation agrees with the recent comprehensive analysis by Dossybayev et al. [34], who also reported strong signals of historical population expansion and high maternal diversity in fat-tailed coarse-wooled sheep breeds from Kazakhstan.

Genetic relationships and the historical implications

One of the key findings of this study is the very low pairwise FST value (0.017) observed between the Kazakh Edilbay and Turkish Morkaraman breeds. These two populations have very low genetic differentiation [27]. Despite the vast geographical distance separating present-day Kazakhstan and Eastern Türkiye, these two breeds exhibit a closer genetic affinity to each other than to the co-geographic Akkaraman or Awassi breeds. This genetic proximity supports the hypothesis of shared maternal lineages. As suggested by historical contexts, this pattern may reflect connections among sheep populations historically linked by pastoral movements, such as the well-documented Oghuz Turk migrations from the Central Asian steppes through the Iranian plateau to Anatolia between the 11th and 13th centuries [28, 29]. This finding is consistent with the theory that human migration routes generally parallel the phylogeography of their livestock.

Contrasting with the Edilbay-Morkaraman cluster, the Awassi breed formed a distinct and genetically distant group. The pairwise FST values between Awassi and other studied populations were notably high (0.309–0.447), and the median-joining network analysis positioned Awassi in a clearly separated cluster. This divergence suggests that while Morkaraman and Akkaraman may share ancestral roots linked to Central Asian waves, the Awassi breed likely represents a distinct lineage more strictly confined to the southern Fertile Crescent and Levantine region. The lack of shared haplotypes between Awassi and the Edilbay-Morkaraman cluster indicates limited gene flow between these distinct lineages, possibly due to different breeding management or geographical isolation in the stepping-stone expansion of sheep. A previous study [30] has also reported that Awassi sheep populations are distinctly different from other native Turkish sheep breeds (Karakaş, Akkaraman, Morkaraman, Kangal-Akkaraman, and Norduz).

Phylogenetic network and haplogroup distribution

The study identified 77 different haplotypes and confirmed the presence of all major sheep haplogroups (A, B, C, D, and E) within the studied populations. The predominance of HPG-B in the Morkaraman, Akkaraman, and Edilbay populations aligns with previous studies suggesting that HPG-B is the most common lineage in European and some Asian sheep breeds [1, 28]. Furthermore, our findings for the Edilbay breed are consistent with recent regional studies focusing on Central Asian sheep populations. For instance, recent studies [31, 32] reported high maternal genetic diversity and the widespread presence of HPG-B in native Kazakh sheep breeds. Similarly, recent comprehensive analyses [31] reveal the genetic links between Central Asian sheep populations and historical pastoral expansion, further supporting the phylogeographic patterns and shared maternal lineages observed between our Kazakh and Turkish cohorts. Additionally, the phylogenetic analysis showed that the groups containing these three breeds (Morkaraman, Akkaraman, and Edilbay) shares close genetic similarity with the wild sheep Ovis gmelini musimon (Mouflon). This is consistent with previous studies suggesting a maternal genetic affinity between these fat-tailed breeds and the Asiatic Mouflon.

The Median-Joining network analysis revealed a complex population structure organized into three main clusters and one unclassified group, providing significant insights into the maternal lineages of the studied breeds. The first two major clusters, dominated by HPG-B and HPG-C, comprised individuals from Akkaraman, Morkaraman, and Edilbay breeds. The predominance of HPG-B (52%) across these populations is consistent with previous findings indicating that HPG-B is the most frequent lineage in European and Near Eastern sheep breeds [1, 12] and in Türkiye [24–26, 33]. The co-occurrence of Edilbay and Morkaraman within these shared clusters reinforces the hypothesis of a common ancestral origin or historical gene flow facilitated by human migrations between Central Asia and Anatolia. A previous study [31] about mitochondrial genome of six different indigenous sheep breeds showed that HPG-A (43.75%) and HPG-B (39.06%) were dominant in Kazakhstan. Recent comprehensive analyses emphasize that Kazakh sheep populations serve as a vital genetic “contact zone” characterized by a near-equal balance of HPG-A and HPG-B, shaped by historical livestock exchanges along the Silk Road [31, 34]. Consistent with this balance, our sampling of Edilbay sheep from the Atyrau region revealed a predominance of HPG-B (66%) over HPG-A (30%). This localized variation highlights the intra-breed variability within geographically widespread populations. It suggests that while the broader Eurasian steppe acts as an admixture zone, the genetic structure of specific herds is finely tuned by localized flock dynamics, founder effects, and targeted pastoral management. Furthermore, the “star-like” topologies observed within these major haplogroup clusters in the median-joining network are strongly indicative of historical population expansions, consistent with the extensive pastoral movements and demographic growth of these flocks across Eurasia.

Interestingly, the third cluster grouped Haplogroups A, D, and E. The presence of HPG-A, typically associated with Asian sheep breeds, and HPG-C, often found in Central Asia and the Middle East [24, 34, 35], within the Turkish native breeds further supports the role of Anatolia as a genetic crossroads. This admixture suggests that Turkish sheep breeds possess a composite maternal heritage, likely resulting from the introgression of Asian lineages into local stocks over centuries. In contrast, the Awassi breed formed a distinct, unclassified group separated from the main clusters and shares close genetic similarity with the wild sheep Ovis ammon (Argali). This clear phylogenetic segregation aligns with the high pairwise FST values observed between Awassi and the other breeds. While Akkaraman and Morkaraman show affinity with the Central Asian Edilbay, the Awassi’s isolation in the network suggests a maternal genetic distinctiveness or a strictly conserved breeding strategy that limited maternal gene flow from the northern or eastern migration waves. This distinction is critical for conservation strategies, highlighting the Awassi as a unique genetic reservoir distinct from the other Anatolian and Central Asian fat-tailed breeds.

Haplogroup distribution and origins

The detection of five mitochondrial haplogroups (A, B, C, D, and E) within the studied populations emphasizes the complex domestication history of these breeds. While HPG-B was predominant, consistent with its ubiquity in European and Near Eastern sheep, the presence of HPG-A and C, often associated with Asian origins, in Turkish breeds further validates the influence of eastward and westward gene flows. The co-occurrence of these diverse haplogroups within the Akkaraman and Morkaraman populations reinforces the status of Anatolia not just as a transit route, but as an “admixture zone” where distinct sheep lineages from Central Asia and the Fertile Crescent converged and admixed.

Population structure and conservation

The Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) revealed that 20.08% of the total genetic variation occurred among populations, while the vast majority (79.92%) was due to diversity among individuals within populations. The decrease in differentiation between populations in different regions can be attributed to the historical movement of sheep between continents as part of the widespread migrations in Central Asia and Anatolia to find fresh pastures and to conduct trade such as the “Silk Road” or the traditional seasonal or annual long-distance migrations of ancient Turkic communities. Compared with the current results, many sheep breeds exhibited higher genetic variation within populations (95.04% to 97.00%) and lower genetic variation between populations/different geographical regions (3.0% to 4.94%) [36, 37]. Liu et al. [38] reported that most of the genetic variation was due to within-population variation (98.23%), while the remaining was due to differences among the studied Tibetan sheep breeds (1.77%).

The high within-population variation indicates substantial maternal genetic variability at the individual level. However, since this observation relies on a single maternally inherited marker, it does not capture male-mediated gene flow (such as the sex-biased migration of rams) or reflect the overall nuclear genomic structure of these populations. Therefore, while this maternal diversity is an asset, future studies incorporating genome-wide markers (e.g., SNP arrays, whole-genome sequencing) are required to comprehensively assess the demographic history and genetic variability. The clear genetic distinction of the Awassi, combined with the close genetic ties between the Edilbay and Morkaraman, provides essential baseline data for conservation strategies. These results suggest that while Edilbay and Morkaraman share a close evolutionary history, they constitute a separate genetic cluster from the Awassi, and conservation programs should respect these historical and genetic demarcations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study indicates a close proximity of maternal lineages between the Kazakh Edilbay and Turkish Morkaraman breeds, supporting the hypothesis that historical Oghuz Turk migrations significantly shaped the sheep biodiversity of Anatolia. The shared mitochondrial haplotypes and low genetic differentiation between these geographically distant populations suggest that these breeds share a common maternal ancestry, likely reflecting historical connections and pastoral movements across these regions. Conversely, the clear genetic divergence of the Awassi breed highlights its maternal genetic distinctiveness rooted in the Fertile Crescent, warranting its recognition as a separate conservation unit. Overall, the high genetic diversity observed in Turkish breeds highlights Anatolia’s role as a genetic crossroads. However, it is important to acknowledge that our conclusions are based on a relatively short (~ 500 bp) single maternally inherited gene region (mtDNA D-loop), which limits our ability to capture male-mediated gene flow or whole-genome de,mographic events. Therefore, future studies incorporating genome-wide SNP arrays or whole-genome sequencing are strongly recommended to overcome these limitations and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the adaptive traits and complete evolutionary history of these resilient fat-tailed breeds.
