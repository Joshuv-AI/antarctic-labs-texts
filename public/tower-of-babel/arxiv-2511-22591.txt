Hilbert metric and Hölder continuity
Şahsene Altınkaya, Masayo FUJIMURA, Marcelina Mocanu, Matti Vuorinen

Abstract
. We prove several formulas for the Hilbert metric in the unit disk and apply these results to study quasiregular mappings of the unit disk 𝔹 2 \mathbb{B}^{2} onto a bounded convex domain D . D\,. The main result deals with the Hölder continuity of these mappings with respect to Hilbert metrics of 𝔹 2 \mathbb{B}^{2} and D . D. Also several open problems are formulated.

D. Hilbert
[

]
introduced one of these metrics defined in a bounded convex domain

for two distinct points

as
follows.
Let

and

be the points of intersection of the line through the points

(1.1)

h_{D}(a,b)=\log\left|u,a,b,v\right|

where

(1.2)

\left|u,a,b,v\right|=\frac{\left|u-b\right|\left|a-v\right|}{\left|u-a\right|\left|b-v\right|}\,.

This metric is the basis of Hilbert’s metric geometry which

[

[

]
and in computer graphics
[

]
. For the special case of the domain

Hilbert’s metric is also known as the Cayley-Klein metric.

We continue here our paper
[
AFV
]
, by proving a Hölder continuity
result for a quasiregular mapping of the unit disk
\mathbb{B}^{2}

onto a bounded convex domain and giving
several formulas involving the Hilbert and other metrics.

After this introduction, the contents of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give some notation and preliminary information and thereafter,
in Section 3, we give several upper and lower bounds
for the Hilbert metric.
In Section 4 we study Hilbert circles in polygonal plane domains and for dimensions

give the formulas for Hilbert spheres in
\mathbb{B}^{n},

which are known to be Euclidean ellipsoids,
extending the result from
[

]
for the case

.
In Section 5 one of the main results is given, we prove a Hölder continuity result
for a quasiregular mapping

onto
a convex domain in terms of the Hilbert metric in the following form.

Theorem 1.3

Let

Preliminary facts

In this section we give some basic notation and facts that will be utilized in the paper.

The
n
-dimensional unit ball is denoted by
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, while the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. A ball with center

B^{n}(x,r)
and its boundary sphere

For
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, let

a^{*}=\frac{a}{|a|^{2}}
.
We identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the complex plane
\mathbb{C}

and for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the complex conjugate
\overline{a}
of
a
is
\overline{a}=\operatorname{Re}(a)-\operatorname{Im}(a)i.

Assume that
L[a,b]
represents the line passing through points
a
and
b
(
b\neq a
). For distinct points
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
, if the lines
L[a,b]
and
L[c,d]
intersect at a single point
w
, then we use the symbol
\text{LIS}[a,b,c,d]
for this point of intersection

w=\text{LIS}[a,b,c,d]=L[a,b]\cap L[c,d].

The formula for
w
is (see e.g.,
[
HKV
, Ex. 4.3(1), p. 57 and p. 373]
)

(2.1)

w=\text{LIS}[a,b,c,d]=\frac{(\overline{a}b-a\overline{b})(c-d)-(a-b)(\overline{c}d-c\overline{d})}{(\overline{a}-\overline{b})(c-d)-(a-b)(\overline{c}-\overline{d})}.

For
a,b,c,d\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
, using
a\overline{a}=b\overline{b}=c\overline{c}=d\overline{d}=1
, we obtain

(2.2)

\mathrm{LIS}\left[a,b,c,d\right]=\frac{ab\left(c+d\right)-cd\left(a+b\right)}{ab-cd}=\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}-\overline{c}-\overline{d}}{\overline{a}\overline{b}-\overline{c}\overline{d}}.

Let
C[a,b,c]
be the unique circle through three distinct, non-collinear points
a
,
b
, and
c
. The formula (
2.1
) easily yields a formula for the center and radius of this circle.

A Möbius transformation is a mapping of the form

z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\ \ \ a,b,c,d,z\in\mathbb{C},\ ad-bc\neq 0.

The most important features of Möbius transformations are that they preserve
the cross-ratio and the angle magnitude, and, because of this, they map every Euclidean line or circle onto either a line or a circle. The special Möbius transformation

(2.3)

T_{a}(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z},\ \ \ a\in\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

maps the unit disk
\mathbb{B}^{2}
onto itself with

T_{a}(a)=0,\ \ \ T_{a}\left(\pm\frac{a}{\left|a\right|}\right)=\pm\frac{a}{\left|a\right|}.

Hyperbolic geometry.

We review fundamental formulas and notation for hyperbolic geometry, for more information, see
[
B1
]
.

The hyperbolic metrics of the unit disk
{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
and the upper half plane
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
are given, respectively, by

(2.5)

\,\textnormal{sh}\frac{\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)}{2}=\frac{\left|a-b\right|}{\sqrt{(1-\left|a\right|^{2})(1-\left|b\right|^{2})}},\ \ \ a,b\in\mathbb{B}^{2},

and

(2.6)

{\rm ch}\rho_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(a,b)=1+\frac{\left|a-b\right|^{2}}{2{\rm Im}(a){\rm Im}(b)},\ \ \ a,b\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\,.

Both metrics are Möbius invariant: if
G,D\in\left\{\mathbb{B}^{2},\mathbb{H}^{2}\right\}

and

is a Möbius transformation, then
\rho_{G}(a,b)=\rho_{D}(f(a),f(b))
holds for all
a,b\in G
. The geodesic line through

is an arc of a circle, perpendicular to
\partial G\,.
The end points of this arc on

\partial G
are
a_{*}
and
b_{*}
with
|a-a_{*}|\leq|b-a_{*}|.
In terms of these end points we have
[
HKV
, (4.9), (4.17)]

(2.7)

\rho_{G}(a,b)=\log|a_{*},a,b,b_{*}|\,.

We shall use the fact that a hyperbolic disk
B_{\rho}(x,M)
with the center
x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{2}
and the radius
M>0
is a Euclidean disk with the following
center and radius
[

, p. 56, (4.20)]

(2.8)

\begin{cases}B_{\rho}(x,M)=B^{2}(y,r)\;,&\\
\vskip 5.0pt\cr{\displaystyle y=\frac{x(1-t^{2})}{1-|x|^{2}t^{2}}\;,\;\;r=\frac{(1-|x|^{2})t}{1-|x|^{2}t^{2}}\;,\;\;t={\rm th}(M/2)\;.}&\end{cases}

Above

{\rm ch},
and
{\rm th}

and their inverse functions are
{\rm arsh},

{\rm arch,}
and

A[a,b]=\sqrt{|a-b|^{2}+(1-|a|^{2})(1-|b|^{2})}=\sqrt{1+\left|a\right|^{2}\left|b\right|^{2}-2a\cdot b}\,.

Here the dot product of two points

is denoted by
a\cdot b
.
For complex numbers

Lemma 2.9
.

The following identity holds
for all

(2.10)

\left|a\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)+b\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}=\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\left|b\right|^{2}\right)^{2}-\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)A[a,b]^{2}.

Proof.

Using

\left|a\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)+b\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}=\left|a\right|^{2}\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)^{2}+\left|b\right|^{2}\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)^{2}+2a\cdot b\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)

and the elementary identity

\left|a\right|^{2}\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)^{2}+\left|b\right|^{2}\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)^{2}-\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\left|b\right|^{2}\right)^{2}=-\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)\left(1+\left|a\right|^{2}\left|b\right|^{2}\right),

-\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)\left(1+\left|a\right|^{2}\left|b\right|^{2}-2a\cdot b\right)\,.

3.
Hilbert metric in the unit disk

In this section we discuss the connection of the Hilbert metric
with the hyperbolic and the Euclidean metrics. We first recall some well-known results and then prove lower and upper bounds for the Euclidean metric
in terms of the Hilbert metric. Note that the above metrics
\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{2}},\rho_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},
and
h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
can be used also in the one-dimensional case.

Recalling that
{\rm th}^{2}(u/2)=\frac{{\rm ch}\,u-1}{{\rm ch}\,u+1}
for
u>0
and
[
RV
, Cor 3.5]

{\rm ch}(h_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(a,b)/2)=\frac{1-a\cdot b}{\sqrt{\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)}}

we have

(3.1)

{\rm th}^{2}\left(\frac{h_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left(a,b\right)}{4}\right)=\frac{1-a\cdot b-\sqrt{\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)}}{1-a\cdot b+\sqrt{\left(1-\left|a\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|b\right|^{2}\right)}}\,.

The geodesic line through
a,b\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
in the Hilbert metric
is a chord of the unit circle joining the points
u,v\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
with

(3.2)

u=c+\frac{(a-b)}{|a-b|}\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}\,,\quad v=c+\frac{(b-a)}{|a-b|}\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}\,,

and the Hilbert distance is
\log|u,a,b,v|

where
c={\rm LIS}[a,b,0,i(b-a)]\,.
The points
u,a,b,v
are on the chord
[u,v]
in this order.

Unlike the hyperbolic metric
\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}
, the Hilbert metric is not invariant under the Möbius automorphisms of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
as indicated by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

(See
[
RV
, Thm 1.2]
)
For all
a,b\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
, the following functional identity holds
between the Hilbert metric and the hyperbolic metric:

(3.4)

\,\textnormal{sh}\left(\frac{h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)}{2}\right)=\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\,\textnormal{sh}\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)}{2}\right),

where
m
is the Euclidean distance from the origin to the line
L[a,b]
. Here equality holds if
m=0.

Proposition 3.5
.

For
t>0,c>0
let

f(t)=2\,{\rm arsh}(c\,{\rm sh}(\frac{t}{2}))
and
g(t)=ct\,.
Then for
c\in(0,1)
the function

f(t)/g(t):(0,\infty)\to(1,1/c)
is increasing and for
c>1
the function
f(t)/g(t):(0,\infty)\to(1/c,1)
is decreasing. In particular, for
c\in(0,1),t\geq 0

we have

ct\leq 2\,{\rm arsh}(c\,{\rm sh}(\frac{t}{2}))\leq t

and for
c>1,t\geq 0

t\leq 2\,{\rm arsh}(c\,{\rm sh}(\frac{t}{2}))\leq ct\,.

Proof.

Differentiation yields

\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{g^{\prime}(t)}=\frac{\,\textnormal{ch}(t/2)}{\sqrt{1+c^{2}\,\textnormal{sh}^{2}(t/2)}}=\quad\sqrt{\frac{1+u^{2}}{1+c^{2}u^{2}}};\quad u=\operatorname{sh}(t/2)

and hence
\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{g^{\prime}(t)}
is increasing for
c\in(0,1)
and decreasing
c>1
.
Now, from
[
HKV
, Thm B.2, p. 465]
, it
follows that also the function
\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}
has the same monotonicity property. In particular, the conclusion holds.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

For all
a,b\in\mathbb{B}^{n}

t=\frac{(1+b)^{2}}{(1-b)^{2}}\,.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{B}^{n}
be the unit ball in the Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h=h_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}
be the Hilbert distance in
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. The sphere
S_{h}\left(c,R\right)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}:h_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left(x,c\right)=R\right\}
, with
c\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
R>0
, is an ellipsoid of revolution, with the center

c^{\prime}=\frac{c}{{\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)-\left|c\right|^{2}{\,\textnormal{sh}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}\,\text{.}

Write

a_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(1-\left|c\right|^{2}\right){\,\textnormal{sh}}(R)}{{\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)-\left|c\right|^{2}{\,\textnormal{sh}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}\,,\quad a_{\max}=\frac{{\,\textnormal{sh}}(\frac{R}{2})\sqrt{1-\left|c\right|^{2}}}{\sqrt{{\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)-\left|c\right|^{2}{\,\textnormal{sh}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}}\,.

This ellipsoid has the minor semi-axis, along
c
, equal to
a_{\min}
and every other semi-axis equal to
a_{\max}\,.
If
c=0
, then
S_{h}\left(c,R\right)
is the Euclidean sphere centered
at
c
with radius
\,\textnormal{th}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, the explicit formula of the Hilbert metric in the
unit ball shows that
h_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left(x,c\right)=R
if and only if

(4.4)

\left(1-c\cdot x\right)^{2}={\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)\left(1-\left|c\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right).

Let
\alpha:={\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)\left(1-\left|c\right|^{2}\right)
. Then
\alpha>0
.

If
c=0
, then (
4.4
) reduces to
\left|x\right|=\,\textnormal{th}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)
, that implies
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

Assume that
c\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. Using the
matrix notation of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
c\cdot x=c^{T}x=x^{T}c
, we
write (
4.4
) under the equivalent form

x^{T}(\alpha I_{n}+cc^{T})x-2c^{T}x+1-\lambda=0.

The above equation defines an
\left(n-1\right)
-dimensional ellipsoid, as
the matrix
\alpha I_{n}+cc^{T}
is positive definite, with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=\alpha+\left|c\right|^{2}
and
\lambda_{k}=\alpha

for
k=2,...,n
and an orthonormal basis of corresponding eigenvectors
v_{1}=\frac{c}{\left|c\right|}
and
v_{k}
with
k=2,...,n
.

In order to obtain a canonical equation of the above ellipsoid, decompose
x=\lambda\frac{c}{\left|c\right|}+z
, where
z
is orthogonal to
c
. These conditions hold if and only if
\lambda=\frac{c\cdot x}{\left|c\right|}
and
z=x-\frac{c\cdot x}{\left|c\right|^{2}}c
. Then
c\cdot x=\left|c\right|\lambda
and
\left|x\right|^{2}=\lambda^{2}+\left|z\right|^{2}
. Substituting
in (
4.4
) we get
(1-\left|c\right|\lambda)^{2}=\alpha\left(1-\lambda^{2}-\left|z\right|^{2}\right)
, i.e.

\left(\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha\right)\lambda^{2}-2\left|c\right|\lambda+\alpha\left|z\right|^{2}=\alpha-1\text{.}

The equation (
4.4
) is equivalent to

(4.5)

\frac{\left(\lambda-\frac{\left|c\right|}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}\right)^{2}}{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\left(\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha-1\right)}}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\left|z\right|^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha-1}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}}\right)^{2}}=1,

which is the equation of an
\left(n-1\right)
-dimensional ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
with the center at
\frac{c}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}
, having the minor semi-axis
a_{\min}=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\left(\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha-1\right)}}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}
and the other semi-axes equal to
a_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha-1}{\left|c\right|^{2}+\alpha}}
.

After some calculations we get

a_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(1-\left|c\right|^{2}\right){\,\textnormal{sh}}(R)}{{\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)-\left|c\right|^{2}{\,\textnormal{sh}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}\,\quad\quad{\rm and}\quad\quad a_{\max}=\frac{{\,\textnormal{sh}}(\frac{R}{2})\sqrt{1-\left|c\right|^{2}}}{\sqrt{{\,\textnormal{ch}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)-\left|c\right|^{2}{\,\textnormal{sh}}^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}}\,.

We proved that
S_{h}\left(c,R\right)\subset\mathbb{B}^{n}\cap\mathcal{E}
. Conversely, we check that
\mathcal{E}\subset S_{h}\left(c,R\right)
.
Let
x\in\mathcal{E}
. Using the decomposition
x=\lambda\frac{c}{\left|c\right|}+z
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
orthogonal to
c
, we see that
\lambda
and
z
satisfy (
4.5
), hence (
4.4
) holds. The right-hand side of (
4.4
) is
non-negative, therefore
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Moreover, (
4.4
) shows
that
h_{\mathbb{B}}\left(c,x\right)=R
. In conclusion,
S_{h}\left(c,R\right)=\mathcal{E}
.
∎

if
f
is
\text{ACL}^{n}
and if there exists a constant
K\geq 1
such that

(5.10)

|f^{\prime}(x)|^{n}\leq KJ_{f}(x),\quad|f^{\prime}(x)|=\max_{|h|=1}|f^{\prime}(x)h|,

a.e. in
G
. Here

denotes the formal derivative of
f
at
x

K\geq 1

(5.3)

J_{f}(x)\leq K\ell(f^{\prime}(x))^{n},\quad\ell(f^{\prime}(x))=\min_{|h|=1}|f^{\prime}(x)h|

too, holds almost everywhere in
G

outer dilation

K_{O}(f)

5.11
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

It follows easily from the definition of the Apollonian metric that

h_{D}(f(a),f(b))\leq\alpha_{D}(f(a),f(b)).

On the other hand, by Theorem
5.4

we can write

h_{D}(f(a),f(b))\leq\alpha_{D}(f(a),f(b))\leq 2\,\rho_{D}(f(a),f(b)).

By Theorem
5.2
and Corollary
3.6
, we get

\displaystyle h_{D}(f(a),f(b))

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{D}(f(a),f(b))\leq 2\,\rho_{D}(f(a),f(b))

\displaystyle\leq 2\,c(K)\max\left\{\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b),\left(\rho_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)\right)^{1/K}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,c(K)\max\left\{\frac{h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}},\left(\frac{h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\right)^{1/K}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\,c(K)}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\max\left\{h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b),\left(h_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(a,b)\right)^{1/K}\right\}.

The claim follows.
\square
