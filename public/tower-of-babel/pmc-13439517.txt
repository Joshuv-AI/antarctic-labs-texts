1. Introduction

Müllerian cysts arise from persistent embryologic remnants of the paramesonephric ducts. During female embryogenesis, these ducts differentiate into the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. Failure of complete regression or ectopic persistence may result in cyst formation. These cysts are commonly found in the vaginal wall or cervix, usually incidentally or on routine gynecologic examination. However, vulvar involvement, especially in the clitoral hood, is exceptionally unusual and scarcely documented. Clinical diagnosis is often challenging due to overlap with other vulvar cystic entities such as epidermal inclusion cysts, Bartholin duct cysts, Gartner duct cysts, and Skene duct cysts. Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis, given the nonspecific clinical presentation and overlap in imaging features. Recent case reports of vulvar cystic lesions continue to emphasize the importance of comprehensive differential diagnosis and histologic confirmation in such rare presentations [1–4].

2. Case Presentation

A 43‐year‐old woman with a history of one vaginal birth and one abortion (P1A1) presented with a mass on the clitoral hood that had been present for 13 years. The lump was initially small and asymptomatic, gradually enlarging over time, and became mildly tender in the week prior to presentation due to progressive growth. The patient denied genital trauma, urinary tract infections or complaints, prior surgery, or sexually transmitted infections.

Physical examination revealed a well‐circumscribed, minimally mobile, cystic mass at the clitoral hood measuring 6 × 2 × 2 cm, without signs of inflammation (Figure 1) and no involvement of the urethra (Figure 2). No inguinal lymphadenopathy was present. An initial clinical diagnosis of clitoral hood cyst, most likely an epidermal inclusion cyst, was made. The patient underwent complete surgical excision under spinal anesthesia with special attention to preserving clitoral neurovascular structures. The cyst was removed intact without rupture or significant bleeding (Figures 3 and 4). Histopathological evaluation demonstrated a cyst wall lined by ciliated cuboidal to columnar epithelium, consistent with a Müllerian cyst. No malignant features were seen (Figure 5).

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. At the 2‐week and 1‐month follow‐up visits, the surgical site healed well with no recurrence or sensory disturbances. The patient expressed satisfaction with the chosen management approach and the clinical results. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying clinical information.

3. Discussion

Müllerian cysts originate from embryologic remnants of the paramesonephric ducts. During early embryogenesis (approximately 6–12 weeks of gestation), the paired Müllerian ducts develop lateral to the mesonephric ducts and subsequently fuse to form the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. Failure of regression or ectopic displacement of Müllerian epithelium may result in residual rests capable of cystic transformation later in life [1].

Although vaginal or cervical Müllerian cysts have been previously reported, vulvar presentations—and particularly those involving the clitoral hood—remain exceedingly rare. According to Blaustein′s Pathology of the Female Genital Tract, Müllerian cysts most commonly occur in the anterolateral vaginal wall. The embryologic explanation for vulvar localization remains speculative. One hypothesis suggests aberrant caudal migration of Müllerian epithelium during organogenesis. Another proposes metaplastic transformation of local epithelium toward Müllerian phenotype under hormonal influence [3].

Müllerian‐derived epithelium is hormonally responsive [3]. Several studies have demonstrated expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and PAX8 in Müllerian cyst epithelium. PAX8 is a transcription factor associated with Müllerian lineage differentiation and is frequently used to confirm Müllerian origin in gynecologic pathology [5–7].

Although immunohistopathology was not performed in this case due to classic histomorphology (ciliated cuboidal to columnar epithelium), ER/PR and PAX8 staining may be useful in diagnostically challenging cases, particularly when differentiating Müllerian cysts from mesonephric (Gartner duct) cysts or paraurethral cysts.

The prolonged indolent growth observed in our patient over 13 years is consistent with hormonally influenced but benign proliferative behavior. The onset of mild pain likely resulted from mechanical stretching of surrounding tissues rather than inflammatory processes. Preoperative ultrasonography was not performed in this case. Although imaging is not routinely required for clinically apparent superficial vulvar cysts undergoing surgical excision, ultrasonography may serve as a useful adjunct by delineating lesion characteristics, assessing involvement of adjacent structures, and narrowing the differential diagnosis, particularly for uncommon lesions arising in the clitoral region [8].

The differential diagnosis for vulvar cystic lesions includes epidermal inclusion cysts, Skene duct cysts, Bartholin gland cysts, Gartner duct cysts, vestibular mucous cysts, and other benign vulvar lesions. Recent case reports illustrate the clinical spectrum of vulvar cysts, including epidermal cysts in adults and children, which further underscores the diagnostic challenge [2, 9]. Histopathology remains the definitive tool for diagnosis. Müllerian cysts are typically lined by ciliated columnar or cuboidal epithelium, resembling fallopian tube or endocervical mucosa. In contrast, epidermal inclusion cysts are lined by keratinized squamous epithelium, and Skene duct cysts often exhibit transitional or pseudostratified epithelium. The characteristic Müllerian epithelium, seen in this case, confirms the diagnosis.

Surgical excision is the treatment of choice and has been shown to be curative with minimal risk of recurrence when complete excision is achieved. The rich neurovascular supply of the clitoral region necessitates meticulous surgical technique to preserve sensory and sexual function. Recent literature supports careful dissection with preservation of surrounding structures to reduce postoperative morbidity [13].

Despite complete surgical excision being the standard and curative treatment for Müllerian cysts, noninvasive management such as ultrasound guided aspiration may be considered in selected uncomplicated cases. This approach reduces the risk of damage to surrounding structures, including the bladder, rectum, pelvic nerves, and urethra. However, the risk of recurrence and the inability to obtain tissue for histopathological confirmation following this choice of treatment should be taken into consideration [14].

Although malignant transformation of Müllerian cysts is rare, such potential—even in vaginal Müllerian cysts—has been reported and warrants thorough histopathologic evaluation and appropriate follow‐up [15]. The absence of atypia, stromal invasion, or complex glandular architecture in our case confirms the benign nature of the lesion.

This case contributes to the limited body of literature describing Müllerian cysts in the clitoral hood. The key academic contributions include the following:1.Documentation of an exceptionally rare anatomical location.2.Long‐standing natural history (13 years) with gradual enlargement.3.Successful neurovascular‐preserving excision of a relatively large lesion.4.Clear histopathologic confirmation of Müllerian differentiation.

Given the rarity of this presentation, clinicians should maintain a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating vulvar cystic lesions, particularly in atypical locations.

4. Limitation

Limitations of the report include the absence of preoperative ultrasonographic evaluation and the relatively short duration of follow‐up. Although not routinely required, preoperative imaging could have provided additional information regarding the lesion′s size, internal characteristics, and relationship to adjacent clitoral structures. Further cases may benefit from ultrasonographic assessment to assist with operative planning. Short follow‐up limits the evaluation of any delayed complications following surgical excision and the potential for recurrence.

5. Conclusion

Müllerian cyst of the clitoral hood is a rare and benign lesion. Clinical diagnosis alone is insufficient due to its resemblance to other vulvar cystic entities. Histopathological examination remains the diagnostic gold standard. Complete surgical excision with careful neurovascular preservation ensures favorable outcomes and minimizes recurrence.
