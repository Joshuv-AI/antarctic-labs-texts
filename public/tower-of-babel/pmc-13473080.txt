The impact of career calling on medical students’ academic burnout: a chain mediation model
Authors: Huanhuan Zhao, Jing Chen, Zengli Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to examine the chain mediating roles of natural connection and perceived social support in the relationship between career calling and academic burnout among medical students. A questionnaire survey was administered to 2,103 medical students using validated scales, including the Career Calling Scale, Academic Burnout Scale, Natural Connection Scale, and Perceived Social Support Scale. Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and regression analysis were performed. Mediation effects were tested using the PROCESS macro (Version 4.2, Model 6) for SPSS, with 5000 bootstrap samples to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Career calling significantly and negatively predicted academic burnout among medical students. Both natural connection and perceived social support independently mediated the relationship between career calling and academic burnout. Furthermore, career calling also influenced academic burnout through a chain-mediated pathway involving natural connection followed by perceived social support. The findings highlight the dual mediating roles of natural connection and perceived social support, suggesting that enhancing these factors could mitigate academic burnout by strengthening career calling in medical education.

Introduction

Given the unique nature of the medical profession, medical students face a heavier academic workload, greater psychological pressure, and stricter training standards compared to non-medical students, making them more susceptible to academic burnout1. Academic burnout is characterized by a negative learning attitude and psychological exhaustion resulting from continuous academic pressure or insufficient learning motivation. Its typical features include a decline in interest in learning, reduced academic efficacy, and significant emotional fluctuations2. This phenomenon not only harms students’ mental health but also significantly negatively impacts their academic performance and quality of life. Since medical students are a crucial reserve force for the healthcare industry, the extent of their academic burnout is directly related to the quality of medical talent cultivation in our country.

Career calling is a complex psychological construct formed by an individual’s value recognition, sense of meaning, and emotional investment in a specific professional field3. For medical students, this concept is specifically manifested as the systematic construction of professional value cognition, the continuous strengthening of professional emotional connection, and the stable development of the professional belief system, which together form the key psychological capital supporting their academic development. Empirical research indicates that individuals with a high level of career calling often exhibit a more pronounced proactive learning tendency, with their academic drive significantly surpassing that of the control group4. Since a lack of learning motivation has been identified as a core mediating variable inducing academic burnout5, strengthening the cultivation of medical students’ career calling can effectively activate their intrinsic learning motivation, thereby promoting the sustained output of positive learning behaviors6. The enhancement of this motivation not only optimizes individual academic engagement but also builds a protective mechanism against academic burnout by increasing psychological resilience. Based on the aforementioned theoretical deduction, we propose the research hypothesis H1: Career calling has a significant negative predictive effect on academic burnout.

Natural connection is a multidimensional psychological construct that emphasizes individuals’ systematic cognition of ecosystem value through active participation in natural interactions, leading to a stable pro-environmental emotional orientation7. The essence of medicine is rooted in a deep understanding of life systems and humanistic care. Research indicates that medical students’ career calling significantly positively predicts their sense of life meaning8. Empirical studies further reveal a significant positive correlation between natural connection and the experience of life meaning, with individuals who have a high level of life meaning experience tending to exhibit a stronger inclination towards natural connection9. Analyzed from the perspective of neurobiology, natural connection, by regulating the prefrontal-limbic system, not only enhances individuals’ mental and physical health level and promotes positive emotional states but also effectively regulates stress-induced negative emotional responses, significantly inhibiting academic burnout10. Based on this theoretical framework, we propose the research hypothesis H2: Natural connection has a mediating effect in the pathway of the influence of career calling on academic burnout.

Social support refers to the multidimensional support system formed through individuals’ interactions within social networks. Its core function is to reduce stress perception, enhance adaptive efficacy, and improve psychological health through resource provision and emotional maintenance. From a structural–functional perspective, Xiao11 deconstructs it into two dimensions: objective support (the actual social resources obtained) and subjective support (individuals’ subjective evaluation of social support). Subsequent research indicates that, compared to objectively measurable support, perceived social support has a stronger moderating effect on individuals’ psychological adjustment and behavioral decision-making12. As a cognitive-emotional complex of professional identity, an increase in the level of career calling can significantly strengthen an individual’s commitment to the value of their professional identity13. According to Social Identity Theory, a strong professional identity can stimulate an individual’s need for group belonging, prompting them to actively build social connections within the professional community14. This identity-based social capital accumulation process can effectively reduce tendencies of social avoidance, thereby enhancing the subjective perception of social support. The empirical study conducted by Ding et al.15 revealed a significant positive correlation between the career calling of nursing professionals and their perceived social support. The strength of this relationship was quantified as an increase of 0.37 units of perceived social support for every one-unit increase in career calling. From the perspective of the Stress-Buffering Model, perceived social support, as a positive psychological resource, can enhance an individual’s resilience to adversity through mechanisms such as reconstructing stress cognitive appraisal and activating coping strategy reserves16. So perceived social support may have a significant negative predictive effect on academic burnout. Based on the aforementioned theoretical constructs and empirical evidence, research hypothesis H3 is proposed: perceived social support mediates the pathway through which career calling influences academic burnout.

In the theoretical construction of career calling, social responsibility, as its core value dimension, significantly deepens individuals’ perception of the systemic impact of their work by enhancing their understanding of the social value of their profession. This leads to a continuous reinforcement of intrinsic motivation17. For those in medical education, this mission-driven approach is reflected not only in the cumulative effect of academic investment over time but also in the tendency to construct positive resources under pressure. When academic challenges exceed psychological thresholds, medical students may be more inclined to adopt natural connection strategies within the framework of ecological psychology, optimizing the function of their emotional regulation systems through environmental embedding behaviors. From an evolutionary psychology perspective, natural contact, as an instinctive human response to environmental stress, shows more pronounced active regulation characteristics in mission-driven individuals. This deep ecological interaction promotes the multidimensional growth of psychological capital by activating positive emotion schemas, specifically enhancing psychological resilience and positively reconstructing explanatory styles. Neuropsychological studies indicate that sustained activation of positive emotions enhances the regulatory efficiency of the prefrontal cortex over the limbic system, enabling individuals to form more adaptive social support perception schemas during cognitive reappraisal processes18. This cognitive-emotional-behavioral linkage mechanism essentially constructs a dynamic transmission path from career calling through natural connection to the comprehension of social support, ultimately exerting an inhibitory effect on academic burnout. Based on this theory, Hypothesis 4 is proposed: There is a significant chain mediation effect of natural connection and perceived social support in the psychological process by which career calling influences academic burnout.

Therefore, based on the above theoretical reasoning, this study constructs a chain mediation model illustrating how career calling influences academic burnout through natural connection and perceived social support (Fig. 1). Participants and procedure

Participants

This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit medical students from three full-time undergraduate medical universities in China. To account for variations in economic development levels and regional characteristics, one university was selected from Northern, Central, and Southern China, respectively. A total of 2103 questionnaires were distributed to medical students enrolled in full-time programs at the participating universities. All respondents were current students, ranging from first-year to fifth-year and above, including both undergraduate and postgraduate students.

After the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied at the participant level, 2043 valid questionnaires were retained for the final analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 97.14%. Among the participants, 587 were male and 1456 were female.

Procedure

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants were required to be medical students currently enrolled in accredited medical programs at the participating universities. (2) Participation had to be voluntary, with informed consent obtained prior to data collection. (3) Participants were required to have no diagnosed mental health disorders, as self-reported. Exclusion criteria: (1) Individuals who were not enrolled in a medical program (e.g., students from non-medical disciplines) were excluded. (2) Individuals who declined to participate or did not provide informed consent were excluded. (3) Individuals with a self-reported history of diagnosed mental health disorders were excluded to avoid potential confounding effects on psychological assessment outcomes.

Data collection was conducted from March 6, 2025, to April 20, 2025, using convenience sampling at three medical universities. Anonymous online questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site during class sessions with the cooperation of faculty advisors. Prior to survey commencement, participants were informed of the study’s purpose, voluntary participation nature, and confidentiality measures. All participants signed electronic informed consent forms.

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time without repercussions. To ensure confidentiality, no personally identifiable information (such as names or student ID numbers) was collected. All responses were securely stored and used solely for academic research. Data screening and analysis were conducted exclusively on a de-identified dataset after excluding invalid or incomplete questionnaires.

The experiment has passed the ethical review standards of Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine and is allowed to proceed with the investigation. All survey respondents have provided informed consent.

Sociodemographic information

A demographic questionnaire was self-compiled based on the reviewed literature. It includes demographic information such as the gender, age, only-child status, family residence, and chosen field of study of the research subjects.

The chinese calling scale (CCS)

The chinese calling scale, developed by Dobrow et al.19 and revised by Zhang et al.20, was used for the assessment. This scale consists of 11 items that measure career calling comprehensively across three dimensions: meaning, value, and guidance and altruistic contribution. It uses a 5-point Likert scale, where “completely disagree” corresponds to “1” and “completely agree” corresponds to “5.” The total score ranges from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of career calling. The internal consistency reliability of the scale, as measured by Cronbach’s α coefficient, was found to be 0.858.

The academic burnout scale (ABS)

The Academic Burnout Scale developed by Lian et al.21 was employed to assess the level of academic burnout among participants. This scale was specifically developed for Chinese undergraduate populations and was constructed with reference to the conceptual framework of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), adapting its dimensions to the academic learning context. The final instrument comprises 20 items distributed across three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, behavioral deviance, and a low sense of achievement, which collectively reflect the multidimensional nature of academic burnout among college students. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater levels of academic burnout. The scale has demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.887 in the present study.

The connectedness to nature scale (CNS)

The connectedness to nature scale, developed by Mayer and Frantz22 and revised by Li and Wu23, was used for the assessment. This scale comprises 14 items and evaluates a single dimension—natural relatedness. It uses a 5-point Likert scale, where “strongly disagree” is scored as “1” and “strongly agree” as “5”. The total score ranges from 14 to 90, with higher scores indicating a greater level of natural connection. The scale’s internal consistency reliability, as indicated by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, is 0.957.

The perceived social support scale (PSSS)

The perceived social support scale, developed by Zimet et al.24 and revised by Yan and Zheng25, was used. This scale consists of 12 items that comprehensively assess an individual’s perceived social support across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support. It employs a 7-point Likert scoring system, where “strongly disagree” corresponds to “1” and “strongly agree” corresponds to “7.” The total score ranges from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating a greater ability to perceive social support. The internal consistency reliability of the scale, as measured by Cronbach’s α coefficient, was found to be 0.957.

Data processing

All data analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0. Descriptive statistical analyses were first conducted to calculate the mean, standard deviation, and correlation coefficients of all study variables. To further verify the absence of common method bias, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted. The single-factor model showed poor fit (χ2/df = 8.25, CFI = 0.56, RMSEA = 0.12), indicating that common method variance was not a serious concern.

A chain mediation model was then constructed to examine the mechanism through which career calling (independent variable) influences academic burnout (dependent variable), with natural connection and perceived social support serving as mediating variables. The analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (version 4.2, Model 6) developed by Hayes (2013), controlling for gender and reasons for choosing a major. The bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples was used to estimate indirect effects, and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Mediation effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include zero.

The total effect of career calling on academic burnout was significant (β = − 0.68, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of career calling were associated with lower levels of academic burnout. Through chain mediation analysis, the direct effect of career calling on academic burnout remained significant after introducing the mediators (β = − 0.42, p < 0.001), suggesting partial mediation. As shown in Fig. 1, career calling significantly and positively predicted natural connection (β = 0.49, p < 0.001) and perceived social support (β = 0.71, p < 0.001). Natural connection significantly and positively predicted perceived social support (β = 0.56, p < 0.001). In turn, both natural connection (β = − 0.13, p < 0.001) and perceived social support (β = − 0.20, p < 0.001) significantly and negatively predicted academic burnout. Discussion

Research has confirmed that career calling negatively predicts academic burnout, aligning with the conclusions of existing literature and thus validating Hypothesis 1. Career calling connects an individual’s self-identity with specific occupational roles. According to role theory, when individuals have a clear and positive attitude toward support and identification with their future occupational roles, they tend to set personal goals based on the norms and expectations of these roles, thereby exhibiting strong goal-oriented behavior. This process enables individuals to direct their efforts effectively, avoiding potential academic burnout caused by a lack of goals or inappropriate learning methods.

Research findings indicate that natural connection can significantly mediate the negative predictive effect of career calling on learning fatigue, supporting Hypothesis 2. Medical students carry the career calling of being “entrusted with health, entrusted with life,” with an intrinsic awe toward life serving as a key psychological antecedent. Existing research indicates that the emotion of awe, through dual cognitive pathways—weakening self-centrality and activating transcendent attention orientation—encourages individuals to shift cognitive resources from academic stressors to the broader natural system, thereby enhancing ecological psychological connections26,27. Interaction with nature helps regulate physiological functions, including lowering blood pressure, enhancing attention, and stimulating positive emotions while suppressing negative emotions28. These positive physiological and psychological changes are crucial for alleviating the symptoms of study burnout among medical students, helping to enhance their overall learning efficiency and mental health.

Research has also found that perceived social support can serve as a mediating factor between career calling and study burnout, thus validating Hypothesis 3. Individuals with stronger career calling are able to perceive social support more deeply and are more open to accepting various forms of it29. Studies have shown that the ability to perceive social support is significantly positively correlated with psychological resilience30. Perceived social support not only provides necessary emotional comfort and positive motivation for medical students, effectively alleviating the stress and fatigue they experience in their studies, but also grants them strong psychological resilience when facing difficulties and challenges. This reduces their tendency to retreat and give up under pressure, and enables them to effectively suppress the growth of negative emotions, thereby reducing the level of study burnout.

Furthermore, this study confirmed that natural connection and perceived social support have a significant chain mediating effect between medical students’ career calling and study burnout, thus supporting Hypothesis 4. From the perspective of social interaction theory, natural connection significantly influences individuals’ social interaction patterns: those who are closely connected with nature tend to build interdisciplinary social networks by participating in ecological practice activities, actively seeking, integrating, and utilizing various social resources in the process31. For medical students, natural connection may influence their perception of social support mainly through two pathways: firstly, it enhances their environmental empathy, prompting them to develop open and inclusive thinking patterns through interactions with the natural environment, thereby making it easier to recognize and accept diverse forms of social support; secondly, natural activities inherently possess social embeddedness, prompting medical students to establish strong connections with peers, mentors, and interdisciplinary teams during cooperation, thereby directly enhancing their perceived intensity of social support7,32. Compared to traditional social channels, social support driven by natural connections is based on a shared natural cognitive foundation, which results in higher emotional density and professional relevance. This characteristic allows medical students to more effectively convert the social support they receive into resources for addressing academic challenges, thereby helping them overcome the negative effects of study burnout.

Limitations and practical implications

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling from only three medical universities in specific regions of China, along with a gender-imbalanced sample (71.3% female), may limit the generalizability and external validity of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and does not eliminate the possibility of reverse causality or unmeasured confounding variables. Third, although confirmatory factor analysis suggested that common method bias was not a serious concern, all variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may still be susceptible to social desirability bias and common method variance. Additionally, the mediation analysis was conducted using a regression-based approach (PROCESS Macro, Model 6), which, while appropriate for testing indirect effects, relies on observed composite variables and does not explicitly account for measurement error. Future research could employ Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to incorporate latent variables and provide more rigorous model evaluation through comprehensive fit indices (e.g., χ2/df, CFI, RMSEA, SRMR). Moreover, longitudinal or experimental designs, the inclusion of objective indicators (e.g., academic performance or physiological stress markers), and broader sampling across different stages of medical training and related health disciplines would further strengthen the reliability, validity, and generalizability of the findings.

For education and healthcare professionals, integrate professional ethics and medical humanities into talent development systems, incorporate nature-based activities to alleviate academic stress, and establish a tripartite support system involving mentors, peers, and families. For individuals with weak professional commitment or insufficient connection to nature, design targeted intervention programs to promote collaboration between academic institutions and healthcare organizations, incorporating burnout monitoring into talent development assessments. Strategically, a prevention framework centered on career calling can be established, comprising “activation of professional identity—cultivation of natural connection—reinforcement of social support.” This should be implemented in phases according to the needs of foundational, clinical, and graduation stages. Institutions should clarify strategies and departmental responsibilities, while the industry should incorporate mission cultivation and psychological support into internship assessments, establish outcome tracking mechanisms, and form a closed-loop system of “training—practice—guarantee.”

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that career calling negatively predicts academic burnout among medical students through three pathways: the mediating roles of natural connection and perceived social support, as well as their chain mediating effect. These findings highlight the interplay between professional identity, natural connection, and social resources in protecting against academic burnout. Educational interventions aimed at strengthening career calling, promoting natural connection, and enhancing social support systems may effectively reduce burnout and improve medical students’ academic experience.
