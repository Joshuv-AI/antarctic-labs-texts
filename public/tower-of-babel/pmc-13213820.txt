Specific Cultural Myths Impacting Antipsychotic Drug Adherence: Reclaiming Healthy Thinking Through Emotional Recovery
Authors: Sarah Merritt Ryan
Published: 2026

To understand why taking antipsychotics and staying on them is difficult, the stigma behind the medicine, similar to psychosis itself and hospitalization, must be considered.1 While anosognosia and the negotiating of side effects with antipsychotics are two established reasons why taking antipsychotics is difficult,2,3 there exists an array of cultural myths based on stigma that assign profound, yet unfounded meanings to antipsychotic drugs that are almost impossible to escape and resist. These myths can feel as real as the illness itself. No other type of pharmaceutical pill on the market bears such deep, yet illogical meanings.

How Emotional Recovery Helps Cut Through Stigma that Produces Illogical Reasoning

In my recent Schizophrenia Bulletin Open piece, I mention that tending to specific emotional concerns in early recovery from psychosis strengthens the likelihood of staying on antipsychotics. I define emotional recovery as trusting and believing in yourself again and wanting to be you.4 These components of emotional recovery are related to “self-esteem,” identified previously as a determinant of adherence to antipsychotic medication.5 Being able to identify stigma and navigate cultural beliefs about antipsychotic use stems directly from the confidence and motivation a patient cultivates through being able to trust and believe in themselves again. Only once they can do so again are they capable of truly thinking for themselves, undoing logical fallacies, and making decisions based on self-care and self-preservation, versus merely worrying what others might think and accepting what common culture tells them to think.

My Lived Experience and Background with Peers

I am a survivor of schizophrenia who has made a full cognitive and emotional recovery. My insight on these cultural myths based on stigma is through making many of these mistakes myself, even as a well-educated, responsible, and conscientious person. The first time I wanted to go off my antipsychotic, my counselor tried to reason with me based on explaining to me what anosognosia is, and how once I’m psychotic, I won’t understand what is happening or have any control. That compelling, logical explanation did not stop me. While I had a serious side effect that was a legitimate factor, so much of my concern was not logical—it was an emotionally charged decision based on stigma. I went off the drug and eventually had my first major psychotic break and then two more.

I learned the hard way that I need to stay on an antipsychotic for life. I saw taking an antipsychotic as holding me back from the rest of my life where I could not move on, but the reality was the complete opposite. I could not move forward in life without an antipsychotic. Once recovered and on medication for life, I have gotten married, given birth to a son, have rebuilt a new career, enjoy a supportive community of good friends, fellow parents, and colleagues, and now have actually accomplished more post-illness than I had in mind for myself pre-illness.

In addition to a Master of Science degree in Communication, I am a Certified Peer Support Specialist through the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill School of Social Work. I have a Personal Perspectives blog with Psychology Today, and I write for Nami.org and Writer’s Digest on topics related to stigma and emotional recovery from psychosis. My insights on cultural myths are confirmed through more than 3 years as lead facilitator for a local, weekly National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI) support group, working with people living with chronic mental health condition, most of whom take antipsychotics. In addition to the myths that influenced me, I have learned of additional cultural myths from my peers as well.

Seven Cultural Myths Perpetuated by Stigma

A deeper awareness and understanding of specific cultural myths by mental health professionals and how to unravel them can help support patients and prevent them from going off antipsychotics for wrong, untimely reasons, leading to relapse. Non-adherence is the greatest predictor of relapse for psychosis in seven long-term studies, increasing chance of relapse by 400%.6

On the most basic level, stigma translates to going off antipsychotics equating to: exoneration; starting over with a clean slate where I have nothing to hide; there was nothing ever really wrong with me; and I can be “normal” just like everyone else now.

Diving deeper though, I identify seven cultural myths that produce reverse logic on why to go off an antipsychotic, along with the reason for non-adherence associated with each myth. I also provide healthy rationales, which I have formed personally, that navigate these myths now that I can identify stigma for what it is.

Conclusion

I believe that understanding the value of emotional recovery, understanding stigma-produced cultural myths, and learning how to address them as mental health professionals with patients would improve medicine adherence and relapse prevention. While I do not provide medical advice to peers and do not use these rationales with them, I do believe there could be a beneficial way to incorporate these healthy rationales in a therapeutic setting that promotes better decision making.
