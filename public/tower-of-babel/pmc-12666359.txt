Prevalence and associated factors of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes in French Polynesia: results from a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Martin Paries, Iotefa Teiti, Maite Aubry, Etienne Patin, Yoann Madec, Kiyojiken Chung, Pauline Boucheron, Stéphane Lastère, Maëlia Etoundi, Tuterarii Paoaafaite, Lisa Dian, Raphaëlle Bos, André Wattiaux, Caroline Michaux, Henri-Pierre Mallet, Lluis Quintana-Murci, Arnaud Fontanet, Van-Mai Cao-Lormeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a major global health challenge, particularly in the Pacific. This study assessed the prevalence and associated factors of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes among adults in French Polynesia.MethodsA cross-sectional survey (2019–2021) was conducted on a nationally representative sample of adults across all five archipelagos (Tuamotu, Gambier, Marquesas, Austral, and Society including the Leeward and Windward Islands). Data included anthropometrics, blood pressure, biochemical markers (lipids, renal function), sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health history. NCDs were defined as hypertension (systolic BP >140 mmHg, diastolic BP >90 mmHg, and/or self-report of anti-hypertension medication use), diabetes (HbA1c >6·5%, and/or self-report of previous medical diagnosis), and obesity (using either BMI ≥30 kg/m2 or a revised definition incorporating ethnicity-specific thresholds and additional anthropometric criteria).FindingsA total of 1893 adults were included (987 women; 906 men). Among them, 669 (weighted prevalence: 35·5%, 95% CI: 33–38) had hypertension, 214 (weighted prevalence: 12·2%, 95% CI: 10–14) had diabetes (including 53 undiagnosed, weighted prevalence: 3·1%, 95% CI: 2–4), and 1022 (weighted prevalence: 51·1%, 95% CI: 48–54) or 847 (weighted prevalence: 42·7%, 95% CI: 40–46) were classified as obese, using BMI-based or revised definitions, respectively. Among those with revised obesity, 439 (weighted prevalence: 52·2%, 95% CI: 48–57) presented metabolic abnormalities typically associated with the condition, while 408 (weighted prevalence: 47·8%, 95% CI: 43–52) were metabolically healthy, a phenotype that declined with age.Women were less likely than men to have hypertension (OR = 0·59, 95% CI: 0·45–0·78), but more likely to present BMI-based obesity (OR = 1·61, 95% CI: 1·23–2·11). Hypertension odds were higher in Leeward versus Windward Islands (OR = 1·61, 95% CI: 1·24–2·09). Revised obesity was associated with low fruit/vegetable intake (OR = 1·53, 95% CI: 1·13–2·09), high meat consumption (OR = 1·52, 95% CI: 1·10–2·10), high fish consumption (OR = 1·54, 95% CI: 1·10–2·16), and low physical activity (OR = 1·58, 95% CI: 1·15–2·15). Obesity increased the odds of diabetes (OR = 2·99, 95% CI: 1·97–4·53) and hypertension (OR = 2·38, 95% CI: 1·80–3·14). Lower odds of obesity were observed in self-declared Polynesians of mixed ancestry (OR = 0·64, 95% CI: 0·42–0·96) and European immigrants (OR = 0·21, 95% CI: 0·07–0·61), as well as lower odds of diabetes in European immigrants (OR = 0·04, 95% CI: 0·01–0·19) compared to self-identified Polynesians.InterpretationThe high prevalence of obesity, a key driver of hypertension and diabetes, highlights the need for both population-wide prevention and targeted interventions across all archipelagos. Using standard indicators alongside locally revised definitions allows a more accurate mapping of NCDs, while the analysis of associated factors supports behavioural changes to reduce risk.FundingResearch Delegation of French Polynesia (Grant N°03557/MED/REC-05/29/2019); ANR France2030-EPIGEMI (Grant N°ANR-23-CHBS-0007); ANRS-MIE EMERGENCES (Grant N°23583).Keywords: Non-communicable diseases, Obesity, Hypertension, Diabetes, French Polynesia, Cross-sectional study

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar using the keywords: “diabetes”, “prediabetes”, “non-communicable diseases”, “hypertension” and “obesity”, in combination with “French Polynesia”. The main nationally representative studies identified were the 2008 PODIUM survey, the 2010 STEPwise survey, a non-communicable diseases (NCDs) prevalence survey conducted in 2019 and the gout focused Ma'i u'u survey in 2021. Additional data were drawn from the International Diabetes Federation Atlas (only for diabetes) and the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration, both of which provided estimates based on the 2010 STEPwise results and/or data from comparable Pacific Island territories. Despite variations between measured and estimated values, all studies consistently reported high prevalence of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes, some indicators (such as obesity) ranking among the highest worldwide.

Added value of this study

This study offers the most comprehensive and current assessment to date of NCDs prevalence and associated factors in French Polynesia. It is the first to fully account for the unique geography of the territory, spread across five archipelagos, by incorporating increased sampling in remote islands. NCDs prevalence was assessed combining biomarkers and self-reported medical information. Obesity was evaluated using both the conventional body mass index (BMI) and a revised definition that incorporates ethnic-specific thresholds as well as additional anthropometric indicator (waist circumference), enabling to distinguish metabolically healthy from unhealthy obesity. Moreover, the study explores associations between NCDs and a broad range of sociodemographic, dietary, lifestyle, and biological variables (including lipid profiles and renal function), to better identify high-risk population groups.

Implications of all the available evidence

NCDs remain highly prevalent in French Polynesia. Obesity is widespread and increasing, even when using ethnic-adjusted definition, mirroring trends across the Pacific region. Hypertension is also on the rise, while diabetes appears to be stable and may have been previously overestimated. Overall, NCDs prevalence exceeds those observed in mainland France and is comparable to or higher than those in other French overseas territories, particularly for obesity. Notably, only half of obese individuals presented at least one associated NCD (metabolic, hypertension, or renal), underscoring the limitations of BMI as a sole indicator of metabolic health. Strong associations were found between NCDs risk and socio-demographic, diet, lifestyle, and metabolic factors. These findings highlight the need for adapted surveillance tools that move beyond standard indicators, and for targeted prevention strategies focussing on the most affected groups.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for 41 million deaths annually and for over half of the global disease burden.1,2 Sustainable Development Goal 3.4 aims to reduce premature mortality from NCDs by one-third between 2015 and 2030 through prevention and treatment, which requires accurate mapping of prevalence and identification of high-risk groups.3 Obesity has risen rapidly in both high-income and low- and middle-income countries, affecting 1 in 8 people worldwide in 2022. It is now considered one of the three major global health threats, alongside undernutrition and climate change.4,5 Obesity significantly increases the risk of premature death and multiple NCDs, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, stroke, cancer, and chronic kidney disease.6 Hypertension, a leading driver of cardiovascular mortality, causes 8·5 million global deaths annually, with a worldwide prevalence of 32% among women and 34% among men aged 30–79 in 2019.7 The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors (GBD) Study 2021 ranked diabetes as the 10th leading cause of death and disability, with a global prevalence of 6·1% in 2021.8 Nearly 75% of NCDs-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, with the Pacific region alone accounting for one-quarter.9,10 In 2019, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, respiratory diseases, and diabetes were responsible for 12 million deaths in the Pacific.10 Recent studies report that 9 of the 10 countries with the highest adult obesity rates are in this region, and that it also has the third-highest prevalence of diabetes worldwide.4,11

French Polynesia, an overseas territory located in the Pacific, has a population of approximately 279,000 spread across 75 islands within five archipelagos: the Society (Windward and Leeward Islands), Austral, Gambier, Marquesas, and Tuamotu Islands.12 The main island, Tahiti (Windward Islands), is home to 68% of the population. Previous national surveys about NCDs were conducted.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 More recent data show a high burden: 48% of adults were obese, 54·4% had hypertension, and 13·4% had diabetes.15 Gout, a common cause of inflammatory arthritis, was also highly prevalent, affecting 14·5% of adults in 2021.16 Additionally, cardiovascular disease was the leading cause of death in 2018.18 However, previous studies focused primarily on the most populated islands (Windward Islands), potentially resulting in low-accuracy prevalence estimates for the other archipelagos. French Polynesia spans an area comparable to that of Europe and includes highly diverse environments, from low-lying coral atolls (e.g., Tuamotu Islands) to mountainous volcanic islands (e.g., Marquesas Islands). This geographic diversity contributes to marked disparities in diet, living conditions, and access to healthcare. Approximately 75% of food is imported, largely composed of ultra-processed, low-fibre foods, particularly in remote islands.19 Healthcare infrastructure is similarly uneven: 80% of medical staff are based on Tahiti, and remote islands experience high turnover. Medical evacuations from remote islands to Tahiti are frequent, costing nearly 2 billion CFP francs (i.e., 16·8 million euros) annually.20

Obesity is traditionally evaluated using Body Mass Index (BMI) measures, which presents some well-documented limitations, including its inability to distinguish fat from lean mass, account for fat distribution, or adjust for age, sex, or ethnicity.21,22 Moreover, BMI thresholds may require adaptation for specific populations, such as Pacific Islanders.23,24 To address these limitations, alternative definitions of obesity have been proposed that combine BMI with additional anthropometric indicators, such as waist circumference, and that consider ethnic-specific thresholds.25 These approaches also distinguish between obesity with associated NCDs, also referred to as clinical obesity or Metabolically Unhealthy Obesity (MUO), and obesity without evidence of NCDs, also known as preclinical obesity or Metabolically Healthy Obesity (MHO).24,26

The MATAEA project (2019–2021) collected a wide range of sociodemographic and health data (i.e., biomarkers, dietary and lifestyle) across all five archipelagos to estimate the prevalence of both NCDs and communicable diseases in French Polynesia.27 Here, we report MATAEA findings on major NCDs (hypertension, diabetes and obesity) and their associated factors, with a focus on obesity assessed using both BMI-based and revised definition.

Methods

Study design

This study is part of the MATAEA project, a nationwide cross-sectional survey conducted in French Polynesia between November 4, 2019, and December 8, 2021, for which the full study design has been previously published.27 The sample size (N = 2100) was determined using the following formula: n = z2p (1 − p)/d2, with z = 1·96 (level of confidence at 95%), p = 50% (conservative prevalence), and d = 3·7% (precision). The target sample size was increased by 20% to account for expected refusals. Briefly, a random sample of adults was selected based on the 2017 population census, stratified by age excluding children and individuals over 69 (18–29, 30–44, 45–69 years), sex (1:1 ratio), and archipelago (Fig. 1). To reflect the heterogeneous geographical distribution of the population across French Polynesia, three strata were defined: Windward Islands, Leeward Islands, and other archipelagos (Tuamotu, Gambier, Marquesas and Austral), which respectively include 75%, 13% and 12% of the total population. Since the population of the Windward Islands is much larger than that of the Leeward Islands and the other archipelagos, the sample size was amplified in the latter two strata to ensure sufficient precision for prevalence estimates of the health conditions of interest. Accordingly, an equal sample size of 700 subjects was allocated for each stratum, allowing a precision never exceeding ±3.7% for prevalence estimates within each stratum (N = 2100 ÷ 3 = 700). Within each stratum, the most inhabited islands with a healthcare centre and an airport were selected (N = 18; Fig. 1). Within each selected island, households were randomly selected by the ‘Institut de la Statistique de la Polynésie française’, using data from the 2017 population census, and one eligible resident from each household was invited to participate. The number of participants to be included was proportional to the population size of the islands.

Map showing the five archipelagos of French Polynesia. The 18 islands selected for the MATAEA project are shown in red. “N” indicates the number of participants (in red) recruited on each island. Inset map at upper left shows location of French Polynesia (red circle) in the Pacific Ocean.

Procedures and data collection

Data were collected from consenting participants using a questionnaire adapted from WHO STEPS studies, covering socio-demographic, behavioural and medical characteristics, as well as additional questions relevant to French Polynesia.27 Physical measurements included height, weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure. Height (cm) was measured with a stadiometer (SECA model 213, France), weight (kg) with an electronic scale (SECA model 813, France), and waist circumference (cm) with a constant tension tape (SECA model 203, France). BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Blood pressure (BP), including systolic and diastolic values (mmHg), was measured on the left arm with an Omron HBP-1320 monitor (Omron Healthcare Co., Japan). Participants rested seated for at least 15 min beforehand, with no recent caffeine intake, and uncrossed legs. Three BP measurements were taken at 3-min intervals while the supported arm remained still and the participant silent.

For each participant, a fasting venous blood sample was collected at home by a nurse using 5 mL serum separation and 5 mL EDTA tubes (Becton Dickinson, USA), following standardised safety and hygiene procedures, after BP measurements to ensure the venipuncture did not affect BP values.27

After centrifugation of the tubes, part of the serum and plasma samples were used to measure several haematological and biochemical parameters.27 HbA1c was measured via capillary electrophoresis (Capillarys 2 Flex Piercing, SEBIA, France). Lipid profiles, including total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides (TG), were measured by enzymatic colourimetric assay (Cobas c501, Roche, Switzerland). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was calculated using the Friedewald formula.28 Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) was estimated using the CKD-EPI equation.29

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables including sociodemographic characteristics, data on diet and lifestyle, lipid levels, and renal health were reported using percentages and counts. Associations between categorical variables were tested using Chi-squared (χ2). Prevalences of diabetes, hypertension, and obesity were weighted to account for the sampling strategy. Inverse probability weights, calculated from the 2017 census as the inverse of the selection probability for characteristics such as age, sex, and archipelago of residence, were applied to ensure population-representative prevalences. Individuals without blood collection (N = 32) or adiposity measures (N = 17) were removed from the analysis (2·8% of total individuals). Remaining missing data (0·8% total, 0·7% for BMI, 1·6% for HbA1c, 1·3% for blood pressure), identified as missing at random, were imputed via k-nearest neighbours' method (K = 5). Separate logistic regression models were fitted for each outcome of interest and provided odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All factors were initially included in the full adjusted model. In the final adjusted model, only statistically significant variables were retained, with significance defined as p < 0·05 for overall or category-specific p-values. Statistical analyses were performed with R (v4.4.2) using the survey and VIM packages.37,38

Sample statistics showed that participants had a median age of 39·0 years (IQR: 29·0–53·0), with 540 (28·5%) aged 18–29 years, 659 (34·8%) aged 30–44 years, and 694 (36·7%) aged 45–69 years. The sex distribution was also balanced with 987 (52·1%) women and 906 (47·9%) men (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). Most participants (1754 individuals, 92·7%) were born in French Polynesia. Self-reported ethnicity was predominantly Polynesian (1496 individuals, 79·0%), followed by “mixed” (272 individuals, 14·4%), European (99 individuals, 5·2%), and Asian (14 individuals, 0·7%). The highest education level had been attained by 348 individuals (18·4%), while the majority had not completed high school (Table 1).

Population estimates revealed high meat (997 individuals, 53·4%) and fish consumption (1238 individuals, 59·7%), yet only 856 individuals (50·5%) met fruit/vegetable intake recommendations. More men reported high meat consumption (516 men, 57·6% vs. 481 women, 49·0%; p = 0·004), as well as low fruit/vegetables intake (511 men, 52·7% vs. 526 women 46.1%; p = 0·026). High alcohol consumption was low (63 individuals, 4·0%), with men reporting greater consumption than women (48 men, 5·3% vs. 15 women 2·6%; p = 0·028). Current smoking prevalence was high (641 individuals, 32·8%), with no significant sex difference. Only 863 individuals (42·1%) met physical activity recommendations, with men significantly more active than women (531 men, 51·5% vs. 332 women, 32·3%; p < 0·0001). Most individuals had normal TC and LDL-C. However, 276 individuals (14·8%) had high TG and 492 (22·9%) had low HDL-C, while 265 (17·2%) had high HDL-C. More men had high TG (167 men, 19·2% vs. 109 women, 10·3%; p < 0·0001) and low HDL-C (274 men, 28·0% vs. 218 women, 17.6%; p < 0·0001) than women. Renal failure affected 48 individuals, representing 3·1% of the population, with no significant sex difference (Table 1).

Prevalence of NCDs and associated factors

Hypertension

The weighted prevalence of hypertension among adults was 35·5% (95% CI: 33–38), affecting 1215 individuals. There were variations between archipelagos (p = 0·015), ranging from 67 individuals (33·2%, 95% CI: 27–40) in the Marquesas to 223 individuals (42·6%, 95% CI: 38–47) in the Leeward Islands. Prevalence was significantly higher in men than in women (375 men, 40·0%, 95% CI: 36–44 vs. 303 women, 30·9%, 95% CI: 27–35; p = 0·0015) and increased markedly with age from 83 individuals (13·0%, 95% CI: 10–17) aged 18–29 years, to 384 individuals (56·8%, 95% CI: 52–62) aged 45–69 years (p < 0·0001) (Table 2, Fig. 2A).

Obesity

Overall, BMI distribution was high (median: 30·1 kg/m2, IQR: 25·7–35·7). The weighted prevalence of BMI-based obesity was 51·1% (95% CI: 48–54), affecting 1022 individuals, with only 351 adults having a normal BMI (20·1%, 95% CI: 18–23) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 2C). Among obese individuals, 459 (23·5%) had moderate obesity, 269 (13·6%) severe, and 294 (14·0%) morbid (Supplementary Table S1).

The weighted prevalence of obesity using the revised definition was 42·7% (95% CI: 40–46), affecting 847 individuals (Table 6). Among these, 408 individuals (47·8%, 95% CI: 43–52) had no associated NCDs (including cardiac, metabolic, or renal conditions) corresponding to the MHO phenotype. The MHO phenotype was more common in women than in men (261 women, 52·4%, 95% CI: 46–58 vs. 147 men, 42·8%, 95% CI: 36–49; p = 0·034) and declined with age (from 162 individuals, 80·5%, 95% CI: 73–86 aged 18–29 years to 72 individuals, 22·3%, 95% CI: 17–29 aged 45–69 years; p < 0·0001) (Table 6, Fig. 2D). Conversely, 439 individuals (52·2%, 95% CI: 48–57) presented associated NCDs, defining MUO phenotype, which increased with age (p < 0·0001) (Table 6, Fig. 2D). The distribution of MHO and MUO varied across archipelagos (p = 0·027), with higher MHO prevalence in the Tuamotu-Gambier Islands (49 individuals, 54·7%, 95% CI: 45–64) and higher MUO prevalence in the Leeward Islands (143 individuals, 62·2%, 95% CI: 56–68) (Table 6, Fig. 2D).

However, while women had higher odds of BMI-based obesity than men (OR = 1·61, 95% CI: 1·23–2·11), odds of MUO did not differ with sex. Similarly, higher educational level was protective against MUO (OR = 0·55, 95% CI: 0·31–0·96) but showed no significant association with BMI-based obesity.

Discussion

The prevalence of hypertension in French Polynesia increased from 26·7% (2010) to 35·5% (95% CI: 33–38) in our study (2019–2021), with a higher prevalence among men (40·0%) than women (30·9%).13,15 For comparison, prevalence was 30·6% in mainland France (2014–2016) and ranged from 20·8% (La Réunion) to 31·5% (Martinique) across French overseas departments in 2021.13,39,40 In the Pacific region, hypertension rates are also high, although based on older age groups (30–79 years); in 2019, 39·6% of men and 46·8% of women in Tonga, and 39·1% of men and 39·7% of women in Niue, were reported as presenting hypertension.7 Hypertension is thus a growing public health issue in French Polynesia, with prevalence among the highest among French territories and comparable to that of other Pacific Island nations.

Diabetes prevalence has remained relatively stable across available studies, although differences in diagnostic methods limit direct comparisons. In 2010, prevalence was estimated at 10% based on self-reports, while in 2019 it was 14% using a single fasting blood glucose measurement. Our study, relying on HbA1c, estimated prevalence at 12·2% (95% CI: 10–14). Comparable results were observed in the Ma'i u'u survey, which reported a prevalence of 13·9% using the same diagnostic criteria.16 Also, similar rates have been observed in French overseas departments (12·1% in Mayotte in 2019, 11·5% and 13·6% in Martinique and La Réunion, respectively, in 2021), whereas mainland France reported a prevalence of 8·0% in 2021.41, 42, 43 Pacific nations showed higher rates, such as Tonga (26·2% in women, 20·1% in men) and Niue (24·2% in women, 22·8% in men) in 2022.44 However, cross-country comparisons are challenging because of difference in diabetes diagnostic methods (self-reports, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c), as well as overestimations in diabetes prevalence among nearby territories as seen in the Fiji 2011, Tonga 2012, and Samoa 2013 STEPS surveys.45 Additionally, the International Diabetes Federation estimated a prevalence of 22·8% for French Polynesia in 2024, notably higher than our findings, suggesting earlier overestimates.11 Our study, using HbA1c, a standardised biomarker for chronic hyperglycaemia, offers a more accurate assessment of diabetes burden in this population.

Despite 40% of individuals with hypertension being aware of their condition, only 25·5% had taken medication two weeks before the study, compared to 47·3% treatment coverage in France (2014–2016).39 Women exhibited greater awareness than men (141 women, 45·6% vs. 118 men 35·7%; p = 0·05), consistent with broader trends of higher awareness and treatment rates in women.10 Regarding diabetes, 3·1% were unaware of their glycaemic issues. Among all individuals with diabetes (diagnosed and undiagnosed), 93 individuals (41·8%) were treated, slightly lower than France (50·2% of women, 46·8% of men, 2019), but still higher than in most Pacific Island nations, where it was the lowest worldwide (e.g., <25% in Fiji and Solomon Islands, 2019).44 However, women were less likely to receive treatment than men (45 women, 31·4% vs. 48 men 52·2%; p = 0·017), likely reflecting a higher prevalence of undiagnosed diabetes in women (34 women, 4·1% vs. 19 men, 2·2%). These findings indicate low treatment coverage for hypertension and moderate coverage for diabetes in French Polynesia, with notable sex differences.

The prevalence of BMI-based obesity increased from 40% in 2010 to 51·1% (95% CI: 48–54) in our study, indicating that 1 in 2 adults in French Polynesia is obese.13 This rate is four times higher than the global average of 1 in 8 individuals, highlighting a severe public health concern.4 In France, obesity prevalence was 17% in 2020, with severe and morbid obesity affecting 3% and 2% of the population, respectively.46 In contrast, rates of severe and morbid obesity were 14% and 14% in our study, indicating a disproportionate burden of extreme obesity. Prevalence also highly exceeds rates found in French overseas departments (e.g., 16% in la Réunion and 26% in Mayotte in 2019).47 These findings are consistent with regional data; in 2022, obesity affected 60% of women and men in eight and six countries, respectively, of the Polynesian and Micronesian region.4

Obesity classifications are evolving to reflect its metabolic and clinical heterogeneity. Emerging frameworks advocated for a more tailored definition, incorporating ethnic-specific thresholds and additional anthropometric criteria. Some distinguish between ‘preclinical’ and ‘clinical’ obesity based on the presence of obesity-related complications, thus questioning whether obesity is a disease or a risk factor.24 Other have proposed the distinction between MHO and MUO.26 Although no standardised definition exists yet, existing frameworks agree that obesity encompasses a spectrum of metabolic profiles, rely on similar biomarkers to characterise affected individuals, and acknowledge that BMI alone is a poor indicator of individual metabolic health. In our study, using ethnic-adapted thresholds and additional anthropometric criteria, we estimated the prevalence of obesity in French Polynesia at 42·4% (95% CI: 39–45). Among obese individuals, 52·2% (95% CI: 48–57) were classified as MUO, indicating that half of them exhibited associated complications. The remaining 47·8% (95% CI: 43–52) were categorised as MHO, which is much higher than the commonly assumed 10–20% of obese individuals considered MHO (e.g., ∼12% measured in Europe), although cross-country comparisons are limited by the lack of a standardised definition.26 Importantly, the MHO phenotype should not be considered benign or exempt from intervention, as it carries elevated cardiometabolic risk compared to metabolically healthy non-obese individuals.26 Consistent with previous findings, we found that MHO rates decrease with age.48 A recent hypothesis suggests that MUO and MHO differ in fat distribution: MUO is characterised by greater visceral fat, whereas MHO has more subcutaneous fat.49 The Ma'i u'u survey further demonstrated that both obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2) and visceral fat strongly associated with gout, which is also linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular events.16 These findings highlight the clinical importance of weight management in reducing cardiometabolic risk. Using a more comprehensive classification of obesity may help shift treatment goals from aggressive weight loss to maintaining metabolic health, which can often be achieved with moderate weight reduction.

Identifying key factors associations with NCDs is essential for designing effective prevention strategies. Here, several socio-demographic factors were significantly associated with NCDs. Age was strongly associated with diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, with odds increasing markedly after age 30 and sharply after age 45. This is particularly concerning in the context of French Polynesia's ageing population, which is expected to intensify healthcare demands and long-term care needs.50 Sex-specific differences were also observed: men had higher hypertension odds, consistent with other Pacific studies,10 whereas women had higher odds of BMI-based obesity, aligning with previous studies.51 This may reflect sex-related differences in body composition, as women tend to have higher body fat percentages at the same BMI, and more peripheral fat distribution.52 These factors may partly explain the higher prevalence of the MHO phenotype in women (261 women, 52·4% vs. 147 men, 42·8%; p = 0·034) in our study. Moreover, no significant sex difference in odds of MUO was observed. These highlight the limitations of BMI-based definitions of obesity and support integrating additional anthropometric and body composition measures to refine sex-specific risk assessment. Furthermore, university education appears protective against MUO, likely reflecting socioeconomic advantages. This aligns with global trends linking lower socioeconomic status to higher NCDs risk.53 Residents of the Leeward Islands exhibited higher odds of hypertension compared to other archipelagos. They also had the highest prevalence of prediabetes (13%), suggesting a potential future rise in diabetes cases. The drivers of these regional differences remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Behavioural factors, including unhealthy diets and low physical activity, emerged as key contributors to obesity. Low fruit and vegetable intake (49·5% of the population), high meat (53·4%) and fish (59·7%) consumption, and low physical activity (57·9%) were associated with increased odds of obesity. As obesity was associated with higher odds of both diabetes and hypertension in our study, these findings highlight the urgent need to address modifiable lifestyle behaviours. However, over half the population may be unable to afford a healthy diet, which is estimated to cost 2·3 times more than the global average54 Local initiatives aim to improve diets and access to fresh products.19,54 In contrast with most existing literature, our study found an inverse association between alcohol consumption and obesity.55 However, this association may be influenced by confounding factors such as socioeconomic status or biases in self-reported consumption.

We also investigated key metabolic biomarkers that contribute to NCDs risk. High TG and low HDL-C affected 14·8% and 22·9% of the population, respectively, notably higher than in France (6% and 14·9%, respectively).33 Both biomarkers were significantly associated with diabetes, as previously observed.56 These lipid abnormalities likely reflect poor dietary habits, rich in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats, combined with low physical activity.56 Although high LDL-C levels were less common (8·9%), it was significantly associated with hypertension. Renal failure was also associated with diabetes, reflecting its known role in renal disease, which is particularly prevalent in French Polynesia (3·1% in our study) compared to mainland France (1·5% in 2014–2016).57,58 These findings underscore the value of integrating these biomarkers into routine screenings to identify individuals at high risk and facilitate earlier intervention.

Ethnic disparities in NCDs risk were also apparent. Compared to self-declared Polynesians, European-ancestry individuals had lower odds of MUO and diabetes, while mixed-ancestry individuals (partly Polynesian) also showed lower odds of MUO. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the self-reported nature of ethnicity and potential unmeasured confounders.

Our study has several limitations. Several key covariates, including ethnicity, dietary intake, lifestyle behaviours, and medical history, were self-reported, which may introduce reporting bias. We were unable to distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Only alcohol consumption frequency was available for all study participants, which differs from the WHO STEPs method that recommends measuring quantity, and may therefore underestimate hazardous drinking and related risks. The classification of MUO participants was based on a limited set of clinical conditions associated with obesity, which may underestimate the true MUO prevalence.24

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive and updated assessment of the status of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity in French Polynesia, using more suitable markers and criteria. Importantly, our results highlight the limitations of relying solely on BMI as a health indicator and support the inclusion of additional anthropometric measures, ethnic-specific thresholds and the consideration of comorbid conditions to improve diagnostic accuracy. Our results offer valuable insights for local public health planning and will serve as new reference indicators for disease monitoring in the region. Additionally, we observed strong associations between NCDs and key determinants, including age, sex, ethnicity, dietary patterns, physical activity, and lipid disorders. Ongoing research under the MATAEA project aims to explore why individuals of Polynesian descent are at higher risk for NCDs, by examining environmental, intrinsic and genetic factors, as well as their interactions.
