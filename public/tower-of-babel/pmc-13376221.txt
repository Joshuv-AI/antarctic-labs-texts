Integrated structural and physiological adaptations of Buffel grass (Cenchrus ciliaris) to aridity gradients
Authors: Safura Bibi, Muhammad Sajid Aqeel Ahmad, Ali Bahadur, Zunaira Naeem, Mansoor Hameed, Yuncong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cenchrus ciliaris (Buffel grass) is a perennial, drought tolerant grass usually colonizes tropical and subtropical areas of the world. It grows for forages and fodder. The selected habitats were classified based on soil depth and Budekyo lettau dryness ratio (8.4-30.49) as least dry habitats NeW (Neela Wahn) and SoV (Soon valley), moderately dry habitats (KKr (Kallar kahar) and NpT (Noorpur Thal), severely dry habitats PaK (PakAnna), and Cho (Cholistan desert). The different ecotypes of Cenchrus ciliaris were evaluated for adaptability potential through morpho- physiological and anatomical attributes under water stress in relation to soil physico-chemical and environmental attributes. Root, shoot and leaf growth was significantly reduced in habitats with a greater dryness ratio such as Cholistan (Cho). The physiological attributes of pigment contents, ionic contents, and antioxidant activities were suppressed with increasing dryness ratio NpT, PaK and Cho. Among root anatomical attributes such as enhanced epidermal thickness, sclerified metaxylem vessels and large pith area was significantly higher in populations of dry land (Pak and Cho). The leaf anatomical attributes showed significant variations including intensive sclerification around the epidermis, cortical and vascular region, small metaxylem vessels that help them to conserve more water under water stress. The morpho-physiological and anatomical modifications provide better resilience for the movement of water and solutes and prevent water loss. These changes also provide mechanical strength for better survival under water stress.

Introduction

Environments are classified as arid or semi-arid based on their extreme temperature conditions and persistently low levels of precipitation1. Arid conditions have a detrimental impact on both plant survival and yields worldwide2. In Pakistan, over 80% of the regions fall under these arid or semi-arid classifications, resulting in constrained crop production due to severe weather conditions and unpredictable rainfall patterns3. Variations in key environmental factors, including temperature fluctuations and changes in rainfall patterns, can substantially reduce crop yields in these regions4. High temperatures and long-term drought are the major ecological factors that affect desert plants physiology. These plants have evolved distinctive morphological and anatomical features enabling them to adapt to these stresses5.

Plants have evolved various morpho-physiological strategies that minimizes water loss and increase water absorption which play an important role in the survival of plants under a limited water supply6. Drought stress stimulates the production of soluble compounds, which help in osmotic adjustment7. These solutes include amino acids, glycerol, sugars, and other micro- molecules. Ionic homeostasis primarily involves the modulation of inorganic ions such as Sodium (Na+), calcium (Ca2+), potassium (K+) and chlorine (Cl-) 8. It serves to mitigate cytosolic ionic imbalance by regulating the influx of sodium (Na+) and vacuolar sequestering9.

Plants have evolved diverse anatomical adaptations, including enhanced leaf succulence, which plays a vital role in ensuring the survival of many grass species under drought conditions10. Other distinctive anatomical features such as broader metaxylem vessels and increased vascular bundle area and bulliform cells in leaves are the most prominent structural modifications that play a pivotal role in better translocation of water and also maintaining turgor pressure during drought periods11. The cortical cells in ground tissues (parenchyma) not only provide mechanical support but also provide storage space for various metabolites. Additionally, the length of xylem vessels serves as an important indicator of various environmental stresses, including heat and drought12.

Drought stress is the major environmental factor that can significantly impair plant growth. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of plant experiencing extreme water deficit can allow to understand how this latter cope with stressful conditions13. Valued as a pasture grass for its drought resistance, rapid growth, extensive root system, and resilience to overgrazing, C. ciliaris plays a crucial role in water and nutrient management as well as environmental stabilization14. This study based on hypothesis that integrated morpho-physiological and structural changes have evolved as adaptive strategies enabling long-term ecological success of Buffel grass in diverse moisture-deficit environments. The current study investigates the following objectives: (1) To assess the influence of soil physio-chemical properties on plant growth, pigment composition, ionic balance, and osmolyte accumulation under moisture deficit conditions. (2) To identify structural traits in roots, stems, and leaves that contribute to enhanced drought resilience in extreme desert climates. (3) To provide ecological insight into survival strategies and stress tolerance mechanisms of Buffel grass in its native habitats, bridging the gap in research beyond its forage potential. The research gap is, the ecological behaviour of C. ciliaris in its natural habitats is largely unexplored, with prior work focusing mainly on its forage potential rather than its role in ecosystem functioning. Secondly, while morphological and physiological adaptations are described, the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms driving drought tolerance are not addressed, leaving a gap in understanding stress-response regulation at the genomic level.

Biochemical traits

The method followed to determine mineral elements by acid digestion is31. Dried plant material weighing 0.1 g was dried and then digested in concentrated H2SO4 overnight at room temperature in digestion flasks. Following this, 1 milliliter of 35% H2O2 was added, and the mixture was heated on a digestion plate at 350 °C for 30 min. Then 1 ml of 35% H2O2 was added, and the heating process was continued until the mixture became colorless, with intermittent repetitions of adding H2O2 and heating. Once colorless, the mixture was diluted. The resulting filtrate was utilized for the determination of Na+, K+, and Ca2+ using a flame photometer, while magnesium was determined via an atomic absorption spectrophotometer.

Anatomical attributes

The structural features of leaf, stem and root were studied by using fresh plant material that was deeply immersed in FAA solution [Formalin (10%), Ethanol (50%), acetic acid (5%), and diluted with distilled water32. Permanent freehand sections were prepared by gradually dehydrating the samples in alcohol solutions of increasing concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and absolute), followed by staining using a double staining method (1% safranine, 5% fast green, and 95% ethanol). Photographs were taken using a microscope equipped with a camera (Nikon 104, Japan). For leaf epidermis a common approach is the clear nail polish or silicone impression technique, where a thin layer is applied to the leaf epidermis, allowed to dry, and peeled off to capture stomatal imprints. These impressions are mounted on microscope slides and examined under light microscopy to measure stomatal density and size.

Statistical analysis

The data was subjected to analysis of variance ANOVA by using COSTAT 8.1 software and Treatment means equated by least significant difference test at 5% probability33. PCA (Factoextra+ factominor), Heatmap (pheatmap, Correlations (corplot) was executed with a code respectively using R studio (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2020) and ecological analysis using CANOCO (4.5 version) software.

Results

Soil physico-chemical attributes

MC of soil varied significantly among habitats and soil depth. Maximum MC was observed at the habitat NeW (3.46%) and minimum at Cho (1.56%). It increased along the soil depth; the maximum moisture contents (MC) were recorded at 25 cm depth (5%) in all selected sites. SP (%) differed among the habitats and soil depth. At NeW habitat high SP (31.66%), KKr (22.3%), NpT (30%), PaK (27%) and Cho (30%) showed variable trend along the habitats. High SP was recorded at topsoil (0–5 cm) and reduced subsequently from 10 to 15 cm and 20–25 cm OM (%) increased along the soil depth for habitat NeW, KKr and Cho. Habitat Cho (9.2) showed more pH while NeW, SoV, KKr and PaK did not show variations in soil pH. Higher ECe was recorded at habitat PaK and Cho while lower at SoV and NpT soils. Soil sodium (Na+) contents showed significant variations among habitats while non-significant for soil depth. PaK (63 mg/L) showed high level of Na+ ion as compared to other habitats in rooting zone of soil. K+ showed statistically significant variations among the habitats and soil depth. Higher potassium (K+) contents were observed at NpT (8 mg/L) habitat and lower at Cho (2.2 mg/L) soil. Higher Ca2+ contents were observed at PaK. Topsoil had more calcium (Ca2+) as related to the bottom soil 20–25 cm. Higher Mg2+ was recorded from the soil sampled at habitats KKr, NpT and PaK (Fig. 2). Higher Chloride (Cl−1) and P were observed at NpT, PaK and Cho rooting zone soil of Cc. Higher NH4+-N was observed at PaK (0.53) rooting zone soil of Cho habitat. NO3–N showed higher content at NeW (0.15) and lower at NpT (0.034) soil. Both inorganic nitrogen forms reduced the increasing soil depth (Fig. 3).

Morphological and physiological attributes

Root: Shoot length increased up to KKr (0.72) and then reduced up to Cho (0.37) for Cc. Shoot and root f: d wt. ratio reduced subsequently as the soil moisture deficit increased along the habitat. Cho habitats plants species Cc exhibited smaller leaf area as compared to NeW (2.4) and SoV (2.0) (Fig. 4). Among physiological attributes, all photosynthetic pigments i.e. chlorophyll (chl. a), chlorophyll (chl. b) and carotenoids (Car) decreased as dryness ratio increased along the habitats (Fig. 5).

NpT, PaK and Cho (15 mg/L) exhibited higher Ca2+contents in both shoot and root tissue of Cc. Potassium (K+) contents were lower at Cho (13 mg/L) where moisture is not sufficient while higher was recorded at NeW (55 mg/L) habitat. Shoot Na+ increased as the stress increased. Sodium (Na+) contents of shoot Cc were lower at NeW (14 mg/L). Phosphorous and Nitrogen contents in root and shoot increased from NeW to NpT and then further reduced from PaK to Cho (Fig. 6).

Organic osmolytes i.e., Total soluble proteins (TSP) (2.68–4.54 mg/L), Total soluble sugars (TSS) (0.1–0.16 µmol/g f.wt.), Total free amino acids (TFAA) (0.16–0.47 mg/g f.wt.), Glycine betaine (GB) (0.01–0.02 µg/g dw) and proline were maximum at PaK and Cho while lower at NeW and SoV (Fig. 7). CAT and SOD activities increased and recorded maximum at more arid habitats i.e. NpT, PaK and Cho (Fig. 8).

Anatomical attributes

The moisture deficit increased epidermal thickness in NpT, PaK and Cho habitats (80.85 μm). Large epidermal cell area (Epi CA) was recorded at NeW (847276 µm2) while small was recorded at Cho (170710 µm2). Thin cortical region (Cor R) was observed along the depth for NeW, NpT and PaK. Smaller cortical cell area (Cor CA) was observed at NeW, SoV and KKr (702–1209 μm). The maximum endodermal thickness was observed at PaK and Cho. But the larger endodermal cell area (End CA) was observed at NpT. Vascular tissues in the smaller metaxylem cell area were observed at NeW and larger metaxylem cell area was recorded at Cho, while larger pith CA (NpT) and Meta XCA were observed for topsoil (0–5 cm) (Figs. 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13). Stem thickest epidermis was observed at NeW, SoV and PaK (21.95–23.62 μm) habitats. Small Epi CA (epidermal cell area) was observed at NpT and Cho (311.8 µm2) while large Epi CA, Meta XCA and Phloem CA were recorded at NeW and SoV (431.05 µm2) (Table 2). Thin and small leaf blade epidermal cell area was observed as water deficit increased. Thick Epi with large CA of leaf blades were observed at NeW. Smaller Cor RCA, increased metaxylem and phloem cell area exhibited as the moisture deficit increased. Number of stomata varied significantly among habitats. Both Ad and Ab Epi consist of a smaller number of stomata under water deficit i.e. Cho. Ad Epi stomatal area (5994 µm2) was higher in the Cho habitat (Table 2).

Discussions

C. ciliaris, C4 perennial grass34 showed survival strategies in diverse habitats under drought stress. Through evolutionary processes, various ecotypes of this species have developed distinctive morpho-physiological and anatomical traits that enable survival under changing environmental conditions35. The present study focuses on exploring morpho-physiological and structural attributes as well as providing deep insight into key mechanisms of underlying stress tolerance of plants to face adversity of their environments. As limited water supply inhibits the growth of plants, it was considered an important criterion to study tolerance mechanisms in plants under water stress. To tolerate stress, plants adjust or regulate their normal growth mechanism to a defensive mechanism leading to the inhibition of growth36. Under moisture deficiency, growth inhibition is directly linked to lower photosynthetic activity and limited ion uptake. Over long periods, species tolerant to soil moisture deficits undergo morphological adaptations, including reduced leaf area, increased root length and succulent leaves help to maximize water use efficiency37,38. The present study showed C. ciliaris population of Cho had a smaller leaf area as compared to other ecotypes. Small leaf areas help minimize the water loss by transpiration. Recent research reported an increase in root-to-shoot length as the dryness ratio increased. The maximum root length was observed in KKr as compared to Cho and NpT populations. This was due to the modulation of several processes, including the reduction of transpiration, promotion of root development and regulation of vegetative growth to manage water loss effectively and enhance water absorption from deeper soil layers during periods of water stress39. Moreover, enhancing root growth can help plant up taking more water from deeper layers of the soil40.

The shoot and root fresh to dry weight ratio significantly decreased with increasing soil dryness ratios. Water deficit condition gradually decreases the growth rate which is crucial to regulate the rate of transpiration and water contents in the plant41. Moreover, water stress is a complex phenomenon that affects the growth and development of plants42. There were significant morpho- physiological adaptations in C. ciliaris under water stress. Drought stress dysfunctions the plastids and disrupts the photosynthetic apparatus, causing damage to mesophyll and dermal tissues that reduce chlorophyll contents43. The decline in photosynthetic pigments (Chlorophyll and carotenoids) was noted in C. ciliaris at dry habitats (Cho and NpT).

Soil containing high amounts of sodium (Na⁺) not only affect plant growth but also, can affect the physiology and structural features of roots and leaves44. Hyper aridity results in the uptake of large quantities of Na⁺ by plant roots, leading to drastic effects on metabolic processes due to ionic and osmotic imbalance, thereby resisting water uptake and nutrient absorption45. Our study investigated, the ionic contents in root and shoot of Cholistan, KKr and NpT populations showed lesser K+, Mg2+, N2, and P contents with high concentrations of sodium, compared to populations inhabiting less arid habitat (NeW). The inefficiency of K+ uptake in arid soils is due to similar physicochemical properties of Na⁺ and K⁺46. The high Na⁺ levels in soil affect the absorbance of K+ ions, that inhabit enzymatic metabolism, loss of turgor and protein synthesis47. Under dry conditions, plants have capability to maintain their water balance to regulate cellular functions. Resultantly, they osmotically adjust themselves by accumulating organic osmolytes48. Soluble sugars, among osmolytes, maintain cellular turgidity by triggering osmotic adjustment and increasing storage reserves to support normal plant functions during water stress49. Osmotic adjustments enable plants to maintain turgor pressure and volume fraction essential to regulate metabolic processes50. The present study describes that there was a significant accumulation of compatible osmolytes i.e., Total soluble sugars, proline and glycine betaine in populations of Cho and NpT populations growing in arid climates. Plants accumulate these substances as a defensive strategy to prevent turgor loss in dry conditions. This defensive strategy has also been previously observed in Vicia faba, Vigna radiata, Morus sp and Arachis hypogea51 and in Spartina alterniflora52 under conditions of low water availability, which may aid in their survival during prolonged periods of water scarcity.

The protection of cell structures against oxidative damage is crucial to maintain cell membrane integrity by the accumulation of enzymatic antioxidants in plants, considered as a defensive strategy against abiotic stress, the key enzymatic antioxidants are SOD, POD, CAT and APX protect plant from oxidative damage53. In recent research, hyper-arid plant populations, such as those from Cho and NpT exhibited notably high activities of enzymatic antioxidants such as ascorbate peroxidase (APX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT). The increase in activities of enzymatic antioxidants considered as an adaptive response to effectively cope with the harsh environmental conditions. Plant structural features are sensitive to environmental cues, particularly drought stress. These structural modifications enhance plant ability to tolerate water stress54. The present findings indicated that C. ciliaris populations from arid habitat Cho had increased root epidermal and endodermal cell areas, as well as thicker epidermal and endodermal layers. It was suggested earlier that a thicker epidermis helps to minimize water loss55,56. Furthermore, a significant increase in the parenchymal area was noted at KKr site, providing ample space for retaining water to withstand prolonged dry conditions. Additionally, larger cortical cells contribute to drought resistance by reducing metabolic demands in roots, promoting root expansion and enhancing water uptake. Another structural change is increased pith cells at the NpT site. Enlarged and loosely packed pith parenchymatous cells help to retain more water during a prolonged period of water shortage57. There is a significant increase in metaxylem cell area at Cho site. Large metaxylem vessels are the most notable anatomical trait for survival in arid environments, facilitating the transport of water, nutrients, and photosynthates58. Plants growing at hyper arid habitat i.e., Cho site showed increased sclerification of dermal and ground tissues, enlarged metaxylem and phloem cell areas under soil moisture deficit. Sclerification is most important to prevent plant tissues from freezing and desiccation59.

Leaf anatomy demonstrates remarkable adaptability to changing environmental conditions. Anatomical adaptations in leaves, such as increasing leaf thickness, and epidermal cells adjustments have been found to be strongly correlated with drought tolerance60. In arid zones, grasses like S. plumosa leaf blades are protected by thickness in the epidermis and cortical region. Large cortical parenchyma in the leaf helps to store more water. This type of succulence has already been reported in grasses61and helps to manage evapotranspiration. Sclerification was recorded in vascular bundles hence making leaves more fibrous and stiff59. Metaxylem vessels were the widest in plants from hyperarid habitats i.e., Cho. Reduction in stomatal density under harsh desert climates results in a reduced transpiration rate62, thus stomatal density reduced in an arid zone i.e., Cho helps conserve water. Moreover, smaller stomata size requires less water to maintain turgor than larger as observed in Cho site.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Cenchrus ciliaris exhibits pronounced morpho-physiological, biochemical, and anatomical adaptations that collectively enhance its survival under varying degrees of aridity in Punjab’s arid and semi-arid zones. Ecotypes from hyper-arid habitats, such as Cholistan, developed smaller leaf areas, reduced stomatal density, thicker epidermal and endodermal layers, enlarged metaxylem vessels, and extensive sclerification to conserve water and maintain structural integrity under drought stress. Physiologically, these populations showed reduced photosynthetic pigments but elevated enzymatic antioxidant activities (SOD, CAT, APX, POD) and higher accumulation of osmolytes (proline, glycine betaine, soluble sugars, proteins), aiding osmotic adjustment and protection against oxidative damage. These integrated structural and physiological adjustments highlight C. ciliaris ecological plasticity and its potential role in vegetation sustainability and environmental stabilization in drought-prone ecosystems.
