Bound Deuteron–Antideuteron System (Deuteronium): Leading Radiative and Internal–Structure Corrections to Bound-State Energies
Gregory S. Adkins, Ulrich D. Jentschura

Abstract
We evaluate the energy levels of the deuteronium bound system, which consists of a deuteron and an antideuteron, with a special emphasis on states with nonvanishing orbital angular momenta. The excited atomic bound states of deuteronium constitute probes for the understanding of higher-order quantum electrodynamic corrections for spin-1 particles in a bound system where the typical field strength of the binding Coulomb field (at a distance of the generalized Bohr radius) exceeds Schwinger’s critical field strength. For states with nonvanishing angular momenta, effects due to the internal structure of the deuteron and virtual annihilation contributions are highly suppressed. Relevant transitions are found to be in a frequency range accessible by standard laser spectroscopic techniques. We evaluate the leading and next-to-leading energy corrections of orders α 3 ​ m d \alpha^{3}m_{d} and α 4 ​ m d \alpha^{4}m_{d} , where α \alpha is the fine-structure constant and m d m_{d} is the deuteron mass, and also investigate internal-structure corrections: hadronic vacuum polarization, finite-size effects, and strong-interaction corrections.

We investigate the

Deuteronium constitutes an analogue of protonium,

;
Pa2008

ZaPa2010

II
Basic Properties

In the center-of-mass frame, the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian
of deuteronium reads as follows,

H_{\rm NR}=\frac{\vec{p}^{\,2}}{m_{d}}-\frac{\alpha}{r}\,,

(1)

where
\vec{p}
is the relative momentum operator in that frame.
Convenient quantum numbers for deuteronium
are the principal quantum number
n
,
the orbital angular momentum quantum number
L
,
the total spin quantum number
S

describing the magnitude of the total spin
\vec{S}=\vec{S}_{+}+\vec{S}_{-}

(where
\vec{S}_{+}
and
\vec{S}_{-}
are the
spin operators for the deuteron and its antiparticle),
the total angular momentum quantum number
J
,
and its projection
J_{z}
.
We denote the states as

|n{}^{2S+1}\!L_{J}(J_{z})\rangle=|nLSJJ_{z}\rangle\,,

(2)

making use of the spectroscopic notation
n{}^{2S+1}L_{J}

(in which states with
L=0

are
S
states, states with
L=1
are
P
states, etc.).
The nonrelativistic wave functions are

\psi_{nLSJJ_{z}}(\vec{r}\,)=R_{nL}(r)\,\Xi^{LS}_{JJ_{z}}(\theta,\varphi)\,,

(3)

where
R_{nL}(r)
is a Schrödinger–Coulomb radial
eigenfunction of a hydrogenlike ion
with nuclear charge number
Z=1
and
reduced mass
m_{r}=m_{d}/2
.
Specifically (see,
e.g.
, Chap. 4 of Ref.
JeAd2022book
) one has

R_{nL}(r)=\left(\frac{4(n-L-1)!}{a_{0}^{3}n^{4}(n+L)!}\right)^{1/2}\rho^{L}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho/2}L_{n-L-1}^{2L+1}(\rho)\,,

(4)

where
a_{0}=1/(\alpha m_{r})=2/(\alpha m_{d})

is the (generalized) Bohr radius, and

\rho=2r/(a_{0}n)
.
The angular part of the wave function
for the state
|n{}^{2S+1}\!L_{J}(J_{z})\rangle
is

\Xi^{LS}_{JJ_{z}}(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{M_{L}M_{S}}C^{JJ_{z}}_{LM_{L};SM_{S}}\,Y_{LM_{L}}(\theta,\varphi)\,\chi_{SM_{S}}\,,

(5)

where
\chi_{SM_{S}}
is the deuteronium total spin state

\chi_{SM_{S}}=\sum_{m_{+}m_{-}}C^{SM_{S}}_{1m_{+};1m_{-}}\;\chi^{(+)}_{m_{+}}\;\chi^{(-)}_{m_{-}}\,.

(6)

Here, the
\chi^{(\pm)}_{m_{\pm}}
are the eigenspinors
of the spin operators of the deuteron and
the antideuteron. A convenient representation of the
spin-1 matrices is

(S_{\pm}^{i})^{jk}=-\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{ijk}
.
These matrices act on the deuteron (
+
) and
antideuteron (
-
) spinors. They fulfill the relations

(S_{\pm}^{z})\,\chi^{(\pm)}_{m_{\pm}}=m_{\pm}\chi^{(\pm)}_{m_{\pm}}
, where
m_{\pm}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
.
In Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), the
Clebsch–Gordan coefficients

C^{JM_{J}}_{j_{1}m_{1};j_{2}m_{2}}
are used
in the notation of Chap. 6 of Ref.
JeAd2022book
.

The lowest-order quantum mechanical eigenproblem
is solved by

H_{\rm NR}\,\psi_{nLSJJ_{z}}(\vec{r}\,)=E_{\rm NR}\,\psi_{nLSJJ_{z}}(\vec{r}\,)\,.

(7)

It gives rise to the nonrelativistic
bound-state energy levels which depend
only on the principal quantum number
n
,

E_{\rm NR}=-\frac{\alpha^{2}m_{d}}{4n^{2}}=-\frac{E_{0}}{n^{2}}\approx-\frac{24969.732\,{\rm eV}}{n^{2}}\,,

(8)

where the Dt Rydberg constant
E_{0}

is defined in Table
1
. One should
take into consideration that, strictly speaking,
the spin-dependent Breit Hamiltonian
(see the following section) is not completely diagonal in the

\psi_{nLSJJ_{z}}
basis, so that certain
mixings between states with “good” quantum numbers
occur in higher orders. Analogous effects occur
in hydrogenlike systems, where the effect is
otherwise known as the fine-structure hyperfine-structure
mixing (for a particularly instructive discussion,
in the context of the mixing of the fine and the hyperfine
structure for muonic deuterium,
see Sec. 6 of Ref.
KrEtAl2016deuteronium
).

The presence of the mixing terms indicates that a careful consideration of the
conserved quantum numbers of deuteronium is indicated. The total angular
momentum
J
and its
z
-component
J_{z}
are, of course, conserved. Parity and
charge parity are conserved in QED. The parity of the antideuteron must be
positive, since it is determined by the intrinsic parities of its constituents
(both
-1
) and the parity of the antideuteron quantum state, which is positive
since it has the same structure as for the deuteron, being a combination of
S

and
D
waves. Then, the parity of a deuteronium state is determined by the
parities of its constituents (both +1) and that of its quantum state
(-1)^{L}
,
for a total parity
P_{\rm Dt}=(-1)^{L}
. So the oddness or evenness of
L
is
conserved. Deuteronium, being formed of a particle and its antiparticle, can
also exist in an eigenstate of charge parity. We determine the charge
conjugation quantum number
C_{\rm Dt}
by considering what happens to a
deuteronium state when its constituents are interchanged. Since
deuterons are bosons,
the effect of this interchange is a factor
+1
for the wave function.
A complete interchange involves the exchange of spatial states [with a factor
of
(-1)^{L}
], spin states [factor
(-1)^{S}
], and charge states (factor

C_{\rm Dt}
). We find that
+1=(-1)^{L}\,(-1)^{S}\,C_{\rm Dt}
, so that

C_{\rm Dt}=(-1)^{L+S}
. In all, we have

P_{\rm Dt}=(-1)^{L}\,,\;C_{\rm Dt}=(-1)^{L+S}\,,\;P_{\rm Dt}C_{\rm Dt}=(-1)^{S}\,.

(9)

So the oddness or evenness of
S
is also conserved.

Good states of deuteronium are specified by specific values of
n
,
J
, and

J_{z}
. In addition, they are constructed of states with either even or odd
values of
L
, and either even or odd values of
S
. One might thus assume
that the Breit Hamiltonian could mix states with different values of
L

provided both states involved in the mixing are even, or, both are odd.
However, it is known that the spin-1/2–spin-1/2 Breit Hamiltonian does not mix
states with different values of
L
. Since the spin-1–spin-1 Breit
Hamiltonian is built of operators with the same types of spatial dependence, it
also does not mix states with different values of
L
.
So,
L
can be considered to be a good quantum number (to all orders).

The caveat for the total spin is that the Breit Hamiltonian, indeed,
mixes spin states, so deuteronium states with even spin
(
S=0
and
S=2
) can be mixed. Odd-spin states have
S=1
.
It means that, strictly speaking,
S
is only a good quantum
number up to order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
, when the Breit Hamiltonian
starts mixing the spin states. However, the same could
be said about the principal quantum number
n
,
since the Uehling potential mixes states with different values of
n
.
We here deal with the Breit Hamiltonian (and the Uehling potential)
perturbatively, including
contributions of first, second, and (eventually) third-order perturbation
theory.

In view of the above mentioned symmetry considerations,

Breit Hamiltonian

In order to derive the spin-dependent corrections
of order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
to the spectrum,
we generalize the Breit Hamiltonian
to the interaction
of two spin-1 particles.
We use the results communicated in
Eq. (3) of Ref.
Pa2007
and
Eq. (17) of Ref.
Pa2008
,
and Eq. (12.100) of Ref.
JeAd2022book

An alternative derivation,

The latter derivation starts from a

\displaystyle H_{\rm BR}=

\displaystyle\;H_{\rm K}+H_{\rm M}+H_{\mathrm{SO}}+H_{\mathrm{FSS}}+H_{\rm Q}+H_{D}\,.

and

\tilde{Q}_{d}=m_{d}^{2}\,Q_{d}=\left(\frac{m_{d}\,c}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\,Q_{d}=25.8120(22)\,,

(13)

leading to relatively large contributions from these quantities.

The Breit Hamiltonian is derived from
the one-photon exchange between the constituent
particles of deuteronium (no virtual loops) and is the
parametrically dominant
nonradiative

correction to the spectrum of deuteronium (order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
).
Moreover, the Breit Hamiltonian leads to
dominant spin-dependent energy corrections in deuteronium.
For comparison, we point out
that (spin-independent) radiative effects due to vacuum polarization
(with a virtual electron-positron loop)
are parametrically larger than the Breit
corrections and commence at order
\alpha^{3}m_{d}
. These
affect the spin-independent structure of deuteronium, with dependence
only of the quantum numbers
n
and
L
, as
discussed in Sec.
IV.1
. Higher-order electron vacuum
polarization corrections (which are still spin-independent
effects) occur at order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
and are
discussed in Secs.
IV.3
—
IV.5
.
Here, we first treat the spin-dependent energy
correction
E_{\rm BR}
which appears
at order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
due to the Breit Hamiltonian:

E_{\rm BR}=\langle H_{\rm BR}\rangle=\langle nLSJJ_{z}|H_{\rm BR}|nLSJJ_{z}\rangle\,.

(14)

We obtain the corrections coming from the Breit
Hamiltonian one term at a time.
We start with the kinetic term
H_{\rm K}
.
In order to evaluate the expectation value
of
\vec{p}^{\,4}
, one observes that
the momentum operator acts
on wave functions as follows
(in our short-hand notation,
\psi

stands for
\psi_{nLSJJ_{z}}
),

\vec{p}^{\,2}|\psi\rangle=m_{d}\,\left(E_{\mathrm{NR}}+\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)|\psi\rangle\,,

(15)

So, one has the relation (we use a further
shorthand notation
\langle K\rangle\equiv\langle\psi|K|\psi\rangle
for any operator
K
),

E_{\rm K}=\left<-\frac{\vec{p}^{\,4}}{4m_{d}^{3}}\right>=-\frac{1}{4m_{d}}\,\left<\left(E_{\mathrm{NR}}+\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)^{2}\right>\\
=-\frac{1}{4m_{d}}\,\left<E_{\mathrm{NR}}^{2}+2E_{\mathrm{NR}}\frac{\alpha}{r}+\left(\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)^{2}\right>\\
=\alpha^{4}m_{d}\left\{\frac{3}{64n^{4}}-\frac{1}{8n^{3}(2L+1)}\right\}\,.

(16)

We have used the following
matrix elements from Eq. (4.346) of Ref.
JeAd2022book
,

\displaystyle\left<\frac{1}{r}\right>=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\alpha m_{d}}{2n^{2}}\,,

(17a)

\displaystyle\left<\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right>=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\alpha^{2}m_{d}^{2}}{2n^{3}\,(2\,L+1)}\,.

(17b)

The magnetic term
H_{\rm M}
is conveniently
evaluated by using the following identity,

H_{\rm M}=-\frac{\alpha}{2m_{d}^{2}}\,p^{i}\left(\frac{\delta^{ij}}{r}+\frac{r^{i}\,r^{j}}{r^{3}}\right)\,p^{j}\\
=-\frac{\alpha}{2m_{d}^{2}r}\,\left(\vec{p}^{\,2}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}[\vec{r}\cdot(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{p}\,)\vec{p}\,]\,\right)\,.

(18)

This relation is consistent with Eq. (84.1) of Ref.
BeLiPi1982vol4
.
Use of Eq. (
15
) leads to the
appearance of matrix elements proportional to

\langle 1/r^{3}\rangle
, which can be
evaluated using Eq. (4.346) of Ref.
JeAd2022book
,

\left<\frac{1}{r^{3}}\right>=\frac{\alpha^{3}\,m_{d}^{3}}{4n^{3}\,L\,(L+1)\,(2L+1)}\,,

(19)

which is divergent for
S
states with
L=0
.
However, a closer inspection reveals that the
matrix element
E_{\rm M}=\langle H_{\rm M}\rangle

is convergent for
S
states, and one obtains
the general formula

E_{\rm M}=\frac{\alpha^{4}m_{d}}{8n^{4}}+\frac{\alpha^{4}m_{d}}{8n^{3}}\delta_{L0}-\frac{3\alpha^{4}m_{d}}{8n^{3}(2L+1)}\,.

(20)

The next term is the spin-orbit term
H_{\rm SO}
.
One writes the total spin operator
(sum for both particles) as

\vec{S}=\vec{S}_{+}+\vec{S}_{-}
,
and obtains

E_{\mathrm{SO}}=\frac{\alpha(2\tilde{g}_{d}-1)}{2\,m_{d}^{2}}\,\langle\vec{L}\cdot\vec{S}\,\rangle\left\langle\frac{1}{r^{3}}\right\rangle\,.

(21)

With use of the definition

B_{LSJ}=\frac{1}{2}\big[J(J+1)-L(L+1)-S(S+1)\big]

(22)

one has

\langle\vec{L}\cdot\vec{S}\,\rangle=B_{LSJ}\,.

(23)

So,

E_{\mathrm{SO}}=\frac{\alpha(2\tilde{g}_{d}-1)}{2\,m_{d}^{2}}\,B_{LSJ}\,\left<\frac{1}{r^{3}}\right>\,.

(24)

The remaining matrix element
\langle 1/r^{3}\rangle
is evaluated
using Eq. (
19
).
The energy shift
E_{\mathrm{SO}}
vanishes for

S
states by spherical symmetry (despite the apparent
1/L
singularity when
L=0
).

The scalar part of the Fermi-spin-spin energy correction,

E_{\mathrm{FSS},1}=\;\frac{2\pi\alpha\,\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}}{3m_{d}^{2}}\,\langle\vec{S}_{+}\cdot\vec{S}_{-}\;\delta^{(3)}(\vec{r}\,)\rangle\,,

(25)

is relatively easy to evaluate.
First, one realizes that
the expectation value of the Dirac delta is

\left\langle\delta^{(3)}(\vec{r}\,)\right\rangle=\frac{(\alpha m_{d})^{3}}{8\pi n^{3}}\,\delta_{L0}
.
With the help of the identity

\langle\vec{S}_{+}\cdot\vec{S}_{-}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\,\big[S(S+1)-4\big]\,,

(26)

one obtains the result

E_{\mathrm{FSS},1}={\tilde{g}}_{d}^{2}\,\frac{\alpha^{4}\,m_{d}}{24\,n^{3}}\,\big[S(S+1)-4\big]\,\delta_{L0}\,.

(27)

For the tensor part of the Fermi spin-spin term,

H_{\rm FSS,2}=\frac{3\alpha\,\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}}{4m_{d}^{2}\,r^{3}}\,(S_{+}^{i}S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}\,(\hat{x}^{i}\,\hat{x}^{j})^{(2)}

(28)

and the quadrupole term

H_{Q}\!=\!-\!\frac{3\,\alpha\,\tilde{Q}_{d}}{2m_{d}^{2}r^{3}}\,\big[(S_{+}^{i}S_{+}^{j})^{(2)}\!+(S_{-}^{i}S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}\big]\,(\hat{x}^{i}\,\hat{x}^{j})^{(2)}\,,

(29)

one needs to carry out some
more angular algebra. We need to evaluate matrix elements involving
different values of the total spin instead of simply expectation values,
since these terms are non-diagonal in the spin variable.
We thus consider an “almost diagonal” matrix element

\big\langle f(r)M\big\rangle_{nLS^{\prime}SJ}\equiv\langle nLS^{\prime}JJ_{z}|f(r)M|nLSJJ_{z}\rangle
,
where all quantum numbers of the bra and ket states
are equal except, possibly, for different
S
and
S^{\prime}
.
The operator
f(r)
is supposed to have
only a radial dependence, and
M
is assumed to
depend only on the elements of the unit vector
\hat{x}
,
and on the spin variables.
With reference to the radial component
of the wave function [see Eq. (
3
)],
we can first separate out the radial dependence using

\Big\langle f(r)M\Big\rangle_{nLS^{\prime}SJ}=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\big[R_{nL}(r)\big]^{2}\,f(r)\;\big\langle M\big\rangle_{LS^{\prime}SJ}

(30)

when
M
is independent of the radial variable and

\big\langle X\big\rangle_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\equiv\big\langle LS^{\prime}J|X|LSJ\big\rangle

(31)

for any operator
X
.
Employing relations derived in Appendix
A
,
which are based on angular reduction,
one derives the following angular matrix elements:

\displaystyle D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\big[(S_{+}^{i}\,S_{+}^{j})^{(2)}+(S_{-}^{i}\,S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}\big](\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j})^{(2)}\right\rangle_{LS^{\prime}SJ}

(32)

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt=(-1)^{J+S^{\prime}}\Big[(-1)^{S}+(-1)^{S^{\prime}}\Big]\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}(2L+1)

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\times\Pi_{S^{\prime}S}\begin{Bmatrix}J&S^{\prime}&L\\
2&L&S\end{Bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}L&2&L\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix}S&S^{\prime}&2\\
1&1&1\end{Bmatrix}\,,

and

\displaystyle C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle(S_{+}^{i}\,S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}(\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j})^{(2)}\right\rangle_{LS^{\prime}SJ}

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{(S+S^{\prime})/2}\,\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta_{S^{\prime}S})D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\,,

where
\Pi_{ab\cdots}\equiv\sqrt{(2a+1)(2b+1)\cdots}
.
Standard notation is used for the
Wigner
3j
and
6j
symbols
(see Chap. 6 of Ref.
JeAd2022book
).
One thus obtains the result

\displaystyle\big\langle H_{\rm FSS,2}+H_{\rm Q}\big\rangle_{nLS^{\prime}SJ}

(34)

\displaystyle\hskip-91.04872pt=\frac{3\alpha}{4m_{d}^{2}}\big\{\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}-2\tilde{Q}_{d}D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\big\}\left\langle\frac{1}{r^{3}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip-91.04872pt=\frac{m_{d}\alpha^{4}}{n^{3}}\times\frac{3\big(\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}-2\tilde{Q}_{d}D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\big)}{16L(L+1)(2L+1)}\,.

Both
C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}
and
D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}
vanish when
L=0
by
spherical symmetry, and this zero in the numerator takes precedence over the
apparent singularity caused by
L
in the denominator.

Finally, for the Dirac delta contribution, one has

E_{D}=\frac{4\pi\alpha}{3m_{d}^{2}}\tilde{r}_{d}^{2}\,\langle\delta^{(3)}(\vec{r}\,)\rangle=\frac{\alpha^{4}m_{d}}{6n^{3}}\,\tilde{r}_{d}^{2}\,\delta_{L0}\,.

(35)

Let us fix the quantum number
n
,
L
,
J
,
and
J_{z}
, and consider the matrix
elements of the Breit Hamiltonian
in the basis of the total spin quantum
numbers
S
and
S^{\prime}
, which takes values
equal to
0
,
1
and
2
. In the basis of the

S
and
S^{\prime}
quantum numbers, one thus
obtains a three-by-three matrix.
According to the above mentioned symmetry
considerations, the resulting matrix is
diagonal except for the case
L=J
,
where one encounters off-diagonal elements
for
S^{\prime}=2
,
S=0
and
S^{\prime}=0
,
S=2
.
Summing up the results given
in Eqs. (

), (
20
), (
24
),
(

), (

) and (

),
one obtains

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{\rm BR}\right\rangle_{nLS^{\prime}SJJ_{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle nLS^{\prime}JJ_{z}|H_{\rm Br}|nLSJJ_{z}\right\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\alpha^{4}m_{d}\,M^{nLJ}_{S^{\prime}S}\,.

The results are,
of course, independent of
J_{z}
. The explicit
expression for
M^{nLJ}_{S^{\prime}S}
is

\displaystyle M^{nLJ}_{S^{\prime}S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{11}{64n^{4}}-\frac{1}{2n^{3}(2L+1)}\right)\delta_{S^{\prime}S}

(37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{L\,0}}{8n^{3}}\left(1+\frac{4}{3}\tilde{r}_{d}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}}{3}\big[S(S+1)\!-\!4\big]\right)\delta_{S^{\prime}S}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{(2\tilde{g}_{d}-1)\,B_{LSJ}}{8n^{3}L(L+1)(2L+1)}\delta_{S^{\prime}S}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{3\big(\tilde{g}_{d}^{2}C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}-2\tilde{Q}_{d}D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\big)}{16n^{3}L(L+1)(2L+1)}\,.

The angular structures
B_{LSJ}
,
C_{LS^{\prime}SJ}
, and

are

IV
Vacuum Polarization

IV.1
One–Loop Electronic VP

In momentum space, the Coulomb potential
takes the form
V(\vec{k}\,)=-4\pi\alpha/\vec{k}\,^{2}
,
where we set the nuclear charge number
Z=1
.
The one-loop correction to
the Coulomb potential, pictured in Fig.
1
(a), is given
by the replacement

-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}\,^{2}}\rightarrow-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}\,^{2}}\,\left[-\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(-\vec{k}\,^{2})\right]\,,

(39)

where
\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(-\vec{k}\,^{2})
is the renormalized one-loop
vacuum polarization function in the conventions of Ref.
LaJe2024
,
evaluated for space-like momentum transfer
k^{2}=-\vec{k}\,^{2}
.
After using a dispersion relation,
one may express the (electronic) vacuum polarization
potential as

V^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}(r)=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{2}{r}\int\limits_{2m_{e}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-qr}}{q}\;\mathrm{Im}\!\left[\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\right]\,.

(40)

With the substitution
Sc1970vol3

v=\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_{e}^{2}}{q^{2}}}\,,\quad q=\frac{2m_{e}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\,,

(41)

one may thus express the one-loop
Uehling potential as follows (see Ref.
Ue1935
),

V^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,f_{1}(v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{e}(v)r}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,,

(42)

where
\lambda_{e}(v)\equiv 2m_{e}/\sqrt{1-v^{2}}

and we define the one-loop spectral function as

f_{1}(v)=\frac{2v}{1-v^{2}}\,\frac{{\rm Im}\,\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(v)}{\alpha}=\frac{v^{2}(1-v^{2}/3)}{1-v^{2}}\,.

(43)

The energy shift due to this potential is

E^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}=\big\langle V^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}\big\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\left[R_{nL}(r)\right]^{2}\,V^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}(r)\,.

(44)

There is no dependence on the spin. Using the non-relativistic wave functions
given in Eq. (
3
),
one finds that

E^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)E_{0}\,C(n,L)\,,

(45)

where
E_{0}
is the deuteronium Rydberg (as given in Table
1
)
and the dimensionless coefficient
C(n,L)
is

C(n,L)=\left(-\frac{2(n-L-1)!}{n^{2}(n+L)!}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,f_{1}(v)\\
\!\!\times\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\rho\,\rho^{2L+1}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho}\left[L_{n-L-1}^{2L+1}(\rho)\right]^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-n\beta_{\mathrm{Dt}}\,\rho/\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\,,

(46)

and
\beta_{\mathrm{Dt}}=m_{e}/(\alpha m_{r})
has been given
in Table
1
. For given
n
and
L
,
the
\rho
integral can easily be performed
analytically, and the
v
integral can be
performed numerically. (We should note that,
for individual states, fully analytic results
exist
Pu1957
;
LaJe2024
, but the
numerical integration proceeds without problems
Wo1999

with the built-in numerical integration
routines of modern computer algebra systems.)
One notes that the potentially singular terms in
1/(1-v^{2})
cancel
naturally after the integration over
\rho
.
The numerical results are shown in Table
4
.
As examples, the
1S
energy correction is mainly determined by electron vacuum polarization:

E^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}(1S)=-125.203\,42\,{\rm eV}\,,

(47)

and the
n=3
Lamb shift due to electron vacuum polarization is

E^{(1)}_{\rm eVP}(3P\!-\!3S)=1.89842\,{\rm eV}\rightarrow 653.09\,{\rm nm}\,.

(48)

The one-loop eVP shift is by far the dominant
radiative correction in heavy bound
systems
Pu1957
;
OwRe1972
;
Pa1996mu
;
JeSoIvKa1997
;
KaIvJeSo1998
;
Bo2012
;
KaIvKo2012
;
ReDi2018a
;
ReDi2018b
.

IV.2
One–Loop Muonic VP

In muonic bound systems, one can
approximate the muonic vacuum polarization potential
by a Dirac delta potential
Pa1996mu
;
Je2011aop1
,
because the mass scale of the bound particle
(the muon) is equal to the mass of the
virtual muon in the vacuum polarization loop.
It means that, for muonic bound systems
and muonic vacuum polarization,
the effective
\beta
parameter in
Eq. (
46
) is large, approximately equal to
1/\alpha
.
Hence, muonic vacuum polarization effects
are drastically suppressed for bound systems
where the orbiting particle is a muon,
and are in fact of order
\alpha^{5}m_{r}
for

S
states and of higher order than
\alpha^{5}m_{r}

for non–
S
states.
It is a characteristic of the
deuteronium bound system that
muonic vacuum polarization even makes a
substantial contribution to the
bound-state energy of non–
S
states,
because the
\beta
parameter for
muonic vacuum polarization in deuteronium,

\beta^{\mu}_{\mathrm{Dt}}=\frac{m_{\mu}}{\alpha m_{r}}\approx 15.4\ll 137.0\approx 1/\alpha\,,

(49)

is much smaller than the inverse of the fine-structure
constant. For muonic vacuum polarization,
one replaces, in Eq. (
46
),

\beta_{\mathrm{Dt}}\to\beta^{\mu}_{\mathrm{Dt}}\,.

(50)

The muonic vacuum polarization potential is

V^{(1)}_{\mu{\rm VP}}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,f_{1}(v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mu}(v)r}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,,

(51)

where

\lambda_{\mu}(v)=\frac{2m_{\mu}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\,,

(52)

and the energy shift is

E^{(1)}_{\mu{\rm VP}}=\big\langle V^{(1)}_{\mu{\rm VP}}(r)\big\rangle\,.

(53)

Results for the one-loop muonic energy
E^{(1)}_{\rm\mu VP}

are summarized in Table
4
.
While results are strongly suppressed for
states with orbital angular momentum
L\geq 2
,
substantial energy shifts are
encountered for
S
and
P
states.

IV.3
Reducible Two–Loop Correction

In momentum space, the reducible two-loop correction [see Fig.
1
(b)],
the loop-after-loop correction,
can be described by the replacement
of the Coulomb potential according to
[cf. Eq. (
39
)]

-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}\,^{2}}\rightarrow-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}\,^{2}}\big[\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(-\vec{k}\,^{2})\big]^{2}\,.

(54)

Hence, we can use the formalism used for the one-loop
correction, but with the
one-loop polarization function
\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}
squared,

V^{(2\rm R)}(r)=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{2}{r}\int\limits_{2m_{e}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-qr}}{q}\;\mathrm{Im}\!\left[-\left(\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\right)^{2}\right]\,.

(55)

One may use the identity

{\rm Im}\!\left[\left(\Pi^{(1)}_{\rm R}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\right)^{2}\right]\!=2\,{\rm Re}\big[\Pi^{(1)}_{\rm R}(q^{2})\big]\;{\rm Im}\big[\Pi^{(1)}_{\rm R}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\big]

(56)

above threshold
q^{2}>4m_{e}^{2}
, in order to
simplify the expression.
We recall from Ref.
LaJe2024
that

\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\biggl[\frac{1}{9}\left(8-3\tilde{v}^{2}\right)\\
-\frac{1}{6}\tilde{v}\left(\tilde{v}^{2}-3\right)\ln\left(\frac{\tilde{v}-1}{\tilde{v}+1}\right)\biggr]\,.

(57)

where

\tilde{v}=\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_{e}^{2}}{q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon}}\,.

(58)

On the cut (
q^{2}>4m_{e}^{2}
),
\tilde{v}
becomes
v+\mathrm{i}\epsilon
, where

v=\sqrt{1-4m_{e}^{2}/q^{2}}
satisfies
0<v<1
.
The logarithm in Eq. (
57
)
transforms as follows

\ln{\left(\frac{\tilde{v}-1}{\tilde{v}+1}\right)}=\ln{\left(\frac{1-v}{1+v}\right)}+\mathrm{i}\pi\,.

(59)

So, on the cut, one has

\mathrm{Im}\,\!\big[\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\big]=\frac{\alpha v}{6}\left(3-v^{2}\right)\,.

(60)

The real part, for
q^{2}>4m_{e}^{2}
, is

\mathrm{Re}\,\!\big[\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\big]=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\left[\frac{8-3v^{2}}{9}-\frac{v^{3}-3v}{6}\ln\left(\frac{1-v}{1+v}\right)\right].

(61)

The one-loop spectral function from Eq. (
43
) is

f_{1}(v)=\frac{2v}{1-v^{2}}\,\frac{{\rm Im}\,\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{R}}(v)}{\alpha}\,.

(62)

For the two-loop reducible diagram,
we can define a corresponding spectral function

\displaystyle f_{2\rm R}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2v}{1-v^{2}}\,\frac{\pi}{\alpha^{2}}{\rm Im}\!\left[-\left(\Pi^{(1)}_{\rm R}(q^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)\right)^{2}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=-2f_{1}(v)\left[\frac{8-3v^{2}}{9}-\frac{v\left(v^{2}-3\right)}{6}\ln\left(\frac{1-v}{1+v}\right)\right]\,.

The two-loop reducible potential thus is

V^{(2\rm R)}_{\rm VP}(r)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,f_{2\rm R}(v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{e}(v)r}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,,

(65)

and the remaining evaluation of

E^{(2\rm R)}_{\rm VP}=\left\langle V^{(2\rm R)}_{\rm VP}(r)\right\rangle

(66)

proceeds as outlined in Sec.
IV.1
.

The vacuum-polarization correction also

(72)

where
\hat{g}_{nL}
is radial component, and
L

is the orbital angular momentum of the reference state.
We should remark
that a couple of corrections are necessary in Ref.
JoHi1979

\ln(x)
in the first line of (2.14) should be
\ln({x_{>}})
,

\mathrm{e}^{x}
and

in the

JoHi1979

e^{x_{<}}
and
({x_{<}})^{k}

JoHi1979

radial reduced Green functions
for

LaFl1977

V
Internal–Structure Effects

V.1
Hadronic VP: Calculation

Even though hadronic VP [see Fig.
1
(f)] constitutes a numerically
small energy correction,
it is an extremely interesting effect to study
as it pertains to the internal hadronic
structure of the virtual photons
exchanged between the constituent
particles of deuteronium.
Our approach, as outlined below,
is inspired by Refs.
JeSoIvKa1997
;
KaIvJeSo1998
,
where hadronic VP effects
were studied for true muonium (alternatively
referred to as dimuonium, the
\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
bound system).
The strategy will be as follows.

(i)

The dominant contribution to hadronic
vacuum polarization comes from the
pion form factor,
which has been given in a particularly useful parameterization in Ref.
GoSa1968
.

(ii)
The
\omega
and
\phi
meson contributions
are taken into account in the pole approximation.

(iii)

Finally, the high-energy background above 1 GeV
is estimated by taking into account the

R
ratio of the relevant cross sections of virtual
photons into hadronic versus light fermionic particles,
in the high-energy region.

First, we deal with the pionic contribution.
The hadronic VP insertion is described
by the following replacement of the momentum space Coulomb potential,
[see Eq. (2.1) of Ref.
SaTeYe1984

and Eq. (1) of Ref.
JeSoIvKa1997
],

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}^{2}}\to

\displaystyle\;-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\,\rho_{\rm had}(s)\,\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}^{2}+s}\,,

(79)

where
\rho_{\rm had}(s)
is the hadronic contribution
to the vacuum polarization spectral function.
We assume that the spectral function

\rho_{\rm had}(s)
has a sufficiently
complicated form so that
analytic evaluations are impossible.
After a transformation to coordinate space,
the hadronic vacuum polarization potential
takes the form

V_{\rm had}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\,\rho_{\rm had}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{s}\,r}\,.

(80)

For the pion insertion, one obtains

V_{\pi}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\,\rho_{\pi}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{s}\,r}\,,

(81)

with
s_{0}=(2m_{\pi})^{2}
.
We are inspired by the approach
outlined in Ref.
SaTeYe1984
. The spectral function is of the form

\rho_{\pi}(s)=\frac{(s-4\,m_{\pi}^{2})^{3/2}}{12\,s^{5/2}}\,\left|F_{\pi}(s)\right|^{2}\,,

(82)

where the pionic form factor is used in the form given by Gounaris and
Sakurai
GoSa1968
:

F_{\pi}(s)=\frac{N}{D_{1}+D_{2}-\mathrm{i}\,D_{3}}\approx\frac{N}{D_{1}-\mathrm{i}\,D_{3}}\,.

(83)

According to Eq. (11) of Ref.
GoSa1968
,
the pion form factor can
be approximated in terms
of the quantities
N
,
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
,
as follows,

\displaystyle N=

\displaystyle\;m_{\rho}^{2}+d\,m_{\rho}\,\Gamma_{\rho}=m_{\rho}^{2}\,\left(1+d\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rho}}{m_{\rho}}\right)\,,

(84a)

\displaystyle d=

\displaystyle\;\frac{3m_{\pi}^{2}}{\pi k_{\rho}^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{m_{\rho}+2\,k_{\rho}}{2\,m_{\pi}}\right)+\frac{m_{\rho}}{2\,\pi\,k_{\rho}}-\frac{m_{\pi}^{2}\,m_{\rho}}{\pi\,k_{\rho}^{3}}\approx 0.48,

(84b)

\displaystyle D_{1}=

\displaystyle\;m_{\rho}^{2}-s\,,

(84c)

\displaystyle D_{2}=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma_{\rho}\frac{m_{\rho}^{2}}{k_{\rho}^{3}}\,\bigg[k(s)^{2}\,\left(h(s)-h_{\rho}\right)+k_{\rho}^{2}\,\,h^{\prime}(m_{\rho}^{2})\,\,(m_{\rho}^{2}-s)\bigg]\,,

(84d)

\displaystyle D_{3}=

\displaystyle\;m_{\rho}\,\Gamma_{\rho}\,\left(\frac{k(s)}{k_{\rho}}\right)^{3}\,\frac{m_{\rho}}{\sqrt{s}}\,,

(84e)

where
h^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
h
,
and the functions
k
and
h
are defined as

k(s)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{s-4m_{\pi}^{2}},\quad h(s)=\frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{k(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{s}+2\,k(s)}{2\,m_{\pi}}\right)\,.

(85)

Furthermore, we use the short-hand notations

k_{\rho}=k(m_{\rho}^{2})
and

h_{\rho}=h(m_{\rho}^{2})
.
The interpretation is as follows:

N
is a normalization factor.

D_{1}
is the dominant term in the
denominator (in fact, for
D_{2}=0
,
and constant
D_{3}
, one would obtain
a Lorentzian resonance profile for the

\pi
resonance). The term
D_{2}
corrects
the Lorentzian profile, while
D_{3}
parameterizes
the width of the
\pi
resonance.
We use the following values from the
latest data compilation of the
Particle Data Group (see Ref.
PDG2024
),

\Gamma_{\rho}=147.4(8)\,{\rm MeV},\qquad m_{\rho}=775.26(23)\,{\rm MeV}\,,

(86)

and the charged pion mass

m_{\pi}=139.57039(18)\,{\rm MeV}
.

Numerically, the contribution of the

D_{2}
is smaller than that of
D_{1}
.
Hence, we can use the following approximation,

|F_{\pi}(s)|^{2}=\frac{N^{2}}{(D_{1}+D_{2})^{2}+(D_{3})^{2}}\approx\frac{N^{2}}{(D_{1})^{2}+(D_{3})^{2}}\,.

(87)

The difference of the results obtained
with the approximation
D_{2}=0
, and without,
is taken as the basis for an estimate
of the theoretical uncertainty
of the pionic contribution to hadronic vacuum polarization.
The same approach was applied in Ref.
SaTeYe1984
.
However, one should note the following
necessary corrections to Eq. (2.12) of Ref.
SaTeYe1984
,

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\Gamma_{\rho}^{d}}{m_{\rho}}\right)^{2}\to

\displaystyle\;\left(1+d\frac{\Gamma_{\rho}}{m_{\rho}}\right)^{2}\,,

(88a)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{s-4m_{\pi}^{2}}{m_{\rho}^{2}-4m_{\pi}^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\to

\displaystyle\;\left(\frac{s-4m_{\pi}^{2}}{m_{\rho}^{2}-4m_{\pi}^{2}}\right)^{3}\,.

(88b)

The relative uncertainties based on this error estimate
range from about 3% (for
S
states) to 11% (for the
4F
state)
for principal quantum numbers
n=1,2,3,4
.

We can now proceed to evaluate

E_{\pi}(nL)=\big\langle V_{\pi}(r)\big\rangle\,=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\left[R_{nL}(r)\right]^{2}\,V_{\pi}(r)\,.

(89)

For example, for the
2P
state of deuteronium, one obtains the results

E_{\pi}(2P)=-4.1(2)\times 10^{-7}\,{\rm eV}\,,

(90)

which constitutes the dominant hadronic VP correction
to the energy of this state.

For
\omega
and
\phi
mesons,
it is adequate to use the pole approximation,

\rho_{p}(s)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{f_{p}^{2}}\,\delta(s-m_{p}^{2})\,,

(91)

where the subscript
p
designates the particle being considered.
After Fourier transformation, one can express
the vacuum polarization potential due to the resonance
with particle
p
as a Yukawa potential,
with a specific coupling parameter,

V_{p}(r)=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{f_{p}^{2}}\,\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}r}\,.

(92)

We can use the parameters

\frac{f_{\omega}^{2}}{4\pi}=\;18(2)\,,\qquad\frac{f_{\phi}^{2}}{4\pi}=\;11(2)\,

(93)

(see Refs.
SaTeYe1984
;
BaSpYePi1978
)
and the mass values
m_{\omega}=782.66(13)\,{\rm MeV}
,

m_{\phi}=1019.461(16)\,{\rm MeV}

of Refs.
TiMoNeTa2021
;
MoNeTaTi2025
.
The hadronic vacuum-polarization potential
V_{p}(r)

due to one of the
\omega
and
\phi
mesons
leads to the energy shift

E_{p}(nL)=\big\langle V_{p}(r)\big\rangle\,=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\left[R_{nL}(r)\right]^{2}\,V_{p}(r)\,.

(94)

The contribution
of the high-energy background above
1\,{\rm GeV}
can be
estimated, in the dispersive approach,
on the basis of the spectral function

\rho(s)=\frac{R}{3s},\qquad m_{1}<\sqrt{s}<m_{2}\,,

(95)

where
R
is the ratio of a virtual
photon going into a hadronic pair,
to a virtual photon going into a
light fermion pair
JeSoIvKa1997
.
Assuming
R
to be piecewise constant is a permissible
approximation, because, according
to Ref.
JeSoIvKa1997
,

\displaystyle R\approx

\displaystyle\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{2}=2&\quad m_{1}\equiv 1\,{\rm GeV}<\sqrt{s}<m_{2}\equiv 4\,{\rm GeV}\\[8.61108pt]
R_{4}=4&\quad m_{2}\equiv 4\,{\rm GeV}<\sqrt{s}<\infty\end{array}\right.\,.

According to Fig. 18 of Ref.
KeNoTe2018
,
the approximation (
V.1
) still represents
a suitable approximation to the additional,
recently available experimental data in the relevant
energy domain.
Hence, the vacuum polarization potential
describing the high-energy background
for
m_{1}<\sqrt{s}<m_{2}

is (in momentum space)

V_{m_{1},m_{2}}(\vec{k})=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{(m_{1})^{2}}^{(m_{2})^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\,\,\frac{R}{3s}\,\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\vec{k}^{2}+s}\,.

(98)

After Fourier transformation, the potential becomes

V_{m_{1},m_{2}}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\frac{2R}{3}\,\Bigl[{\rm Ei}(-m_{2}r)-{\rm Ei}(-m_{1}r)\Bigr]\,.

(99)

Here, the exponential integral
{\rm Ei}(z)
is given as follows,

{\rm Ei}(z)\equiv-\int_{-z}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}e^{-t}\,.

(100)

The hadronic vacuum polarization potential
due to the high-energy background
can thus be approximated as

\displaystyle V_{b}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\left(-\frac{2\alpha}{3r}\right)\,\bigg[R_{2}\Big\{{\rm Ei}(-m_{2}r)-{\rm Ei}(-m_{1}r)\Bigr\}

(102)

\displaystyle\hskip 54.06006pt+R_{4}\Bigl\{0-{\rm Ei}(-m_{2}r)\Bigr\}\bigg]\,,

leading to the energy shift

E_{b}(nL)=\big\langle V_{b}(r)\big\rangle\,=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\left[R_{nL}(r)\right]^{2}\,V_{b}(r)\,,

(103)

which can easily be evaluated numerically.

According to Eqs. (
89
), (
94
), and (
103
),
the result for the hadronic vacuum polarization energy shift

E_{\rm had}(r)
is

E_{\rm had}(nL)=E_{\pi}(nL)+E_{\omega}(nL)+E_{\phi}(nL)+E_{b}(nL)\,.

(104)

For the states of interest for the current investigation,
one obtains

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(2P)=

\displaystyle\;-4.8(5)\times 10^{-4}\,{\rm meV}\,,

(105a)

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(3P)=

\displaystyle\;-1.7(2)\times 10^{-4}\,{\rm meV}\,,

(105b)

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(3D)=

\displaystyle\;-2.9(3)\times 10^{-9}\,{\rm meV}\,,

(105c)

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(4P)=

\displaystyle\;-7.5(8)\times 10^{-5}\,{\rm meV}\,,

(105d)

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(4D)=

\displaystyle\;-1.8(2)\times 10^{-9}\,{\rm meV}\,,

(105e)

\displaystyle E_{\rm had}(4F)=

\displaystyle\;-1.8(2)\times 10^{-14}\,{\rm meV}\,.

(105f)

We have included an uncertainty of about 10%, as suggested by the effect of
the approximation (
87
) in the pion contribution,
the simplicity of our approximations, and the size of the error in the estimate
of the hadronic VP contribution to the muon anomalous moment, as
described below. These energy corrections are small compared to other
contributions of order
\alpha^{3}
or
\alpha^{4}
.

V.2
Hadronic VP: Verification

In order to verify our approximate approach for the
treatment of hadronic vacuum-polarization, we have carried
out a calculation of the corresponding
contribution to the muon anomalous magnetic moment
a_{\mu}
.
The leading two-loop hadronic
contribution to the muon anomalous
magnetic moment is
LaPedR1972
;
Ad1974prd1
;
Je2011aop1

\delta a_{\mu,{\rm had}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{2}\,\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,K_{\mu}(s)\,\rho_{\rm had}(s)\,,

(106)

where the kernel function is

K_{\mu}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x^{2}\,(1-x)}{x^{2}+(1-x)\,s/m_{\mu}^{2}}\,.

(107)

As a check of the formulas, we consider the replacements

m_{\mu}\rightarrow m_{e}
and

\rho_{\rm had}(s)\rightarrow\rho_{e}(s)=\frac{1}{\alpha s}{\rm Im}\left[\Pi^{(1)}_{R}(s)\right]=\frac{v(1-v^{2}/3)}{2s}\,

(108)

in Eqs. (
106
) and (
107
). With those replacements, one obtains the
known contribution to the electron anomalous moment due to one-loop electron vacuum
polarization (see p. 852 of Ref.
BaMiRe1972a
),

(109)

\displaystyle\;+\underbrace{0.51\times 10^{-8}}_{\mbox{$\phi$ meson}}+\underbrace{1.35\times 10^{-8}}_{\mbox{background}}

\displaystyle\;6.75\times 10^{-8}\,.

V.3
Finite–Size Effect

The deuteron has a finite size; its root-mean-square (RMS)
charge radius is
r_{d}=2.12778(27)\,{\rm fm}
. So, the interaction between deuteron and antideuteron is not really
that between two point-like particles. The fact that the charge is spread out
means that the average potential will not
be quite as negative as it would be were
the particles pointlike, so the binding energy will be less in magnitude that
it would be for pointlike particles. That is, the energy correction due to the
finite size of the particles will be positive. We can make an estimate of the
size of this correction by calculating the interaction energy for two uniformly
charged spheres of radius
r_{d}
. This estimate is fully sufficient
for our purposes, because the nuclear-structure effects
are numerically dominated by the strong-interaction correction
discussed in Sec.
V.5
.

Consider first the case of a pointlike particle of mass
m_{1}
and charge

q_{1}=-e
in the field of a uniformly charged spherical shell
of radius
r_{d}
, mass
m_{2}
, and charge
q_{2}=e
.
The Schrödinger equation describing this situation is

-\frac{1}{2m_{r}}\nabla^{2}\psi(\vec{r}\,)+V(r)\psi(\vec{r}\,)=E\psi(\vec{r}\,)\,,

(111)

where

V(r)=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\alpha}{r_{d}}&{\rm if}\;r<r_{d}\\[17.22217pt]
-\dfrac{\alpha}{r}&{\rm if}\;r>r_{d}\end{cases}\,.

(112)

We write
\psi(\vec{r}\,)=R_{\nu L}(r)\,Y_{Lm}(\theta,\varphi)

and
R_{\nu L}(r)=\frac{u_{\nu L}(r)}{r}
.
In terms of
u_{\nu L}(r)
, the radial equation is

-\frac{1}{2m_{r}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}u_{\nu L}(r)+\left(V(r)+\frac{L(L+1)}{2m_{r}r^{2}}\right)u_{\nu L}(r)\\
=E_{\nu}\,u_{\nu L}(r)\,.

(113)

We define
\nu
so that the energy eigenvalue is

E_{\nu}=-\frac{m_{r}\alpha^{2}}{2\nu^{2}}
,
and define a scaled radial variable
\rho
with

\rho=\sqrt{-8\,m_{r}\,E_{\nu}}\,r=\frac{2m_{r}\alpha}{\nu}r\,.

(114)

In terms of
\rho
, the sphere boundary is at
\rho_{d}=\frac{\kappa}{\nu}
,
with
\kappa=\frac{2r_{d}}{a_{0}}
where
a_{0}=\frac{1}{m_{r}\alpha}
is the Bohr radius.
Numerically, one has
a_{0}=28.834\,200\,570(10)

(see Table
1
) and
\kappa=0.147587
.
For
\rho<\rho_{d}
, the radial equation becomes

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-\frac{L(L+1)}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{4}\right)u_{\nu L}(\rho)=0\,,

(115)

and for
\rho>\rho_{d}
it is

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-\frac{L(L+1)}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{\nu}{\rho}-\frac{1}{4}\right)u_{\nu L}(\rho)=0\,.

(116)

The solution for Eq. (
115
) that is finite as
\rho\rightarrow 0
is

u_{\nu L}(\rho)=\sqrt{\rho}\,J_{L+1/2}\left(\rho\sqrt{\frac{\nu^{2}}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{4}}\right)\,,

(117)

where
J_{\ell}(r)
is a Bessel function of the first kind,
and the solution for Eq. (
116
) that
is finite as
\rho\rightarrow\infty
is

u_{\nu L}(\rho)=W_{\nu,L+1/2}(\rho)\,,

(118)

where
W_{\nu,\ell}(r)
is a Whittaker
W
function. Continuity of the wave
function and its first derivative at
\rho=\rho_{d}
requires the continuity of
the derivative of the logarithm
\ln[u_{\nu L}(\rho)]
. Upon solving this continuity
equation for a given
L
and for
\nu\approx n
, one finds the appropriate
value of
\nu
to use in the energy
E_{\nu}=-\frac{m_{r}\alpha^{2}}{2\nu^{2}}
. The
finite-size energy correction is thus

E_{\rm FS}^{{\rm exact}}=E_{\nu}-E_{n}=-\frac{m_{r}\alpha^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\,.

(119)

For deuteronium, this expression must be doubled
since both deuteron and antideuteron have extension.

One could also approach the finite size correction perturbatively,
with a perturbing potential

\delta V(r)=\left(-\frac{\alpha}{r_{d}}+\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\,\Theta(r<r_{d})\,,

(120)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function.
Results for the perturbative shifts
E_{\rm FS}^{\rm pert}
are given in Table
5
.

We have already obtained a “finite-size” effect in our calculation of the
Breit correction, namely the
r_{d}
term in
E_{\rm BR}
. This term is

E_{\rm D}=\frac{\alpha^{4}\,m_{d}\,\tilde{r}_{d}^{2}}{6n^{3}}\delta_{L,0}

(121)

where
\tilde{r}_{d}=m_{d}r_{d}/(\hbar c)\approx 20.2248(26)
[see
Eq. (
12
)]. The Breit finite-size correction
E_{\rm D}
vanishes
for
L>0
. The calculation above shows how the extended deuteron charge
distribution affects states with
L>0
. Due to the small size of
r_{d}/a_{0}
,
which equals
0.0738
, the finite size correction decreases rapidly as
L

increases.

Numerical results for deuteronium states with
n
= 1, 2, 3, 4 are shown in
Table
5
. The numerical values obtained from the perturbative
calculation (
E_{\rm FS}^{\rm pert}
) are consistent with the exact results
from actually solving the differential equation and finding the energy
eigenvalue (
E_{\rm FS}^{\rm exact}
) for all states. The Breit contribution

E_{\rm D}
is roughly consistent with those two for
S
states, but misses the
small corrections for non-
S
states.

V.4
Deuteron Polarizability

The deuteron can be deformed by an external electric field. The extent
of this deformation is described by polarizability constants.
For the (static) scalar and tensor polarizabilities of the deuteron,
we use the calculated results of Friar and
Payne
FrPa2005deuteron
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.6330(13)\,{\rm fm}^{3}\,,

(122a)

\displaystyle\tau_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.0317(3)\,{\rm fm}^{3}\,.

(122b)

These constants enter the polarizability tensor

(\alpha_{P})^{ij}
of the deuteron,
which is expressed as

(\alpha_{P})^{ij}=\alpha_{E}\frac{\delta^{ij}}{3}+\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{d}\,\epsilon^{ijk}\frac{S^{k}}{2}+\tau_{d}\left(S^{i}S^{j}\right)^{(2)}\,.

(123)

Here,
the constants
\alpha_{E}
,
\sigma_{d}
and
\tau_{d}
parameterize the
electric scalar (
\ell=0
), vector (
\ell=1
) and tensor (
\ell=2
)
components of the deuteron’s polarizability, respectively.
These results are expressed in terms of a polarization
volume,
i.e.
, in units of fermi cubed.
Temporarily restoring SI units according to standard
procedures
Ja1998
, we indicate the conversion of the
polarization volume to the (static) SI polarizability,
which, for the scalar polarizability, reads as follows,

\alpha_{E,{\rm SI}}\,=4\pi\epsilon_{0}\,\alpha_{E,{\rm vol}}\,,

(124)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity.
For a recent overview of the use of the SI polarizability
(units are those of a dipole moment squared divided by
an energy) in a related context, see Ref.
Je2024multipole
.
The numerical values in Eq. (
122
)
thus have to be interpreted in terms of volume
polarizabilities.

Measurements of the deuteron scalar polarizability were made by two groups and
date a few decades back. The first is by Rodning
et al.
in 1982 (see
Ref.
RoEtAl1982
). They give references to six prior theoretical
calculations of the deuteron polarizability with values ranging from
0.21\,{\rm fm}^{3}
to
0.64\,{\rm fm}^{3}
, with a best estimate close to
0.60\,{\rm fm}^{3}
. They measured a value
(from now on, we assume that all polarizabilities
are indicated in the “volume” conventions)

\alpha_{E}=0.70(5)\,{\rm fm}^{3}\,,

(125)

based on elastic scattering of deuterons on lead. A second experimental value
was reported by Friar
et al.
in 1983 (see Ref.
FrEtAl1983
),
based on deuteron
photoabsorption data, a dispersion relation and other theoretical input, and a
number of corrections. Their result was

\alpha_{E}=0.64(5)\,{\rm fm}^{3}\,.

(126)

A weighted average of these two values is

\alpha_{E}=0.645(32)\,{\rm fm}^{3}\,,

(127)

which is consistent with the calculated value
of Eq. (
122a
). To the best of our knowledge,
there are currently no experimental values
available for the deuteron’s tensor polarizability.

A comprehensive derivation of the polarizability
correction of an atomic core for non-
S
states
of a bound system is presented
in Sec. 6.6 of Ref.
JeAd2022book
.
Because the focus in the current work is on
S
states,
we adopt this treatment here.
A generalization of
Eq. (6.177) of Ref.
JeAd2022book
to account for the
tensor structure of the polarizability, and also
accounting for the two polarizable
constituent particles, allows us to find the
polarizability contribution
to the interaction Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\hat{x}^{i}}{r^{2}}\left[3\alpha_{P}^{ij}(d)+3\alpha_{P}^{ij}(\bar{d})\right]\frac{\hat{x}^{j}}{r^{2}}

(128)

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\!\left\{2\alpha_{E}\delta^{ij}\!+\!3\tau_{P}\big[(S_{+}^{i}S_{+}^{j})^{(2)}\!+\!(S_{-}^{i}S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}\big]\right\}\frac{\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}}{r^{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt=-\alpha_{E}\frac{\alpha}{r^{4}}-\frac{3}{2}\tau_{P}\big[(S_{+}^{i}S_{+}^{j})^{(2)}\!+\!(S_{-}^{i}S_{-}^{j})^{(2)}\big](\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j})^{(2)}\frac{\alpha}{r^{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\equiv H_{\rm PS}+H_{\rm PT}\,.

Here,
H_{\rm P}
is the sum of scalar
(
H_{\rm PS}
) and tensor (
H_{\rm PT}
) components.
Throughout the above derivation,
we have converted the static polarizability

\alpha(\omega\!=\!0)
used in
Eq. (6.177) of Ref.
JeAd2022book

to a volume polarizability by dividing by
4\pi
.

The energy correction due to the scalar polarizability factor is

\displaystyle E_{\rm PS}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha_{E}\left\langle\frac{\alpha}{r^{4}}\right\rangle

(129)

\displaystyle\hskip-39.83368pt=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{E}E_{0}\alpha^{3}[3n^{2}-L(L+1)]}{L(L+1)(2L-1)(2L+1)(2L+3)n^{5}}\,,

where
E_{0}
is given in Table
1
,
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{E}
is the dimensionless scalar polarizability factor,

\tilde{\alpha}_{E}=m_{d}^{3}\,\alpha_{E}=\frac{(m_{d}\,c^{2})^{3}\alpha_{E}}{(\hbar c)^{3}}=543.6(1.1)\,.

(130)

We used Eq. (4.346d) of Ref.
JeAd2022book
for the expectation value of

1/r^{4}
. Results for the scalar polarizability energy corrections are
tabulated in Table
4
. Due to the large size of
\tilde{\alpha}_{E}
,
the order-
\alpha^{5}
scalar polarizability contributions are comparable in size
to the
\alpha^{4}
contributions listed in Table
4
for the states
considered.

The matrix elements of the tensor polarizability Hamiltonian involve the

D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}
angular factor found in (
32
). One has

\displaystyle(H_{\rm PT})_{nLS^{\prime}SJ}

(131)

\displaystyle\hskip-68.28644pt=-\frac{3\tilde{\tau}_{P}E_{0}\alpha^{3}D_{LS^{\prime}SJ}\,[3n^{2}-L(L+1)]}{2L(L+1)(2L-1)(2L+1)(2L+3)n^{5}}\,,

where the dimensionless tensor polarizability factor is

\tilde{\tau}_{P}=m_{d}^{3}\,\tau_{P}=\frac{(m_{d}\,c^{2})^{3}\tau_{P}}{(\hbar c)^{3}}=27.2(0.3)\,.

(132)

The tensor polarizability shifts (using the calculated value of
\tau_{P}
) are
relatively small compared to the Breit energy corrections due to the extra
factor of
\alpha
. For example, the shifts for the
4^{3}D_{J}
levels are

\displaystyle E_{\rm PT}(4^{3}D_{1})

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle 0.00860(8)\,,

(133a)

\displaystyle E_{\rm PT}(4^{3}D_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.00860(8)\,,

(133b)

\displaystyle E_{\rm PT}(4^{3}D_{3})

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle 0.00246(3)\,,

(133c)

which are small compared to the corresponding Breit shifts from
Table
3
. Contributions of this size should be noticeable in the
data given precise enough measurements of the
4F\rightarrow 4D
transition
energies.

V.5
Strong Interaction Correction

Just as in protonium
KlBaMaRi2002
, the strong interaction
modifies the deuteron-antideuteron
interaction at short distances of about 1 fm (the effective range of the strong interaction) in a way that is easily estimated.
One can assume that the probability density vanishes for distances

r\lesssim 1\,
fm due to
strong interaction mediated annihilation
(see the comprehensive discussion
in Sec. 3.12 of Ref.
KlBaMaRi2002
for protonium).
Hence, we treat the problem
as if there was a hard cutoff at
a=a_{\rm had}=1\,{\rm fm}
.
We estimate the effect based on the Deser–Trueman
formula
DeGoBaTh1954
;
BedH1955
;
By1957
;
Tr1961
;
KlBaMaRi2002
;
AdJe2025trueman
,
for which a suitable generalization to
excited states has recently been found in Ref.
AdJe2025trueman
.
We find
AdJe2025trueman
that the non-relativistic energy levels
in the cut-off potential are shifted from their pure Coulomb
values by the positive amount

E_{S}=\frac{2\alpha_{nL}\,\beta_{L}}{n^{3}}\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{2L+1}\,\alpha^{2}\,m_{r}\,,

(134)

where
a_{0}
is the deuteronium Bohr radius given in Table
1
, and

\alpha_{nL}=\prod_{s=1}^{L}\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\,,\quad\beta_{L}=\frac{2L+1}{\left[(2L+1)!!\right]^{2}}\,

(135)

with
\alpha_{n0}\equiv 1
.
Corresponding results are entered in Tables
4
and
5
.
We conservatively estimate their uncertainty
as 100% of the obtained results.
The
S
-wave scattering length generalizes the concept of the hard-sphere
radius
a_{h}
for general electromagnetically bound systems with
orbiting hadrons
DeGoBaTh1954
;
BedH1955
;
By1957
;
Tr1961
;
KlBaMaRi2002
;
AdJe2025trueman
.
Our estimate, given in Eq. (
134
),
is supported by relatively recent investigations which
indicate that the scattering length, as compared to
protonium, is a decreasing function
of the atomic weight (see the conclusions of Ref.
PrBoLRZe2000
).
The strong-interaction shifts follow the same general
pattern as a function of
L
as the finite-size corrections of the previous section,
and are even more drastically suppressed for higher angular
monenta in view of the squared double factorial
[(2L+1)!!]^{2}

in the denominator of Eq. (
134
).
Still, for typical states under investigation
here, they are larger than the finite-size corrections by an order of magnitude.

VI
Dipole Transitions

VI.1
General Properties of the Spectrum

Our investigations have revealed
that in deuteronium, a bound system with two spin-1
particles, the spin-dependent (hyper)fine structures
are composed very differently in comparison
to bound systems where the orbiting particles
have half-integer spin (
e.g.
, positronium).
However, there are also similarities:
Namely, just as in positronium, the spin-dependent
corrections in deuteronium all have the
same order-of-magnitude (
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
).
We have studied, in Sec.
IV
,
the leading radiative corrections
(one-loop and two-loop eVP) for the lowest
non-
S
levels in the bound system.
For states with the same principal
quantum number
n
, the dominant radiative correction
(one-loop eVP) to the energy
is more strongly negative (hence, more attractive)
for states low orbital angular momenta, leading to the
dominant contribution
to the Lamb shift in the bound system.

VI.2
Selection Rules

One aspect we have not treated so far in this work concerns selection rules for
dipole transitions. Here we work out the selection rules for electric dipole
transitions. The interaction operator is proportional to
\vec{r}\cdot\vec{E}
. The electric field acts to create a photon–our concern is with the
position operator
\vec{r}
, which involves the variables of the deuteronium
state. Specifically, we find that single-photon electric dipole transitions
are allowed between states with quantum numbers
n_{1},L_{1},S_{1},J_{1},J_{z1}
and

n_{2},L_{2},S_{2},J_{2},J_{z2}
(where
J_{z}
represents the magnetic quantum number of the
total angular momentum) when

\big\langle n_{2},L_{2},S_{2},J_{2},J_{z2}\big|(\vec{r}\,)_{1,m}\big|n_{1},L_{1},S_{1},J_{1},J_{z1}\big\rangle\neq 0\,.

(136)

Here,
(\vec{r}\,)_{1,m}
is the spherical tensor of rank one
(and spherical component
m=-1,0,1
)
corresponding to the position vector
\vec{r}
. It is immediately clear
from the general rules of addition of angular momentum that a state with total
angular momentum
J_{1}
and
z
component
J_{z1}
, acted on by an operator with
angular momentum one and
z
component
m
, only has overlap with a state of
total angular momentum
J_{2}=J_{1}\pm 1
or
J_{2}=J_{1}
, and
J_{z2}=J_{z1}+m
. In
addition, parity conservation implies that
(-1)^{L_{2}}=(-1)^{L_{1}+1}
.
Additional conditions can be found by looking more deeply into the condition
(
136
). The states, as given in (
3
), have the form

\psi_{nLSJM}(\vec{r}\,)=R_{nL}(r)\,\Xi^{LS}_{JM}(\theta,\varphi)

(137)

where

\Xi^{LS}_{JM}(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{M_{L},M_{S}}C^{JM}_{LM_{L};SM_{S}}Y_{LM_{L}}(\theta,\varphi)\chi_{SM_{S}}\,,

(138)

where
\chi_{SM_{S}}
is the spin-state of deuteronium, built from two spin-one
constitutents. Also, one has
(\hat{r})_{1m}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}Y_{1m}(\theta,\varphi)
. Hence, the angular part of Eq. (
136
) is proportional to

\sum_{M_{L_{1}},M_{L_{2}},M_{S_{1}},M_{S_{2}}}C^{J_{2}J_{z2}}_{L_{2}M_{L_{2}};S_{2}M_{S_{2}}}C^{J_{1}J_{z1}}_{L_{1}M_{L_{1}};S_{1}M_{S_{1}}}\\
\times\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\,Y^{*}_{L_{2}M_{L_{2}}}(\theta,\varphi)\,Y_{1m}(\theta,\varphi)\,Y_{L_{1}M_{L_{1}}}(\theta,\varphi)\\
\times\langle\chi^{\dagger}_{S_{2}M_{S_{2}}}\cdot\chi_{S_{1}M_{S_{1}}}\rangle\,.

(139)

Here, we denote the scalar product of the spin states
by a central dot (
\cdot
), for absolute clarity.
The spin inner product enforces the selection rule
S_{2}=S_{1}
.
The integral over the solid angle can be done for general
quantum numbers [see Eq. (6.64) of
JeAd2022book
] and contains the
3J
symbol

\begin{pmatrix}L_{2}&1&L_{1}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

(140)

as a factor, which vanishes unless
L_{2}=L_{1}\pm 1
.
As a result, the selection rules for single-photon electric dipole transitions are

J_{2}=J_{1}\pm 1\;{\rm or}\;J_{2}=J_{1}\,,\quad L_{2}=L_{1}\pm 1\,,\quad S_{2}=S_{1}\,.

(141)

3D
–
3P
and
4F
–
4D
transitions are displayed graphically in
Figs.

. Transition energies and wavelengths are shown
in Tables

and

VII
Conclusions

The bound system of a deuteron and its
antiparticle offers some unique
elements in comparison with other two-body
bound states where the partners have
equal mass. In comparison to positronium

BePe1980
;
Ka2004ijmpa
;
AdCaPR2022
,
deuteronium offers drastically enhanced
characteristic field strengths,
which allow the study of QED effects
under extreme conditions.
In comparison to protonium
Ba1989
;
KlBaMaRi2002
,
deuteronium offers the added complexity of
the spin-1 character of the bound particles,
which opens the door for an investigation
of the much more complex QED of
spin-1 particles as compared to
the well-understood spin-
1/2
case.
In comparison to dimuonium (or, true muonium,
see Refs.
JeSoIvKa1997
;
KaIvJeSo1998
;
KaJeIvSo1998
;
BrLe2009
),
deuteronium offers the enhanced stability
of the deuteron, which does not
decay on a scale of microseconds (like the
muon and antimuon).
Hence, the deuteronium bound system
offers unique characteristics
for a detailed study of QED effects
of spin-1 particles in combination
with highly nontrivial spin-induced relativistic
effects and radiative (vacuum polarization)
corrections.

Here, we have evaluated the dominant features of
the spectrum of deuteronium.
The main terms are the nonrelativistic Schrödinger–Coulomb energy
(order
\alpha^{2}m_{d}
) and the one-loop eVP correction
(order
\alpha^{3}m_{d}
).
The dominant spin dependence is contained in the
Breit corrections (order
\alpha^{4}m_{d}
, see Sec.
III
).
The muonic VP is enhanced in deuteronium (see Sec.
IV.2
),
and reducible as well as irreducible eVP corrections
are well under control (see Secs.
IV.3
—
IV.5
).
In Sec.
V
, we have demonstrated that strong-interaction and
internal-structure effects do not represent obstacles
to a detailed study of the very interesting
spin-induced relativistic and radiative effects.
Namely, for non-
S
states, the hadronic VP
effect are numerically small (see Secs.
V.1

and
V.2
). Finite-size effects
can be treated perturbatively and are
likewise numerically small for states with
L\geq 2
(see
Sec.
V.3
). Strong-interaction corrections can be
obtained on the basis of the generalized Deser-Trueman
formula
DeGoBaTh1954
;
BedH1955
;
By1957
;
Tr1961
;
KlBaMaRi2002
;
AdJe2025trueman
.

Certain Lamb shift
transitions in deuteronium are in the optical or infrared range
and are easily accessible by laser spectroscopy.
Two very attractive transitions appears to
be the
3D
–
3P
and
4F
–
4D
Lamb shift transitions,
which are analyzed (with their dipole-allowed
(hyper)fine-structure components in Figs.
2
and
3
.
(See also Tables
6
and
7
.)
An additional important conclusion of our
investigations is that internal-structure
corrections, for the mentioned transitions,
are numerically suppressed and do not
preclude precision experiments on the bound system
of a deuteron and its antiparticle.

JeAd2022book
