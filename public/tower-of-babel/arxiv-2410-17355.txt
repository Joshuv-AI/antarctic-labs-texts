All Entities are Not Created Equal: Examining the Long Tail for Fine-Grained Entity Typing
Advait Deshmukh Ashwin Umadi Dananjay Srinivas Maria Leonor Pacheco University of Colorado Boulder {advait.deshmukh, ashwin.umadi, dananjay.srinivas}@colorado.edu

Abstract
Pre-trained language models (PLMs) are trained on large amounts of data, which helps capture world knowledge alongside linguistic competence. Due to this, they are extensively used for ultra-fine entity typing tasks, where they provide the entity knowledge held in its parameter space. Given that PLMs learn from co-occurrence patterns, they likely contain more knowledge or less knowledge about entities depending on their how frequent they are in the pre-training data. In this work, we probe PLMs to elicit encoded entity probabilities and demonstrate that they highly correlate with their frequency in large-scale internet data. Then, we demonstrate that entity-typing approaches that rely on PLMs struggle with entities at the long tail on the distribution. Our findings suggests that we need to go beyond PLMs to produce solutions that perform well for rare, new or infrequent entities.

Introduction

Ultra-fine entity typing (UFET) is the task of inferring the type of an entity with high specificity
Choi et al. 2018
.
For example, in the sentence “
Barrack Obama
attended Bidens’s presidential inauguration on Wednesday.”, the entity “
Barrack Obama
” would have a coarse entity-type of
‘‘person’’
, but a more specific, ultra-fine entity type could be
‘‘politician’’
,
‘‘democrat’’
or
‘‘ex-president’’
. Prior work on UFET has heavily relied on pre-trained language models (PLMs) to elicit entity types
Li et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2021
;
Pan et al. 2022
.
When using PLMs for UFET, approaches often position the task close to PLM learning objectives such as masked language modeling.
For example,
Pan et al. 2022
appends an entity-mention and mask to a sentence, allowing BERT to fill in the mask to elicit a type.
Such approaches are attractive as they benefit from the large amount of training data the PLM has seen to make common associations, thus helping them determine the most likely type of an entity based on the context provided. Relatedly, other studies have explored how language models trained on extremely large datasets capture world knowledge that can aid in related tasks such as relation extraction and entity linking
Petroni et al. 2019
;
Schouten et al. 2022
.

Albeit successful, PLMs are limited by the data that they have been exposed to and their reliability can be affected when working with infrequently used language constructions such as rarely/never seen entities. Since PLMs rarely encounter these entities, it has fewer opportunities to capture knowledge about them as compared to frequent entities.
Past work has mostly overlooked this issue, assuming that the embedding space will be enough to capture similarities between rare and less-rare entities, and generalize across them. The studies that have looked into infrequent entities usually characterize them in terms of the task-specific training data
Schouten et al. 2022
, rather than the data used to pre-train PLMs.

In this paper, we investigate the extent to which PLM-based methods struggle when determining the ultra-fine grained type of entities that occur infrequently in their pre-training data.
This is a challenging endeavour, as the data used for pre-training PLMs is generally unavailable. In order to do this, we first develop a proxy to determine the frequency of an entity by following the conventional wisdom that modern PLMs have been trained on “all of the internet”. Namely, we estimate the frequency of an entity by querying Google to estimate the number of documents containing that entity. Then, we contrast this number with the probability of eliciting that entity from a comprehensive set of PLMs in numerous contexts, and show that our probability estimates highly correlate with the entity prevalence in internet searches.

Finally, we divide entities into bins based on our resulting frequency estimates and report the performance of various published approaches for UFET across different frequency bins, most of which build on PLMs and other co-occurrence embedding methods
Choi et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Sevgili et al. 2024
. Our results show that all of these competitive solutions struggle at the long tail of the pre-training entity distribution, suggesting that we need to go beyond PLMs to produce solutions that perform well for rare, new or infrequent entities.

Related Work

In this section, we cover the related work across three main areas: fine-grained entity typing, PLM solutions for the task of fine-grained entity typing, and entity knowledge representations in PLMs.

Ultra-fine entity typing

Ultra-fine entity typing (UFET) was proposed in
Choi et al. 2018
to generate free-form noun phrases that aptly describes the type of a target entity mention.
UFET has found several downstream applications such as co-reference resolution
Durrett and Klein 2014
, entity linking
Onoe and Durrett 2020
and relation extraction
Yaghoobzadeh et al. 2017
.

PLM solutions for UFET

PLMs such as BERT
Devlin et al. 2019
and LLaMA
Touvron et al. 2023
were trained on large amounts of data via self-supervised objectives, and have been shown to capture world knowledge in their parameter space
Roberts et al. 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2020
. This ability has allowed PLMs to perform well on ‘fill-in-the-blank’ style problems, where the goal is to elicit an answer to a query based on the context provided (i.e., “Dante was born in
[MASK]
in the year 1265.”)
Petroni et al. 2019
. Many UFET approaches have capitalized on this ability;
Dai et al. 2021
looked at introducing Hearst patterns with
[MASK]
tokens to describe entity-types that can be infilled by BERT,
Li et al. 2022
looked at using the language inference capabilities of BERT to determine the ultra-fine type of entities, and
Pan et al. 2022
forced BERT to fill-in an ultra-fine type by appending the entity mention and a
[MASK]
token to a sentence.

While these methods report some of the best metrics for UFET, they do not explore how their approaches fare on infrequent, rare entities that PLMs may not have been sufficiently exposed to. In this work, we explore the effect of entity representation strength on a variety of PLMs, in order to determine if rarer entities cause significant issues for PLM-based approaches.

Knowledge about entities in PLMs

Prior studies have investigated the entity knowledge captured by LLMs. For example,
Petroni et al. 2019
showed that BERT could be used to answer open-ended questions and extract relations between entities without fine-tuning. Similarly,
Schouten et al. 2022
explored entity representations encoded in BERT via two mechanisms: 1) comparing performance on an entity-linking task with and without fine-tuning, and 2) enhancing BERT with external knowledge about entities. They found that while fine-tuning does lead to a 6% improvement in performance, additional knowledge is not beneficial.

In this work, we are interested in how the entity knowledge encoded in PLMs affects entity-typing performance, particularly for rarely occurring entities. While prior work has explored rare entities by looking at specific entity-typing training data
Schouten et al. 2022
, we focus on entity frequency in the large corpora on which LLMs are pre-trained, and how that is implicitly encoded in their parameter space.

3
Experiment Design

In this section we outline the two broad experiments we conduct.
First, we look at whether parametric representations match real world entity frequency by comparing the likelihood of a PLM suggesting a target entity against how prominently the term is indexed by a Search Engine API.
Second, we select four models to do ultra-fine entity typing, and measure typing performance across entity groups of varying frequency.

3.1
Establishing the Long Tail

If an entity lies on the long tail of the distribution, it follows that its frequency would follow a similar pattern across most large-scale corpora. Assuming that internet is a fairly balanced representation of a real world distribution, we use internet search hits as a measure of entity frequency. We quantify the occurrence in-the-wild of each entity in our dataset by conducting strict searches using the Google Custom Search API.

As most datasets used to pre-train modern PLMs come from a subset of internet, we hypothesize that such “Long Tail” entities would be underrepresented in the parameter space of these models as well. Following our hypothesis -models learn what they see-, we evaluate how well entities are represented within the PLM parameter space. We do this by querying the models and assess whether an entity is considered rare based on its ability to be predicted or reconstructed accurately. Finally, we measure the correlation between internet frequencies and the model-estimated probabilities.

To perform model estimations reliably, we must balance different considerations. First, given that PLMs learn word representations from in-context examples, we need a representative set of in-context examples for each of the entities that we want to estimate. To do this, we prompt
LLaMA-8B-Instruct
to generate 10 different sentences that include the target entity. Second, we need to consider the training objective the PLM when querying it for the probability of a given entity in a given context. We consider two types of PLMs according to their learning objectives: masked language models (MLMs) and causal language models (Causal LMs). Below, we outline the strategy to elicit probabilities for each type of model.

MLMs

To recover entity probabilities for MLMs, we first replace the entity tokens with
[MASK]
tokens. Then, we generate a probability distribution over all candidate tokens. We use the probability that the model assigns to the tokens of the target entity to represent its salience in the model’s parametric knowledge. We use this process for both discriminative and generative MLMs.

Causal LMs

To deal with Causal LMs, we reframe the task as a
Fill-in-the-Blank
problem. We provide the model with a prompt (see App
A
) and compute the probabilities through a conditional generation process.

In both cases, we use the probability that the model assigns to the token of the target entity to represent its salience in the model’s parametric knowledge. Since the sentences provide context, we should expect that models would always predict a high probability for the target entity.
However, if some target entities are scored with higher probability than others, we can infer that the model has more knowledge about some entities over others. More details about the probability estimation process can be found in App.
B
.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we explored how a PLM’s encoded probabilities of entities are highly correlated with the frequency with which the entity occurs in The Internet.
Additionally, we also measured the performance of four published UFET models that build on PLMs or similar distributions, on entities with different frequencies and probabilities in large datasets, and found that all models performed better on frequent/most-probable entities as opposed to rarer/less-probable entities.

This study shows that PLMs are limited when it comes to knowledge about rarely seen or unseen entities. The next step for this line of research is to explore strategies to inject knowledge about rare entities using external resources to obtain more robust language models that can generalize better.

Limitations

Our paper has two main limitations:

(1) While using internet data -specifically search API results as a proxy for ground truth measure for entity frequency- is convenient, it is not an exact representation of the dataset that each of the discussed models (BERT/BART/LLAMA) were trained on. A closer analysis of their specific datasets (when available) against their predictions would be a fair comparison to evaluate how well the training techniques/objectives translate to the proxy that we define. Nevertheless, the study we presented provides an alternative to analyze models that do not make their pre-training data publicly accessible.

(2) Using the same family of models (LLAMA) for both paraphrasing and estimating entity frequency might also introduce bias in the evaluation pipeline. This could be mitigated by using a paraphrasing model that is unrelated to the models being evaluated.

Appendix B
Method for Generating Masks and Calculating Entity Probability

An entity in this context can be comprised of a single token or a multi-token phrase.
For multi-token entities we employ a conditional generation approach where we generate the entity sequentially, one token at a time, moving from left to right. The probability of the entire entity is determined by product of the conditional probability of each token
t_{i}
conditioned on all preceding tokens and the surrounding context
C
, where
i
represents the position of the token in the sequence and
n
is the total number of tokens comprising the entity.

P(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}\mid C)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P\left(t_{i}\,\bigg|\,t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{i-1},C\right)

(1)

We use this approach to benchmark three models with comparable sizes and distinct pre-training objectives.

BERT (MLM):

To recover an entity using BERT, we first replace the entity with an equal number of
[MASK]
tokens. Then we calculate the probability the entity being recovered as described before, replacing one
[MASK]
per iteration.

BART (Seq2Seq):

For BART
Lewis et al. 2020
, we take a similar approach but with only a single
(mask)
token. We progressively expand the
(mask)
one token at a time, calculating the probability for each subsequent token until the entire entity is recovered.

LLAMA (Causal LM):

Since LLAMA
Dubey et al. 2024
is not pre-trained with a MLM objective, we reframe the task as a
Fill-in-the-Blank
problem using
Instruct-LLAMA-1B
. We provide the model with a prompt(provided in the appendix) and compute the probabilities through a conditional generation process, as previously described.
