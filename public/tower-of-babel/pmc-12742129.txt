Development of a clinical prediction model for falls in individuals with COPD
Authors: Khang Trong Nguyen, Dina Brooks, Luciana G Macedo, Cindy Ellerton, Roger S Goldstein, Jennifer Alison, Gail Dechman, Samantha Harrison, Anne E Holland, Annemarie Lee, Alda Marques, Lissa Spencer, Michael K Stickland, Elizabeth H Skinner, Kimberley Joy Haines, Marla K Beauchamp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is linked to an increased risk of falls, however, there is no accurate method for predicting falls in this population. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a clinical prediction model for falls in individuals with COPD.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted using data from a recent fall prevention trial. Participants with COPD who reported a 12-month history of falls, concerns with balance or recent near falls were tracked for falls over 12 months prospectively. Baseline data included demographics and measures of balance, mobility and health status. A predictive model was developed using backward-selected multivariate logistic regression with fall status (no falls versus ≥1 fall) as the dependent variable and 17 baseline candidate predictors as independent variables. Using the bootstrap resampling method for internal validation, model performance was assessed for discrimination by the concordance (c) statistic and calibration by the expected to observed (E:O) ratio, calibration in the large (CITL) and calibration slope. The final model was adjusted for optimism using the bootstrap shrinkage factor.ResultsOf 178 participants (mean age 73±9 years; 83 females), 74 (42%) reported ≥1 fall over 12 months, totalling 188 falls. The predictive model identified three factors associated with 12-month future falls: reporting a 12-month history of ≥2 falls (OR=3.59, CI (1.65 to 7.82)), more chronic conditions (OR=1.14, CI (1.01 to 1.28)) and worse Timed Up and Go Dual-Task test scores (OR=1.04, CI (1.00 to 1.09)). The final prediction model achieved acceptable discrimination (c-statistic=0.69, CI (0.61 to 0.78)) and calibration (E:O ratio=1.01, CITL=−0.01 and calibration slope=0.93).ConclusionsA history of ≥2 falls, having more chronic conditions and impaired mobility under cognitive demand predicts future falls in individuals with COPD. The prediction model showed acceptable internal validation. External validation is needed to confirm these findings.Trial registration numberNCT02995681; clinicaltrials.gov.Keywords: COPD, Physical Examination

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide.1 2 Accidental falls, another leading cause of disability,1 emerges as a significant concern in individuals with COPD.3 A fall can lead to a series of negative consequences like hospitalisation, loss of independence and decreased health-related quality of life.4 5 Across 13 chronic conditions, COPD was the strongest predictor for falls in a population-based study of 16 000 older adults.3 In a systematic review of 23 studies, the pooled 1-year fall prevalence in individuals with COPD was 30% (range 5%–51%).6 Falls may occur due to various factors, but COPD-specific predictors must be considered to inform prevention.

The identification of fall predictors among individuals with COPD may direct targeted fall prevention interventions to reduce the likelihood of falls. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 cohort studies, fall risk factors in this population include older age, female sex, 1-year fall history, smoking history, polypharmacy, total comorbidities, use of supplemental oxygen and impaired balance performance.6 Although these factors are associated with an increased risk of falls, there are currently no validated prediction models that accurately predict falls in individuals with COPD.

A prediction model is a statistical equation that combines multiple predictors to identify the risk of an outcome prospectively, such as falls.7 Several prediction models have been previously developed and validated for falls among the general older adult population.7 However, given the increased likelihood of falls in individuals with COPD,3 there is a surprising lack of developed and validated prediction models for falls, prompting a timely need for research in this area. Therefore, our aim was to develop and internally validate a clinical prediction model for predicting future falls in a cohort of individuals with COPD.

Methods

Study design

This study is a secondary analysis of an international multicentre randomised controlled trial (RCT) of fall prevention in COPD in Canada, the United Kingdom, Portugal and Australia (clinicaltrials.gov: NCT02995681). Ethics approval was obtained from each site-specific research authority (online supplemental materials).8 Study details for the present analysis were reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.9

Patient and public involvement

This study did not include patient and public involvement in the research process.

Participants

Members of the RCT research team recruited individuals on enrolment into outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programmes from nine sites.8 Control participants received 8–12 weeks of PR and intervention participants received PR plus balance training.8 Full trial details are reported elsewhere.8 Participants were included if they had: (1) a medical diagnosis of COPD according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines,10 (2) a 24-month history of ≥1 fall, self-reported balance concerns or a recent near fall, and (3) were able to provide written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they: (1) were unable to communicate in English (or Portuguese if recruited in Portugal) or (2) presented with a significant hearing or cognitive impairment and/or a condition that limited their ability to safely participate in assessments of balance and mobility (eg, dementia, Parkinson’s Disease, cerebrovascular accident, or other neurological conditions).

Primary outcome

Falls were tracked in accordance with the Prevention of Falls Network Europe recommendations, using the following definition of a fall: ‘an unexpected event in which the participants come to rest on the ground, floor or lower level’.11 Falls were tracked prospectively on a monthly basis for 12 months using participant-reported monthly fall calendars, which were returned at the end of each month using prepaid envelopes. If a participant failed to send their monthly fall calendar, a research assistant called the participant to collect the information. Fall status for this study was considered as yes (≥1 fall) or no (zero falls) at the end of the 12 months of follow-up.

Candidate predictors

Candidate predictors for this analysis included baseline descriptive measures (n=12), measures of balance and mobility (n=3) and self-reported health status (n=2) for a total of 17 candidate predictors, table 1. Balance and mobility measures were selected if there was evidence of an association with falls in individuals with COPD or the general older adult population in previous literature and/or could feasibly be used to screen for fall risk in primary care settings as defined by an administration time of <10 min.

Descriptive measures

Descriptive measures included the following: age, sex, body mass index, fall history, self-reported balance concerns, smoking history, use of supplemental oxygen, use of a gait aid, total chronic conditions, total medications, pulmonary function tests (forced expiratory volume in 1 s per cent (FEV1%) predicted, forced vital capacity per cent (FVC%) predicted and FEV1/FVC ratio), COPD severity as per the GOLD spirometric classification,10 exercise tolerance as measured by the 6 min walk test (6MWT)12 and severity of breathlessness/dyspnoea as measured by the Baseline Dyspnea Index (BDI).13

For fall history, we considered ≥1 fall and ≥2 falls in the previous 12 months. The total number of chronic conditions was extracted from participants’ clinical records and encompassed cardiac, respiratory (including COPD), rheumatologic, musculoskeletal, vascular, oncologic, gastrointestinal, neurological and psychological disorders. Total medications included a broad range of medications, including respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, gastrointestinal, neuropsychiatric and analgesic agents. Specific drug classes were not analysed; instead, the total number of medications was used to represent overall medication burden and potential impact of polypharmacy. COPD severity was classified using the GOLD spirometric classification based on FEV1% predicted scores: mild ≥80%, moderate=50 to <80%, severe=30 to <50% and very severe ≤30%.10 The 6MWT is a functional exercise tolerance test measuring the total distance (m) walked in 6 min with longer distances indicating greater exercise tolerance.12 The BDI is an interviewer-administered questionnaire rating dyspnoea severity across three categories (functional impairment, magnitude of task and magnitude of effort) with total scores ranging from 0 to 12 (higher scores indicating more severe dyspnoea).13

Balance and mobility

The Single Leg Stance (SLS) test is a timed performance-based test to assess static balance while standing on one leg and is scored as the duration the participant could maintain this position, with longer durations indicating better static balance (60 s maximum).14 The SLS test has shown excellent test-retest reliability (ICC1,2=0.91, CI (0.87, 0.94)),15 and has evidence of borderline acceptable accuracy for screening for previous falls (area under the curve of 0.68, CI (0.57 to 0.78)) in people with COPD.16

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test is a timed performance-based test to assess mobility and is scored as the total time to go from a seated position in a chair, stand up, walk 3 m away at a comfortable pace, turn around, walk back and sit down (shorter completion times indicate better mobility).17 The TUG test has demonstrated excellent inter-rater (ICC2,1=0.99, CI (0.99 to 0.99)) and intrarater reliability (ICC2,1=0.92, CI (0.86 to 0.96))18 and predictive validity for 6-month future falls in people with COPD.19

The TUG Dual-Task (TUG-DT) test is a timed performance-based test to assess mobility under cognitive demand.17 For this test, participants are asked to complete the previously mentioned TUG test sequence while counting backward by threes starting from 100, with lower completion times indicating better mobility.17 TUG-DT test scores have shown excellent inter-rater (ICC2,1=0.94)20 and intrarater reliability (ICC3,3=0.99)21 among community-dwelling older adults.22 The TUG-DT test lacks previous evidence for an association with falls in COPD; however, given the potential feasibility of incorporating a concurrent cognitive task with the established TUG test, we included the TUG-DT test.

Health status

The European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level (EQ-5D-5L) is a self-report health status measure addressing five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and discomfort, and anxiety and depression.23 Based on the previous literature and to reduce redundancy in this analysis, we only included the dimensions for pain and discomfort,24 and anxiety and depression.25 Each dimension is scored from 0 to 5, with 0 indicating no problems, and 5 indicating extreme problems.23 For the purposes of this analysis, each dimension was further dichotomised into no problems (score of 0) versus any problems (score of ≥1). Good to excellent test–retest reliability has been demonstrated for the EQ-5D-5L dimensions for pain and discomfort (ICC2,1=0.64 to 0.74), anxiety and depression (ICC2,1=0.67 to 0.76) in general populations,26 and has also been validated in people with COPD.27

Missing data and sensitivity analysis

As recommended, missing data of baseline measures were addressed by multiple imputation if the missing data were deemed either missing completely at random (administrative error) or missing partially at random (due to known participant characteristics) and was not imputed if the data was missing not at random (due to the primary outcome).28 Since our primary outcome (future falls) could not directly affect baseline data collection, all missing baseline data met the missingness criteria. Multiple imputation was conducted by chained equations with the dependent variable being the variables with missing data and the independent variables being fall status, age, fall history and total number of chronic conditions were used to create 20 imputed data sets.28 The types of regression equations corresponded with the type of data imputed for the variable with missing data: logistic regression for binomial variables, Poisson regression for count variables and predictive mean matching for continuous variables.29 The primary outcome (fall status) was included as an independent variable for multiple imputation, as recommended.30 Additional independent variables for multiple imputation included age, 12-month fall history and total chronic conditions, selected based on their complete data and prior evidence of association with falls in individuals with COPD.6

Statistical analysis

Data were summarised as means and SD for continuous data, median and IQR for skewed data, and counts and percentages for categorical data. The unadjusted prediction model was developed using backward-selected multivariate logistic regression (cut-off set at p>0.05),31 32 with fall status at 12 months (no falls versus ≥1 fall) as the dependent variable and 17 baseline measures as the candidate predictors (table 1).

Model internal validation was measured using the bootstrap resampling method for 500 randomly resampled data sets for each of the 20 imputed data sets.28 Bootstrap resampling randomly resamples the data based on the observed range of data points for each variable in the original sample and is recommended as a method for evaluating internal validation of clinical prediction models.9 28 Model performance was evaluated by model discrimination (how accurate the prediction model is at identifying those with, vs without, the primary outcome) and model calibration (the level of agreement and consistency between the predicted and observed outcomes).28 Model discrimination was assessed using the concordance (c) statistic with scores ranging from 0.5 to 1 (higher values indicate better discrimination).28 33 Model calibration was assessed using the expected to observed (E:O) ratio (ideal value of 1), calibration-in-the-large (CITL) (ideal value of 0) and calibration slope (ideal value of 1; acceptable range of 0.8–1.0).28 33 Internal validation was conducted separately within each of the 20 imputed datasets, and model performance results (discrimination and calibration) were combined using Rubin’s rules to generate pooled estimates that account for both within-imputation and between-imputation variability.28 34 A calibration plot was generated to visually inspect model calibration by examining how closely the observed outcomes aligned with the reference line of predicted values.28 33

The final predictive model was reported with beta (β) coefficients and ORs with 95% CIs. To address overfitting, the final predictive model was adjusted for optimism by multiplying the β coefficients of each predictor by the bootstrap shrinkage factor (equal to the calibration slope).28 33 The prediction model can be interpreted as a predicted probability, P (X), ranging from 0 to 1, for a future fall in individuals with COPD using the inverse logit equation,

Sample size

Since this is a secondary analysis of a fall prevention RCT, the study sample size reflects the number of participants with completed baseline and follow-up data.8 Although the sample size for this study was not determined a priori, we have included a post hoc sample size calculation in online supplemental materials for reporting purposes. Based on the results of our analysis, the projected sample size needed was n=1,333.35

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 178 participants were included for this analysis (mean age 73±9 years; 83 females) (table 2). At baseline, 87% of participants had self-reported balance concerns, 53% reported a 12-month history of ≥1 fall, and 21% reported a 12-month history of ≥2 falls. Approximately 55% of participants had severe to very severe COPD, a median of 5 (3,7) chronic conditions and a median of 7 (5,9) prescribed medications at baseline. Overall, 74 (42%) participants reported ≥1 fall and 42 (24%) reported ≥2 falls (188 total falls) over 12 months.

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed that reporting two or more falls in the previous 12 months, a higher number of chronic conditions and worse mobility under cognitive demand increased the likelihood of 12-month future falls in individuals with COPD. Our prediction model demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration within our sample; however, given the modest sample size, its accuracy should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed through external validation in similar COPD populations. We have recently conducted one such validation study that supports the current findings;36 however, further studies in larger and more representative cohorts are warranted.

Our prediction model is the first to be internally validated in individuals with COPD, in contrast to the only other prediction model developed for falls in this population.37 Roig and colleagues proposed a prediction model for 6-month future falls (n=101 patients with COPD), which featured 6-month history of one or more falls, age, sex and diagnosis of coronary heart disease as significant predictors.37 Our contrasting findings in significant predictors may be attributable to a few notable differences. First, Roig and colleagues reported a shorter time frame of longitudinal follow-up.37 Longer term follow-up across 12 months captures falls across a wide range of environmental and seasonal contexts that better represent an individual’s experience with falls over time. Additionally, fall prevention guidelines in older adults recommend inquiring about 12-month fall history, which may suggest that a timeframe of 6 months may not optimally capture clinically relevant fall history.11 38 Finally, our study had a larger sample size, lending to a more robust prediction model, which improves model performance for application to external samples in individuals with COPD.28 Regardless of these differences, fall prediction model development and validation is relatively new and under developed in COPD, making model comparisons in this specific clinical population challenging.

Although studies developing and validating clinical prediction models for falls are quite common in the general older adult population, a surprising proportion of the studies available fail to meet TRIPOD guidelines, demonstrating a high risk of bias.7 In a systematic review, among 72 fall prediction models across 30 studies in community-dwelling older adults, all 72 models were deemed at high risk of bias, primarily due to poor management of missing data and lack of internal validation.7 By adhering to the TRIPOD guidelines, future studies for predicting falls in COPD can sufficiently compare and contrast prediction model performance to inform clinical utility.

Model discrimination reflects the ability of a prediction model to accurately predict the occurrence of an event (eg, falls) over time.28 When looking at previous fall prediction model discrimination in older adults, C-statistic values ranged from 0.49 to 0.87 for non-validated models and 0.62 to 0.69 for validated models.7 Clinical prediction model discrimination is an integral performance measure for clinical utility, with some literature suggesting a C-statistic value of ≥0.70 as acceptable while others suggest ≥0.80.39,41 However, these qualitative labels for C-statistic values are arbitrary and cannot solely determine model performance.28 42 It is recommended to consider both discrimination and calibration when assessing and comparing the performance of prediction models.28 41 42

Model calibration reflects the consistency and agreement of observed versus predicted outcomes.28 In a recently published longitudinal cohort study including 5722 older adults living in Europe, fall prediction model performance achieved an average C-statistic of 0.65 with CITL and calibration slopes ranging from −0.02 to 0.02 and 0.67 to 1.12, respectively.43 Despite our limited sample size, our model demonstrated discrimination and calibration values consistent with those reported in larger studies of older adult populations, underscoring the strength and stability of our findings. Although our model has already been externally validated in a preliminary sample of COPD,36 updating this model in larger, nationally representative COPD cohorts or registry-based datasets is warranted to enhance its generalisability and predictive precision.

Of note, our sample included a highly selected group of individuals with COPD that presented with a 24-month history of ≥1 fall, self-reported balance concerns and/or a recent near fall, which may have impacted the generalisability of our findings. However, given that the rate of falls prospectively in our findings is in line with the pooled prevalence of 30% (CI (19 to 42)) previously reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis across 23 studies in people with COPD,6 our sample was considered representative of the fall risk associated with the general COPD population.

Fall history, typically considered as ≥1 fall in the previous 12 months, has consistently been identified as a fall risk factor in individuals with COPD6 and in older adults.44,46 Our prediction model revealed that ≥2 falls in the previous 12 months was able to significantly predict future falls in individuals with COPD. Similarly, the previously mentioned European longitudinal cohort study in older adults developed and validated a fall prediction model that included ≥2 falls in the previous 12 months as a significant predictor of future falls (OR=1.81 CI (1.48 to 2.19)).43 Additionally, the World Guidelines for Falls Prevention and Management in Older Adults recommends classifying individuals as high fall risk if ≥2 falls are reported in the past 12 months.38 Clinicians should strongly consider inquiring about fall history, particularly a 12-month history of ≥2 falls as a component of fall risk screening for patients with COPD.

In our model, a higher number of total chronic conditions significantly increased the likelihood of a fall. This was expected since previous studies have identified that a higher number of comorbidities is significantly correlated with 6–12 month future falls in individuals with COPD.37 47 Additionally, among a population-based sample of the 16 357 community-dwelling older adults, a higher number of chronic conditions has been shown to significantly increase the likelihood of falls.3 Frailty, which affects nearly half of individuals with COPD (estimated prevalence 47%, IQR 39.3%–66.3%), is a syndrome in which multiple factors reduce physiological capacity and increase vulnerability to adverse health outcomes following even minor stressors.48 As a known risk factor for falls in older adults,49 50 frailty may contribute to the relationship between multimorbidity and fall risk. Although not measured in our study, frailty should be considered in future model updates. Overall, our findings highlight the importance of accounting for total chronic disease burden when assessing fall risk in COPD.

Our prediction model highlights a 4% increased likelihood of falls for every second taken to complete the TUG-DT test. This comes as no surprise since balance impairment is an established risk factor for falls in individuals with COPD.6 51 52 In a previous comparative study, 35 individuals with COPD observed more pronounced impairments to cognitive performance than impairments to motor performance during the TUG-DT test when compared with 27 healthy controls.53 This may suggest that dual-tasking results in the prioritisation of addressing motor over cognitive demands, which may lead to poor decision-making when navigating riskier activities and/or environments. Moreover, in a cross-sectional study among 382 community-dwelling older adults with COPD, poor cognitive function was associated with worse standing balance performance.54 The authors of the cross-sectional study suggested that cognitive impairment in COPD may be due to persistent hypoxemia, resulting in ischaemic lesions within neuronal structures, but did not comment on how this may have directly contributed to poorer balance performance.54 More research is needed to further understand the role of cognitive impairment in falls in this population.

Our study had a few notable strengths, particularly in our methodological rigour. First, our sample included participants across Canada, the United Kingdom, Portugal and Australia, increasing the generalisability of our findings. Second, our study followed the TRIPOD guidelines,9 which included addressing missing data using multiple imputation and internally validating our prediction model. Third, our study complies with the recommendations set in the Prevention of Falls Network Europe consensus statement through our monthly fall tracking and 12-month follow-up term.11 Finally, our model includes predictors that may be feasible for assessment in rehabilitation and other clinical settings.

Our findings must be considered in light of some limitations. First, this was a highly selective sample of individuals with COPD recruited for a fall prevention trial, with a majority of participants reporting a history of ≥1 fall in the previous 12 months. However, since the rate of falls in this study aligns with previous studies in COPD,6 this likely had little impact on the generalisability of our findings. Second, since this study was a secondary analysis, the sample size was limited, which likely resulted in mild overfitting. However, since our prediction model achieved ideal/acceptable calibration, we are confident in the stability of our findings. Third, our C-statistic fell below 0.70 after internal validation, which is sometimes considered insufficient for predictive validity.39 40 However, when compared with previous fall prediction models in the general older adult population, our model performed similarly or better.7 43 Fourth, we addressed missing data for nine candidate predictors using multiple imputation, all of which had <5% missing data with the exception of the SLS test. After sensitivity analysis, our predictors were significant in both the non-imputed and imputed prediction models, suggesting a robust relationship regardless of missing data. Finally, we chose not to control for trial group allocation, which, after sensitivity analysis revealed no significant association with 12-month future fall status.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study showed that reporting two or more falls in the previous 12 months, having a higher number of chronic conditions and longer TUG-DT test completion times increased the likelihood of future falls over 12 months in people with COPD. Our prediction model demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration, identifying factors associated with fall risk in individuals with COPD. While these findings are encouraging and have been preliminarily confirmed in our external validation study,36 further validation in larger, independent COPD cohorts is needed to refine model performance.
