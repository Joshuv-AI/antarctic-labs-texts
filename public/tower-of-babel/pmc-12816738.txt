Patient satisfaction and disease-related knowledge in otorhinolaryngology outpatient care in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Alya AlZabin, Alanoud AlOmair, Ebtesam Almajed, Abdulrahman AlHumaizi, Reem AlJurayyed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatient satisfaction and knowledge are recognized indicators of healthcare quality in specialized services such as otorhinolaryngology (ORL). This study aimed to assess patient satisfaction and knowledge to identify opportunities for improvement in a Saudi outpatient ORL setting. A cross-sectional study was carried out from July 2024 to January 2025 involving 180 adult patients at the outpatient ORL clinic of King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz University Hospital. A validated self-administered electronic questionnaire evaluated sociodemographic data, satisfaction across five domains, and disease-related knowledge. Satisfaction was measured with a 7-point Likert scale. The statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and comparative tests. The majority of participants were Saudi (176, 97.8%) and female (135, 75%), with a mean age of 33.5 years (SD = 12.5). Overall satisfaction was high (mean = 6.14 ± 1.12), with the highest scores in Staff Quality (6.31) and Communication (6.17), and the lowest in Access and Efficiency (5.79). Strong positive correlations were observed among satisfaction domains, particularly between Overall Experience and Staff Quality (r = 0.893, p < 0.001). Disease-related knowledge was also high (mean = 81.93%), particularly regarding medication administration (95.71%) and diagnosis awareness (93.29%). However, knowledge gaps were noted in the understanding of medication side effects (62.75%) and surgical complications (74.31%). Surgical patients reported significantly higher satisfaction (mean difference = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.02–0.68, p = 0.039). Patients expressed high satisfaction with ORL care, particularly in terms of staff quality and communication. Nonetheless, access and patient education on treatment risks require attention. Introduction

Healthcare quality is increasingly driven by patient-centered care. When considering quality, the Institute of Medicine recommends that the patient’s experience be the basis for defining it1. Healthcare management relies heavily on patient satisfaction as an indicator of high-quality care2. The more satisfied a patient is, the more likely they are to maintain an ongoing relationship with an individual provider and to adhere to specific treatments and regimens3. To raise the quality of healthcare in a country, it is essential to continuously evaluate patients’ perceptions regarding the various dimensions of services they receive. Health services can be improved by considering patients’ experiences4. Despite a growing focus on patient experience in healthcare, little is known about patient satisfaction scores in otorhinolaryngology–head and neck (ORL) surgery in Saudi Arabia. In the United States, a study aimed to describe patient satisfaction scores in ambulatory ORL and examine the association of teaching status across multiple service domains. A total of 36,089 surveys were included in this study. The study concluded that ORL patients in teaching settings report lower overall patient satisfaction, mainly related to access to care and visit-specific processes5. A crucial part of patient care is ensuring patients’ awareness of their diagnosis and treatment plan. Makaryus A et al. (2005) investigated whether patients knew their discharge diagnoses, treatment plans, and common side effects of prescribed medications upon discharge from a municipal teaching hospital. The study findings revealed that less than half of patients could correctly identify their diagnoses, medication(s), purpose, or significant side effects6. Although patient’s experience of care and patient awareness of their condition is an increasingly fundamental priority in healthcare, little is known of them in ORL surgery.

Several international studies have examined patient satisfaction in ORL services. For example, studies in Europe and North America have consistently highlighted staff quality and communication as the strongest predictors of positive patient experience, while long waiting times and limited access reduce satisfaction5,7. Similar patterns have been reported in Asian and Middle Eastern contexts, where cultural expectations strongly influence reported satisfaction levels8. Despite these insights, evidence specific to Saudi Arabia remains limited, underscoring the importance of the present study. Herein, the present research aimed to examine patients’ overall satisfaction and disease-related knowledge to measure of the quality of the ORL care provided at a single center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University (IRB approval number: 23–0365). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and responses were anonymous and confidential.

Study design, setting, and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAAUH) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The study employed a simple random sampling technique to recruit participants from the outpatient ORL clinic. The study included patients aged 18 years and older who were recently diagnosed cases or had recently undergone surgery to reduce recall bias and ensure their healthcare experiences were current. Patients were excluded if they were under 18 years of age, declined participation, or had cognitive impairment that prevented questionnaire completion. The target sample size was calculated to be 180 participants, based on an 80% power, 95% confidence level, and a ± 6% margin of error, using G-Power software.

Data collection instrument

The questionnaire was created based on the study’s goals and incorporated items from prior research with similar objectives9. The questionnaire was originally developed in English and subsequently translated into Arabic using forward–backward translation. A pilot test with 20 patients ensured clarity, cultural appropriateness, and content validity. The instrument was divided into three main sections: sociodemographic data, patient experience and satisfaction assessment, and evaluation of disease-related knowledge. The patient satisfaction section included 22 items that measured various aspects of ORL care using a 7-point Likert scale. The disease-related knowledge section consisted of nine questions that evaluated understanding of diagnosis, medications, surgery details, and treatment instructions, with response options of “yes,” “no,” “I do not know,” or “not applicable.” Data collection was conducted electronically via Google Forms with QR code implementation for easy access. The internal consistency of the overall satisfaction scale and each of the five satisfaction domains was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient. Values ≥ 0.9 were interpreted as excellent, 0.80–0.89 as very good, 0.70–0.79 as acceptable, and < 0.70 as poor.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for all variables. Bivariate associations were analyzed using t-tests and chi-square tests. Multiple linear and logistic regression models identified predictors of satisfaction and knowledge scores. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to explore the factor structures of satisfaction items, while k-means clustering identified satisfaction profiles. Correlation analysis assessed relationships between satisfaction domains and knowledge. One-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc tests compared satisfaction across education levels and other multi-group variables. Data visualization included distribution, correlation, violin, and ridge plots. Missing data patterns were reviewed, and complete case analysis was used for multivariate models.

Results

The study included 180 participants (Table 1). The mean age was 33.5 years (SD = 12.5). Females made up 75% (n = 135), and nearly all participants were Saudi (97.8%, n = 176). Over half were married (53.3%, n = 96), while 42.2% (n = 76) were single. Most held a bachelor’s degree (56.7%, n = 102), with 19.4% (n = 35) having postgraduate education and 23.9% (n = 43) a school-level education. Employment was reported by 56.7% (n = 102), while 27.8% (n = 50) were students. Others were housewives (6.11%), unemployed (6.67%), or retired (2.78%). Monthly income exceeded 10,000 SAR for 45%, was below 5,000 SAR for 29.4%, and ranged between 5,000 and 10,000 SAR for 25.6%. Surgical history was almost equally split, 49.4% had surgery and 50.6% with no surgical history. Most participants (68.3%) had no chronic illness; 23.3% had one, and 8.3% had multiple conditions. Asthma (16.1%) was the most common, followed by diabetes (8.9%), hypertension (8.3%), and thyroid disease (7.2%).

Table 2 shows the reliability analysis of the included scales. All satisfaction scales demonstrated high internal consistency scores. The total satisfaction scale, consisting of 22 items, demonstrated excellent reliability (α = 0.98). Among the five domains, Staff Quality (α = 0.95), Access and Efficiency (α = 0.90), Communication (α = 0.94), and Overall Experience (α = 0.94) all exhibited excellent reliability. The Environment and Comfort domain, comprising five items, showed very good internal consistency (α = 0.85). These results indicate that the satisfaction questionnaire items were highly cohesive and reliably measured their respective constructs across the domains.

Table 3 displays the patients’ disease-related knowledge. Nearly all respondents reported knowing how to take their medication (95.71%) and were aware of their condition or diagnosis (93.29%). A similarly high proportion knew the names of their medications (87.26%) and the surgery they underwent (87.16%). Understanding of post-operative instructions was also prevalent (85.05%). However, knowledge of medication side effects was comparatively lower, with only 62.75% reporting awareness of them. Awareness of surgical complications was also moderate (74.31%), and 75.69% of participants reported having received a summary of their disease and treatment.

Figure 1 presents patient satisfaction across five domains, showing generally high ratings. In the environment and comfort domain, cleanliness (6.43 ± 1.1) and privacy (6.28 ± 1.2) scored highest, while the waiting area scored lowest (5.83 ± 1.6). For staff quality, patients rated doctors’ kindness (6.42 ± 1.1), knowledge (6.34 ± 1.2), and nurses’ professionalism (6.36 ± 1.1) highly, with similarly strong ratings for empathy, listening, attentiveness, and overall confidence in staff. This reflects consistent perceptions of competence and compassion. Access to services received moderately high scores, with exam wait times (5.82 ± 1.7) slightly higher than service wait times (5.76 ± 1.7); about half of respondents gave top ratings in both. In communication, the most praised item was allowing patient questions (6.25 ± 1.3), followed by lifestyle and preventive advice. Over 58% selected the highest score for all items. For overall experience, top scores were given for feeling serene after visits (6.18 ± 1.3), ideal service (6.12 ± 1.4), and overall satisfaction (6.07 ± 1.5), with over half of participants choosing the highest rating, though “Above expectations” had slightly fewer (51.67%).

Figure 2 shows the correlation matrix of satisfaction domain scores and knowledge scores. Strong positive correlations were observed among the satisfaction domains, particularly between Overall Experience and Staff Quality (r = 0.893, p < 0.001), Communication (r = 0.846, p < 0.001), and Environment (r = 0.794, p < 0.001). The Staff Quality domain was also strongly associated with communication (r = 0.870, p < 0.001) and environment (r = 0.826, p < 0.001). The Knowledge Score showed weaker but significant positive correlations with all satisfaction domains, with the highest correlation observed with Staff Quality (r = 0.311, p < 0.001) and Environment (r = 0.300, p < 0.001).

Supplementary Table S1 demonstrates participants’ knowledge scores by demographic variables. The knowledge scores did not significantly differ across any sociodemographic, income, or comorbidity subgroups (all p.adj = 1.000). Females (82.5 ± 23.74) and males (80.11 ± 26.55) showed comparable levels of disease-related knowledge. Participants with postgraduate education had numerically higher scores (89.49 ± 15.75) compared to those with other education levels; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Notably, retired individuals reported the lowest knowledge scores (58.00 ± 34.43), while those with single chronic conditions had the highest mean scores (88.05 ± 16.71). Although participants with asthma or thyroid disease also reported relatively higher knowledge levels (88.08 ± 16.33 and 89.03 ± 15.44, respectively), none of these differences reached statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Supplementary Table S2 illustrates the distribution of patients’ overall satisfaction scores in relation to sociodemographic characteristics. No statistically significant differences were found across any sociodemographic or clinical subgroup (all p > 0.24). Females (6.18 ± 1.10) reported slightly higher satisfaction than males (6.04 ± 1.17), though not significant (p = 0.458). Participants with postgraduate degrees reported slightly lower satisfaction (5.86 ± 1.12) compared to those with lower education levels; however, this difference was not significant (p = 0.249). Similarly, differences by income, comorbidity status, or specific chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, asthma) were small and non-significant, suggesting that satisfaction with care was broadly consistent across patient groups. Patients who had undergone surgery reported significantly higher satisfaction scores (6.32 ± 0.86) compared to those who had not (5.97 ± 1.30), with the difference reaching statistical significance (p = 0.039).

Table 6 compares patient characteristics with their perceived experiences versus expectations. Most participants (76.1%) reported experiences matching expectations, 15.6% found them better, and 8.3% worse. Satisfaction was highest among those with better-than-expected experiences (6.75 [6.20–7.00]), slightly lower in the matched group (6.64 [5.95–6.95]), and significantly lower in the worse-than-expected group (5.27 [4.23–6.32], p = 0.002). Similar patterns appeared across domains, with significant differences in staff quality (p = 0.001), access (p = 0.010), environment (p = 0.041), and overall experience (p = 0.003). Communication was lower in the worse group but not statistically significant (p = 0.074). Educational level differed significantly (p = 0.033), with more postgraduates in the worse group (46.7%), possibly indicating higher expectations. Regional differences were noted (p = 0.031), though most participants were from the Central Region. Other demographics and comorbidities showed no significant differences (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

Although patient satisfaction is increasingly recognized as a critical metric for healthcare quality, there remains a paucity of data specific to outpatient services in ORL in Saudi Arabia. Existing studies have primarily focused on primary care or general hospital settings, neglecting the nuanced expectations and experiences of patients seeking specialized ORL care. This study provides valuable insight into patient satisfaction and disease-related knowledge among ORL patients in a secondary care setting in Saudi Arabia.

Overall satisfaction scores were notably high across all five assessed domains. The uniformly positive results may reflect the cultural context of Saudi Arabia, where several studies have noted that patient expectations and satisfaction patterns differ significantly from those in Western healthcare systems10. However, these findings are limited to a single-center sample and may not generalize to all ORL settings in Saudi Arabia. Recent regional studies have demonstrated a high level of satisfaction within Saudi healthcare settings. For instance, Owaidh et al. (2018) reported that 91.3% of patients in southern Saudi Arabia expressed satisfaction with their care.

Additionally, Al-Khashan et al. (2021) emphasized improvements in service delivery and patient satisfaction as part of Saudi Arabia’s broader healthcare reforms11,12. In contrast, international studies in academic ORL settings have reported lower average satisfaction levels. Redding et al. (2023) found that only 83.6% of patients rated their visit as fully satisfactory in a large academic US ORL clinic7. The satisfaction levels observed in the present study are notably higher than those in global settings, suggesting favorable patient experiences in our setting.

The Staff Quality domain achieved the highest satisfaction scores, with patients particularly appreciating the kindness of physicians, their clinical knowledge, and the professionalism of nursing staff. These findings align with extensive global evidence that highlights provider behavior and interpersonal qualities as the strongest and most consistent predictors of patient satisfaction across diverse healthcare systems13,14. High staff quality reflects not only technical expertise but also interpersonal behaviors that strongly influence patients’ trust, adherence, and perception of care. In ORL practice, where conditions often require long-term follow-up, staff quality becomes central to maintaining continuity and patient loyalty.

Communication scores were similarly high, with patients valuing opportunities to ask questions and receive health education, consistent with international evidence linking effective physician-patient communication to improved satisfaction outcomes15,16. The correlation analysis revealed the strongest relationships between Staff Quality and other satisfaction domains, particularly Communication, supporting the central role of provider-patient interactions in healthcare satisfaction17. These results align with previous international reports, which show that interpersonal qualities and provider communication are consistently among the strongest drivers of satisfaction in ORL and other specialties14,15. At the same time, the relatively lower scores in Access and Efficiency reflect a pattern widely observed in the literature, where wait times and service logistics are recurrent sources of dissatisfaction18,19. By situating our findings within this broader body of research, it is evident that while some factors affecting satisfaction appear universal, others, such as cultural context, have a unique influence in Saudi Arabia.

A 2019 study of 54,686 hospital patients found that direct communication with medical staff was the strongest predictor of overall satisfaction, highlighting its key role in patient experience20. The high staff satisfaction in our study reflects the effectiveness of patient-centered care and cultural competency training, aligning with reviews that show communication skills training improves patient satisfaction across cultures21,22. The cultural context of Saudi Arabia, where patients traditionally expect detailed explanations and guidance from healthcare providers, may contribute to the high appreciation for communication quality observed in our study11. Additionally, the high staff quality scores reflect Saudi Arabia’s investment in training under the National Transformation Program 2020 (Vision 2030 healthcare initiatives).

Our study revealed high overall disease-related knowledge scores, particularly in medication administration and awareness of diagnosis. However, significant knowledge gaps emerged in medication side effects and surgical complications, highlighting critical areas for improvement in patient education. The knowledge gap regarding medication side effects is concerning, as research has shown a link between awareness and adherence, safety, and satisfaction23,24. Similarly, studies in Arab healthcare settings show patients understand their conditions but have limited awareness of treatment risks and complications25.

The lack of association between knowledge scores and satisfaction domains suggests that while knowledge contributes to satisfaction, other factors play more prominent roles in determining patient experience. The disparity between high knowledge scores for basic treatment information and lower scores for adverse effects mirrors global patterns identified in systematic reviews of patient education effectiveness26. Evidence suggests that healthcare providers often focus on conveying essential treatment information while inadequately addressing potential complications and side effects27,28. In the Saudi healthcare context, cultural factors may influence information-sharing patterns, as patients may prefer reassurance over detailed risk discussions29,30. The knowledge assessment revealed that patients who underwent surgery scored slightly higher overall than those who did not, possibly due to more intensive pre-operative education31.

The Access and Efficiency domain scored the lowest among all satisfaction categories, reflecting global challenges in healthcare delivery related to wait times and service accessibility. This finding aligns with numerous international studies identifying wait times as primary sources of patient dissatisfaction across diverse healthcare systems7,18. The lower Access scores mirror patterns found in academic medical centers globally, where Boss and Thompson (2012) identified Access-related factors as primary contributors to reduced satisfaction in teaching hospitals32. Regional studies from Saudi Arabia have consistently identified waiting times as significant sources of patient dissatisfaction across various healthcare settings33,34. The access challenges observed in our study reflect broader pressures within the healthcare system, including increasing patient volumes, specialized service demands, and resource allocation constraints typical in academic medical centers35.

Despite the lower relative scores for Access, the average satisfaction level still reflects generally positive patient experiences, suggesting that while improvements are needed, the current system provides reasonable accessibility. International benchmarking studies indicate that wait time satisfaction varies significantly across healthcare systems, with patients in publicly funded systems often expressing higher tolerance for delays36. Our findings suggest that Saudi patients, like their international counterparts, value timely access to care and efficient service delivery. The Access and Efficiency domain’s correlation with other satisfaction measures (r = 0.667 with Total Satisfaction) suggests that improving the waiting times and service efficiency could significantly enhance overall patient experience. Studies from other Gulf countries have identified similar access challenges, with efforts to implement appointment scheduling systems and workflow optimization showing promising results19. The Saudi healthcare system’s digital transformation initiatives under Vision 2030 may provide opportunities to address these access challenges through electronic scheduling, telemedicine, and service optimization technologies37.

Those who underwent surgery reported significantly higher satisfaction scores. This counterintuitive result contrasts with much of the surgical satisfaction literature, where surgical patients often report lower satisfaction due to increased complexity, risk, and recovery challenges38. The higher satisfaction among surgical patients in our study may reflect several factors unique to the ORL specialty and our healthcare setting. ORL procedures often address chronic, quality-of-life-impacting conditions such as chronic sinusitis, hearing loss, or sleep disorders, where surgical intervention can provide dramatic symptom relief and functional improvement39. International studies have demonstrated that patients with chronic ORL conditions often experience significant satisfaction improvements following successful surgical intervention, particularly for conditions affecting daily functioning40. The enhanced satisfaction among surgical patients may also reflect more intensive preoperative counseling, clearer expectations of treatment, and more frequent follow-up care, which are typical of surgical practice41.

The analysis of recovery expectations provided additional insights, with 76.1% of patients reporting experiences that matched expectations, while 15.6% exceeded expectations and only 8.3% fell short. Patients whose experiences exceeded expectations demonstrated the highest satisfaction scores across all domains, while those with unmet expectations showed significantly lower satisfaction. This pattern aligns with expectation confirmation theory, which suggests that satisfaction results from the relationship between pre-service expectations and perceived performance42,43. The relatively low proportion of patients with worse-than-expected experiences suggests that effective preoperative counseling and realistic expectation setting are in place in our clinic. Nonetheless, the results should be viewed within the cultural and institutional context, where social desirability bias may have contributed to high satisfaction scores. Educational level showed significant differences in expectation fulfillment, with higher proportions of postgraduate-educated patients in the “worse-than-expected” group, potentially reflecting more critical expectations among highly educated patients8. This finding is consistent with the literature, which demonstrates that higher education levels are associated with more demanding healthcare expectations and more critical evaluations of care quality44. The cultural context of Saudi Arabia, where surgical procedures may carry particular significance for family and social considerations, may contribute to the generally positive surgical experiences observed in our study45.

Strengths of this study include its focus on a specialty outpatient ORL clinic, the use of a structured and reliable questionnaire with excellent internal consistency, and the inclusion of both surgical and nonsurgical patients. The large proportion of Saudi nationals enhances the relevance of these findings to local healthcare reform efforts, and the results provide valuable insights for quality improvement in ORL care in Saudi Arabia.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and the single-center setting may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare institutions or regions. The sample was predominantly composed of Saudi nationals and females, which may not represent diverse patient populations. Selection bias may have occurred due to simple random sampling of clinic attendees, potentially excluding patients with negative experiences who avoid follow-up visits. The high satisfaction scores reflect cultural tendencies toward positive healthcare evaluations in Saudi Arabia; however, social desirability bias may have influenced the responses. Additionally, recall bias may have affected knowledge assessments, particularly for patients with longer intervals since their last visit.

Based on the findings, it is recommended that healthcare organizations prioritize optimizing workflows and enhancing appointment scheduling systems to tackle access and efficiency concerns, as this area received the lowest satisfaction scores. Targeted patient education programs should be developed that specifically focus on medication side effects and surgical complications, where the most significant knowledge gaps exist. Staff training programs should continue to emphasize communication skills and empathy, building upon the already excellent staff quality scores. Standardizing pre-operative counseling protocols is essential to uphold the high satisfaction levels observed among surgical patients. Future research should utilize longitudinal designs across multiple centers with diverse patient populations to improve generalizability. Regular satisfaction monitoring should be integrated into quality improvement initiatives, with a particular focus on managing patient expectations and addressing the needs of highly educated patients who tend to conduct more critical evaluations. The high satisfaction scores observed may reflect cultural or social desirability bias, a phenomenon well-documented in healthcare surveys in Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients at the ORL clinic expressed high satisfaction, particularly regarding the quality of staff and communication, affirming the effectiveness of patient-centered care delivery. While disease-related knowledge was generally strong, gaps in understanding medication side effects and surgical complications indicate areas for targeted educational interventions. The relatively lower scores in access and efficiency suggest opportunities to improve appointment scheduling and workflow processes. These findings provide actionable insights for enhancing healthcare quality in specialized outpatient settings. These conclusions should be interpreted cautiously, as satisfaction levels may have been influenced by cultural norms favoring positive evaluations and social desirability bias.
