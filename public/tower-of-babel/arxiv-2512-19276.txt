On Lie-holomorphs of Leibniz algebras
G. La Rosa, M. Mancini

Abstract
. We study the notion of the Lie-holomorph of a Leibniz algebra, recently introduced by N. P. Souris as a generalisation of the classical holomorph construction for Lie algebras. We establish a connection between the Lie-holomorph construction and the Leibniz algebra of biderivations defined by J.-L. Loday, and we prove that a linear endomorphism is a Lie-derivation if and only if it is simultaneously a derivation and an anti-derivation. As an application, we classify the Lie-holomorph algebras of all low-dimensional non-Lie Leibniz algebras over a field of characteristic different from 2 2 .

1.
Introduction

In 1965, A. Blokh introduced algebraic structures under the name of
D
-algebras; they are now known as Leibniz algebras
[
4
]
. Later, in 1993, they were popularized by J.-L. Loday
[
24
]
, who presented them as a non-anticommutative version of Lie algebras. A right Leibniz algebra
L
is a vector space equipped with a bilinear product
[-,-]
, usually called
bracket
or
commutator
, such that the right multiplications act as derivations. With this definition, Lie algebras appear as a special class of Leibniz algebras.

The notion of the holomorph of a Lie algebra is well known in Lie theory
(see, for instance,
[
20
]
). More precisely, given a Lie algebra
L
, the vector space
L\times\Der(L)
, where
\Der(L)
denotes the Lie algebra of derivations of
L
, can be endowed with a Lie algebra structure via the bilinear multiplication

[(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})]=\left([x,y]+d(y)-d^{\prime}(x),\,d\circ d^{\prime}-d^{\prime}\circ d\right).

The resulting Lie algebra
(L\times\Der(L),[-,-])
is called the
holomorph

of
L
, and it is usually denoted by
\hol(L)
. The study of this construction
goes back at least to the end of the nineteenth century and originates in group
theory, where the holomorph encodes, in a single object, a group together with its
automorphism group (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).

In the context of Leibniz algebras, the notion of holomorph was first introduced by K. Boyle, K. C. Misra, and E. Stitzinger in
[
6
]
. With their definition, the authors were able to extend several results from complete Lie algebras to the setting of complete Leibniz algebras. However, in this framework the Leibniz algebra
L
is not embedded as an ideal in its holomorph, and when
L
is a Lie algebra, the classical holomorph construction is not recovered.

For these reasons, in this manuscript we adopt the definition of
Lie-holomorph
given by N. P. Souris in
[
30
]
. It turns out that the study of the Lie-holomorph of a Leibniz algebra
L
is closely related to the Leibniz algebra of biderivations
\Bider(L)
, which was introduced by J.-L. Loday in
[
24
]
in terms of pairs consisting of derivations and antiderivations of
L
satisfying a compatibility condition. This connection represents the core of the present work.

After recalling some basic definitions and preliminaries in
Section

2
, we present the main results concerning biderivations and Lie-holomorphs of Leibniz algebras in
Section

3
. Eventually, in
Section

4
we provide a complete classification of the Lie-holomorphs of all low-dimensional non-Lie Leibniz algebras, by applying the results developed here together with the classification of Leibniz algebras of dimension at most four given in
[
1
,
15
,
9
]
, and the classification of biderivations given in
[
26
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

We assume that
\mathbb{F}
is a field with
\bchar(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. For the general theory we refer to
[
2
,
24
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
24
]

A
right Leibniz algebra
over
\mathbb{F}
is a vector space
L
over
\mathbb{F}
endowed with a bilinear map (called
bracket
or
commutator
)
\left[-,-\right]\colon L\times L\rightarrow L
which satisfies the
right Leibniz identity

\left[\left[x,y\right],z\right]=\left[[x,z],y\right]+\left[x,\left[y,z\right]\right],

for any
x,y,z\in L
.

In the same way we can define a left Leibniz algebra, using the left Leibniz identity

\left[x,\left[y,z\right]\right]=\left[\left[x,y\right],z\right]+\left[y,\left[x,z\right]\right].

A Leibniz algebra that is both left and right is called
symmetric
Leibniz algebra.

Remark 2.2
.

Given a right (resp. left) Leibniz algebra
L
, the multiplication

[x,y]^{\op}=[y,x]

defines a left (resp. right) Leibniz algebra structure the same underlying vector space. The resulting Leibniz algebra is denoted by
L^{\op}
. Hence, there is an equivalence between left and right Leibniz algebras.

From now on, throughout the rest of the paper, we work with right Leibniz algebras.

Every Lie algebra canonically defines a Leibniz algebra, and a Leibniz algebra whose bracket is skew-symmetric necessarily satisfies the Jacobi identity, hence is a Lie algebra. This correspondence gives rise to an adjunction
[
25
]
between the category
\mathsf{Lie}
of Lie algebras over
\mathbb{F}
and the category
\mathsf{Leib}
of Leibniz algebras over
\mathbb{F}
. More precisely, the full inclusion functor
i\colon\mathsf{Lie}\to\mathsf{Leib}
admits a left adjoint

p\colon\mathsf{Leib}\to\mathsf{Lie},

which assigns to each Leibniz algebra
L
the quotient
L/\Leib(L)
. Here

\Leib(L)=\langle[x,x]\mid x\in L\rangle

denotes the
Leibniz kernel
of
L
, which is the minimal two-sided ideal such that the quotient
L/\Leib(L)
becomes a Lie algebra, and it is, in particular, an abelian algebra.

The left and the right center of a Leibniz algebra
L
are, respectively,

\Zl(L)=\left\{x\in L\;|\;\left[x,L\right]=0\right\}\;\text{and}\;\Zr(L)=\left\{x\in L\;|\;\left[L,x\right]=0\right\},

while the
center
of
L
is

\Z(L)=\Zl(L)\cap\Zr(L).

One may check that
\Zr(L)
and
\Z(L)
are ideals of
L
, meanwhile the left center is not even a subalgebra. Moreover,
\Leib(L)\subseteq\Zr(L)
.

In
[
12
]
, the authors introduced the notion of
Lie-center
of a Leibniz algebra
L

\ZL(L)=\{x\in L\;|\;[x,y]+[y,x]=0,\,\forall y\in L\}.

One may check that
\ZL(L)
is a characteristic ideal of
L
containing
Z(L)
. Furthermore,
\ZL(L)=L
if and only if
L
is a Lie algebra.

We further recall the definitions of nilpotent and solvable Leibniz algebras.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra and let

L^{(0)}=L,\quad L^{(k+1)}=[L^{(k)},L],\quad\forall k\geq 0

be the
lower central series
of
L
.
L
is
n
-nilpotent
if
L^{(n-1)}\neq 0
and
L^{(n)}=0
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra and let

L^{0}=L,\quad L^{k+1}=[L^{k},L^{k}],\quad\forall k\geq 0

be the
derived series
of
L
.
L
is
n
-solvable
if
L^{n-1}\neq 0
and
L^{n}=0
.

Remark 2.5
.

If
L
is
2
-nilpotent, i.e., if
[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]=0
for any
x,y,z\in L
, then the derived subalgebra
[L,L]
lies in the Lie-center
\ZL(L)
.

The definition of a derivation for a Leibniz algebra is the same as in the case
of Lie algebras, and in fact it coincides with the standard notion of derivation
in the broader setting of non-associative algebras.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra. A
derivation
of
L
is a linear map
d\colon L\rightarrow L
such that

d(\left[x,y\right])=\left[d(x),y\right]+\left[x,d(y)\right],

for any
x,y\in L
.

The right multiplications are particular derivations called
inner derivations
, and an equivalent way to define a Leibniz algebra is to saying that the adjoint map
\ad_{x}=\left[-,x\right]
is a derivation, for every
x\in L
.

The set
\Der(L)
is a Lie algebra with the natural bracket given by the commutator
d_{1}\circ d_{2}-d_{2}\circ d_{1}
. The subset
\Inn(L)
of all inner derivations of
L
is an ideal of
\Der(L)
.

The definitions of
anti-derivation
and
biderivation
for a Leibniz algebra were first given by J.-L. Loday in
[
24
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

[
24
]

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra. An
anti-derivation
of
L
is a linear map
D\colon L\rightarrow L
such that

D([x,y])=[D(x),y]-[D(y),x],

for any
x,y\in L
.

For a left Leibniz algebra, one needs to ask that

D([x,y])=[x,D(y)]-[y,D(x)],

for any
x,y\in L
.

We observe that in the case of Lie algebras, there is no difference between a derivation and an anti-derivation. Moreover it is easy to check that, for any
x\in L
, the left adjoint map
\Ad_{x}=[x,-]
is an anti-derivation.

Remark 2.8
.

[
24
]

The set
\ADer(L)
of anti-derivations of a Leibniz algebra
L
has a
\Der(L)
-module structure with the action

d\cdot D=D\circ d-d\circ D,

for any
d\in\Der(L)
and for any
D\in\ADer(L)
.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
D\colon L\rightarrow L
be an anti-derivation. Then,
D(\Leib(L))=0
since

D([x,x])=[D(x),x]-[D(x),x]=0,

for any
x\in L
.

Definition 2.10
.

[
24
]

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra. A
biderivation
of
L
is a pair

(d,D)\in\Der(L)\times\ADer(L)

such that

[x,d(y)]=[x,D(y)],

for any
x,y\in L
.

The set of all biderivations of
L
, denoted by
\Bider(L)
, has a Leibniz algebra structure with the bracket

[(d,D),(d^{\prime},D^{\prime})]=(d\circ d^{\prime}-d^{\prime}\circ d,D\circ d^{\prime}-d^{\prime}\circ D),

for any
(d,D),(d,D^{\prime})\in\Bider(L)
, and it is possible to define a Leibniz algebra morphism

L\rightarrow\Bider(L)\colon x\mapsto(-\ad_{x},\Ad_{x}).

The pair
(-\ad_{x},\Ad_{x})
is called
inner biderivation
of
L
, and the set of all inner biderivations forms a Leibniz subalgebra of
\Bider(L)
.

Remark 2.12
.

In the literature on Leibniz algebras, there coexist two different notions of biderivation. Following J.-L. Loday, biderivations are defined as pairs consisting of a derivation and an antiderivation satisfying a compatibility condition, while more recent works treat biderivations as bilinear maps satisfying Leibniz-type identities in each argument.

These definitions are not equivalent, and the objects they produce differ substantially. In this paper, we adopt the latter perspective, and throughout we refer to biderivations the sense of J.-L. Loday.

3.
The Lie-holomorph of a Leibniz algebra

In this section we recall the definition of
Lie-holomorph
of a Leibniz algebra, which was recently introduced in
[
30
]
in order to generalise the holomorph construction for Lie algebras.

Given any Lie algebra
L
, the holomorph
\hol(L)
is defined as the semidirect product
L\rtimes\Der(L)
endowed with the bracket

[(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})]=([x,y]+d(y)-d^{\prime}(x),[d,d^{\prime}]),

for any
(x,d),(y,d)\in L\rtimes\Der(L)
. One may check that
\hol(L)
is a Lie algebra, and
L
embeds as an ideal of
\hol(L)
via the canonical inclusion

i_{1}\colon L\rightarrowtail\hol(L)\colon x\mapsto(x,0).

This construction has analogies with the notion of holomorph in the category
\mathsf{Grp}
of groups, where the holomorph of a group
G
is defined as
\hol(G)=G\rtimes\Aut(G)
, with multiplication

(g,\varphi)\cdot(h,\varphi^{\prime})=(g\cdot\varphi(h),\varphi\circ\varphi^{\prime}).

In fact, both the categories
\mathsf{Lie}
and
\mathsf{Grp}
are action representable
[
5
]
: this means that, for any object
X
, there exists an object
\Act(X)
, called the
actor
of
X
, together with a natural isomorphism of functors

\SplExt(-,X)\cong\Hom(-,\Act(X)).

In the case of Lie algebras, the actor of a Lie algebra
L
is
\Der(L)
; while for groups, the actor of a group
G
is the automorphism group
\Aut(G)
. Hence, the holomorph construction of an object
X
of an action representable category is then precisely the semidirect product
\hol(X)=X\rtimes\Act(X)
.

However, the notion of action representable category has been proved to be quite restrictive. For instance, it was proved in
[
19
]
that the only action representable varieties of non-associative algebras over an infinite field
\mathbb{F}
, with
\bchar(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, are the varieties of abelian algebras and Lie algebras.

On the other hand, the article
[
10
]
showed that the weaker notion of
universal strict general actor
(USGA, for short) is available for any Orzech category of interest
[
27
]
. For instance, in the category
\mathsf{Leib}
,
\USGA(L)=\Bider(L)
for any Leibniz algebra
L
.

It was also proved in
[
10
, Theorem 3.9]
that an object
X
of a category of interest
\mathsf{C}
has an actor
\Act(X)
if and only if the semidirect product
X\rtimes\USGA(X)
is an object of
\mathsf{C}
. If this is the case, then
\USGA(X)=\Act(X)
.

As a consequence, since
\mathsf{Leib}
is not action representable, then the semidirect product
L\rtimes\Bider(L)
does not give in general a Leibniz algebra

1

1

It is proved in
[
10
, Section 5]
that
L\rtimes\Bider(L)
is a Leibniz algebra whenever
\Z(L)=0
or
[L,L]=L
.
. Hence, the holomorph construction
L\rtimes\USGA(L)
does not extend from Lie algebras to Leibniz algebras. However, two different notions of holomorph in the variety
\mathsf{Leib}
are available in the literature.

The authors of
[
6
]
defined the holomorph of a left Leibniz algebra
L
as the vector space
L\times\Der(L)
endowed with the bilinear multiplication

[(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})]=([x,y]+d(y),[\Ad_{x},d^{\prime}]+[d,d^{\prime}])

for any
(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})\in L\times\Der(L)
.

This notion has the advantage that certain results for complete Lie algebras extend naturally to complete Leibniz algebras. On the other hand, one may check that
L
does not embed as an ideal of its holomorph. Moreover, if
L
is Lie algebra, the multiplication above does not define a Lie algebra structure on
L\times\Der(L)
, so the construction does not recover the classical holomorph
\hol(L)=L\rtimes\Der(L)
.

In order to solve this issue, N. P. Souris considered in
[
30
]
the ideal

\DerL(L)=\{d\in\Der(L)\;|\;\Imm(d)\subseteq\ZL(L)\}

of
Lie-derivations
of
L
, and defined the
Lie-holomorph
of a Leibniz algebra
L
as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

[
30
]

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra. The
Lie-holomorph
of
L
is the semidirect product

\holL(L)=L\rtimes\DerL(L)

endowed with the bracket

[(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})]=([x,y]+d(y)-d^{\prime}(x),[d,d^{\prime}]),

for any
(x,d),(y,d^{\prime})\in L\rtimes\Der(L)
.

He also proved that
\holL(L)
is a Leibniz algebra, and that
L
can be embedded as an ideal into
\holL(L)
via the canonical map
i_{1}\colon x\mapsto(x,0)
. Furthermore, if
L
is a Lie algebra, then
\DerL(L)=\Der(L)
, but the converse does not hold in general as the following example shows.

Example 3.2
.

Let
L
be the
3
-dimensional Leibniz algebra with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
and non-zero commutators
[e_{3},e_{2}]=-[e_{2},e_{3}]=e_{1}
and
[e_{3},e_{3}]=-e_{1}
. One has that
\Leib(L)=\langle e_{1}\rangle
, hence
L
is not a Lie algebra. We want to show that
\DerL(L)=\Der(L)
.

We firstly observe that
e_{1}\in Z(L)
. Moreover,
x=x_{2}e_{2}+x_{3}e_{3}\in\ZL(L)
if and only if
x_{3}=0
. Thus,
\ZL(L)=\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle
.

From the classification of biderivations of low-dimensional Leibniz algebras given in
[

, it turns out that
\Der(L)
can be represented as the Lie subalgebra

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0&b\\
0&a&c\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}\right\}

of
\mathfrak{gl}(3,\mathbb{F})
. Thus,
\Imm(d)\in\ZL(L)
for any

, and
\DerL(L)=\Der(L)
.

One may check that the Leibniz algebra
L
considered above is symmetric. This is not a case, as the following result shows.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra. If
\DerL(L)=\Der(L)
, then
L
is symmetric.

Proof.

If
\DerL(L)=\Der(L)
, then the left adjoint map
\Ad_{x}=[x,-]
is a derivation, for any
x\in L
. Indeed, one has

[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]-[[x,z],y],

for any
y,z\in L
, since
\Ad_{x}\in\ADer(L)
, and

[[x,z],y]=-[y,[x,z]]

since
\ad_{z}=[-,z]\in\DerL(L)
. Hence, the left Leibniz identity

[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]+[y,[x,z]]

holds in
L
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In general, the set
\Inn(L)

in the category
\mathsf{Leib}

, for any

. Thus,
\DerL(L)\cong\holL(L)/L
, and the acting morphism associated with (

) is the Leibniz algebra monomorphism

\DerL(L)\rightarrowtail\Bider(L)\colon d\mapsto(-d,-d),

which gives a canonical embedding of
\DerL(L)
into
\Bider(L)
. Actually, one can prove the following.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
L
be a Leibniz algebra and let
d\in\Der(L)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

d\in\DerL(L)
;

(ii)

d\in\ADer(L);

(iii)

(d,d)\in\Bider(L)
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii)

If
d\in\DerL(L)
, then
[d(x),y]+[y,d(x)]=0
, for any
x,y\in L
. This implies that
d
is also an anti-derivation. Thus, the pair
(d,d)
is a biderivation of
L
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii)

If
(d,d)\in\Bider(L)
, then trivially
d\in\ADer(L)
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)

If
d\in\ADer(L)
, then

d([x,y])=[d(x),y]-[d(y),x],

for any
x,y\in L
. Since
d
is also a derivation, one gets

[d(y),x]=-[x,d(y)],

which implies that
d\in\DerL(L)
.

∎

As an immediate consequence, we get that

\DerL(L)=\Der(L)\cap\ADer(L)=\{d\in\Der(L)\;|\;(d,d)\in\Bider(L)\}.

4.1.

Let

\end{pmatrix}\right)\;\middle|\;a,b\in\mathbb{F}\right\}.

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{e_{2}},\widetilde{e_{3}}\right]=\left[(e_{2},0),(e_{3},0)\right]=(e_{1},0)=\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle\left[\overline{e_{12}},\widetilde{e_{2}}\right]=\left[(0,e_{12}),(e_{2},0)\right]=(e_{12}\cdot e_{2},0)=(e_{1},0)=\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{e_{2}},\overline{e_{12}}\right]=\left[(e_{2},0),(0,e_{12})\right]=(-e_{12}\cdot e_{2},0)=(-e_{1},0)=-\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle\left[\overline{e_{13}},\widetilde{e_{3}}\right]=\left[(0,e_{13}),(e_{3},0)\right]=(e_{13}\cdot e_{3},0)=(e_{1},0)=\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{e_{3}},\overline{e_{13}}\right]=\left[(e_{3},0),(0,e_{13})\right]=(-e_{13}\cdot e_{3},0)=(-e_{1},0)=-\widetilde{e_{1}}.

∎

We observe that the dimension of the Lie-holomorph algebra of a
3
-dimensional Leibniz algebra lies between
3
and
6
. Furthermore:

–

The
4
-dimensional Leibniz algebras
\holL(L_{12})
and
\holL(L_{13})
are not isomorphic. Indeed, the former is
3
-nilpotent, since

\holL(L_{12})^{(1)}=\langle\widetilde{e_{1}},\widetilde{e_{2}}\rangle,\quad\holL(L_{12})^{(2)}=\langle\widetilde{e_{2}}\rangle,\quad\holL(L_{12})^{(3)}=0,

whereas the latter is not nilpotent, because

\holL(L_{13})^{(1)}=\langle\widetilde{e_{1}},\widetilde{e_{2}}\rangle,\quad\holL(L_{13})^{(k)}=\langle\widetilde{e_{1}}+\widetilde{e_{2}}\rangle,\quad\text{for any }k\geq 2.

We observe that
\holL(L_{12})
is isomorphic to the Leibniz algebra
\mathfrak{R}_{5}
of
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
, which has basis
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}
and non-trivial commutators

[x_{1},x_{1}]=x_{3},\quad[x_{1},x_{2}]=[x_{3},x_{1}]=x_{4}.

An explicit isomorphism is given by the linear map
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{R}_{5}\rightarrow\holL(L_{12})
defined by

\varphi(x_{1})=\widetilde{e_{3}},\quad\varphi(x_{2})=\widetilde{e_{1}}-\overline{e_{23}},\quad\varphi(x_{3})=\widetilde{e_{1}},\quad\varphi(x_{4})=\widetilde{e_{2}}.

Furthermore, since passing to the opposite algebra reverses the Leibniz identity (turning right Leibniz algebras into left Leibniz algebras), it is natural to compare
\holL(L_{12})^{\op}
with the classification of
4
-dimensional nilpotent left Leibniz algebras given in
[
1
]
. One may verify that
\holL(L_{12})^{\op}
is indeed isomorphic to the left Leibniz algebra
\mathscr{A}_{24}
of
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
, since
\mathscr{A}_{24}^{\op}\cong\mathfrak{R}_{5}
.

Finally, the Lie-holomorph
\holL(L_{13})
is isomorphic to the Leibniz algebra
\mathfrak{L}_{39}
of
[
9
, Proposition 3.11]
, which has basis
\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\}
and non-zero brackets

[f_{1},f_{4}]=-[f_{4},f_{1}]=f_{2},\quad[f_{3},f_{4}]=f_{3},\quad[f_{4},f_{4}]=f_{2}.

Indeed, one may check that the linear map
\psi\colon\mathfrak{L}_{39}\rightarrow\holL(L_{13})
defined by

\psi(f_{1})=\widetilde{e_{2}}-\overline{e_{23}},\quad\psi(f_{2})=-\widetilde{e_{2}},\quad\psi(f_{3})=\widetilde{e_{1}}+\widetilde{e_{2}},\quad\psi(f_{4})=-\widetilde{e_{1}}+\widetilde{e_{3}},

is a Leibniz algebra isomorphism.

–

The algebra
\holL(L_{2}(\alpha))
is not isomorphic to any of the other
5
-dimensional Lie-holomorphs listed in
Theorem

4.7
, since it is the only one among them with a
3
-dimensional derived subalgebra.

–

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{\tfrac{1}{4}\}
, the Leibniz algebra
\holL(L_{6}(\alpha))
is not isomorphic to any of the other
5
-dimensional Lie-holomorphs listed in
Theorem

4.7
, since it is the only one among them with a
4
-dimensional Lie-center.

–

The Lie-holomorphs
\holL(L_{5}(-1))
and
\holL(L_{7})
are isomorphic. Indeed, the change of basis

\displaystyle\widetilde{e_{1}}\mapsto\widetilde{e_{1}},\quad\widetilde{e_{2}}\mapsto\widetilde{e_{2}}+\widetilde{e_{3}}+\overline{e_{12}},\quad\widetilde{e_{3}}\mapsto\widetilde{e_{2}}-\widetilde{e_{3}},

\displaystyle\overline{e_{12}}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\overline{e_{12}}+\overline{e_{13}}),\quad\overline{e_{13}}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\overline{e_{12}}-\overline{e_{13}})

defines a Leibniz algebra isomorphism
\holL(L_{5}(-1))\rightarrow\holL(L_{7})
.

–

The algebras
\holL(L_{7})
and
\holL(L_{4})
are not isomorphic over a general field
\mathbb{F}
. Indeed, suppose to have an isomorphism
\phi\colon\holL(L_{4})\rightarrow\holL(L_{7})
. Then

\phi(\widetilde{e_{1}})=k\widetilde{e_{1}},

for some
k\neq 0
, since
\left[\holL(L_{7}),\holL(L_{7})\right]=\left[\holL(L_{4}),\holL(L_{4})\right]=\langle\widetilde{e_{1}}\rangle
, and

\phi(\widetilde{e_{2}})=\alpha_{1}\widetilde{e_{1}}+\alpha_{2}+\widetilde{e_{2}}+\alpha_{3}\widetilde{e_{3}}+\alpha_{4}\overline{e_{12}}+\alpha_{5}\overline{e_{13}},

\phi(\widetilde{e_{3}})=\beta_{1}\widetilde{e_{1}}+\beta_{2}+\widetilde{e_{2}}+\beta_{3}\widetilde{e_{3}}+\beta_{4}\overline{e_{12}}+\beta_{5}\overline{e_{13}},

for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{F}
. Thus, one has

\displaystyle k\widetilde{e_{1}}=\phi([\widetilde{e_{2}},\widetilde{e_{2}}])=

\displaystyle[\phi(\widetilde{e_{2}}),\phi(\widetilde{e_{2}})]=\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle k\widetilde{e_{1}}=\phi([\widetilde{e_{3}},\widetilde{e_{3}}])=

\displaystyle[\phi(\widetilde{e_{3}}),\phi(\widetilde{e_{3}})]=\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle 0=\phi([\widetilde{e_{2}},\widetilde{e_{3}}])=

\displaystyle[\phi(\widetilde{e_{2}}),\phi(\widetilde{e_{3}})]=(\alpha_{2}\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{4}-\alpha_{3}\beta_{5}+\alpha_{4}\beta_{2}+\alpha_{5}\beta_{3})\widetilde{e_{1}},

\displaystyle 0=\phi([\widetilde{e_{3}},\widetilde{e_{2}}])=

\displaystyle[\phi(\widetilde{e_{3}}),\phi(\widetilde{e_{2}})]=(\beta_{2}\alpha_{3}-\beta_{2}\alpha_{4}-\beta_{3}\alpha_{5}+\beta_{4}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{5}\alpha_{3})\widetilde{e_{1}}.

It follows that
k=\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}=\beta_{2}\beta_{3}
and
\alpha_{2}\beta_{3}+\alpha_{3}\beta_{2}=0
. Hence,
\alpha_{i}\neq 0\neq\beta_{i}
for any
i=2,3
, and

k\left(\dfrac{\alpha_{2}}{\beta_{2}}+\dfrac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\right)=0.

Hence, we have
\alpha_{2}^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}=0
, which has a non-trivial solution if and only if
-1
is a square in
\mathbb{F}
. In this case, we recall from
[
29
]
that
L_{5}(-1)
is isomorphic to
L_{4}
, which implies

\holL(L_{4})\cong\holL(L_{5}(-1))\cong\holL(L_{7}).

Therefore, we conclude that
\holL(L_{7})
is isomorphic to
\holL(L_{4})
if and only if
x^{2}+1=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{F}
.

–

The
6

\holL(L_{3})
and
\holL(L_{6}\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right))

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left[\holL(L_{3}),\holL(L_{3})\right]=2\neq 3=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left[\holL(\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)),\holL(L_{6}\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right))\right].
