Association between serum preptin levels and bone metastasis in newly diagnosed prostate cancer: a prospective exploratory study
Authors: Burcu Gülbağcı, Fatma Akdağ, Deniz Gül, Hatice Halis, Esra Ozen Engin, Hacı İbrahim Çimen, İlhan Hacıbekiroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone health plays a critical role in prostate cancer irrespective of metastatic status at diagnosis. Preptin, a peptide hormone co-secreted with insulin, exerts osteoanabolic effects and may influence tumor-related processes through insulin/IGF-related signaling pathways. However, its role in the development of bone metastasis remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between serum preptin levels and bone metastasis in newly diagnosed prostate cancer patients.MethodsThis prospective exploratory biomarker study included patients with newly diagnosed prostate cancer. Patients with testosterone levels <25 ng/dL, diabetes mellitus, or unreliable preptin measurements were excluded. Serum preptin levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Bone metastatic status was determined using 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT findings. Clinical and biochemical parameters were compared between patients with and without bone metastasis. Statistical analyses were performed according to data distribution, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsFifty patients were included, of whom 23 had bone metastasis and 27 did not. Median age was similar between groups (68.9 vs 69.7 years, p = 0.734). Baseline prostate-specific antigen levels were significantly higher in patients with bone metastasis (p < 0.001). Serum preptin levels were comparable between groups (388.6 vs 399.6 ng/L, p = 0.763). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated no meaningful discriminatory performance of serum preptin for identifying bone metastasis (AUC = 0.525, 95% CI 0.357–0.693, p = 0.763).ConclusionSerum preptin levels were not associated with bone metastasis in newly diagnosed prostate cancer patients. These findings suggest that circulating preptin may not adequately reflect tumor–bone microenvironment interactions, highlighting the limitations of systemic biomarkers in metastatic processes. Larger longitudinal and tissue-level studies are needed to clarify the biological and clinical relevance of preptin in prostate cancer.Keywords: Biomarkers, Bone metabolism, Bone metastasis, Preptin, Prostate cancer

Introduction

Preptin is a peptide hormone co-secreted from pancreatic β-cells together with insulin and amylin and has been shown to exert anabolic metabolic effects. It has been suggested to play a role in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus by enhancing insulin secretion in response to plasma glucose levels [1], [2]. Preptin has also been shown to influence bone metabolism; it acts on osteoblasts by promoting proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis [3]. Serum preptin concentrations in humans, after adjusting for age and body mass index (BMI), were found to be correlated with bone density; furthermore, preptin levels were observed to decrease in osteoporosis and osteopenia in elderly men [4].

Insulin is thought to accelerate oncogenic processes by increasing cell proliferation through the activation of various intracellular signaling pathways, particularly those involving growth factors. Furthermore, elevated levels of insulin-dependent hepatic insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) have been associated with an increased susceptibility to certain cancers, particularly breast and prostate cancers [5], [6]. However, it remains unclear whether preptin, a member of the insulin family and derived from pro-insulin-like growth factor-2 (pro-IGF-2), exerts similar effects [2]. It has been hypothesized that preptin may contribute to tumor cell migration and proliferation via the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal kinase (MAPK/ERK) signaling pathways in breast cancer, although direct clinical evidence remains limited [7], [8].

The development of bone metastases is highly common throughout the disease course in patients with prostate cancer, with rates reaching up to 80% - 90% [9]. Bone metastasis is a complex process driven by the interaction between tumor cells—via their expressed receptors and adhesion molecules—and the bone microenvironment, which is rich in cytokines and growth factors. This process is explained by the “seed and soil” hypothesis [10]. Bone metastases in prostate cancer are predominantly osteoblastic; however, they also involve a dynamic and multistep bone remodeling process in which osteoclastic activity plays a role. Therefore, the bone microenvironment is not only a passive target but also an active regulator of the metastatic process. In recent years, some studies have suggested that low bone mineral density at the time of diagnosis may influence the development and timing of bone metastases in certain cancer types; however, this relationship remains inconsistent and the available evidence is heterogeneous [11], [12]. In this context, host-related factors influencing bone metabolism may also play a role in shaping the metastatic process.

Given its anabolic effects on bone metabolism, preptin may influence the development of bone metastases by modulating osteoblast activity and the bone microenvironment. However, as a member of the insulin peptide family, preptin may also exert proliferative effects through insulin/IGF-related signaling pathways, suggesting a potentially complex and context-dependent role in tumor biology. Importantly, circulating levels of preptin may not directly reflect its local activity within the tumor–bone microenvironment, and its net biological effect may vary depending on the balance between metabolic and proliferative signaling pathways. Therefore, despite its biological plausibility, the clinical relevance of preptin in cancer progression—particularly in bone metastasis—remains uncertain. To address this gap, we aimed to explore the relationship between serum preptin levels and the presence of bone metastases in patients with prostate cancer. To our knowledge, no prior clinical study has specifically evaluated this association.

Methods

This prospective study was designed as an exploratory biomarker analysis to investigate the potential relationship between serum preptin levels and bone metastasis, bone mineral density, and metabolic parameters in patients with prostate cancer.

Study population

This study was conducted at the Medical Oncology Clinic of Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine. Following ethics committee approval, patients with histopathologically confirmed prostate cancer who presented between 1 November 2024 and 1 November 2025 were screened for eligibility. Patients with known diabetes mellitus, conditions affecting bone metabolism (such as chronic kidney disease, secondary malignancy, rheumatologic diseases, and chronic liver disease), or prior androgen deprivation therapy administered after definitive surgery or radiotherapy were excluded. Additionally, patients scheduled to receive androgen deprivation therapy due to disease recurrence during follow-up were also excluded. The detailed patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of patient selection in this prospective study with newly diagnosed prostate cancer. A total of 88 patients were initially assessed for eligibility, of whom 68 met the inclusion criteria. Patients were excluded due to low testosterone levels (< 25 ng/dL) suggestive of prior androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) (n = 4), presence of diabetes mellitus (fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) (n = 6), or unavailable/unreliable preptin measurements (n = 8). Ultimately, 50 patients were included in the final analysis (27 without bone metastasis and 23 with bone metastasis).

Abbreviations: ADT, androgen deprivation therapy; FPG, fasting plasma glucose; HbA1c, glycated hemoglobin.

Laboratory measurements

After an overnight fast of at least 8 h, venous blood samples were obtained prior to the initiation of definitive or systemic therapy to assess routine laboratory parameters related to cardiovascular and bone health, including fasting plasma glucose, insulin, lipid profile, calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, and vitamin D levels. Prostate cancer–related laboratory parameters, including prostate-specific antigen (PSA), total testosterone, and complete blood count (CBC), were also evaluated. Fasting plasma glucose and other routine biochemical parameters were measured using standard automated laboratory methods. Additional serum samples were collected for preptin measurement and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). BMI was categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2). Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as fasting insulin (μIU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 405. HOMA-IR values were analyzed as continuous variables without predefined cut-off thresholds.

Preptin measurement

Serum preptin levels were measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer's instructions using a polyclonal anti-preptin antibody (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA; catalog no. H-035-24). The assays were performed in an external certified laboratory in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, ensuring standardized measurement conditions and analytical reliability. The laboratory personnel who performed the ELISA measurements were blinded to the patients' clinical information, including their bone metastasis status. Preptin concentrations were calculated from a standard calibration curve generated using serial dilutions of the assay standards according to the manufacturer's protocol. The obtained values were used for statistical analysis.

Bone mineral density assessment

Bone mineral density (BMD) was assessed using Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA). Measurements were obtained at the lumbar spine (L1–L4) and femoral neck regions. BMD results were expressed as T-scores according to the WHO criteria. A T-score ≥ −1.0 was considered normal, osteopenia was defined as a T-score between −1.0 and − 2.5, and osteoporosis was defined as a T-score ≤ −2.5. The lowest T-score obtained from the measured sites was used for analysis.

Prostate cancer staging

Prostate cancer staging was performed according to the Tumor–Node–Metastasis (TNM) classification system. Clinical staging was determined based on digital rectal examination, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, and histopathological findings from prostate biopsy. Metastatic status was determined using 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT findings in all patients. Multiparametric prostate MRI performed at the time of biopsy was available in 23 patients at diagnosis. Among patients with bone metastasis, five had visceral metastases and five had distant lymph node metastases.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 23, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Student's t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test.

Factors associated with the presence of bone metastasis were evaluated using univariable logistic regression analysis. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Serum preptin was additionally included irrespective of its univariable significance because it represented the primary biomarker and the main hypothesis of the study. Hemoglobin, although significant in the univariable analysis, was not entered into the multivariable model because it was considered more likely to reflect a consequence of bone metastatic disease rather than an independent predictor, and its inclusion would have increased the risk of overfitting given the limited number of metastatic events. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the proportion of variance explained by the model was reported as Nagelkerke R2. Correlations between serum preptin levels and metabolic or bone-related parameters were analyzed using Spearman correlation analysis. Differences in serum preptin levels across DEXA categories were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of serum preptin levels for identifying osteoporosis. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The median age of the patients was 69.3 years, and there was no significant difference between patients with and without bone metastasis (68.9 vs 69.7 years, p = 0.734). Baseline PSA levels were significantly higher in the bone metastatic group (p < 0.001), whereas serum preptin levels were similar between the two groups (399.6 vs 388.6 ng/L, p = 0.763). Univariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors associated with the presence of bone metastasis. Higher baseline PSA levels were significantly associated with bone metastasis (OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.000–1.010, p = 0.039). Lower Gleason tumor groups were significantly less likely to have bone metastasis compared with Gleason group 5, particularly Gleason group 2 (OR 0.062, 95% CI 0.009–0.406, p = 0.004) and Gleason group 3 (OR 0.080, 95% CI 0.007–0.918, p = 0.042). Similarly, lower T stage was associated with a reduced likelihood of bone metastasis, with T2 (OR 0.058, 95% CI 0.010–0.343, p = 0.002) and T3 disease (OR 0.162, 95% CI 0.033–0.787, p = 0.024) showing lower risk compared with T4 disease. Regional lymph node involvement was strongly associated with bone metastasis (cN0 vs cN+, OR 0.048, 95% CI 0.009–0.250, p < 0.001). Hemoglobin levels were inversely associated with bone metastasis (OR 0.641, 95% CI 0.445–0.924, p = 0.017) (Table 2).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, baseline PSA level (OR 1.004, 95% CI 1.000–1.008, p = 0.040) and regional lymph node metastasis (OR 0.055, 95% CI 0.006–0.521, p = 0.012) remained independent predictors of bone metastasis. T stage showed a borderline association with bone metastasis in multivariable analysis (overall p = 0.052), with T2 (OR 0.077, 95% CI 0.007–0.910, p = 0.042) and T3 (OR 0.082, 95% CI 0.009–0.731, p = 0.025) disease associated with significantly lower odds of bone metastasis compared to T4 disease. Given its strong univariable significance (overall p = 0.006) and established clinical relevance, T stage was retained in the final model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated adequate model fit (χ2 = 11.260, p = 0.187), and no multicollinearity was detected among the included variables (VIF: PSA = 1.061, T stage = 1.255, regional LN metastasis = 1.300). The model explained 60.3% of the variance in bone metastasis status (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.603). Although hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in patients with bone metastasis in univariable analysis (p = 0.017), hemoglobin was not included in the multivariable model, as it was considered more likely to reflect a consequence of metastatic bone involvement rather than an independent predictor, and its inclusion would have increased the risk of overfitting given the limited number of outcome events.

Serum preptin levels were not associated with the presence of bone metastasis in either univariable or multivariable analyses (Table 2).

Correlation analysis revealed no significant association between serum preptin levels and metabolic or bone-related parameters, including age, BMI, insulin, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, Ca × P product, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, albumin, total testosterone, and PSA (Table 3). Although Ca × P product (r = −0.274, p = 0.062) and PSA (r = 0.255, p = 0.073) showed borderline correlations with preptin levels, these did not reach statistical significance.

In logistic regression analysis, serum preptin levels were not associated with osteoporosis risk (OR = 1.000, 95% CI 0.999–1.001, p = 0.929). Similarly, serum preptin levels did not differ significantly across DEXA categories (normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis) in the Kruskal–Wallis test (p = 0.406).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated no meaningful discriminative ability of serum preptin levels for identifying bone metastasis (AUC = 0.525, 95% CI 0.357–0.693, p = 0.763). For osteoporosis, serum preptin showed a modest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.684, 95% CI 0.471–0.898, p = 0.180). However, given the very small number of osteoporosis cases (n = 5), this estimate should be interpreted with caution, and no meaningful clinical conclusions can be drawn from this finding.

Discussion

Bone health is of critical importance in prostate cancer, regardless of the presence of bone metastasis at diagnosis. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), which constitutes the backbone of treatment, increases the risk of osteoporosis in these patients, and skeletal-related events may result in significant morbidity and mortality [9]. Furthermore, the high prevalence of bone metastases in prostate cancer exacerbates this burden.

The development of metastasis in cancer patients is a complex, multistep, and multifactorial process. In the context of bone metastasis, this process is driven by the interaction between tumor cells and the bone microenvironment, which may be initiated even before overt tumor dissemination through tumor-derived factors that contribute to the formation of a pre-metastatic niche. Continuous remodeling of the local bone milieu is thought to create a permissive environment that facilitates the colonization of circulating tumor cells [13].

Bone mineral density (BMD) represents a clinical reflection of these ongoing bone remodeling processes. Age-related and multifactorial declines in BMD are primarily the result of an imbalance in bone remodeling, characterized by increased osteoclastic activity and relatively decreased osteoblastic activity. Emerging evidence suggests that alterations in bone remodeling and homeostasis may contribute to the formation of a favorable bone microenvironment; however, their role in the development of bone metastases remains debated, with conflicting findings across tumor types. While some studies have reported no significant association, others—particularly in prostate cancer—have demonstrated a significant relationship between reduced bone mineral density and an increased risk of bone metastasis. Notably, large-scale population-based analyses in urological cancers have reported up to a two- to threefold increased risk of bone metastasis in patients with low pre-diagnostic BMD, further supporting a potential link between bone health and metastatic susceptibility [11], [12].

Bone tissue, the most common site of metastasis in prostate cancer, has attracted significant interest regarding the roles of osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity, as well as microenvironmental factors that promote tumor cell adhesion and proliferation [10]. In this context, considering the pivotal role of osteoblast-driven bone formation in maintaining bone homeostasis, we investigated the role of preptin—a novel member of the insulin family known to enhance osteoblastic activity—in the development of bone metastasis. However, no statistically significant difference in serum preptin levels was observed between patients with and without bone metastases (p = 0.763).

Despite the known anabolic effects of preptin on bone tissue, this finding may reflect the complex and context-dependent biological role of preptin. While preptin enhances osteoblastic activity, its association with insulin-related signaling pathways may also contribute to proliferative processes, potentially resulting in a neutral overall effect. Circulating preptin levels may not adequately reflect its local activity within the tumor–bone microenvironment, where metastatic progression is regulated by complex cellular interactions.

Consistent with this interpretation, DEXA findings and host-derived bone- and metabolism-related parameters, including vitamin D, Ca × P product, and HOMA-IR, were comparable between groups, further suggesting that circulating metabolic markers may inadequately reflect the biological processes underlying bone metastasis.

Furthermore, although prostate cancer cells have been reported to express IGF-II, there is currently no published evidence demonstrating preptin secretion by prostate cancer cells [[14], [15]]. Because preptin is primarily co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic β-cells, serum preptin levels are likely to predominantly reflect systemic metabolic physiology rather than local tumor-derived production [1]. This may partly explain why circulating preptin was not associated with bone metastasis in our cohort. Accordingly, tissue-level preptin expression and local IGF-related signaling within the tumor–bone microenvironment may provide more biologically relevant information than a single measurement of circulating preptin.

A notable finding in our study was the considerable inter-individual variability observed in serum preptin concentrations, with IQR values spanning more than tenfold in both groups (66.3–651.6 ng/L in the non-metastatic group; 81.1–931.8 ng/L in the metastatic group). This degree of variability is not unique to our cohort; comparable variability has been reported in other clinical contexts. Kaluzna et al. documented a standard deviation excee[ding the mean in hemodialysis patients (1110.6 ± 1747 ng/L), and a recent meta-analysis evaluating preptin in polycystic ovary syndrome identified conflicting results across studies, partly attributable to methodological heterogeneity in measurement conditions [[16], [17]].

This pattern of high biological variability is biologically plausible: preptin is co-secreted with insulin in a glucose-dependent and pulsatile fashion, rendering circulating levels susceptible to transient fluctuations in metabolic state, nutritional status, and physical activity — factors that are difficult to fully standardize in a clinical study setting. Consequently, single time-point measurements may inadequately capture an individual's representative preptin exposure, potentially attenuating any true associations with clinical outcomes. This inherent limitation of systemic preptin measurement, independent of sample size, represents a fundamental challenge for its utility as a circulating biomarker. We therefore suggest that future investigations incorporate repeated fasting measurements, preptin-to-insulin ratios, or tissue-level quantification to more accurately characterize preptin biology in the oncologic context.

Preptin has been more extensively studied in the context of bone metabolism. In a study by Li et al., the relationship between preptin-associated obesity, reduced osteocalcin levels, and coronary artery calcification was investigated, and a significant association was identified. Furthermore, the relationship between preptin and various metabolic parameters was evaluated in the same study, with no significant correlations observed with age, BMI, insulin, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, calcium, or phosphorus levels [17]. In our cohort, no significant associations were observed between preptin levels and metabolic or biochemical parameters.

The ROC analysis demonstrated no meaningful discriminatory performance of serum preptin for identifying bone metastasis (AUC = 0.525, 95% CI 0.357–0.693). Nevertheless, the relatively wide confidence interval reflects the limited precision of the estimate and may be attributable, at least in part, to the modest sample size. Therefore, although our findings do not support a clinically relevant role for circulating preptin as a biomarker of bone metastasis, the possibility of a type II error cannot be completely excluded. Accordingly, the absence of a statistically significant association in the present study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that no biological relationship exists between circulating preptin and bone metastasis.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, baseline PSA level, regional lymph node metastasis, and T stage emerged as the primary determinants of bone metastasis in our cohort, consistent with their well-established roles in prostate cancer staging and risk stratification. The borderline association of T stage (overall p = 0.052) likely reflects statistical constraints imposed by the limited sample size rather than a true absence of independent effect, given its strong univariable significance (p = 0.006) and established biological plausibility.

Several host-related inflammatory and nutritional parameters also showed borderline or significant associations with bone metastasis in univariable analysis. Hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in the metastatic group, most likely reflecting marrow infiltration and chronic disease-related anemia rather than an independent causal relationship. Similarly, borderline differences observed in albumin and platelet counts may reflect the systemic inflammatory burden associated with advanced disease stage. These findings underscore the influence of host-related factors on the metastatic phenotype, although their independent predictive value warrants confirmation in larger cohorts.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of patients with osteoporosis, may have reduced the statistical power of subgroup and ROC analyses and limited the robustness of multivariable modeling. Given the number of metastatic events, the possibility of model overfitting cannot be entirely excluded.

Second, although this study was designed prospectively, the analysis was based on baseline cross-sectional data, which precludes any inference regarding causal relationships or the predictive value of preptin for future metastatic progression. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify its potential temporal association with disease evolution.

Third, circulating preptin measurements may not fully capture local tumor–bone interactions.

Fourth, although all patients underwent 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT, which is currently the most sensitive imaging modality for detecting bone metastases in prostate cancer, the possibility of occult micrometastatic disease below the detection threshold cannot be completely excluded. Such undetected lesions may have resulted in misclassification of a small number of patients as metastasis-negative and could have attenuated potential differences in circulating preptin levels between the study groups.

Finally, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. External validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is required to confirm these results and better define the clinical relevance of preptin as a biomarker in prostate cancer.

In conclusion, this prospective exploratory study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between serum preptin levels, bone metabolism, and bone metastasis in patients with prostate cancer. A key strength of our study is the assessment of preptin levels prior to the initiation of systemic therapy, minimizing the potential confounding effects of androgen deprivation therapy and other treatments. Overall, our findings suggest that serum preptin levels are not significantly associated with bone metastasis, bone mineral density, or metabolic parameters in prostate cancer patients, indicating limited clinical utility as a biomarker in this context. Further larger-scale, longitudinal, and tissue-level studies are needed to better define the potential role of preptin in cancer biology.
