BMI and PNI as predictors of treatment completion and survival in locally advanced HNSCC receiving sequential chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Esra Zeynelgil, Engin Eren Kavak, İsmail Dilli, Özlem Aydın İsak, Doğan Yazılıtaş, Gökşen İnanç İmamoğlu, Ömer Bayır
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and laboratory factors affecting treatment response and treatment tolerance in patients diagnosed with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and treated with chemoradiotherapy (CRT) after three cycles of induction chemotherapy.Method Patients who were followed up in the oncology clinic between January 2014 and December 2024 and who could not undergo an organ-sparing approach or surgery were analyzed retrospectively. Demographic, clinical, biochemical and inflammatory parameters of the patients were examined. Binary-logistic regression analysis was used for patients who could not complete the treatment due to toxicity, Cox-regression analysis was used to investigate the factors affecting overall survival (OS), and Roc-curve analysis was used to determine the ideal-cut-off values ​​for blood markers. ResultsA total of 92 patients with HNSCC were included in the study. In univariate logistic regression analysis, age ≥ 60 (p = 0.006), presence of comorbidity (p = 0.029), body mass index (BMI) < 23.25 (p = 0.005), poor ECOG performance score (2–3) (p < 0.001) and low prognostic nutritional index (PNI) (p = 0.011) were found to be significant risk factors for not completing treatment. Multivariate logistic regression of age, BMI, ECOG, and PNI together formed a predictive model for treatment incompletion.In Cox regression analysis, BMI <23.25 (p=0.016), poor ECOG performance score (2-3) (p=0.002), advanced disease stage (p=0.002), and low PNI (<51.1) (p=0.006) were the main risk factors for unfavorable overall survival. Gender, smoking, tumor location, treatment regimen, and hematological parameters had no significant effect on survival and treatment completion.ConclusionIn HNSCC patients who underwent post-induction CRT, nutritional parameters such as BMI and PNI and performance status play a determining role on treatment tolerance and survival. Detailed assessment of nutritional status before treatment may have an impact on treatment success and survival.

Introduction

Head and neck cancers constitute approximately 5–6% of male cancers in the United States, and despite developing treatment modalities, they constitute approximately 5% of male cancer-related deaths and approximately 1.5% of female cancer-related deaths [1]. Head and neck cancers usually begin in the squamous cells that cover the moist mucosal surfaces of the head and neck and are generally known as head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) [2]. Chronic exposure to toxic agents represents a primary etiological factor in cancer development. Among the various risk factors, tobacco use constitutes the most significant cause, while alcohol consumption and exposure to oncogenic viruses also contribute to carcinogenesis [3–5].

Histologically, squamous cell carcinoma accounts for approximately 90% of all head and neck cancers [6]. In patients with locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck region, chemotherapy has been integrated into various multimodality treatment approaches to improve treatment outcomes and functional results [7]. At the time of diagnosis, approximately 47% of cases are seen as loco-regional and 20% have a metastatic stage [8].

In the treatment of HNSCC, concurrent use of chemotherapy and radiotherapy is particularly prominent in cases where organ function preservation is prioritized or surgery is not feasible. These treatment modalities may be administered as concurrent (concomitant) chemoradiotherapy (CRT) or as sequential chemotherapy followed by radiotherapy [9, 10]. Decisions regarding the optimal sequencing and selection of surgery, radiotherapy, and/or chemotherapy require the consensus of a multidisciplinary board.

Chemoradiotherapy treatment describes the simultaneous administration of radiotherapy and chemotherapy. However, in certain cases, it also includes a sequential treatment approach that includes induction chemotherapy followed by chemoradiotherapy [11, 12]. Induction chemotherapy administered before CRT may increase the efficacy of radiotherapy or CRT by reducing tumor size, strengthening local-regional control, and contributing to the preservation of organ functions [9]. Induction chemotherapy allows early treatment of subclinical distant metastatic disease, especially in advanced-stage patients with lymph node involvement and a high risk of distant metastasis [13]. A positive initial response to induction chemotherapy may predict the efficacy of more advanced functional organ-sparing therapy such as chemoradiotherapy. In addition, a poor response to induction chemotherapy allows continuation of treatment with a different chemotherapy.

Although the MACH-NC meta-analysis by Pignon et al. showed that CRT increased overall survival, the effect of pretreatment induction chemotherapy remained unclear [10]. One of the major reasons for this was the toxicity induced by this treatment. Toxicity is an important treatment limitation in post-induction CRT, and a cumulative effect occurs with radiotherapy [14]. Therefore, there is a decrease in quality of life, treatment failure, and indirectly a decrease in survival [15]. It is of clinical importance to determine which patients with toxicity are candidates for this strategy.

This study aimed to evaluate BMI and PNI as potential predictors of treatment completion and survival in patients with locally advanced HNSCC treated with sequential chemoradiotherapy.

Material method

Patient characteristics

Patients who were followed up in the oncology clinic between January 2014 and December 2024, diagnosed with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), and were scheduled for chemoradiotherapy (CRT) after induction chemotherapy due to an organ-sparing approach or resection not being possible were included in this study. Data were obtained retrospectively. Patients received three cycles of induction chemotherapy with Docetaxel (75 mg/m² on day 1), Cisplatin (75 mg/m² on day 1), and 5-Fluorouracil (750 mg/m²/day as a continuous infusion on days 1–5), repeated every 21 days, followed by definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy with radiotherapy delivering a total radiation dose of 65 to 75 Gy. (2 Gy/day, 5 days per week) administered concurrently with Cisplatin (100 mg/m² on days 1, 22, and 43 intravenously or 40 mg/m² once a week). All clinical data of the patients included in the study were collected retrospectively through the hospital electronic record system and entered into the database. Clinical staging of the patients was determined by the multidisciplinary tumor board from PET-CT or contrast-enhanced CT films taken before the start of chemotherapy. Other laboratory data were obtained by calculating from the blood samples taken before the first chemotherapy application. Comorbidities were defined as diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, and history of coronary artery disease.

Response to treatment was evaluated with radiological imaging methods (CT or MR) based on (Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumors v.1.1 (RECIST) criteria. Toxicity during the treatment process was determined using the v.5 Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) scale. In the first stage, patients were given 3 cycles of induction chemotherapy, standard chemotherapy regimens were preferred and dose adjustments were made in accordance with international guidelines. Patients who responded to induction therapy or were stabilized were treated with concurrent CRT. Patients with a history of previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy, those with an inflammatory disease, those with a second malignancy, and those for whom the first blood test could not be obtained were excluded from the study.

Additionally, the following inflammatory biomarkers were calculated using a complete blood count and biochemical parameters taken before treatment:

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 24 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Survival analyses were performed utilizing the Kaplan-Meier approach, and differences between groups were assessed via the log-rank test. To evaluate factors influencing treatment response, binary logistic regression analysis was performed using the enter method following univariate analysis. Variables that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were added to the multivariate analysis and analyzed with the Forward-LR stepwise method, and the last significant step was presented as the final model. The Cox proportional hazards model was used for univariate analysis of variables impacting survival. Hazard ratios (HR) with associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up were censored at that date. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, data from 92 HNSCC patients who received CRT after induction chemotherapy were analyzed. 12 patients (13%) could not receive chemotherapy despite being planned, and a total of 14 patients (15.2%) could not complete the ideal treatment course (induction + CRT) due to toxicity and progression. Patients aged 60 years and over had an increased risk of approximately 9 times, patients with co-morbidities had an increased risk of 4 times, and patients with ECOG 2–3 had an increased risk of approximately 28 times. Patients with BMI < 23.25 and PNI < 51.1 had poorer treatment tolerability. In terms of overall survival, BMI ≥ 23.25 (HR:0.33) and PNI ≥ 51.1 (HR:0.26) were associated with improved overall survival, while ECOG ≥ 2 (HR:5.36) and increased stage (HR:2.67) were poor prognostic indicators.

Induction therapy in head and neck cancer was first investigated in patients with larynx cancer by the VALCSG study, and it was shown to be equivalent to post-induction radiotherapy compared to post-surgical radiotherapy [16]. The similarly designed EORTC 24,891 study found similar 5-year outcomes between post-induction RT and post-surgical RT in hypopharyngeal cancer [17].

To date, chemoradiotherapy (CRT) has been validated by numerous studies as an effective modality in patients with unresectable head and neck cancer or those considered for organ-preserving approaches. In the phase III 2503 Trial conducted by the TTCC group, which compared induction chemotherapy followed by CRT versus upfront CRT, no significant difference in progression-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS) was observed between the arms [18]. However, when patients who were unable to complete the planned treatment were excluded from the analysis, a PFS advantage was noted for the induction plus CRT arm, with the greatest benefit seen in patients with good ECOG performance status and those with laryngeal or hypopharyngeal tumors [18]. In the phase III study by Cohen et al., induction chemotherapy was shown to be beneficial in the subgroup of patients with N2c and N3 disease, although the small number of patients in this group represented a significant limitation [19]. In that study, 3.5% of patients died due to intolerance to induction chemotherapy, and 17.4% (n= 24) discontinued the planned treatment protocol [19]. In our study, 15.2% of patients were unable to complete the treatment process, and when a predictive model for treatment intolerance was established, age, BMI, ECOG performance status, and PNI were identified as predictive factors for toxicity.

Prognostic nutritional index (PNI), which is an indirect indicator of systemic inflammation, together with malnutrition and its effect on treatment tolerance and prognosis, has been investigated in many types of cancer [20, 21]. In their study on head and neck cancer patients receiving induction and/or concomitant chemotherapy, Fanetti et al. found the median PNI to be 50and concluded that low PNI was associated with both poor prognosis and increased toxicity [20]. George et al. found that low PNI was predictive of treatment completion and that other inflammatory markers (NLR, PLR) were not predictive in their study on 82 patients with head and neck cancer [22]. Bruixola et al. showed that PNI was an independent predictive marker for patients who received CRT after induction [23]. Chang et al. found that pretreatment PNI was a predictive marker for treatment tolerance and toxicity in their study [24]. McRackan et al. found that low BMI was associated with an increased risk of treatment discontinuation and decreased early disease recurrence and overall survival in patients [25]. In a cohort study including 2,204 patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), of whom 81.1% received radiotherapy, Yang et al. found that low BMI was significantly associated with poorer survival outcomes [26]. Higher BMI could function as a buffer against therapy-related nutritional decline from side effects such as mucositis, swallowing difficulties, and appetite loss, ultimately affecting overall survival [27, 28]. In our study, we concluded that both BMI and PNI individually predicted treatment tolerance and also demonstrated that PNI was a marker for prognosis. These results indicate that a detailed assessment of nutritional status is essential in patients with head and neck cancer.

The biological, immunological, metabolic, and inflammatory mechanisms underlying the predictive significance of BMI and PNI for treatment-related toxicity and prognostic significance for survival are complex and multifactorial. Tumor-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha play a crucial role in this process [29]. These mediators contribute to a clinical picture characterized by loss of appetite, increased basal metabolic rate, and muscle catabolism [30]. Inflammation exerts multiple metabolic effects; cytokines are known to induce insulin resistance, suppress appetite, and inhibit the entry of nutrients into cells [31, 32]. As a result of the cytokine storm, a progressive decline in BMI is observed, accompanied by increased frailty among patients [33].

Malnutrition often occurs as a result of this inflammatory response and leads to decreased serum albumin levels [34]. This decrease may alter the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of various drugs, increasing the free fraction of chemotherapy agents and thus increasing the risk of toxicity [35, 36]. Additionally, malnutrition indirectly suppresses the immune system, leading to the development of lymphopenia and decreased host defense capacity [37]. Cytokines are associated with effects such as insulin resistance, decreased appetite, and blocking the entry of nutrients into cells [32]. All these interconnected processes reveal the complex relationship between inflammation, nutritional status, and clinical outcomes in patients with malignancy.

There were some limitations regarding our study. One of the limitations was the retrospective loss. Another limitation was that Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and human papillomavirus (HPV), which affect head and neck cancers, could not be included in our study due to the small number of patients tested. Additionally, the ROC-derived cutoff values ​​determined in this study (BMI 23.25 and PNI 51.1) may not be universally applicable and representative. These cutoffs require external validation in independent cohorts. However, the fact that the study was conducted in a single center and in a homogeneous population as much as possible, and that comprehensive analyses were provided for the ITT population, makes our study strong.

As a result, we concluded that nutritional status, ECOG, and age were associated with treatment toxicity in head and neck cancer patients receiving post-induction CRT. Additionally, good nutritional status (BMI ≥ 23.25 and PNI ≥ 51.1) was linked to a favorable prognosis. Future studies should investigate the effects of nutritional interventions on survival and toxicity.
