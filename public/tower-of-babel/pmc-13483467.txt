A comprehensive benchmark of multi-generation YOLO architectures for forest species identification from macroscopic wood images
Authors: Jandrei Sartori Spancerski, Pedro Luiz de Paula Filho, Mauricio Kugler, Fabio Kurt Schneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate identification of forest species from wood samples is essential for combating illegal logging and supporting sustainable wood trade. This study benchmarks 15 deep learning models from three YOLO generations (YOLOv8, YOLO11, and YOLO26), each in five sizes (nano through extra-large), to classify 73 forest species from macroscopic wood images. A dataset of 77,865 patches (448 × 448 pixels) was assembled from three heterogeneous image capture protocols with spatial resolution normalization. Four models achieved a test accuracy of 99.87% (MCC = 0.9987), with YOLO26-medium offering the best efficiency at 232.5 frames per second. All 15 models reached 100% Top-3 accuracy, and error analysis revealed that misclassifications were confined to individual patches. Image-level majority voting across patches yielded 100% accuracy for YOLO26-medium on test images. Multi-scale RISE saliency maps confirmed that the models attend to anatomically relevant features, such as pore arrangements and parenchyma patterns, while t-SNE analysis showed that YOLO26 produces the best-separated feature spaces. An out-of-distribution detection evaluation using three complementary methods (Maximum Softmax Probability, energy-based scoring, and Mahalanobis distance) confirmed strong model discriminability against both general out-of-domain content and unseen wood species acquired under the same protocols. These results establish a new benchmark for macroscopic wood species identification and suggest that medium-sized YOLO models are promising candidates for future deployment on resource-constrained devices for field inspection.Keywords: Forest species, Classification, Deep learning, Computer vision, YOLOSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology

Introduction

Accurate identification of forest species is a fundamental requirement for sustainable forest management, biodiversity monitoring, and enforcement of regulations governing wood trade. In species-rich biomes, such as tropical and subtropical forests, the capacity to distinguish among wood species, including both closely related taxa that share anatomical features due to common ancestry and taxonomically distant species that may exhibit convergent macroscopic patterns, directly supports efforts to suppress illegal logging, verify the legality of traded wood, and comply with international instruments, such as the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)1. Despite its recognized importance, species-level identification of sawn wood remains a technically demanding task, since wood lacks the external morphological markers (leaves, flowers, bark, and fruit) that facilitate botanical classification2.

Conventional wood identification relies on the expertise of wood anatomists who examine anatomical features visible on prepared transverse surfaces using hand lenses or stereomicroscopes. On the cross-sectional plane, the principal diagnostic characters include vessel (pore) arrangement, diameter, and frequency; distribution and type of axial parenchyma; ray width, height, and spacing; and the nature of growth ring boundaries3,4. Although experienced specialists can achieve high accuracy using this approach, several practical limitations constrain its scalability. The limited availability of specialists in wood anatomy creates persistent bottlenecks in inspection and enforcement workflows, whereas the inherently subjective nature of visual assessment introduces inter-observer variability that compromises reproducibility5,6.

Automated methods for forest species identification have been explored along two principal lines of investigation: spectral analysis and image-based classification. Spectral techniques, including Raman spectroscopy7, fluorescence spectroscopy8, and Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS)9, characterize the chemical composition of wood through its interaction with electromagnetic radiation. Although these approaches offer high discriminative power under laboratory conditions, their reliance on specialized and often expensive equipment, controlled acquisition environments, and skilled operators limits their deployment in field scenarios, such as roadside wood inspections or on-site environmental monitoring10. Image-based methods, particularly those leveraging macroscopic images acquired at magnifications of up to 10×, offer practical advantages in terms of portability, acquisition speed, and cost-effectiveness, making them more suitable for large-scale in-situ applications11.

The advent of deep learning has substantially advanced the state-of-the-art in image-based wood identification. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) learn hierarchical feature representations, progressing from low-level edges and contours in the initial layers to complex spatial arrangements in deeper layers12. This hierarchical capacity has proven particularly effective for texture recognition tasks, where CNN-based representations consistently outperform classical descriptors such as LBP, GLCM, and SIFT across established benchmarks13. Since wood species discrimination relies fundamentally on textural patterns formed by the spatial configuration of anatomical elements rather than isolated pixel-level attributes, CNNs constitute a natural architectural choice for this domain.

In the broader forestry domain, deep learning has been applied with considerable success to a range of visual recognition problems beyond wood anatomy. For instance, Lou et al.14 demonstrated the effectiveness of Faster R-CNN, YOLOv3, and SSD for measuring loblolly pine crown widths from drone imagery, while Li et al.15 developed a spatio-temporal multi-scale fusion algorithm based on YOLOv8 for detecting pine wood nematode disease in UAV remote sensing images. Recently, Xu et al.16 proposed YOLO-DS, an enhanced YOLOv8n framework incorporating SPDconv and Mamba modules, for identifying desert shrub species and estimating vegetation coverage. In the specific context of wood analysis, Xie et al.17 combined a modified YOLOv8n-seg architecture with an improved VTK pipeline for the three-dimensional reconstruction of internal wood defects from computed tomography scans. These studies confirm the versatility of YOLO family architectures across diverse forestry applications.

Although these examples illustrate the breadth of deep learning in forestry, the specific problem of wood species recognition from anatomical images has followed its own trajectory. Early studies employed handcrafted feature descriptors such as Local Binary Patterns (LBP), Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM), and Gabor filters combined with Support Vector Machines (SVMs), achieving recognition rates of up to 97.77% on macroscopic datasets18 and 93.03% on microscopic datasets19. The transition to CNN-based approaches yielded further gains, with subsequent works reporting accuracies of 95.77% for macroscopic and 97.32% for microscopic images using relatively shallow architectures20. More recent efforts employing transfer learning with deeper networks have pushed these figures higher: de Geus et al.21 achieved 98.75% on microscopic cross-sectional images using DenseNet, while Ravindran et al.5 reported 97.7% on macroscopic wood and 98.7% on charcoal images through the XyloTron platform. The highest macroscopic recognition rate prior to this work was 98.73%, obtained by de Oliveira22 using a multi-stage CNN pipeline on a dataset of 41 Brazilian species.

Despite these advances, several directions for further investigation remain. First, most existing studies have focused on a relatively small number of species under controlled acquisition conditions, and generalisation to larger, more heterogeneous datasets that better reflect field variability has received limited attention. Second, while recent studies have applied individual YOLO variants to forestry tasks such as tree crown measurement14, disease detection15, and wood defect analysis17, and initial efforts have explored YOLO for wood type classification23, a systematic comparison spanning multiple YOLO generations and model sizes for multi-species wood identification has not been reported. Third, although interpretability techniques have been applied in related areas of wood analysis24,25, their systematic use to elucidate the anatomical basis of classification decisions across multiple architectures has not been addressed.

In this context, the present study investigates the application of three generations of the YOLO architecture, namely YOLOv8, YOLO11, and YOLO26, to the classification of 73 forest species from macroscopic wood images acquired under three distinct acquisition protocols. To this end, a unified dataset was assembled from three heterogeneous sources with varying imaging devices, illumination, and spatial resolution, and each architecture was evaluated across all five available model sizes (nano through extra-large), yielding a total of 15 trained models. Beyond conventional accuracy metrics, the evaluation framework incorporates the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) to account for class imbalance, three complementary Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection methods (Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP), energy-based scoring, and Mahalanobis distance) to assess model robustness against unknown species and non-wood content, and an interpretability analysis combining multi-scale RISE saliency maps with t-SNE feature space visualizations. This combination of architectural benchmarking, robustness assessment, and anatomically grounded interpretability has not been previously reported for macroscopic wood species identification, and is intended to narrow the distance between laboratory-scale classification experiments and the practical requirements of field-deployable inspection systems (Project page: https://jandrei.dev/projects/wood-species-identification).

Handcrafted feature approaches

The earliest automated wood identification systems relied on manually designed feature descriptors combined with classical machine learning classifiers. Paula Filho et al.18 constructed a dataset of 2,942 macroscopic images from 41 Brazilian forest species and proposed a divide-and-conquer classification protocol based on color and texture features extracted through techniques such as GLCM, LBP, LPQ, and Gabor filters. Using pairwise SVMs with low-level and high-level fusion strategies, they achieved a recognition rate of 97.77%. In a complementary microscopic study, Martins et al.19 investigated the identification of 112 forest species using 2,240 images, employing a combination of SIFT, SURF, LBP, LPQ, GLCM, and Gabor descriptors with SVM classifiers, and reported a recognition rate of 93.03%.

Souza et al.26 explored early and late fusion of LBP operators with SVM classifiers on a dataset of 1,901 macroscopic images encompassing 46 Brazilian species, reaching an accuracy of 97.67%. More recently, Sugiarto et al.27 proposed a Random Forest-based approach that relied on pore distribution features extracted from macroscopic images, achieving 92.00% accuracy on six species. While these methods demonstrated the discriminative potential of texture-based representations, their reliance on manually engineered features limits their adaptability to new species and acquisition conditions.

Deep learning and transfer learning methods

The transition from handcrafted features to learned representations marked a turning point in wood species identification. Hafemann et al.20 were among the first to apply CNNs to this task, evaluating a shallow architecture with two convolutional layers and three fully connected layers on the datasets introduced by Paula Filho et al.18 and Martins et al.19. Their model achieved recognition rates of 95.77% for macroscopic and 97.32% for microscopic images, confirming the potential of deep learning for wood recognition.

Subsequent works leveraged transfer learning with deeper architectures to further improve classification performance. De Geus et al.21 introduced a large microscopic dataset comprising 281 species captured in transverse, radial, and tangential sections, and reported a classification accuracy of 98.75% for cross-sectional images using DenseNet with transfer learning. Lopes et al.28 proposed an InceptionV4_ResNetV2-based model to identify hardwood species from smartphone-acquired macroscopic images, achieving an adjusted accuracy of 92.60% on 10 species. Ravindran et al.5 presented the XyloTron platform, which integrates a dedicated imaging system with visible and ultraviolet illumination and open-source deep learning software for macroscopic wood and charcoal identification, reporting accuracies of 97.7% for wood and 98.7% for charcoal.

De Oliveira22 compared traditional texture-based methods (GLCM combined with a multilayer perceptron) with CNN-based approaches on macroscopic and microscopic datasets, achieving the highest reported accuracies at that time: 98.73% for macroscopic and 99.11% for microscopic images through a multi-stage classification pipeline.

More recent studies have expanded the scope of deep learning for wood identification. He et al.29 trained and evaluated nine CNN architectures on two macroscopic wood image datasets, with their proposed network achieving a 98.81% test recognition rate on a 41-species dataset after iterative training. Sun et al.30 proposed a transfer learning method based on ResNet50 feature extraction for identifying 25 wood species from limited data, reporting 99.6% accuracy. Ergun31 conducted a comprehensive evaluation of nine pre-trained deep networks (including ResNet18, GoogLeNet, VGG19, DenseNet201, and ShuffleNet) with transfer learning on macroscopic images of 10 Turkish wood species, demonstrating that deep network models are effective for species recognition, with ShuffleNet offering competitive accuracy at reduced computational cost. Kırbaş and Çifci32 compared transfer learning performance of ResNet50, InceptionV3, Xception, and VGG19 on the WOOD-AUTH dataset of 12 species, finding that Xception achieved the highest classification accuracy.

In the microscopic domain, Zheng et al.24 collected 850 images from the cross and tangential sections of 15 endangered tree species and achieved 99.3% classification accuracy using deep learning. They employed feature visualization methods (Grad-CAM) to reveal that axial parenchyma arrangements and vessel distribution in cross sections, along with wood rays in tangential sections, were the primary features underlying model decisions. Liu et al.25 investigated the discrimination of seven closely related Pterocarpus species using four CNN architectures (ResNet-50, ResNet-152, WideResNet-50, and SEResNet-50) trained on microscopic cross-sectional images with specimen-level data splitting, and used Grad-CAM to visualize the anatomical features captured by the models.

Kim et al.33 examined the performance and influencing factors of CNN-based classification for six Korean Quercus species, finding that data augmentation was the primary factor affecting classification accuracy. Ma et al.34 validated the practical accuracy of CNN-based identification for 50 Japanese hardwood species, highlighting that segregated data splitting (distinct specimens for training, validation, and testing) produces substantially lower but more realistic accuracy estimates than non-segregated approaches.

Herrera-Poyatos et al.35 proposed the TDLI-PIV methodology, which exploits patch-based inference voting on high-resolution macroscopic images to capture fine-grained wood patterns. This approach, evaluated on the GOIMAI-Phase-I dataset, demonstrated that patching strategies can substantially improve classification accuracy compared to conventional whole-image resizing, an observation that is consistent with the patch-based preprocessing adopted in the present study.

YOLO-family architectures in forestry

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) family of architectures has gained increasing attention across computer vision domains due to its favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. Although early YOLO generations were designed exclusively for object detection, the three architectures evaluated in the present study were developed as unified multi-task frameworks that natively support five distinct vision tasks: object detection, instance segmentation, image classification, keypoint-based pose estimation, and multi-object tracking36–38 (Overview of task modes supported by the Ultralytics YOLO family: https://docs.ultralytics.com/tasks/). Image classification is implemented as a dedicated task mode in which the convolutional backbone extracts hierarchical feature representations from the input image and a lightweight classification head produces class probabilities via a softmax layer, with no dependence on the localization components of the architecture. This multi-task design, documented across the three generations evaluated here36–38, is precisely what enables their direct application to species-level classification without any structural modification.

Lorenzo et al.23 deployed a YOLOv8-based model on a Raspberry Pi 4B to classify four wood types from 400 macroscopic images, achieving 72.50% accuracy with performance strongly affected by lighting conditions. More broadly, Li et al.15 developed a spatio-temporal multi-scale fusion algorithm built upon YOLOv8 for detecting pine wood nematode disease from UAV imagery, demonstrating the architecture’s adaptability to complex forestry detection tasks. Xu et al.16 proposed YOLO-DS, an enhanced YOLOv8n framework incorporating SPDconv and Mamba modules for desert shrub identification and coverage estimation. In the domain of wood defect analysis, Xie et al.17 combined a modified YOLOv8n-seg model with an improved VTK pipeline for three-dimensional reconstruction of internal wood defects from computed tomography scans.

Beyond YOLOv8, the YOLO family has continued to evolve. YOLO11, released in 2024, introduced hybrid task assignment and efficiency-focused modules37, while YOLO26, released in September 2025, introduced the MuSGD optimizer for more stable training convergence, along with architectural simplifications that improve inference speed on edge devices38. Despite the growing adoption of YOLO architectures in forestry, no prior study has systematically compared multiple YOLO generations across their full range of model sizes (nano through extra-large) for the task of wood species classification from macroscopic images.

Model interpretability and robustness

As deep learning models achieve increasingly high classification accuracies, the need to understand the anatomical basis of their decisions has become a pressing concern, particularly for applications in regulated domains such as wood trade enforcement. Zheng et al.24 applied Grad-CAM to microscopic wood images and identified axial parenchyma and vessel arrangements as the features most strongly associated with correct classification of endangered species. Liu et al.25 extended this approach to closely related Pterocarpus species, using Grad-CAM to reveal that the models attended to species-specific anatomical patterns in the transverse plane. In a different application context, Liu et al.39 employed a ResNet model with an embedded self-attention mechanism to discriminate CITES-listed species from their look-alikes, achieving 95.6% accuracy on a dataset of 28 species and using attention maps to highlight diagnostically relevant image regions.

Despite these advances, most interpretability studies have focused on Grad-CAM and related gradient-based methods. Alternative approaches such as RISE (Randomized Input Sampling for Explanation)40, which generates model-agnostic saliency maps by systematically masking input regions, remain unexplored in the wood identification literature. Similarly, the use of t-SNE41 for visualizing learned feature spaces and assessing inter-species separability has received limited attention in this domain.

Regarding robustness, the capacity of classification models to reject unknown or OOD samples is required for field deployment, where the model may encounter species or materials not represented in the training set. Although OOD detection has been extensively studied in the broader machine learning literature, its application to wood species identification remains largely unaddressed, representing a dimension that the present study directly addresses.

Summary and positioning of the present study

Table 1 consolidates the main results reported in the image-based wood species identification literature. Several observations emerge from this survey. First, the number of species evaluated in most studies remains relatively low (typically fewer than 50), with few exceptions exceeding 100 species. Second, the vast majority of works have relied on a single homogeneous dataset, limiting the assessment of generalization capacity. Third, while deep learning approaches have progressively outperformed handcrafted feature methods, the most recent architectures from the YOLO family have not been systematically evaluated for this task. Fourth, interpretability and OOD robustness remain underexplored dimensions.

The present study responds to these observations directly. The use of three heterogeneous datasets encompassing 73 species expands the scope beyond the small, homogeneous corpora typical of the literature. The systematic evaluation of 15 models across three YOLO generations and five model sizes fills the gap in multi-generation architectural comparison for wood species classification. The integration of MCC, three OOD detection methods, and a combined RISE and t-SNE interpretability analysis addresses the underexplored dimensions of robustness and explainability identified in the discusions.

Materials and methods

Figure 1 presents the experimental pipeline adopted in this study. The following subsections detail each stage.

Overview of the experimental pipeline. Original images from three datasets are first subjected to scale calibration to normalize spatial resolution, then subdivided into 448×448 patches with 20% overlap. The patches are split into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets using a stratified 3-fold repeated split. Fifteen YOLO models (three architectures × five sizes) are trained with Mosaic augmentation and evaluated using classification metrics, OOD detection, RISE saliency maps, and t-SNE visualization.

Datasets

Three macroscopic wood image datasets were combined to form a unified corpus for species classification, each reflecting a distinct acquisition protocol. The DS1 dataset contains 2,942 images of 41 species at a resolution of 3264×2448 pixels, acquired with a Sony DSC-T20 digital camera43 under controlled laboratory conditions. The DS2 dataset comprises 2380 images of 40 species at 2080×1540 pixels, also captured in a laboratory environment with a Zeiss Discovery V12 stereomicroscope44. The DS3 dataset consists of 6,104 images of 72 species at 640×480 pixels, obtained with a HAIZ USB digital microscope45 and provided by the Technical-Scientific Sector of the Brazilian Federal Police in the Amazon region. Figure 2 illustrates the variability in image appearance across the three acquisition protocols for the same species.

Sample of images from the DS1 (a), DS2 (b) and DS3 (c) databases of the species Hymenolobium petraeum.

Although DS3 relies on the most modest imaging hardware of the three sources, its acquisition protocol enforces a standardized strict procedure. The device must be pressed directly against the wood surface, adjusting the working distance and eliminating perspective variability. The microscope housing blocks ambient illumination, and the integrated LED ring constitutes the sole light source, ensuring uniform and reproducible lighting across all acquisitions. These characteristics make DS3 the most representative of real field inspection conditions among the three datasets, as its images reflect the protocol employed by the Brazilian Federal Police during on-site wood enforcement operations. However, images from DS3 are distributed across all three subsets of the unified corpus, so the models encounter this acquisition protocol during training.

The three datasets jointly encompass 106 forest species, of which 73 met the minimum threshold of 50 original images required for inclusion in the final corpus. All images were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets at the image level prior to any patch extraction, so that all patches derived from a given source image are confined to a single partition. The source datasets do not provide specimen-level identifiers linking individual images to the physical wood block from which they were acquired. This limitation is common to most publicly available wood image corpora and is discussed alongside related methodological considerations in section “Methodological considerations”. After the scale calibration and patch extraction procedures described in section “Scale calibration and patch-based preprocessing”, the corpus yielded a total of 77,865 patches. A complete catalog of the 73 retained species, together with the per-dataset image counts and resulting patch totals, is provided in Appendix A.

Scale calibration and patch-based preprocessing

A central challenge arising from the integration of three datasets acquired with different devices is the heterogeneity in spatial resolution. Images from DS1, DS2, and DS3 exhibit substantially different pixel densities, meaning that the same anatomical structure (e.g., a vessel or a parenchyma band) occupies a different number of pixels across datasets. If left unaddressed, this inconsistency could introduce a confounding factor, as the models might learn to distinguish acquisition sources rather than genuine anatomical differences between species.

To mitigate this issue, a scale calibration procedure was implemented prior to patch extraction. A reference scale was established by identifying the dataset with the lowest spatial resolution (i.e., the smallest number of pixels per unit area of wood surface). For each dataset, a calibration factor was computed as the ratio between the reference pixel density and the pixel density of the source dataset. This factor was then applied to resize all images to a common effective spatial resolution before any further processing. The calibration parameters (original resolution, target pixel density, and scaling factor) were stored in a JSON configuration file to ensure reproducibility.

Following scale calibration, each resized image was subdivided into non-overlapping and overlapping patches of 448×448 pixels. An overlap ratio of 20% was applied between adjacent patches, corresponding to a stride of 358 pixels along both spatial axes. This overlap ensures that anatomical structures located near patch boundaries are fully captured in at least one patch, reducing the risk of information loss at the edges. The patch size of 448×448 was selected to balance two competing requirements: providing sufficient spatial context for the models to capture discriminative textural patterns, and maintaining compatibility with the input resolutions natively supported by the YOLO architectures evaluated in this study. Cross-validation strategy

A stratified repeated split scheme was adopted to ensure statistical robustness and to preserve the class distribution across partitions. Three independent folds were constructed with disjoint test sets, each following a 70/15/15 allocation of images into training, validation, and test subsets, respectively. Because patch counts per image vary substantially across acquisition protocols, the resulting patch-level proportions differ from the image-level ratios. The test set of 8,640 patches corresponds to approximately 15% of the original images rather than 15% of the total patch count.

Fold assignment was performed at the image level. Each image was allocated exclusively to one fold before any patch extraction took place, so that all patches derived from a given source image are confined to a single partition. A patch-level split would allow patches from the same source image to appear simultaneously in training and test sets, artificially inflating performance estimates.

Within each fold, images were distributed into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets at the image level, with stratification applied at the species level to maintain approximately equal class proportions across all three partitions. Patches were then extracted exclusively from the images assigned to each subset, following the procedure described in section “Scale calibration and patch-based preprocessing”. All 15 models were trained and evaluated independently on each fold, and the results reported in this study correspond to the fold that achieved the highest overall performance. Mean and standard deviation across the three folds are reported alongside the best-fold results in Table 3, providing a measure of inter-fold consistency.

Data augmentation

To mitigate class imbalance and improve generalization, a comprehensive data augmentation pipeline was employed during training through the Ultralytics framework36–38. Mosaic augmentation was applied with a probability of 100% during the first training epochs, combining four randomly selected images into a single composite image to increase variability in scale, context, and spatial arrangement46. This technique was automatically disabled during the final 10 epochs (close_mosaic parameter) to allow the model to fine-tune on unmodified samples.

Additional augmentation strategies included random erasing with a probability of 40%, horizontal flipping with a probability of 50%, and random scaling with a factor of ±50%. Color-space augmentations were also applied through HSV adjustments, with hue shift of 1.5%, saturation variation of 70%, and value variation of 40%. These techniques collectively enhance model robustness and reduce overfitting, particularly in scenarios with limited or imbalanced data. Mixup augmentation was not employed (probability set to 0%), as preliminary experiments indicated that Mosaic augmentation alone provided sufficient regularization for the classification task.

Model architectures

Three generations of the Ultralytics YOLO architecture were evaluated for the classification task: YOLOv836, YOLO1137, and YOLO2638. Each architecture was trained in all five available model sizes: nano (n), small (s), medium (m), large (l), and extra-large (x), yielding a total of 15 distinct models.

YOLOv8, released in January 2023, introduced a redesigned backbone with the C2f (Cross-Stage Partial with two convolutions) module, which enhances multi-scale feature fusion by concatenating outputs from multiple bottleneck layers, producing richer intermediate representations36. YOLO11, released in September 2024, refined the backbone with efficiency-focused modules that reduce computational cost while preserving representational capacity, aiming to maintain accuracy with fewer parameters37. YOLO26, released in September 2025, introduces the MuSGD optimizer for more stable training convergence, along with architectural simplifications that improve inference speed on edge devices38.

As described in section “YOLO-family architectures in forestry”, all three architectures implement image classification as a dedicated mode in which the backbone extracts hierarchical feature representations and a lightweight classification head produces class probabilities via a softmax layer36–38. This unified design allows all 15 models to be trained and evaluated under identical conditions, ensuring that observed performance differences reflect architectural properties rather than methodological variation. An empirical comparison with Swin Transformer Base and ConvNeXt Base, reported in section “Architecture comparison”, further supports the selection of YOLO-family models for this task.

The five model sizes within each architecture differ in network depth and width, with the nano variant being the smallest (fewest parameters and lowest computational cost) and the extra-large variant being the largest (highest parameter count and greatest computational demand). This systematic variation enables the analysis of trade-offs between model capacity, classification accuracy, and inference efficiency for the wood species identification task.

Training protocol

All 15 models were trained on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 5900XT 16-core processor, 32 GB DDR4 RAM (3200 MT/s), and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti GPU (12 GB VRAM). Training was conducted using the Ultralytics framework with PyTorch as the backend. The input image resolution was set to 448×448 pixels, consistent with the patch size used in preprocessing. The SGD optimizer was employed with a learning rate of 0.01 and a momentum of 0.937. A batch size of 16 was used for all models.

Training was governed by an early stopping criterion. The process was terminated when the validation loss ceased to improve for a predefined number of consecutive epochs (patience parameter). A maximum of 50 epochs was set as the upper bound. Under this protocol, the actual number of training epochs varied across models, ranging from 31 to 50 depending on the convergence behavior of each architecture and model size. Specifically, larger models (medium, large, and extra-large variants) in some architectures converged earlier due to their greater representational capacity, while smaller models (nano and small) more frequently reached the maximum epoch limit.

Inference efficiency was assessed by measuring the forward-pass time per patch (in milliseconds) and the corresponding throughput in frames per second (FPS), both recorded on the same hardware used for training. Pre- and post-processing times were excluded to isolate the computational cost of the neural network itself. All evaluations on the test set were performed without data augmentation, using the best model weights selected by the early stopping criterion during training.

Out-of-distribution detection

To assess the robustness of the trained models against inputs from classes not encountered during training, three complementary OOD detection methods were incorporated into the evaluation pipeline. Each method operates post-hoc on the output of the trained classification model without requiring architectural modifications or retraining.

Evaluation sets and protocol

Two OOD evaluation scenarios were designed to assess detection performance at different levels of visual dissimilarity from the training distribution. The first scenario, referred to as general OOD, comprises up to 1,000 images drawn jointly from two publicly available sources: natural landscape photographs from the Nature Dataset54 and dermatological images from the Skin Cancer Image Classification dataset55. These sources represent strongly out-of-domain inputs whose visual properties differ substantially from macroscopic wood images, evaluating the models’ capacity to reject entirely unrelated content.

The second scenario addresses a more demanding and practically relevant challenge, the detection of wood species not seen during training. The unified dataset assembled for this study encompasses 106 species across the three acquisition protocols, of which 73 were selected for model training based on representativeness criteria. The remaining 33 species, acquired under the same imaging protocols and preprocessing pipeline as the training data, constitute a set of morphologically plausible OOD inputs. Unlike natural landscapes or dermatological images, these unseen wood species share the anatomical texture patterns characteristic of the training distribution, making their rejection a considerably more stringent test of the models’ capacity to identify genuinely unknown species under realistic inspection conditions.

Performance was assessed using two complementary metrics. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) serves as the primary threshold-independent measure of separability between in-distribution and OOD score distributions. An AUROC of p% indicates that with probability p/100, a randomly drawn in-distribution sample receives a higher OOD score than a randomly drawn OOD sample, with 50% corresponding to chance-level discrimination and 100% to perfect separation. This probabilistic interpretation enables direct comparison across methods regardless of threshold calibration. Additionally, detection rates at are reported for MSP, while Energy and Mahalanobis detection rates are reported at thresholds calibrated to match equivalent false rejection rates.

Interpretability analysis

Two complementary techniques were adopted to support the interpretability analysis: RISE-based saliency maps for spatial attention visualization and t-SNE for feature space analysis.

RISE saliency maps

Randomized Input Sampling for Explanation (RISE)40 was applied to generate saliency maps that highlight the spatial regions of each input image that most strongly influence the model’s classification decision. Unlike gradient-based methods such as Grad-CAM, RISE is a model-agnostic, black-box approach that does not require access to internal network gradients or activations. Instead, it operates by systematically masking random subsets of the input image with binary masks, observing the effect of each mask on the model’s output confidence, and aggregating the results to produce a pixel-level importance map.

The resulting saliency map assigns higher values to regions whose occlusion leads to a greater decrease in classification confidence, thereby identifying the anatomical features that the model relies upon for its predictions.

In this study, a multi-scale RISE approach was adopted to capture anatomical features at different spatial granularities. For each image, random masks were generated at five grid resolutions: 4 × 4, 8 × 8, 16 × 16, 32 × 32, and 64 × 64, with a masking rate of 50%. The input images were processed at 448×448 pixels, consistent with the training resolution. The coarser grids (e.g., 4 × 4) capture broad anatomical features such as growth ring patterns and large pore arrangements, while the finer grids (e.g., 64 × 64) reveal localized features such as parenchyma cell distributions and ray structures.

RISE was applied to both correctly classified and misclassified test samples from the best-performing models. This dual analysis enables identification of the anatomical structures, including pore arrangements, parenchyma patterns, and ray distributions, that underlie correct predictions, as well as the regions that contribute to confusion between morphologically similar species. The fidelity of the generated saliency maps was quantitatively assessed through deletion and insertion AUC40, complemented by a weight randomisation control in which all network weights were randomly reinitialised before RISE was applied.

t-SNE feature space visualization

The t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)41 was used to project the high-dimensional feature representations learned by the classification models into a two-dimensional space for visualization. For each test image, the feature vector was extracted from the second-to-last layer (embedding dimension of 1,280) of each model. These high-dimensional representations were then projected using t-SNE with two perplexity values (30 and 50) and two iteration settings (1,000 and 2,000) to assess the stability and sensitivity of the resulting projections. Test set performance

Table 3 presents the classification performance of all 15 models on the held-out test set comprising 8,640 patches from 73 species.

Four models achieved the highest Top-1 accuracy of 99.87% with only 11 misclassifications out of 8,640 test patches, namely YOLO26-medium, YOLO26-large, YOLOv8-large, and YOLOv8-x-large. Cross-validation results confirmed the stability of these rankings, with mean Top-1 accuracy across the three folds ranging from 99.49 ± 0.07% (YOLO11-nano) to 99.83 ± 0.04% (YOLO26-medium), and standard deviations not exceeding 0.09 percentage points across all 15 models. All four attained an MCC of 0.9987, consistent with the class distribution across the 73 species evaluated. Among these top-performing models, YOLO26-medium achieved the same accuracy at an inference time of 2.13 ms per patch (232.5 FPS), which is 1.6 times faster than YOLOv8-large (3.40 ms, 179.5 FPS) and 2.1 times faster than YOLOv8-x-large (4.38 ms, 152.9 FPS).

All 15 models achieved 100% Top-3 and Top-5 accuracy. This means that for every test patch, the correct species was always among the three most probable predictions, regardless of the architecture or model size employed. This finding has practical implications for field deployment, as a system presenting the top-3 candidates to the user would achieve perfect coverage across all evaluated models.

Patch-level versus image-level accuracy

The reported misclassifications occur at the patch level, not at the image level. Each original macroscopic image was subdivided into multiple 448×448 patches during preprocessing, and the classification errors consistently affect only isolated patches from a given source image while the remaining patches from the same image are correctly classified. For instance, the patch identifier “3129_p0002” indicates that only the third patch extracted from image 3129 was misclassified, while the other patches from the same image were assigned to the correct species.

For YOLO26-medium, the 11 misclassified patches originated from 10 distinct source images. Of these, 9 images had only a single misclassified patch, while one image (Euxylophora paraensis, specimen 3129) contributed two misclassified patches. This pattern was consistent across all 15 models, with most images contributing at most one misclassified patch out of the multiple patches extracted from each source image.

To assess the practical implication of this finding, a majority-voting scheme was applied at the image level for YOLO26-medium, in which the final species assignment for each source image was determined by the most frequent prediction across all patches extracted from that image. Applied to the 1,714 original images in the test set, this scheme yielded 100% image-level accuracy, as the majority vote corrected every patch-level error. This result confirms that the patch-based preprocessing pipeline provides a built-in redundancy mechanism that substantially enhances classification reliability in deployment scenarios where multiple patches can be extracted from a given specimen.

For general OOD content, the energy-based method and MSP achieved near-identical and near-perfect discrimination, with mean AUROC values of 99.9% and 99.8% respectively, and six models reaching 100.0% AUROC under energy scoring. The Mahalanobis distance method was substantially less effective in this scenario, with AUROC values ranging from 67.0% to 83.4% (mean 78.8%). This divergence reflects a structural limitation of the Mahalanobis method in strongly out-of-domain scenarios. Because the shared covariance matrix is estimated exclusively from the 73 training species, it encodes the geometric relationships among those classes and may not generalise reliably to inputs that bear no visual resemblance to wood, producing inconsistent distance scores for such samples. An inverse pattern was observed between Mahalanobis AUROC and MSP AUROC across models for the general OOD scenario. Architectures that produced higher softmax-based discrimination tended to yield less compact feature distributions in the embedding space, while those with more compact distributions tended to be less discriminative at the output level.

The analysis of mean softmax confidence (MC) illuminates the overconfidence phenomenon underlying MSP performance. General OOD images received a mean confidence of 0.623 across all models, compared to a mean in-distribution confidence of 0.998, yielding a gap of 0.375 that explains the high MSP AUROC. Despite this gap, threshold-based detection remains demanding at conservative thresholds. At τ = 0.5, only 23.8% to 41.2% of general OOD samples are rejected with a false rejection rate below 0.1%, rising to 85.6%–94.4% at τ = 0.95 with a false rejection rate below 1.7%. Energy-based detection at calibrated thresholds reaches 99.5%–100.0% at the equivalent false rejection rate, confirming that operating on pre-softmax logits substantially alleviates the overconfidence limitation without modifying the trained architecture.

The unseen wood scenario presents a distinct challenge, as the mean OOD confidence rises to 0.801, reducing the softmax gap to 0.197 and reflecting the tendency of the models to assign high confidence to anatomically plausible inputs that activate feature combinations similar to known species. Despite this reduction, MSP maintains strong discriminability, with a mean AUROC of 97.7% and a best individual result of 98.5% for YOLO11-s. Energy scoring follows closely, achieving a mean AUROC of 96.5% and a best result of 98.0% for YOLO26-l. The Mahalanobis method achieves a mean AUROC of 91.1%, with YOLOv8-n reaching 93.0%. Although this represents a reduction relative to MSP and Energy, it is substantially higher than the Mahalanobis performance observed for general OOD content, a pattern consistent with the closer proximity of unseen wood species to the known class manifolds in the embedding space. At τ = 0.95, threshold-based MSP detection rejects 48.5%–72.5% of unseen species with a false rejection rate below 1.7%, while calibrated Energy detection reaches 75.8%–89.5% at an equivalent false rejection rate.

False accepts across all three methods were predominantly assigned to species with broad or relatively uniform textural patterns, notably Manilkara baccata, Aspidosperma pyrifolium, and Mimosa tenuiflora. This distributional tendency carries a taxonomic dimension, as the unseen wood species most frequently misassigned to these classes belong to the same botanical families or genera as the attracting training species. Aspidosperma macrocarpum, one of the most frequently misassigned OOD species, shares the genus Aspidosperma with A. pyrifolium within the family Apocynaceae. Similarly, Chrysophyllum lucentifolium belongs to the family Sapotaceae alongside both Manilkara baccata and Pouteria pachycarpa, and Piptadenia sp. belongs to the mimosoid group within the family Fabaceae, the same lineage as Mimosa tenuiflora. Species within the same genus or family typically share vessel arrangement, parenchyma distribution, and ray structure, producing macroscopic patches that the model might not reliably distinguish from those of the corresponding training species. The prominence of these species as false-accept attractors is explained not by their proximity to OOD inputs in the global feature space, as centroid distances in the t-SNE projection do not reveal systematic clustering between training and unseen species (Fig. 5), but by patch-level anatomical ambiguity. Individual patches from OOD species within the same genus or family occasionally present textural characteristics that fall within the intra-species variability distribution of the attracting training species, producing localised misassignments that do not reflect a broader failure of the learned representations.

t-SNE projection (YOLO26-medium, 2000 iterations, perplexity = 30) highlighting the three training species most frequently involved in OOD false accepts: Manilkara baccata (orange), Aspidosperma pyrifolium (lime green), and Mimosa tenuiflora (lilac). The remaining 70 species are shown in grey. Each highlighted species forms a large peripheral cluster. False accepts from taxonomically related OOD species arise from patch-level anatomical ambiguity rather than from global cluster proximity in the learned feature space.

Taken together, these results indicate that all three methods provide effective OOD detection for strongly dissimilar inputs, with energy scoring offering the highest practical detection rates. For the more challenging near-domain scenario involving morphologically plausible wood species, MSP and energy scoring achieve comparable and high AUROC values, with differences between methods across the 15 models remaining within a narrow range. The Mahalanobis method yields a lower mean AUROC in this scenario but operates on a fundamentally different principle, relying on feature-space geometry rather than classifier confidence, which may offer advantages in deployment contexts where softmax-based calibration is less reliable.

Two methodological limitations of the current OOD evaluation are here addressed. First, the energy-based method was applied with temperature T = 1.0 without systematic tuning, given that prior work has shown that the energy score can be sensitive to this parameter, and calibration against a held-out OOD validation set may improve detection rates52. Second, the Mahalanobis distance estimator relies on a shared covariance matrix computed from 73-class training embeddings of dimension 1,280. The sample covariance may be poorly conditioned for classes with few training images in this high-dimensional regime, and regularisation techniques such as shrinkage estimation could improve the reliability of the distance scores53.

This spatial proximity in the learned representation confirms that the false accepts are not artefacts of classifier overconfidence alone, but reflect genuine anatomical similarity encoded in the feature space.

To quantify the relationship between feature-space proximity and classification errors, centroid-to-centroid distances were computed for all species pairs in the t-SNE projection. The mean distance across all pairs was 68.28 units, and error pairs showed a mean distance of 68.89 units, a ratio of 1.009 indicating that misclassified species pairs are not systematically closer in the projected feature space than random pairs. This finding reflects that the model has learned well-separated representations for the vast majority of species, including those that are botanically related. The principal exception is the pair Astronium concinnum and Astronium graveolens, which ranks third among all pairs by centroid proximity (distance = 2.26 units) and accounts for the largest share of classification errors in the benchmark. The proximity of these two congeneric species in the embedding space is consistent with their shared anatomical characteristics, including similar vessel arrangement and parenchyma distribution. For other error-prone pairs such as the three Manilkara species and Euxylophora paraensis/Lecythis pisonis, centroid distances are substantially larger (108–136 and 62 units, respectively), suggesting that their occasional misclassifications arise from patch-level ambiguity rather than from overall cluster confusion. These results reinforce the interpretation that residual classification errors are localised phenomena involving specific patches whose visual texture falls outside the typical intra-species distribution, rather than a systematic failure of the learned feature space to discriminate between species.

RISE saliency map analysis

Quantitative validation of saliency maps

To assess whether the RISE saliency maps faithfully reflect the classification behaviour of the trained models at the corpus level, two perturbation-based metrics were computed over 100 randomly sampled test patches for each of the four best-performing models, yielding 400 image-model evaluations in total. This sample size characterises global fidelity properties of the saliency maps but does not support species-level analysis, as the 100 patches are distributed across 73 species at an average of approximately 1.4 patches per species. A stratified evaluation with larger per-species samples would be required to assess inter-class variation in saliency fidelity, and represents a direction for subsequent work. The deletion metric progressively removes pixels in descending order of saliency, replacing them with zeros, and records the resulting model confidence at each step. A faithful saliency map produces a steep confidence decline as the most informative pixels are removed first, yielding a low area under the deletion curve (AUC). The insertion metric reveals pixels in the same order on a Gaussian-blurred baseline, measuring how rapidly confidence is restored as discriminative regions are progressively uncovered; a faithful map yields a high insertion AUC. Figure 7 presents the mean deletion and insertion curves for the four models.

Mean deletion and insertion curves for the four best-performing models, each evaluated over 100 randomly sampled test patches. Deletion curve (left): model confidence declines as the most salient pixels are progressively replaced with zeros. Insertion curve (right): confidence recovers as the most salient pixels are progressively revealed on a Gaussian-blurred baseline. The consistent behaviour across two architectural families confirms that the saliency patterns reflect shared discriminative features rather than properties specific to any single architecture.

The mean deletion AUC across all models and images was 0.743 ± 0.105. The deletion curves share a characteristic shape in which confidence remains relatively stable during the removal of the first 40% of pixels, then declines steeply between 60% and 90% removal before collapsing near zero. The slow initial decline reflects the high spatial redundancy of macroscopic wood texture, where the same anatomical pattern repeats across the patch surface, so that removing the most salient pixels does not immediately destroy all discriminative signals. The steep drop beyond 60% removal confirms that the saliency maps identify the regions most relevant to classification, as confidence falls sharply once a substantial fraction of those regions is removed.

The mean insertion AUC was 0.888 ± 0.046, with all four models showing a rapid confidence recovery as the most salient pixels are progressively revealed on the blurred baseline. Confidence rises from approximately 0.5 at the blurred baseline to above 0.95 within the first 25% of pixels revealed, indicating that the regions assigned the highest saliency scores by RISE are the primary contributors to the classification decision. The consistency of insertion curves across all four models suggests that the identified saliency patterns are not idiosyncratic to a particular architecture but reflect a shared set of discriminative features underlying the classification of wood texture.

To verify that the saliency maps encode model-specific learned representations rather than generic image properties, a weight randomisation control was performed by applying RISE to each model after randomly reinitialising all network weights. The Spearman rank correlation between maps produced by the trained models and those produced by their randomised counterparts was r = 0.006 ± 0.046 across all 400 evaluations, indistinguishable from zero. This result confirms that the attributions produced by RISE reflect the representations learned during training and are not artefacts of image texture or the perturbation procedure.

RISE analysis of misclassified species

Multi-scale RISE saliency maps were generated for all misclassified test patches across all 15 models, using five grid resolutions (4 × 4, 8 × 8, 16 × 16, 32 × 32, and 64 × 64) with 2,000 masks per scale. The analysis of these maps, particularly for the universal error cases identified in section “Qualitative error analysis”, revealed consistent patterns regarding which anatomical regions drive the models’ classification decisions.

For the Euxylophora paraensis patches misclassified as Balfourodendron riedelianum, the RISE maps at intermediate grid resolutions (8 × 8 and 16 × 16) showed that the model’s attention concentrated on regions containing marginal parenchyma bands and densely arranged pore clusters. These features are diagnostically shared between both species, which explains the high-confidence misclassification. At coarser scales (4 × 4), the saliency maps highlighted broad growth ring patterns, while at finer scales (32 × 32 and 64 × 64), the attention focused on individual pore openings and surrounding parenchyma cells. The consistency of the attention pattern across scales confirms that the model correctly identified anatomically relevant features but was unable to discriminate between two taxa that express these features in a nearly identical manner at macroscopic resolution.

In the Bowdichia sp. patch misclassified as Bagassa guianensis (3819_p0003), the saliency maps revealed strong attention to solitary, occluded pores and the surrounding fibrous tissue, with reduced attention in regions between ray structures. This pattern aligns with the anatomical overlap described in section “Qualitative error analysis”: both species present medium-thickness rays, similar vessel diameters, and frequent pore occlusion by tyloses, producing visual signatures that are difficult to distinguish without higher-magnification analysis.

The Couratari sp. patch misclassified as Hura crepitans (4039_p0002) exhibited a dispersed saliency pattern at medium grid resolutions, with attention distributed across pore arrangements and parenchyma boundaries rather than concentrated on a single discriminative feature. This diffuse attention pattern suggests that the model relied on global textural similarity rather than specific anatomical markers, which contributed to the misclassification. RISE analysis of correctly classified species

To complement the analysis of misclassified samples, RISE saliency maps were also generated for correctly classified test patches from three anatomically distinct species using the four best-performing models: YOLO26-medium, YOLO26-large, YOLOv8-large, and YOLOv8-x-large.

For Araucaria angustifolia, a gymnosperm lacking vessels and characterised by a tracheid-based anatomy, the saliency maps reveal a distributed but structured attention pattern rather than the discrete high-saliency regions observed in vessel-bearing species. High-attribution regions correspond to the transition zones between earlywood and latewood bands, which constitute the primary macroscopic diagnostic criterion for this species through the alternation of large-lumen thin-walled and small-lumen thick-walled tracheids. This pattern is consistent across all four models, indicating that the network has adapted its attention strategy to the available anatomical information. In the absence of discrete pores, the spatial organisation of growth ring alternation serves as the discriminative signal.

The saliency maps for Cedrelinga catenaeformis, a species with large conspicuous pores and banded axial parenchyma, present a distinct pattern in which high-attribution regions coincide with vessel lumens and their immediate surroundings. The model consistently attends to individual pore openings across all four architectures, with the highest saliency concentrated at the vessel boundaries and within the lumen itself. This attention pattern is consistent with the diagnostic practice of wood anatomists, who assess vessel size, shape, and spatial distribution as primary identification criteria3.

For Dipteryx sp., a species with diffuse porosity and aliform-to-confluent axial parenchyma, the saliency maps show attention distributed across both pore openings and the surrounding parenchyma tissue. The pattern is consistent with the anatomical characteristics of the species, in which the spatial relationship between small uniform vessels and the connecting parenchyma bands constitutes a primary diagnostic criterion. The best-performing models in this study achieved a test accuracy of 99.87% on 73 macroscopic wood species, establishing a new benchmark in the macroscopic wood identification literature. This result surpasses the previous best macroscopic accuracy of 98.73% reported by De Oliveira22 on 41 species, representing a 1.14 percentage point improvement while simultaneously evaluating nearly twice as many species. The improvement over the handcrafted feature approach of Paula Filho et al.18 (97.77% on 41 species) is even more pronounced, at 2.10 percentage points.

The accuracy achieved in this study also exceeds the best reported results for microscopic wood images. De Oliveira22 achieved 99.11% on 112 species using microscopic images, and De Geus et al.21 reported 98.75% on 281 species. While direct comparison across different datasets and species compositions requires caution, the finding that macroscopic image analysis can achieve comparable or superior accuracy to microscopic approaches has significant practical implications, as macroscopic image acquisition is substantially faster, less expensive, and more amenable to field deployment.

Several factors contribute to the high performance achieved in this study. First, the scale calibration procedure ensured that anatomical structures maintained consistent pixel dimensions across the three source datasets, enabling the models to learn scale-invariant representations of species-specific features. Second, the patch-based preprocessing with 20% overlap increased the utilisation of available image data while ensuring that anatomical structures near patch boundaries were fully captured. Third, the 448×448 pixel patch size (section “Scale calibration and patch-based preprocessing”) provided sufficient spatial context to capture parenchyma arrangements, pore distributions, and ray structures spanning multiple anatomical elements. Fourth, the augmentation pipeline described in section “Data augmentation” enhanced model robustness to variations in illumination, orientation, and partial occlusion that are common in field acquisition conditions.

The comparison also reveals a consistent pattern regarding the relationship between model complexity and classification accuracy for this task. While previous studies typically evaluated a single architecture, the systematic comparison of 15 models across three YOLO generations demonstrates that medium-sized models (specifically YOLO26-medium) can match the performance of substantially larger models. This observation is relevant to future deployment considerations, as it suggests that the representational capacity required for high-accuracy wood species classification may be attainable within the parameter budgets typical of resource-constrained hardware.

From a practical standpoint, these results have direct implications for the operational workflows currently employed in timber inspection. In many field settings, species identification still depends on capturing macroscopic images of wood surfaces with portable devices and transmitting them to remote specialists through informal communication channels, a procedure that is time-consuming, costly, and susceptible to errors arising from inadequate image quality or insufficient contextual information6. The inference time of 2.13 ms per patch achieved by YOLO26-medium, measured on a desktop GPU, is consistent with the computational throughput requirements for real-time operation and constitutes a promising indicator for future deployment on dedicated edge hardware, though formal validation on mobile platforms remains as a direction for subsequent work.

Methodological considerations

Two methodological aspects of the experimental design warrant explicit discussion to support an accurate interpretation of the reported results and to delineate the boundaries within which the present findings should be read.

The first consideration concerns the granularity of the cross-validation partitioning. The three source datasets adopted acquisition protocols in which each physical wood block was photographed in multiple orientations to record the cross-sectional plane independently of growth ring direction11, so that two or more images may originate from the same physical specimen. The source datasets, however, do not include specimen-level identifiers that would allow per-block grouping at training time. In the absence of such metadata, the partitioning described in section “Cross-validation strategy” was performed at the image level, which guarantees that no two patches from a single source image appear simultaneously in training and test sets and removes the most direct source of patch-level leakage. The residual possibility that images from a single physical specimen are distributed across folds cannot be ruled out a priori, and the reported accuracy figures should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound relative to a strict specimen-level evaluation. This methodological limitation between image-level and specimen-level splits has been documented as an open problem of the field. Liu et al.25 explicitly identified dataset partitioning as one of the fundamental problems in computer vision applied to wood identification, and Ma et al.34 reported that segregated (specimen-level) splits produce substantially lower but more realistic accuracy estimates than non-segregated approaches on hardwood microscopy. The construction of next-generation wood image corpora that include per-image specimen identifiers, enabling true specimen-level partitioning, represents a priority direction for future dataset releases (section “Conclusion”).

The second consideration concerns the OOD evaluation, whose specific methodological limitations regarding the temperature parameter of the energy-based method and the covariance estimation underlying the Mahalanobis distance method are already discussed at the end of section “Out-of-Distribution detection”.

Conclusion

This study evaluated 15 YOLO-family models spanning three architectural generations (YOLOv8, YOLO11, and YOLO26), each in five model sizes (nano through x-large), for the automated identification of 73 forest species from macroscopic wood images. The evaluation encompassed classification accuracy, computational efficiency, OOD detection, and model interpretability through RISE saliency maps and t-SNE feature space analysis.

Four models achieved the highest test accuracy of 99.87% with only 11 misclassifications out of 8,640 test patches, namely YOLO26-medium, YOLO26-large, YOLOv8-large, and YOLOv8-x-large. All 15 models attained 100% Top-3 and Top-5 accuracy, indicating that the correct species was invariably among the three most probable predictions. Furthermore, image-level majority voting across patches yielded 100% accuracy for YOLO26-medium on the 1,714 test images of the best-performing fold, indicating that the patch-based pipeline provides a redundancy mechanism that mitigates residual patch-level errors under this configuration.

Among the best-performing models, YOLO26-medium emerged as the most efficient choice for deployment, matching the accuracy of larger models while requiring 2.13 ms per inference (232.5 FPS), approximately 1.6 times faster than YOLOv8-large. This finding demonstrates that medium-sized models from the latest YOLO generation possess sufficient representational capacity for macroscopic wood species classification, suggesting that high-accuracy identification may be achievable on resource-constrained hardware, pending formal validation on edge platforms.

The systematic comparison across architectures revealed that YOLO11 produced 68% more total errors than YOLOv8 across all five model sizes. This difference was statistically significant for smaller variants (McNemar’s exact test, p < 0.001 for YOLO11-n versus YOLOv8-l) but attenuated with increasing model capacity, with YOLO11-x showing no statistically significant difference from the top-performing models (p = 0.057). These results suggest that architectural modifications designed for general-purpose detection tasks do not necessarily translate into gains for fine-grained texture classification tasks such as wood species identification, particularly at reduced model sizes.

The qualitative error analysis identified seven patches that were universally misclassified across all 15 models, confirming that the residual errors are driven by fundamental anatomical similarities between certain species pairs (e.g., Euxylophora paraensis and Balfourodendron riedelianum) rather than by model limitations. The RISE saliency maps corroborated this finding, showing that the models correctly attend to diagnostically relevant anatomical structures but cannot resolve the inter-species ambiguity when these structures are shared between taxa at macroscopic resolution.

The OOD detection evaluation, conducted across three complementary methods, revealed consistent and high discriminability for strongly dissimilar inputs, with energy scoring and MSP achieving mean AUROC values of 99.9% and 99.8% respectively for general OOD content, and all three methods maintaining strong performance for the more demanding scenario of morphologically plausible unseen wood species (mean AUROC 97.7%, 96.5%, and 91.1% for MSP, energy scoring, and Mahalanobis distance, respectively). The overconfidence phenomenon was quantified through the softmax confidence gap between in-distribution and OOD samples, which narrows substantially for near-domain wood inputs, illustrating a fundamental limitation of output-based detection methods in realistic field inspection scenarios.

The t-SNE feature space analysis revealed a divergence between classification accuracy and feature space separability. YOLO26 models produced the best-separated clusters despite not always achieving the highest accuracy, suggesting that they develop more geometrically coherent internal representations.

First, integrating macroscopic and microscopic image features through multi-modal fusion architectures may help resolve the inter-species ambiguities identified in the error analysis. Second, extending the OOD evaluation to additional near-domain scenarios, such as species from adjacent geographic regions or different wood preparation protocols, would characterise the boundaries of the models’ rejection capability more fully. Third, expanding the dataset with additional species and acquisition conditions, particularly from underrepresented geographic regions, would strengthen the generalization capacity of the models. Fourth, a cross-protocol evaluation in which models are trained exclusively on DS1 and DS2 and evaluated on DS3 would provide a more demanding assessment of robustness to acquisition variability than the mixed-corpus design reported here. Fifth, while image-level majority voting yielded 100% accuracy for YOLO26-medium (section “Patch-level versus image-level accuracy”), its systematic evaluation across all model sizes and operational acquisition conditions warrants further investigation. Sixth, the RISE-based interpretability analysis was limited to 100 randomly sampled patches, which characterises global saliency fidelity but does not support species-level conclusions; a stratified evaluation with larger per-species samples would enable assessment of inter-class variation in saliency patterns. A complementary priority is the assembly of next-generation wood image corpora that include explicit per-image specimen identifiers. Such metadata, absent from the source datasets used in the present study, would enable strict specimen-level partitioning of training, validation, and test subsets and would substantially strengthen the assessment of generalization across distinct physical samples, addressing a methodological consideration shared with most publicly available macroscopic wood collections (section “Methodological considerations”). Finally, the formal validation of the best-performing models on representative edge platforms, including smartphones and single-board computers, represents a natural next step toward translating these findings into practical tools for forest conservation and sustainable wood trade management, and should include benchmarking of inference latency and classification accuracy under real field acquisition conditions.
