Proof of the exact diffusion constant via first passage time in quasi-periodic potentials
Ming Gong

Abstract
Brownian motion in terms of Lifson and Jackson (LJ) formula has been widely explored in periodic systems and it has been believed for a long time that the LJ formula only applies to periodic potentials. Recently we show that for the following Brownian motion γ ​ x ˙ = − U ′ ​ ( x ) + ξ \gamma\dot{x}=-U^{\prime}(x)+\xi , where U ⁡ ( x ) U(x) is the quasi-periodic potential, the effective diffusion constant can still be described by the LJ formula D ∗ = D / ( ⟨ exp ⁡ ( β ​ U ) ⟩ ​ ⟨ exp ⁡ ( − β ​ U ) ⟩ ) D^{*}=D/(\langle\exp(\beta U)\rangle\langle\exp(-\beta U)\rangle) , where the average is redefined as ⟨ exp ⁡ ( β ​ U ) ⟩ = lim L → ∞ L − 1 ​ ∫ 0 L exp ⁡ ( β ​ U ​ ( x ) ) ​ 𝑑 x \langle\exp(\beta U)\rangle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}L^{-1}\int_{0}^{L}\exp(\beta U(x))dx . In this manuscript we prove this result exactly using the mean first passage time τ ⁡ ( x ) \tau(x) , with boundary conditions τ ⁡ ( ± L ) = 0 \tau(\pm L)=0 , and show that the effective diffusion constant can be determined using D ∗ = lim L → ∞ L 2 / ( 2 ​ τ ​ ( 0 ) ) D^{*}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}L^{2}/(2\tau(0)) , where ± L \pm L is the two positions of the absorbing boundary. We exactly solve the equation of motion of τ ⁡ ( x ) \tau(x) and obtain the above result with the aid of Jacobi-Anger expansion method. Our result can be generalized to the other potentials and even higher dimensions, which can greatly broaden our understanding of Brownian motion in more general circumstances. The requirement for a well-defined effective diffusion constant D ∗ D^{*} in more general potentials is also discussed.



Brownian motion, studying of the erratic random motion of microscopic particles suspended in a fluid, has been one of the most revolutionary concepts in statistical physics
Bian et al. 2016
;
Chandrasekhar 1943
;
Mel’nikov 1991
;
Reimann 2002
;
Hänggi and Marchesoni 2009
;
Hänggi et al. 1990
. In free space, this motion has been well understood since the pioneer works by Einstein, von Smoluchowski and Langevin in the beginning of the 20th century
Einstein 1905
;
Bian et al. 2016
. In a periodic potential, say
U(x)=U(x+a)
, with
a
being the period, the Brownian motion described by the Langevin equation can be written as

\gamma\dot{x}=-U^{\prime}(x)+\xi,

(1)

where
\gamma
is the viscosity coefficient and the stochastic force satisfies
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
,
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\gamma k_{B}T\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. The effective diffusion constant
D^{*}
is given by the well-known Lifson and Jackson (LJ) formula
Lifson and Jackson 1962
;
Festa and d’Agliano 1978
;
Gunther et al. 1979
;
Weaver 1979

D^{*}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\langle x^{2}\rangle\over 2t}={D\over\langle\exp(\Phi(x))\rangle\langle\exp(-\Phi(x))\rangle},

(2)

where
\Phi(x)=\beta U(x)
,
\beta=1/k_{B}T
with
k_{B}
and
T
being the Boltzmann constant and temperature, respectively, and
\langle x^{2}\rangle
is the variance of position during random motion. Here the average is defined in a full period as
Weaver 1979

\langle\exp(\pm\Phi(x))\rangle={1\over a}\int_{0}^{a}\exp(\pm\Phi(x))dx.

(3)

It has been widely believed that the periodicity is necessary for the above definition of
D^{*}
. In the rough potential, it has been argued by Zwanzig
Zwanzig 1988
that when the system has two different periods, say
a
and
b
, then the above definition may become valid (to some extent of approximation) when and only when one of the period is much larger than the other period, say
a\gg b
.

Recently in Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
we show that the above definition can be extended to the more general potentials. We consider the quasi-periodic potential and show that the above LJ formula can still be applied to determine the effective diffusion constant, yet the average should be taken in a sufficiently large interval, that is

\langle\exp(\pm\Phi(x))\rangle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}{1\over L}\int_{0}^{L}\exp(\pm\Phi(x))dx,

(4)

assuming both limit exist. This average naturally includes the result of Eq.
3
. We demonstrate this result using several different approaches, in which our key argument is that since the quasi-periodic potential
U(x)
can be approximated using periodic potential with arbitrary accuracy with a sufficient large period
L
, then we can still use the LJ formula to derive
D^{*}
. Furthermore, we show that if this diffusion constant is well defined, then the whole probability function
p(x,t)
should be related to the Gaussian function in some way
Yang et al. 2025
;
Sivan and Farago 2018
;
Defaveri et al. 2023

p(x,t)=\mathcal{A}\exp(-\Phi(x)){1\over\sqrt{4\pi D^{*}t}}\exp(-{x^2 \over 4D^* t}),

(5)

in which
\mathcal{A}
is determined by the normalization condition. The accuracy of these two parts have been numerically verified. With this solution, the effective diffusion constant can be obtained from the Fokker-Planck equation.

To make our conclusion much more rigorous, it is necessary to prove the above conclusion in terms of mean first passage time (MFPT)
\tau(0)
starting at
x_{0}=0
with two absorbing boundaries at
\pm L
, in which the diffusion constant
D^{*}
can be defined using

D^{*}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}{L^{2}\over 2\tau(0)}.

(6)

This is one of the most standard approach to derive the LJ formula, yet in periodic system
L
is set to the period
a
. This new definition will make the boundary condition to be insignificant anymore. Here we would like to thank the referee during the review of Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
, in which he/she insisted that our proof is not exact and the periodicity of the potential is required or
a\gg b
should be satisfied in using of the LJ formula
Zwanzig 1988
, thus the effective
D^{*}
should be proven using MFPT, leading to this manuscript.

Let us assume the probability to be
p(x,t|x_{0})
, where
x_{0}
is the initial position with two absorbing boundaries at
\pm L
. This corresponds diffusion equation reads as
Gitterman 2000
;
Dybiec et al. 2006

{\partial p(x,t|x_{0})\over\partial t}=D{\partial^{2}p(x,t|x_{0})\over\partial x^{2}},

(7)

subject to the following boundary conditions

\displaystyle p(x,0|x_{0})=\delta(x-x_{0}),\quad p(\pm L,t|x_{0})=0.

(8)

We assume
D
to be position independent, yet its generalization to position dependent is straightforward. We have to solve the partial differential equation with proper boundary conditions, which makes
p(x,t|x_{0})
totally different from that in Eq.
5
. With this we can define the survival probability in the interval
[-L,L]
as

s(t,x_{0})=\int_{-L}^{L}p(x,t|x_{0})dx.

(9)

Obviously,
s(0,x_{0})=1
, and when
t
approaches infinity, the particle should inevitably hit the boundaries, thus
s(\infty,x_{0})=0
. Physically, it should be a monotonic decreasing function of
t
, thus we can define
\rho=\rho(t,x_{0})=-\partial s/\partial t
as a probability distribution function, that is
\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(t,x_{0})dt=1
for all
x_{0}\in(-L,L)
, with
\rho(t,x_{0})\geq 0
. In this way we can calculate the MFPT as
Weaver 1979

\tau(x_{0})=\langle t\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\rho(t,x_{0})dt.

(10)

We have
a^{2}=2D^{*}\tau(0)
in periodic potentials via Eq.
6
.

We can calculate the solution of
p(x,t|x_{0})
directly. The general solution with the absorbing boundary conditions, in terms of plane wave basis, can be written as

p(x,t|x_{0})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}(t)\sin({n \pi(x-L) \over 2L}),

(11)

with
\dot{c}_{n}(t)=-n^{2}\pi^{2}D/(4L^{2})c_{n}(t)
. With the initial condition that
p(x,0|x_{0}=\delta(x-x_{0}))
, we obtain

\displaystyle p(x,t|x_{0})

\displaystyle={1\over L}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp(-{n^2 \pi^2 D t \over 4L^2})

(12)

\displaystyle\sin({n \pi(x_0-L) \over 2L})\sin({n \pi(x-L) \over 2L}).

A direct calculation shows that
\int_{-L}^{L}p(x,t|x_{0})\neq 1
when
t\neq 0
due to the escape of particle from this finite interval. Then we can calculate the survival probability as

s(t,x_{0})=\sum_{n\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1}-{4\over n\pi}\exp(-{n^2 \pi^2 D t \over 4L^2})\sin({n \pi(x_0-L) \over 2L}),

(13)

where
2\mathbb{Z}+1
means that only the odd terms (1, 3, 5,
\cdots
) contribute to the total survival probability. Obviously, when
t
is large enough, we should have

s(t,0)\rightarrow{4\over\pi}\exp(- {\pi^2 D \over 4L^2} t).

(14)

With this solution we can determine the probability distribution of first passage time using
\rho=-\partial s(t,x_{0})/\partial t
, yielding

\tau(x_{0})=\sum_{n\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1}-{4\over n\pi}{n^{2}\pi^{2}D\over 4L^{2}}{(4L^{2})^{2}\over n^{4}\pi^{4}D^{2}}\sin({n \pi(x_0-L) \over 2L}),

(15)

which obviously satisfy the boundary conditions
\tau(x_{0}=\pm L)=0
. The above expression yields the MFPT

\tau(x_{0})={L^{2}-x_{0}^{2}\over 2D},

(16)

with the aid of the following identity

\sum_{n=1,3,5,\cdots}-{4\over n\pi}{4\over n^{2}\pi^{2}}\sin(nA)=-{1\over 2}+{2\over\pi^{2}}(A-{\pi\over 2})^{2}.

(17)

Hereafter, let us change
x_{0}
to
x
. It has been prove that the MFPT satisfies the following differential equation
Weaver 1979

De^{-\Phi(x)}{\partial\over\partial x}e^{\Phi(x)}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)=-1,

(18)

with boundary conditions
\tau(x=\pm L)=0
. This equation is valid for the first-order Langevin equation with time independent potential, without the requirement that
U(x)
should be a periodic functions. Physically,
\tau(x)
is the averaged first passage time the system will hit the boundaries
\pm L
. This definition is different from the diffusion constant defined in the Brownian motion, in which the time is fixed, and the variance of position
\langle x^{2}\rangle
is calculated.

(I) In free space we have
U(x)=0
, the above equation for MFPT is reduced to

D{\partial^{2}\over\partial x^{2}}\tau(x)=-1,\quad\tau(x)={L^{2}-x^{2}\over 2D},

(19)

which has been obtained in Eq.
16
. We therefore have Eq.
6
.

(II) With the above result, we then consider Brownian motion in the following periodic potential

\Phi(x)=U\cos(x),

(20)

with period
a=2\pi
. We can move
e^{-\Phi(x)}
to the right hand side and make an integration in
[0,x]
, yielding

D(e^{\Phi(x)}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)-B_{0})=-\int_{0}^{x}e^{\Phi(x)}dx,

(21)

where
B_{0}=e^{\Phi(x)}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)|_{x=0}
. Use the Jacobi-Anger expansion
\exp(U \cos(x))=\sum_{n}I_{n}(U)\exp(inx)
, we have

D(e^{\Phi(x)}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)-B_{0})=-I_{0}(U)x-\sum_{n\neq 0}I_{n}(U)g_{n}(x),

(22)

where
g_{n}(x)=(e^{inx}-1)/(in)
. Obviously,
g_{0}(x)=x
and when
n\neq 0
,
|g_{n}(x)|\leq{2\over|n|}
. It should be noticed that the modified Bessel function
I_{n}(U)
will quickly decays to zero with the increasing of
n
. The second term, which is an oscillation function of
x
, can be estimated using

|\sum_{n\neq 0}I_{n}(U){e^{inx}-1\over in}|\leq\sum_{n\neq 0}{2|I_{n}(U)|\over n},

(23)

which is a finite upper bound independent of
x
. Thus in the large
x
limit, we have

\int_{0}^{x}e^{\Phi(x)}dx\rightarrow I_{0}(U)x.

(24)

This average has a very clear physical meaning, which will be useful for us to understand the results in the quasi-periodic potentials. Physically
I_{0}(U)
is nothing but the average of
e^{\Phi(x)}
in the whole regime, that is

I_{0}(U)=\langle e^{\Phi(x)}\rangle=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}{1\over x}\int_{0}^{x}e^{\Phi(x^{\prime})}dx^{\prime}.

(25)

This function can also be obtained in the periodic system averaged in a full period; however, the above definition has the advantage that the boundary condition is insignificant anymore in the large
x
limit, which will become essential in the quasi-periodic potentials.

Now, let us move
e^{\Phi(x)}
to the right hand side again and we have

\displaystyle D\int_{0}^{L}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DB_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\Phi(x)}dx

(26)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{n}I_{n}(U)\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\Phi(x)}g_{n}(x)dx.

Using the boundary condition that
\tau(L)=0
, we should have the MFPT with initial point at
x=0
as

D\int_{0}^{L}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)dx=-D\tau(0).

(27)

Thus using the Jacobi-Anger expansion and we have

\displaystyle-D\tau(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DB_{0}(I_{0}(U)L+\sum_{n\neq 0}I_{n}(-U)g_{n}(L)

(28)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle I_{0}(U)I_{0}(U){L^{2}\over 2}-I_{0}(U)\sum_{n\neq 0}k_{n}(L),

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq 0,m}I_{n}(U)I_{m}(-U)\int_{0}^{L}e^{imx}g_{n}(x)dx,

where we have defined

\displaystyle k_{n}(L)=I_{n}(-U)\int_{0}^{L}\exp(inx)xdx.

(29)

Using
|g_{n}(L)|\leq 2/|n|
, which is a bounded function when
n\neq 0
. We find that in the large
L
limit,
k_{n}(L)=I_{n}(-U)(L+{g_{n}(L)\over(in)^{2}})\rightarrow I_{n}(-U)L
, and the next-leading terms will become negligible.

In the similar way, we can calculate
\int_{0}^{L}e^{imx}g_{n}(x)dx
exactly, which is an oscillating function of
x
, yielding that this term should at most proportional to
L
. This can be estimated using the following way

|\int_{0}^{L}e^{imx}g_{n}(x)dx|\leq\int_{0}^{L}|e^{imx}g_{n}(x)|dx\leq{2\over|n|}L.

(30)

Collecting all these results together, we find that

-D\tau(0)=-I_{0}(U)I_{0}(-U){L^{2}\over 2}+CL+\cdots,

(31)

where
C
is related to
DB_{0}I_{0}(U)
and
-I_{0}(U)I_{n}(-U)L

etc
. Here the suspension points represent the next sub-leading terms with amplitude much smaller than the second term
CL
in the large
L
limit. In this way, in the large
L
limit
-D\tau(0)\rightarrow-I_{0}(U)I_{0}(-U){L^{2}\over 2}
,
yielding the effective diffusion constant give by the LJ formula in terms of MFPT as

D^{*}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}{L^{2}\over 2\tau(0)}={D\over\langle\exp(\Phi)\rangle\langle\exp(-\Phi)\rangle}.

(32)

Here the average is taken in a full period
[0,a]
; however when it is averaged in much longer interval
[0,L]
when
L
is large enough, we can obtain the same expression.

(III) We next want to derive the effective diffusion constant in some more general quasi-periodic potentials, in which the simplest form can be written as
López-Alamilla et al. 2020

\Phi(x)=U_{a}\cos({2\pi x \over a})+U_{b}\cos({2\pi x \over b}),

(33)

using the notation in Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
. It should be noticed that in our model,
a
and
b
are not commensurate period, thus
a/b
is an irrational number. We do not require
a\gg b
or
b\gg a
. Using the Jacobi-Anger expansion we will have

\exp(\Phi(x))=\sum_{n,m}I_{n}(U_{a})I_{m}(U_{b})e^{i({n\over a}+{m\over b})x}.

(34)

For convenience, let us define
{\bf k}=(n,m)
,
{\bf r}=(1/a,1/b)
,
{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}=n/a+m/b
, and
I_{{\bf k}}=I_{n}(U_{a})I_{m}(U_{b})
, then
\exp(\Phi(x))=\sum_{{\bf k}}I_{\bf k}e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}x}
. We write it in this way so that the whole expressions in following will be similar to that with a single period, from which the generalization is rather straightforward. For instance, we have

\displaystyle D(e^{\Phi}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)-B_{0})=-\sum_{\bf k}I_{\bf k}g_{{\bf k}}(x),

(35)

with
g_{\bf k}(x)=(e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}x}-1)/(i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r})
. Similarly when
{\bf k}\neq 0

\displaystyle|\sum_{{\bf k}\neq 0}I_{{\bf k}}{e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}x}-1\over i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}|\leq\sum_{n,m\neq 0}{2|I_{n}(U_{a})I_{m}(U_{b})|\over|{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}|},

(36)

which is finite and independent of
x
, thus we should have

\int_{0}^{x}e^{\Phi(x)}dx\rightarrow I_{0}(U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})x.

(37)

Notice that
a
and
b
are two incommensurate periods, so
\bf{k}=0
when and only when
n=m=0
.

Now, let us move
e^{\Phi(x)}
to the right hand side again and by using the same procedure we will have

\displaystyle D\int_{0}^{L}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DB_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\Phi(x)}dx

(38)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle I_{0}(U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\Phi(x)}xdx

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf k\neq 0}I_{\bf k}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\Phi(x)}g_{{\bf k}}(x)dx.

Using the boundary condition
\tau(\pm L)=0
, we should have
D\int_{0}^{L}{\partial\over\partial x}\tau(x)dx=-D\tau(0)
; see Eq.
27
.
We solve this problem using the Jacobi-Anger expansion again and obtain (with
I_{n}(-A)=(-1)^{n}I_{n}(A)
)

\displaystyle-D\tau(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle DB_{0}I_{0}(U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})L+DB_{0}\sum_{\bf k^{\prime}\neq 0}I_{\bf k^{\prime}}g_{\bf k^{\prime}}(L)

(39)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle I_{0}^{2}(U_{a})I_{0}^{2}(U_{b}){L^{2}\over 2}-I_{0}(U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})\sum_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\neq 0}k_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(L)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf k\neq 0,k^{\prime}}I_{\bf k}I_{\bf k^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{L}e^{i{\bf k^{\prime}}\cdot{\bf r}x}g_{{\bf k}}(x)dx,

where

\displaystyle k_{n,m}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{n}(-U_{a})I_{m}(-U_{b})\int_{0}^{L}\exp(i{\bf k} \cdot{\bf r} x)xdx

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{n}(-U_{a})I_{m}(-U_{b})(L+{g_{\bf k}(L)\over(i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}}).

Using
|g_{\bf k}(L)|\leq 2/|n/a+m/b|
, which is a bounded function when
n,m\neq 0
, we expect that in the large
L
limit,
k_{n,m}(L)\rightarrow I_{n}(-U_{a})I_{m}(-U_{b})L
.

In the same way we can calculate
\int_{0}^{L}e^{i{\bf k^{\prime}}\cdot{\bf r}x}g_{\bf k}(x)dx
, yielding that this term should, at most, proportional to
L
. This can be estimated using the following way

\displaystyle|\int_{0}^{L}e^{i{\bf k^{\prime}}\cdot{\bf r}x}g_{{\bf k}}(x)dx|\leq\int_{0}^{L}|e^{i{\bf k^{\prime}}\cdot{\bf r}x}g_{{\bf k}}(x)|dx\leq\frac{2L}{|{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}|}.

Collecting all these results, we finally find that

-D\tau(0)=-I_{0}^{2}(U_{a})I_{0}^{2}(U_{b}){L^{2}\over 2}+CL+\cdots,

(42)

where
C
is related to
DB_{0}I_{0}(U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})
and
-I_{0}(U_{a})I_{n^{\prime}}(-U_{a})I_{0}(U_{b})I_{m^{\prime}}(-U_{b})

etc
. The suspension points represent the next sub-leading terms with amplitude much smaller than the second term
CL
in the large
L
limit. In this way, in the large
L
limit we have
-D\tau(0)\rightarrow-I_{0}^{2}(U_{a})I_{0}^{2}(U_{b}){L^{2}\over 2}
, yielding

\displaystyle D^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}{L^{2}\over 2\tau(0)}={D\over\langle\exp(\Phi)\rangle\langle\exp(-\Phi)\rangle}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{D\over I_{0}^{2}(U_{a})I_{0}^{2}(U_{b})}.

In this way we extend the LJ formula to the realm of quasi-periodic potentials, showing that it is still valid, as long as the diffusion constant is redefined properly using Eq.
6
, and the averaged is taken in a large enough interval using Eq.
4
. Strikingly, we find that
D^{*}
is always decreased with the increasing of incommensurate periods, since
|I_{0}(U)|>1
when
U\neq 0
.

Several remarks are in order based on the above results. Firstly, the generalization of our results to much more complicated potentials are straightforward. This result is a direct logical consequence that if we can approximate
U(x)
using a periodic function, then the LJ formula should be applied immediately to study the diffusion constant
D^{*}
. In Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
, we show that for the following potential

U(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}\cos({2\pi x \over a_i} + \theta_i),

(44)

where
a_{i}
are all pairwise incommensurate numbers and
\theta_{i}
are their initial phase, then we should have

\displaystyle D^{*}={D\over\prod_{i=1}^{N}I_{0}^{2}(U_{i})},

(45)

which is independent of the phases
\theta_{i}
. We see that the cross terms from the interference between different periods are not presented strictly anymore.
Secondly, for all these general potentials we should always have

-\tau(x)=-{L^{2}-x^{2}\over 2D^{*}}+\text{sub-leading terms}.

(46)

With this result we find that when
L
and
x
are large enough (assuming
|x|<L
)

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}{\partial^{2}\tau(x)\over\partial x^{2}}=-{1\over D^{*}},

(47)

following Eq.
19
, which is another way to define
D^{*}
. The sub-leading terms will become insignificant when
x
approaches infinity. From Eq.
14
, it is also expected that
D^{*}
may also be reflected from the long time tail of the survival probability. Thirdly, our result means that we can generalize the results to much more general potentials, in which the only requirement is the existence of the following limit

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}{1\over L}\int_{0}^{L}\exp(\pm\Phi(x))dx=\text{finite},

(48)

for which reason the results should have broad applicability in Brownian motion. Obviously, when
\Phi(x)=kx^{2}
for a trapped harmonic potential, the above limit is either zero or infinity, thus
D^{*}=0
. Finally, with the results in Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
, we can obtain
p(x,t|x_{0})
by solving the Fokker-Planck equation analytically, which can be used to calculate the survival probability
s(t,x_{0})
, distribution of first passage time
\rho(t,x_{0})
and the associated MFPT, following Eq.
9
and Eq.
16
, with the aid of Jacobi-Anger expansion. This may lead to intriguing distribution of the first passage time
\rho(t,x_{0})
and its higher-order moments, which deserve to be explored in future.

The significance of the above results is that, we prove exactly that the LJ formula, if properly redefined, can be used to study the diffusion in much more general potentials. This can greatly broaden the applicability of this formula to study the Brownian motion in more general potentials. As compared with Ref.
Yang et al. 2025
, we prove this conclusion exactly in terms of MFPT. We hope this result and the method to exactly calculate
\tau(x)
can find immediate applications in Brownian motion in some concrete physical systems with some general potentials, in which the strict requirement of periodicity is abandoned, thus the cost of experimental realization is greatly reduced. Intriguing applications of our theory may also include the anomalous diffusion in periodic potentials and quasi-periodic potentials
Gitterman 2000
;
Dybiec et al. 2006
. This formula can also be used to study the giant diffusion in Brownian motion with tilted potentials
Reimann et al. 2001
;
Reimann and Eichhorn 2008
;
Reimann et al. 2002
;
Iida et al. 2025
.
