Postoperative Hypocalcemia Following Total Thyroidectomy Versus Completion Thyroidectomy
Authors: Mehmet Turan Cicek, Çiğdem Firat Koca, Mehmet Tan, Baransel Algan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompletion thyroidectomy performed with at least a three‐month interval demonstrates superior parathyroid preservation compared to primary total thyroidectomy, as evidenced by significantly higher postoperative parathyroid hormone levels (51.90 ± 30.26 pg/mL vs. 30.90 ± 17.10 pg/mL, p = 0.006). Postoperative calcium supplementation requirements were significantly lower in the completion thyroidectomy cohort compared to the total thyroidectomy group (4.2% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.049). A staged surgical approach provides a clear functional advantage in preserving parathyroid health and reducing the risk of transient hypocalcemia.Keywords: completion thyroidectomy, postoperative hypocalcemia, total thyroidectomy

Introduction

Total thyroidectomy (TT) is the primary surgical treatment for conditions including toxic multinodular goiter, thyrotoxicosis, and thyroid malignancies. Postoperative hospitalization is necessary to monitor for complications requiring immediate intervention. Surgery may result in complications such as hemorrhage, hematoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and hypocalcemia [1, 2, 3]. Among these, postoperative hypocalcemia is a frequent side effect, with an incidence ranging from 1% to 50%. This condition typically results from parathyroid gland malfunction caused by devascularization, “stunning,” or unintentional removal during the procedure. Hypocalcemia can be permanent (0.5%–10.6%) or temporary (3%–30%) [4, 5]. Numerous mechanisms have been proposed for its onset, including hungry bone syndrome, calcitonin release during manipulation, hemodilution, or increased urinary calcium excretion due to surgical stress [3, 6]. Because its onset and severity are unpredictable, various predictors have been investigated. Recently, serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels have gained attention as an early indicator. Researchers have attempted to predict postoperative hypocalcemia by measuring PTH levels at different intervals following surgery [5, 7, 8, 9]. Acute hypocalcemia may manifest as circumoral paresthesia, numbness in extremities, carpopedal spasms, seizures, or arrhythmias. While thyroid lobectomy is rarely linked to hypocalcemia, the risk is highest after complete thyroidectomy where parathyroid injury is more likely [3, 10]. Completion thyroidectomy (CT) is technically more challenging than initial surgery due to scarring and distorted neck anatomy. Consequently, complications like hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve damage are assumed to be more frequent [11, 12, 13, 14]. A common indication for CT is discovering malignancy in final pathology after hemithyroidectomy. CT is necessary to address potential malignancy in the remaining lobe and enable radioactive iodine therapy [14]. Surgeons must balance surgical risks with oncologic progression to optimize timing and minimize complications [15, 16]. Although CT safety is documented, the biological advantage of the surgical interval in allowing parathyroid recovery remains under‐explored. This study compares the incidence of postoperative hypocalcemia between primary TT and CT groups, while assessing the impact of demographic and clinical variables including age, sex, smoking, and comorbidities on outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval and Study Design

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine. This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Inonu University. Medical records of patients aged > 18 years who underwent TT or CT between 2018 and 2025 were reviewed, and patients were allocated into two groups according to the surgery type.

Patient Selection and Matching Strategy

Out of the 128 patient records screened between 2018 and 2025, exactly 24 patients underwent definitive CT. To prevent statistical bias arising from highly unequal group sizes and to control for key demographic confounders, a strict 1:1 manual case–control matching strategy was applied based on exact age and sex distribution. Consequently, 24 patients from the larger TT pool who perfectly matched the CT cohort were randomly selected. The remaining 80 TT patients were excluded not due to clinical bias, but strictly because they fell outside the tight age‐and‐sex matching pairs required to maintain baseline homogeneity between the cohorts. The study included patients operated on by two different senior surgeons, and both the TT and CT groups consisted of an equal number of patients operated on by each senior surgeon to balance potential operator bias. Furthermore, due to the retrospective nature of the study and the limited available cohort size, patients could not be simultaneously matched based on their final pathological diagnoses (benign vs. malignant), which stands as an additional baseline limitation.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients with preoperative hypocalcemia or abnormal parathyroid function were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included central or extended neck dissection, parathyroid surgeries, and a diagnosis of Graves' disease. Demographic and clinical variables collected included age, sex, smoking status, comorbidities, diagnosis, corrected serum calcium levels before and after surgery, and length of hospital stay (LOS). For patients undergoing CT, the LOS was calculated based solely on the admission for the definitive secondary surgery. The initial hospital stay for the prior hemithyroidectomy was not included in this analysis, as the primary objective was to compare the immediate postoperative burden and recovery profile of the definitive procedure against primary TT.

Laboratory Assessment and Follow‐Up

Prior to surgery and on the first postoperative day (POD 1), serum calcium, albumin, and PTH levels were routinely assessed, and baseline corrected calcium values were calculated. Hypocalcemia was strictly defined as a corrected serum calcium level < 8.0 mg/dL on POD 1, or the development of clinical neuromotor symptoms (e.g., perioral numbness, carpopedal spasm, positive Trousseau's sign) regardless of the absolute calcium value. Per institutional protocol, routine prophylactic calcium supplementation was not administered to patients undergoing TT or CT. Instead, therapeutic oral calcium carbonate (1200–3000 mg/day) combined with calcitriol (0.25–0.5 mcg/day) was initiated as rescue therapy only upon meeting these specific clinical or biochemical hypocalcemia criteria.

Surgical Technique and Intraoperative Care

The surgical reports provided information on the intraoperative treatment of the parathyroid glands. At our clinic, autotransplanting a parathyroid gland into the ipsilateral sternocleidomastoid muscle is a common procedure if the gland is removed during surgery or appears devascularized. The surgical reports also included information on the intraoperative care of the parathyroid glands.

Surgical Interval and Rationale

The time between the initial procedure and the completion of the thyroidectomy is known as the surgical interval, and it was at least 3 months in our patients, as recommended [14, 17]. Instead of performing a thyroidectomy right away (within the first 3 days), our clinic's typical procedure is to wait at least 3 months. The turnaround time for the final histological investigation is the main factor determining this approach. In‐depth paraffin‐embedded section analysis and immunohistochemistry are frequently needed for a conclusive diagnosis of cancer; results are often reported after the safe 72‐h window for prompt reoperation. Operating during the subacute inflammatory phase (1 week to 3 months) increases the risk of surgical morbidity once this first window passes. To minimize the risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and lifelong hypoparathyroidism, it was deliberately decided to postpone treatment for at least 3 months in all patients. This allowed for the full resolution of tissue edema, inflammation, and adhesions. The existence of parathyroid glands in the surgical material was taken into consideration when final pathology results were retrieved.

Statistical Analysis

The study data were evaluated using SPSS 17.0 software. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for qualitative variables, while mean ± standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values were calculated for quantitative variables. The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was applied to assess the distribution characteristics. Non‐parametric tests were preferred for variables that were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare differences between the two groups, the Pearson chi‐square test for qualitative variables, and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for preoperative and postoperative measurements. For variables with normal distribution (p > 0.05), the Independent Samples t‐test was applied. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 128 patient charts were screened, with 24 being eligible for final inclusion and distributed into the study groups through a 1:1 manual matching process.

Demographic Characteristics

Gender distribution was identical in the CT and TT groups, with 9 males (37.5%) and 15 females (62.5%) in both. No significant difference was found between the groups (Pearson χ 2 = 0.000, p = 1.000), indicating complete homogeneity regarding sex. The mean age was 46.88 ± 12.09 years in the CT group and 48.33 ± 14.38 years in the TT group (t(46) = −0.379, p = 0.707), confirming the success of the age‐matching strategy (Table 1, 2).

Comorbidity and Smoking Status

Comorbidity rates did not differ significantly between groups (χ 2 = 0.356, p = 0.551). In the CT group, 83.3% were non‐smokers and 16.7% were smokers, whereas in the TT group, 66.7% were non‐smokers and 33.3% were smokers. This distribution showed no statistically significant difference (χ 2 = 1.778, p = 0.180) (Table 2).

LOS

The mean hospital stay was 3.29 ± 0.95 days in the CT group and 3.33 ± 0.91 days in the TT group, with a median of 3 days for both. No significant difference was detected (Z = −0.180, p = 0.857), indicating similar postoperative hospitalization times regardless of surgery type (Table 2).

Preoperative Laboratory Parameters

Mean preoperative corrected calcium was 9.59 ± 0.40 mg/dL in the CT group and 9.73 ± 0.47 mg/dL in the TT group (t(46) = −1.130, p = 0.264), ensuring comparable baseline levels (Table 2). Preoperative albumin levels differed significantly between CT (4.20 ± 0.37 g/dL) and TT (4.51 ± 0.35 g/dL) groups (p = 0.005), though both remained within physiological ranges.

Postoperative Day 1 Laboratory Parameters

On POD 1, mean calcium levels were 8.87 ± 0.72 mg/dL in the CT group and 8.55 ± 0.62 mg/dL in the TT group (p = 0.157). Corrected calcium levels also showed no significant difference (CT: 9.04 ± 0.68 mg/dL; TT: 8.75 ± 0.49 mg/dL; p = 0.140). However, postoperative PTH levels were significantly higher in the CT group (51.90 ± 30.26 pg/mL) compared to the TT group (30.90 ± 17.10 pg/mL) (p = 0.006, Figure 1), indicating greater parathyroid preservation in CT.

Calcium Supplementation Requirement

Calcium supplementation was required in 25% of patients in the TT group (6/24), compared with only 4.2% (1/24) in the CT group (p = 0.049, Fisher's exact test). No cases of severe symptomatic hypocalcemia requiring prolonged biochemical management were documented during the immediate post‐operative follow‐up period. However, definitive long‐term rates of permanent hypocalcemia could not be established due to the lack of systematic, long‐term follow‐up data beyond the immediate discharge and early outpatient clinic window. Regarding parathyroid management, two patients in the TT group and one in the CT group required autotransplantation. Transient hypocalcemia developed in one TT patient who underwent autotransplantation, while no hypocalcemia was observed in the CT patient. Pathology specimens reported parathyroid glands in 37% of the TT group (9/24) and 20% of the CT group (5/24). Notably, the TT group required supplementation six times more frequently (p = 0.049).

Pathological Diagnosis

In both groups, thyroidectomy was primarily performed for multinodular goiter (CT: 11/24; TT: 14/24). Papillary thyroid carcinoma was the most frequent malignancy, with 5 cases in the CT group and 4 in the TT group (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the incidence of hypocalcemia and postoperative parathyroid function between individuals receiving primary TT and those undergoing CT. Our findings demonstrate that the CT group experienced a significant clinical benefit over the TT group, evidenced by significantly higher postoperative PTH levels (51.90 ± 30.26 pg/mL vs. 30.90 ± 17.10 pg/mL; p = 0.006) and a decreased need for calcium supplementation (4.2% vs. 25.0%; p = 0.049). The nearly six‐fold decrease in supplementation requirements provides strong evidence that a staged surgical approach preserves parathyroid function more effectively than primary total thyroidectomy. Our 1:1 matching technique was successful, as shown by the lack of significant age differences between groups (p = 0.707). Our findings demonstrate that the CT group exhibited significantly higher postoperative PTH levels and a lower subsequent need for rescue calcium supplementation, suggesting a favorable clinical association with the staged approach, rather than a definitive causal mechanism, given the study's retrospective nature.

Postoperative hypocalcemia remains a common side effect following thyroid surgery, necessitating careful management of blood calcium levels [18]. In our clinical practice, patients exhibiting symptoms such as digital paresthesia, perioral numbness, or a positive Trousseau's sign are treated according to endocrinology recommendations. This includes oral calcium and vitamin D for moderate cases and injectable calcium for symptomatic instances. Merchavy et al. observed a 1.5% hypocalcemia rate in CT against 12.5% in TT (p = 0.02), supporting the lower risk observed in our sample [19]. This two‐stage procedure likely decreases hypocalcemia frequency because parathyroids on the side of the initial lobectomy have time to recover function prior to the final surgery [20].

Similarly, Rafferty et al. (2007) found that TT patients had a higher rate of transient hypoparathyroidism (27.1%) than CT patients (7.0%; p = 0.006) [21]. These consistent results imply that phased treatments result in less cumulative surgical stress. The undisturbed state of the contralateral side during the original procedure is the physiological basis for this preservation. The contralateral parathyroid glands remain unaffected by hemithyroidectomy, maintaining their natural blood supply through the inferior thyroid artery. Conversely, primary TT increases the cumulative risk of devascularization or “stunning” of all glands due to bilateral dissection in a single session [22, 23, 24, 25]. Our results align with the hypothesis that CT, performed after an appropriate interval, does not increase the risk of permanent hypocalcemia.

Our pathology results confirm this, showing the TT group had more parathyroid tissue in surgical specimens (37%) than the CT group (20%). This underscores the technical challenge of identifying and preserving all four glands in a single session. These ratios align with Merchavy et al., who found parathyroid tissue in 18% of CT cases and 32% of TT specimens [19]. Furthermore, Rafferty et al. noted that lifelong hypoparathyroidism significantly correlates with the presence of parathyroid glands in the pathologic specimen (p = 0.035) [21].

The surgical interval is crucial for CT success. A three‐month interval is the ideal time in our facility, especially when the preoperative diagnosis is not conclusively malignant or when intraoperative nerve monitoring reveals a loss of signal. Since reversible paresis can occur even without nerve transection, we verify vocal cord recovery before performing completion surgery. This three‐month window likely serves as a critical recovery period; resolution of inflammation and potential collateral revascularization on the initially operated side may mitigate cumulative risks. This approach is consistent with Glockzin et al., who suggested a three‐month delay to allow devitalized glands time to regenerate [14]. Merchavy et al. reported a mean interval of 95 days, similar to our 90‐day protocol [19]. Additionally, we excluded Graves' disease patients as their altered vascularization could negatively impact parathyroid perfusion.

PTH is an essential early predictor of surgical hypocalcemia [19, 26]. Carr et al. demonstrated that a single PTH level on postoperative day 1 (POD 1) was highly specific for predicting outcomes [4]. Our findings support the idea that sustaining elevated postoperative PTH levels lowers the risk of long‐term hypocalcemia. Interestingly, no patients had a PTH value of zero; the TT group had the lowest result (6.40 pg/mL). Thakur et al. noted that PTH values as early as 4–12 h post‐thyroidectomy are good indicators of hypocalcemia risk [5]. These results suggest that managing postoperative hypocalcemia in thyroid cancer patients can be as challenging as treating the disease itself.

Our data showed no significant difference in the LOS between TT and CT groups after their final procedures (p = 0.857). This aligns with clinical practice; postoperative care methods, including monitoring for hematomas and assessments of PTH and calcium, are identical for both surgeries. Despite a significant difference in preoperative albumin levels (p = 0.005), both mean values were within normal physiological ranges, making this baseline variance clinically inconsequential.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single‐center design may introduce biases. Second, the sample size (N = 48) is relatively small compared to larger studies like Rafferty et al. [21]. Third, we utilized a single postoperative 24‐h measurement of PTH and calcium, whereas other institutions might employ serial measurements at 4, 6, or 12 h [3, 4]. However, previous research has not demonstrated a significant difference between PTH levels evaluated at 4 and 23 h [27, 28].

Our decision for a single POD 1 PTH measurement is a practical, cost‐effective strategy influenced by logistical realities. Institutional protocol requires 24‐h surveillance for surgical safety and drain monitoring. Furthermore, laboratory infrastructure can take up to a day for PTH reporting. An urgent early PTH measurement contributes little to clinical decision‐making when patients are already staying in the hospital. By avoiding conventional serial measurements, we eliminate significant costs and reduce patient stress from multiple venipunctures, creating a patient‐friendly norm that matches real‐world data.

Fourth, a statistical correlation between autotransplantation and hypocalcemia was not established due to the small number of cases, similar to Merchavy et al. (p = 0.45) [19]. Fifth, the absence of six‐month follow‐up data prevented determining precise rates of permanent hypoparathyroidism. Finally, the definition of LOS in the CT cohort focused only on the secondary surgery. This does not account for the entire healthcare burden or cumulative anesthetic risks associated with a two‐stage technique, though it accurately reflects the immediate recovery of the definitive treatment.

Conclusions

Our research concludes that CT is linked to improved parathyroid function and a decreased risk of temporary hypocalcemia compared to primary total thyroidectomy. Higher postoperative PTH levels and decreased calcium supplementation needs indicate that a staged surgical approach, paired with an interval of at least three months, can preserve parathyroid health. For individuals requiring bilateral thyroid resection, CT serves as a safe and functionally protective alternative.
