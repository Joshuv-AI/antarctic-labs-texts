To the Editor:

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in people with chronic respiratory disease. Although COPD is now included in cardiovascular risk tools, bronchiectasis remains under-recognised despite similar systemic inflammation, hypoxaemia and exacerbation burden [1]. Epidemiological data, including a meta-analysis of >22 000 people, confirm increased CVD incidence in those with bronchiectasis [2]. However, prior studies have been single-centre, administrative or outcome-focused rather than examining risk assessment. To our knowledge, this is the first national, registry-based evaluation of cardiovascular screening in bronchiectasis, providing new insight into investigation patterns and calculated risk across a Canadian cohort.

Adults (≥18 years of age) enrolled in the Canadian Bronchiectasis and Nontuberculous Mycobacterial Infections Registry (CanBE-NTM) were included [2, 3]. Registry participants had physician-confirmed bronchiectasis based on clinical symptoms and radiological evidence. Patient charts were reviewed to identify completion of cardiovascular investigations, including haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), lipid profile, lipoprotein(a), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), ECG and echocardiography. Investigations were considered current if performed within the past 5 years, in keeping with the 2021 Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) guidelines [4]. For each patient, all variables required to calculate cardiovascular risk scores were extracted including selected lipid values (total and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and active lipid-lowering therapy). Three validated calculators were applied: the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) [5], the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) pooled cohort equation [6] and QRISK3 [7]. Risk calculations were performed according to published definitions for their respective validated age range. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic and clinical characteristics, comorbidities, investigation frequencies and cardiovascular risk estimates. Pearson's Chi-squared test was performed to determine the significance between categorical variables. Complete-case calculations without imputations were performed for missing data.

We analysed 259 patients enrolled in CanBE-NTM (median age 70 years, interquartile range 62–79 years; 56% female) (figure 1a). The cohort reflected a typical bronchiectasis population, with a high prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities: dyslipidaemia (39%), hypertension (36%) and chronic kidney disease (17%). 4% and 31% identified as active and ex-smokers respectively. COPD was documented in 16% of patients and atrial fibrillation was present in 10%.

Cardiovascular risk factor screening was nearly universal (figure 1b). HbA1c was measured in 98.5% of patients, and lipid profiles in 96.1%, with >90% of individuals aged 40–74 years having both investigations performed within the recommended 5-year window according to Canadian guidelines. Nevertheless, a small minority lacked recent screening, 6% without lipid testing and 3% without HbA1c. ECG was completed in 83% of patients, indicating broad use of baseline cardiac assessment. More advanced investigations were performed less consistently: echocardiography in 27% and NT-proBNP in 35%. Notably, among patients with coexisting COPD, the frequency of NT-proBNP testing was substantially higher than in patients without COPD (44.9% versus 28.3%, p=0.01). This difference was not observed for echocardiography testing between patients with coexisting COPD and patients without COPD (29.9% versus 24.3%, p=0.32). Lipoprotein(a), recently recommended by the CCS and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association for once-in-lifetime measurement, was obtained in only 8% of patients.

When cardiovascular risk was estimated using validated calculators applied to their respective validated age range, median 10-year risk ranged from 7% (FRS, n=150) to 13% (QRISK3, n=197), with the highest values consistently identified by QRISK3 amongst a unified 40–74 year old age cohort (8% in FRS, 7% in ASCVD and 10% in QRISK3) (figure 1c).

This first national audit of cardiovascular evaluation in bronchiectasis shows strong adherence to guideline-based screening but notable care gaps. Nearly all patients had HbA1c and lipid testing, with >90% completing these within 5 years. This near-universal assessment likely reflects heightened clinician awareness of cardiometabolic risk in chronic respiratory disease.

ECG was common (83%), while echocardiography and NT-proBNP were used more selectively (27% and 35%). NT-proBNP testing was more frequent in patients with coexisting COPD, which may reflect clinician awareness of cardiopulmonary overlap. Echocardiography showed a similar, though nonsignificant, trend (29.9% versus 24.3%), likely limited by sample size. These patterns parallel COPD, where high baseline cardiovascular risk and post-exacerbation events are well established. COPD's inclusion in QRISK3 as an independent risk factor underscores this link, yet bronchiectasis, despite similar cardiovascular associations, remains absent from validated risk tools. However, further interpretation is limited and future studies are needed to draw more conclusive understanding of these practice patterns.

The 10-year cardiovascular risk in this cohort (median 7–13%) exceeds general population estimates (<10% for similar ages), placing many people with bronchiectasis in a higher risk category. These findings align with the consistently higher risk estimates identified by QRISK3, which includes a broader set of unique but significant variables such as body mass index (BMI) and atrial fibrillation. Bronchiectasis alone is an independent risk factor supported by Navaratnam et al. [8], who reported increased cardiovascular events in people with bronchiectasis independent of smoking and comorbidities, and the meta-analysis by Gramegna et al. [2], which confirmed this elevated risk across >22 000 such people. Although QRISK3 identified a higher missing value rate (<17% versus <4% in FRS/ASCVD cohorts) mainly driven by incomplete clinical assessments (e.g. BMI and blood pressure), these findings suggest perhaps underappreciated vascular risk factor burden, and underestimated cardiovascular risk of patients with bronchiectasis and related chronic respiratory inflammatory disorders. Existing cardiovascular risk calculators may not be relevant markers of chronic inflammation despite the same goal of cardiovascular risk stratification. Our findings extend this literature by moving beyond incidence data to describe real-world practice patterns in cardiovascular evaluation, highlighting that while routine risk factor testing is broadly implemented, newer recommendations, such as once-in-lifetime lipoprotein(a) assessment, remain underutilised, with uptake in only 8% in our cohort. This finding is important given that lipoprotein(a) is independently associated with CVD and recurrence risk of CVD, resulting in the adoption of lipoprotein(a) testing into major dyslipidaemia guidelines since 2019, and is increasingly recommended by major CVD society guidelines [4, 9, 10]. The observed gap between current clinical practice and evidence-based care in bronchiectasis patients underscores an important opportunity to improve cardiovascular risk assessment and management.

Although NT-proBNP and echocardiography are not currently recommended for routine risk stratification, these tests are often used when cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms overlap, supported by ∼30% use across our study cohort, reflecting that bronchiectasis – like COPD – is a systemic disorder with important cardiovascular implications. Our findings reveal both the burden of cardiovascular risk and the current state of screening in bronchiectasis. Given that COPD, but not bronchiectasis, is included in QRISK3, a similar approach may ultimately be warranted. Integrating systematic cardiovascular risk assessment into bronchiectasis care pathways, particularly in specialist clinics, could help mitigate excess morbidity and mortality.

We acknowledge limitations in our study. The CanBE-NTM Registry continues to grow relying on active recruitment, which may over-represent more complex patients at academic centres with closer follow-up and more concentrated resources, which may limit the generalisation of findings in the community. The use of registry data restricted further analysis of lipid profile characterisation and their role in interpreting risk estimates. Longitudinal data were not included given the cross-sectional nature of this retrospective chart review study, limiting the interpretation of our measured risk. Future studies should determine whether systematic cardiovascular screening improves outcomes and whether exacerbation-related risk mirrors the acute vascular vulnerability observed in COPD.
