Universal Solution to Kronecker Product Decomposition
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
This paper provides a general solution for the Kronecker product decomposition (KPD) of vectors, matrices, and hypermatrices. First, an algorithm, namely, monic decomposition algorithm (MDA), is reviewed. It consists of a set of projections from a higher dimension Euclidian space to its factor-dimension subspaces. It is then proved that the KPD of vectors is solvable, if and only if, the project mappings provide the required decomposed vectors. Hence it provides an easily verifiable necessary and sufficient condition for the KPD of vectors. Then an algorithm is proposed to calculate the least square error approximated decomposition. Using it finite times a finite sum (precise) KPD of any vectors can be obtained. Then the swap matrix is used to make the elements of a matrix re-arranging, and then provides a method to convert the KPD of matrices to its corresponding KPD of vectors. It is proved that the KPD of a matrix is solvable, if and only if, the KPD of its corresponding vector is solvable. In this way, the necessary and sufficient condition, and the followed algorithms for approximate and finite sum KPDs for matrices are also obtained. Finally, the permutation matrix is introduced and used to convert the KPD of any hypermatrix to KPD of its corresponding vector. Similarly to matrix case, the necessary and sufficient conditions for solvability and the techniques for vectors and matrices are also applicable for hypermatrices, though some additional algorithms are necessary. Several numerical examples are included to demonstrate this universal KPD solving method.

Although many theoretical and numerical solutions for KPD have been obtained for various situations and to meet different demands, but in general it is still a challenging problem. For instance, to our best knowledge, theoretically an easily verifiable necessary and sufficient condition for KPD solvability is still open, and numerically a universal efficient computation is still developing.

Although there has been some efforts in KPD of hypermatrices
[
3

, the main efforts have been focused on the KPD of matrices. Strangely enough, the KPD of vectors are not discussed enough.

Recently, an easily computable method, called the monic decomposition algorithm (MDA) has been proposed by us
[
9
]
to solve the decomposition of vectors. The decomposable vectors are called the hypervectors. MDA consists of a set of mappings, which map a vector to its factor-dimensional subspaces. When a vector is decomposable, the projected vectors form its factor components.

MDA provides not only a necessary and sufficient condition for the MDA of vectors, but also a constructive algorithm to realize the factorization. It is obvious that the algorithm is robust, because it consists of a set of linear mappings, which are continuous. Hence if a higher dimensional vector is near decomposable, the MDA will provide an approximate decomposition.

If a vector is not decomposable, this paper develops an algorithm, which use the approximate decomposition obtained from MDA as its initial values and provides a decomposition with least square error. Secondly, if the finite sum decomposition is required, we can use the algorithm on the error vector (the difference between original vector and its approximated hypervector) to get second term approximate decomposition. Keep doing finite times, the algorithm yields the finite sum (precise) KPD of the original vector.
In one word, the paper develops a numerical method to solve the three kinds of KPD for vectors.

Next, as a key step, we prove a result which shows that the KPD of a matrix is equivalent to the KPD of its corresponding vector.
Hence the necessary and sufficient condition for the decomposability, the exact KPD, the least square error approximate KPD, and the precise finite sum KPD of matrices are also solved completely.

Finally, we consider the KPD of hypermatrices. For a hypermatrix of order
d
, we suggest a standard type of matrix expression as its default matrix form. Then the Kronecker product of hypermatrices can be performed via their matrix forms.
When
d=3
, the hypermatrices are also called the cubic matrices. The a default matrix expression may be considered as the one used in papers of t-product
[
15
,
1
]
.

Using permutation matrix, the elements of a hypermatrix can be re-arranged. By the light of the permutation, the KPD of hypermatrices can also be reduced to the KPD of its corresponding vectors. That means
the necessary and sufficient condition for the decomposability, the exact KPD, the least square error approximate KPD, and the precise finite sum KPD of an arbitrary hypermatrix are also solved completely
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 contains some preliminaries including the semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, hypervectors, algebraic structure of index set, and hypermatrix with its matrix expression. The permutation matrix is also introduced. Section 3 shows the difference between matrix set and matrix form. It is emphasized that a hypermatrix is not a matrix but a matrix set. Section 4 considers the KPD of vectors. The MDA is introduced. Then the exact KPD is discussed. Using MDA, a necessary and sufficient condition for exact KPD is obtained, which is constructive via a decomposition algorithm. The approximate MDA and finite sum (precise) MDA of vectors are then following. The kernel technique for KPD is developed in this section. The MDA of matrices is discussed in Section 5. The key point of this KPD is to convert it to the corresponding KPD of vectors, and the equivalence is proved. Then the necessary and sufficient condition, the exact, approximate, and the finite sum KPD of matrices are all obtained. In Section 6 the formal definition of Kronecker product of hypermatrices is defined, and three kinds of Kronecker products are presented, which are outer product, partition-based product, and paired product. The KPD of the first two products are solved in this secton. Section 7 devotes to the paired product of hypermatrices and its KPD. The normal matrix form of order
d
hypermatrices is constructed. The Kronecker product of hypermatrices is described via the matrix forms of the factor hypmatrices. Then the KPD of an arbitrary order
d
hypmatrix is solved via converting it to the KPD of its corresponding vector form. It follows that the necessary and sufficient condition, the exact, approximate, and the finite sum KPD of hypermatrices are all solvable. Section 8 is a brief conclusion.

III
Matrix Set vs Matrix Form

Consider a hypermatrix with order
2
, say,
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i,j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
. It is worth to emphasize that

{\mathcal{A}}
differs from a matrix. In fact it has
6
different matrix expressions as

\begin{array}[]{l}A_{1}=M^{\{i,j\}\times\emptyset}({\mathcal{A}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn},\\
A_{2}=M^{\{j,i\}\times\emptyset}({\mathcal{A}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn},\\
A_{3}=M^{\{i\}\times\{j\}})({\mathcal{A}})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},\\
A_{4}=M^{\emptyset\times\{i,j\}}({\mathcal{A}})=A_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn},\\
A_{5}=M^{\emptyset\times\{j,i\}}({\mathcal{A}})=A_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn},\\
A_{6}=M^{\{j\}\times\{i\}})({\mathcal{A}})=A_{3}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\times m},\\
\end{array}

When we consider the matrix properties, a special matrix expression must be pre-assigned!

Even when an order
1
hypermatrix, it has two matrix forms as row vector and column vector. To distinct the elements in a matrix and the spacial matrix form of a set of data, we call a set of ordered data the
matrix set
, and the special matrices constructed from this data set the
matrix form
.

It is worth to emphasize that a hypermatrix is a “matrix set” but not a “matrix form”. It is easy to verify that a hypermatrix of order
d
has
(d+1)!
different matrix forms. To some extent, the name “hypermatrix” is confusing. It is a matrix set but not a matrix form. The objects discussed in Linear Algebra or Matrix Theory are matrices. Only when a special matrix expression of a hypermatrix is pre-assigned, the results from matrix theory can then be applied. Ask a hypermatrix to have matrix properties is ridiculous.

The following property is matrix form expression independent.

Definition III.1

[
16
]
Given
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and a permutation
\sigma\in{\bf S}_{d}
.
The
\sigma
-transpose of
{\mathcal{A}}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma}
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma}:=(a_{\sigma(1),\cdots,\sigma(d)}).

(25)

The vector form of a hypermatrix could be one of the most convenient matrix forms of a hypermatrix.

A natural question is: how to get
V({\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma}
from
V({\mathcal{A}})
? It will be seen in the sequel that this is a key issue in the further discussion.

Consider
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and a permutation
\sigma\in{\bf S}_{d}
. Construct a matrix set of column vector as

D_{\sigma}:=\left\{\delta^{j_{1}}_{n_{\sigma(1)}}\delta^{j_{2}}_{n_{\sigma(2)}}\cdots\delta^{j_{d}}_{n_{\sigma(d)}}\;|\;j_{k}\in[1,n_{\sigma(k)}],\;k\in[1,d]\right\}.

Then arrange the vectors in

is the order as

Id(j_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},j_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\cdots,j_{\sigma^{-1}(d)};n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{d}\}.

The resulting matrix is denoted by

W^{\sigma}_{[n_{1}\times n_{2}\times\cdots\times n_{d}]}

is called the

-permutation matrix.

Proposition III.3

[

The

Remark III.4

Formula (
26
) is a fundamental tool in our later study. In fact, it says that if we has a matrix set
as

D=\{a_{i_{1},\cdots.i_{d}}\;|\;i_{k}\in[1,n_{k}],~k\in[1,d]\},

We can arrange the elements into a vector using different orders. The order

Monic Decomposition Algorithm (MDA) was firstly proposed by us in

Proposition IV.1

Assume

, where
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{i}}
,
i\in[1,d]
, and
e(x)=e
, and

,

.
Set
j:=e
and
(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}):=(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{d})

(e_{1},\cdots,e_{d})
satisfy (
(i)
)-(
19

Proof.
Using
\vec{j}=\vec{i}_{1}\vec{i}_{2}\cdots\vec{i}_{d}
to label the elements of

h_{0}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{0}(x_{i})

\displaystyle x_{e}=(x_{1})_{e_{1}}(x_{2})_{e_{2}}\times(x_{d})_{e_{d}}.

(27)

Hence, the index

\Box

Definition IV.2

(KPD for vectors) Consider

, where

x

\displaystyle x=\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}.

(28)

It is easy to verify the following result
[
8
]

Proposition IV.3

Assume
0\neq x=\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}

\displaystyle z_{i}=\mu_{i}x_{i},\quad i\in[1,d],

(29)

where

Using Proposition
IV.1
, for a given vector

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\Xi^{e}_{(i;d)}:=[\delta_{n_{1}}^{e_{1}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\cdots\otimes[\delta_{n_{i-1}}^{e_{i-1}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes I_{n_{i}}\otimes\\
~~[\delta_{n_{i+1}}^{e_{i}+1}]^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\cdots\otimes[\delta_{n_{r}}^{e_{r}}]^{\mathrm{T}},\quad i\in[1,d].\end{array}

The following Proposition shows how to calculate the component vectors from a hypervector. It is called the monic decomposition algorithm (MDA).

Proposition IV.4

Consider
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\bf n}}
, where
{\bf n}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}

\displaystyle x_{i}:=\Xi^{e}_{(i;d)}(x_{0}),\quad i\in[1,d],

(32)

then all

\displaystyle x=a\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}.

(33)

Proof
Note that for tow column vectors
\xi

\begin{array}[]{l}[(\delta_{n_{k}}^{e_{k}}]^{\mathrm{T}}x_{k}=1,\quad k\in[1,d]~\mbox{and}~k\neq i,\\

Using the formula that
(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)
, Equations (
32

\Box

(i)

Approximate Decomposition

Given an

and assume
{\bf n}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}

Example IV.7

Consider Example
IV.6
again. Let

be the same as in Example

\displaystyle E=(x_{0}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})^{\mathrm{T}}(x_{0}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}).

Then it is easy to calculate that for the solution in (

As aforementioned that when

To get the least square approximated KPD for non-decomposable vectors, we provide the following algorithm.

Algorithm IV.8

Given
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\bf n}}
, with
{\bf n}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
. The least square approximated KPD
can be obtained as follows.

(i)

Calculate
e=e(x)
,
h_{0}=x_{e}
,
x_{0}=x/h_{0}
, and then using Proposition
IV.1
to calculate
e_{i}
,
i\in[1,d]
.

(ii)

Define candidate component vectors as

\displaystyle x_{i}=(\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{e_{i}-1},1,u^{i}_{1},\cdots,u^{i}_{n_{i}-e_{i}})^{\mathrm{T}},\quad i\in[1,d].

(38)

(iii)

Using MDA to get the initial values of the iteration as

\displaystyle x_{i}(0)=\Xi^{e}_{(i;d)}x_{0},\quad i\in[1,d].

(39)

(
39
) is equivalent to initialize
u=u(0)
, where
u=\{u^{i}_{j}\;|\;j\in[1,n_{i}-e_{i}],~i\in[1,d]\}
.

(iv)

Consider the square error

\displaystyle E(u)=(x_{0}-\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}x_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}(x_{0}-\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}).

(40)

Its gradients are

\displaystyle D_{i,j}:=\frac{\partial E(u)}{\partial u^{i}_{j}},\quad j\in[1,n_{i}-e_{i}],~i\in[1,d].

(41)

Using gradient descent method, we choose a step length
h>0
and define the iteration as

Step 1:
Set
u^{i}_{j}(0)
as in (
39
).

Step 2:
Calculate
E(0)
by (
40
).

Step k:
Set

u^{i}_{j}(k+1)=u^{i}_{j}(k)-D_{i,j}*h,\quad j\in[1,n_{i}-e_{i}],~i\in[1,d].

Calculate

E(k+1)=E(u(k+1)).

If
E(k+1)<E(k)
, set
u^{i}_{j}(0)=u^{i}_{j}(k+1)
and go to Step 2.

Else Stop.

Proposition IV.9

The algorithm
IV.8
converges to the least square error solution.

Proof.
It is easy to see that for a fixed
u^{s}_{t}
, assume
u^{i}_{j}
,
(i,j)\neq(s,t)
are fixed, then the
E(u)
is a quadratic form of
u_{i,j}
. Hence
E(u)
is a higher dimensional paraboloid. The minimum value point is unique, and it is the only stationary point. Hence the gradient descent algorithm must converge to the minimum value.

\Box

N\in{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bf m}\times{\bf n}}
with
{\bf m}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}
, and
{\bf n}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
. The KPD problem is: finding
B_{i}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
,
i\in[1,d]
, such that

\displaystyle N=B_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes B_{d}.

(52)

Here we consider only the case when
d=2
. Given
N=(n_{u,v})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{mp\times nq}
, our goal is to find
A=(a_{i,j})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
B=(b_{r,s})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
, such that

N=A\otimes B.

First, we calculate
V_{c}(A\otimes B)
: Note that

A\otimes B=[a^{1}\otimes b^{1},\cdots,a^{1}\otimes b^{q},a^{2}\otimes b^{1},\cdots,a^{n}\otimes b^{q}],

where
a^{i}=\Col_{i}(A)
,
i\in[1,n]
,
b^{i}=\Col_{i}(B)
,
i\in[1,q]
.
It follows that

V_{c}(A\otimes B)=\begin{bmatrix}a^{1}\otimes b^{1}\\
a^{1}\otimes b^{2}\\
\cdots\\
a^{1}\otimes b^{q}\\
a^{2}\otimes b^{1}\\
\vdots\\
a^{n}\otimes b^{q}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Setting

c^{i,j}_{r,s}:=a_{i,j}b_{r,s},

then a straightforward computation shows that the elements of
V_{c}(A\otimes B)
are

D=\{c^{i,j}_{p,q}\;|\;i\in[1,m],j\in[1,n],r\in[1,p],s\in[1,q]\},

and they are arranged in the order of

Id(j,s,i,r;n\times q\times m\times p)=\vec{j}\vec{s}\vec{i}\vec{r}.

On the other hand
V(A)=V_{c}(A)
with elements in

D_{A}=\{a_{i,j}\;|\;i\in[1,m],j\in[1,n]\},

and they are arranged in the order of
Id(j,i;n,m)=\vec{j}\vec{i}
.

V(B)=V_{c}(B)
with elements in

D_{B}=\{b_{r,s}\;|\;r\in[1,p],s\in[1,q]\},

and they are arranged in the order of
Id(s,r;q,p)=\vec{s}\vec{r}
.
Then
V(A)\ltimes V(B)
has elements in
D
with the order as

Id(j,i,s,r;n,m,q,p)=\vec{j}\vec{i}\vec{s}\vec{r}.

Now to convert
\vec{j}\vec{i}\vec{s}\vec{r}
to
\vec{j}\vec{s}\vec{i}\vec{r}
, a swap matrix
W_{[m,q]}
can be used to construct

\displaystyle\Psi:=I_{n}\otimes W_{[m,q]}\otimes I_{p}.

(53)

Then it is clear that

\displaystyle V(A\otimes B)=\Psi V(A)\ltimes V(B).

(54)

Since
\Psi
is an orthogonal matrix, (
55
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}V(A\otimes B)=V(A)\ltimes V(B).

(55)

This leads to the following result immediately.

Theorem V.2

Given
N\in{\mathcal{M}}_{mp\times nq}
.
N
is paired Kronecker product decomposable w.r.t.
(m\times n,~p\times q)
, if and only if,
\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}V(N)
is decomposabe w.r.t.
mn\times pq
, where
\Psi
is defined in (
53
).

We use an example to descripe the decomposition process.

Example V.3

Given

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1&2&-1\\
0&0&0&1&0&-2\\
-1&-2&1&1&2&-1\\
-1&0&2&1&0&-2\\
\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{4\times 6}.

Consider its paired KPD w.r.t.
2\times 2,2\times 3
.

Using (

), we construct

\begin{array}[]{l}\Psi=I_{2}\otimes W_{[2,3]}\otimes I_{2}=\delta_{24}[1,2,7,8,3,4,9,10,\\
~~5,6,11,12,13,14,19,20,15,16,21,22,17,18,23,24].\end{array}

Then

\begin{array}[]{l}V:=\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}V(A)=[0,0,0,0,0,0,-1,-1,\\
~~-2,0,1,2,1,1,2,0,-1,-2,1,1,2,0,-1,-2]^{\mathrm{T}}.\end{array}

Hence
e(V)=7
and
h_{0}(V)=-1
. Set
x=V/h_{0}
and then use MDA on

.
First it is easy to calculate the

and

. Then the decomposition mappings can be constructed as

\begin{array}[]{l}\Xi^{e}_{(1;2)}=I_{4}\otimes[\delta_{6}^{1}]^{\mathrm{T}},\\

Remark V.4

(i)

Consider the multi-fold decomposition

. Assume

Next, we consider the approximate KPD. It is immediate consequence of Theorem

that if the exact decomposition does not exist, then, similarly to vector case, we can use the components obtained from decomposition mappings as the initial value and use the gradient descent method to find the least square error approximate decomposition. We use an example to demonstrate this.

Example V.5

Consider

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1&2&-1\\
0&0&0&1&0&-2\\
2&-2&1&1&2&-1\\
-1&0&2&1&0&-2\\
\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{4\times 6}.

It is similar to the one in Example

only the

is changed from

to

.

and

\begin{array}[]{l}x:=\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}x_{0}=[0,0,0,0,0,0,1,-0.5,-1,0,0.5,1,\\
~~0.5,0.5,1,0,-0.5,-1,0.5,0.5,1,0,-0.5,-1].\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{l}x_{1}=(0,1,u,v)^{\mathrm{T}},\\

~~(u-0.5)^{2}+(uy_{1}-0.5)^{2}+(uy_{2}-1)^{2}+(uy_{3})^{2}\\
~~+(uy_{4}+0.5)^{2}+(uy_{5}+1)^{2}+(v-0.5)^{2}+(vy_{1}-0.5)^{2}\\
~~+(vy_{2}-1)^{2}+(vy_{3})^{2}+(vy_{4}+0.5)^{2}+(vy_{5}+1)^{2}.\end{array}

The gradients are

\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\partial E}{\partial u}=2[(u-0.5)+y_{1}(uy_{1}-0.5)+y_{2}(uy_{2}-1)+y_{3}(uy_{3})\\

Choosing

, setting

~y_{3}(0)=0,~y_{4}(0)=0.5,~y_{5}(0)=1,\end{array}

VI
Kronecker Products of HyperMatrices

VI-A

Form-based Kronecker Product

Consider
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{u\times v}
. Can we define a Kronecker product for them? In fact, the answer is “No”. Then the Kronecker Product of
A=(a_{i,j})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
B=(b_{r,s})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{u\times v}
is properly defined. Where the
A
and
B
are considered as two normal matrix expressions of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
respectively.
If we change the matrix expressions
V({\mathcal{A}})
and
V({\mathcal{B}})
, the corresponding Kronecker product is completely different from the previous one.

From this perspective one sees easily that the Kronecker product of two order
2
hypermatrices, as two matrix sets, can not be uniquely defined. While the Kronecker product of two matrices, as two matrix forms, is properly defined.

Because of this, we may preassign two special matrix forms, say
A
and
B
for the hypermatrices
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
respectively. Then we define a form-based Kronecker product of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}:=A\otimes B.

(56)

In this way, many different kinds of Kronecker products can be defined.

In fact, whole matrix theory is based on the fixed matrix forms of order
1
and order
2
hypermatrices,
which are vectors and matrices respectively. That is, let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then we use

x=M^{i\times\emptyset}({\mathcal{A}})
as its default matrix form, which is a vector. Similarly, let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
. Then we use

A=M^{i\times j}({\mathcal{A}})
as its default matrix form, which is a matrix. Then the whole matrix theory is based on these default matrix forms. We may call these matrix forms as normal matrix form. When other forms are required, the
x^{\mathrm{T}}
, or
A^{\mathrm{T}}
,
V_{r}(A)
,
V_{c}(A)
etc. must be defined.

To develop the matrix theory for hypermatrices, the (default) normal matrix form of order
d
hypermatrices is very reasonable.
In a normal form the operations and the numerical computations become much easier.

For instance, consider a cubic matrix
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k,i,j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n\times s}
. To investigate the t-product of cubic matrices and its applications, a normal form of cubic matrices is proposed as follows
[
1
,
7
]
:

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}a^{1}_{1,1}&a^{1}_{1,2}&\cdots&a^{1}_{1,n}\\
a^{1}_{2,1}&a^{1}_{2,2}&\cdots&a^{1}_{2,n}\\
\cdots&~&~&~\\
a^{1}_{m,1}&a^{1}_{m,2}&\cdots&a^{1}_{m,n}\\
\vdots&~&~&~\\
a^{s}_{1,1}&a^{s}_{1,2}&\cdots&a^{s}_{1,n}\\
a^{s}_{2,1}&a^{s}_{2,2}&\cdots&a^{s}_{2,n}\\
\cdots&~&~&~\\
a^{s}_{m,1}&a^{s}_{m,2}&\cdots&a^{s}_{m,n}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{(1)}\\
A^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
A^{(s)}\end{bmatrix},

(57)

where
a^{k}_{i,j}=a_{k,i,j}
.

VI-B

Outer and Partition Based Kronecker Products

The Kronecker product of two matrices
A=(a_{i,j})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
B=(b_{r,s})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{u\times v}
can be realized in the following two steps:

Step 1: Produce a set of data, called the Kronecker set, as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}D_{AB}=\left\{a_{i,j}b_{r,s}\;|\;i\in[1,m],\right.\\
~~\left.j\in[1,n],~r\in[1,u],~s\in[1,v]\right\}.\end{array}

Step 2: Arrange the data in
D_{AB}
into a matrix in the order that
Id(\alpha,\beta;mu,nv)
, where
\vec{\alpha}=\vec{i}\vec{r}
is the row index and
\vec{\beta}=\vec{j}\vec{s}
is the column index. Then the resulting matrix is
A\otimes B
.

In fact, we can define some other Kronecker products of matrices. Say,

A\boxtimes B:=B\otimes A.

Based on this observation, the Kronecker product of hypermatrices can also be defined through these two steps.

Definition VI.1

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{r}}
and

{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{s}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{s}}
.

(i)

The Kronecker matrix set is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}D_{{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}}=\left\{a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}}b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{s}}\;|\;i_{k}\in[1,m_{k}],k\in[1,r],\right.\\
~~\left.~j_{q}\in[1,n_{q}],~q\in[1,s]\right\}.\end{array}

(ii)

A special arrange of the elements of
D_{{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}}
is called a Kronecker product of hypermatrices.

Let
A
and
B
be two matrix expressions of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
under a pre-assigned matrix form. Then a Kronecker product of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
can be defined by
A\otimes B
.
Next, we consider some commonly used Kronecker products of hypermatrices. They are defined by using pre-assigned special matrix forms.

•

Outer Kronecker Product.

Definition VI.2

[
16
]
Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{s}}
and

{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{t}}
. The outer-product of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, denoted by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\circledcirc{\mathcal{B}}:={\mathcal{C}}=(c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s},j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{s}\times n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{t}},

(62)

where

c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s},j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}}=a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}}b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}}.

Remark VI.3

(i)

Although the product defined by (
62
) is commonly called the outer product of hypermatrices, it is easy to see that it is a Kronecker product, so we call it the outer Kronecker product.

(ii)

The outer product of two hypermatrices is commonly denoted by
\otimes
. But to distinct it with other Kronecker product of hypermatrices, we use
\circledcirc
instead.

(iii)

It is easy to verify that this Kronecker product is based on the matrix form
V({\mathcal{A}})
of
{\mathcal{A}}
. That is:

\displaystyle V({\mathcal{A}}\circledcirc{\mathcal{B}}):=V({\mathcal{A}})\otimes V({\mathcal{A}}).

(63)

•

Partition-Based Kronecker Product.

Definition VI.4

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{r}}
and

{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{s}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{s}}
. Assume
\vec{i}=\vec{a}\vec{b}
and
\vec{j}=\vec{c}\vec{d}
.
Based on these two index partitions, we have spacial matrix expressions as

\begin{array}[]{l}A:=M^{\vec{a}\times\vec{b}}({\mathcal{A}}),\\
B:=M^{\vec{c}\times\vec{d}}({\mathcal{B}}).\\
\end{array}

Then the partition-based Kronecker product of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, denoted by

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{A}}\boxtimes{\mathcal{B}},

is defined by

\displaystyle M^{\vec{a}\vec{c}\times\vec{b}\vec{d}}({\mathcal{C}}):=A\otimes B.

(64)

Note that (
64
) itself shows that partition-based Kromecker product is
defined by using pre-assigned special matrix forms.

•

Paired Kronecker Product.

Definition VI.5

[
3
]
Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}}
and

{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
.
The Kronecker product of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, denoted by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}}:={\mathcal{C}}=(c_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{u_{1}\times\cdots\times u_{d}},

(65)

where
\vec{k}_{s}=\vec{i}_{s}\vec{j}_{s}
,
u_{s}=m_{s}\times n_{s}
, and

\displaystyle c_{k_{1},\cdots k_{s}}=a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}}b_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{s}},\quad s\in[1,d].

(66)

This kind of Kronecker products is one of the most commonly accepted Kronecker products of hypermatrices.

Remark VI.6

(i)

In Definition
VI.5
since
\vec{k}_{s}=\vec{i}_{s}\vec{j}_{s}
we have

\displaystyle k_{s}=(i_{s}-1)n_{s}+j_{s},\quad s\in[1,d].

(67)

We recall (
(i)
)-(
19
) for the index conversion.

(ii)

This definition coincides the conventional definition of Kronecker product of matrices, so it is widely accepted.

(iii)

When the order of
{\mathcal{A}}
is not the same as
{\mathcal{B}}
the the dumy index
\emptyset
can be used to make the orders being formally equal. In this way, the Paired Kronecker Product is applicable to two arbitrary hypermatrices.

It is easy to see that this Kronecker product is independent of particular matrix expression as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition VI.7

Assume

\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{C}}=(c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}n_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}n_{d}},\\
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{r_{1},\cdots,r_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}},\\
{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{s_{1},\cdots,s_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}.\\
\end{array}

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}},

if and only if, for any
0\leq k\leq d

\begin{array}[]{l}C=M^{\vec{i}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{k}\times\vec{i}_{k+1}\cdots\vec{i}_{d}}({\mathcal{C}}),\\
A=M^{\vec{r}_{1}\cdots\vec{r}_{k}\times\vec{r}_{k+1}\cdots\vec{r}_{d}}({\mathcal{A}}),\\
B=M^{\vec{s}_{1}\cdots\vec{s}_{k}\times\vec{s}_{k+1}\cdots\vec{s}_{d}}({\mathcal{B}}),\\
\end{array}

satisfy

C=A\otimes B.

VII
Solvability of KPD of Hypermatrices

VII-A

KPD of Outer and Partition-Based Kronecker Products

First, we consider the outer product decomposition, which is defined as follows.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{r}\times n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{s}}
. To find
{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\times m_{r}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}=(c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{s}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\times n_{s}}
,
d=r+s
, such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}\circledcirc{\mathcal{C}},

(68)

the index

\vec{k}=\vec{i}\vec{j}.

Then the following result is obvious.

Proposition VII.1

{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{r}\times n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{s}}
,
d=r+s
is outer Kronecker product decomposable w.r.t.
r\times s
, if and only if,

V({\mathcal{A}})
is decomposable w.r.t.
{\bf m}\times{\bf n}
, where
{\bf m}=\prod_{k=1}^{r}m_{k}
and
{\bf n}=\prod_{k=1}^{s}n_{k}

Now the problem is converted to the decomposition of vectors, so its solvability is well known.

Next, we consider the partition-based KPD of hypermatrices. The following result is obvious.

Proposition VII.2

{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}}
, where
m_{k}=u_{k}v_{k}
,
k\in[1,d]
, is partition-based KPD ronecker product decomposable w.r.t. a partition

\vec{k}=\vec{i}\vec{j}
,
if and only if,

M^{\vec{i}\times\vec{j}}({\mathcal{A}})

is decomposable w.r.t.
u^{i}\times v^{i}
and
u^{j}\times v^{j}
, where

u^{i}=\prod_{k\in{\bf i}}u_{k}
,
u^{j}=\prod_{k\in{\bf j}}u_{k}
,

v^{i}=\prod_{k\in{\bf i}}v_{k}
,
v^{j}=\prod_{k\in{\bf j}}v_{k}
.

Now the partition-based KPD of hybermatrix is converted to the matrix KPD problem, which has been completely solved before.

The paired KPD is relatively complicated. Some preparations are necessary.

VII-B

Matrix Expression of Paired Kronecker Product

As aforementioned that the Kronecker product of matrices can be considered as the Kronecker product of order
2
hypermatrices under the default matrix form
A=M^{r\times n}({\mathcal{A}})
for
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
.
Motivated by this, it seems to be convenient to express the paired Kronecker product of hypermatrices in a “normal form”.
Then the paired KPD can also be expressed via the normal form.

To make the study easier, we first consider the case of order
3
(i.e., cubic matrices).

Assume the normal form of
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m\times n}
is as in (
57
). Then the following result comes from straightforward computations.

Proposition VII.3

Let
A=(a_{k,i,j})
and
B=(b_{r,p,q})
be the (default) matric expressions of
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times m\times n}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{t\times u\times v}
respectively. Then,

(i)

\displaystyle V({\mathcal{A}})=V_{r}(A),

(69)

Motivated by the normal form of cubic matrix, the normal form expression of a hypermatrix with order

Similarly to cubic matrices, setting a default form for order
d>3
set of hypermatrices is of first importance. Because only under a certain matrix form the paired Kronecker product can be computed by the matrix product of their default matrix forms. Then further manipulating hypermatrices, such as KPD etc., becomes executable.

The idea for calculating paired Kronecker product of hypermatrices via the product of their default matrix forms is as follows.
Let
A
and
B
be certain matrix expressions of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
respectively. Then the matrix set of

A\otimes B
is the same as the matrix set of
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}
. Hence, we can get the matrix set of
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}
from
A\otimes B
. Then we need only to arrange the elements of this matrix set in the order of the default matrix form of

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}
.

Assume
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{d}}
. Fix an index partition as
\vec{k}=\vec{i}\vec{j}
. Say,
\vec{i}=\vec{k}_{1}\cdots\vec{k}_{s}
and
\vec{j}=\vec{k}_{s+1}\cdots\vec{k}_{d}
. Based on this fixed index partition we can express

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s},j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots m_{s}\times n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{t}}.

(72)

Similarly, another order
d
hypmatrix
{\mathcal{B}}
can also be expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{p_{1},\cdots,p_{s},q_{1},\cdots,q_{t}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{u_{1}\times\cdots u_{s}\times v_{1}\times\cdots\times v_{t}}.

(73)

Then the default matrix express of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, based on the fixed index partition, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}A=M^{\vec{i}\times\vec{p}}({\mathcal{A}}),\\
B=M^{\vec{j}\times\vec{q}}({\mathcal{B}}).\\
\end{array}

Set

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}}.

Then the default matrix form of
{\mathcal{C}}
is

\displaystyle C=M^{\vec{a}\times\vec{b}}({\mathcal{C}}),

(76)

where
\vec{a}=\vec{a}_{1}\cdots\vec{a}_{s}
,
\vec{b}=\vec{b}_{1}\cdots\vec{b}_{t}
, and
\vec{a}_{\ell}=\vec{i}_{\ell}\vec{p}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in[1,s]
,

\vec{b}_{\ell}=\vec{j}_{\ell}\vec{q}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in[1,t]
.

Consider
C
, its
rows
are labeled by
\vec{i}_{1}\vec{p}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{s}\vec{p}_{s}
and its columns are labeled by

\vec{j}_{1}\vec{q}_{1}\cdots\vec{j}_{t}\vec{q}_{t}
. That is how the matrix set elements

\left\{a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}j_{1},\cdots j_{t}}b_{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}q_{1},\cdots q_{t}}\right\}

are arranged in
C
.

On the other hand, we consider

E:=A\otimes B,

where the same matrix set elements are arranges as follows:
The rows are labeled by
\vec{i}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{s}\vec{p}_{1}\cdots\vec{p}_{s}
and the columns are labeled by

\vec{i}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{s}\vec{p}_{1}\cdots\vec{p}_{s}
.

Now we know how to get the default matrix expression
C
of the paired Kronecker product from
E
.

(i)

The row index should be changed from
\vec{i}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{s}\vec{p}_{1}\cdots\vec{p}_{s}
to
\vec{i}_{1}\vec{p}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{s}\vec{p}_{s}

A permutation
\sigma\in{\bf S}_{2s}
is required as

\sigma(i)=\begin{cases}2i-1,\quad i\in[1,s],\\
2(i-s),\quad i\in[s+1,2s].\end{cases}

Hence we construct a permutation matrix as

\displaystyle W_{L}:=W^{\sigma}_{(m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{s}\times u_{1}\times\cdots\times u_{s})}.

(77)

(ii)

The column index should be changed from
\vec{j}_{1}\cdots\vec{j}_{t}\vec{q}_{1}\cdots\vec{q}_{t}
to
\vec{j}_{1}\vec{q}_{1}\cdots\vec{j}_{t}\vec{q}_{t}
.
A permutation
\mu\in{\bf S}_{2t}
is required as

\mu(i)=\begin{cases}2i-1,\quad i\in[1,t],\\
2(i-t),\quad i\in[t+1,2t].\end{cases}

Hence we construct a permutation matrix as

\displaystyle W_{R}:=W^{\mu}_{(n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{t}\times v_{1}\times\cdots\times v_{t})}.

(78)

Motivated by the normal forms of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

VII-C

Solving Paired KPD of Hypermatrices

Definition VII.6

Given a hypermatrix
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{d}}
, where
q_{k}=m_{k}n_{k}
,
k\in[1,d]
.
The paired KPD problem is finding
{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}}
and

{\mathcal{C}}=(c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{C}}.

(84)

Note that both
V({\mathcal{B}}\times_{d}{\mathcal{C}})
and
V({\mathcal{B}})\ltimes V({\mathcal{C}})
have the same matrix set as

\left\{b_{i_{1},\cdots.i_{d}}c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{d}}\right\}.

The only difference is in
V({\mathcal{B}}\times_{d}{\mathcal{C}})
they are arranged in the order of

\vec{i}_{1}\vec{j}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{d}\vec{j}_{d}
while in
V({\mathcal{B}})\ltimes V({\mathcal{C}})
they are arranged in the order of

\vec{i}_{1}\cdots\vec{i}_{d}\vec{j}_{1}\cdots\vec{j}_{d}
.
So a permutation
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2d}
is required, which is

\displaystyle\sigma(i)=\begin{cases}2i-1,\quad i\in[1,d],\\
2(i-d),\quad i\in[d+1,2d].\end{cases}

(85)

Then the corresponding permutation matrix can be constructed as

\displaystyle\Psi:=W^{\sigma}_{(m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}\times n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d})}.

(86)

Here
\Psi
can convert the element arrangement in
V({\mathcal{B}})\ltimes V({\mathcal{C}}))
to the element arrangement in

V({\mathcal{B}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{C}})
.

Note that since
\sigma(1)=1
and
\sigma(2d)=2d
. Then
\Psi
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\Psi=I_{m_{1}}\otimes W^{\mu}_{m_{2}\times\cdots m_{d}\times n_{\times}\cdots\times n_{d-1}}\otimes I_{n_{d}},

(87)

where
\mu\in{\bf S}_{2(d-1)}
with

\displaystyle\mu(i)=\begin{cases}d+(i-1),\quad i~\mbox{is odd},\\
i/2,\quad i~\mbox{is even}.\end{cases}

(88)

The above argument leads to the following result.

Lemma VII.7

Assume
{\mathcal{B}}=(b_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}}
and

{\mathcal{C}}=(c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
. Then

\displaystyle V({\mathcal{B}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{C}})=\Psi(V({\mathcal{B}})\ltimes V({\mathcal{C}})),

(89)

where
\Psi
is defined in (
86
).

Note that
\Psi
is an orthogonal matrix, then (
89
) can be rewritten to

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}V({\mathcal{B}}\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{C}})=(V({\mathcal{B}})\ltimes V({\mathcal{C}})).

(90)

Now the problem of paired KPD has been converted into the corresponding KPD of vectors. Hence Lemma
VII.7
with the
result of MDA, described in Proposition
IV.4
and the Remark
IV.5
, leads to our main result, which is a general necessary and sufficient condition for arbitrary hypermatrix. It is so imaginary simple!

Theorem VII.8

The paired KPD problem for hypermatrix
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{d}}
with
q_{k}=m_{k}n_{k}
,
k\in[1,d]
is solvable, if and only if, the vector

\displaystyle V:=W^{\mathrm{T}}V({\mathcal{A}})

(91)

is decomposable w.r.t.
m\times n
, where
m=\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}
and
n=\ltimes_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
.

Remark VII.9

(i)

Using the MDA to the vector
V
in (
91
), if the
V
is successfully decomposed into
V=h_{0}V_{b}\otimes V_{c}
. Then it is a straightforward to find
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
V({\mathcal{B}})=V_{b}
and
V({\mathcal{C}})=V_{c}
.

(ii)

The techniques developed for least square approximated KPD and the finite sum (precise) KPD for arbitrary vectors can also used the corresponding problems of KPD of hypermatrices.

(iii)

The paired Kronecker product can be used for two hypermatrices of different orders by adding dummy index
\emptyset
. Hence the series KPD of hypermatrices can also be done for factor hypermatrices with different orders.

The following corollary is easily verifiable.

Corollary VII.10

(i)

If
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}_{1}\otimes_{d}\cdots\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}_{1}\otimes_{d}({\mathcal{B}}_{2}\otimes_{d}(\cdots\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}}_{k})\cdot).

(92)

Hence, the multi-fold paired KPD can be done step by step.

(ii)

The paired KPD is unique up to a set of constant product coefficients. That is, if
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}_{1}\otimes_{d}\cdots\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{B}}_{k}={\mathcal{C}}_{1}\otimes_{d}\cdots\otimes_{d}{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
, then there is a set of coefficients
\mu_{i}
,
i\in[1,k]
such that

{\mathcal{B}}_{i}=\mu_{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{i},\quad i\in[1,k],

with
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}=1
.

VIII
Conclusion

The KPD of vectors has firstly discussed in detail. The MDA is developed to solve it. When a vector is not decomposable, the MDA provides an approximate decomposition. Starting from this approximate solution, a gradient descent based algorithm is developed to provide least square error approximate decomposition. Using this algorithm again on the difference of
x
and its approximate decomposed form several times a (precise) finite sum decomposition can be obtained. Hence the KPDs of vectors are completely solved. Then the KPD of matrix is investigated. By converting it to its equivalent KPD of the corresponding vector, the KPD is also completely solved. Finally, the matrix expression of a hypermatrix is discussed and by using its matrix expression the KPD of hypermatrix can also be converted to its equivalent KPD of corresponding vector, which also provide complete solution to KPD of hypermatrix. The results are surprisingly simple and numerically straightforward computable, comparing with existing results. We conclude that the technique developed in this paper provides a universal solution to KPD problems.

The author would like to emphasize the following fact. A hypermatrix is a matrix set but a matrix form. All the results obtained in this paper about hypermatrices are based on their matrix expressions. This is exactly the same as in Linear Algebra (or Matrix Theory), where they use particular matrix or vector form, but not the matrix set. Hence the author believes that a successful hypermatrix theory must be built on the matrix expressions of hypermatrices.
