Measuring Complexity in Symptom Prevalence and Severity Among Palliative Care Patients Using Patient-Centered Outcome Measures in Real World Setting: A Multi-Center, Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Khek Tjian Tay, Fei Yee Lee, Wi Han Hiew, Mei Ling Look, Chian Hui Yeoh, Yoke Yeng Leong, Win Lin Chai, Nurfarah Dina Azmi, Cy Oun Teoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePalliative care patients experience symptoms and concerns in the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains, which can negatively affect their quality of life. Understanding the prevalence of symptoms and problems is crucial in the face of increasing palliative care needs to tailor individualized patient-centered care plans. This study aimed to examine the prevalence and severity of symptom burden and explore the factors associated with high symptom burden in palliative care patients.MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study included adult palliative care patients at the beginning of an episode of care across four palliative care units in Malaysia between July and September 2024. Demographic data, functional status, and the Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS) were extracted from medical records and clinical databases.ResultsThis study recruited 405 patients with a mean age of 65.4±14.1 years and a slight predominance of men (53%) and cancer diagnoses (58%). Most patients were in the unstable phase of illness (62%), with 8.5±4.8 symptoms per patient. The most prevalent symptoms/concerns were family anxiety (86%), poor mobility (82%), and lethargy (80%). At least 50% of the patients had ≥5 items with an IPOS score ≥2. Cancer diagnosis (adjusted OR 2.350; 95% CI 1.030, 5.358) and unstable phase (4.272; 1.657, 11.011) were factors associated with severe overall and physical symptom burdens (≥1 item with IPOS score ≥2), after adjusting for other possible confounders.ConclusionThe prevalence and severity of symptoms and concerns among palliative care patients were high, suggesting complex needs.Keywords: Palliative care, Prevalence, Symptom burden, Patient reported outcome measures

INTRODUCTION

Global estimates indicate that 56.8 million people, including 25.7 million in the last year of life, require palliative care annually, whereas only approximately 14% of people receive it [1]. In Malaysia, the estimated number of patients requiring palliative care is more than 56,000 annually, of which only 8.3% have their needs met [2]. Furthermore, this number is projected to increase to more than 240,000 by 2030 [3].

Palliative care patients experience symptoms and concerns in the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains that can negatively impact their quality of life [4]. Therefore, distressing symptom burdens must be identified and managed appropriately. Understanding the prevalence of symptom burdens is important in facing increasing palliative care needs to identify the symptom burdens that are most important to palliative care patients and tailor individualized patient-centered care plans.

The symptom burden is high in patients with advanced cancer [5,6] and those with non-cancer diagnoses [7,8]. A systematic review [9] comparing symptom prevalence between patients with cancer and non-cancer diagnoses showed similarities in both groups, with fatigue, anorexia, dyspnea, and anxiety reported as highly prevalent problems. In addition to physical symptoms, issues related to the psychological, social, practical, and communication domains are highly prevalent in both patient groups [10]. More recently, Van Lancker et al. [11] reported a higher prevalence of physical symptoms and functional dependence among patients without cancer than among patients with cancer. Regardless of the diagnosis group, higher symptom prevalence and severity are associated with increased psycho-spiritual distress [12], poorer functional status [13], and decreased quality of life [14].

Multidimensional patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly being used as part of routine clinical assessments to measure the symptom burdens and concerns of palliative care patients [15]. PROMs are standardized, validated questionnaires completed by patients to measure their perceptions of their health status. However, patients in palliative care settings may be too unwell or lack the cognitive ability to complete PROMs. To address the risk of excluding these less-well patients, the term “patient-centered outcome measures” (PCOM) is used to include patient- and proxy-reported outcome measures [15]. Outcome measurements were recently implemented in some inpatient palliative care services in Malaysia using tools such as the Palliative Phase of Illness, Australian Karnofsky Performance Scale (AKPS), Resource Utilization Groups – Activities of Daily Living (RUG-ADL), and Integrated Palliative Care Outcomes Scale (IPOS).

A retrospective study conducted in Malaysia reported that weakness (89%), pain (77%), and poor appetite (76%) are the most prevalent symptoms [16]. However, this study only included patients with advanced cancer who died under the care of a specialist palliative care service in an urban private tertiary hospital setting. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to the overall palliative care population in Malaysia [16]. Gaining further knowledge on the prevalence of symptom burdens will help palliative care providers better understand the country’s palliative care needs. This study aimed to 1) examine the prevalence and severity of symptom burden and 2) explore factors associated with high symptom burden in palliative care patients in Malaysia.

METHODS

1. Study design

We conducted a multicenter, cross-sectional study to examine the prevalence of symptoms in palliative care patients across Malaysia over a 3-month period between July and September 2024.

2. Study participants

From all patients receiving palliative care at four hospitals with established palliative care services from the Northern, Southern, Central, and Borneo regions of Malaysia, 1078 new episodes of care were included in this study. Additional inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients aged 18 years and above and 2) patients with IPOS documentation at the beginning of an episode of care. An episode of care is defined as “a period of in-person contact between a patient and a palliative care service that is provided by one palliative care service and occurs in one setting [17].” The start of an episode of care may occur upon inpatient admission, consultation referral, or outpatient review. An episode of care ends when the care setting changes (upon death or discharge from current care setting) [17]. Patients were excluded if they had no documented IPOS or their only documented IPOS was at a change of phase or the end of episode of care.

Sample size was calculated using the ScalexSP Calculator, which incorporates the following formula for a prevalence study: n=(Z2 P (1-P))/d2 (Z=statistic for a level of confidence, P=expected prevalence or proportion, and d=precision) [18]. The estimate is considered sufficiently accurate if the deviation between the estimate and parameter is within ±5%. Based on previous studies that included both cancer and non-cancer populations [5-9], the symptom with the highest estimated prevalence was fatigue (78%). For the calculation, the type 1 error was fixed at 0.05, representing an estimation for a 95% confidence interval (CI). Considering a nonresponse rate of 20% and using these estimates, the required sample size was 340.

3. Data collection

The medical records of patients attending the palliative care clinic, admitted to the palliative care ward, and referred to the palliative care team for consultation during the study period were reviewed for eligibility. Upon confirmation of eligibility for inclusion in the study, the participants’ demographic data, diagnosis, medical history, AKPS, RUG-ADL, Palliative Phase of Illness, and IPOS scores during the episode were extracted from the medical records and clinical databases maintained by the respective departments.

4. Ethical approval

Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health, Malaysia, which waived the need for written informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study design. (NMRR ID: 24-03477-FXR (IIR))

5. Instruments

The IPOS is a previously validated questionnaire that assesses patients’ palliative care symptom burden with 17 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 [best] to 4 [worst]) [19]. The IPOS comprises three domains: 1) physical domain (10 items): pain, breathlessness, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, appetite, sore/dry mouth, drowsiness, poor mobility, and constipation; 2) emotional domain (4 items): depression, anxiety, family anxiety, and feeling at peace; and 3) communication/practical domain (3 items): information needs, ability to share feelings, and practical needs [19]. Presence of a symptom burden for each item is defined by an IPOS score ≥1, while a high symptom burden is defined as any individual IPOS item score ≥2 (moderate to overwhelming) [7]. The IPOS may be patient- or proxy-reported (caregiver or healthcare provider) [19].

The Palliative Phase of Illness identifies a clinically meaningful period in a patient’s condition determined based on a holistic clinical assessment considering the needs of patients and their families and caregivers. The phases are as follows: stable, unstable, deteriorating, terminal, and bereavement [17,20].

The AKPS measures a patient’s performance across the dimensions of activity, work, and self-care [17]. It includes an 11-point scale (score range: 0~100), with higher scores representing better performance status. The AKPS was assessed daily by the palliative care team.

The RUG-ADL is an ordinal scale (score range: 4~18) used by the palliative care team to measure the level of daily functional dependence across four items: bed mobility, transfer, toileting, and eating/feeding. Higher scores indicate greater need for external assistance [17].

6. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Science for Windows version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) and R 4.5.0. Symptom prevalence, severity, and demographic data were described using descriptive statistics and expressed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous data were assessed for normality using histograms. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR).

The presence of symptom burden by domain was compared between participants with and without cancer using Pearson’s chi-square test for independence. Multiple logistic regression was used to investigate the associations between the independent variables and high symptom burden (defined as moderate to overwhelming for any IPOS item). All variables were simultaneously entered into a multiple logistic regression model to minimize residual confounding and overfitting. Geographical location and reporter type (proxy- vs. patient-reported) were excluded because of the sparse distribution across categories. Primary diagnosis was excluded because of substantial diagnostic heterogeneity, instead represented by the binary cancer versus non-cancer classification. Model fit was examined using several methods. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was performed to test model fit, with P<0.05 as the threshold of poor fit. Multicollinearity among candidate variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) with a cutoff of 10 as the threshold. Interaction terms were tested and adjusted accordingly. Classification tables were checked, and model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Non-response to individual IPOS items was coded as the absence of a significant symptom burden for that item. Multiple imputations were explored as a sensitivity analysis but yielded unstable models due to near-universal outcome prevalence following imputation. Therefore, the primary analysis was retained.

RESULTS

1. Patient demographics

A total of 405 patients were included in this study. The study population was predominantly male (n=213, 52.6%). The mean participant age was 65.4±14.1 years. Cancer diagnosis was slightly more common (58.5%). Gastrointestinal cancer (48.1%) and breast cancer (16.5%) were the most common cancer diagnoses, whereas end-stage kidney disease (28.0%), cerebrovascular disease (19.6%), and chronic lung disease (16.7%) were the most common non-cancer diagnoses.

Mean AKPS and RUG-ADL were 38.1±21.2 and 12.6±4.8, respectively. Participants’ documented phases of illness at the beginning of the episode of care were as follows: 91 were stable (22.5%), 250 were unstable (61.7%), 28 were deteriorating (6.9%), and 36 were terminal 36 (8.9%) (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis showed that cancer diagnosis was associated with a high symptom burden in the physical (aOR 2.298; 95% CI 1.310, 4.030; P=0.004) and communication/practical (aOR, 2.059; 95% CI 1.325, 3.198; P=0.001) domains. Female sex was associated with a high symptom burden in the communication/practical domain (aOR, 1.570; 95% CI 1.015, 2.428; P=0.043), but not in the physical or emotional domains. Regarding phase of illness, the unstable phase was also associated with the physical domain (aOR, 3.346; 95% CI 1.810, 6.185; P<0.001), but not the communication/practical domain. RUG-ADL score was negatively correlated with high symptom burden in the emotional (aOR 0.943; 95% CI 0.893, 0.996; P=0.036) and communication/practical (aOR 0.903; 95% CI 0.857, 0.851; P<0.001) domains (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, family anxiety was the most prevalent concern (86%), with 30.9% of patients reporting the highest severity (IPOS score of 4). This finding is consistent with those of a previous multicenter study [21]. Unmet palliative care needs, high care provision demand, ongoing anticipatory grief, and critical decision-making processes contributed to family distress and anxiety [22]. In Malaysia, long-term care remains predominantly informal, and deep-rooted cultural expectations of filial piety place a significant assumed burden on family caregivers [23]. This systemic reliance on informal networks is further strained by high out-of-pocket healthcare expenditures, which account for approximately 36% of total health expenditures, directly compounding family distress [24]. Hence, it is imperative to strengthen the support provided to family caregivers, in line with the recommendations of the European Association for Palliative Care Task Force on Family Carers [25]. A critical first step is to gain a better understanding of caregiver and family needs, which should include routine assessment of caregiver burden using validated tools such as the Zarit Burden Interview or the Carer Support Needs Assessment Tool as part of standard clinical practice in palliative care [26,27]. Aggregating these data can offer policymakers and healthcare stakeholders the empirical evidence required to design targeted interventions to better support the needs of family caregivers.

In the physical domain, poor mobility (82.2%), lethargy (79.5%), and poor appetite (67.7%) were the most common symptoms, which is consistent with prior studies.[7,9,10,19,28] This highlights the need for better screening, allied health referrals, and healthcare provider training. Less prevalent physical symptoms, such as breathlessness (57%), pain (56.9%), dry mouth (56.5%), drowsiness (55.2%), constipation (42.1%), nausea (31.8%), and vomiting (19.7%), may reflect the availability of established interventions and familiarity among healthcare providers.

This study revealed high symptom/concern prevalence and severity in Malaysia’s palliative population, with at least 50% of patients reporting ≥8 items with IPOS scores ≥1, 5 items with IPOS scores ≥2, and 2 items with IPOS scores ≥3. These findings differ slightly from the results of a systematic review that showed a prevalence exceeding 50% for five symptoms. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the heterogeneity of the measurement tools employed, variations in the delivery of palliative care services across healthcare systems, and diverse sociocultural backgrounds of the study populations [9]. Furthermore, most studies in the systematic review [9] were conducted in high-income countries, with only 7 out of 147 including countries in East or South Asia. Symptom prevalence data is scarce among the palliative care population in Malaysia, with only one retrospective review examining patients with advanced terminal cancer at a tertiary center [16]. The findings of our study reflect our ultimate objective to contribute to the understanding of palliative care needs in Malaysia and underscore the importance of developing palliative care service delivery tailored to meet the specific needs of the palliative care population.

We found that the unstable phase was associated with a higher severity of symptom burden. This is consistent with the findings of a previous validation study, which showed a correlation between the unstable phase and high symptom severity, as demonstrated by higher total IPOS scores [19]. The high proportion of patients in the unstable phase (61.7%) on the first encounter with palliative care teams indicates that most patients require urgent intervention and changes in care plans to address their urgent and unexpected symptoms and concerns [17,20].

Notably, our study showed a higher symptom prevalence among patients with cancer than among those without cancer. This observation differs from the findings of a recent study that reported significantly more physical symptoms and functional dependence among patients without cancer than among those with cancer [11]. The findings of their study may not fully reflect baseline palliative care needs, as their cohort comprised patients already receiving palliative care [11]. In contrast, our study may capture a more representative baseline symptom prevalence between patients with and without cancer, as symptom assessments were conducted during their initial encounters with palliative care services before the initiation of any palliative care interventions. Although differences in referral timing or pathways by cancer status could be a potential underlying reason for these findings, they warrant confirmation in future studies. These findings emphasize the importance of early identification of symptoms and concerns, which can facilitate more timely and appropriate referrals to palliative care services by primary care teams.

A main strength of this study is that it utilized real-world clinical data from the ongoing implementation of outcome measurements in clinical practice. The tools used to measure the outcomes have been validated and are widely used in many palliative care settings [19-21]. The use of the IPOS as the multidimensional palliative care-specific measure incorporates domains other than physical symptoms, which is considered important in palliative care patients [28]. This helps provide a clearer picture, as we attempted to measure the complexity of needs among palliative care patients.

This multicenter study included palliative care services and populations from the northern, central, and southern regions of West Malaysia, as well as the Borneo state in East Malaysia. Therefore, the findings of this study may be generalizable to the Malaysian population. Although the study population included patients from urban, suburban, and rural settings, the relative representation of each group could not be determined. Furthermore, the outpatient and community representation in our study was low. Further studies that include outpatient and community populations are required to better capture palliative care needs in different settings.

This study was limited by its design, which did not allow for the determination of causality. The low documentation completion rate is another limitation of this study, which reflects the practical challenges in implementing PCOMs in clinical settings, particularly in resource-limited environments. This pattern of low documentation is consistent with previously reported challenges in embedding structured outcome tools into routine palliative care practice [29]. This reflects an implementation gap in routine outcome measurement rather than post-enrolment attrition. Patients without IPOS documentation were not recruited for the study, and no parallel data collection was performed for this group. Therefore, a formal comparison between the included and excluded patients was not feasible. Future qualitative studies are warranted to explore the barriers and facilitators of PCOM implementation in clinical practice in resource-limited settings. A notable proportion of documented IPOS were proxy reports (caregiver- and staff-reported) can create discrepancies between patient and proxy perspectives, leading to under- or over-reporting of symptom burden and severity [30]. Furthermore, defining high symptom burden as any individual IPOS item score ≥2 implies limited generalizability in predicting factors for cumulative severity.

Symptom burden prevalence and severity are high among palliative care patients in Malaysia, with family anxiety, poor mobility, and practical needs being the most common concerns. Cancer diagnosis and an unstable phase of illness are significant predictors of a high symptom burden. These findings highlight the complex multidimensional needs of palliative care patients and underscore the urgent need for comprehensive support systems that address not only physical symptoms but also the psychosocial and practical concerns of patients and their families. Further studies that include outpatient and community populations, as well as measures of caregiver burden, are required to better capture palliative care needs and guide service development, resource allocation, and policy formulation to improve access to and quality of palliative care services in Malaysia.
