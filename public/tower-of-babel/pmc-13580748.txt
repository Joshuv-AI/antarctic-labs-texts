EWSR1::FLI1 promotes neocentromere formation in ewing sarcoma cells
Authors: Risa Kitagawa, Ediri E. Metitiri, Bin Yu, Changxian Shen, Alexander J.R. Bishop, Siyuan Zheng, Peter J. Houghton, Katsumi Kitagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYEwing sarcoma (EwS) is characterized by chimeric fusions such as EWSR1::FLI1. Chromosome gain or loss is prevalent in EwS, and this chromosome instability (CIN) phenotype correlates with poor treatment outcomes. We previously showed that CENP-A, a centromere-specific histone H3 variant, interacts with EWSR1 via its SYGQ2 motif, an interaction extending to the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein. CENP-A is highly expressed in EwS cells, driven by EWSR1::FLI1. EwS cells exhibit significant CIN, mitigated by depleting EWSR1::FLI1 or CENP-A, and frequently assemble neocentromeres. Using CENP-A ChIP-seq, we identify neocentromere-associated DNA sequences, and ChIP assays confirm EWSR1::FLI1 binds these sequences, suggesting EWSR1::FLI1 recruits CENP-A to drive neocentromere assembly. Notably, overexpressing EWSR1::FLI1, but not CENP-A, induces neocentromeres in non-EwS cells. These neocentromeres likely produce dicentric chromosomes, explaining the high incidence of chromosome bridges and chromoplexy underlying the CIN phenotype in EwS.

INTRODUCTION

Ewing sarcoma (EwS) is the second most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents and is characterized by the expression of chimeric fusions of the EWSR1 (also known as EWS) and ETS family transcription factors, e.g., EWSR1::FLI1.1,2 Response of children with metastatic or relapsed EwS is poor despite intensive and multimodality therapy.3,4 Further, survivors who underwent this intensive chemotherapy display increased long-term morbidity.5 Therefore, new, more targeted, and less toxic therapies for EwS are eagerly sought.

Exome sequencing of childhood tumors, in general, has revealed a silent genomic landscape relative to adult malignancies with regard to mutation accumulation.6 Though coding mutations are rare,7 many tumors, particularly sarcomas, are associated with frequent chromosome gain or loss.8 EwS tumors isolated from patients are relatively genetically normal cancers, exhibiting only EWSR1ETS rearrangements and an average of 5 additional mutations. However, EwS tumors display frequent chromosome gain or loss,9–13 and this chromosome instability (CIN) phenotype is associated with lower cumulative overall survival.10 Ozaki et al. reported that chromosome gain or loss (a type of aneuploidy) was detected in 39 of 52 (75%) primary EwS tumors and 9 of 10 (90%) relapsed EwS tumors.10 These observations are consistent with other studies (Table S1).12,14–21 Three genomic landscape sequencing analysis reports11–13 and the most recent international working group consensus report22 also support frequent chromosome gain or loss in EwS cells.

CIN is a hallmark of cancer7,15 and is thought to contribute to cancer evolution, metastasis, and therapy resistance.23–30 Suppression of CIN reduces metastasis.23,31 Therefore, understanding the mechanism of CIN in EwS will provide viable therapeutic targets for drug resistance and metastasis in EwS. However, virtually nothing is known about the driver of CIN in EwS.

CENP-A is a centromere-specific histone H3 variant that is required for kinetochore assembly and proper chromosome segregation.32 Except in the budding yeast, centromere identity relies not on DNA sequence, but on the presence of a special nucleosome that contains CENP-A.32 Thus, CENP-A is regarded as the epigenetic mark of the centromere.32–34

Molecular profiling of CENP-A in human cancers revealed that CENP-A expression is elevated in around 20 types of solid tumors.35 Elevated CENP-A levels are coupled with malignancy and poor treatment outcomes.35 Overexpression of CENP-A results in CIN.36–43 Notably, Shrestha et al. reported that overexpression of CENP-A in HeLa and chromosomally stable diploid RPE1 cells causes chromosome missegregation defects.43 In human cells, overexpression of CENP-A induces misloading of CENP-A at non-centromeric regions, which assembles a subset of kinetochore components, including CENP-C, hSMC1, and HZwint-1.44 However, in these studies, the microtubule-associated proteins CENP-E and HZW10 were absent and centromeric activity was not detected,44 suggesting that in humans, overexpression of CENP-A alone is insufficient for driving the formation of neocentromeres. In colon cancer cells, although overexpressed CENP-A is mistargeted to non-centromeric chromatin in cancers,39 this mislocalization of CENP-A depends on the chaperone DAXX (Death domain-associated protein).45

In this study, we have found that CENP-A is highly expressed in EwS cells, which is induced by EWSR1::FLI1, and that EwS cells exhibit substantial CIN that can be suppressed by depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 or CENP-A. We found neocentromeres in EwS cell lines and that overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1 alone, but not CENP-A, can induce neocentromere formation. Our ChIP data suggest that neocentromeres are formed at EWSR1::FLI1 chromatin binding sites in EwS cells. These findings allow us to propose a novel molecular mechanism of neocentromere formation in EwS cells.

RESULTS

CENP-A is highly expressed in Ewing sarcoma cells

Because elevated CENP-A levels are coupled with malignant progression and poor patient outcome,35 we determined CENP-A levels in pediatric tumor xenografts from PDX models (patient-derived xenografts) and CDX models (cell culture-derived xenografts).46 We found that CENP-A is highly expressed in pediatric sarcomas including EwS (Figure S1A). Consistently, CENP-A is highly expressed in EwS cell lines as well (Figure S1B). We found that depletion of the oncogenic fusion protein EWSR1::FLI1 decreases CENP-A in EwS (ES-2) cells (Figure S1C) and that EWSR1::FLI1 expression increases CENP-A levels in HeLa and hTERT-RPE1 cells (Figure S1D). Consistent with these findings, the previous genome-wide transcriptional analyses by Gordon et al.47–49 (Figure S1E) indicate that EWSR1::FLI1 expression induces transcriptionally high levels of CENP-A in EwS. We confirmed that EWSR1::FLI1 expression induces CENP-A transcription by RT-PCR (Figure S1F) and protein expression by immunoblotting (Figure S1G). EWSR1::FLI1-R2L2 mutant (a DNA-binding-deficient mutant)50,51 failed to induce CENP-A transcription and protein expression (Figures S1F and S1G), supporting that EWSR1::FLI1 induces CENP-A expression transcriptionally. EWSR1::FLI1-ΔSYGQ2 (CENP-A binding-deficient mutant)52 was able to induce CENP-A expression (Figures 1A and 1B), suggesting that EWSR1::FLI1-CENP-A interaction does not affect CENP-A levels. Interestingly, FLI1 overexpression did not induce high levels of CENP-A in HeLa cells (Figure S1H), implying that CENP-A is a specific oncogenic target of EWSR1::FLI1.

EWSR1::FLI1 and CENP-A promote chromosome instability in EwS cells

The frequent chromosome gain or loss observed in EwS cells cannot be completely explained by the loss of STAG2 (Cohesin subunit SA-2) because it is absent in ~15% of EwS.10 In most EwS cell lines, TP53 is mutated (Table S2), but published studies have shown that cells completely lacking p53 do not exhibit substantial CIN, although a slight tendency toward tetraploidization has been observed.53

Chromosome missegregation and chromosome gain or loss (aneuploidy)26,54,55 are direct indicators of CIN in EwS. Therefore, to evaluate CIN, chromosome missegregation was quantified. Consistent with frequent chromosome gain or loss in EwS, we found that EwS cell lines (ES-2 and ES-4) exhibit chromosome missegregation phenotypes (misaligned chromosomes, lagging chromosomes, and chromosome bridges) (Figures 1C, 1D, and S1I). Notably, the frequency of “chromosome bridge” formation was relatively high (Figures 1D and S1I), which was consistent with the finding by Park et al.56 Micronuclei, a consequence of lagging chromosomes, are also found in ES-2 and ES-4 cells (Figure S1J). We note that even though ES-4 has wild-type TP53 and wild-type STAG2 with normal expression (Table S2), it exhibits CIN significantly (Figure 1D).

Importantly, we found that depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 suppresses chromosome missegregation, thus CIN in ES-2 and ES-4 cells (Figures 1E, S1K, S1L and S1N), suggesting that EWSR1::FLI1 is a critical regulator of CIN in EwS cells. Given that overexpression of CENP-A causes CIN,39,57 we hypothesized that high levels of CENP-A are a driver of CIN in EwS. Our data showed that depletion of CENP-A substantially suppresses CIN in ES-2 cells (Figures 1F, S1M, and S1O), suggesting that high levels of CENP-A are a primary mechanism that induces CIN in EwS cells. Interestingly, EWSR1::FLI1 depletion or CENP-A depletion preferably reduced the “chromosome bridge” phenotype (Figures 1E, 1F, S1K, and S1M), suggesting that the mitotic defects caused by high levels of CENP-A via EWSR1::FLI1 expression result in “chromosome bridging”.

EwS cells harbor neocentromeres

In colon cancer cells, overexpressed CENP-A is mistargeted to non-centromeric chromatin but overexpression of CENP-A alone does not induce neocentromere formation.39 Neocentromeres are defined by ectopic centromeres, which are generated at non-centromeric locations: normally, centromeres are associated with highly repetitive alpha-satellite DNA.58,59 Thus, we examined whether high levels of CENP-A induce CENP-A loci at non-centromeric regions of chromosomes in EwS cells. We analyzed the distribution of CENP-A using 3-color immunostaining [DAPI (blue), CENP-A (green), and CENP-B (red)] in combination with chromosome spreads. Endogenous CENP-B is stained only at “native” centromeres because CENP-B binds to repetitive alphoid DNA directly.60–62

We found that paired CENP-A-positive and CENP-B-negative signals at the primary constriction of the chromosomes, for example, in RD-ES and ES2 cells (Figures 2A and S2A), which suggests that the CENP-A-positive and CENP-B-negative loci are functional centromeres, thus potentially neocentromeres. We also found that paired CENP-B-positive but CENP-A-negative signals (inactivated centromeres) on the same chromosomes (Figures 2A and S2A). These results suggest that, when neocentromeres were formed, the authentic centromeres were inactivated to avoid being dicentric chromosomes. Note that, if neocentromeres are formed on chromosomes with active native centromeres, either the neocentromere or the endogenous centromere must be inactivated to maintain the chromosomes stably because a chromosome with two centromeres (dicentric) is unstable.63

To further analyze the CENP-A-positive/CENP-B-negative loci, we tried to isolate cell lines stably harboring neocentromeres by single colony isolation. We were able to isolate and establish cell lines harboring CENP-A-positive/CENP-B-negative loci stably from only 2 EwS cell lines (RD-ES and ES2) out of 13 cell lines. This suggests that most of the CENP-A-positive/CENP-B-negative loci are transiently formed.

In isolated RD-ES and ES-2 cells, we found that HEC1 (Ndc80; the kinetochore-microtubule interface protein64)-positive or CENP-I (an inner kinetochore protein; a CCAN protein64)-positive but CENP-B-negative signals on chromosomes (Figures 2B and S2B), indicating that the loci are intact kinetochores. These results suggest that RD-ES and ES-2 cells have neocentromeres. We applied the same analysis to other EwS cell lines. We found CENP-A-positive/CENP-B-negative loci (we defined the loci as CENP-A loci) in 13 of 13 EwS cell lines and CENP-A-positive/HEC1-positive/CENP-B-negative loci (we defined the loci as neocentromeres) in 11 of 13 EwS cell lines (Table S3 and Mendeley). “Inactivated authentic” centromeres (CENP-A-negative/HEC1-negative/CENP-B-positive) were also found in all the EwS cell lines (Table S3 and Mendeley).

Neocentromeres are formed at EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites

To determine DNA sequences of neocentromeres in isolated RD-ES and ES-2 cells, we performed ChIP-seq using anti-CENP-A antibodies. DNA-bound CENP-A in EwS cell lines with neocentromeres was immunoprecipitated with anti-CENP-A antibodies. Our CENP-A ChIP-seq of RD-ES cells revealed an over 500 kb CENP-A binding region on the p arm of chromosome 11 (Figure 2C). We performed FISH-IF using a BAC clone with this genomic locus (RP11–698B3) and confirmed that this region colocalized with CENP-A (but not CENP-B) (Figure 2D). Interestingly, the highest peak in this neocentromere region contains a previously identified in vivo EWSR1::FLI1-binding site (Figure 2C).65,66 This result implies that the neocentromere was formed at an EWSR1::FLI1 binding site. We have previously shown that CENP-A binds to EWSR1::FLI1 through the SYGQ2 motif of EWSR1 (Figure S3).52 Thus, it is plausible that EWSR1::FLI1 brings CENP-A to an EWSR1::FLI1 binding site (Figure 3F).

On the other hand, CENP-A ChIP-seq of ES-2 cells revealed a ~10 kb CENP-A binding region on the q arm of chromosome 4 (Figure 2E). We performed FISH-IF using a BAC clone with this genomic locus (RP11–626H4) and confirmed that this region colocalized with CENP-A (but not CENP-B) (Figure 2F). This region does not contain any previously identified in vivo EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites or any GGAA microsatellite sequences (Table S4).65–69

To test if this genomic sequence can be targeted by EWSR1::FLI1, we expressed EWSR1::FLI1 transiently by the Tet-ON system in HeLa cells that do not have any neocentromeres, and determined whether EWSR1::FLI1 binds to the neocentromere regions. Our ChIP assay revealed that the region on chromosome 4 identified as the CENP-A binding region in ES-2 cells (Ch4_NeoCEN region) and the region on chromosome 11 identified as the CENP-A binding region in RD-ES cells (Ch11_NeoCEN region) as well as two previously known EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites,66 CACNB2 promoter region and TGFBR2 promoter region, are bound to EWSR1::FLI1 in HeLa cells expressing EWSR1::FLI1-Flag (Figures 3A and 3C). Furthermore, in the same cells, CENP-A is bound to these EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites, Ch4_NeoCEN region, Ch11_NeoCEN region, CACNB2 promoter region, and TGFBR2 promoter region in an EWSR1::FLI1-dependent manner (Figures 3A and 3C), indicating that CENP-A is recruited by EWSR1::FLI1 to EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites. These results support the hypothesis that the neocentromere in EwS cells is formed at an EWSR1::FLI1 binding site (Figure 3F).

As a control for the CENP-A ChIP assay using HeLa cells expressing EWSR1::FLI1-Flag, we performed A Flag-CENP-A ChIP assay using HeLa cells expressing Flag-CENP-A (Figures 3B and 3C). There is no significant CENP-A binding to each region compared to HeLa cells expressing Flag-CENP-A (Figure 3B), indicating that CENP-A localization to those regions is dependent on EWSR1::FLI1. Furthermore, our ChIP assays confirmed that EWSR1::FLI1 binds to Chr4_NeoCEN in ES-2 and RD-ES2 cells (Figures 3D and 3E; see also the Limitations of the Study section).

Overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1 induces neocentromere formation

Although EWSR1::FLI1 can induce high levels of CENP-A, over-expression of CENP-A alone cannot induce neocentromere formation in human cells.44 Because our ChIP seq data suggest that the neocentromere is formed at an EWSR1::FLI1 binding site, we examined whether overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1 can induce neocentromeres in non-EwS cells. Interestingly, we found that transient overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1, but not of CENP-A, induced HEC1-positive, CENP-A-positive and CENP-B-negative signals on chromosomes in HT1080 (human fibrosarcoma) cells, suggesting that EWSR1::FLI1 overexpression, but not CENP-A overexpression, induced neocentromere formation (Figures 4A, 4B, 4C, S4A, and S4B). 8 out of 22 of CENP-B-, CENP-A+, HEC1+ loci were “single” loci, which are presumably newly formed neocentromeres (Figure 4A). 14 out of 22 of CENP-B-, CENP-A+, HEC1+ loci were paired (Figure 4A). Presumably, after DNA replication, they are “paired”. Overexpression of CENP-A induced CENP-B-, CENP-A+, HEC1-loci (“CENP-A loci”), which were observed in colon cancer cells expressing CENP-A highly (Figure 4A).39

We were also able to stably express Flag-EWSR1::FLI1 in U2OS (osteosarcoma) cells, which is known to tolerate EWSR1::FLI1 expression, and we found that overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1 induced HEC1-positive, CENP-A-positive and CENP-B-negative loci on chromosomes in U2OS cells, supporting that EWSR1::FLI1 overexpression induces neocentromere formation (Figures S4C–S4E). We also found that EWSR1-ΔSYGQ2-FLI1 (a deletion mutant that lacks the CENP-A binding domain)52 failed to form the neocentromere in U2OS cells, suggesting that CENP-A-EWSR1::FLI1 interaction is important for neocentromere formation (Figures S4C–S4E).

Next, we tested whether tethering EWSR1::FLI1 to artificial ectopic loci would be enough to trigger the formation of a neocentromere. We used U2OS-lacO cells (U2OS cells containing 200 copies of an array of 256 tandem repeats of the 17-bp LacO sequence on chromosome 1)70 expressing dCas9-V5-EWSR1::FLI1 and guide RNA targeting the LacO sequence. 8 of 23 EWSR1::FLI1 foci were co-localized with CENP-A and CENP-T at the LacO repeats (Figures 4D and 4E). This result suggests that tethering EWSR1::FLI1 to artificial ectopic loci is sufficient to induce neocentromere formation because CENP-T recruits the outer kinetochore (Ndc80 complex) directly to centromeres independently of the CENP-A pathway.71,72 This result supports the hypothesis that the neocentromere in EwS cells is formed at an EWSR1::FLI1 binding site (Figure 3F).

Consequently, overexpression of EWSR1::FLI1-Flag in HT1080 cells induced substantially more mitotic errors (Figures S4F and S4G), as observed in EwS cells (Figures 1C and 1D).

Chromosome painting reveals that chromosomes with neocentromeres exhibit chromoplexy

The “chromosome bridge” phenotype of EwS cell lines (Figures 1C and 1D and S1I) can be explained by dicentric chromosomes caused by frequent formation of neocentromeres. To investigate the possible effects on the karyotype by neocentromere formation leading to dicentric chromosomes, we performed multiplex in situ hybridization (m-FISH) for EwS cells with stable neocentromeres (RD-ES and ES-2 cells). Our m-FISH revealed that in RD-ES cells, the chromosome harboring neocentromeres is rearranged and possibly originated from three chromosomes, including chromosomes 3, 9, and 11, or 9, 11, and 22 (Figures 5A and S5A). In ES-2 cells, m-FISH showed that the chromosome harboring the neocentromere consists of rearranged scattered chromosomes 4 (green) and 5 (red) (Figures 5B and S5B). These results indicate that chromoplexy (defined by large chains of rearrangements coordinately affecting multiple chromosomes)73 or a similar event occurred on these chromosomes with neocentromeres (Figure 5C). This finding is consistent with the high occurrence of chromoplexy (42%) in EwS.74

DISCUSSION

CIN plays an important role in cancer evolution, metastasis, and therapy resistance. However, the molecular mechanism of CIN in cancer cells is largely unknown. In this study, we found that EWSR1::FLI1 expression can induce neocentromere formation, which we suggest underpins the CIN phenotype of EwS cells.

We have shown that EWSR1::FLI1 induces high levels of CENP-A mRNA (Figures 1B and S1F). In addition to its canonical role in transcription, EWSR1::FLI1 also functions as an mRNA decay factor.75 Thus, one may suspect that EWSR1::FLI1 stabilizes CENP-A mRNA. However, we found that the EWSR1::FLI1-R2L2 mutant (a DNA-binding-deficient mutant)50,51 failed to induce CENP-A transcription (Figure S1F), which excludes the possibility that CENP-A mRNA is stabilized by EWSR1::FLI1. In addition, we found that overexpression of FLI1 (an ETS transcription factor)76 alone did not induce CENP-A expression (Figure S1H), suggesting that CENP-A is a specific transcriptional target of EWSR1::FLI1.

CENP-A expression was elevated in many cancers, which are coupled with malignant progression and poor patient outcomes.35 Overexpressed CENP-A is mistargeted to non-centromeric chromatin but neocentromeres have not been found in those cancers. It was previously known that in human cells, overexpression of CENP-A alone cannot induce neocentromere formation.33,44 Therefore, we wondered how EWSR1::FLI1 can induce neocentromere formation. The finding that the highest CENP-A binding peak in the RD-ES neocentromere region contains a previously identified in vivo EWSR1::FLI1-binding site65 led us to think that the neocentromere region might be formed at the EWSR1::FLI1-binding site. As EWSR1::FLI1 binds to CENP-A, it could recruit CENP-A to the EWSR1::FLI1-binding site. The ES-2 neocentromere region does not include previously known EWSR1::FLI1-binding sites (Table S4). Nevertheless, we found that the region is bound to EWSR1::FLI1 when it is transiently expressed. Because neocentromeres can be generated artificially at ectopic sites through the assembly of LacI-CENP-A nucleosomes at Lac operator (LacO)-containing arrays,70,77,78 we propose the model in which EWSR1::FLI1 recruits CENP-A to an EWSR1::FLI1-binding site and thus a neocentromere is formed at the EWSR1::FLI1 chromatin binding site (Figure 3F). Indeed, we found that tethering EWSR1::FLI1 to LacO repeats is sufficient to induce neocentromeres by recruiting CENP-A at the LacO loci (Figures 4D and 4E).

Having an “established” neocentromere appears to be a rare event, as we were able to isolate only 2 cell lines carrying chromosomes with stable neocentromeres. However, EWSR1::FLI1 expression keeps inducing the formation of neocentromeres frequently (Figures 4A–4C and S4), which contributes to CIN in EwS cells.

We proposed that EWSR1::FLI1-induced neocentromeres created transient dicentric chromosomes, which resolved into stable neocentromeric chromosomes with inactivated native centromeres (Figure 5C). To support this model, we followed the formation of dicentric chromosomes over time using the system used in Figures 4A–4C (the EWSR1::FLI1 conditional expression system). The expression of EWSR1::FLI1 was induced by addition of doxycycline (Dox) in HT1080 cells. The number of chromosomes with a single neocentromere and a pair of the authentic centromeres (presented as “single” in Figures S4H and S4I), the number of chromosomes with a pair of neocentromeres and a pair of authentic centromeres (“dicentric chromosomes”; presented as “paired di-CENs”), and the number of chromosomes with a pair of neocentromeres without authentic centromeres (no CENP-B signals; presented as “mono-CENs”) were monitored over time (24, 48, 72, and 96 h) (see Figures S4H and S4I). As expected, the number of dicentric chromosomes (paired di-CENs) increased at 48 h and decreased at 72 and 96 h (Figures S4H and S4I). The number of mono-CENs increased over time as the number of di-CENs decreased (Figure S4I). Interestingly, we could not find any chromosomes with a pair of neocentromeres with authentic “deactivated” centromeres (CENP-B- and CENP-A+) in this system, although we found chromosomes with a pair of neocentromeres with authentic “deactivated” centromeres in EwS cells (Figures 2 and S2; Table S3). This absence suggests that, because dicentric chromosomes are unstable, chromosome break probably occurs as described in Figure 5C, and then the authentic centromere is lost. Presumably, deactivation of the authentic centromere is a rare event, and the mechanism is unknown (see Limitations of the Study). The number of chromosomes with a single neocentromere (“single”) decreased overtime (Figure S4I), presumably because EWSR1::FLI1 expression is toxic to cells.

EwS cell lines show a high rate of chromosome bridge phenotype (Figures 1C and 1D and S1I). Strikingly, depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 or CENP-A in ES-2 cells abolished the chromosome bridge phenotype (Figures 1E and 1F, S1K and S1M). Since dicentric chromosomes induced by neocentromere formation are likely to lead to chromosome bridges (Figure 5C), our findings can explain the high rate of chromosome bridges in EwS cells.

Anderson et al. reported that in EwS, chromoplexy was found to be quite common, occurring in 42% of cases (52/124).74 Chromoplexy was significantly more likely to be present in EwS cases that are metastatic at presentation.79 From the published bioinformatic analysis, it has been suggested that this is an early event in tumor evolution.74 Although the authors of this study have proposed a replication-associated mechanism for induced chromoplexy,74 why it is specific to EwS is not known. We note that chromosome breakage stemming from neocentromere formation and resulting dicentric chromosomes, as is proposed in the “closed chain” model by Yi and Ju,80 which likely induces chromoplexy. Thus, we propose that the EWSR1::FLI1-CENP-A-neocentromere axis leads to chromoplexy in EwS (Figure 5C).

In addition, dicentric chromosomes created by neocentromere formation can also induce whole chromosome loss or gain, as dicentric chromosomes do not always break, as previously shown by Gascoigne and Cheeseman.63

Collectively, our findings provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying EwS pathogenesis and open new possibilities for developing therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting CIN and the processes associated with neocentromere formation.

Limitations of the study

Since the exact origin of EwS is still unknown,81 we used HT1080 and U2OS cells as controls because they have been used as controls in the past since they are sarcoma cell lines. Especially when we wanted to express EWSR1::FLI1 constitutively, we had to use U2OS (osteosarcoma) cells that are known to tolerate EWSR1::FLI1 expression.82 For other cell lines, we had to express EWSR1::FLI1 transiently by transient transfection or by the inducible expression system (pTet-One system). We used HeLa cells when we wanted to use non-sarcoma cells and for testing general biological questions traditionally.

The difference between Figures 1D and 1E/1F is the absence or the presence of siRNA treatment only, which affected chromosome missegregation phenotypes substantially. This is a technical limitation.

In Figure 2, CENP-B signals on the chromosomes with neocentromeres (“deactivated centromeres” as they lost CENP-A signals) were much weaker than those on other chromosomes. To establish a stable neocentromere, the authentic centromere on the same chromosome must be deactivated (Figure 5C). However, we do not know the mechanism to deactivate the authentic centromere. The weak CENP-B signals suggest a reduction of α-satellite DNA on the dicentric chromosomes. In the future, it would be interesting to investigate the molecular mechanism by which the authentic centromere is deactivated, which may involve the reduction of α-satellite DNA.

Although not perfect, we observe some correlation between neocentromere formation and CENP-A expression levels. For instance, RD-ES1 and ES2 cell lines express the highest levels of CENP-A and have the highest number of neocentromeres. SKES1 cells have no neocentromeres and have low levels of CENP-A. However, TC71 cells have the lowest level of CENP-A, yet they have more neocentromeres than cells with higher levels of CENP-A. Therefore, CENP-A expression level appears not to be the only factor that determines neocentromere formation.

Our ChIP assays revealed that EWSR1::FLI1 binds to Chr4_NeoCEN in ES-2 and RD-ES2 (Figures 3D and 3E). For an unknown reason, however, binding to Chr4_NeoCEN is relatively lower in ES-2 than to other binding sites (Figure 3D). In ES-2 cells, Chr4_NeoCEN is where the neocentromere was formed. Once the neocentromere is established, EWSR1::FLI1 is not necessary to be at the neocentromere if EWSR1 is only required for CENP-A recruitment to the EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites. It would be interesting to test whether establishment of the neocentromere protein complex would prevent EWSR1::FLI1 localization to the neocentromere.

As a control for the CENP-A ChIP assay using HeLa cells expressing EWSR1::FLI1-Flag, we performed a Flag-CENP-A ChIP assay using HeLa cells expressing Flag-CENP-A (Figures 3B and 3C). Interestingly, CENP-A co-precipitated slightly more with the Chr 4 region than with other regions. However, the contribution to neocentromere formation induced by EWSR1::FLI1 is unlikely to be significant, because we know that overexpressing CENP-A in HeLa cells does not induce neocentromere formation.39,44

We recognize the limitations of the model that proposes the EWSR1::FLI1-CENP-A neocentromere axis leads to chromoplexy in EwS because we have not reproduced chromoplexy formation through EWSR1::FLI1 expression in non-EwS cells.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell lines

HeLa, 293 T, U2OS, HT1080 cells, or Ewing sarcoma A673 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY). hTert-RPE-1 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (GE Healthcare) with 10% FBS, and HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s SA medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Ewing sarcoma CHLA-9, CHLA-10 and CHLA258 cells were cultured in IMDM medium (Lonza bioscience) with 10% FBS and 1% ITS (Insulin, Transferrin, Selenium) solution (Gibco). All other Ewing sarcoma cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium (GE Healthcare) with 10% FBS. The status (e.g., sex, age, etc.) of Ewing sarcoma cell lines is summarized in Table S2. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using PCR Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Alfa Aestar Cat#: J66117) upon initial thawing and then further tested as needed. Cell line identity was verified using short tandem repeat profiling service from Cell Line Authentication Services/Labcorp DNA identification Lab.

METHOD DETAILS

Plasmids and cloning

The doxycycline-inducible expression construct, B3804 [plix403(pTET-ONE)-dCas9-V5-EWSR1::FLI1], was generated by PCR-amplifying the V5-tagged EWSR1::FLI1 coding sequence from B3399 [pLIV-V5-EWSR1::FLI1] and inserting it downstream of dCas9 into the plix403(pTET-One) vector using the HiFi DNA Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs). The guide RNA (gRNA) expression vector targeting the repetitive LacO sequence B3806 [Lentiguide-hygro-dTomato-gRNA-lacO] was constructed by ligating an oligo-nucleotide duplex (5′-GCCACATGTGGAATTGTGAG −3′) into the BsmBI site of Lentiguide-hygro-dTomato (Addgene #99376).

Cell culture and transfection

For culture of stably transfected cells with B3309 (plix_403-pTet-One-EWSR1::FLI1-Flag), B3383 (plix_403- pTet-One-Flag-CENP-A) or B3399 (pLIV-V5-EWSR1::FLI1), 5 μg/mL of puromycin was added. Cells were transfected with annealed double-stranded siRNA(s) or mammalian expression plasmids by using siLentFect Lipid Reagent (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) or Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), respectively. For retrovirus production, 1 × 106 of 293 T cells were co-transfected with 1 μg of retrovirus vector (pQCXIP vector or pMSCV vector) and 0.5 μg each of B3189 (pGP) and B3163 (VSV-G) using Lipofectamine 3000. Supernatant containing virus particles was collected 48 h after transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane, and kept on ice until use. 8 μg/mL of polybrene was added to the supernatant immediately before transduction. For transduction, media for the target cells was replaced with virus suspension, and cells in the virus suspension were incubated for 6 to 12 h. For lentivirus production, 1 × 106 of 293 T cells were co-transfected with 6 μg of retrovirus vector (pLIX_403 vector), 0.5 μg of B3196 (pMD2G), and 3.5 μg of B3197 (VSV-G) using Lipofectamine 3000.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblot analysis was performed as described previously.91–93 Cells were suspended in Wang buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 50mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5% of deoxycholate, and 0.5% SDS) supplemented with complete mini protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore/Sigma, Burlington, MA). The cell suspension was sonicated, cleared by centrifugation, and supernatant was separated on Bolt 4–12% Bis-Tris mini protein gel (Invitrogen), and transferred to Immobilon-FL (Millipore/Sigma). The membranes were blotted with primary antibodies, then secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase. For scanning the immunoblots, ECL reagent (Pierce) and the Odyssey Fc Infrared imaging System (Li-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE) were used. To quantify the signal intensities, Image Studio Version 5.2.5 Software (Li-COR Biosciences) was used.

Immunoblotting of PPTP tumor xenograft models

The pediatric preclinical testing program (PPTP) was described previously.46 In brief, CB17SC scid / female mice (Taconic Farms, Germantown, NY) were used to propagate subcutaneously implanted PPTP models. All mice were maintained under barrier conditions and experiments were conducted using protocols and conditions approved by the institutional animal care and use committee. Snap-frozen xenograft tumors were pulverized under liquid N2 and lysed with RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA), supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche), and 1mM PMSF (Sigma). Equal amount of protein (20 μg) was separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore).

Cell culture and immunofluorescence

U2OS cells containing an engineered array on chromosome 1 (256 tandem repeats of a 17-bp LacO sequence, approx. 200 copies) were provided by Dr. Ben Black.70 Cells were co-transfected with B3804 and B3806. Following transfection, expression was induced with 10 μg/mL doxycycline (Dox) for 48 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for immunofluorescence using antibodies against V5, CENP-A, and CENP-T. V5 and CENP-A were detected with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor Plus 555 and 647, respectively, and CENP-T was identified by primary antibody directly conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 using Zenon Rabbit IgG labeling Kit (Invitrogen). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. EWSR1::FLI1 foci were identified via Olympus confocal microscopy, and the co-localization of CENP-A and CENP-T was subsequently analyzed.

Immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization

Indirect immunofluorescent staining was performed as described previously,34,94,95 but with the following minor modifications. Approximately 1 × 105 cells per well were seeded into 6-well plates, and incubated for 16 h, then transfected with siRNA(s) or protein expression vector by using siLentFect Lipid (BioRad) or Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), respectively. Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were trypsinized and one-quarter of total cells were seeded on a cover glass. The remaining cells were re-seeded into other 6-well plates (to be used for Western blot analysis) and incubated an additional 24 h. Cells on the cover glass were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 4°C and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature. Alternatively, cells were fixed with 75% cold acetone for 30 min at −20°C.

For cytogenetic analysis, chromosome spreads were obtained from various cell lines as previously described.33 Briefly, cells were treated with 400nM of TN-16 for 8 h or 100 ng/mL of KaryoMax Colcemid solution for 12 h. Cells arrested in mitosis were collected by mitotic shake-off method, and treated in hypotonic buffer [0.2% Na3 citrate, 0.2% KCl] for 10 min. Cell density was adjusted 20,000~50,000 cells/mL, and 100 μL of cell suspension was transferred to cytofunnels. Cells were attached on slides by spinning at ~900 rpm for 10 min in Cytospin centrifuge (Cyto-Tek). Slides were incubated in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, then 100% cold methanol for 10 min at 20°C. Slides were immediately subjected to indirect immunofluorescent staining.

For staining CENP-A protein and CENP-B protein, anti-CENP-A monoclonal antibody and anti-CENP-B polyclonal antibody diluted at 1:100 dilution in buffer K (10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl) containing 0.5% BSA were added to chromosome spreads on slides, and incubated at 4°C for 12 h. For staining CENP-I and CENP-B, anti-CENP-I rabbit polyclonal antibody and anti-CENP-B mouse monoclonal antibody diluted at 1:100 dilution in buffer K containing 0.5% BSA were used. Slides were washed with buffer K containing 0.5% BSA three times and incubated in secondary antibody solution (1:100 dilution of Alexa Fluor Plus 647 Goat-anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor Plus 555 Goat-anti-rabbit IgG, 0.5 μg/mL of DAPI in buffer K containing 0.5% BSA) at 37°C for 1 h. Slides were washed three times with buffer K containing 0.5% BSA and then mounted. For staining CENP-A protein and CENP-B protein together with HEC1 protein, slides were first incubated in primary antibody solution (anti-CENP-A rabbit polyclonal antibody diluted at 1:100 dilution and anti-HEC1 mouse monoclonal antibody diluted at 1:1000 dilution in buffer K containing 0.5% BSA) at 4°C for 12 h. After three times of washing with buffer K containing 0.5% BSA, slides were incubated in secondary antibody solution (1:100 dilution of Alexa Fluor Plus 647 Goat-anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor Plus 555 Goat-anti-rabbit IgG) at 37°C for 1 h. Following three times of washing with buffer K containing 0.5% BSA, slides were incubated in tertiary antibody solution (anti-CENP-B monoclonal antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 diluted at 1:100 in buffer K containing 0.5% BSA and 0.5 μg/mL of DAPI) at 37°C for 1 h. Cells were washed three times with buffer K containing 0.5% BSA and then mounted.

In situ hybridization with BAC clone FISH probes (Empire Genomics) were performed on immunostained slides. Coverglasses attached on slides with nail polish were removed. Chromosome spreads were post-fixed with 2% formaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 10 min, denatured with 0.1 N HCl at room temperature for 10 min then washed with PBS and rinsed with 2 × SSC for 5 min. Slides were incubated at room temperature for 30 min in hybridization buffer [50% v/v formamide, 10% Dextran, 0.1% BSA, 2xSSC], then incubate at room temperature for 30 min. The prehybridization buffer was replaced with 4 μL of hybridization buffer containing 1 μL of the FISH probe, then the slides were incubated at 75°C for 3 min, transferred to the humid chamber at 37°C and incubated for 36 h. After hybridization, slides were washed with 2 × SSC at 37°C for 10 min × 3 times, 0.1 × SSC at 55°C for 10 min twice, 2 × SSC for 5min, post-fixed with 2% form-aldehyde/PBS at room temperature for 10 min, rinsed with 2 × SSC for 5min. Slides were incubated in 0.2 μg/mL of DAPI in 2xSSC at room temperature for 30 min, rinsed with 2 × SSC for 5min, then mounted with mounting media containing antifade.

Cells or chromosome spreads were observed through an Olympus FluorView confocal microscope FV3000 equipped with 100× oil immersion lens. Image acquisition and processing were performed by using FluorView Imaging Software (Olympus).

Chromosome segregation defect assay

Asynchronously growing cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde on glass coverslips and stained with DAPI, anti-tubulin, and anti-CENP-B antibodies. Mitotic cells were analyzed and categorized into six distinct phenotypes: 1) normal, 2) metaphase with misaligned chromosomes, 3) anaphase with lagging chromosomes, 4) anaphase/telophase with chromosome bridges, 5) multipolar spindles, and 6) other abnormalities (e.g., bipolar but distorted spindles). Mitotic cells were scored across three independent replicates to achieve a total sample size (n) of at least 30 cells per condition; exact values for n are indicated in the respective figure legends. For each replicate, the proportion of each defect type and the overall rate of defective mitotic events relative to the total number of mitotic cells were calculated. To visualize the datasets, the mean proportions of individual categories and total defective divisions were plotted as a stacked bar graph. Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) of the total defective mitotic rate, and statistical significance was evaluated using p-values. Finally, the individual category data were replotted as a Trellis chart to clearly resolve category-specific error bars.

Preparation of chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-DNA

Exponentially growing Ewing sarcoma cells were collected. 1 × 108 cells were washed in D-PBS (Gibco) and incubated in RSB buffer [10 mM HEPES-NaOH pH7.4; 15 mM NaCl; 1.5 mM MgCl2] containing complete mini EDTA-free protease inhibitors (Roche) for 10 min on ice. Cells were resuspended in RSB buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 and homogenized by tight fit homogenizer (x10 strokes). Nuclei were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in Buffer A (15 mM HEPES-NaOH pH7.4; 15 mM NaCl; 60 mM KCl; 340 mM sucrose; 0.5 mM Spermidine; 0.15 mM Spermine; 1 mM DTT) containing protease inhibitors. Nuclei were incubated with 2000 units of micrococcal nuclease (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 1 h in the presence of 1 mM CaCl2. Micrococcal nuclease-treated chromatin was collected by centrifugation and the pellet was suspended in Buffer C (500 mM NaCl 10 mM EDTA), then diluted in Buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0]; 5 mM EDTA; 500 mM NaCl; 0.2% Tween 20; protease inhibitor(-EDTA). Goat anti-mouse IgG Dynabeads M-280 (Invitrogen) were coupled with mouse anti-CENP-A antibody [3–19] (a gift from Tatsuo Fukagawa) or mouse IgG for 2 h in PBS/0.5% BSA at 4°C, washed twice in PBS/0.5% BSA, and added to 500 μL of the micrococcal nuclease-treated chromatin, then incubated for 12 h at 4°C. Beads were then washed 4 times with buffer B containing protease inhibitors. DNA in immunoprecipitates (bound fraction) and input were treated with proteinase K and purified with a PCR purification kit (Qiagen), then eluted with water. If concentration allowed, ChIP-DNA were checked for size distribution using a Fragment Analyzer High Sensitivity DNA assay (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

For Chromatin-Immunoprecipitation assay for EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites and CENP-A binding sites, micrococcal nuclease-treated chromatin fraction was prepared from HeLa cells stably transfected with B3309 (plix_403-pTet-One-EWSR1::FLI1-Flag) or with B3383 (plix_403-pTet-One-Flag-CENP-A) and treated with 10 μg/mL doxycycline for 48 h. Flag tagged EWSR1::FLI1 or Flag tagged CENP-A was immunoprecipitated from the chromatin fraction using Goat anti-mouse IgG Dynabeads M-280 coupled with anti-Flag mouse monoclonal antibody M2 or mouse IgG. For immunoprecipitation of endogenous CENP-A, Goat anti-mouse IgG Dynabeads M-280 coupled with anti-CENP-A mouse monoclonal antibody was used. For the assay for EWSR1::FLI1 binding sites in Ewing sarcoma cell line, ES-2 and RDES, endogeneous EWSR1::FLI1 was immunoprecipitated from the chromatin fraction using Goat anti-rabbit IgG Dynabeads M-280 coupled with anti-FLI1 rabbit polyclonal antibody or rabbit IgG.

DNA in immunoprecipitates (bound fraction) and input were treated with proteinase K and purified with a Qiagen PCR purification kit, then eluted with water. Using 1 μL of each elutant, PCR was performed using primer sets to amplify specific genomic regions.

The experiments were performed in triplicate. The resulting PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel via electrophoresis and visualized by staining with ethidium bromide. Gel images were captured using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, and signal intensities were quantified using Bio-Rad Image Lab software (version 6.1). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent samples. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test.

ChIP-seq library preparation and next-generation sequencing

ChIP-seq sequence data were generated by the Genome Sequencing Facility, UT Health San Antonio. Approximately 0.1ng–20ng ChIP DNA was fragmented to size range around 200bps using Covaris (shearing parameter 2 min in LV tubes using 15 μL microTUBE-15 AFA Beads Screw-cap, Cat: 520145), and used for ChIP-seq library preparation by following the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library preparation Kit for Illumina (Cat. No: E7645L, New England Biolabs). This kit utilizes a proprietary adapter attachment chemistry which minimizes bias and supports very low amounts of input samples. ChIP-seq libraries were quantified by Qubit and Bioanalyzer, pooled, and subsequently sequenced with 75 bp paired read sequencing run on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform. After the sequencing run, demultiplexing with Bcl2fastq2 was employed to generate the fastq file for each sample. Sequenced data were mapped into human genome assembly hg38 (GRCh38 Genome Reference Consortium Human Reference 38) and analyzed by The Computational Biology and Bioinformatics Initiative, Greehey Children’s Cancer Research Institute, UT Health San Antonio.

PCR detection of CENP-A transcripts

B3117(pMSCV-hygro-3xflag-EWSR1::FLI1) was a gift from S. Lessnick. B3708(pMSCV_hygro_3xFlag-EWSR1::FLI1R383L/R386L(R2L2 mutant)) was generated by introducing two nucleotide substitutions into coding region of B3117 using Quikchange site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent). 5×106 of HeLa cells were transfected with 5 μg of B3117 or B3708 using Lipofectamine 3000. Forty-eight hours after transfection, media was removed from the culture dish, and 1 mL of TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher) was added directly to culture dish to lyse the cells. Homogenized cell lysate was transferred to centrifuge tube and incubated at room temperature for 5 min 200 μL of chloroform was added to the lysate and mixed thoroughly. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000xg for 15 min at 4°C, and RNA was collected from upper aqueous phase by adding 500 μL of isopropanol and centrifuge at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4°C. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, and air-dried, then resuspended in 20 μL DEPC-treated water. cDNA pool was generated from 5 to 8 μg of total RNA using SuperScript IV Reverse transcriptase with Oligo(dT)20 primer (Invitrogen) by following manufacturer’s instruction. Using 0.1~1 μL of 20 μL first-strand cDNA pool, PCR with CENP-A cDNA specific primers and actin cDNA specific primers were performed, and PCR products are analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

GraphPad Prism 9 software and Microsoft Excel software were used for statistical analysis. The results were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD), and SD was indicated as error bars. Differences between groups were analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t test. p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant or statistical significance is indicated in the figures as *p < 0.5, **p < 0.1, ***p < 0.05, ****p < 0.01; n.s. indicates not significant.
