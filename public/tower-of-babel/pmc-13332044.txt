Sodium nitrate supplementation prevents beneficial cardiac adaptations to running in female mice with few effects on male hearts
Authors: Elise S. Bisset, Gracious D. S. Kasheke, W. Glen Pyle, Scott A. Grandy, Susan E. Howlett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While dietary nitrate supplements combined with aerobic activity are thought to enhance cardiac function, this has not been well investigated, and studies have used male animals only. We provided 7–9-month-old male and female mice with sodium nitrate (1 mM), running wheel access, both, or neither for 12 weeks and measured heart structure/function (echocardiography), contractions/calcium transients (ventricular myocytes), calcium handling genes and myofilament function (ventricles). In females, running increased heart mass and wall thickness. Nitrates alone had no impact, but nitrates plus running reduced global longitudinal strain, indicative of worse ventricular function. Running increased myocyte contraction and relaxation speed due to parallel changes in calcium transients in females. Interestingly, these benefits were abolished by nitrates but restored upon nitrate washout. Running also reduced Mg-ATPase activity in females at calcium > 1 µM, but not when given nitrates. In males, who ran less than females, nitrates (with or without running) had no effect on myocytes, but nitrates plus running prolonged isovolumic relaxation times. Mg-ATPase activity was unaffected at physiological calcium in males and there were few impacts on calcium handling in either sex. Thus, contrary to what is commonly thought, nitrate supplementation can have detrimental, if temporary, effects on cardiac contractility, especially in females.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50082-4.

Introduction

Aerobic activity is often combined with dietary supplements to increase running volume and cardiac health. Survey data suggest that more than 40% of Canadians have consumed a dietary supplement, with supplement use being even higher in athletes1,2. Supplement consumption differs between the sexes with men consuming more protein supplements and women consuming more vitamins and minerals2. While athletes surveyed claim to know why they took supplements, there are critical gaps in their knowledge compared to the literature3. Similarly, health care providers report a lack of training or information concerning dietary supplements4. Thus, while supplements are frequently consumed, their advantages and potential adverse effects are not well understood.

The dietary supplement sodium nitrate is commonly used for its putative benefits on athletic performance and cardiac function. Sodium nitrate is a prodrug, which is transformed into nitric oxide through a series of reduction reactions. Multiple enzymes facilitate each reaction5,6. The first reduction from nitrate to nitrite is primarily performed by bacterial nitrate reductases in the oral microbiome of both humans and mice7,8. Interestingly, this nitrite reduction differs between the sexes in humans but not in rodents8. The second reduction from nitrite to nitric oxide occurs through multiple pathways including reductions mediated by hemoglobin, stomach acid, bacterial enzymes (e.g. copper nitrate reductase), and mammalian enzymes (e.g. xanthine oxidoreductase)6. Nitrates can be reduced by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (NOS-3) under low oxygen conditions9. Nitrates also can alter the phosphorylation of NOS-3, which can activate or inhibit it depending upon the site10. Thus, sodium nitrate can increase nitric oxide and can affect the endogenous pathways involved in nitric oxide production.

Nitric oxide has multiple biological functions including direct actions on the heart. Nitrate supplementation (1 mM in drinking water) for 1–2 weeks increases cardiac contractility by altering internal calcium handling in ventricular cardiomyocytes from male mice11. Nitric oxide can also affect ryanodine receptor function in male hearts12, which could increase calcium release in cardiomyocytes. On the other hand, 8-weeks of treatment with sodium nitrate (1 g/L in drinking water) has no effect on systolic or diastolic function in vivo in male mice13. Thus, the impact of nitrates on cardiac contraction is controversial and relatively little is known about underlying mechanisms. As previous studies used only male mice, whether sodium nitrate affects the heart in females and whether effects differ between the sexes is unclear.

Aerobic activities like running have well-established benefits in mouse models including favourable remodeling of heart structure and function14. Running increases heart size, increases cardiac metabolism, and enhances cardiac contractile function in mammals including humans15 and mice16. Sodium nitrate supplementation also increases running volume in humans17 and in mice18, but whether it enhances physical performance in females is unclear. It is possible that combining sodium nitrate supplementation and running will have additive beneficial effects on the heart, although this has not been investigated in either males or females.

We used echocardiography to investigate the impact of nitrates with and without exercise on in vivo cardiac structure and function in male and female mice. We also explored whether any changes could be explained by alterations in cardiomyocyte calcium handling, calcium handling proteins and/or cardiac myofilament function and whether effects differed between the sexes. We used 8- to 9-month-old mice and provided them with access to a running wheel, dietary nitrates in their drinking water, both, or neither for 12 weeks, so the mice were approximately 12 months old at the study endpoint. We used middle-aged adult mice, an age where signs of age-associated cardiac remodeling begin to arise19.

Results

Impact of nitrates on voluntary running

Female and male mice were given 12 weeks of either nitrate supplementation, access to a voluntary running wheel, both, or neither. Females, regardless of supplementation, ran further than males (Fig. 1a). Nitrate supplementation increased the total distance run in females but not males (Fig. 1a). When we investigated treadmill time to exhaustion, we found time to exhaustion was longer in females than males, regardless of nitrate supplementation (Fig. 1b). Impact of nitrates on blood pressure

As sodium nitrate can increase levels of the vasodilator nitric oxide, we investigated whether nitrates affected in vivo blood pressure. Supplementary Figure S1 shows systolic and diastolic blood pressure over the course of the experiment for sedentary and exercise groups, in the presence and absence of nitrates. Nitrates had no significant impact on blood pressure in any of the experimental groups.

Effect of nitrates with or without running on ventricular structure and function in vivo

We followed body weight over the course of the study (Supplementary Fig. S2). Changes in weight were modest and occurred primarily in sedentary mice. Sedentary females treated with nitrates, but not controls, gained weight over the 12-week intervention and the opposite effect was observed in sedentary males. By contrast, there was virtually no change in body weight in mice who ran. Both raw and normalised (to body weight or tibia length) wet heart weights did not differ between treatment groups (Supplementary Table 1).

We also used echocardiography to assess left ventricular (LV) structure at baseline, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks to determine if nitrates alone, or when combined with running, altered heart structure. There were no effects of nitrates on cardiac structure in sedentary female mice (Supplementary Fig. S3a-d). However, nitrates affected heart structure when combined with exercise. By contrast, LV mass increased over the study in control female runners but not in those treated with nitrates (Fig. 2d). Anterior wall thickness did not change over time (Fig. 2e), but posterior wall thickness increased in female runners, although not when they were given nitrates (Fig. 2f). While running increased LV diameter over time, this was not seen when males were given nitrates plus running (Fig. 2i). In contrast to results in females, nitrate supplementation with or without exercise, had virtually no effect on heart structure in males.

We also used pulse wave Doppler to measure LV function in males (Fig. 3g, h). In sedentary males, nitrates reduced cardiac output (Supplementary Fig. S4e) and shortened isovolumic contraction time over the course of the study (Supplementary Fig. S4g). Unlike females, there were no effects of running or nitrates combined with running on cardiac output (Fig. 3i), global longitudinal strain (Fig. 3j), or isovolumic contraction time (Fig. 3k) in males. Thus, nitrates had few effects on male heart function, although they did reduce cardiac output in sedentary mice and prolong isovolumic relaxation times when combined with running.

Impact of nitrates with or without running on cardiomyocyte contractions and calcium transients

To investigate mechanisms involved in the effects of nitrates on cardiac function, individual ventricular cardiomyocytes were isolated at the study endpoint. Cells from all four groups (sedentary and running, plus and minus nitrates) were paced at 2 Hz to simultaneously measure contractions and underlying calcium transients. We found no differences in myocyte length (Supplementary Fig. S6a,c) or width (Supplementary Fig. S6b,d), regardless of sex or treatment. Figure 4a shows representative examples of contractions recorded from female cardiomyocytes. Neither running nor nitrates affected peak contraction in females (Fig. 4b) but running increased velocity to peak contraction (Fig. 4c) and relaxation (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, these beneficial effects of running were abolished when female mice also consumed nitrates (Fig. 4c, d). Figure 4e shows examples of calcium transients recorded from female cardiomyocytes. Interestingly, although running did not affect the size of cardiac contractions, it increased the amplitude of underlying calcium transients (Fig. 4f). Running also increased the velocities to peak calcium transient (Fig. 4g) and calcium transient decay (Fig. 4h). Influence of nitrates and/or running on cardiac myofilaments

As running increased peak calcium transients with no corresponding effect on contractions in females, this suggests that myofilament sensitivity to calcium may be reduced by running. Therefore, we measured the sensitivity of actomyosin Mg-ATPase to calcium in myofilaments isolated from the ventricles. In the absence of nitrates, female runners had significantly lower Mg-ATPase activity at calcium concentrations greater than 1 µM (Fig. 6a) than sedentary mice. However, running had no effect when females were given nitrates (Fig. 6b). There were no effects of these interventions on EC50 values (calcium concentration producing 50% activation) or Hill coefficients in females (Fig. 6c, d). In the absence of nitrates, male runners had higher maximum actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity compared to their sedentary counterparts at supraphysiological calcium concentrations (Fig. 6e). There was no effect of running on Mg-ATPase activity in males given nitrates (Fig. 6f). The interventions also had no effect on EC50 values or Hill coefficients in males (Fig. 6g, h). The degree of phosphorylation for major myofilament proteins was also investigated in ventricular tissue. The gels in Supplementary Figures S7 and S8 illustrate phosphorylation staining (ProQ Diamond) and total protein staining (Coomassie blue) for all samples assessed. There were no significant differences in total protein levels between the sexes or between any group within a sex. There were few differences in phosphorylation between groups, regardless of sex. In females, running reduced phosphorylation of myosin light chain 1, but only in those not taking nitrates (Supplementary Fig. S9). In males, both running and nitrates alone (but not together) reduced phosphorylation of troponin I (Supplementary Fig. S10). Consequently, these interventions induced few changes in myofilament protein phosphorylation in either sex.

Nitrates and/or running did not affect calsequestrin (Fig. 7c) or ryanodine receptor (Fig. 7d) expression in females. However, the expression of nitric oxide synthase (NOS)-3 increased with running, but only in control female mice (Fig. 7e). Nitrates and/or running did not affect SERCA2A expression in males (Fig. 7f), although unlike females there was no change in phospholamban expression (Fig. 7g). Impact of nitrate washout in females

To determine if nitrate effects were reversible, a washout trial was performed. As deleterious effects of nitrates were primarily seen in female mice who ran, this trial only used female runners. Mice were given 12 weeks of nitrates plus running, followed by 4 weeks of running only. Echocardiography showed that neither the reduction in global longitudinal strain nor the increase in LV diameter induced by nitrates plus running were reversed by washout (Supplementary Table S3). Nitrates had no effect on cardiomyocyte contractions in female mice who ran (Fig. 8a–c), but contractions were smaller after washout compared to both control and nitrate-supplemented mice (Fig. 8a). The velocity to peak contraction was also slower in washout compared to controls (Fig. 8b), but there was no difference in relaxation speed (Fig. 8c). As seen earlier, nitrates reduced calcium transient amplitude (Fig. 8d), velocity to peak calcium (Fig. 8e), and calcium transient decay rate (Fig. 8f). Critically, after 4-weeks of nitrate washout, calcium transient amplitudes and the velocity to peak calcium transient no longer differed from control (Fig. 8d,e) and the effect on calcium transient decay rate was reversed (Fig. 8f). These findings suggest that effects of nitrates on intracellular calcium handling in the heart are reversible.

Discussion

We determined if the combination of sodium nitrate and running improved cardiac function more than either intervention alone, investigated underlying mechanisms, and explored potential sex differences. Nitrates alone had few effects on cardiac structure and function in females, but it did reduce cardiac output in males. Running alone had beneficial effects on cardiac structure and function in females, where it increased heart mass plus wall thickness and reduced isovolumic relaxation times. These effects were attributable, in part, to changes in intracellular calcium handling. However, nitrates plus running led to dilated ventricles and reduced global longitudinal strain, indicative of worse ventricular function in females. Nitrates also removed the beneficial effects of running on calcium handling. Interestingly, these effects were reversible as a 4-week nitrate washout attenuated many of the detrimental effects of nitrates. By contrast, the combination had no impact in males. Thus, while nitrates alone had few effects on cardiac function in either sex, the combination of nitrates plus running impaired myocardial function selectively in females.

Sodium nitrate supplements are often combined with running to increase running volume20. We used a dose and route of administration of sodium nitrate (1 mM/drinking water) that increases voluntary running volume in male mice18. Interestingly, we showed that sodium nitrate consumption only increased running volume in females, with no impact in males. However, the “adult” male mice (age unspecified) in this earlier study18 were likely younger than the middle-aged mice used here. This is evidenced by the fact that mice in the Ivarsson et al. study18 ran much further than mice in our study and running volume declines with age21. We also found that females ran more and had higher fitness levels than males, as seen previously22. Mice may need to run a threshold volume to observe a beneficial effect of nitrates, although additional studies are required to test this.

We found that nitrates alone, running alone and the combination affected ventricular structure in a sex specific manner. Running increased LV wall thickness and mass without altering LV diameter in females but not males, compatible with evidence that aerobic exercise induces more physiological hypertrophy in females than males23. We found no corresponding increase in heart weight in response to exercise, as reported previously24. This may be because echocardiography detects the subtle LV remodeling associated with exercise better than wet heart weight24. This physiological hypertrophy occurred without changes in cardiomyocyte length or width. Exercise-induced changes in non-cardiomyocyte cells including fibroblasts, endothelial cells and immune cells25 may contribute to this hypertrophy. Cardiomyocyte proliferation could also contribute, although a proliferation assay would be required to test this directly. In males, running increased LV diameter without a corresponding change in wall thickness, a pattern also seen in females who consumed nitrates and ran. LV dilation with no increase in wall thickness can occur with exercise but may also reflect pathological remodeling and impaired systolic function16. Thus, females exposed to nitrates plus running exhibit a different and potentially maladaptive form of cardiac hypertrophy.

Nitrates, running and the combination modified ventricular function differently in males and females. Running alone increased cardiac output and improved diastolic function in females, consistent with previous mouse studies26,27. While running alone had no impact on cardiac function in males, this may be because females ran more than males. Nitrates alone had no impact on females and only reduced cardiac output in males. A shorter exposure (8 weeks) to a much higher dose of nitrate (12 mM) had no impact on ventricular function in male mice13. Thus, nitrates alone have few effects on heart function, and if anything, these effects are deleterious. Likewise, the combination of nitrates and running impaired systolic function in females as shown by worse global longitudinal strain and compromised diastolic function in males, as shown by prolonged isovolumic relaxation times. We saw no corresponding changes in E/A and E/E’ ratios, but these measures are challenging in mice and may be less reliable than isovolumic relaxation times28. Together, these findings indicate that nitrates taken alone or especially in combination with exercise, can have sex-specific and deleterious effects on cardiac function.

To explore underlying mechanisms, we investigated contractions and calcium handling in ventricular myocytes. As with our echocardiography data, we found that beneficial adaptations to exercise were apparent in cells from females but not males. These changes included an increase in the speed of contraction and relaxation, with parallel changes in calcium transients and higher calcium transient amplitudes, as reported previously29. This may be due to the higher running volumes in females compared to males. However, female mice who consumed nitrates had the highest running volumes, and nitrates eliminated the beneficial effects of exercise on cardiomyocytes. Thus, running volume alone cannot explain our findings. In addition, nitrate washout restored some detrimental changes in calcium handling, indicating that nitrates inhibit favourable effects of running on the heart in females.

We found that running increased peak calcium transients but not contractions in myocytes from females, which suggests running may reduce actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity. This is somewhat surprising, as exercise can increase myofilament calcium sensitivity29, although this can be modulated by female sex hormones so may differ between the sexes30. However, studies that report increased myofilament calcium sensitivity measured force31 while studies of actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity show no changes after training32,33. We found calcium-dependent actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity declined in exercise-trained female mice at higher levels of calcium, consistent with our cellular data. Discrepancies between force and ATPase activity may be due to differences in force assays versus biochemical assays31. Another explanation for lower actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity at higher calcium in exercised females is increased myofilament efficiency in converting ATP consumption into force. Whether actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity is unchanged or slightly reduced, increased force development associated with exercise would be advantageous and consistent with cardiovascular benefits of exercise. This could be tested with simultaneous measurement of tension development and ATP consumption in myofilaments in an intact 3-dimensional lattice, which is beyond the scope of this study. Thus, running increased the amount and speed of calcium release, and desensitization meant that contractions got faster, not larger. As this was not seen in females treated with nitrates plus running, nitrates may disrupt these running-induced adaptations. This provides a potential mechanism to explain why nitrates impair LV systolic function.

The role of myosin light chain 1 phosphorylation in regulating myofilament function is not well understood. Deleting the 11 amino acid C-terminal region containing the phosphorylatable serine 195 (S195) reduces sliding velocity but does not affect maximum actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity34. This disconnect between sliding filament velocity and actin-activated ATPase activity suggests the effects of myosin light chain 1 phosphorylation at S195 reflect a change in cross-bridge detachment rate34. Our finding that myosin light chain 1 dephosphorylation correlates with a decline in maximum actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity and the lack of effect shown by Sheid et al.34 suggests that this site is unlikely to be the driving force behind the functional change reported here. However, an additional threonine at residue identified at 64 (T64)35 could also be phosphorylated in myosin light chain 1. The functional impact of this residue in cardiac muscle has not been investigated, but an analogous threonine in skeletal muscle light chain 1 increases actin-activated ATPase activity36. Assuming dephosphorylation of this threonine would yield opposite effects, our observation of a decline in actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity and myosin light chain 1 dephosphorylation may be mediated through T64. Further studies are needed to determine how T64 dephosphorylation impacts cardiac function in general, and myofilament activity in particular.

In the female running group, we observed an increase in calcium transients that did not translate into an increase in cellular contractility. Pseudo-phosphorylated myosin light chain 1 with an aspartic acid substitution at S195 (S195D) increases cardiac contractility37 without impacting actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity34. If the dephosphorylation of myosin light chain 1 S195 were to mirror the effects of phosphorylation, we expect that myofilament force development would decline. Interestingly, work in skeletal muscle suggests the effects of phosphorylated S193 and T65 in myosin light chain 1 may be additive in terms of actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity36. Thus, while S195 dephosphorylation does not explain the decrease in actomyosin Mg-ATPase activity in running female mice, it can explain the apparent reduction in myofilament force development. This reduction in myofilament force development in a milieu of increased calcium transients could offset to yield no functional change at the cellular level.

In males, the only change in myofilament protein phosphorylation was reduced troponin I phosphorylation induced by nitrates alone or running alone, but not the combination. Troponin I phosphorylation declines with running in male rats38. It has a complex relationship with myofilament activity as numerous phosphorylatable residues across the protein affect myofilament function in many ways39. Cardiac troponin I engineered with alanine in place of serine at amino acids 43 and 45 to mimic a dephosphorylated protein increases maximum actomyosin ATPase activity40 as we see in running males. Reduced troponin I phosphorylation occurred in nitrate-treated males (with a trend in nitrate plus running males), with a similarly elevated maximum actomyosin ATPase activity compared to sedentary controls. This supports the hypothesis that reduced troponin I phosphorylation at S43 and S45 could produce this functional outcome.

We investigated whether mRNA for key calcium handling genes was affected by running, nitrates or both together. In females, running increased mRNA for the endogenous SERCA inhibitor phospholamban, as seen in young male rats41. SERCA inhibition attenuates sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium reuptake unless phospholamban is phosphorylated, which removes SERCA inhibition and increases calcium uptake42. Phospholamban phosphorylation may be increased to account for the rapid calcium transient decay in females, although demonstrating this would require additional studies. In males, nitrate supplementation and running, but not the combination, increased ryanodine receptor mRNA. The ryanodine receptor regulates intracellular calcium release and is positively impacted by running29. Interestingly, nitric oxide alters ryanodine receptor function in male mice12,43 but also increases the sodium-calcium exchanger expression, which prevents high systolic calcium levels43. It is possible that nitrates, once metabolised into nitric oxide, alter sodium-calcium exchanger expression, which could explain why higher ryanodine expression did not affect intracellular calcium in males. Running also increases nitric oxide levels by altering the endogenous NOS pathway10. Indeed, in female mice running increased mRNA levels for endothelial NOS (NOS-3), without increasing levels for inducible NOS (NOS-2) or neuronal NOS (NOS-1). Male mice may not have run enough to increase endothelial NOS, although this needs to be confirmed.

This study has limitations. We used middle-aged mice where signs of age-related cardiac remodeling may occur19. As exercise capacity declines with age in mice44 and cardiac adaptations to exercise are modulated by age45, findings may differ in younger or older mice. We investigated long-term nitrate supplementation in combination with exercise, but nitrates may have different effects if they are taken acutely and/or less frequently. In future work, it would be important to measure serum nitrate/nitrite levels. We measured mRNA, but studies of protein expression and the phosphorylation state of calcium-handling proteins should be assessed in future. Other calcium handling proteins (e.g. sodium-calcium exchanger) should be investigated. RyR2 and SERCA2a mRNA data in females were variable and changes might have been statistically identifiable with a larger sample size. The mRNA changes we observed could reflect cells other than cardiomyocytes, as exercise can promote changes in cells like fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells in the heart25. The use of molecular and histological approaches to assess changes like interstitial fibrosis in response to exercise and nitrate treatment could be enlightening. We used echocardiography to assess ventricular function in vivo, but this does not allow preload and afterload to be controlled. Although we found no impact of nitrates on blood pressure, it would be important to assess the effect of nitrates (with or without exercise) on function in either a perfused heart or with pressure–volume loops where preload and afterload can be controlled. In addition, studies with a consistent treadmill running program could help determine if sex differences persist when both sexes run comparable amounts.

In conclusion, this study shows that long term sodium nitrate supplementation abolishes many beneficial cardiac adaptations to running in female mice. This occurred, in part through changes in calcium handling that were not observed when nitrates were delivered alone. There were fewer effects in males, potentially because they exercised less. Still, sodium nitrate supplementation was detrimental to cardiac function in males, both alone and in combination with running. Together, these findings suggest that sodium nitrates may be harmful to cardiac function, especially when combined with exercise in both sexes. This is contrary to what has traditionally been thought and suggests that future studies should investigate whether sodium nitrates do indeed have positive health benefits, including effects on cardiovascular health, especially when combined with exercise.

Methods

Animal husbandry

Male and female C57Bl/6 mice were purchased from Charles River (St. Constant, QC, Canada) at five weeks of age and allowed to age naturally to between 8 and 9 months in the Carleton Animal Care facility at Dalhousie University. Littermates were housed in groups of 4–5 per cage and maintained at 21 °C with 35% humidity on a 12-h light–dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to food (ProLab RMH3000; LabDiet, MO) and water. All experiments were approved by the Dalhousie University Committee on Laboratory Animals and followed the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines46.

Interventions

The mice were placed in individual cages for the 12-week intervention trial. The mean (± SEM) age of the female mice was 8.8 ± 0.1 mos (range 8.0–9.5 mos) and the mean (± SEM) of the male mice was 8.4 ± 0.1 mos (range 8.0–9.4 mos). The exercise groups were provided with access to a voluntary running wheel, while the sedentary mice were not. Half the mice in the exercise group and half in the control group were given drinking water supplemented with 1 mM sodium nitrate (Sigma-Aldrich, #221,341), which was replaced once a week. This dose was chosen as previous work shows that it increases running volume in C57Bl/6 mice18. This resulted in 4 experimental groups: sedentary control (female n = 8, male n = 7), sedentary supplemented with nitrates (female n = 8, male n = 7), running control (female n = 8, male n = 8) and running supplemented with nitrates (female n = 8, male n = 8). The impact of nitrate washout also was investigated in a small group of retired breeder female C57Bl/6 mice (9 months old, n = 5) purchased from the same supplier (St. Constant, QC, Canada). These mice were provided with both running wheel access and sodium nitrate for 12 weeks, as described above. After this, the mice continued to run but the nitrates were washed out for 4 weeks.

A treadmill time to exhaustion test was conducted to assess fitness. All fitness tests were done during daytime hours with a single lane treadmill (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus, Barcelona, Spain). Mice were acclimatized to the treadmill over two consecutive days with a 5-min period of no belt movement, followed by 5 min of low speed (5 cm/s, 0° incline) belt movement. For the fitness test, mice were acclimatized to the treadmill for 5 min and then the test started at a low speed (5 cm/s, 0° incline) for 3 min. Next, the treadmill was moved to a 15° incline and speed was increased by 1 cm/s every minute until exhaustion. If mice were unwilling to run, they were gently nudged to encourage running. Exhaustion was confirmed if: 1) the mouse spent more than 1 min in the bottom third of the treadmill; 2) the mouse required more than 5 nudges to encourage running; or 3) the mouse would not return to the treadmill from the non-electrified metal grid at the base. The time to exhaustion was recorded.

Blood pressure

At the end of the intervention, blood pressure was measured in vivo with the IITC Life Sciences tail-cuff system (Woodland Hills, CA) as previously described47. Mice were acclimatized to the procedure for two consecutive days by being placed in the blood pressure tube and left alone for 10 min. Next, blood pressure was measured for two consecutive two days. The mice rested for 10 min in the blood pressure cuff, before 3 sets of 5 recordings of blood pressure were completed. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured for each mouse. Recordings with too much noise (e.g. tail movements) were discarded.

Echocardiography

Cardiac structure and function were measured by echocardiography at three time points (baseline, 6 weeks and 12 weeks). Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane in oxygen gas exhausting at 0.8 L/min, then maintained at 2–2.5% isoflurane throughout the procedure. Anaesthetized mice were placed in a supine position on a heated multiaxial platform. This allowed the mice to maintain physiologic temperature throughout, as verified with a rectal thermometer. Their paws were attached to recording electrodes on the platform with surgical tape to record the electrocardiogram. The chest fur was then removed with depilatory cream and transmission gel was applied. The mouse heart was imaged with a Vevo 2100 imaging platform (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto CA) using a high-resolution linear transducer (18–38 MHz, MS400). Short axis M-mode images were used to determine LV structure and function. The apical 4-chamber view was used for pulse wave Doppler recordings to measure diastolic function. Strain analysis (Vevo Strain 1.0) was also completed using the long axis view. All images were analyzed with Vevo lab (Version 3.1.1, FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto CA).

Tissue collection

At the end of the experiment, animals were weighed then euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg, and 100 U of heparin, IP). The depth of anesthesia was checked using the paw pinch method and loss of blinking reflex to ensure lack of sensation. In half of the animals, the entire heart was removed, weighed, and the ventricles were dissected and divided into two sections. The tibia was also removed for wet heart weight normalization. Ventricular tissue was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen then stored at -80 °C. This tissue was used in subsequent experiments to isolate mRNA and to measure myofilament function and protein phosphorylation, as described later. Hearts from the remaining mice were used for cell isolation, as detailed next.

Cell isolation

Ventricular myocytes were enzymatically dissociated with our established techniques48–52. After deep anesthesia was achieved, the chest was opened, the aorta was cannulated and the heart was perfused for 5 min with heated (37 °C; pH 7.4 with NaOH) buffer (NaH2PO4: 0.33 mM, MgCl2: 1.0 mM, glucose (C6H12O6): 20.0 mM, sodium pyruvate (C3H3NaO3): 3.0 mM, NaCl: 105.0 mM, KCl: 5.0 mM, HEPES (C8H18N2O4S): 25.0 mM, lactic acid: 1.0 mM) supplemented with 100 µM CaCl2. Next, the heart was perfused for 10 min with calcium-free buffer. Finally, the heart was perfused for 8 to 10 min with this buffer supplemented with calcium (50 µM), collagenase type 2 (8 mg/30 mL; Worthington Biochemical), dispase 2 (3.5 mg/30 mL; neutral protease grade 2, Godo Shusei Co) and trypsin (0.5 mg/30 mL; Sigma Life Sciences). Then the ventricles were removed, minced and stored in high potassium buffer solution (pH 7.4 with KOH; KCl: 30 mM, MgSO4*7H2O: 3 mM, KH2PO4: 30 mM, L-glutamic acid: 50 mM, taurine (C2H7NO3S): 20 mM, EGTA (C14H24N2O10): 0.5 mM, HEPES (C8H18N2O4S): 10 mM, glucose: 10 mM). Cell suspensions were filtered through a 225 μm polyethylene mesh (Spectra/Mesh) to yield isolated ventricular myocytes.

Measurement of calcium transients and contractions

Suspended cells were incubated in 2.5 µM fura-2 AM (Invitrogen, F-1221; dissolved in anhydrous DMSO) on the stage of an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse TE200, Nikon Canada, Mississauga, ON) for 30 min in the dark with our established techniques48–52. Cells were then superfused with a heated (37 °C; pH 7.4 with NaOH) solution (NaCl: 135 mM, KCl: 4 mM, HEPES: 10 mM, MgCl2: 1 mM, CaCl2: 2 mM, glucose: 10 mM) at 3 mL/min. Cells were field-stimulated at 2 Hz with bipolar pulses via platinum electrodes coupled to a stimulus isolation unit (Model # SIU-102; Warner Instruments, Hamden, CT) controlled by pClamp 8.2 software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA). Contractions and calcium transients were recorded simultaneously recorded by splitting the microscope light between the camera (model TM-640, Pulnix America) and photomultiplier tube (PTI, Brunswick NJ, USA) with a dichroic mirror (Chroma Tech. Corp. Rockingham, VT). Cells were visualized on a CCTV monitor coupled to a video edge detector (Crescent Electronics, Sandy, UT) to measure cell length (120 samples/sec). Calcium transients were measured with a DeltaRam fluorescence system and Felix 32 software (Photon Technologies International, Birmingham, NJ). Cells were excited at 340 and 380 nm with fluorescence emission at 510 nm recorded for both wavelengths at 200 samples/sec. A background measurement of fluorescence was performed next to each experimental cell, and the background was subtracted from each recording. Emission ratios were converted to calcium concentrations with an in vitro calibration curve as in our earlier studies48–52.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

RNA was extracted from ventricular tissue using an RNA extraction and purification kit (Aurum™ Total RNA Fatty and Fibrous Tissue Kit, catalog # 732–6830, Bio-Rad, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration was measured using a nanodrop to ensure similar concentrations of cDNA in later steps. The RNA solution was stored at -80 °C until it was used. The RNA was converted into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR, Catalog # 1,708,841, Bio-Rad, CA) at 42.5 ng/µL and stored at -20 °C. Primers were purchased already pre-designed from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, CA, USA). The primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S4. Primers were reconstituted with 1 × Tris solution (IDTE 1 × TE solution, pH 8, catalog 43 # 11–05-01–13, IDT, USA). Primers were mixed with a SYBR green super mix (SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR™ Green Supermix, catalog # 172,570, Bio-Rad, CA) following the manufacturers suggested concentrations. All qPCRs were run to amplify at 60 °C and for 39 cycles on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, model: CFX Connect Optics Module, CA, USA).

Myofilament proteins

Ventricular tissue was prepared for myofilament isolation with our established techniques, as described previously52,53. In brief, ventricular tissue was homogenized in ice-cold standard buffer (60 mM KCl, 30 mM imidazole (pH 7.0), 2 mM MgCl2, 0.01 mM leupeptin, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.2 mM benzamidine, and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail containing cantharidin, (-)-p bromolevamisole oxalate, and calyculin A (P0044, Millipore Sigma)). The homogenate was spun at 14,100 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in cold standard buffer mixed with 1% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The resulting solution was washed three times at 1100 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was resuspended in cold standard buffer and myofilaments were either flash frozen then stored for later myofilament protein phosphorylation assays) or maintained on ice and used immediately to measure actomyosin MgATPase activity.

Myofilament protein (25 mg) was incubated in activation buffer with increasing levels of free calcium. Free calcium concentrations were calculated using the Max-Chelator program Winmax (version 2.05). Myofilament protein was incubated in the activation buffer for 10 min at 32 °C and then stopped with ice-cold 10% trichloroacetic acid. Inorganic phosphate produced during the incubation, indicative of myofilament ATPase activity, was then measured using equal volumes of 0.5% FeSO4 and 0.5% ammonium molybdate in 0.5 M H2SO4. The production of inorganic phosphate was quantified as the absorbance at 630 nm. Protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay.

To measure myofilament protein phosphorylation, myofilament proteins (10 µg) were separated using an SDS PAGE (12% resolving), then fixed in 50% methanol plus 10% acetic acid at room temperature overnight. Total protein phosphorylation was determined by staining the gel with ProQ Diamond phosphorylation stain (Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON). Total protein load was determined using a Coomassie staining gel with actin as a loading control, as in our previous work54. Both stains were quantified by imaging gels with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories Ltd., Mississauga, ON) and data were analysed with ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analyses

Statistics were performed using Prism (GraphPad version 10.4.1, Boston USA) and Sigmaplot version 14 (Grafiti LLC, Paolo Alto USA). Cardiac parameters measured over time were analyzed with a linear mixed model. Analyses conducted at the endpoint used a 2-way ANOVA or a 2-way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey or Fisher LSD post-hoc tests, as described in the legends. All tests used considered p ≤ 0.05 as significant. Outliers were tested with a Grubb’s outlier test with α = 0.05. No more than 1 outlier was removed from any one data set.

Contractions were averaged using Clampfit 8.2 (pCLAMP, Axon Instruments), but with the first and last recordings removed. Individual contractions that were affected by noise or arrythmias were also removed from the average. Calcium transient data were exported into Clampfit 8.2 to allow individual transients from a single cell to be averaged. As with contractions, the first and last measured transients were removed. These averages were used to determine peak responses as well as the velocity to peak contraction/calcium transient and the decay velocity for each response.

Gene expression was analysed with CFX Maestro 2.2 (Bio-Rad, version 5.2.008, CA). Both HPRT and RPLP0 were run as housekeeping genes, and all genes had a no-reverse transcriptase control and a no template control. All expressions were normalised to the sedentary controls for that age and sex. Amplifications that occurred after 37 cycles were not considered successful.
