Varietal development and rice yield growth in India: assessing IRRI’s germplasm contribution
Authors: Aldas Janaiah, Ashok K Mishra, Valerien O Pede, Sheelam Kaviraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study analyzed the contribution of International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) improved germplasm to rice varietal development and yield growth in India. Out of the total of 1436 released high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice, 769 (53%) were developed using IRRI improved germplasm at different stages of the breeding cycle. IR8 alone was used 245 times during this period. Most of IRRI’s elite lines were used for the improvement of important varietal traits, such as short duration and resistance to biotic stresses. The share of IRRI elite germplasm in all HYVs released in India was 22.8%. The average rice area withIRRI improved germplasm was 16.6% of the total rice area per year from 1966 to 2021. In addition, 11% of rice yield growth was contributed by IRRI improved germplasm each year for the 1966–2021 period. The results indicate that the IRRI-India partnership in germplasm exchange has contributed immensely to rice improvement in India. However, this contribution started declining after 2005, probably due to the strengthening of the national rice research system in India.Keywords: Partnership research, High-yielding varieties, Impact, Genetic improvement indexSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Rice is the lifeline and a major source of livelihood for about 120 million households in India. The level of growth in the rice sector has economy-wide effects through inter-sectoral linkages1–3. India is home to the widely cultivable indica rice, with about 60,000 landraces distributed all over the country. The improvement of traditional rice landraces has been a continuous process in India since the late 19th and early twentieth centuries to increase rice supplies through enhanced yield. The rice improvement program in India, however, gained momentum with the introduction of semi-dwarf input-responsive modern varieties in the 1960s and ’70s. The modern varieties were quickly adopted by farmers, especially in the favorable irrigated ecosystem, which led to the emergence of the Green Revolution in India3,4.

India’s national agricultural research system (NARS) has a well-developed infrastructure with an organized institutional setup in the public sector. The Indian NARS has two components. At the central government (or federal government) level, about 100 research centers/institutes/directorates under the leadership of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) operate in different regions of the country, primarily focusing on problems of regional/national importance. At the state (provincial) level, about 80 state agricultural universities are under the control of state governments. Every state has at least one state agricultural university that concentrates on state-specific problems besides offering education in agricultural science. The contribution of public-sector agricultural research and development (R&D) in achieving India’s food security is known worldwide5. Several studies have reported high payoffs to investments made in the Indian NARS6–10.

Research through partnership mechanisms among the different research institutions is a common practice in the Indian NARS,but it did not occur in an organized form until the 1960s. The Indian NARS has witnessed several milestones over the past half a century in achieving the national goal of food security. One of the significant milestones in its history is the establishment of All India Coordinated Research Programs (AICRPs) in the 1960s. The scientists from across the Indian states and institutions getting together for extensive and free sharing of information and breeding materials was a dream in such a big country until the early 1960s. Still, it became a reality through the institutional innovation of AICRPs. The success of the coordinated maize improvement program that ICAR initiated on a pilot basis in 1957 triggered interest among research managers and policymakers in re-introducing institutional innovation in rice and wheat in 1965. Subsequently, this was extended to many agricultural research areas. There are now about 100 AICRPs under the direct control of ICAR, covering all major crops, livestock, dairy, fisheries, and natural resources.

The AICRPs have institutionalized partnership research in public-sector R&D. All concerned scientists from every corner of the country are allowed free sharing of information and materials. Further, the Indian NARS has a close partnership with international agricultural research centers, such as the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) and the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT)11. At the same time, the significant role of modern technologies from the NARS and international agricultural research centers in achieving India’s food security is widely recognized by several studies2,4,12. The contribution of national and international partnerships in agricultural R&D has not received the attention of the impact studies.

Therefore, our study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by analyzing the synergetic effects of the partnership between the Indian national rice research system and IRRI on varietal development and yield growth of rice in India. Specifically, it evaluates the contribution of IRRI-derived germplasm to rice varietal development in India over 55 years (1966-2021). The specific objectives of the study are as follows. First, to compute the contribution of IRRI improved germplasm to the overall genetic improvement in released high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice during the period 1966-2021; second, to estimate the share of IRRI improved germplasm in HYVs; and third, to assess the contribution of IRRI improved germplasm to yield growth and financial gains in rice.

The study is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a brief description of the partnership mechanisms between the Indian NARS and IRRI. Section 3 presents data sources and methods of estimation of genetic improvement indices and share in yield growth. Section 4 discusses the contribution of IRRI germplasm to overall genetic improvement by ecosystem, period, and varietal traits. Section 5 analyzes the adoption and dis-adoption (dropout) patterns of high-yielding rice varieties in India, evaluates their impact on rice yield growth, and highlights IRRI’s contributions. The last section concludes the study.

Background: IRRI-India partnership

The IRRI-India partnership is as old as IRRI, since its inception in the 1960s. India continues to be a major partner with IRRI in terms of the exchange of knowledge, research materials, and human resources. The All India Coordinated Rice Improvement Project (AICRIP) of ICAR became a role model for the establishment of the International Rice Testing Program (IRTP) at IRRI in 1975, which was reorganized as the International Network for Genetic Evaluation of Rice (INGER) in 1986. India played a significant role as a founding member of IRTP/INGER in the exchange of improved germplasm of rice between India and IRRI. Many other Asian countries benefited from India’s rice germplasm, which was made accessible through IRRI’s IRTP/INGER networks. A recent study reported that nearly half of the released HYVs of rice in Nepal have Indian-linked germplasm13. Similarly, about 40 percent of the released HYVs of rice in Bangladesh and Myanmar were based on India-linked germplasm, whereas 22 percent of Vietnam’s HYVs have germplasm of Indian origin13.

The IRRI-India partnership was significantly strengthened with the release of the first high-yielding variety, IR8, in 1966, marking a major milestone in the Green Revolution14,15. The improved germplasm of IRRI, as well as that of other Asian countries that were members of the IRTP/INGER networks, was made accessible to the Indian rice research program under AICRIP. Much germplasm introduced by IRRI was used in the development of several HYVs in the subsequent periods. Further, some of the promising IRRI germplasm lines were directly released as HYVs in India. The first such IRRI line released in India was IR8 in 1966, followed by IR20 in 1970 and another nine (IR22, IR24, IR26, IR28, IR30, IR32, IR34, IR36, and IR50) in subsequent periods. It is also worth mentioning that a promising rice HYV, Intan, which was introduced from Indonesia through the IRRI network, is widely cultivated in Karnataka, India, and was released in 197516,17. Likewise, several Indian HYVs also have IRRI-linked germplasm. This highlights the synergic role of the IRRI-India partnership in rice research that mutually benefited IRRI, India, and other countries.

Methodology

Data sources

Our study has used required data on rice varietal releases and their pedigree (genealogy), and adoption pattern of HYVs, primarily from both published and unpublished reports of ICAR and the Indian Institute of Rice Research (IIRR), and various state agricultural universities. The list of HYVs of rice released from 1966 to 2021, along with their basic features, was compiled from annual reports of ICAR-IIRR and other state agricultural universities. The pedigree of each released HYV, which has three stages (parents, grandparents, and great-grandparents), was collected from the reports of AICRIP, ICAR-IIRR. Variety-wise, area coverage is not available in any published reports in India. However, variety-wise breeder seed production of all HYVs as per the demand and preferences of state agricultural departments is available. Based on the conversion ratio of breeder seed to foundation seed to certified seed, the total area under HYVs was computed and is available from 1966. From 1991, variety-wise breeder seed production was used to estimate the area under a particular HYV. In addition to the above data, secondary data on rice area, production, yield, and farm harvest prices from 1966 to 2021 were collected from published sources of the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare, Government of India.

Results and discussion

IRRI germplasm’s contribution to varietal development in India

India’s rice varietal development program started with the release of the first HYV of rice, Jaya, in 1968. However, Indian farmers experienced unprecedented benefits from IRRI’s first HYV, IR8, during the mid-1960s, prior to the release of Jaya18. In India, IRRI conducted the first on-farm demonstration of IR8 on a small farm during the wet season in 1966 at two locations near agricultural research stations, Maruteru (Andhra Pradesh) and Aduthurai (Tamil Nadu). IRRI scientists directly supervised planting methods and other agronomic practices in these demonstration plots. In addition, farmers planted leftover IR8 seeds separately on small plots in the same locations during the same season. The demonstration plots recorded yields of about 7 t/ha under improved agronomic practices, including irrigation and fertilizer application, under the supervision of IRRI agronomists. In contrast, farmers who used leftover seeds achieved yields of about 6.5 t/ha without applying chemical fertilizer. This successful performance of IR8 attracted the attention of the Indian Agricultural Secretary, who visited the demonstration sites. In the following dry season of 1967, nearly 400 farmers cultivated IR8 on about 2000 acres in villages around Maruteru, using seeds supplied by IRRI as well as those retained from the previous season. Thereafter, IR8 spread rapidly among farmers across the southern regions of India19,20.

Share of IRRI Varieties in total released high-yielding varieties of rice

After the IR8 experience in 1966, several HYVs were developed and released by the Indian rice research system, including those elite IRRI lines such as IR20, IR36, etc. A total of 1436 HYVs of rice were released by both the ICAR system and state agricultural universities for different ecosystems and states over 55 years (1966-2021). Out of these, 839 varieties (58.4%) were released for a favorable irrigated ecosystem, which accounts for 56 percent of India’s rice area. The partnership between IRRI and the Indian NARS has continued since the inception of IRRI in 1960. In particular, the collaboration in the exchange of improved rice germplasm between India and IRRI gained momentum with the establishment of the International Rice Testing Program (IRTP) at IRRI during 1975. As a result, IRRI improved germplasm played a significant role in strengthening rice breeding programs in India through its widespread introduction. Similarly, India’s rice germplasm was shared with IRRI for its breeding program and was also made available to rice breeding programs in other countries through IRRI’s networks. Therefore, India, IRRI, and other countries mutually benefited from their respective rice breeding programs through IRRI’s networks. For instance, of the 1436 high yielding rice varieties released in India (from 1966 to 2021), 769 HYVs were developed using IRRI-derived germplasm as a parent, grandparent, or great-grandparent (Table 2). This implies that more than half of the total HYVs released in India have contained IRRI-derived germplasm at different stages of their pedigree. It is pertinent to note that nearly 60 percent of the IRRI-based HYVs in India are mainly for the irrigated ecosystem. Among the 769 IRRI-based HYVs, 11 elite IRRI lines were directly released in India. Similarly, in 533 HYVs, one of the parents has IRRI germplasm. Further, much IRRI germplasm was also used as one of the grandparents or great-grandparents or both in many HYVs that were released over the 55 year period (Table 3).

One of the priorities in the rice breeding program was to reduce the maturity period in newly developed HYVs. Most of the traditional varieties grown during the pre-1960s were long-maturity varieties, ranging from 150 to180 days of crop duration. Therefore, the rice breeding program, especially after the mid-1970s, aimed at developing short-duration varieties to have two crops in a year on the same piece of land. As Table 5 demonstrates, nearly two-thirds of the HYVs released are either short- or medium-duration varieties. Similarly, the improvement of resistance to biotic stresses such as diseases and pests was a major breeding priority after the mid-1980s. Therefore, the number of HYVs with resistance to one or more diseases has increased substantially after the mid-1980s. The varieties’ resistance to one or more insect pests has also increased considerably after the mid-1980s.

Share of IRRI germplasm in overall genetic improvement

As explained in the above section, several germplasms from different sources are used for varying stages of varietal improvement programs to develop a particular HYV, which could take an average of 8–10 years. Therefore, each HYV has parents, grandparents, and great-grandparents in its pedigree. In our study, parents, grandparents, and great-grandparents (three stages) were taken into account to identify the sources of germplasm at each stage and assign scores, accordingly, as explained in the previous section. We computed the genetic improvement index (GII) for each HYV using the assigned scores and the share of IRRI germplasm to estimate overall GII for each variety. The share of IRRI germplasm is observed only in those HYVs wherein IRRI germplasm was used in any of its three stages. If no IRRI germplasm was found in a particular HYV, the share of IRRI germplasm was zero in the HYV. A share of IRRI germplasm was found in 769 HYVs out of 1436 HYVs released over the 55-year period. However, the average contribution of IRRI germplasm to the overall genetic improvement of all released varieties was computed, taking the total HYVs into account.

The share of IRRI improved germplasm in the overall genetic improvement of released HYVs during the period 1966-2021 is estimated at 22.79 percent. Table 7 shows a declining trend in the share of IRRI improved germplasm in genetic improvement from 1966 to 2021. During the initial period (1966-1975), IRRI germplasm accounted for nearly 40 percent of the varietal releases, which declined to only 15.35 percent by 2016-2021. These estimates suggest that the rice breeding program in the Indian NARS was strengthened over the period, thereby reducing the dependency on IRRI for improved germplasm. Further, Table 7 shows that the share of IRRI germplasm was higher in those HYVs that were released for the irrigated ecosystems (24.46%).

Share of IRRI germplasm in adoption rate and yield growth

It is not unusual to observe that all released HYVs are not necessarily accepted and adopted by farmers. The sustained adoption rate of HYVs is established only after a certain period after their release to farmers. The HYVs of any crop, particularly self-pollinated crops such as rice, are made available to farmers through the distribution of seeds of those HYVs. In India, it is the mandatory responsibility of research institutions to produce a limited quantity of breeder seed of released HYVs under the direct supervision of the concerned breeder every year based on requests and indents from the Ministry of Agriculture, Government of India. The breeder seeds of HYVs produced at research stations are further distributed to seed development agencies/corporations in the country for the multiplication of foundation seed and certified seed in the subsequent crop seasons. The certified seeds are finally distributed to farmers either through public-sector agencies or private-sector outlets for commercial cultivation. The breeder seed production of any HYV is undertaken at different research institutions only if there is a request or indent from the Ministry of Agriculture.

The absence of an indent for a particular HYV indicates a lack of farmer demand, resulting in its gradual replacement and eventual discontinuation from cultivation. The responsibility of compilation and allotment of HYV-wise breeder seed production among different rice research institutions in India is assigned to AICRIP of ICAR-IIRR. For our study, we compiled HYV-wise breeder seed production of different HYVs from the various reports of ICAR-IIRR for the period 1991-2021. Similar data were not available in systematic reports prior to 1991. The number of HYVs under breeder seed production in a year is considered the number of cultivated or adopted varieties since farmers access seeds of any HYV if it is under a breeder seed production program. Therefore, the number of adopted/cultivated varieties in a farmer’s field is nearly the same as the number of HYVs under the breeder seed production program. Based on breeder seed production data, we estimated the area cultivated under each HYV according to its varietal share.

Adoption rates of HYVs vis-à-vis the share of IRRI germplasm

We note that breeder seed of a total of 1051 HYVs was produced during the period 1991-2021 (Fig. 3). Among the HYVs, only six were continuously under breeder seed production almost every year from 1991 to 2021. Another 19 HYVs were under breeder seed production for 26 to 30 years during the same period (Fig. 3). Thus, 25 HYVs out of 1051 under the breeder seed production program have been adopted by farmers for more than 25 years (from 1991 to 2021). Many of them are still in farmers’ fields in different parts of the country. Figure 3 further reports that nearly 30 percent of the total adopted varieties from 1991 to 2021 were discontinued after only one year of their adoption. Another one-third of the total adopted HYVs were dropped from farmers’ fields within five years of their adoption during the same period (Fig. 3). Only about 10 percent of the total adopted HYVs were found in farmers’ fields for more than 15 years (from 1991 to 2021). Therefore, the average adoption rate of HYVs, as well as the dropout rate, was almost the same during the period 1991-2021. This implies that, on average, there was not a significant change between the number of HYV dropouts and the number of newly adopted HYVs over the 55-year period (Table 9).

Contribution of IRRI germplasm to area coverage of HYVs

India’s farmers started growing HYVs of rice only after the release of IRRI’s first HYV, IR8, in 1966. However, the widespread adoption of HYVs of rice increased after the release of India’s first HYV, Jaya, in 1968. The average area covered by HYVs from 1966 to 1975 was 6.43 million hectares, accounting for 17 percent of India’s rice area. From 2016 to 2021, about 95 percent of India’s rice area was covered under HYVs (Table 11). On average, 26.73 million hectares (about 65%) of the total rice area was covered under HYVs every year during the period 1966-2021. The average contribution of IRRI germplasm to the overall HYV area was estimated at 28.21 percent during this 55-year period. Accordingly, the HYV area accounted for by IRRI improved germplasm was 16.63 percent of the average total rice area during this period. However, the contribution of IRRI germplasm to area coverage increased from 1966-1975 to 1996-2005 and subsequently declined (Table 11). This indicates that the contribution of IRRI germplasm to HYV area has declined after 2005, primarily due to the strengthening of national rice research programs.

Contribution of IRRI germplasm to production gains and yield growth

Total rice production, on average, was 61 million tons of paddy per year during the period 1966-1975. Additionally, yield increased threefold during the period 2016-2021. The share of IRRI improved germplasm in India’s overall rice production was 12.3 percent during 1966-1975, but declined to 7.5 percent during 2006-2015. On average, 12.9 percent of India’s rice production per year was accounted for exclusively by IRRI improved germplasm during 1966-2021 (Table 12). In monetary terms, about USD 2.35 billion in financial gains accrued to IRRI improved germplasm per year from 1966 to 2021 (Table 12). This indicates high payoffs to the Indian rice sector due to IRRI improved germplasm, which was made possible through the IRRI-India partnership in rice research. Similarly, 11 percent of India’s rice yield growth was accounted for by IRRI improved germplasm every year from 1966 to 2021. The share of IRRI germplasm in yield growth declined after 2005 in India (see Table 13).

Conclusions

The IRRI-India partnership is as old as IRRI, since its inception in 1960. This partnership has covered the exchange of knowledge, research materials, and human resources between IRRI and India on the one hand and India and other countries through IRRI networks on the other hand. This partnership contributed significantly to the genetic improvement of rice through the exchange of improved germplasm. Our study analyzed the contribution of IRRI improved germplasm to varietal development and yield growth in India from 1966 to 2021. Out of the total of 1436 HYVs of rice released in India, 769 (53%) were developed using IRRI-derived improved germplasm at various stages of the varietal development process. In 533 HYVs, IRRI germplasm was used as one of the parents during the period 1966-2021. In sum, 307 elite IRRI lines were used 1259 times at different stages of the varietal development program during this period. IR8 alone was used 245 times during this period. Most of these elite IRRI lines were used for the improvement of important varietal traits such as short duration, resistance to biotic stresses, etc. IRRI germplasm accounted for in all released HYVs was estimated at 22.79 percent. However, this share declined after 2005, indicating that the national rice research system had been strengthened. The irrigated ecosystem benefited with a higher share of IRRI germplasm vis-à-vis the rainfed ecosystem during this period.

A total of 1051 HYVs of rice were found in farmers’ fields during the period 1991-2021, of which 25 popular HYVs were grown for more than 25 years during this period. Nearly two-thirds of the 1051 adopted HYVs were discontinued from farmers’ fields within five years of their adoption during this period. The 25 most popular HYVs, which were grown for more than 25 years from 1991 to 2021, have occupied 28 percent of India’s rice area. These 25 popular HYVs were developed and released from 25 to 40 years ago. The average rice area accounted for by IRRI improved germplasm was estimated to be 16.63 percent of the total rice area every year (6.9 million hectares) during the 1966-2021 period. Similarly, 12.9 percent of India’s rice production every year was accounted for by IRRI improved germplasm over the 55-year period. In addition, 11 percent of rice yield growth was contributed by IRRI improved germplasm every year during the same period.

The results indicate that the IRRI-India partnership in germplasm exchange has contributed immensely to rice improvement in India. However, this contribution started declining after 2005, primarily due to the strengthening of the national rice research system in India. Further, there is scope for understanding the probability of success in rice varietal improvement programs based on the past 60 years of varietal development and evaluation data. Such a study helps to understand the opportunity cost of time in the varietal development process and suggests initiating steps to shorten breeding cycles.
