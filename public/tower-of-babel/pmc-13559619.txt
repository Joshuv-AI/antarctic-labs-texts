Survival outcomes of postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy in early-stage cervical cancer: a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database-based stratified analysis
Authors: Changmei Weng, Yan Wang, Wenbin Chen, Shengchi Chen, Li Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor early-stage cervical cancer, surgery constitutes the standard therapeutic approach. Whether adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy should be administered to patients with intermediate- or high-risk pathological characteristics continues to generate debate. Controversy persists regarding the ideal postoperative adjuvant treatment approach for this patient population. Using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, this study compared survival outcomes among early-stage cervical cancer patients managed with observation, postoperative radiotherapy (PORT), or postoperative concurrent chemoradiotherapy (POCRT).MethodsPatients with stage I–II cervical cancer who underwent surgical treatment from 2004 to 2020 were identified through the SEER database. Patients were classified into three cohorts based on their postoperative management: observation, PORT, and POCRT. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were assessed through Cox proportional hazards regression, competing risk modeling, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and subgroup analyses.ResultsAmong 13,687 eligible patients, 9,868 underwent postoperative observation, 1,237 received PORT, and 2,582 received POCRT. In the unmatched overall cohort, patients receiving adjuvant therapy had higher mortality rates and higher-risk baseline profiles. Stratified analysis demonstrated that postoperative observation was associated with better survival than PORT and POCRT in stage I patients (P<0.001), whereas POCRT was associated with better survival compared with observation and PORT in stage II patients (P=0.04). Subgroup analyses suggested associations between postoperative adjuvant therapy and better survival among patients with T2 disease, N1 status, multiple primary malignancies (≥2), or age ≥60 years.ConclusionsFor patients with early-stage cervical cancer, the decision regarding PORT and chemotherapy should remain guided by current guideline-based risk stratification. Our findings suggest that tumor (T) stage, node (N) stage, multiple primary malignancies, and age warrant further investigation as potential effect modifiers.Keywords: Cervical cancer; adenocarcinoma (ADC); squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); prognosis; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database (SEER database)

Highlight box

Key findings

• This Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results-based study identified survival differences by postoperative management in early-stage cervical cancer. For stage I patients, observation was associated with better survival compared to postoperative radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (POCRT). In contrast, POCRT was associated with significantly better survival among stage II patients. Adjuvant therapy was associated with better survival in high-risk subgroups: T2 disease, N1 status, multiple primary malignancies, and age ≥60 years.

What is known and what is new?

• While surgery is standard for early-stage cervical cancer, the optimal postoperative adjuvant strategy (radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy) for intermediate- or high-risk patients remains debated.

• Among patients with stage I disease, observation was associated with better survival than adjuvant treatment, possibly reflecting treatment selection and unmeasured confounding. Conversely, concurrent chemoradiotherapy was associated with significantly better survival among stage II patients. Adjuvant therapy was also associated with better survival in subgroups: T2, N1, multiple primary malignancies, and age ≥60 years.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The observed patterns raise questions about possible overtreatment in some patients with stage I disease and possible underuse of risk-adapted therapy in high-risk groups; these possibilities cannot be resolved using observational data.

• The associations between postoperative management and survival varied by stage and risk profile (T2, N1, age ≥60 years, multiple primary malignancies); these subgroup findings are exploratory and require prospective confirmation.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide. The World Health Organization reported that cervical cancer accounted for approximately 661,000 new diagnoses and 348,000 deaths worldwide in 2022, representing 6.8% of all incident female cancers (1). Cervical cancer incidence varies considerably by region, with the highest rates documented in countries characterized by a low Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), especially across Africa and sub-Saharan Africa (2,3). In developed countries, the widespread implementation of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and cancer screening programs has led to substantial declines in both incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer (4-6). The incidence of cervical cancer begins to increase after the age of 25 years, showing a trend toward younger onset, and presents a bimodal distribution or a single peak followed by a gradual decline across different age groups (7,8). Cervical cancer severely affects fertility and imposes significant burdens on families and society, resulting in considerable consumption of medical resources and loss of productivity (9).

International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage and pathological risk factors form the principal basis for adjuvant therapy decisions in patients with early-stage cervical cancer (10). Patients with FIGO stage IB–IIA disease who meet the Sedlis criteria because of intermediate-risk factors such as lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), deep stromal invasion, and large tumor size are candidates for postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy (11). However, retrospective studies have suggested that patients stratified according to the Sedlis criteria may not experience a significant benefit from postoperative adjuvant therapy (12,13). Meta-analyses have further reported no significant improvement in recurrence-free survival among early-stage cervical cancer patients with intermediate-risk pathological features who received adjuvant therapy compared with postoperative observation, while treatment-related toxicity is markedly increased (14,15). Perineural invasion has emerged in recent years as an additional independent prognostic factor in cervical cancer (16,17). Recurrence patterns following surgery differ by histological type: in squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), depth of invasion is a key determinant, whereas tumor size plays a more prominent role in adenocarcinoma (ADC) (18). A European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) survey indicated that most experts believe that postoperative adjuvant treatment decisions should incorporate tumor size, histological grade, pathological subtype, age, surgical margin status, surgical approach, and lymph node involvement (19). Evidence from recent reviews supports observation alone for low-risk patients and adjuvant chemoradiotherapy for those at high risk due to lymph node metastasis, parametrial invasion, or bulky tumors; however, the optimal management of intermediate-risk patients remains unsettled (15).

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database records multiple clinicopathological variables, including tumor size, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, pathological subtype, age, surgical treatment, histological grade, and Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) stage. This study used the TNM classification recorded in SEER to evaluate associations between postoperative treatment and survival across stage combinations in early-stage cervical cancer and to explore whether these associations varied across patient subgroups. The findings were intended to generate hypotheses for future studies of risk-adapted postoperative management rather than to guide clinical decisions. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1431/rc).

Methods

Data source

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. SEER*Stat software version 8.4.5 was used to retrieve study data from the SEER database. Cervical cancer cases diagnosed from January 1, 2004, through December 31, 2020, were identified for inclusion. Administered by the National Cancer Institute, data from 18 population-based cancer registries, encompassing approximately 34.6% of the U.S. population, are consolidated by the SEER program. The data are publicly available through the SEER website. Given that the study utilized anonymized and de-identified publicly available SEER data, institutional review board approval and informed consent were waived.

Staging was based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification recorded in the SEER database at diagnosis. The definitions of the T1, T2, N0, and N1 categories have remained consistent across AJCC editions for early-stage cervical cancer. Under the 2009 FIGO system, however, stage I disease with lymph node metastasis would be classified as stage IIIB, whereas the 2018 FIGO system classifies such cases as stage IIIC.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised: (I) age at least 18 years; (II) histopathological diagnosis of cervical ADC [International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD-O-3) codes 8140/3, 8260/3, 8262/3, 8144/3, 8384/3, 8380/3] or SCC (ICD-O-3 codes 8070/3, 8072/3, 8071/3, 8076/3, 8052/3, 8083/3, 8073/3, 8074/3, 8082/3, 8084/3, 8050/3, 8075/3, 8078/3, 8085/3); (III) diagnosis from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2020; (IV) early-stage disease (TNM stage I or II), staging was based on the AJCC TNM classification recorded in the SEER database; (V) primary cervical origin; (VI) available follow-up and death cause data; and (VII) documented surgical, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy information.

The exclusion criteria included incomplete information on race, marital status, follow-up time, or income; patients who did not undergo surgical treatment; stage I or II disease with T stage classified as T3 or T4; and the presence of distant metastasis (M1). Figure 1 presents the patient selection process. The final analysis included 13,687 patients who satisfied all eligibility criteria. Patients were classified into three cohorts according to postoperative adjuvant management: observation (n=9,868), postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) (n=1,237), and postoperative concurrent chemoradiotherapy (POCRT) (n=2,582). SEER*Stat software was employed to extract data from the SEER database. Variables retrieved from the database comprised race, marital status, histopathological subtype, income level, surgical procedure, radiotherapy, chemotherapy records, tumor size, pathological stage, survival time, vital status, cause of death, and cancer-specific death. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) served as the primary endpoints. OS was defined as the time from cancer diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. CSS was calculated from cervical cancer diagnosis to disease-specific death. In addition to treatment strategy, covariates included age, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, histopathological subtype, income level, surgical procedure, multiple primary malignancies, stage, and N stage. Because the overall AJCC stage (stage I or stage II) is determined by a combination of T, N, and M stages and was included as a categorical covariate in the analytical models, T stage was not entered separately to avoid multicollinearity. Patients who received both radiotherapy and chemotherapy were assigned to the POCRT group, whereas those who received radiotherapy alone were assigned to the PORT group. The SEER chemotherapy variable was used only to define these treatment groups and was not included as a separate covariate in the multivariable regression models.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages [n (%)] were used to describe clinicopathological categorical variables. Complete-case analysis was performed when covariates contained missing values. Prognostic factors for OS and CSS were identified through univariate Cox proportional hazards regression and competing risk analyses, with calculation of hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Fine-Gray competing risk model was used to analyze competing risk data and to assess the effects of covariates on CSS. In the multivariable analyses, selected or all covariates were included, and the results were reported as subdistribution HRs (SHRs) with 95% CIs. To assess the independent prognostic effect of treatment strategies with adjustment for potential confounders, three multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression and Fine-Gray competing risk models were established. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method for each treatment group, with subgroup analyses stratified by stage, histopathological subtype, and age. Intergroup differences were compared using the log-rank test. Forest plots were generated to display the results of subgroup analyses and interaction tests for prognostic factors associated with OS and CSS. R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for all statistical analyses. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess potential bias arising from the overrepresentation of stage IA1 patients in the observation group, we performed a sensitivity analysis that excluded all patients with stage IA1 disease (n=4,246). Multivariable Cox regression and competing-risk models were repeated in the non-IA1 cohort, with additional stratification by stage I vs. stage II disease.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 13,687 patients comprised the final study cohort (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Among all included patients, 6,265 (45.77%) were aged 40–59 years. White patients constituted the majority of the cohort, comprising 10,614 cases (77.55%). Cervical SCC was the predominant histopathological subtype, accounting for 11,184 patients (81.71%), whereas cervical ADC was diagnosed in 2,503 patients (18.29%). A total of 6,008 patients (43.90%) were in the middle-income category. Standard hysterectomy was performed in 6,038 patients (44.11%). Only 2,582 patients (18.86%) received chemotherapy. Most patients presented with stage I disease, comprising 12,214 cases (89.24%). Among stage I patients, 12,048 cases (98.64%) were classified as T1N0 and 166 cases (1.36%) as T1N1.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis demonstrated that OS was significantly associated with age, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, histopathological subtype, income level, surgical procedure, chemotherapy, multiple primary malignancies, overall stage, N stage, and postoperative treatment strategy (all P<0.05). Univariate competing risk analysis revealed that CSS was significantly associated with age, race, marital status, histopathological subtype, income level, surgical procedure, chemotherapy, multiple primary malignancies, overall stage, N stage, and postoperative treatment strategy (all P<0.05). During follow-up from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2020, the median follow-up duration was 100 months. A total of 2,254 patients (16.47%) died, including 1,160 deaths (8.48%) attributable to cervical cancer. Patients were stratified by postoperative management: observation (n=9,868; 72.10%), POCRT (n=2,582; 18.86%), and PORT (n=1,237; 9.04%). Significant differences were detected across the three groups (P<0.001).

Model results

In Model 1, which adjusted only for treatment strategy, both Cox proportional hazards and Fine-Gray competing risk analyses revealed that PORT and POCRT were linked to significantly elevated mortality risk compared with observation for OS and CSS (both P<0.001; Table 2). In Model 2, following adjustment for age, surgical procedure, overall stage, and treatment strategy, Cox proportional hazards and Fine-Gray competing risk analyses both showed that the PORT and POCRT groups had elevated mortality risk compared with the observation group (both P<0.001). Model 3 incorporated further covariates—age, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, histopathological subtype, income level, surgical procedure, multiple primary malignancies, overall stage, N stage—alongside treatment strategy. The Cox proportional hazards model demonstrated that, compared with postoperative observation, the treatment groups remained associated with higher mortality risk even after multivariable adjustment (P<0.001), as shown in Table 2. Similarly, Fine-Gray competing risk analysis indicated that the treatment groups exhibited significantly increased cancer-specific mortality risk compared with postoperative observation (P<0.001). Specifically, the POCRT group showed an SHR of 2.828 (95% CI: 2.392–3.343) compared with the observation group (P<0.001). These findings consistently demonstrated that postoperative adjuvant therapy in early-stage cervical cancer was associated with higher mortality risk relative to observation, whether in univariate analyses or after covariate adjustment.

Survival outcomes

Survival analyses demonstrated that, in the overall cohort, both OS and CSS were significantly better in the observation group than in the PORT and POCRT groups (all P<0.001; 5-year OS: 94% vs. 80%) (Figure 2A,2B). In multivariable models, PORT and POCRT were associated with higher risks of all-cause and cancer-specific mortality (all P<0.001). Stratified analyses by stage demonstrated that stage I patients, particularly those with N0 disease, had the most favorable outcomes with postoperative observation, whereas POCRT was associated with better survival than observation and PORT in stage II patients (5-year OS: 74% vs. 67%) (Figure 2C,2D). The observation group demonstrated significantly better survival than PORT and POCRT in patients with N0 disease (P<0.001); however, survival did not differ significantly among treatment groups in patients with N1 disease (Figure 2E,2F). ADC showed a slightly more favorable prognosis compared with SCC (5-year OS: 93% vs. 89%, P<0.001) (Figure 2G,2H). However, the associations between postoperative treatment and survival were similar across histopathological subtypes, with observation associated with better survival in stage I disease and POCRT associated with better survival in stage II disease and other high-risk groups. Survival rates declined with increasing age. Among high-risk patients aged ≥60 years, the survival gap between the observation group and the POCRT group narrowed, suggesting that this population may be more likely to show better survival with POCRT.

Subgroup analysis

Overall analyses in stage I and stage II cervical cancer patients demonstrated that the postoperative observation group had significantly better survival than the PORT and POCRT groups. Forest plots for OS and CSS revealed significant interactions between treatment strategy and age, surgical procedure, multiple primary malignancies, and stage (all P<0.05). In general, patients with stage II disease, N1 status, age ≥60 years, and multiple primary malignancies were more likely to show better survival with postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy than with observation, as illustrated in Figures 3,4 (P<0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

After excluding stage IA1 patients, multivariable Cox regression in the overall early-stage cohort (stage I–II) demonstrated that both PORT (HR 1.645, 95% CI: 1.447–1.870) and POCRT (HR 1.648, 95% CI: 1.466–1.853) were independently associated with worse OS than observation, with consistent findings in competing-risks analysis for cancer-specific mortality (PORT—sHR 2.123, 95% CI: 1.764–2.555; POCRT—sHR 2.402, 95% CI: 2.031–2.841) (Table 3). On stage-stratified analysis, these associations persisted in stage I (PORT—HR 1.672, 95% CI: 1.449–1.929; POCRT—HR 1.846, 95% CI: 1.622–2.100), whereas in stage II, no significant survival difference was observed (PORT—HR 0.960, P=0.79; POCRT—HR 0.867, P=0.25) (Table 4). These findings indicate that the association between observation and better survival in stage I was not explained solely by the inclusion of IA1 patients and that the observed associations varied by stage.

Discussion

In this study, data on early-stage cervical cancer diagnosed between 2004 and 2020 were extracted from the SEER database. According to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, relevant clinical data were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were categorized into a postoperative observation group, a PORT group, and a POCRT group based on postoperative adjuvant treatment strategy. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, Fine-Gray competing risk modeling, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and subgroup analyses comprised the analytical framework. Overall, the findings suggest that postoperative observation was associated with favorable survival among stage I patients without identified high-risk factors in this retrospective cohort. However, associations favoring adjuvant therapy were observed in specific subgroups, particularly patients with stage II disease or N1 status. Across three Cox proportional hazards and competing risk models, PORT and POCRT were consistently associated with higher mortality than postoperative observation. Subgroup analyses and Kaplan-Meier curves showed better survival among stage I than stage II patients, better survival among patients with ADC than SCC, an association between POCRT and better survival in stage II disease, and worse survival among N1 than N0 patients. Interaction analyses for OS and CSS subgroups demonstrated significant interactions between treatment strategy and age, surgical procedure, multiple primary malignancies, and stage (P<0.05). After covariate adjustment, Fine-Gray competing risk models continued to show associations of PORT and POCRT with higher cancer-specific mortality than observation in early-stage cervical cancer.

The more favorable outcomes observed with postoperative observation in stage I cervical cancer may partly reflect differences in baseline risk between treatment groups, including the concentration of lower-risk patients in the observation group. Given the toxicity associated with radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the balance between benefit and harm may vary according to baseline risk. A population-based multinational cohort study reported that, for patients with FIGO 2018 stage IB2–IIA2 early-stage cervical cancer, postoperative adjuvant therapy did not improve OS, and postoperative observation was recommended to reduce radiotherapy-related toxicity (20). Treatment selection bias may also contribute, as stage I patients at higher risk of recurrence are more likely to receive POCRT or PORT, and this subgroup inherently has a poorer prognosis. The present results showed that 1.36% of stage I patients had regional lymph node metastasis, and prognosis in these patients was significantly worse than in node-negative patients, with 3-year survival rates of 83.65% vs. 93.42%, respectively (P<0.001). Consistent with established evidence from the Peters criteria and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, regional lymph node metastasis was associated with worse survival in this cohort. The observed survival difference between node-positive and node-negative patients (3-year OS: 83.65% vs. 93.42%) aligns with the well-documented prognostic significance of nodal involvement. As a category 1 recommendation per NCCN guidelines, adjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains the standard of care for these high-risk patients. Similarly, a Turkish multicenter retrospective study identified lymph node metastasis as the primary independent prognostic factor for disease-free survival (DFS) and OS, reporting 5-year survival rates of 89% and 97% in node-positive and node-negative patients, respectively (21). A meta-analysis reported that a higher proportion of metastatic lymph nodes was associated with worse prognosis in stage I and II early-stage cervical cancer (22). These findings are consistent with the established prognostic relevance of regional lymph node status.

Patients with stage II disease in this cohort showed an association between postoperative chemoradiotherapy and better survival, consistent with current guideline recommendations for adjuvant therapy in stage II disease (23). However, because the SEER database lacks data on parametrial involvement, LVSI, surgical margin status, and surgical approach, we cannot determine how these specific risk factors contributed to the observed association. In stage II cervical cancer, the HR estimates were more favorable for concurrent chemoradiotherapy than for radiotherapy alone, but this observational comparison does not establish relative treatment efficacy.

The 2004–2020 study period encompassed substantial changes in cervical cancer management. Following publication of the LACC trial in 2018, open radical hysterectomy became the standard surgical approach in preference to minimally invasive surgery (24); however, surgical approach is not recorded in the SEER database. During the same period, advances in radiation techniques, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and image-guided adaptive brachytherapy, were associated with improvements in the therapeutic ratio of radiotherapy, as demonstrated in the EMBRACE studies (25). Survival outcomes may therefore have varied over time, but these temporal changes could not be accounted for in the present analysis. Nevertheless, a subgroup analysis by year of diagnosis (2004–2012 vs. 2013–2020), including an interaction test, detected no significant effect modification.

In this study, ADC was associated with better survival than SCC, consistent with some previous SEER-based analyses (26). However, findings in the literature are mixed, with many studies reporting worse or comparable outcomes for ADC depending on stage distribution and treatment context (27,28). The association observed in our study may reflect residual confounding related to differences in stage distribution, age, and treatment selection between histological subtypes, which could not be fully adjusted for in this retrospective analysis. Because the SEER database lacks central pathology review and information on LVSI and other pathological risk factors, this survival difference should not be interpreted as evidence of an inherent biological difference between histological subtypes. Comparisons based on pathological subtype were performed using unmatched data, and baseline imbalance may have introduced bias.

Age-stratified analyses indicated that postoperative chemoradiotherapy was associated with a larger survival difference in patients aged ≥60 years than in younger patients. This pattern may reflect residual confounding related to age, disease characteristics, comorbidity, or treatment selection. However, given the retrospective nature of this analysis, these results must be interpreted with caution. Residual confounding by age-related differences in pathological risk factors, comorbidity burden, and treatment selection cannot be excluded. Regarding surgical approach, postoperative adjuvant therapy was associated with a smaller survival difference among patients undergoing fertility-sparing surgery, possibly because these patients are usually diagnosed at an earlier stage with lower recurrence risk, and the long-term toxicity of adjuvant therapy may offset potential benefits (29,30). The present results also showed that postoperative adjuvant therapy was associated with a larger survival difference among patients with multiple primary malignancies. This association may reflect several possible explanations, including closer oncologic surveillance and more comprehensive management in patients with a history of multiple primary malignancies (surveillance bias), or shared germline susceptibility factors that influence treatment response. Further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms. Importantly, regardless of T1 or T2 classification, patients in the N1 subgroup consistently showed better outcomes with postoperative adjuvant therapy than with observation, indicating an association between postoperative chemoradiotherapy and better outcomes among patients with early-stage cervical cancer and regional lymph node metastasis, consistent with published evidence (21,22). A critical limitation of this study is that the SEER database lacks data on performance status, comorbidities, and reasons for treatment decisions. Patients in the observation group who did not receive adjuvant therapy despite having high-risk features may represent a population with poor performance status, high tumor burden, or inability to tolerate therapy—factors that are independently associated with worse survival. Conversely, patients who received adjuvant therapy represent a selected group deemed fit enough for treatment. This confounding by indication biases any comparison favoring adjuvant therapy and represents a fundamental limitation of retrospective analyses using administrative databases, precluding causal inference about treatment efficacy. Therefore, based on existing evidence and the present findings, postoperative adjuvant treatment decisions for early-stage cervical cancer should continue to be guided by FIGO and Sedlis criteria, as recommended by current guidelines. Factors identified in this study, such as T stage, N stage, age, and multiple primary malignancies, warrant further investigation in prospective studies.

The retrospective design is susceptible to selection bias because postoperative treatment decisions in early-stage cervical cancer are closely related to recurrence risk. Notably, the observation group in our study was enriched with stage IA1 patients (approximately 31.02%), who have an excellent prognosis without adjuvant therapy per standard of care. Nonetheless, a sensitivity analysis excluding IA1 patients showed that the survival differences in stage I remained consistent, suggesting that IA1 enrichment alone does not fully explain the observed associations. However, residual confounding by indication remains a critical limitation that constrains interpretation and precludes causal inference. The SEER database lacks important covariates, including reproductive history, laboratory parameters, HPV infection status, and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression. Detailed information on radiotherapy dose, chemotherapy regimens and dosage, and lines of therapy is not available, nor are data on novel treatments such as immunotherapy or bispecific antibodies. Changes in staging systems over time may also have had a minor impact on the results. Furthermore, changes in surgical techniques, radiation technology, and chemotherapy regimens over the 16-year study period represent additional unmeasured confounders that may affect the interpretation of our results.

Conclusions

This study suggests that postoperative observation is associated with favorable outcomes for patients with stage I, lymph node-negative early-stage cervical cancer. In contrast, POCRT was associated with better survival among high-risk patients with stage II disease or regional lymph node metastasis (N1). Associations favoring postoperative adjuvant therapy were also observed among patients aged 60 years or older and those with multiple primary malignancies. These findings are consistent with the established prognostic significance of lymph node status and suggest that age and multiple primary malignancies may be potential effect modifiers requiring further investigation. As a hypothesis-generating analysis, this study underscores the need for prospective studies to refine adjuvant therapy decision-making in early-stage cervical cancer.
