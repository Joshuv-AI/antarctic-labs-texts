Prevalence of poor sleep quality among critical care unit nurses: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Neda Sanaie, Sogand Sarmadi, Akbar Zare-Kaseb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNurses often suffer from poor sleep due to shift work and stress, affecting their performance and patient safety. Sleep issues like insomnia and sleepiness are common, leading to burnout and cognitive impairment. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality (primary outcome) and, as secondary outcomes, the prevalence of sleepiness and insomnia severity among nurses in critical care units.MethodA comprehensive search was conducted across the following databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. Each database was searched from its inception to February 24, 2025. The keywords “Sleep Quality”, “Insomnia”, Sleepiness”, “Prevalence”, “Nurses”, “Critical care unit”, and their alternatives were searched using MeSH, EMTREE, free-text methods, and expert opinion. A pooled prevalence estimate with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated, and the random-effects model was used to represent the data. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies, and the studies were categorized as having a low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Analysis was performed using STATA version 17 statistical software.ResultsThis review included 25 studies with a total sample size of 3,710 nurses. According to the results of the risk of bias assessments, 24% of studies were rated as having a low risk of bias, and 76% were rated as having a moderate risk. The overall prevalence of poor sleep quality was 69.2% (95% CI: 62.4%–76.1%). Heterogeneity was significantly high (I² = 94.49%), and the Q Cochrane test for heterogeneity was significant (p-value < 0.001), indicating substantial variability between studies. Subgroup analyses by country, continent, risk of bias assessment results, year of publication, and mean age of participants contributed to a reduction in heterogeneity. The mean score for sleep quality was 8.14 (95% CI: 6.51–9.77). Additionally, the pooled prevalence of sleepiness and mild to severe insomnia was 64.5% (95% CI: 56.4%–72.6%) and 63.1% (95% CI: 36.4%–89.7%), respectively.ConclusionsGiven the high prevalence of poor sleep quality, sleepiness, and insomnia among critical care nurses, healthcare managers should implement practical measures such as evidence-based interventions, including behavioral, educational, and policy strategies, to enhance sleep outcomes among critical care nurses and ensure patient safety.Implications for clinical practiceOur study’s findings provide policymakers with a more comprehensive understanding of sleep disorders among critical care nurses.Protocol registrationPROSPERO registration number (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) CRD420251009448 corresponds to the registered protocol for this systematic review.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04094-6.Keywords: Sleep quality, Sleepiness, Insomnia, Critical care unit nurses

Introduction

To ensure the continuity of patient care, nurses must adhere to rotating shift patterns, which frequently disrupt their daily schedules [1]. The occupational nature of nursing increases the likelihood of insufficient and poor-quality sleep, as well as chronic sleep deprivation [2]. Adequate sleep is essential for hormonal regulation, energy restoration, memory consolidation, and enhanced learning [3]. An individual’s sleep quality is defined by their overall satisfaction with various aspects of their sleep experience over time [4]. The term “poor sleep quality” refers to subjectively inadequate levels of sleep satisfaction, characterized by symptoms such as frequent nocturnal awakenings, decreased sleep efficiency, and subsequent impairments in cognitive function during the day [5]. A study showed that 61% of nurses experienced poor sleep quality [6]. A separate study conducted in Iran revealed that 74.2% of nurses reported poor sleep quality during the COVID-19 pandemic [7]. The reported figures were 35.3% for China and 46.3% for India [8, 9].

Insufficient sleep negatively impacts nurses’ concentration and judgment, potentially resulting in mistakes and injuries. For instance, an association was observed between medication errors and nurses’ poor quality of sleep [2]. While all nurses report impaired sleep quality, this phenomenon is especially pronounced among critical care unit staff [10]. Several factors contribute to the compromised sleep quality of critical care nurses, including high workload, the complexity of patient care, performing intricate procedures, and the severity of patient conditions [10, 11]. A study showed that nurses working night or rotating shifts experience high rates of insomnia and significant negative consequences on physical, mental, and social well-being, highlighting the pervasive impact of shift work on their health. Thus, it is of high importance to assess nurses’ sleep quality continuously [10, 12].

According to the Job Demand-Resource (JD-R) model, high job demands result in increased strains and hinder recovery without adequate job resources, and empirical studies on nurses suggest that higher job demands and lower resources are associated with lower sleep quality, greater fatigue, and greater burnout [13, 14]. Additionally, circadian disruption frameworks explain how night and rotating shifts disrupt endogenous circadian rhythms as well as sleep-wake timing, resulting in physiological sleep loss, insomnia symptoms, and daytime sleepiness, a phenomenon recently demonstrated in nursing cohorts and chronotype studies [15, 16]. As a result of these models, plausible pathways have been identified from critical-care work conditions to impaired sleep, subsequent cognitive and emotional outcomes, and ultimately to the risk associated with patient safety [17, 18].

Findings from a nationwide sleep quality research team suggest that sleep quality is a more significant indicator of sleep than sleep duration, and that the level of restfulness achieved through sleep is crucial in assessing quality [19]. The assessment of sleep quality is inherently subjective, encompassing individual emotional and sensory perceptions [8]. The evaluation of sleep quality involves the utilization of multiple tools [20]. The most commonly used tool is the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Inventory (PSQI). The Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) both measure perceived severity of insomnia. There are many other subjective tools to assess sleep quality [21–23].

A diagnosis of insomnia is derived from a person’s description of sleep difficulties. Insomnia is characterized by extended sleep onset latency, repeated nocturnal awakenings, or sustained wakefulness during sleep, as well as frequent brief awakenings [24]. The prevalence of insomnia in the overall population of nurses was determined to be 65.4% [25].

Sleepiness is defined as a tendency to fall asleep easily. Sleep deprivation often results in the simultaneous experience of drowsiness and tiredness [26]. A study of nurses revealed that 45% exhibited high levels of sleepiness during at least one work shift. Also, it was shown that a progressive increase in sleepiness was observed among nurses across shifts; night shift nurses showed greater sleepiness at the end of their shifts compared to the beginning [27]. The effects of drowsiness on the quality of nursing care and patient safety remain undetermined. However, findings from studies emphasize the importance of coping strategies to maintain sufficient vigilance to ensure that the quality of care is facilitated [28].

A complex interplay of factors affects nurses’ sleep quality [29]. Contributing factors include occupational stress, workload intensity, excessive work hours, inadequate nurse-to-patient ratios, insufficient social support, night shift assignments, years of professional experience, and concerns regarding contamination and infection [30–32]. Additionally, environmental factors, including light, temperature, and noise, can affect sleep quality. As adequate sleep is crucial for health, sleep disorders present a range of negative consequences [33]. Insufficient sleep is associated with increased depression and irritability among the nursing profession [34]. A correlation exists between sleep deficiency and the prevalence of burnout among nurses [8]. Nurses suffering from poor sleep quality experience cognitive impairment, specifically manifesting as memory loss and difficulty concentrating [35]. Consequently, sleep disruption may negatively affect nursing staff performance [33]. A direct causal link is observed between impaired sleep and both medication errors and delayed response to patient needs in nurses [30].

Due to the crucial role of nurses’ sleep quality in patient care, as well as the implications and contributing factors involved, implementing educational and supportive strategies is essential to improve sleep quality. Prior research had documented high rates of poor sleep among nurses; therefore, a thorough understanding of their sleep patterns is crucial for developing effective solutions. Although a previous review had reported the prevalence of sleep problems among nurses, it often pooled heterogeneous nursing populations and diverse measurement tools, limiting the applicability of its results for high-risk subgroups [36]. To address this gap, the present study focuses specifically on critical-care nurses, applies instrument-based inclusion criteria for each outcome to improve measurement comparability, updates the search, and uses more extensive analyses to identify sources of heterogeneity. This approach enables us to produce more precise, context-specific prevalence estimates and provide more actionable guidance for clinicians, policymakers, and managers in critical care settings. Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality (the primary outcome) and, as secondary outcomes, the prevalence of sleepiness and insomnia severity among nurses in critical care units.

Methods

Our systematic review and meta-analysis were written in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines [37]. The protocol of this systematic review study has been registered in the PROSPERO system with the code: CRD420251009448.

Review question

The POLIS (Population, Outcome, Location, Indicator, and Study design) method was used to categorize studies to address the primary review question (Table 1). Our review question was as follows: “What is the prevalence of poor sleep quality among critical care unit nurses worldwide?” The primary objective of our systematic review was to assess the prevalence of poor sleep quality in critical care unit nurses.

Population

The inclusion criteria for this systematic review and meta-analysis included nurses working in critical care units (ICU, NICU, CCU, etc.) with no restrictions on experience. Studies that included nurses in critical care units along with nurses in other units and did not separate the measures were also excluded.

Outcome

The primary outcome of this review was poor sleep quality as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Secondary outcomes were insomnia measured by the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and daytime sleepiness measured by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). We considered only studies that reported prevalence or mean scores using one of these instruments (PSQI, ISI, or ESS). To ensure measurement comparability across included studies, this restriction was applied. We excluded studies that used other instruments or failed to report the required outcome statistics.

Study design

Observational studies, including cohort and cross-sectional studies, and secondary analyses of these studies were included. Mixed-method studies were also included. Interventional studies, case-control studies, case reports, case series, letters, editorials, and review studies were excluded.

Data sources and search strategy

Relevant studies were searched in six databases on February 24, 2025: Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. Each database was searched from the beginning of each database to February 24, 2025. No time restrictions were applied. The keywords “Sleep Quality”, “Insomnia”, “Sleepiness”, “Prevalence”, “Nurses”, “Critical care unit”, and their alternatives were searched using MeSH, EMTREE, free-text methods, and expert opinion. The full search syntax is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. We also searched the references of included articles and relevant reviews to identify other eligible publications. No language restrictions were applied to the included studies. To extract data from non-English-language published articles, Google Translate was used, and a foreign-language expert was consulted. For further assistance, we contacted the corresponding authors for detailed information in English. To ensure the comprehensiveness of the searches, Google Scholar was also searched.

Study selection

During study selection, we entered all studies obtained from a comprehensive database search into the Hub Meta. Duplicates were automatically removed using this software. Screening was performed in the first stage by reviewing the title and abstract by one of the authors. Then, two authors independently reviewed the full text of the articles. At this stage, any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third, more experienced researcher. If only the abstract were available, we made three attempts to obtain the full-text articles for evaluation. To avoid duplication, when two studies used data from the same source during the same time period, we included only the study with the larger sample size. The reason for the exclusion of each study in full-text reviews is fully explained in the supplementary appendix 2.

Data extraction

A standard data extraction form was developed in accordance with the guidance provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. Data extraction from three studies was piloted using a data extraction sheet to demonstrate the diversity of methodologies. Two authors independently extracted data at this stage. The items in the data extraction file were: Author’s name, year, country, age, ethnicity, conduct time, outcome, outcome measurement tool, risk factors, measurement tool`s cut-off, sample size, number of outcome positive participants, risk of bias, continent, study design, and follow-up duration. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third, more experienced researcher.

Risk of bias assessments

Two independent reviewers assessed the quality of the included studies. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies was used to evaluate the potential bias of the included studies [38]. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion. The JBI consists of nine items. Each item is scored as follows: yes = 1, no = 0, and unclear = 0. The overall score for each study is reported as a percentage. The classification of each study is determined by the level of risk of bias, with high risk of bias defined as 20–50% of cases with yes, moderate risk of bias defined as 50–80% of cases with a yes, and low risk of bias defined as 80–100% of cases with yes based on the JBI checklist scoring [39].

Data synthesis

Prevalence was calculated separately for each study that reported sample size and number of participants with the outcome of interest by dividing the number of outcome-positive individuals by the total sample size [40]. If a study did not provide the necessary information on the number of participants with the outcome of interest, we derived these figures from the reported measures or percentages. The exact binomial method was used to independently calculate standard errors for the proportion of poor sleep quality in each primary study if there was a study with an estimated prevalence below 10% or above 90% [41, 42]. Studies that did not report sufficient information on key elements, such as sample size or the number of participants with the desired outcome for any reason, were excluded due to poor data quality reporting.

The I2 statistic was used to examine the large heterogeneity among studies. These statistics provide quantitative information about the percentage change in estimates. The higher the I2 percentage reported, the more significant the heterogeneity between studies. Cochrane provides a basic framework for interpretation that was used in our meta-analysis. Heterogeneity between 0% and 40% may be insignificant, 30% to 60% may indicate moderate heterogeneity, 50% to 90% may indicate significant heterogeneity, and 75% to 100% indicates considerable heterogeneity [43]. In pooled analyses, we used a random-effects model on logit-transformed prevalence estimates. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and is reported alongside I² [44]. The pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality, along with its 95% confidence interval (CI), is presented as a forest plot. We conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity by analyzing predefined variables, including geographical location (country and continent), World Bank income classifications, publication years, and bias risk levels (categorized as low, moderate, and high in each domain). Based on logit-transformed prevalence estimates, each subgroup was analyzed using a random effects model, and differences between subgroups were tested [45, 46]. Due to the limited number of studies for secondary outcomes, subgroup analyses were only planned for the primary outcome. Data analysis was performed using STATA version 17 software.

Publication bias

Different methods were used to examine the risk of publication bias. One of these methods was the use of the Begg and Egger tests [47, 48]. Also, the logit scale of prevalence was used to calculate these measures [40]. Given the sufficient number of studies, funnel plots were also used to assess the risk of publication bias. For the Begg and Egger tests, publication bias was considered if at least one of the tests reached a significance level. The significance level of the test was set at 0.05 (p-value < 0.05) [47, 48].

Sensitivity analysis

This systematic review and meta-analysis used different approaches to sensitivity analysis. First, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of excluding any study on the overall estimates. Also, post-hoc sensitivity analysis approaches were assessed [46, 49, 50]. To ensure robustness of the findings, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted. Specifically, small-sample studies (n < 100) and studies from low-income countries were examined separately. This decision was theoretically based on evidence suggesting that small studies may yield unstable prevalence estimates [51] and that structural and workload differences in low-income healthcare systems may influence sleep-related outcomes among nurses.

Results

Our literature search yielded 15,102 articles, of which 25 [52–76] met the inclusion criteria for our overall systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of poor sleep quality, mild-to-severe insomnia, and sleepiness (Fig. 1). Most excluded studies did not use the desired questionnaire to evaluate our study outcome, or did not report prevalence data specifically for critical care unit staff.

PRISMA flowchart

A total of 25 unique studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Seventeen studies reported the prevalence of poor sleep quality [52–60, 64, 66, 67, 72–76]; however, 13 studies reported the mean score of sleep quality as measured by PSQI [52, 56, 58, 60, 61, 66–72, 75]. Four studies were eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis of the prevalence of sleepiness [56, 64, 72, 76], and three studies were included in the analysis of the prevalence of insomnia severity among critical care unit nurses [62, 63, 65]. Several studies contributed to multiple outcomes. Eight studies reported both poor sleep prevalence and mean sleep quality scores; all four sleepiness studies are included in the poor sleep prevalence analysis, and two of them additionally reported mean sleep quality scores. The three insomnia studies were distinct from the other outcome groups. Therefore, although the sum of outcome-specific counts (N = 37) exceeds the number of included studies, there are only 25 unique studies overall.

Overall, 25 unique studies were included, with a total of 3710 nurses. Sample sizes ranged from 40 to 291 nurses. Most studies were cross-sectional in nature (24 studies [96%]); the minority were prospective cohort studies (1 study [4%]). Eighteen studies (72%) used the PSQI questionnaire only. Four studies (16%) utilized both the PSQI and ESS questionnaires; however, three remaining studies (12%) used the ISI questionnaire. Six [54, 57, 59, 61, 64, 73] (24%) studies were categorized as having low risk of bias; however, 19 (76%) were classified as moderate quality. The mean JBI score was 6.68 (median, 6; range, 5–9), indicating that the overall quality of articles was moderate (Tables 2 and 3).

Sleep quality

The pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality among critical care nurses, as measured by the PSQI, was 69.2% (95% CI: 62.4–76.1). Heterogeneity among the included studies, as measured by the I2 statistic, was 94.49%. Also, the Q Cochrane heterogeneity test yielded a p-value < 0.001, indicating significant heterogeneity among studies (Table 4; Fig. 2).

To examine the sources of heterogeneity among studies, subgroup analyses were conducted by country, continent, World Bank income classification, risk of bias assessment results, year of publication, and mean age of participants. The highest prevalence of poor sleep quality was reported in Ethiopia 87.3% (95% CI: 76.5–94.4). However, the lowest prevalence was observed in Portugal, at 24.1% (95% CI: 15.1–35). Results showed a significant difference in the prevalence of poor sleep quality across countries (p < 0.001). There was a significant decrease in heterogeneity among studies conducted in Iran (85.93%). Stratifying studies regarding continent showed a reduction in I2 statistics among studies conducted in Asia (91.53%). The lowest prevalence of poor sleep quality was observed among studies conducted in South America 58.7% (95% CI: 52.2–65.2). The pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality was 73.0% (95% CI: 65.4–80.5) across countries classified as upper-middle-income. This figure was also 60.4% (95% CI: 45.2–75.6) in high-income countries. The prevalence of poor sleep quality differed significantly across countries with varying income classifications (p < 0.001). Heterogeneity among studies with an upper-middle-income classification was 93.37%. Subgroup analysis also showed that higher-quality studies demonstrated a higher prevalence of poor sleep quality. Also, I2 statistics were 77.44% among studies with low risk of bias. Regarding the year of publication, a 2025 study found a high prevalence of poor sleep quality (87.0%). I2 statistics for studies conducted in 2023 were 84.4%, indicating a significant reduction in heterogeneity. Sub-group classification based on the mean age of participants revealed that studies with a mean age greater than 35 years had a pooled prevalence of 71.7% (95% CI: 68.2–75.3). However, the difference in pooled prevalence between groups was not significant (p = 0.62). I2 statistics were 0.00% for studies with participants older than 35 years old (p = 0.77) (Table 5). Subgroup analysis, examining country, continent, risk of bias assessment results, year of publication, and mean age of participants, revealed a significant reduction in I2 Statistics. Despite applying stratified analyses, substantial heterogeneity (I² >90%) remained in most models (Table 5). This may be attributed to conceptual and methodological variability across studies, including differences in cut-off thresholds, pandemic-related stressors, and variations in healthcare systems and work environments across regions.

Totally, four studies evaluated the prevalence of sleepiness. The meta-analysis showed a pooled prevalence of sleepiness of 64.5% (95% CI: 56.4–72.6). I2 statistics were 83.6% among included studies. Insomnia

Three studies evaluated the level of insomnia among critical care nurses. The pooled prevalence of mild to severe insomnia was 63.1% (95% CI: 36.4–89.7). The I2 measure for heterogeneity among included studies was 98.37%. The Q Cochrane for these included studies yielded a significant result (p < 0.001). Begg and Egger’s test didn’t reach significant results (p = 0.30 and 0.54) (Supplementary appendix 4).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that more than half of nurses working in critical care units experience poor sleep quality, with a substantial proportion also reporting insomnia and excessive sleepiness. This high prevalence underscores the widespread nature of sleep disturbances in this workforce and highlights the potential implications for nurses’ health and patient care. The term “sleep quality” currently lacks a standardized definition and is commonly evaluated using either expensive polysomnographic techniques in sleep laboratories or highly subjective sleep questionnaires [77]. Our systematic review and meta-analysis to assess sleep quality in critical care nurses included only studies that used the PSQI instrument. The PSQI is a self-reported questionnaire that evaluates sleep quality and disturbances over one month [78, 79]. Despite their limitations as screening measures for polysomnographic sleep disorders, these instruments remain the most prevalent methods for evaluating sleep quality across different societies because of certain benefits [80]. A similar review study in 2019 found that the overall prevalence of poor sleep quality among nurses was 61%, and it was 59% among intensive care unit (ICU) nurses [36]. Our pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality among critical care nurses is markedly higher than recent general population estimates for insomnia and sleep disorders and higher than many pooled estimates for healthcare workers, while aligning with prior syntheses specific to critical care nurses that reported prevalences in the range of 63–77% [81–83]. Moreover, the research showed that nurses working in ICUs exhibited a higher prevalence of poor sleep quality compared to their counterparts in other hospital units. Consistent with our findings, the cited study revealed a significant prevalence of compromised sleep quality. Our study, when compared with previous research, shows a 10% increase in the prevalence of poor-quality sleep over the past five years. Another study in China found that the mean sleep quality score of healthcare workers, as measured with the PSQI, was 9.82 [84]. Our research determined this value to be 8.14. Both our study and the study mentioned above revealed that healthcare workers, particularly nurses, exhibited sleep disturbances, as indicated by mean sleep quality scores.

A Nigerian study established a 42% prevalence of poor sleep quality amongst the general population, while the present study showed a substantially elevated rate of 87.3% across Africa [85]. A recent study conducted on older adults living in the community revealed an 18% prevalence of low sleep quality [86]. The figure pertaining to critical care nurses was 69.2%. The prevalence of sleep disorders among nursing students was documented as 27% [87]. Research showed that sleep disorders are disproportionately prevalent among nurses, with critical care nurses exhibiting the highest prevalence. Furthermore, a comprehensive scoping review by Matricciani et al. (2023) mapped the existing literature on nurses’ sleep, identifying 1040 studies worldwide. Most studies were focused on acute care nurses, exploring sleep in relation to workforce issues, lifestyle behaviors, and overall health. This review highlighted a growing interest in nurses’ sleep and emphasized the need for methodological consistency, standardized definitions, and objective sleep measures. Incorporating insights from this scoping review strengthens the context of our findings, underscoring that sleep disturbances are a widespread issue in nursing practice and that further research is required to develop evidence-based interventions [88].

Poor sleep quality is associated with a wide range of diseases. Given the high prevalence of poor sleep quality among nurses, addressing this issue is more necessary than ever before. Previous studies have shown that poor sleep quality is linked to decreased QoL, especially when some or severe issues with anxiety or depression are present [89]. Another study found that poor sleep quality was linked to a higher risk of new-onset hypertension in young and middle-aged populations. Since nurses often fall within this group, ongoing assessment of their health is essential [90].

Several factors can be associated with the high prevalence of sleep disorders in nurses, with burnout, high workload, stress, and anxiety being the main ones [91, 92]. Our findings align with the Demand-Control-Support (DCS) model, which posits that high job demands coupled with low control and insufficient social support elevate stress levels, potentially leading to poor sleep quality. In the context of critical care nursing, the high workload and emotional demands inherent in the role may contribute to increased stress and compromised sleep. Additionally, the DCS model suggests that enhancing job control and social support can mitigate stress and improve well-being. Implementing strategies that increase nurses’ autonomy and provide adequate support could, therefore, be beneficial in addressing sleep disturbances [13, 14].

Several recent reviews have found that sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among nurses and are associated with organizational and psychosocial factors such as burnout, occupational stress, and work–family conflict. An extensive scoping review showed that the literature is predominantly observational and focused on sleep quality. It emphasized the need to investigate organizational factors such as shift type and scheduling patterns [88]. Meta-analyses have reported significant associations between burnout and sleep problems, suggesting that burnout may be an essential mechanism in the development or exacerbation of poor sleep [17, 93, 94]. An analytical study had also indicated that sleep disturbances can mediate the pathway from work–family conflict to burnout, implying that interventions targeting sleep could have protective effects against occupational exhaustion [95]. Additionally, systematic reviews of sleep interventions and reviews focused on shift workers indicate that combined behavioral, educational, and organizational strategies (shift-schedule modification, controlled napping, and sleep-hygiene education) can improve sleep outcomes [96, 97]. A systematic review by Zhang et al. (2023) synthesized experimental and quasi-experimental studies addressing sleep interventions for nurses. This review included 38 studies, covering interventions such as aromatherapy, dietary supplements, cognitive behavioral therapy, light therapy, mind-body therapy, sleep education, exercise, napping, shift schedule modification, and multicomponent approaches. Notably, 36 of the 38 studies reported positive effects on at least one sleep outcome, demonstrating moderate effectiveness of these interventions. However, due to variations in study designs, outcome measures, and quality, definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of specific interventions remain limited [96]. These findings support the potential benefits of targeted behavioral, educational, and organizational interventions to mitigate poor sleep quality among critical care nurses, aligning with the recommendations of our study.

Another important cause, especially for night shift nurses, could be postponing bedtime. A study of students found an independent association between bedtime procrastination and the prevalence and severity of self-reported poor sleep quality [98]. Given the high prevalence of poor sleep quality, insomnia, and sleepiness among critical care nurses, behavioral, psychological, and educational interventions are needed to reduce these consequences. Changes in policies, a reduction in workload, and positive adjustments to work routines can also be effective. Recent evidence highlights interventions and strategies designed to improve sleep among nurses.

Implications for policy and practice

Due to the high prevalence of sleep disorders among critical care nurses, our findings have important implications for mental health policy and organizational intervention design. It is recommended that healthcare organizations incorporate sleep health assessments into routine occupational health programs and implement evidence-based interventions such as flexible scheduling, workload management, sleep education, and behavioral strategies. It may be possible to mitigate the adverse effects of sleep deprivation on nurses by implementing policies that support staff well-being, including structured rest periods, mental health support, and counseling services. Additionally, international collaborations and standardized monitoring systems could facilitate the development of targeted interventions across a broad range of healthcare settings, enabling benchmarking, policy harmonization, and other benefits.

Strengths and limitations

A thorough review of the literature concerning sleep disturbance in critical care nurses is presented in this study. Employing a rigorous methodological approach, we provide accurate and current estimates of the prevalence of self-reported insomnia, sleepiness, and poor sleep quality. Another positive aspect was the inclusion of studies published in all languages. The limitations of this review include the omission of grey literature and non-hospital-based samples, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies. Evaluating sleep quality is inherently multidimensional, ideally incorporating clinical assessments along with self-report questionnaires and sleep diaries; reliance on subjective self-reported instruments may introduce measurement bias. Furthermore, the lack of longitudinal studies limits the ability to infer causality. In addition, only subgroup analyses were performed for prevalence of poor sleep quality due to the limited number of studies available for sleepiness and insomnia, which may limit their generalizability.

Conclusions

We found that critical care nurses are highly susceptible to poor sleep quality, insomnia, and sleepiness. Analyzing poor sleep quality by subgroup based on country, continent, risk of bias assessment, year of publication, and mean age helped explain the observed heterogeneity. The objective of future studies should be to implement practical, evidence-based strategies to mitigate sleep disturbances in critical care nurses, such as shift scheduling optimization, sleep hygiene education, and organizational support. In addition, international collaborations should be established to standardize occupational health surveillance systems for monitoring and addressing these issues around the world.
