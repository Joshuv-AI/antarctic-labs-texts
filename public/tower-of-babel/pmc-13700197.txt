Impacts of short-term queen banking on virus infection and immune-oxidative stress responses in honey bee queens
Authors: Sagar Bhandari, Somayeh Mehrparvar, Michael Simone-Finstrom, Esmaeil Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQueen banking is a common management practice primarily in commercial beekeeping or queen-producing operations, used to either temporarily store surplus mated queens during beekeeping season or to ensure their availability in the early spring. However, the effects of this practice on viral infection dynamics and queen physiology (immune and stress response) are not well understood. To address this knowledge gap, we examined the dynamics of viral infection, oxidative stress, and immune responses in virgin and mated queens during a three-week spring banking period. We performed qRT-PCR to quantify four common honey bee viruses (DWV-A & B, BQCV, and LSVs), as well as three immune genes (dicer-like, hymenoptaecin, defensin-1) and three stress-related genes (CuZnSOD, catalase, and vitellogenin).ResultsViral loads in queens and their attendant workers stayed mostly stable, indicating that short-term banking is not a major route of virus proliferation. However, gene expression analysis revealed that immune and stress responses varied significantly between weeks, with a general decline by the third week—possibly due to reduced immunocompetence. Unlike mated queens, virgin queens in bank colonies exhibited higher CuZnSOD and catalase expression, lower vitellogenin expression, and higher mortality, suggesting greater sensitivity to banking, likely due to differences in pheromone profiles and the subsequent feeding and care from attendants.ConclusionsStress from confinement and social crowding, rather than viral activity, primarily influences queen physiological responses during banking. These responses also appear to depend on the reproductive status of the queen, which may modulate interactions between viral infection and host gene expression. These findings offer new insights into how short-term queen banking impacts viral infection and physiological responses, with potential implications for queen quality and post-storage performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05760-w.

Background

High annual honey bee (Apis mellifera) colony losses in the northern hemisphere have raised concerns regarding the need to increase colony numbers to meet pollination service demands [1, 2]. In response, beekeepers increase colony numbers by splitting existing colonies, producing package honey bees and young queens each spring [3, 4]. This practice is particularly prominent in countries such as the United States, Canada, and Australia, where the beekeeping industry has developed large-scale package bee and queen production systems [5]. Within this system, queen production has become a specialized and critically important component, requiring large-scale production to meet spring demand.

In colder climates like Canada, the demand can be met by either importing queens or storing them over winter to make them available in early spring [6–8]. Therefore, successful queen banking requires maintaining a sufficient population of young workers to provide nutrition, removing laying queens, ensuring adequate sucrose reserves in colonies, and providing insulation to prevent worker clustering [6]. Long-term queen storage has been investigated during both winter and summer months [9, 10]. Stored queens can regain weight and resume egg laying soon after being introduced into a productive colony [7]. However, maintaining queens alive, healthy, and robust throughout storage remains challenging, and post-storage performance can vary substantially depending on the banking period and stock density [7, 8, 11]. While some studies reported no impact on colony performance [8, 12], others have reported reduced oviposition in the initial days [7, 11], which might ultimately delay and reduce overall colony productivity. Such a delay in initial oviposition might be due to the inactivation of the ovary or the termination of oviposition during the banking period [13]. As a result, long-term banking may compromise the physiological quality of queens [14], with issues in reproductive parameters, including reduced ovary mass, low sperm viability, and semen retention in the oviduct [8, 15, 16]. In addition to the physiological and reproductive alterations, stored queens may sustain common physical injuries to the arolium, antennae, and wings due to continuous contact with wire gauze and aggression from workers [17, 18].

In warmer regions, the demand for young queens can be met by producing large numbers of queens to maintain a stable supply in early spring [19, 20]. However, in many cases, the production of large numbers of queens during beekeeping season requires producers to store young, mated queens for a short period—ranging from a few days to weeks—until customer orders are received [21]. Banking virgin queens is also a common practice in breeding operations, where virgin queens are banked for 1–3 weeks prior to artificial insemination [22]. Such practice of banking virgin queens has been reported to be the most cost-effective queen maintenance system compared to the queen cage and nucleus system for implementing artificial insemination [23].

Beekeepers have been successful in storing surplus mated or virgin queens alive for short periods, but queen banking may pose a risk to queen health, as banking young queens might increase their exposure to viral pathogens. Infected workers can transmit viruses horizontally via trophallaxis when they feed caged queens [24, 25]. In addition, queens held in banks experience a reduced social environment, leading to less food and care from workers and potentially exposing them to confinement-related stressors [26, 27]. Therefore, we hypothesize that these stressors weaken immunity and make queens susceptible to opportunistic pathogens, including viruses, which could explain their high mortality during storage.

Here, we evaluated the impact of banking on both virgin and mated queens for deformed wing virus type A and B (DWV-A and DWV-B), black queen cell virus (BQCV), and Lake Sinai viruses (LSVs), as these viruses were highly prevalent in queens in our previous experiment [28] and are also economically important for queen producers. To better understand the physiological perturbations associated with queen banking, the expression levels of six genes were evaluated in virgin and mated queens, given their established roles in oxidative stress, immunity, and nutrition. For instance, dicer-like, hymenoptaecin, and defensin-1 represent antiviral innate immunity (reviewed in Brutscher & Flenniken [29]), CuZnSOD and catalase have been reported to have an oxidative stress response [27, 30], and vitellogenin is well established as an antioxidant and also represents nutritional status in honey bees [31–33]. Overall, this study aimed to determine whether short-term banking during beekeeping season increases the risk of viral infection in queens, and whether this could be at least in part due to differential stress. We were also able to address two specific questions: (1) What are the temporal patterns of viral loads and immune and stress-related gene expression in virgin and mated queens while caged; and (2) Does banking in groups or holding queens in individual colonies alter these patterns?

Methods

Experimental overview

This experiment was conducted in collaboration with a commercial queen producer in Mississippi from April to May 2024 using the Minnesota Hygienic honey bee stock [34]. Viral infection levels and immune responses were compared between mass-stored (banked) mated and virgin queens and those maintained individually in colonies during a three-week banking period. To assess the effects of queen banking independently of mating-related factors, including potential venereal virus transmission, virgin queens were included alongside mated queens. An overview of the experimental design and sample collection is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Banking of mated queens

Two batches of queens were produced following the beekeeper’s standard commercial practices. Briefly, it involves grafting young larvae (12–24 h old) from multiple breeder queens, rearing queens in cell builder colonies, harvesting queen cells (10–11 days after grafting), and introducing newly emerged queens in mating nucleus colonies [35]. In the first batch, 73 queens were reared and allowed to mate freely in mating colonies, all in the same apiary. Three weeks later, following confirmation of successful mating and the onset of oviposition, queens were prepared for the experiment. Ten queens were collected individually in microcentrifuge tubes and immediately stored in the freezer as baseline samples. The remaining queens were individually caged (JZs-BZs queen cages, Mann Lake Ltd.), without allowing workers to enter, but they could interact with and feed queens through holes. Thirty-one caged queens were mounted in two frame holders and introduced into a pre-established bank colony, while the remaining queens (n = 32) were caged individually in their own colonies.

Banking of virgin queens

In the second batch, 106 queens were produced following the same procedures, originating from the same breeder colonies described above. Two days prior to emergence and immediately after emergence, 10 queen pupae and 10 newly emerged queens were randomly sampled, individually in microcentrifuge tubes, and stored in a freezer as baseline samples. The remaining 86 virgin queens were caged individually and assigned to one of the following treatment groups: 42 caged queens were introduced into a pre-established bank colony, whereas the remaining 44 queens were held individually in cages in each colony that had been prepared in advance.

Experimental colony setup

The two banked colonies were established as double-deep Langstroth queen-right colonies. The upper chamber of each bank colony, where the experimental queens were stored, was supplied with frames of capped brood along with honey and pollen to provide a population of nurse bees. The resident queen was confined to the lower box, where she continued oviposition to produce young workers, and was separated by a double-screen mesh from the upper box. Following the introduction of the experimental queens, no additional brood or bee frames were added to the bank colonies. One bank colony was assigned to virgin queens and the other to mated queens. For queens held individually, individual colonies were established in standard 10-frame Langstroth hives, standardized to contain comparable numbers of adult workers, brood, and food resources. These colonies were rendered queen-less prior to the introduction of experimental queens. As a common practice, all the colonies, including breeder colonies, nurse colonies, and mating colonies, were treated against Varroa mites using oxalic acid at the end of January before the start of beekeeping activities. In addition, to improve the hygienic behavior of Minnesota hygienic stock, breeding colonies are selected based on a freeze-killed brood assay using liquid nitrogen every spring.

Sample collection

Following the experimental setup, 10 live queens were sampled randomly from each of the four treatment groupsࣧvirgin queens from the bank colony, virgin queens from individual colonies, mated queens from the bank colony, and mated queens from individual coloniesࣧat weekly intervals over three consecutive weeks. Queens were sampled individually in microcentrifuge tubes. In addition, dead queens were recorded weekly in each treatment group and sampled when found (Table S1). Pools of ~ 150 workers were sampled from brood frames of the corresponding colonies at each sampling event to assess virus load and Varroa infestation [36]. During the third week, in addition to routine sampling, extra queens were sampled if available, after which the experiment was terminated.

Sample preparation and molecular assays

Mite infestation in ~ 150 workers per sample was estimated using the alcohol wash method (70% ethanol) with a Varroa EasyCheck kit. Prior to alcohol washing, 50 workers from each sample were separated randomly and visually checked for Varroa mites. These 50 workers were pooled to create a single sample for each colony for RNA extraction. Individual queens were also processed to extract RNA.

To initiate RNA extraction, 3 metal beads (2.4 mm, PerkinElmer) were added to each tube, and samples were homogenized using a Bead Ruptor Elite (OMNI International Inc.). Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the MagMAX mirVanna Total RNA Isolation Kit in a KingFisher Magnetic extractor (KingFisher Duo Prime, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with a minor adaptation. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured using a NanoDrop One Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The total RNA concentration was then adjusted to 50 ng/µL with the elution buffer provided in the extraction kit. Individual RNA samples were extracted and transferred into 96-well plates, then stored at -80 ℃ for further use. A two-step real time-qPCR assay was performed for the absolute quantification of 4 honey bee viruses (DWV-A, DWV-B, BQCV, and LSVs) and the relative quantification of 3 immune (dicer-like, hymenoptaecin, and defensin-1) and 3 oxidative stress-related genes (CuZnSOD, catalase, and vitellogenin). To do so, the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse-Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used to synthesize cDNA. The reaction mixture was prepared by adding 10 µL of RNA template (500 ng) to 10 µL of the supplied cDNA master mix. The reaction was incubated as per the manufacturer’s instructions: 10 min at 25 °C, 120 min at 37 °C, and 5 min at 85 °C. The resulting cDNA solution was then diluted 10-fold in nuclease-free water to serve as a template in subsequent qPCR.

The qPCR was conducted in duplicate using 384-well plates on a QuantStudio 6™ Pro (Applied Biosystems) apparatus. The reactions were performed using previously validated primers (Table S3) and the PowerUp SYBR Green master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Baltics UAB, Vilnius, Lithuania), in a total volume of 12 µL with a final primer concentration of 0.4 µM. RPS-5 and GAPDH were used as internal reference genes for confirming the integrity of sample processing and functionality of the assay because of their stable expression across honey bee tissues and experimental conditions [37]. On each plate, a ten-fold serial dilution of synthesized PCR products served as a positive control, RNase-free water as a template for a No Target Control (NTC), and a No Reverse Transcriptase (NRT) control as an additional negative control [38]. The thermal cycling conditions were initialized by hold stage for 2 min at 50 °C and 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles consisting of a denaturation stage at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min, and a melt curve stage (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and dissociation at 95 °C for 15 s). Fluorescence measurements were taken at the end of each cycle, and samples were considered positive for a virus if the quantification cycle (Cq) value was recorded as 32 or lower. This cutoff was determined based on the standard curves, as Cq values beyond 32 were outside the validated linear quantitative range. However, for the quantification of immune genes, all the values of Cq were considered. For analytical specificity, melt curves were analyzed to detect the formation of any primer dimers, and non-specific amplicons were discarded as described by Bustin et al. [38]. Viral load in each sample was quantified using absolute quantification based on standard curves obtained by serial dilutions of a known amount of viral amplification, as described before [39]. For relative quantification of immune genes, normalized expression values relative to the reference genes were computed using the 2−ΔCq method [40]. Briefly, the geometric mean was computed using Cq values of two reference genes (RPS-5, GAPDH), and the Cq value of the target gene was subtracted from the geometric mean to obtain ΔCq, and subsequently, 2−ΔCq was computed.

Data handling and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis and visualization were performed using R version 4.4.1 [41] in RStudio version 2024.04.2 + 764 [42]. Data handling and organization were performed using the packages: readxl [43] and tidyverse [44]. Model fitting and pairwise comparison for viral load and gene expression were performed using the packages: brms [45], bayestestR [46], glmmTMB [47], emmeans [48], and contrast [49]. Correlation analysis was performed using Hmisc [50] and reshape2 [51] packages. Data visualization and figure assembly were performed using tidyverse [44], ggsignif [52], cowplot [53], patchwork [54], and ggh4x [55] packages.

Before performing statistical analysis, the viral load and gene expression datasets were divided into four subsets by sample types. The first subset includes workers sampled during the banking period from both the bank and individual colonies. The second subset consists of pupae and newly emerged queens. The third subset includes virgin queens, while the fourth contains mated queens from both bank and individual colonies. Virgin and mated queens could not be directly compared statistically because of age and physiological differences related to mating and reproductive status. The original viral load and gene expression scales were preserved for analysis. To improve visualization, viral loads were log-transformed to log10(virus copies + 1)/ng RNA, and gene expression values were displayed on a log10-scaled y-axis. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed data normality and outliers. Because viral loads were not normally distributed and contained many zeros, a Bayesian model was applied, using a hurdle lognormal family. An identity link was used for non-zero viral loads, while a logit link (logistic regression) estimated the probability of zero viral loads. Models for the first, third, and fourth subsets included the interaction between colony type and collection week as fixed effects, while the second model used sample type as the fixed effect. Model estimation employed Hamiltonian Monte Carlo for efficient posterior sampling. Convergence was confirmed by ensuring all R-hat statistics were below 1.01.

Gene expression was analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model with a Gamma distribution and a log link function. Fixed effects for different subsets were similar to the virus model; however, gene expression was not computed for the first subset (workers). Pairwise contrasts compared viral loads and gene expression across different sample types within each subset. Among queens, comparisons focused on pupae and newly emerged queens, queens between individual and bank colonies, and queens within the bank and individual colonies each week. For each comparison, the median or mean ratio, 95% credible interval, and p-value were reported.

Results

Temporal variation of viral load in workers during the banking period

Virus dynamics (DWV-A, DWV-B, BQCV, and LSVs) were assessed in 71 pools of worker samples, out of which 6 pools were collected from the bank colonies, while 65 pools were collected from the individual colonies (Fig. 2). Pairwise comparisons of viral loads were made within the bank and individual colonies among each collection week. Direct comparisons of viral loads between workers from the bank and individual colonies were not made due to the large disparity in sample sizes. No significant differences were observed in any comparisons for all the viruses over the three weeks. The median DWV-A load was below 2 log10 (DWV-A copies + 1)/ng RNA in both colony types, while the DWV-B load exceeded 2 log10 (DWV-B copies + 1)/ng RNA during the banking period. Additionally, BQCV and LSVs showed median viral loads above 4 log10 (virus copies + 1)/ng RNA in workers from both the bank and individual colonies, without any significant differences. Varroa infestation was also assessed in workers; however, mites were detected in only 5 of 71 samples, all with infestation levels below 1%, and therefore were not analyzed statistically (Fig. S1).

For instance, the average log-transformed viral loads for BQCV and DWV-B were 4.54 and 3.35 in week 2, and 4.99 and 2.71 in week 3, respectively.

Changes in immune gene expression

Newly emerged queens showed significantly lower expression of dicer-like than pupae (mean ratio = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.39–0.60, p < 0.001); however, expression was significantly higher in newly emerged queens than in pupae for hymenoptaecin (mean ratio = 6.76, 95% CI: 3.53–12.95, p < 0.001) and defensin-1 (mean ratio = 9.48, 95% CI: 5.32–16.89, p < 0.001). In bank colony, expression of dicer-like was significantly higher in the first week than in the second (mean ratio = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.13–2.39, p < 0.001) and third weeks (mean ratio = 3.78, 95% CI: 2.31–6.20, p < 0.001). Expression of hymenoptaecin did not vary significantly in banked virgin queens, whereas defensin-1 was significantly lower in the first week than in the second (mean ratio = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.15–0.74, p < 0.001). Virgin queens confined in individual colonies showed similar expression patterns for dicer-like and defensin-1, with a decreasing trend in the following weeks compared to the first week. For example, defensin-1 was highly expressed in the first week compared to the second (mean ratio = 3.17, 95% CI: 1.44–6.99, p < 0.001) and third weeks (mean ratio = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.45–5.48, p < 0.001). Expression of hymenoptaecin decreased from the first to the second week (mean ratio = 7.73, 95% CI: 2.03–29.44, p < 0.001) and then increased in the third week compared to the second (mean ratio = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.09–0.89, p = 0.02). Compared with queens maintained in individual colonies, the expression of hymenoptaecin (week 2: p < 0.001; week 3: p = 0.004) and defensin-1 (week 2: p < 0.001; week 3: p < 0.001) was significantly higher in bank colonies.

Changes in stress-related gene expression

Both catalase (mean ratio = 1.27, 95% CI: 1-1.60, p = 0.049) and vitellogenin (mean ratio = 448.17, 95% CI: 210.37-954.77, p < 0.001) exhibited significantly higher expression in newly emerged queens than in pupae. In bank colonies, virgin queens exhibited similar expression patterns for all three stress-related genes, CuZnSOD, catalase, and vitellogenin, with the highest expression in the first week, followed by a progressive decline over the banking period. For example, catalase showed higher expression in the first week than the second (mean ratio = 4.12, 95% CI: 2.45–6.92, p < 0.001) and the third (mean ratio = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.98–7.82, p < 0.001) weeks. Similar to the pattern observed in bank colonies, the expression of all stress-related genes in queens maintained in individual colonies decreased progressively over the assessment period. Significant differences were observed between queens from the bank and individual colonies for all stress-related genes during at least one sampling week (Table 1B). For instance, bank colonies showed higher expression of CuZnSOD in the first (mean ratio = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.24–2.69, p < 0.001) and third weeks (mean ratio = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.15–2.93, p = 0.004) than individual colonies. Additionally, catalase showed higher expression in bank colonies than in individual colonies for all three weeks. In contrast, queens from bank colonies showed lower vitellogenin expression than those from individual colonies in the second week (mean ratio = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39–0.96, p = 0.029).

Temporal variation of viral load in mated queens during the banking period

Changes in immune gene expression

In the bank colony, the expression of both dicer-like (mean ratio = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.47–1.03, p = 0.03) and defensin-1 (mean ratio = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.00-0.16, p < 0.001) increased from the first to the second week. By the third week, dicer-like expression had declined to levels below those observed in both the first (p < 0.001) and second (p < 0.001) weeks, whereas defensin-1 expression decreased relative to the second week (p < 0.001). Hymenoptaecin was significantly less expressed in the second week than in the third (mean ratio = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–0.90, p = 0.033). In individual colonies, dicer-like showed significantly higher expression in the first (mean ratio = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.29–2.84, p < 0.001) and second weeks (mean ratio = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.04–2.30, p = 0.01) than in the third week. Similarly, defensin-1 was significantly decreased in the third week compared to the first (mean ratio = 6.57, 95% CI: 0.98–44.01, p = 0.04). When comparing expression levels in mated queens between the bank and individual colonies, significant differences were observed for all immune-related genes during at least one sampling event (Table 1B). Unlike in virgin queens, the patterns were not consistent in mated queens. For instance, mated queens from bank colonies exhibited higher expression of dicer-like in the second week (mean ratio = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.19–2.43, p = 0.001); however, the expression of hymenoptaecin was significantly lower in bank colonies in the first (mean ratio = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.021–0.587, p = 0.003) and second weeks (mean ratio = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.005–0.163, p < 0.001). Similarly, the expression level of defensin-1 in bank colonies was significantly lower in the first week (mean ratio = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.51, p = 0.002) and higher in the second week than in individual colonies (mean ratio = 11.27, 95% CI: 2.0-63.33, p = 0.002).

Changes in stress-related gene expression

CuZnSOD, catalase, and vitellogenin showed similar expression patterns in bank colonies, with expression being statistically similar during the first two weeks and significantly lower in the third week. For instance, CuZnSOD exhibited significantly lower expression in the third week compared to the first (mean ratio = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.8–3.29, p < 0.001) and the second (mean ratio = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.63–2.97, p < 0.001) weeks. Mated queens from individual colonies also exhibited similar expression patterns for all the stress-related genes, with expression between the first two weeks statistically similar and significantly lower in the third week. Unlike in virgin queens, the expression of CuZnSOD and catalase in mated queens did not vary between the bank and individual colonies. However, the expression level of vitellogenin was higher in bank colonies than in individual colonies in the second week (mean ratio = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.0-3.45, p = 0.048), though in the third week, vitellogenin levels decreased to similar levels in both colony types.

Correlation analysis between viral loads and gene expression

Correlation analysis of gene expression and viral loads (DWV-B & BQCV) was performed separately in virgin and mated queens from both the bank and individual colonies (Fig. S2). We did not observe any significant correlations between viral loads and gene expression in virgin queens from the bank colonies, while we found a significant positive correlation in those from the individual colonies. However, such differences in virus-gene correlation patterns between the bank and individual colonies were less pronounced in mated queens. DWV-B consistently exhibited significant positive correlations with antimicrobial peptides (hymenoptaecin and defensin-1) in virgin queens from the individual colonies, and in mated queens. However, it showed significant negative correlations with the rest of the genes in mated queens.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that short-term queen banking during spring queen production influences the physiological status of honey bee queens by altering the expression of immune- and oxidative stress-related genes. Because queens from both the bank and individual colonies were confined in cages, both groups might have been exposed to confinement or restraint-related stress. In addition to this stress, queens from the bank colonies might have been exposed to stress from overcrowding or high-density stocking. Here, overcrowding refers to forcing animals into a space smaller than what is required for their normal physiological and behavioral needs [56]. Therefore, the term banking indicates both confinement and overcrowding.

We also measured viral loads in associated workers to understand the dynamics of virus infection at the colony level during banking. However, no significant differences were observed in workers from the bank and individual colonies across collection weeks. DWV-B exhibited overall higher loads than DWV-A, indicating general replacement of the latter as a current global trend [57–60]. As the queen source of those workers originated from the Minnesota Hygienic Stock [34], in which the breeder colonies are selected using the liquid nitrogen freeze-killed brood assay, this might explain the lack of differences in viral load and low Varroa level over the banking period. Overall, it is revealed that banking has little impact on the variation of viral loads in workers for at least three weeks.

Baseline samples were excluded from statistical analyses with queens from bank and individual colonies to avoid the confounding effects of age and maturation. However, we compared pupae and newly emerged queens based on viral loads and gene expression. Although we did not find significant differences in viral loads between those pairs, we observed significant differences in gene expression. For instance, the expression levels of several genes related to both the immune defense (hymenoptaecin and defensin-1) and oxidative stress (catalase and vitellogenin) were notably higher in newly emerged queens than in pupae. This likely reflects the transition from a relatively protected, capped environment to one where queens face various environmental stressors. We also previously reported lower overall virus incidence in pupae than in other queen developmental stages [28]. Additionally, the higher vitellogenin expression observed in newly emerged queens compared to pupae could be due to assimilation of vitellogenin from worker-provided royal jelly [61]. Besides environmental exposure, the increased gene expression in newly emerged queens could indicate preparation for mating flight. A prior study also found upregulation of certain transcripts related to flight behavior in virgin queens [62]. However, whether genes associated with oxidative stress and immune defense are upregulated in anticipation of mating flight requires further research.

In virgin queens, DWV-A and LSVs loads did not vary significantly over the assessed period. However, DWV-B loads showed increased expression in the third week compared to previous weeks in both bank and individual colonies. Although the BQCV load was decreased in virgin queens from individual colonies in the third week, it was not significantly different from bank colonies. On the other hand, mated queens did not show significant variations in any viruses over the assessed period. When comparing viral loads between bank and individual colonies, no significant differences were observed in either queen type. This finding aligns with our previous study in which we found no significant differences in viral loads between mated queens from bank colonies and nucleus colonies [28]. This suggests that queen banking or stress due to overcrowding might not have a direct impact on virus infection, at least for three weeks. Our findings are further supported by previous studies, which highlight that apicultural intensification or overcrowding has no significant impact on disease prevalence [63]. Additionally, we reported dead queens with high BQCV and DWV-B loads, suggesting these viral infections are likely predictors of mortality during banking, not a consequence of banking.

In virgin queens from both the bank and individual colonies, a similar temporal expression pattern was observed for the three stress-related genes (CuZnSOD, catalase, and vitellogenin) and the RNAi pathway gene dicer-like, with higher expression in the first week that gradually decreased in the following weeks. In mated queens, the stress-related genes exhibited similar expression during the first two weeks, whereas dicer-like and defensin-1 showed increased expression in the second week. However, the expression of all genes declined in the third week, as in virgin queens. Overall, both virgin and mated queens exhibited higher gene expression in the initial weeks. Alburaki et al. [64] also reported higher expression of antioxidant and immune defense genes in caged workers compared to hive bees in the initial weeks, but these genes were downregulated at week 4. Such higher expression in the initial weeks could be due to oxidative stress from both overcrowding and confinement, as the expression patterns of CuZnSOD and catalase were similar in virgin and mated queens from both bank and individual colonies. Copeland et al. [27] also reported that banked queens exhibited high expression of oxidative stress-related genes, such as CuZnSOD, and harbored gut microbiota similar to those of old queens after 3 weeks of banking. In response to reactive oxygen species (ROS), several insect species exhibit an adaptive antioxidant response by increasing the expression of endogenous antioxidants; however, it is often reported that these protective enzymes are insufficient under excessive ROS [65–67]. Therefore, prolonged or severe oxidative stress disrupts redox homeostasis, leading to cellular damage and dysregulation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, potentially decreasing their expression [67, 68], as observed in our study in the third week.

Moreover, another plausible cause behind the decreased immune and oxidative stress response by the third week might be due to poor quality and quantity of nutrition, thereby reducing immunocompetence [69]. A honey bee queen is generally surrounded by retinue workers in the colony, which provide her with continuous care and enough nutrition [70, 71]. However, confined individuals are isolated from their normal social groups and have limited feeding opportunities [72]. Our finding of decreased vitellogenin expression over the period in confined queens is consistent with previous studies, which also reported higher titers of juvenile hormone in banked queens compared to free-range queens, suggesting poor nutritional status in the confined and overcrowded environment [27, 73]. Vitellogenin and juvenile hormones are often negatively correlated in honey bees, and high vitellogenin expression suggests a nutrition-rich environment, contributing to extended queen longevity [31, 32]. Future studies incorporating additional physiological measurements, such as queen body weight, fat body reserves, and total lipid content, will provide further insight into the nutritional and energetic status of queens during banking.

Additionally, the decreased expression of vitellogenin in mated queens might also be due to the restriction of egg laying during confinement [74]. As a yolk protein precursor, vitellogenin shows a high level of expression in mated queens, and even higher in laying queens [62, 75]. Therefore, a temporary decline in synthesis and uptake of vitellogenin might have been observed as reduced expression, which might be associated with decreased ovary size, as well as oocyte deterioration and reabsorption observed in previous studies in caged queens [11, 13, 76]. Such impacts of restrictive egg laying in mated queens might be responsible for the delayed initial oviposition often observed when they are introduced into a new colony after banking [7, 11].

Gene expression profiles of queens from the bank and individual colonies were compared to further understand the sole impact of banking or overcrowding on oxidative stress and immune response. Virgin queens from the bank colony exhibited higher expression of both immune-related (hymenoptaecin and defensin-1) and stress-related (CuZnSOD and catalase) genes than those from the individual colonies during at least one of the collection weeks. However, mated queens did not exhibit consistent gene expression patterns between the bank and individual colonies. For instance, defensin-1 showed higher expression in mated queens from the individual colonies in the first week, while it was higher in those from bank colonies in the second week. These findings suggest that virgin queens are really stressed by banking or overcrowding, whereas mated queens seem to be less affected. This idea is further supported by the mortality of virgin queens, which was observed only in the bank colonies.

These observed physiological differences based on queens’ reproductive status might reflect differences in their pheromone profiles. Queens’ pheromones influence the physiology and behavior of workers, including retinue response [77–79]. Mated queens were reported to have significantly higher retinue response than virgin queens [80], which indicates that they might have received more care from workers and showed better survival than virgin queens. This interpretation is further corroborated by our observation that vitellogenin expression was lower in virgin queens from bank colonies than in those from individual colonies. It also helps explain the increased mortality of virgin queens in the third week of banking compared to the second week, as they were less cared for over three weeks. In addition to the physical setup of queen banking, the physiological differences observed between treatments may be partly due to differences in colony environment, as banked queens were maintained in queen-right colonies, whereas individually maintained queens were housed in queenless colonies. Although the resident queen in the bank colony was separated from banked queens by a double-screen mesh, differences in worker care may have contributed to the observed differences in mortality and physiological responses, particularly in virgin queens, whereas these effects were less pronounced in mated queens. As mated queens from the bank and individual colonies exhibited similar patterns in viral loads, oxidative stress responses, and even mortality, these findings suggest that mated queens might be primarily affected by confinement rather than by overcrowding or banking.

Regarding the correlations between viral loads and gene expression, the correlation patterns differ between the bank and individual colonies in virgin queens, whereas they appear nearly identical in mated queens. These findings again suggest that banking greatly influences response to stressors in virgin queens, while mated queens are comparatively less affected. DWV showed significant negative correlations with oxidative stress genes in mated queens. Earlier research also found decreased expression of sod, catalase, peroxidases, and overall antioxidant capacity in DWV-infected workers compared to controls [81]. In our study, we did not observe such a correlation in virgin queens, suggesting that differences in correlation patterns could be due to varying reproductive statuses. During mating, queens receive accessory gland proteins, antimicrobial agents, and sperm, which induce behavioral and physiological changes, including immune activation, thereby increasing immunocompetence [82–84]. In addition, mated queens are more robust, in part due to higher vitellogenin levels and more developed fat body tissue than virgin queens [75, 85], which act as antioxidants, and produce antimicrobial peptides and other immune effectors [33]. Overall, these results imply that the reproductive status of queens modulates the interaction between viruses and immune response during banking.

Conclusions

To summarize, we showed that short-term banking or stress from overcrowding does not affect virus infection levels in queens and attendant workers. However, stress response and immune defense in virgin and mated queens differed due to both confinement and overcrowding. These findings indicate that viruses mainly act as opportunistic pathogens in stressed queens rather than as primary causes of stress and death during banking. Based on mortality and stress response, virgin queens were more vulnerable to both confinement and overcrowding stress, while mated queens were less affected, possibly because of differences in pheromone profiles and the resulting variation in worker care or feeding. Overall, this study emphasizes that queens experience measurable stress within three weeks of banking, and its effects depend on their reproductive status. Future research should investigate longer-term impacts of banking, post-storage performance of queens, and the role of nutritional factors influencing stress resilience.
