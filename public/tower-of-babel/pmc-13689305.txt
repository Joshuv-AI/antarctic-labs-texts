Genomic and functional characterization of Bacillus siamensis AFSC23 isolated from the gut of European conger (Conger conger) as a probiotic candidate for aquaculture
Authors: Fatimazahra Jouga, Fatima Mourabiti, Souraya Sakoui, Reda Derdak, Ana L. Diéguez, Ana Otero, Sergio Silva-Bea, Javier Dubert, Abdelaziz Soukri, Juan L. Barja, Bouchra El Khalfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The intestinal microbiota of fish represents a valuable source of antimicrobial and health-promoting bacteria. In this context, we report for the first time the isolation of Bacillus siamensis strain AFSC23 from the intestine of Conger conger. The strain was comprehensively characterized using an integrated genomic and functional approach to assess its safety and probiotic potential in aquaculture. The strain exhibited resistance to lysozyme (100 mg/L), gastrointestinal conditions, bile salts (0.3%), high salinity (11%), promising cell surface properties, coupled with inhibitory activity against A. salmonicida, V. anguillarum, P. damselae subsp. damselae and P. damselae subsp. piscicida. Genome analysis revealed the presence of several probiotic gene markers and biosynthetic gene clusters encoding bacteriocins and other antimicrobial compounds. Safety assessment demonstrated susceptibility to most tested antibiotics, with no detectable hemolytic, lipolytic, or DNase activity and it was not able to degrade mucin. Analysis of virulence factors and mobile genetic determinants did not reveal detectable safety concerns associated with the strain. Moreover, extracellular products were non-toxic, with no cytotoxicity observed in Epithelioma papulosum cyprini cells, and in vivo assays indicated no substantial adverse effects in Ruditapes philippinarum under the evaluated conditions. These findings highlight the potential of B. siamensis AFSC23 as a promising probiotic candidate for sustainable aquaculture.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05447-0.

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance is a critical One Health issue and a growing global public health concern [15, 32]. In aquaculture, the misuse and overuse of antibiotics to control infections contribute to the spread of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria [54]. In 2032, aquaculture production is expected to reach 111 million tonnes, showing the capacity of the sector to meet the rising demand for seafood worldwide [23]. However, the sector faces many challenges including high stocking densities and anthropogenic stressors that can lead to disease outbreaks, metabolic reorganization, reduced growth and impaired immunity [13, 31, 54]. These conditions favor the proliferation of bacterial pathogens particularly species from the genera Vibrio, Photobacterium, Aeromonas, Flavobacterium, and Streptococcus that are prevalent in aquaculture systems [1, 43]. Although antibiotics remain a common control strategy, their extensive use promotes resistance, contaminations, and the suppression of beneficial microbes [4, 31, 43, 64] posing significant risks to the environment, aquatic organisms, and human health.

In this context, fish and shellfish gut microbiota has gained considerable attention as a reservoir of autochthonous and allochthonous beneficial bacteria. These microbial communities contribute to host physiological homeostasis by promoting disease resistance, immune defense, and metabolism. Therefore, exploiting these microorganisms as probiotics has emerged as a promising eco-friendly alternative strategy to reduce reliance on antibiotics in aquaculture [34, 53]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), probiotics are defined as “live microorganisms which when administered in adequate amounts confer a health benefit on the host” [24]. The main criteria selection for probiotics includes their resistance to host-associated stress, their ability to adhere to the epithelial surfaces, their antimicrobial activity, and their safety profile [12].

Genera developed for use in aquaculture include Bacillus, Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, Phaeobacter and Enterococcus [33, 60]. Most of these probiotics are sourced from terrestrial environments [30]. However, studies revealed that host-associated probiotics exhibit better efficacy for colonization and improving growth and health in aquatic organisms than terrestrial-derived probiotics [39]. Therefore, attention has focused on isolating probiotics from fish species or water for application in aquaculture [30].

Among the various probiotics used, Bacillus species have gained significant interest as they have proven higher probiotic properties and a wide range of beneficial effects in contrast to other probiotics like Lactobacillus spp [31, 38]. Their sporulation ability and their capacity to produce a wide arsenal of beneficial enzymes, antimicrobial compounds and non-toxic substances, enable them to rapidly replicate, tolerate various aggressive environmental conditions and exhibit numerous physiological properties, making them a great promising probiotic for promoting sustainable practices in aquaculture [38, 59].

Previous studies have reported the probiotic properties of terrestrial-derived B. siamensis. For instance, Meidong et al. [46] isolated B. siamensis B44v from Thai pickled vegetables and demonstrated its probiotic effects in catfish farming, including growth promotion and disease resistance. Despite these advantages, the successful application of a probiotic strain requires comprehensive evaluation of its functional, safety and genomic attributes. Whole genome sequencing is of great importance for predicting probiotic, antimicrobial and virulence markers, thereby, understanding the underlying mechanisms of action of probiotics [52].

To our knowledge, no study has reported the isolation of Bacillus siamensis from Conger conger. Therefore, in this study, B. siamensis AFSC23, isolated from the intestinal microbiota of C. conger, was characterized using phenotypic, genomic and functional approaches. The strain was initially screened for antibacterial activity and subjected to taxonomic identification. Genomic analysis was performed, and probiotic-related traits were evaluated in vitro. Additionally, the biosafety profile of AFSC23 was evaluated through phenotypic assays, in vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity assessments, along with genomic prediction of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and virulence factors (VFs). The results suggest that this strain represents a promising probiotic candidate for aquaculture applications.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation and screening for antibacterial activity

A single European conger (Conger conger) was captured for this study by fishermen from the Moroccan Atlantic Ocean of the region of Safi (31°51′55.9″N/10°24′59.9″W). The study focused on marine fish as a potential reservoir of bacteria adapted to the marine environment, with probiotic potential for aquaculture applications. Subsequent to capture, the sample was transported on ice immediately to the laboratory. Intestines were removed, weighed and homogenised for one minute using sterile saline solution (SSS) (0.9% NaCl). Serial dilutions (10–1 – 10–7) were prepared, and 0.1 mL of each homogenate was spread on De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar plates (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 48–72 h. MRS agar was used without any selective modification or pretreatment in order to recover a broad range of potentially probiotic bacteria from the intestinal microbiota. 10 isolated colonies with typical characteristics were selected and pure cultures were obtained by streak plate isolation and stored at – 80 °C in MRS broth supplemented with 20% of glycerol for further experiments.

The bacterial isolates were screened for antibacterial activity using intact cells (IC) against marine bacterial pathogens namely Vibrio europaeus CECT8136, Aeromonas salmonicida VT45.1 DR6, Vibrio anguillarum RV22, Photobacterium damselae subsp. damselae RM.71 and Photobacterium damselae subsp. piscicida DI21 [31]. In brief, both the isolated and pathogenic strains were grown in Trypto-Casein Soy Agar containing 1% NaCl (TSA-1) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 22 °C, respectively. Around 106 CFU/mL (colony forming unit per mL) of pathogenic strains was spread in TSA-1 plates and the isolated colonies were deposited on the surface of the plate. Antibacterial activity was assessed after 24 h of incubation at 22 °C by the presence of inhibition zones around the colony. The isolate showing antibacterial activity was further investigated to determine whether the inhibitory activity was associated with secreted extracellular compounds by evaluating the cell-free supernatant against the indicator pathogens using agar well diffusion assay.

Identification and functional characterization

Morphological, phenotypic, biochemical and molecular identification

The bacterial isolate exhibiting antibacterial activity was selected and identified according to its biochemical, phenotypic and morphological characteristics, namely, catalase test (3% H₂O₂), oxidase test, Kligler’s iron agar test, citrate utilization, colony morphology, Gram staining, and the KOH test to determine Gram reaction [16, 34, 56].

Further, molecular identification was performed by extracting genomic DNA using the Maxwell® RSC Cell DNA kit (RefAS1370 Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA yield and purity were determined using the NanoDrop™ Lite Thermo Scientific™. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal forward and reverse primers (Fd1 5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′ and RP2 5′-ACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3'). PCR was performed in a 25 µL reaction volume and comprised of 1X buffer (Promega), 1.5 mM MgCl 2 (Promega), 0.8 mM dNTP (Promega), 0.022 U/µL Taq polymerase (Promega), 80 nM of each primer and 100 ng of genomic DNA. The thermal cycling program was set at 95 °C, 2 '/(95 °C, 40' '- 55 °C, 40' '- 72 °C, 1′) × 35; 72 °C/5 '/4 °C) [21]. The PCR products were evaluated using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis in Tris–Borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer. Then, they were sequenced using the BigDye Terminator Kit version 3.1 (Applied Biosystems). The alignment analysis was conducted using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) of the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database [21].

Quorum quenching activity

Quorum quenching (QQ) activity of the isolate was evaluated using the well diffusion method with the biosensor strains Chromobacterium violaceum CV026 for short-chain acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs) and Chromobacterium violaceum VIR07 for long-chain AHLs [48]. An overnight culture of the isolate was centrifuged at 8950 g for 5 min and the pellet was resuspended in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), (pH 6.5). in order to prevent the spontaneous inactivation of AHLs at basic pH. N-hexanoyl-L-homoserine lactone (C6-HSL) or N-dodecanoyl-L-homoserine lactone (C12-HSL) were added at a final concentration of 2 µg/mL. The mixtures were incubated at 22 °C for 24 h under agitation, then centrifuged at 8950 g for 5 min. The supernatants were collected for the quorum quenching activity. Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates were overlaid with soft LB agar (0.8%) containing an overnight culture of C. violaceum CV026 for the detection of C6-HSL or C. violaceum VIR07 for the detection of C12-HSL. Wells were made in the agar, and filled with 100 µL of each supernatant. Supernatant containing PBS served as a negative control and the corresponding AHL incubated in PBS pH 6,5 were used as controls. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Inhibition of violacein production around the wells indicated quorum quenching activity.

Whole genome sequencing and annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN®) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The yield and purity of the DNA were determined by quantification using the nanodrop and Qubit 3 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Whole genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Novogene Co., Ltd. Further, adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic 0.39, the genome was assembled with Unicycler 0.4.4, and annotation was performed using Prokka 1.14.4.

The phylogenomic tree was constructed by the Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny method (GBDP) using the TYGS server (https://tygs.dsmz.de/) and visualized using iTOL v.7 (https://itol.embl.de/). Average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis was conducted using the EzBioCloud ANI Calculator (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani). In addition, ten genomes of B. siamensis obtained from NCBI database were used to compare the ANI with the AFSC23 genome using JSpeciesWS (https://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws//). The percentage similarity was represented in a heatmap graph constructed using ChiPlot tools. To provide an overview of genomic organization, a circular representation of the AFSC23 genome was visualized using Proksee server (https://proksee.ca/). To elucidate gene functions and biological pathways, a functional annotation was performed using RAST (https://rast.nmpdr.org/), eggnog (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/), and BLASTKOALA (https://www.kegg.jp/blastkoala/) servers, while Bakta (v1.12.0) annotation was used to assign locus tags.

To investigate the presence of antimicrobial compounds, secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters were predicted using antiSMASH version 8.0.1, and potential bacteriocin gene clusters were identified using BAGEL4. The antibiotic resistance genes were identified based on a homology search using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) (https://card.mcmaster.ca/). Phage genome elements were annotated and analyzed using PHASTEST (https://phastest.ca/submissions/ZZ_bf12daf3c5), while plasmid content was detected using the PlasmidFinder 2.0 server. The presence of CRISPR/Cas system was evaluated using CRISPR/Cas Finder and insertion sequences were identified using IS Finder (https://isfinder.biotoul.fr/). MobileElementFinder (version 1.03) was used to detect mobile genetic elements (MGEs). Additionally, the pathogenicity was assessed using PathogenFinder 1.1, and virulence-associated genes were identified using the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB) (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/cgi-bin/VFs/v5/main.cgi).

Probiotic properties

Resistance to gastrointestinal environment and high salinity

Resistance to lysozyme was assessed according to Nandha and Shukla [49], with minor modifications. To simulate salivary conditions, 1% of bacterial overnight culture was inoculated into a sterile electrolyte solution containing NaCl (6.2 g/L), CaCl2 (0.22 g/L), NaHCO3 (1.2 g/L), KCl (2.2 g/L) and 100 mg/L of lysozyme (Sigma Aldrich, USA). The same solution without lysozyme was used as a control. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C and viable cell counts were determined after 4 h of exposure.

Survival under simulated gastric juice (SGJ) and bile salts was investigated as per Sakoui et al. [56], and Margalho et al. [44], with some modifications. Cell suspension containing 108 CFU/mL of the identified bacteria was inoculated into MRS supplemented with 0.3% Bile Oxgall and into SGJ prepared by suspending pepsin in sterile saline solution (0.5% NaCl) to a final concentration of 3 g/l and adjusting the pH to 3. The mixtures were then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Colonies were enumerated following the spread-plate method and results were expressed as log (CFU/mL).

Resistance to high salinities was assessed in 96-well plate inoculated with Trypto-Casein Soy Broth (TSB) and supplemented with NaCl concentrations ranging from 7 to 12%. 8 µL of overnight culture of the bacterial strain was added to the wells. The tolerance of the strain in response to different concentrations of NaCl was determined by measuring the optical density after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C using BioTek Epoch 2 Microplate reader, operating at a wavelength of 600 nm.

The auto-aggregation and co-aggregation assays were conducted according to the methods described previously [19] with slight modifications. Overnight culture of the strain (108 CFU/mL) was harvested by centrifugation (5700 g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was discarded and the pellets were washed with PBS, pH = 7.4 and resuspended in the same buffer. Subsequently the optical density was adjusted to 0.3 at 600 nm (A0). The cell suspension was vortexed for 30 s and was allowed to aggregate by incubating at 37 °C. The absorbance of 1 mL of the upper suspension was measured at 600nm after 16 h of incubation.

Safety assessment

Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotic susceptibility was evaluated using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method as an initial phenotypic screening against 17 antibiotics representing different antimicrobial classes. Bacterial culture was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland and spread on TSA-1 plates. Antibiotic discs were placed on the agar surface and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Inhibition zones were measured in millimeters and interpreted as resistant or susceptible according to the available EUCAST guidelines. The antibiotics tested included oxytetracycline (30 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), florfenicol (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), cefaclor (30 µg), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (20/10 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), piperacillin (100 µg), enrofloxacin (5 µg), cephalexin (30 µg), penicillin G (10 µg), sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (25 µg) amoxicillin (25 µg), and vancomycin (30 µg).

Virulence factors

Phenotypic virulence assays were performed on the isolated strains to evaluate their biosafety characteristics.

Mucin degradation test was assessed by spotting 10 µL of overnight culture (108 CFU/mL) on the surface of LB agar plates containing 0.3% of partially purified hog gastric mucin (HGM type III) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) [11]. The plates were incubated 48 h at 37 °C and subsequently stained with 0.1% amido black in 3.5 M acetic acid for 30 min. The plates were washed with 1.2 M acetic acid until discoloration. Halos surrounding the colonies indicated positive mucin degradation.

The hemolytic activity was assessed according to Kang et al. [34], with slight modifications. Briefly, fresh overnight culture of our strain was streaked on the surface of blood agar plates supplemented with 1% of blood and incubated at 37 °C for 48h.

DNase activity was assessed by spotting 1 µL aliquot of the overnight cultures onto the surface of DNase agar plates that were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The presence of a distinctive pink zone surrounding the colony indicates the production of DNase [2].

Lipolytic activity was identified by spotting 1 µL of fresh culture of the isolate onto the surface of LB plate supplemented with 0.2% CaCl2 and 1% Tween 80. The plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C, and the lipase production was determined by the formation of clear halos around the colonies [51].

In vitro cytotoxicity

To assess the biosafety of the strain, extracellular products (ECPs) were extracted using the method of cellophane [41]. Overnight culture of the strain grown in TSB was spread over the TSA plate overlayed with sterile cellophane paper and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Further, the culture was collected in PBS and centrifuged at 4000 g, for 40 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and filtered using 0.22 µm sterile filters and then stored in aliquots at −20 °C for further analysis. The concentration of the ECPs was determined using Thermo Scientific Pierce™ Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit and enzyme profiling was conducted using an API® ZYM kit (BioMérieux) following the manufacturer's instructions.

The cytotoxic activity of the ECPs of the strain was evaluated using the fish cell line Epithelioma papulosum cyprini (EPC), provided by the Aquatic One Health Research Center (iARCUS), University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain. Briefly, the EPC cells were grown into 24-well plate using the Minimum Essential Medium, supplemented with 10% of Fetal Bovine Serum (MEM-FBS 10%) and incubated at 25 °C. Further, the cells were exposed to a 1:10 dilution of the ECPs of our strain and consecutively incubated at 25 °C for 24 h and 48 h. Two control groups were incorporated in the study. The negative control involved treating cells with sterile PBS while the positive control group was treated with 1:10 dilution of the ECPs of V. europaeus. The cell destruction was visualized using inverted microscope after 24 h and 48 h of exposition.

In vivo cytotoxicity in Manila clam

To evaluate the short-term safety of the ECPs of the isolate, 30 healthy Manila clam (Ruditapes philippinarum) juveniles (shell length 17.45 ± 1.53 mm) were supplied by Proameixa (Spain) and used to assess the in vivo toxicity of the isolated strain as described by Martinez et al. [45], with slight modifications. Prior to the experiment, juveniles were anesthetized with a magnesium chloride solution bath (25 g/L) for approximately 1 h until they lost responsiveness to tactile stimuli, ensuring unconsciousness. The animals were then divided into three groups. The assay group consisted of injecting 10 µL of pure ECPs of the strain into the pedal sinus using a sterile 0.5 mL syringe. The negative control group was injected in the same conditions with sterile PBS, and the positive control was injected with ECPs of V. europaeus. Subsequent to injections, the animals were maintained in oxygenated tanks containing filtered seawater, at room temperature, and subjected to a 12-h light/dark photoperiod. The mortality and health status of each clam were recorded daily. Deceased organisms were removed immediately from tanks, followed by the replacement of the seawater. At the end of the experiment, any remaining live animals were euthanized by rapid freezing, which was selected as the most suitable method to minimize stress in accordance with standard procedures for invertebrates.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, using independent biological replicates. The results obtained were presented as mean ± standard deviation that were calculated using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Formal evaluation of the assumptions underlying parametric analyses was constrained by the limited number of biological replicates (n = 3). Student's t-test was applied for the resistance to lysozyme assay. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used for salinity tolerance assay, whereas it was followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for aggregation and hydrophobicity tests. Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied to survival analysis under bile salts and simulated gastric juice conditions over time. Survival curves from the in vivo cytotoxicity assay were compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Statistical significance for all analysis was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Bacterial identification and phylogenomic analysis

In this study, a total of ten isolated colonies were obtained on MRS plates following 48 h of incubation. All the isolates were purified and investigated for their antibacterial activity against gram negative marine pathogens. Only one strain exhibited antibacterial activity and was selected for this present study. The strain was catalase-positive, oxidase positive, gram-positive, spore-forming and rod-shaped. It fermented glucose and lactose and it did not exhibit quorum quenching activity. The general features of the strain are summarized in Table S1. The 16S rRNA gene sequences of the isolate were identified as B. siamensis with a percentage of similarity of 99.47% based on BLASTn analysis. The sequence data of the isolated bacterial strain has been submitted to GenBank database under the accession number [PQ425483].

The phylogenomic tree, based on whole-genome sequences showed that the isolated strain clustered within the clade of B. siamensis confirming its taxonomic level assignment at the species level (Fig. 1a). However, the low bootstrap values among them suggest minor genomic variations or unresolved branching due to high similarity. Closely related species such as Bacillus velezensis and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens form a close branch supporting that AFSC23 is clearly distinguished from them. Interestingly, while the phylogenomic analysis clustered AFSC23 closely with B. siamensis BCL, the pairwise whole-genome comparisons revealed that AFSC23 shared the highest ANI value with B. siamensis KCTC 13613 (98.51%), RHF4.1–25, B07 (98.49%) and NRS-1099 (98.47%) (Fig. 1b). Whereas the remaining strains clustered within the range of 98.36% and 97.84%. Antibacterial activity and predicted biosynthetic gene clusters

The antibacterial efficacy of the isolated strain was evaluated against five marine pathogens according to the inhibition zone (IZ). As shown in Fig. 2a, the strain demonstrated inhibitory activity against all pathogens except V. europaeus. the strongest activity was recorded against V. anguillarum and P. damselae subsp. piscicida with an IZ diameter of 13 mm, followed by A. salmonicida and P. damselae subsp. damselae with an IZ of 11 mm and 10 mm respectively. Similar studies also reported that Bacillus strains exhibited antagonistic activity against pathogenic vibriosis [31]. Genomic analysis of the strain AFSC23 predicted the presence of various biosynthetic gene clusters (BGC) involved in antimicrobial activity. Four types of bacteriocins were predicted using BAGEL4, namely, lanthipeptide class II, lanthipeptide class IV, sactipeptides and amylocyclicin (Fig. 2b). Comparative genomic analysis with nine other B. siamensis strains showed that six strains encoded only one or two bacteriocins primary amylocyclicin and sactipeptides, suggesting their conserved roles across the strains (Fig. 2c). However, a putative lanthipeptide class 1 was predicted exclusively in strain Bcl, indicating a more restricted distribution. In contrast, AFSC23 uniquely harbored gene clusters for both lanthipeptides class II and IV, showing a broader diversity compared to the other strains. Further analysis using antiSMASH predicted 15 gene clusters associated with secondary metabolites synthesis, covering 13 types of biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 2d). These include known antimicrobial compounds such as fengycin, bacillaene, butirosin A/butirosin B, gramicidin S, bacillibactin, amylocyclicin, difficidin, surfactin and plipastatin. Comparative profiling of secondary metabolite biosynthetic classes revealed that AFSC23 appears to be biosynthetically rich carrying a wide array of BGC with lanthipeptide class II that appears to be more restricted to the strain.

The antibacterial activity exhibited by viable cells combined with the absence of inhibitory activity of the cell-free supernatant suggests that the antibacterial properties of the strain against the tested pathogens may involve competitive interactions for binding sites and/or nutritional resources within the gastrointestinal tract [3, 31]. Moreover, the absence of inhibitory activity in the cell-free supernatant could be explained by the regulated expression of the predicted BGCs, which may depend on bacterial growth stage, environmental conditions and cell density, or that some compounds remain associated with the bacterial cell surface rather than being released into the medium [7]. Further functional and biochemical characterization of these BGCs is required to determine their conditions of expression and to elucidate their contribution to the probiotic potential of the strain.

Genomic features and probiotic marker genes

The whole genome sequencing of AFSC23 showed that the strain had a genome size of 3,810,418 bp with a G + C content of 46.25%. It encoded a total of 3716 coding sequences, 75 tRNA, 1 tmRNA, and 3 rRNA genes (Fig. 3a). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis showed that a total of 2417 genes were annotated across 40 KEGG pathways (Fig. 3b). The majority of these genes fall under the category of metabolism with the highest levels observed in carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. The Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification further indicated that the strain was mainly involved in amino acid transport and metabolism, transcription, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, inorganic ion transport and metabolism, energy production and conversion and other unknown functions (Fig. 3c).

Genome annotation of AFSC23 predicted the presence of several gene markers associated with essential probiotic functions, including acid, bile salt tolerance and adhesion (Table S2). Among the acid tolerance gene markers, sigB has an important role in the adaptation to various environmental stresses [14]. In addition, the nhaC gene, which encodes a Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter, is crucial for maintaining intracellular pH homeostasis under acidic conditions [65]. In respect of bile salt tolerance, five genes were identified. Among them, the yocM gene has been reported to play an important role in maintaining protein homeostasis and protecting against salt stress [29]. Regarding the adhesion potential, numerous genes have been identified, including those encoding binding proteins, flagellar hook-associated proteins, exopolysaccharide biosynthesis, regulatory factors and moonlighting proteins such as glnH, eno, tuf and tpiA. These adhesion-related genes facilitate colonization and contribute to gut mucosal interactions, which are essential for maintaining host-microbe homeostasis [17]. Furthermore, various genes associated with temperature stress tolerance were detected, including chaperone proteins and heat and cold shock proteins that are vital for maintaining cellular functions during temperature fluctuations. These detected genes are in line with those reported by [37, 57, 61].

In vitro probiotic properties

Resistance to gastrointestinal environments and high salinity

To exert their health benefits, every candidate probiotic needs to withstand the stress conditions that the body imposes [44]. Lysozyme is one of the first barriers a probiotic must encounter, it catalyzes the hydrolysis of bacterial cell walls, leading to cell death [49]. Prior to reaching the gastrointestinal tract, probiotic bacteria must survive gastric acid in the stomach (pH 1.0–4.0) [62]. In the liver, bile acids are synthesized using cholesterol as a precursor and secreted from the gallbladder into the duodenum to help fat digestion and nutrient absorption [62]. Bile acids are known to be bactericidal as they are able to damage the DNA and the bacterial cell membrane [20, 50]. Therefore, the selected isolated strain was investigated for its ability to tolerate lysozyme, bile salts and simulated gastric juice. B. siamensis AFSC23 tolerated all three stress conditions (Fig. 4). It resisted to 100 mg/L of lysozyme, showing no significant difference between the normal conditions (Fig. 4a), as well as it resisted against 0.3% of bile salts with a survival rate of 98.77% (6.541 log CFU/mL) after 4 h of exposure, as there was no significant difference with the control where the strain was inoculated in normal conditions in MRS medium. Exposure to simulated gastric juice (pH 3.0) resulted in a slight reduction in bacterial viability after 2 h of incubation. However, no statistically significant difference was observed among the evaluated time points (p < 0.05). After 4 h of incubation, bacterial count reached 6.013 log CFU/mL, maintaining 90.78% of viability (Fig. 4b).Fig. 4Tolerance of B. siamensis AFSC23 to (a) lysozyme, (b) bile salts and simulated gastric juice throughout 4 h, and (c) to various concentrations of NaCl after 24 h. Species of the genus Bacillus exhibit a remarkable resilience to extreme physical and chemical environments. They are able to survive across a range of ecological niches, including freshwater ecosystems, marine sedimentary environments, and the digestive systems of finfish and shellfish [59]. Our results show higher survival rates than those reported by Dabiré et al. [10] where the strain F20 belonging to Bacillus cereus isolated from Burkinabe Soumbala, demonstrated a survival rate of 87.91% and 44.10% after 3 h exposure to 0.3% of bile salts and gastric juice (pH 2), respectively. Moreover, in another study, the strain T242 of Bacillus coagulans showed a viability rate of 87% after 3 h of incubation in gastric juice pH 3 [62]. Tolerance to high salinity is a desirable characteristic for probiotic candidates intended for industrial applications, as changes in NaCl concentration may affect the production of various metabolites with potential health benefits [9]. In this present study, the isolate was examined precisely for its ability to tolerate increasing NaCl concentrations during 24 h, in order to assess its halotolerance capacity. The results showed that AFSC23 was able to survive at most concentrations but in various growth patterns (Fig. 4c). The concentrations 7% and 8% did not show any significant difference compared to the normal conditions during 24 h of incubation. Nevertheless, the strain was able to survive up to 11% NaCl reaching OD600nm 0.478 ± 0.218, while it did not tolerate the concentration 12%. This suggests that the isolate could be considered as moderately halophile under the tested conditions [42].

Cell surface properties

Auto-aggregation is linked to the ability of probiotics to adhere together and achieve high cell density, thereby improving their colonization in gastrointestinal environments, inhibition of pathogens and immunomodulation of intestinal mucosa [9]. Conversely, co-aggregation involves their interaction with pathogenic bacteria, thus, preventing these harmful agents from attaching to the gut [70]. In the present study, the strain AFSC23 exhibited high auto-aggregation with a percentage of 89.31% after 16 h of incubation (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the strain was able to co-aggregate with the marine pathogens, showing no significant differences between P. damselae subsp. piscicida (61.61%), V. europaeus (57.92%), P. damselae subsp. damselae (47.96%) and V. anguillarum (47.52%). Our results are close to those reported by Jlidi et al. [31], where Bacillus subtilis demonstrated an auto-aggregation rate of 88.74%. However, their results showed co-aggregation rates above 55%. In vitro cell surface hydrophobicity enables probiotics to adhere to intestinal epithelium through nonspecific, noncovalent weak interactions, referred to as hydrophobic interactions where the intensity of the forces involved is positively correlated with hydrophobicity [68]. In this study, results showed that the strain exhibited adherence percentages varying between 38.68% and 83.8% (Fig. 5b). Among the tested organic compounds, ethyl acetate showed the maximum rate, followed by toluene, hexane, chloroform and xylene with percentages of 83.8%, 53.6%, 45.75%, 41.6% and 38.68%, respectively. Various investigations have documented varying adherence rates of Bacillus strains. Athulya et al. [5] reported bacterial adhesion of Bacillus spp. to xylene with a rate of 63 ± 3.32% after 4 h of incubation with no significant variation from the 1 st to 4th hour. Our results are in accordance with the strain B. velezensis R-71003 isolated from the intestine of common carp that showed higher hydrophobicity with ethyl acetate and chloroform than with xylene [34]. In contrast, the strain B31 belonging to Bacillus weihenstephanensis showed a significant hydrophobicity to hexane followed by chloroform and ethyl acetate with percentages of 88.41 ± 0.2%, 88.34 ± 0.4% and 33.14 ± 0.20% respectively, [56]. Although the strain showed notable surface hydrophobicity, the test is considered as indirect indicator of intestinal adhesion and it is not sufficient to confirm this property of AFSC23. Therefore, further studies using direct adhesion assays, including adhesion to intestinal mucus, fish mucus or epithelial cells are required to validate its adhesion potential.

Based on a homology search using CARD, the results unveiled the presence of 8 putative ARGs that were associated with the class of glycopeptide (vanY, vanT), tetracycline (tet(45)), disinfecting agents and antiseptics (qacJ), phosphonic acid antibiotic (FosBx1) and cephalosporin (Bcl). The genomic profile aligns closely with the phenotypic profile observed using disk diffusion method, however, the determination of minimum inhibitory concentration would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the antibiotic susceptibility profile. In the present study, the prediction of tet(45) gene is correlated with resistance to both oxytetracycline and tetracycline. While the presence of glycopeptide resistance genes supports the observed resistance to vancomycin, likely due to the antibiotic target alteration. Furthermore, the detection of the Bcl gene, which is known to confer β-lactam resistance, explains the resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid as they are both β-lactam antibiotics [36]. The resistant profile to known antibiotics such as tetracycline, erythromycin, chloramphenicol, penicillin and streptomycin is commonly observed among Bacillus spp. [63]. However, varying patterns of resistance and susceptibility have been documented across different studies [18, 31, 56]. Notably, antibiotic resistance, in itself, does not pose a safety concern. When the resistance is intrinsic, it cannot be transmitted. The risk emerges when the resistance is acquired, as it can be transmitted to pathogenic bacteria through mutations or horizontal gene transfer [58]. For this reason, we investigated the genome of AFSC23 for MGEs and other transferable determinants, and assessed the mobility potential of the detected ARGs. The analysis revealed the absence of plasmids and CRISPR-Cas systems. Moreover, MobileElementFinder identified only a highly truncated Tn6216-like unit transposon fragment (11.95% coverage and 79.75% identity), which was not associated with any of the detected antimicrobial resistance genes. ISfinder analysis revealed only low-confidence matches to several insertion sequence families, and no insertion sequences were found in association with the detected antimicrobial resistance genes. Although, two prophage regions located at positions 66,170–69,283 bp and 268,846–308,543 bp were identified, none of the detected antimicrobial resistance genes, including qacJ, tet(45), vanT, vanY, Bcl, and FosBx1, were located within or adjacent to these regions. These findings suggest that, although several ARGs were detected, the lack of association with MGEs indicates a low potential for horizontal gene transfer.

Virulence factors and associated putative gene homologues

While probiotics are widely recognized for their health benefits, their safety profile constitutes an essential aspect for their use [70]. Therefore, it is mandatory to assess their pathogenicity before introducing a new probiotic strain to the market. Accordingly, our strain AFSC23 was investigated for its virulence activity through phenotypical tests. The results showed that the strain did not exhibit mucin degradation, it was non-hemolytic (γ-hemolytic), and did not present any lipolytic or DNase activities.

Mucin plays a significant role in enhancing the functionality of the gastrointestinal tract involving adherence of beneficial microbes, protection against pathogenic organisms and active transport of various metabolites [55]. It has been demonstrated that its degradation can be beneficial as it can promote the production of short-chain fatty acids and stimulates goblet cells to increase mucus production [11, 67]. However, it is also considered as a pathogenic mechanism as it may facilitate the translocation of bacteria to extraintestinal tissues, which can lead to predisposition of the host to clinical complications and microbial dysbiosis [47, 55]. Besides, α-hemolytic and β-hemolytic strains are considered pathogenic as they can disturb erythrocytes leading to the release of hemoglobin [27]. α-hemolysis involves partial lysis of red blood cells due to the transformation of hemoglobin into methemoglobin [35]. While β-hemolysis is characterized by complete lysis of red blood cells, indicating the presence of cytotoxic phospholipase within the strain [27, 28]. Similarly, lipolytic and DNase activities are also considered as virulence factors [51]. Overall, the phenotypic assays did not reveal detectable harmful effects under the tested conditions.

To ensure the safety of the isolate and avoid adverse outcomes while its application, the genome of AFSC23 was investigated for its pathogenicity and VFs. Pathogenicity assessment using PathogenFinder 1.1, predicted the strain to be a non-human pathogen. Additionally, the VFDB analysis identified 35 putative virulence-associated gene homologues (Table S4). Functional annotation using BLASTP against NCBI database revealed that these homologues are mainly associated with adhesion (fbpA), motility (fliP, fliQ), chemotaxis (cheY), capsule formation (capD, capC, capB, capA, cps4I), immune evasion and modulation (wcaJ, LOS, polysaccharide capsule), iron acquisition (dhbA, dhbB, dhbC, dhbE, dhbF), secretion (cdsN, lspA), survival and oxidative stress resistance (ureB, katA) and transport (vctC). Moreover, a homologue of the hemolysin III gene (hlyIII) associated with hemolysis was detected by VFDB analysis. However, BLASTP annotation identified the corresponding protein as a PAQR family membrane homeostasis protein TrhA, a conserved membrane-associated protein that has been reported to share structural similarity with hemolysin-type III protein [66]. Furthermore, the predicted hlyIII homologue did not correlate with the phenotypic profile, as no hemolytic activity was observed under the tested conditions. These findings are consistent with those reported by Qin et al. [52]. Nevertheless, additional studies are required to evaluate whether this homologue is expressed and functionally active and to determine its biological relevance and safety implications.

In vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity

Species of Bacillus are known to be metabolically active; their probiotic characteristics frequently reside in the production of a broad spectrum of useful enzymes exhibiting varied biological functions [59]. The results of the enzymatic profile showed that AFSC23 was positive to alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C 4), esterase lipase (C 8), trypsin and naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase (Table S3). Alkaline phosphatase is essential in aquaculture systems as it supports the recycling of phosphate through enzymatic hydrolysis, which considerably enhances the bioavailability of phosphorus, a vital nutrient for aquatic organisms [6]. In relation to esterases, these enzymes are involved in catalyzing both the hydrolysis and synthesis of esters, thereby contributing significantly in the degradation and cycling of organic carbon within marine ecosystems [69]. Trypsin is a key factor in enhancing the digestive enzyme activity of aquatic organisms. It helps break down indigestible components, increases vitamin production, and detoxifies compounds in the diet. Collectively, these functions improve feed utilization, digestibility, and growth performance of the host [30]. Naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase is also among the enzymes that contributes to digestion [22]. Moreover, the results showed that the strain did not produce harmful enzymes such as ß-glucuronidase, which promotes tumor induction in the colon by converting non-toxic substances into carcinogens [40].

Further investigations were conducted to evaluate the safety of the isolated strain for its use in aquaculture practices. The EPC cell line is derived from fish and act as a relevant model to assess the cytotoxicity of various substances in aquatic studies. Notably, the results showed that the integrity of the cells remained unaffected after 24 and 48 h of exposure, indicating the absence of cytotoxicity in the extracellular products of the isolated strain (Fig. 6a). These results were comparable to the negative control treatment. However, the positive control treatment which consisted of inoculating the ECPs of V. europaeus manifested cell destruction after 2 h of exposure. To provide a preliminary assessment of the safety of the isolate, in vivo assay was performed by injecting its ECPs into Manila clam. The results showed that no mortality was recorded during the whole experiment in the negative control group that was injected with PBS. The group treated with AFSC23 exhibited a survival rate of 90% within 24 h and remained stable until 3 days where the survivability of the individuals reached 80% until the end of the experiment, with no statistical difference compared with the negative control (Fig. 6b). However, the positive control group injected with ECPs of V. europaeus showed a survival rate of 70% after 24 h and reached 10% by the end of the experiment and differed significantly from the negative control (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the ECPs of the strain did not induce detectable adverse effects on the health of aquatic animals under the tested conditions. Similar results were observed by Bacillus strains isolated from marine aquaculture system [26]. On that account, B. siamensis AFSC23 could be a promising candidate strain to enhance nutritional benefits in aquaculture practices. Nevertheless, the limited number of experimental animals used represents a limitation for the study. Consequently, studies including oral administration of live cells and the use of a larger number of experimental animals, are required to validate its probiotic potential and its long-term safety and efficacy.

Conclusion

The present study reports the isolation and comprehensive characterization of B. siamensis, strain AFSC23 from European conger (Conger conger). It exhibited key probiotic traits, showing high survival under lysozyme, bile salts, gastric juice and high salinity, along with promising surface properties and notable inhibitory effects against V. anguillarum, A. salmonicida, P. damselae subsp. damselae and P. damselae subsp. piscicida. Genome mining revealed several probiotic gene markers and BGCs. Safety assessment showed susceptibility to most antibiotics, absence of hemolytic, lipase, DNase and mucin-degrading activity. Genomic analysis predicted several ARGs, however none were associated with MGEs. Furthermore, extracellular products of the strain appeared non-toxic under the tested conditions, and they showed no detectable cytotoxicity on the EPC cell line, along with its potential safety for Manila clam. Taken together, B. siamensis AFSC23 may represent a promising probiotic candidate for sustainable aquaculture. However, as bacterial isolation was performed from a single C. conger specimen, this may have limited the diversity of isolated bacteria. Therefore, further studies involving a larger number of fish are recommended to identify additional probiotic candidates. Additionally, as the strain remains in the early stages of research, and this study was limited to genomic characterization, in vitro assays and short-term in vivo safety on Manila clams, further investigations are required to validate its in vivo probiotic efficacy and safety in aquaculture species. Future studies should evaluate the metabolomic and transcriptomic profile of the strain, together with oral administration trails and the investigation of its effects on microbiome modulation, growth performance, immune responses, and disease resistance in different aquaculture species, as well as confirming its in vivo long term safety profile, prior to its application at an industrial scale.
