RA-QA: Towards Respiratory Audio-based Health Question Answering
Gaia A. Bertolino, Yuwei Zhang, Tong Xia, Domenico Talia, Cecilia Mascolo

Abstract
Respiratory diseases are a leading cause of death globally, highlighting the urgent need for early and accessible screening methods. While some lung auscultation analysis has been automated and machine learning audio based models are able to predict respiratory pathologies, there remains a critical gap: the lack of intelligent systems that can interact in real-time consultations using natural language. Unlike other clinical domains, such as electronic health records, radiological images, and biosignals, where numerous question-answering (QA) datasets and models have been established, audio-based modalities remain notably underdeveloped. We curated and harmonized data from 11 diverse respiratory audio datasets to construct the first Respiratory Audio Question Answering (RA-QA) dataset. As the first multimodal QA resource of its kind focused specifically on respiratory health, RA-QA bridges clinical audio and natural language in a structured, scalable format. This new data resource contains about 7.5 million QA pairs spanning more than 60 attributes and three question types: single verification, multiple choice, and open-ended questions. Building upon this dataset, we introduce a novel benchmark that compares audio-text generation models with traditional audio classifiers to evaluate their respective performance. Our experiments reveal interesting performance variations across different attributes and question types, establishing a baseline and paving the way for more advanced architectures that could further improve the performance. By bridging machine learning with real-world clinical dialogue, our work opens the door to the development of more interactive, intelligent, and accessible diagnostic tools in respiratory healthcare.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases remain one of the leading causes of death worldwide, underscoring an urgent need for scalable, accessible, and early screening solutions

Auscultation, the clinical practice of listening to lung sounds, has long been a cornerstone of respiratory assessment. Audio cues such as wheezing, crackles, or stridor can reveal early signs of conditions like asthma, bronchitis, pneumonia, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). These cues are subtle, yet diagnostically powerful, and lend themselves well to automation through machine learning

Models have demonstrated strong performance in classifying respiratory pathologies from audio recordings

While they can output discrete predictions, such as labelling an audio clip as containing wheeze or crackles, they do not support interactive dialogue or contextual explanation. To move toward more intelligent and accessible respiratory care, there is a pressing need for systems that can also engage in natural language conversations, enabling clinicians and patients to ask specific questions and receive comprehensible, context-aware responses.

Existing Question-Answering (QA) datasets in healthcare have largely focused on textual content and structured data, overlooking the diagnostic richness and complexity that respiratory audio offers. While some multimodal QA systems have been developed for clinical data types such as ECG signals

(

(

, none are tailored to the unique clinical characteristics of respiratory sounds, such as wheezes, crackles, or breath timing. These acoustic patterns carry essential diagnostic information that, if properly integrated, could significantly enhance the responsiveness and utility of healthcare QA systems.

To address this gap, we introduce the first Respiratory Audio Question Answering (RA-QA) dataset, a large-scale and diverse resource built by curating and harmonizing data from 11 publicly available respiratory datasets. RA-QA includes approximately 7.5 million QA pairs, covering more than 60 clinical and demographic attributes, and spans three question types: single-verify, multiple-choice, and open-ended queries (see Figure
1
). The dataset combines structured metadata with raw respiratory audio recordings, many of which were collected in uncontrolled, real-world environments, including through mobile devices, further enhancing the robustness and applicability of the resource.

Moreover, we introduce a benchmark comparing audio-text generation models and traditional audio classifiers, evaluating their ability to generalize and respond accurately across diverse input types and question formats. This benchmark allows for both quantitative assessment and qualitative insights into model behavior, revealing the potential of generative systems to move beyond rigid classification toward more expressive and informative interactions.

By bringing together multimodal data, natural language reasoning, and diagnostically meaningful auscultation sounds, our work bridges the gap between machine learning and real-world respiratory care. We envision RA-QA as a foundational resource to catalyze further research at the intersection of language, audio, and healthcare, paving the way for more intelligent, conversational, and accessible diagnostic tools for both clinicians and end-users.

Our contribution can be summarized as follows:

•

A Novel Approach for Respiratory Audio.

We present the first study introducing a natural language-based approach to respiratory auscultation.
This framework enables an interactive QA paradigm in which clinical insights can be extracted from auscultation data through conversational queries. By bridging audio perception with language understanding, our system offers a new, intuitive method for engaging with respiratory health information, paving the way for dialogue-based decision support tools and accessible screening applications.

•

An open-source RA-QA Dataset.
We gathered RA-QA, the first dataset of its kind, built specifically for question answering based on respiratory audio recordings. The dataset includes a wide variety of question types and covers multiple clinical attributes, enabling rich and diverse QA scenarios (see Figure
1
). RA-QA provides structured pairs of audio inputs and natural language responses, making it a valuable resource for modeling audio-language relationships. Additionally, RA-QA is open-source, and the recordings from 11 diverse datasets are freely available online. While a few datasets are under controlled access, they are still openly obtainable, ensuring broad accessibility for researchers and practitioners working in the field of respiratory health.

•

A Benchmarking Study for Respiratory QA Models.
We conducted a benchmarking study by training both a traditional audio classifier and large language model (LLM)-based architectures using the RA-QA dataset to showcase its potential in respiratory healthcare.
Through these experiments, we demonstrated the versatility of RA-QA, showing how it can be used to build both precise feature-based classification systems and more complex, interactive AI solutions, which are essential for developing accessible and scalable diagnostic tools in respiratory healthcare.

A recent advancement in this area is the introduction of OPERA (OPEn Respiratory Acoustic foundation model pretraining and benchmarking system), which curates a large-scale dataset (around 136K samples, over 400 hours) of respiratory audio and pretrains generalizable respiratory acoustic models

The pretrained models outperform existing acoustic models in 16 out of 19 tasks, demonstrating their generalizability and potential for further research in respiratory health. This work encourages the use of OPERA as an open resource for advancing respiratory audio research.

In a similar vein, RespLLM
(
40
)
proposes a novel multimodal large language model (LLM) that unifies text and audio representations to predict respiratory health outcomes. By combining prior knowledge from pretrained LLMs with respiratory audio data, RespLLM enables effective audio-text fusion using cross-modal attentions and instruction tuning. This model shows improved performance, outperforming leading baselines by an average of 4.6% on trained tasks and 7.9% on unseen datasets, while also facilitating zero-shot predictions for new tasks. Therefore, RespLLM lays the groundwork for integrating respiratory audio and textual questions, offering a scalable framework for health prediction. While currently based on a classification approach, it establishes a strong baseline for future models that generate natural text responses, moving toward more generalizable and interactive clinical tools.

Despite these advances, there is a notable gap in the availability of multimodal QA datasets, models, and benchmarks specifically designed for respiratory audio analysis that provide textual answers.

This work addresses this gap by introducing the RA-QA dataset, a pioneering resource that combines respiratory audio with textual responses, enabling the development of QA systems tailored for respiratory health. To further advance the field, we also benchmarked various models in this dataset to evaluate their performance and potential for real-world applications in respiratory healthcare.

3
QA Dataset Construction

This section provides an overview of our unique RA-QA dataset, detailing the standardized pipeline used to build a consistent and comprehensive collection of data. This approach ensures the dataset’s relevance and usability for various machine learning models. We also provide an summary of key data characteristics to better understand the diversity of the dataset.

3.1
Datasets overview

RA-QA was developed using 11 distinct datasets, each contributing a unique set of demographic, audio, and health-related attributes, collectively forming a comprehensive resource for respiratory audio analysis (see Figure
2
). These datasets represent a broad spectrum of clinical conditions and encompass various audio types, including breathing, coughing, lung sounds, snoring, and vocalizations (see Figure
3
), all of which are vital for diagnosing and monitoring respiratory conditions.

Figure 3:
Examples of sound waves present in the dataset.

Additionally, the datasets incorporate disease-specific attributes, such as self-reported health conditions (e.g., asthma, COPD, COVID-19), symptoms, and test results, which can enhance models’ ability to discern and classify respiratory conditions accurately.

The attributes selected for the questions in
our dataset can be categorized into four key groups, each providing valuable insights into different aspects of respiratory health.

•

Acoustic features
Acoustic features focus on the quality of the audio recordings, as well as the presence of crackles or wheezing, which are crucial indicators of underlying respiratory conditions.

•

Consultation context
Consultation context questions capture the immediate clinical context of symptoms and diagnoses, including whether the patient is experiencing symptoms like coughing or has tested positive for conditions like COVID-19, helping to link specific symptoms to audio data.

•

Demographics and health profile
These includes essential background information such as age, gender, medical history, and pre-existing medical conditions, all of which play a significant role in understanding the patient’s health status.

•

Recording context
These consider factors related to how and when the audio data was collected, including the recording phase (e.g., before or after physical activity) and the participant’s sleeping position, which can affect respiratory sound characteristics.

By categorizing these attributes into four groups, we take a comprehensive approach to analyzing respiratory health, which enhances the accuracy of machine learning models in predicting and diagnosing respiratory conditions. The chosen features cover a broad range of possible questions and patient conditions, ensuring the model learns not only from diverse audio features but also from the broader context of each patient’s situation.
The diversity in the dataset’s background makes it more resilient, enabling potential machine learning models to generalize better across different patient populations and health contexts.

3.2
Data Processing Pipeline

A robust pipeline was designed and implemented to uniformly process the datasets (see Figure
4
). This pipeline ensures that each dataset follows the same structure, thus maintaining consistency across the system. The processing workflow was broken down into several stages:

1.

Metadata Standardization and Label Mapping
The initial step involved standardizing the metadata from each dataset by using the provided information to create a unified metadata file for each dataset.
To improve the quality of data across all datasets, a series of additional filtering steps were applied to the original data.

In the data extraction process, we mapped the original labels to strings to create a textual system, ensuring that the resulting format was suitable for a question-answering framework (see Table
8
for an example). While some assumptions were made during this translation, they were carefully supported by established medical terminology and aligned with the information provided by the dataset creators.

Additionally, the metadata containing the original labels is included in the open source repository, along with the script used to generate the question-answer pairs from the templates. This enables full transparency, ensuring reproducibility and clarity in the data processing.

2.

Template Structuring
From each metadata file, we developed JSON question templates for all relevant attributes in the datasets (see Listing
1
for an example

We designed three types of questions: open-ended questions, where the answer is a free-text string with no suggested options in the question; multiple-choice questions, where possible answers are predefined and suggested in the question; and single-verify questions, where responses are limited to binary choices. Additionally, a standardization process was applied to harmonize attributes that were labelled differently across datasets, ensuring that equivalent attributes were consistently assigned the same representation.
The mapping is explicitly implemented in the Python QA generator scripts available in the repository systematically defined for each dataset to ensure consistency and accuracy in the question generation process (see Table
8
for an example).

3.

QA Pair Generation
Once the templates and standardized metadata were defined, we programmatically generated QA pairs for each individual patient in the datasets. For every patient entry, relevant question templates were instantiated using the patient’s mapped attribute values. This resulted in a collection of personalized question-answer pairs, where each question targets specific metadata fields (e.g., symptoms, diagnosis, age), and each answer is drawn from the corresponding patient data. These QA pairs are then associated with the patient’s respiratory audio recordings, creating a multimodal instance that links textual input, audio context, and textual output. This patient-level generation strategy ensures that the resulting dataset is coherent, scalable, and suitable for training and evaluating multimodal QA systems in respiratory health contexts (see Table
2
).

We adhered to the provided split of the original dataset whenever available, and included these splits within our system to facilitate consistent benchmarking and model evaluation across different use cases. In cases where specific splitting instructions were not provided, we performed a stratified split based on patient gender (see Figure
7
). When gender information was unavailable, we used the primary diagnosis label to guide the split. The data was divided into training (75%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets, ensuring a balanced distribution of key attributes across all subsets. In cases where only train and test splitting where provided, the training set was divided using a 85/15 split in training and validation set. This ensured that the resulting datasets followed a consistent and widely accepted approach to training and evaluation.

The public version of the generated question-answer pairs will be released later.

4
Model benchmarking

In this section, we describe specific experiments to evaluate the models’ performance in answering questions using the RA-QA dataset, exploring both classification and text generation tasks.

4.1
Models

We benchmarked three models: a traditional classifier trained solely on audio features, a second classifier incorporating both audio and associated questions, and a generative LLM-based architecture. This setup enabled us to assess the representativeness and informativeness of audio features alone as well as to explore how incorporating textual context influences models performances.

Audio Classifiers

We first focused on a classification approach, where the goal is to classify audio samples into distinct attributes, without incorporating the associated questions. This allowed us to isolate the model’s ability to recognize and categorize specific respiratory sounds independently of the textual question, providing a baseline for attribute-specific classification performance. We trained a support vector machine (SVM) classifier
(
10
)
on Mel-spectrogram features extracted from the audio recordings, evaluating its performance on various attributes such as recording position and symptom presence. This served as a reference point to understand how much information can be extracted from audio alone and considering a single attribute per training per model, without leveraging multimodal context.

Additionally, we trained a classifier that incorporates questions via a text encoder and a classifier layer (see Figure
5
a), using a subset of our QA dataset that includes samples from all datasets to assess classification generalization capabilities. Indeed, although our QA dataset is extensive, we designed a lightweight benchmark to facilitate quick model comparisons by utilizing only subsets of the data. For our empirical experiments, we selected different subsets of examples from each dataset aiming for a consistent distribution of question types across the datasets by using subsets of sizes 100, 1000, and 4000 examples per dataset. This approach ensured a uniform distribution of question types (open-ended, multiple-choice, and single-verify) for each attribute, allowing us to compare the models’ performance across varying dataset sizes. The same subsets were also used to test the LLM-based architecture, detailed in the next subsection.

LLM-based model architecture

We adapted the R2GenGPT
(
33
)
architecture to work within the RespLLM framework
(
40
)
, originally designed for binary classification (yes/no answers). This framework, which processes both audio and text, was re-engineered to support our QA system, enabling it to handle multiple labels and generate semantically accurate responses. We also experimented with a causal version of the model, where a random one-hot encoding vector was generated for classification. However, due to the task’s complexity, especially for the large number of possible answer classes, this approach resulted in zero accuracy.

To align the audio representation with the LLM input, we used a trainable linear projection layer.

We evaluated the LLM-based models using a combination of NLP metrics and exact match criteria, alongside cross-entropy loss on the logits during training. The exact match evaluation allowed us to measure the accuracy of the model in predicting the correct answer, while NLP metrics provided a more granular analysis of the model’s performance, especially in terms of semantic similarity. These metrics ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the models, accounting for both syntactic correctness and semantic accuracy in answering the respiratory health questions.

Multimodal classifier

LLM-based model results

To benchmark the previous results, the LLM-based generative model was trained and compared to the classifier on individual attributes. In some cases, the classifier performed better than the generative model, demonstrating stronger performance on certain attributes. However, in other instances, the generative model was more effective. These differences could be influenced by factors such as dataset label balancing and the unique characteristics of each dataset, which may favor one model over the other (see Table

To evaluate the generalization performance of the LLM-based approach, we focused on the results from the largest tested subset of samples from all datasets (4000 examples). This subset was chosen as the model exhibited consistent behaviour across all tested sizes and we were able to observe more stable and reliable results by using a larger sample. In the validation set, for both the classification and encoder-decoder versions, accuracy remained around 50%, indicating that the model produced completely correct answers in only half of the cases. Nonetheless, we observed a steady improvement in NLP metrics across epochs for the generative model. While BLEU and ROUGE scores, focused on surface-level n-gram overlap, remained relatively low, they still showed gradual progress, suggesting enhancements in the fluency and structure of generated responses. The CIDEr score for the validation set ranged between 1.1 and 1.4, reflecting modest improvements in consensus-based evaluation of content relevance.
Interestingly, despite low BLEU and ROUGE scores, the BERT score for the test set was significantly high. This indicates that the model’s responses were often semantically similar to the reference answers, even if they differed lexically, an important distinction, especially for open-ended questions where phrasing can vary.

This analysis highlights that while the model occasionally struggles with precise responses, the BERTScore remains high due to the semantic alignment between the ground truth and predicted labels. Despite some inaccuracies, such as misclassifying “yes” as “no” or “before running” as “after running,” these semantic overlaps suggest that the model is capturing the underlying intent. This shows promising results, indicating that while accuracy may currently be low, improvements can be made by refining the model’s ability to more accurately generate the expected responses. Additionally, data balancing and more targeted sampling could help alleviate some of these issues by ensuring that the model is exposed to a more representative distribution of examples, which would help improve its ability to differentiate between subtle variations in the responses and ultimately enhance overall performance.

5
Discussion

Our QA system represents a novel approach to bridging audio and textual data for respiratory health analysis, offering a flexible framework capable of handling both classification and generative tasks. Through extensive experimentation across diverse datasets and question types, we demonstrated that while the task remains inherently challenging due to the breadth of possible answers and semantic variability, the system achieves consistent performance. Generation models reached approximately 50% accuracy, while classification results varied depending on the size and diversity of the data related to the attributes, as well as the datasets used, with a high BERT scores suggesting that the model often captures the semantic intent of the answers, even when exact phrasing differs.

Our results were obtained using GPT-2, a dated language model trained for a limited number of epochs with a lightweight architecture, chosen for its open availability, low resource requirements, and reliable performance, making it a practical baseline for respiratory audio QA systems. Given its well-established capabilities and limitations, the performance here represents a lower bound. We expect significantly better results with larger, more recent models and more extensive training.

We present this QA dataset and multimodal framework as an open resource to foster and accelerate future research in this direction. By making this benchmark publicly available, we aim to support the development of more accurate, context-aware, and clinically relevant AI systems for respiratory health assessment.

Limitations
Our QA framework primary limitation stems from the task’s complexity, which involves both audio features and nuanced text making open-ended question answering particularly challenging, with performance being heavily influenced by the model’s capabilities. While GPT-2 served as a solid baseline, there is clear potential for improvement with more advanced models and greater computational resources. Additionally, the dataset’s reliance on simple label mappings restricts the evaluation of more complex responses and advanced generative capabilities.
Future work includes expanding label complexity, ensuring more uniform label distribution, and leveraging more powerful models as this can significantly enhance the utility and impact of this QA system in real-world clinical and research settings.

. The UK COVID-19 Vocal Audio Dataset is designed for training and evaluating machine learning models to classify SARS-CoV-2 infection status and associated respiratory symptoms using vocal audio. The dataset was collected by the UK Health Security Agency from March 2021 to March 2022, during the prevalence of the Alpha and Delta SARS-CoV-2 variants, as well as some Omicron sublineages. Participants were recruited through the national Test and Trace programme and the REACT-1 survey. Audio recordings of voluntary coughs and exhalations were gathered through the ‘Speak up to help beat coronavirus’ digital survey, alongside demographic data, self-reported symptoms, and respiratory conditions. These recordings were linked to SARS-CoV-2 test results, although speech recordings are not included in the open access version of the dataset and were not used in this study. The study was approved by The National Statistician’s Data Ethics Advisory Committee (reference NSDEC(21)01), the Cambridge South NHS Research Ethics Committee (reference 21/EE/0036), and the Nottingham NHS Research Ethics Committee (reference 21/EM/0067). . The ICBHI Respiratory Sound Database includes audio samples collected by two independent research teams from different countries over several years. Ethical approval was granted by the relevant ethics committees of the respective institutions. The majority of the database comprises audio samples recorded by the School of Health Sciences, University of Aveiro (ESSUA) research team at the Respiratory Research and Rehabilitation Laboratory (Lab3R) in Aveiro, Portugal, and Hospital Infante D. HF Lung
(
11
)
The HF Lung V2 dataset is composed of two versions: HF Lung V1 and HF Lung V1 IP. The lung sound recordings in HF Lung V1 were sourced from two distinct datasets. The first was collected during a datathon organized by the Taiwan Smart Emergency and Critical Care (TSECC) in 2020, with data made available under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0) license by the Taiwan Society of Emergency and Critical Care Medicine (TSECCM). This dataset includes lung sound recordings from 261 patients. The second source of data includes recordings from 18 patients in a respiratory care ward (RCW) or respiratory care center (RCC) in Northern Taiwan, collected between August 2018 and October 2019. These recordings were approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of Far Eastern Memorial Hospital (case number: 107052-F), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The lung sound recordings in HF Lung V1 IP are also sourced from two sets of data. The first set, provided by TSECCM, includes recordings from 32 patients, captured using a Littmann 3200 commercial digital stethoscope (3M). The second set consists of recordings from 7 residents of a respiratory care ward (RCW) or respiratory care center (RCC) in Northern Taiwan, collected between August 2019 and December 2019. These recordings were also approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of Far Eastern Memorial Hospital (case number: 107052-F), and written informed consent was obtained from the 7 patients or their statutory agents.

MM Lung
(
19
)
The MMLung dataset was collected from 40 participants (20 male, 20 female) aged 18 to 85 years old, all of whom are English speakers from the UK. Among the participants, 12 were healthy, while the others included seven self-reported COPD patients, seven self-reported asthma patients, and 14 individuals with other long-term conditions. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the University of Southampton. Data were gathered using three devices: a Google Pixel 6 smartphone with a custom app for data collection, and an Easy on-PC ultrasonic spirometer from ndd Medical Technologies. The audio data was collected in stereo mode at a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz, saved in WAV format, and recorded in a silent room to ensure optimal conditions. The data collection included four audio modalities: cough, vowels, mobile spirometry, and speech, through a series of tasks performed in a single session by each participant. For this paper, only the deep breath and the vowel sound of ‘o’ are included. Ground truth data were obtained using a medical-grade spirometer, with measurements taken by a healthcare professional according to European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) clinical standards. However, it is important to note that objective measurements can be subject to individual effort, which may introduce some errors (e.g., effort-dependent blows). This dataset is available upon request.

Coswara
(
3
)
The Coswara dataset consists of respiratory sounds recorded between April 2020 and February 2022 from 2,635 individuals, including 1,819 SARS-CoV-2 negative, 674 positive, and 142 recovered participants. The dataset includes nine categories of respiratory sounds related to breathing, coughing, and speech. Metadata accompanying the recordings contains demographic details such as age, gender, and geographic location, as well as health-related information including symptoms, pre-existing respiratory conditions, comorbidities, and SARS-CoV-2 test status. The data collection was approved by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee at the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore. Informed consent was obtained from all participants who uploaded their data, and the collected data was anonymized to exclude any personally identifiable information.

KAUH
(
9
)
The KAUH dataset includes audio recordings of lung sounds from patients with seven different respiratory conditions: asthma, heart failure, pneumonia, bronchitis, pleural effusion, lung fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as normal breathing sounds. The recordings were made using an electronic stethoscope, with the chest wall examined at various points by a specialist physician. Each sound was recorded three times with different frequency filters to highlight specific bodily sounds. This dataset is valuable for the development of automated systems designed to detect pulmonary diseases or classify lung sounds. All participants, or their parents in the case of minors, provided written informed consent for their inclusion in the study and the sharing of their data. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at King Abdullah University Hospital and Jordan University of Science and Technology (Ref. 91/136/2020). Data collection adhered to all relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, and the authors have the right to share the data publicly.

SSBPR
(
36
)
The SSBPR dataset is a sleep body position recognition dataset based on snoring recordings, consisting of 7,570 snoring samples with six distinct labels for sleep body positions: supine, supine with left lateral head, supine with right lateral head, left-side lying, right-side lying, and prone. One label, present in only a few subjects, is excluded from the task, following the 5-class setup described in [71]. The data was collected from 20 adult patients who underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) at a local Sleep Medicine Research Center. The study received approval from the local medical ethics committee, and all participants provided signed consent for their involvement, which included audio and video recordings during sleep. Personal information was anonymized to ensure privacy protection.

NoseMic
(
4
)
The NoseMic dataset is a subset of data collected for a respiratory rate estimation project. The audio recordings were captured using microphones positioned close to the participants’ noses, while respiratory dynamics were measured using a Zephyr pressure sensor on the chest. Data collection took place in a stationary environment, both before and after physical exercise. A total of 21 participants were involved in the study, with data from some participants excluded due to poor sensing quality. The benchmark includes audio recordings from before and after running, with each recording segmented into 30-second windows that overlap by 15 seconds. The average respiratory rate for each window was used as the ground truth.

Respiratory@TR
(
2
)

The Respiratory@TR dataset contains lung sounds recorded from both the left and right sides of the posterior and anterior chest wall, as well as the back, using two digital stethoscopes at Antakya State Hospital. In addition to the lung sound recordings, the dataset includes chest X-rays, pulmonary function test (PFT) variables, spirometric curves, and the St. George Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ-C) as multimedia and clinical functional analysis data. The lung sounds were captured across 12 channels, focusing on the upper, middle, and lower lung regions, as well as the costophrenic angles of both the posterior and anterior chest sides. The recordings were validated and labeled by two pulmonologists, who assessed the chest X-rays, PFT results, and auscultation sounds of the subjects. The labels correspond to five levels of COPD severity (COPD0, COPD1, COPD2, COPD3, COPD4). The dataset was released by Iskenderun Technical University, Turkey. Participation was voluntary, and patients, aged 38 to 68, were selected from various occupational groups, socio-economic backgrounds, and genders to ensure a diverse representation of the disorders.

A.2
Metrics Overview

Metrics
We evaluated the models using a combination of NLP metrics and exact match criteria. The exact match evaluation allowed us to measure the accuracy of the model in predicting the correct answer, while NLP metrics provided a more granular analysis of the model’s performance, especially in terms of semantic similarity for open-ended questions. These metrics ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the models, accounting for both syntactic correctness and semantic accuracy in answering the respiratory health questions. The following is the list of the evaluation metrics used:

•

BLEU:

(
24
)
The BLEU score evaluates the precision of n-grams in generated text compared to a reference. BLEU-1 measures unigrams (single words), BLEU-2 focuses on bigrams (two-word sequences), BLEU-3 looks at trigrams (three-word sequences), and BLEU-4 assesses four-word sequences. As the n-gram length increases, the score provides a deeper measure of fluency, coherence, and grammaticality.

•

ROUGE:

(
17
)
ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) is a set of metrics that assess machine-generated text by comparing it to reference text. ROUGE-1 measures the recall of unigrams (single words), ROUGE-2 evaluates bigrams (two-word sequences), and ROUGE-L focuses on the longest common subsequence (LCS), which measures sentence-level coherence.

•

CIDER:

(
31
)
CIDER evaluates the coherence and informativeness of the generated responses. This metric focuses on the contextual consistency of the response, examining whether the output makes logical sense and provides relevant information. A higher CIDE score indicates that the generated text is more coherent and contextually appropriate.

•

BERTScore:

(
38
)
BERTScore is a semantic similarity metric that leverages pre-trained BERT embeddings to evaluate the quality of generated text. Unlike traditional n-gram-based metrics like BLEU, BERTScore considers the contextual meaning of words in the generated and reference text. It computes cosine similarity between the embeddings of tokens in the predicted output and the reference, providing a more nuanced evaluation of the semantic alignment. A higher BERTScore indicates that the generated text better captures the meaning and context of the reference, making it a valuable metric for assessing the quality of natural language generation tasks.

•

Exact match accuracy
We evaluate the exact match of the string for both models. For the LLM model, we check for an exact string match, while for the classifier model, we generate a vector and compare it with the original answer vector. However, this approach is limited, as answers can be syntactically diverse yet semantically similar. While this method works well for classification questions, it is not ideal for open-ended questions, where answers can vary in syntax but still convey the same meaning.
