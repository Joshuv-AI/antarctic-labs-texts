Dengue virus infection and Guillain–Barré syndrome: a systematic review of clinical characteristics, outcomes, and predictors of severity
Authors: Moses Gregory Effiong, Aliu O Yakubu, Farouk Ishola Lawal, Usman Hosea Ojoh, Temilade Oluwatoyosi Adeniyi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue virus (DENV) is a mosquito-borne flavivirus causing significant morbidity in tropical and subtropical regions. While not classically neurotropic, DENV has been increasingly associated with neurological complications, including Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS). This review synthesizes evidence on epidemiology, clinical features, outcomes, and predictors of severity in dengue-associated GBS.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed and Embase (April 2025) using terms for dengue and GBS. Eligible studies were case reports/series with individual-level data on laboratory-confirmed or clinically diagnosed dengue preceding GBS.ResultsThirty-six publications describing 56 patients from Asia, Latin America, and other endemic regions were included. The mean age was 34.9 years (range 1.5–73), with 57% male. 95% had symptomatic dengue; thrombocytopenia occurred in 43%. Median latency from dengue onset to GBS was 7 days. Motor weakness was universal; facial palsy (32%), bulbar weakness (28.6%), sensory deficits (33.9%), and autonomic dysfunction (18%) were common. Electrophysiology showed AMSAN (33.9%), AIDP (32.1%), and AMAN (12.5%). Albuminocytologic dissociation was present in 62.5% of CSF samples. IVIG was administered in 76.7%, plasmapheresis in 14.3%; 25% required mechanical ventilation. Outcomes were favorable in most: 57% achieved full recovery, 32% partial recovery, and 5.4% mortality.ConclusionsDengue-associated GBS resembles other post-infectious forms but shows a high proportion of axonal subtypes. Prognosis is generally good with standard therapy. Although severe cases, particularly those with thrombocytopenia, are more likely to require ICU care. Background

Dengue virus (DENV) is a mosquito-borne flavivirus that continues to pose a significant global health burden. It is estimated that about 390 million infections occur annually, mostly in tropical and subtropical regions of the world, with the majority of the burden across Asia, Africa, and Latin America [1, 2]. DENV is a single-stranded, non-segmented RNA virus from the flavivirus genus; it has a positive-stranded RNA genome encased within a capsid, known as a nucleocapsid. The nucleocapsid is enveloped and appears spherical, giving a spherical shape [3]. It exists in four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), all capable of causing a clinical spectrum that ranges from mild febrile illness to severe manifestations such as DENV hemorrhagic fever and DENV shock syndrome [4, 5].

The severity of disease is influenced by both host immunological responses and viral genetic determinants [6]. Viral genomic studies have identified serotype-specific mutations associated with increased disease severity, particularly in DENV-2, which is hypothesized through two main disease processes: the first is antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), where prior infection with a different DENV serotype worsens the second infection by promoting viral entry into immune cells, leading to severe symptoms, while the other suggests genetic variations in DENV strains that increase the virulence and epidemic potential of the virus. Both viral and host immune factors play key roles in the severity of DENV disease [5, 7, 8].

While traditionally considered non-neurotropic, DENV has increasingly been implicated in neurological complications, with up to 21% of hospitalized patients during outbreaks presenting with central or peripheral nervous system involvement, including encephalitis, myelitis, myositis, and acute neuropathies [9–12]. The neuroinvasive potential of DENV parallels that of other emerging arboviruses such as Zika, Chikungunya, and Mayaro, all of which have demonstrated the capacity to infect neuronal and glial cells [13–16]. Immunopathogenic mechanisms of the DENV virus, which is implicated in disease causation, including cytokine dysregulation and immune complex deposition, are postulated to contribute to this neurovirulence [17].

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive weakness, areflexia, and variable sensory or autonomic involvement. It encompasses several variants, including acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) and acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), and can lead to respiratory failure in severe cases [18, 19]. GBS is classically post-infectious, with established antecedents including Campylobacter jejuni, cytomegalovirus, and Zika virus [20, 21]. Pathophysiologically, molecular mimicry between microbial and neural antigens plays a central role in the autoimmune attack on peripheral nerves [22].

An increasing body of literature, including case reports and cohort studies from dengue-endemic regions such as Brazil, Sri Lanka, and Malaysia, has reported temporal associations between dengue infection and the onset of GBS [23–26]. The proposed mechanisms involve both direct viral neurotropism and immune-mediated post-infectious responses [16, 27]. However, despite this emerging association, there remains a lack of consolidated evidence regarding incidence, timing of symptom onset, diagnostic criteria fulfillment, and clinical outcomes of dengue-associated GBS. This diagnostic ambiguity is particularly problematic in outbreak settings, where neurological symptoms may be misattributed to other etiologies [28–30].

This systematic review aims to critically appraise and synthesize the existing evidence on the association between DENV virus infection and Guillain-Barré syndrome, intending to characterize epidemiological trends, clinical features, proposed mechanisms, and implications for clinical practice in endemic and outbreak-prone settings.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, with its protocol registered in the Open Science Framework (Identifier: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/HSZ2D).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in APRIL 2025 in PubMed and Embase. Boolean search terms were applied in the following combination: (“Dengue” OR “dengue fever” OR “dengue virus infection” OR “dengue hemorrhagic fever” OR “dengue shock syndrome” OR “dengue virus” OR “DENV” OR “flavivirus infection”) AND (“Guillain-Barré syndrome” OR “Guillain Barre syndrome” OR “GBS” OR “acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy” OR “AIDP” OR “acute motor axonal neuropathy” OR “AMAN” OR “acute motor-sensory axonal neuropathy” OR “AMSAN” OR “Miller Fisher syndrome” OR “MFS”). Search results from both databases were combined and exported in Rayyan, and duplicates were removed. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible articles. The reference lists of included articles were also manually screened to identify additional studies. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through consensus.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools for case reports and case series [31, 32]. Each study was independently evaluated by two reviewers using the appropriate JBI checklist. The studies were classified into three quality tiers: those scoring 70% or higher were considered high quality, those scoring between 50% and 69% were categorized as moderate quality, and those with scores below 50% were deemed low quality.

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted data using a standardized Excel form. Extracted information included bibliographic details, patient demographics, dengue-related characteristics, neurological features, investigations, treatments, and clinical outcomes.

Data analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as means, medians, and ranges, where applicable.

A total of 36 publications met the inclusion criteria, contributing 56 individual cases of GBS associated with DENV infection (shown in Fig. 1).

Dengue’s hematologic effects were evident in many: thrombocytopenia was documented in roughly 43% of all cases (24/56), and over 96% had no significant prior neurological disorders. The time interval between the acute DENV illness and the onset of GBS was generally short, consistent with a post-infectious pattern. In 50 cases where the timing was documented, neurologic symptoms began within 1 to 20 days after DENV onset. The median latency was approximately 7 days. Other common antecedent infections known to trigger GBS (such as Campylobacter jejuni, cytomegalovirus, Zika virus, etc.) were negative in all of the reported patients, strengthening the attribution of GBS to the preceding DENV infection in this series.

Risk of bias

Using our JBI checklist, 34 of the 36 studies were rated as high quality and 2 as moderate quality (Supplementary file).

Clinical features of Dengue-Associated GBS

The clinical presentation of GBS in the context of DENV was largely similar to typical GBS in terms of symptoms and neurologic deficits. Motor weakness was universal, often with an ascending paralysis pattern starting in the legs and progressing upward. About one-third of the cases (32%) had associated facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) palsy, and roughly 28.6% had bulbar weakness such as dysarthria and dysphagia from lower cranial nerve involvement. Oculomotor palsy was less common (9.8%). Sensory symptoms or deficits were reported in approximately 33.9% of cases, and autonomic dysfunction in about 18% of patients.

For GBS subtype, the axonal variants were prominent: acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) and acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN) together accounted for roughly 46% of cases (19 AMSAN cases [33.9%] and 7 AMAN cases [12.5%]) (shown in Fig. 2).

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed the characteristic albuminocytologic dissociation in 62.5% of cases. Anti-ganglioside antibodies were performed in relatively few of the dengue-related cases. Among the cases where such testing was reported were positive results for anti-GD1b IgG and anti-GM1 IgG antibodies.

Treatment and outcomes

Management and therapy for GBS were described in all reported cases and generally adhered to standard GBS treatment protocols. Immunotherapy, including either plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), was utilized in 89.2% of cases. IVIG was the most common treatment, administered in about 76.7% of all cases, and 2 cases were managed by supportive therapy only. Notably, the patients who received only supportive treatment had no respiratory involvement and showed recovery without immunomodulatory therapy due to mild symptoms. Both had a power grade of 3 at onset and demonstrated rapid improvement to full recovery, with recovery times of 7 and 3 months, respectively (36, 38).

The overall prognosis of dengue-associated GBS was favorable; outcomes were documented for all 56 patients, with follow-up ranging from hospital discharge up to several months. A majority, 57% (32 patients), achieved full recovery, regaining essentially normal neurologic function by the time of the last follow-up. Among these, 26 had documented recovery times, with a median of 23 days (range: 5–365 days). Partial recovery was observed in 32% of patients (18 cases), of which 16 had documented recovery durations, with a median of 39 days (range: 14–90 days). There were 3 fatalities in the cohort (5.4% mortality), each resulting from complications including pneumonia and myocardial infarction. Fourteen patients (25% of the cohort) required mechanical ventilation due to respiratory failure. Among these ventilated patients, outcomes were markedly poorer: only 1 of the 14 made a full recovery, 8 had partial recovery with significant residual deficits, and 5 had poor outcomes, including all 3 deaths. By contrast, among the 42 patients who did not require mechanical ventilation, nearly 75% attained a full recovery.

The presence of dengue-related thrombocytopenia appeared to influence ICU admission in patients with Guillain–Barré Syndrome (GBS). Among patients who developed thrombocytopenia during their DENV infection, a greater proportion required ICU care (10 out of 24 patients, 41.7%) compared to those with normal platelet counts (6 out of 15 patients, 40.0%). ICU admission was observed across different GBS electrophysiological subtypes, with rates of 41% in AIDP (7 out of 17), 21% in AMSAN (4 out of 19), and 29% in AMAN (2 out of 7). For cerebrospinal fluid findings, 34% of patients with albuminocytologic dissociation required ICU admission, compared with 20% of those without this feature.

Recovery outcomes varied by electrophysiological subtype. Full recovery was most frequent in AMSAN (73.7%, 14/19), followed by AIDP (57.1%, 8/14) and AMAN (42.9%, 3/7). Partial recovery predominated in AMAN (57.1%, 4/7). Among patients with bulbar weakness who did not require ICU admission, 90.0% (9/10) achieved full recovery. In contrast, among those requiring ICU admission, full recovery was absent (0%), partial recovery occurred in 83.3% (5/6), and severe or no recovery was observed in 16.7% (1/6).

Discussion

In this systematic review, we compiled the largest set of dengue-associated Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) cases to date (56 cases from 36 reports), characterizing this rare complication across geography, demographics, and clinical features.

Our analysis demonstrates that although GBS is an uncommon outcome of DENV, it occurs globally, with cases reported from Asia (Sri Lanka, Malaysia, India, the Philippines, Taiwan, Nepal, and Bangladesh), South America (Brazil, Peru, Paraguay, and Colombia), North America (Mexico), Africa (Sudan), and Oceania (New Caledonia). Most of these reports originate from tropical regions where the DENV remains endemic [1]. The clustering of cases in South Asia and Latin America likely reflects higher DENV incidence and more active reporting, while the paucity of African cases may be due to underdiagnosis. Serotype data were sparse, and no clear serotype predilection was identified, indicating that all four DENV serotypes may be capable of triggering GBS. The condition affected patients across a wide age range (1.5–73 years), with a mean of 34.9 years and a slight male predominance. consistent with the known male bias in GBS incidence generally [18]. Nearly all patients had typical DENV symptoms before the onset of weakness, underscoring the importance of neurological vigilance during DENV recovery.

The temporal relationship in most cases supports a post-infectious mechanism, with a median latency of approximately one week. Absence of other recognized precipitants strengthens attribution to DENV. Epidemiological evidence supports this link: a 2022 study in Mexico estimated 1.45 GBS cases per 1000 DENV infections, comparable to Zika virus and higher than chikungunya [28]. In Mexico, GBS incidence increased by 77.8% weekly after 2016, correlating positively with DENV and Zika activity, while an outbreak report from Peru in 2023 similarly observed clustering of GBS cases following peak DENV incidence [66]. Although ecological analyses cannot establish causality, they are consistent with our findings of a real but rare association.

Pathophysiologically, the leading mechanism involves molecular mimicry, where antiviral antibodies cross-react with peripheral nerve components, producing demyelination and weakness [3, 19]. A recent multinational study demonstrated that anti-dengue immunoreactivity was significantly associated with the demyelinating subtype of GBS [67]. Our data similarly revealed AIDP (32.1%) and AMSAN (33.9%) as the most common variants, with antiganglioside antibodies such as anti-GM1 and anti-GD1b identified in some patients, supporting autoimmune neuropathogenesis. DENV infection also induces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL-6, IL-8) and complement activation, which may disrupt the blood–nerve barrier and potentiate immune-mediated injury [25]. In addition, direct neuroinvasion remains plausible. Experimental murine studies confirm that DENV can breach the blood–brain barrier (BBB), infecting endothelial cells or altering junctional proteins via cytokine-mediated inflammation, resulting in detectable viral RNA within brain parenchyma [68]. Viral nonstructural proteins such as NS1 disrupt endothelial tight junctions (claudin-1, occludin) through TLR4 activation [69, 70], while DENV infection reduces transendothelial resistance and upregulates adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, PECAM) and chemokines (MCP-1, TNF-α), further facilitating CNS entry [70]. Together, these findings highlight the dual potential of immune-mediated and direct viral mechanisms.

Diagnosis of dengue-associated GBS is challenging. Established diagnostic criteria, such as the Brighton criteria, rely heavily on clinical interpretation, leading to subjectivity and potential misclassification [71]. Overlap with dengue-related myositis, hypokalemic paralysis, or encephalopathy can mislead clinicians [53, 72]. Electrophysiological studies and CSF analysis remain critical: 62.5% of reported CSF samples showed albuminocytologic dissociation, while electrodiagnostics revealed demyelinating features typical of GBS [18, 26]. However, diagnostic access is limited in many low- and middle-income countries, complicating case recognition. Thrombocytopenia, a hallmark hematologic abnormality of DENV [73] adds another barrier, as lumbar puncture is contraindicated in patients with low platelet counts due to risk of hemorrhage. In our review, thrombocytopenia was documented in 43% of cases, often associated with more severe disease requiring ICU admission. This underscores thrombocytopenia as both a marker of severity and a diagnostic obstacle.

Treatment strategies mirrored global standards for GBS. Most patients (76.7%) received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), while plasmapheresis, corticosteroids, or supportive care were used in others. Recovery outcomes were favorable in over half of cases, with a mean recovery time of 48.9 days. However, 25% required mechanical ventilation, reflecting a severe disease burden similar to classic GBS cohorts [74, 75]. Mortality was concentrated among older patients (ages 45–60), consistent with evidence that advanced age and severe thrombocytopenia predict poorer outcomes [18, 73]. Younger patients, even when critically ill, showed higher rates of recovery. These findings reinforce the importance of early recognition and timely initiation of immunotherapy to reduce disability and mortality.

Clinical and public health implications

DENV remains a major global health threat, with an estimated 390 million infections annually [1, 2]. Neurological complications, including GBS, have been reported from at least 25 countries across Asia-Pacific, the Americas, Africa, and the Mediterranean [26]. Despite its rarity, dengue-associated GBS is clinically and public health significant. Current case definitions by the Centre of Disease Control [76] do not include neurological features such as paresis or paralysis, risking under-recognition. Our findings highlight the importance of neurological monitoring between days 7 and 14 of DENV illness, particularly in endemic areas. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, and early access to IVIG or plasmapheresis is essential for improving outcomes [26]. Strengthening arboviral surveillance systems to routinely capture neurological complications and integrating DENV testing into GBS evaluations in endemic regions could reduce underdiagnosis and improve preparedness.

Limitations and potential biases

This review has several limitations. Reliance on case reports and small series introduces publication bias, with a tendency to over-represent severe cases while underestimating mild or unreported instances. Heterogeneous reporting resulted in incomplete data, particularly regarding latency intervals, serotype confirmation, and CSF results. Observational designs preclude definitive causal inference; while temporal relationships and clinical consistency strongly suggest an association, chance co-occurrence cannot be excluded. Diagnostic confirmation relied variably on NS1 antigen, PCR, or IgM assays, the latter of which may yield false positives due to cross-reactivity [particularly with Zika virus and other flaviviruses]. Finally, follow-up data were short-term in most reports, limiting assessment of long-term functional outcomes, which are often achieved over 12 months or more.

Recommendations for future research

Accurate diagnostic criteria are essential for patient care and research, including clinical trials and vaccine safety studies [75]. Future research should prioritize standardized neurological follow-up protocols for DENV patients to enable early detection of GBS, particularly in endemic regions. Immunological profiling may help identify predictive markers across different DENV serotypes, offering insights into disease mechanisms and risk stratification. There is also a need for harmonized diagnostic criteria and laboratory procedures to improve consistency and accuracy in identifying GBS cases. Investigating host and viral factors—including genetic predispositions and strain variations—could further clarify susceptibility patterns. Addressing current evidence gaps and implementing standardized case reporting will strengthen surveillance and data comparability. Additionally, comparing GBS risks across other arboviruses such as Zika, chikungunya, West Nile virus, and yellow fever virus could broaden the relevance of findings and inform cross-pathogen preparedness. Ultimately, the focus should remain on improving clinical outcomes through better diagnostics, monitoring, and healthcare system readiness.

Conclusion

Dengue-associated GBS, though rare, represents a serious neurological complication with significant clinical and public health implications. The global distribution of cases underscores its relevance to endemic regions, and both immune-mediated and direct viral mechanisms appear contributory. While most patients recover with standard therapy, advanced age, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory failure are poor prognostic markers. Greater clinical awareness, timely treatment, and strengthened surveillance are essential to reduce disability and mortality. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized diagnostics and long-term follow-up will be critical to fully define disease burden and outcomes.
