Abstract
Multicalibration ( 29 ) is an algorithmic fairness perspective which demands that the predictions of a predictor are correct conditional on themselves and membership in a collection of potentially overlapping subgroups of a population. The work of 38 established a surprising connection between multicalibration for an arbitrary property Γ \Gamma (e.g., mean or median) and property elicitation: a property Γ \Gamma can be multicalibrated if and only if it is elicitable, where elicitability is the notion that the true property value of a distribution can be obtained by solving a regression problem over the distribution. In the adversarial (online) setting, 38 proposed an inefficient algorithm that achieves 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}) ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -multicalibration error for a hypothesis class of group membership functions and an elicitable property Γ \Gamma , after T T rounds of interaction between a forecaster and adversary. In this paper, we generalize multicalibration for an elicitable property Γ \Gamma from group membership functions to arbitrary bounded hypothesis classes and introduce a stronger notion — swap multicalibration, following 25 . Subsequently, we propose an oracle-efficient algorithm which when given access to an online agnostic learner, achieves 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T 1 r + 1 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{r+1}}) ℓ r \ell_{r} -swap multicalibration error with high probability ( r ≥ 2 ) (r\geq 2) for a hypothesis class with bounded sequential Rademacher complexity and an elicitable property Γ \Gamma . For the special case of r = 2 r=2 , this implies an oracle-efficient algorithm that achieves 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T 1 3 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}}) ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -swap multicalibration error, which significantly improves on the previously established bounds for the problem ( 38 ; 18 ; 35 ) , and completely resolves an open question raised in 17 on the possibility of an oracle-efficient algorithm that achieves 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}) ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -mean multicalibration error by answering it in a strongly affirmative sense.

Introduction

With the rise of machine learning models in several real-world applications such as healthcare, medical support, law, finance, and safety-critical control, quantifying the model’s confidence in its predictions is becoming an increasingly serious problem. A widely studied metric to quantify the uncertainty in a model’s predictions is
calibration

(
10
)
, which demands that the model’s predictions are correct conditional on themselves. To define calibration more formally, we set up some notation. Let
\mathcal{X}
be the instance space,
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
be the label space,
D\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
be an unknown distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and
p:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be a predictor. The predictor
p
is (mean) calibrated if
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}[y|p(x)=v]=v
for all
v\in\text{Range}(p)
. Calibration ensures a certain level of trustworthiness since the predictions are a true representation of the ground truth. However, calibration only ensures unbiasedness at the scale of the level sets of the predictor and cannot address broader reliability and fairness concerns, e.g., when the instance space
\mathcal{X}
also consists of several refinements corresponding to potentially overlapping demographic groups, and the model’s predictions should not lead to any detectable bias among the groups. To incentivize predictors to be unbiased not just marginally, but also for a collection of protected subgroups of a population,
29
introduced
multicalibration
. Given a set
\mathcal{S}
of potentially overlapping subsets of
\mathcal{X}
, the predictor
p
is (mean) multicalibrated if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}[y|p(x)=v,x\in s]=v,\text{for all }v\in\text{Range}(p),s\in\mathcal{S}.

Although multicalibration was initially proposed in the distributional (offline) setting by
29
as a mechanism to incentivize fair predictions, recent years have witnessed surprising connections with several different areas, e.g., complexity theory
(
6
;
7
)
, algorithmic fairness
(
39
)
, learning theory
(
23
;
24
)
, indistinguishability and pseudo-randomness
(
12
;
22
;
30
;
13
)
, and so on, leading to a surge of interest in the community. Despite its conceptual importance and broad influence, achieving multicalibration in a sample-efficient manner has remained an outstanding problem, with existing constructions
(
29
;
23
;
19
)
requiring
\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-10}
samples to efficiently learn a predictor with multicalibration error at most
\varepsilon
. The above problem also manifests in the more challenging adversarial (online) setting, where an online forecaster and adversary interact sequentially for
T
rounds, and the goal of the forecaster is to minimize the multicalibration error at the end of their interaction. Notably, existing online multicalibration algorithms suffer from one or more of the following issues: (a) they achieve error rates that scale poorly with
T
; (b) are inefficient; (c) offer strong theoretical guarantees only under specific settings; and (d) do not readily generalize beyond multicalibration for means (we define multicalibration for an arbitrary property in
Subsection
1.2

Although in recent years, online multicalibration has gained significant attention
(
26

27
;
17

37

, the aforementioned issues remain inherent to the proposed algorithms. The motivating goal of this paper is to devise algorithms that simultaneously circumvent the challenges posed above.

1.1
Online (Mean) Multicalibration

Motivated by the above concerns, in this paper, we primarily consider online multicalibration — a sequential decision-making problem between an online forecaster and an adversary over
T
rounds of interaction. In online (mean) multicalibration, at each time
t\in[T]
, (a) the adversary reveals a context
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the instance space; (b) the forecaster predicts a distribution
p_{t}\in[0,1]
over binary outcomes; (c) the adversary reveals the true label
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
. For a bounded hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subset[-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}
, the set of forecasts
p_{1},\dots,p_{T}
is multicalibrated if no hypothesis
f
in
\mathcal{F}
is marginally able to detect any correlation
f(x_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})
with the residual error
y_{t}-p_{t}
when conditioned on the rounds where the prediction made is
p_{t}=p
. Mathematically, this is quantified by minimizing the
\ell_{r}
-multicalibration error
(r\geq 1)
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{MCal}_{\text{mean},r}(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}f(x_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert^{r},

(1)

where
n_{p}\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}
denotes the number of rounds where the prediction made is
p_{t}=p
. Note that the summation
\sum_{p\in[0,1]}
is an abuse of notation and that it is really over the (at most
T
) distinct predictions made by the forecaster. Online calibration (
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},r}
) is a special case of multicalibration where
\mathcal{F}=\{1\}
and
1
denotes the constant function that evaluates to
1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},r}\coloneqq\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert^{r}.

Throughout the paper, we consider forecasters that make predictions in a finite discretization
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
of
[0,1]
, where
z_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
and
N
is a parameter to be specified later. We let
n_{i}
be a shorthand for
n_{z_{i}}
.

In a line of work initiated to understand the implications of multicalibration towards
omniprediction
— a simultaneous loss-minimization paradigm introduced by
23
,
25
introduced a related notion called
swap multicalibration
. Informally, while multicalibration requires a marginal (over
p
) guarantee for the conditional correlation errors, swap multicalibration demands that the conditional correlation error for each
p
be small. Formally, the
\ell_{r}
-swap multicalibration error incurred by the forecaster is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{SMCal}_{\text{mean},r}(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}f(x_{t})(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert^{r}.

(2)

Compared to multicalibration, swap multicalibration is a stronger notion which requires that for all
p\in[0,1]
, even when conditioned on the rounds where the prediction made is
p_{t}=p
, the no correlation guarantee holds for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Clearly, we have
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\text{mean},r}(\mathcal{F})\geq\mathsf{MCal}_{\text{mean},r}(\mathcal{F})
.

1.2
A Connection between Multicalibration and Property Elicitation

Although conventionally stated in the context of means, mutlicalibration can be defined more generally for any distributional property
\Gamma
, e.g., median, variance and higher
k
-th order
(k\geq 2)
moments,
q
-th quantiles, etc. In the distributional setting, let
D\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
be an unknown distribution, where, as usual,
\mathcal{X}
denotes the instance space and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[0,1]
represents the label space (not necessarily
\{0,1\}
). Let
\mathcal{S}
be a collection of potentially overlapping subsets of
\mathcal{X}
and
\Gamma:\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
be a property (without any loss of generality, we assume
\text{Range}(\Gamma)=[0,1]
), where
\mathcal{P}
is a family of probability distributions over
\mathcal{Y}
. A predictor
\gamma:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is
\Gamma
-multicalibrated
if
\Gamma(D|\gamma(x)=v,x\in s)=v
holds for all
v\in[0,1],s\in\mathcal{S}
.
In other words, the predictions of
\gamma
about the property
\Gamma
are correct even when conditioned on the level sets of
\gamma
and membership in a collection of potentially overlapping subsets of
\mathcal{X}
. Similarly,
\Gamma
-calibration
is the marginal requirement that
\Gamma(D|\gamma(x)=v)=v
for all
v\in[0,1]
.
32
initiated the study of moment multicalibration and showed that it is impossible to multicalibrate variance and other higher moments since there exists a simple distribution
D
on which even the true distributional predictor for variance
\gamma_{\text{var}}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[(y-\mathbb{E}[y|x])^{2}|x]
is not calibrated.

1

To observe this, let
D
be supported on
(x_{1},1)
and
(x_{2},0)
with
\mathbb{P}(x=x_{1})=\mathbb{P}(x=x_{2})=0.5
. Then the true distributional predictor for variance
\gamma_{\text{var}}
predicts
\gamma_{\text{var}}(x_{1})=\gamma_{\text{var}}(x_{2})=0
, where as
\Gamma(D|\gamma_{\text{var}}(x)=0)=0.5
is the variance of a
\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)
random variable. Compare this to mean multicalibration, where the true distributional predictor
\gamma_{\text{mean}}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[y|x]

\gamma

\Gamma

Subsequent work by
38
established a remarkable connection between multicalibration and property elicitation
(
34
;
46
)
. Formally, a property
\Gamma
is
elicitable
if there exists a loss function
\ell:[0,1]\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
whose minimizer in expectation over
P
yields the true property value
\Gamma(P)
, i.e.,
\Gamma(P)\in\argmin_{\gamma\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[\ell(\gamma,y)]
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
(we say
\ell
elicits
\Gamma
). For instance, the squared loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-y)^{2}
elicits the mean, where as the
\ell_{1}
-loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert\gamma-y\right\rvert
elicits the median; more generally, the
pinball
loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=(\gamma-y)\left((1-q)\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-q\mathbb{I}{\left[y>\gamma\right]}\right)
elicits the
q
-th quantile for
q\in[0,1]
(median corresponds to
q=0.5
). A striking result due to
38
is that a property
\Gamma
can be multicalibrated if and only if
\Gamma
is elicitable. In both the distributional and online settings,
38
propose canonical algorithms for achieving multicalibration for an elicitable property
\Gamma
. The result of
38
builds on the following key characterization of property elicitation established by
46
: a property
\Gamma
is elicitable if and only if there exists an
identification
function for
\Gamma
— a function
V:[0,1]\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[V(\gamma,y)]=0\iff\gamma=\Gamma(P)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. In other words, the true property value
\Gamma(P)
is a root of the expected identification function for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, e.g., for mean
V(\gamma,y)=\gamma-y
, for median
V(\gamma,y)=\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-\frac{1}{2}
, where as for the
q
-th quantile
V(\gamma,y)=\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-q
. More generally, for an elicitable property
\Gamma
,
V(\gamma,y)=\frac{\partial\ell(\gamma,y)}{\partial\gamma}
, where
\ell
is the loss function that elicits
\Gamma
. Based on the concept of an identification function, we generalize the definition of online multicalibration for an elicitable property beyond group membership functions and introduce swap multicalibration. Specifically, for an elicitable property
\Gamma
with identification function
V
(without any loss of generality, we assume
\text{Range}(V)=[-1,1]
since the loss that elicits a property can be scaled appropriately),
r\geq 1
, and a hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subset[-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}
, we define the
\ell_{r}
-multicalibration and
\ell_{r}
-swap multicalibration errors incurred by the forecaster respectively as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{MCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert^{r},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert^{r}.

(4)

Calibration with a property
\Gamma
(
\mathsf{Cal}_{\Gamma,r}

\mathcal{F}=\{1\}
, therefore is non-contextual.
When

is the mean (and thus

\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{F},n)=\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n))
, where
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)
is the sequential Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}
over
n
rounds of interaction between an algorithm and adversary. Therefore, whenever
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
, we obtain
\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{F},n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F}))
and
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{r+1}})
. Both the linear class and finite
\mathcal{F}
satisfy
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
with
\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{O}(1)
for the linear class and
\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F})=\log\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert
for the finite class, however, importantly, they admit an explicit algorithm that achieves
\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{F},n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F}))
. For hypothesis classes (beyond the above two classes) for which
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Comp}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
, we refer to
44
.

When instantiated for means, the above set of results not only completely resolves the open question raised in
17
on the possibility of an oracle-efficient algorithm that achieves
\mathsf{MCal}_{\text{mean},2}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
, but also shows that it is possible to achieve a much stronger guarantee
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\text{mean},2}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
. More generally, for an elicitable property
\Gamma
,
Algorithm
2
achieves
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
, thereby improving upon several previous works
38
;
17
;
18
;
35
.

Remark 1
.

For
\ell_{1}
-multicalibration, a recent work
18
proposed an inefficient algorithm that achieves
\mathsf{MCal}_{\text{mean},1}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
for a finite class
\mathcal{F}
(see also
37
for a different approach to obtain the same result) and an oracle-efficient algorithm that achieves
\mathsf{MCal}_{\text{mean},1}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
for a general hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
. Since
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,1}(\mathcal{F})\leq\sqrt{T\cdot\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,2}(\mathcal{F})}
, we achieve
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\text{mean},1}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
in an oracle-efficient manner, thereby also significantly improving upon
37
;
18
.

2
Technical Overview

We provide a technical overview of our work in this section.

2.1
Warm up with
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean, 2}}
: A new general approach

We begin by considering the simpler problem of online
\ell_{2}
-calibration for means. Prior works for calibrating means achieve
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
by either using the equivalence between
\ell_{2}
-calibration and swap regret of the squared loss
(
36
;
14
)
or via the concept of calibeating
(
15
)
. However, in
Lemma
7
in the appendix, we show that when calibrating
q
-th quantiles, the
\ell_{2}
-quantile calibration error cannot be bounded by any strictly increasing and invertible function
\zeta
of the swap regret of any loss function, thereby rendering the swap regret-based approach futile. Furthermore, the calibeating-based approach also does not readily generalize beyond means.

To circumvent this, we discover yet another approach to achieve
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
, which, unlike the previous approaches, can be directly generalized to properties beyond means.
Specifically, we observe that to achieve
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
, it suffices to
derive a high probability bound of the form

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{n_{i}}+\frac{n_{i}}{N}\right),

(6)

where we have ignored dependence on the failure probability
\delta
for simplicity.
Before explaining why this is sufficient, we first point out that
(
6
) is a natural goal due to the following interpretation: if the forecaster could witness the conditional distribution of
y_{t}
(e.g., if the adversary moves first instead and at every time
t\in[T]
, it reveals the conditional distribution of
y_{t}
),
then a simple truthful strategy which computes
\tilde{p}_{t}
as the conditional expectation of
y_{t}

and predicts
p_{t}=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lvert z-\tilde{p}_{t}\right\rvert
by rounding
\tilde{p}_{t}
to the nearest point in
\mathcal{Z}
achieves (
6
). This is because, such a forecasting strategy has to pay for the rounding error
\frac{1}{N}
per round for
n_{i}
rounds, and a variance term
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n_{i}})
due to not predicting the true label but rather its expectation.
Of course, since the forecaster has to decide
p_{t}
without any knowledge about
y_{t}
, it cannot implement this strategy, however, the discussion points towards the possibility that (
6
) might be achievable.

Notably, (
6
) indeed implies that
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
with high probability. This is because,

n_{i}\left(\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right)^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(1+\frac{n_{i}}{N^{2}}\right),

which when summed over
i
yields
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\sum_{i\in[N]}n_{i}\left(\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right)^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(N+\frac{T}{N^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
on choosing the discretization size
N=\Theta(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
. More generally, for
r>2
, we obtain

n_{i}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert^{r}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n_{i}^{1-r/2}+\frac{n_{i}}{N^{r}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(1+\frac{n_{i}}{N^{r}}\right),

and thus
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},r}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(N+\frac{T}{N^{r}}\right)=\tilde{\Theta}(T^{\frac{1}{r+1}})
on choosing
N=\Theta(T^{\frac{1}{r+1}})
.
Furthermore, this approach is readily generalizable to an arbitrary elicitable property
\Gamma
with an identification function
V
, by simply replacing
y_{t}-p_{t}
in (
6
) with
V(p_{t},y_{t})
.

So how can one achieve (
6
)?
In fact, a recent work by
31
almost achieves (
6
)
in their algorithm designed for a different problem of simultaneous swap regret minimization. The only caveat is that their algorithm only ensures an in expectation bound on
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert
,
which, as one can verify, is not sufficient to achieve
\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
or even
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Cal}_{\text{mean},2}]=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{1}{3}})
. Since the algorithm of
31
uses the expert algorithm McMwC of
(
8
)
as a subroutine, a natural approach to achieve the desired high probability bound would be to obtain a high probability external regret guarantee for McMwC, which is unfortunately mentioned as an open problem in
8
and is still unsolved to the best of our knowledge.

Despite this fact, it turns out that (
6
) can still be achieved in a simple manner that is agnostic to the high probability external regret guarantee of McMwC, which is quite surprising. Towards achieving this, we propose two simplifications to the algorithm of
31
, the first of which is critical for achieving (
6
) and the other is important for
subsequent developments in the contextual setting (
Subsection
2.2
). We start with a brief introduction of the algorithm of
31
. For each
i\in[N-1]
, let
\mathcal{I}_{i}=\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)
and
\mathcal{I}_{N}=\left[\frac{N-1}{N},1\right]
, so that
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{N}
represents a partition of
[0,1]
.
Informally, their algorithm defines a
2N
-expert problem corresponding to a careful choice of the gain function
\ell_{i,\sigma}(p,y)\coloneqq\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(p-y)
for each expert
(i,\sigma)\in[N]\times\{\pm 1\}
. At each time
t\in[T]
, the algorithm obtains a probability distribution
\{w_{t,i,\sigma}\}
over the experts via an expert problem subroutine
\mathsf{ALG}
, which is instantiated to be the McMwC algorithm. Subsequently, it hedges against the experts by defining a function
h_{t}(P)
that depends on the weights
\{w_{t,i,\sigma}\}
and the gains
\{\ell_{i,\sigma}\}
as (with
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
being the filtration generated by the random variables
p_{1},\dots,p_{t-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{t-1}
)

\displaystyle h_{t}(P)\coloneqq\max_{y\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P}\left[\sum_{(i,\sigma)}w_{t,i,\sigma}\ell_{i,\sigma}(p,y)|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right],

obtains a distribution
P_{t}
satisfying
h_{t}(P_{t})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
, samples
\tilde{p}_{t}\sim P_{t}
, and forecasts
p_{t}=z_{i_{t}}
, where
i_{t}\in[N]
is such that
\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{t}}
. Finally, after observing
y_{t}
, we feed the gain
\ell_{t,i,\sigma}\coloneqq\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})
for each
(i,\sigma)
to
\mathsf{ALG}
. Our proposed modifications are as follows:

1.

Instead of feeding the loss
\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})
, we feed a modified loss
\ell_{t,i,\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}[\ell_{i,\sigma}(p,y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
for each
(i,\sigma)
.

2.

While
31
argue that a distribution
P_{t}
satisfying
h_{t}(P_{t})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
can be obtained in
\text{poly}(T)
time by using the FTPL-based approach suggested by
37
, we provide a simple closed-form construction of
P_{t}
that is supported on at most two points, following a similar idea utilized in
26
;
40
. To state our construction, we define the function
\Phi_{t}:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
as
{\Phi_{t}(p)\coloneqq\sum_{(i,\sigma)}w_{t,i,\sigma}\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}}
. If
\Phi_{t}(0)>0
, we choose
P_{t}
that is only supported on
0
; else if
\Phi_{t}(1)\leq 0
, we choose
P_{t}
that is only supported on
1
; else we choose
i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
such that
\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\leq 0
and
P_{t}
such that

\displaystyle P_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)=\frac{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert},\quad P_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)=\frac{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}.

To see why the first modification leads to (
6
), we realize the following:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(\tilde{p}_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})+\frac{n_{i}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i,\sigma}+\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})-\ell_{t,i,\sigma}+\frac{n_{i}}{N},

where the second inequality follows from the definition of
\ell_{i,\sigma}
and
\left\lvert\tilde{p}_{t}-p_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{N}
. Since the McMwC algorithm receives gain
\ell_{t,i,\sigma}
for each
(i,\sigma)
, it follows from the regret guarantee of McMwC that (with
\mathbb{P}_{t}[.]\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(.|\mathscr{F}_{t-1})
)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i,\sigma}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}w_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\ell_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i,\sigma}^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}\right),

where the equality follows since for each
t
,
\sum_{(i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}w_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\ell_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\leq h_{t}(P_{t})={\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{T})
and
\ell_{t,i,\sigma}^{2}\leq\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
by the definition of
\ell_{t,i,\sigma}
. Next, observe that the sequence
X_{t}\coloneqq\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})-\ell_{t,i,\sigma}=\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}[\ell_{i,\sigma}(p,y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
is a martingale difference sequence (conditioned on
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
, the random variables
\tilde{p}_{t}
(or
p_{t}
) and
y_{t}
are independent) with cumulative variance
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\ell_{i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})^{2}]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
, where
\mathbb{E}_{t}[.]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[.|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
. Therefore, applying Freedman’s inequality (
Lemma
6
), we obtain
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}\right)
with high probability. Combining everything,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(y_{t}-p_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}+\frac{n_{i}}{N}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{n_{i}}+\frac{n_{i}}{N}\right),

since
\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}_{i})
with high probability by applying Freedman’s inequality again to the sequence
Z_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})-\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}
.
This thus achieves (
6
) without the need of getting a high-probability version of the regret guarantee of McMwC.

We remark again that while the second modification has not been used yet in the above discussion, it will subsequently be quite helpful in the contextual setting, where we utilize it to obtain tail bounds on the sum of a sequence that is not necessarily a martingale difference sequence. Moreover, we do not flesh out details of the above discussion in the paper since it is a special case of the contextual setting (
Subsection
4.1
) and
Lemma
2
is a generalized version of (
6
).

2.2
An inefficient generalization to the contextual setting

By combining the two modifications above,
we generalize to an arbitrary elicitable property and also to the contextual setting where the forecaster receives a context
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, predicts
p_{t}\in[0,1]
, and finally observes
y_{t}\sim Y_{t}
, where
Y_{t}
is a distribution over
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[0,1]
that is chosen by the adversary. In
Subsection
4.1
, we propose an inefficient algorithm (
Algorithm
1
), which for a fixed
r\geq 2
, finite
\mathcal{F}
, and an elicitable property
\Gamma
with an identification function
V
so that the marginal
V(.,Y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[V(.,y)]
is

\rho
-Lipschitz (
Assumption
1
), achieves

\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left({\rho^{r}T^{\frac{1}{r+1}}+T^{\frac{1}{r+1}}\left(\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}}}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta
(
Theorem
2
).
Algorithm
1
defines a
2N\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert
-expert problem corresponding to a choice of the gain function
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x,y)\coloneqq\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x)V(p,y)
for each
(f,i,\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\times[N]\times\{\pm 1\}
. As mentioned in
Subsection
2.1
above, at each time
t\in[T]
, the algorithm hedges against the experts by defining the function

h_{t}(P)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P}\left[\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x_{t},y)|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right],

where
\{w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\}
is the probability distribution over experts as output by the expert problem subroutine
\mathsf{ALG}
.
Subsequently, it obtains a distribution
P_{t}
satisfying
h_{t}(P_{t})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
in a similar manner as
Subsection
2.1
, samples
\tilde{p}_{t}\sim P_{t}
, and forecasts
p_{t}=\frac{i_{t}}{N}
, where
i_{t}\in[N]
is the unique index such that
\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{t}}
. Finally, after observing
y_{t}
, we feed the gain
{\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}\left[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]}
for each
(f,i,\sigma)
to
\mathsf{ALG}
.

Although the proposed modifications
and generalization to the algorithm of
31
are simple, their combination with a martingale-based analysis using Freedman’s inequality (
Lemma
6
) in the style of
35
results in a substantially different and technical proof compared to both
31
and
35
. Moreover, the central idea in
35
;
17
is a reduction of swap multicalibration to contextual swap regret, which is quite different from our approach. As a highlight of our proof, we utilize our explicit construction of
P_{t}
to obtain tail bounds on
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert
for a non-martingale sequence
\{W_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. To elaborate on this further, we provide a detailed proof sketch of
Theorem
2
here. Similar to calibration for means (
Subsection
2.1
), we begin by deriving a high probability bound on the quantity
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert
that holds for all
i\in[N]
(
Lemma
2
), in the following manner:

(i)

Fix
i\in[N],f\in\mathcal{F}
, and failure probability
\delta
. Recall that
n_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}
is the number of time instants the prediction made is
p_{t}=z_{i}
, or equivalently
\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
. It follows from the triangle inequality that
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert
, where the sequences
\{X_{t}\},\{U_{t}\},\{Z_{t}\}
, and
\{W_{t}\}
are defined as

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot\left(V(z_{i},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(z_{i},y_{t})]\right),

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot(\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(z_{i},y_{t})]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})]),

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t}),

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot(\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})]-V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})).

In the next steps, we discuss our approach to bound the sum of each individual sequence. As already mentioned before, conditioned on
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
, the random variables
p_{t}
(or
\tilde{p}_{t}
) and
y_{t}
are independent. Since
V(.,Y_{t})
is
\rho
-Lipschitz and
\left\lvert p_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{N}
, we immediately obtain
\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{N}
.

(ii)

Observe that
\{X_{t}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with
\left\lvert X_{t}\right\rvert\leq 2
. Applying Freedman’s inequality carefully over a dyadic partition of the interval
\left[\tilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\sqrt{T}}\right),\frac{1}{2}\right]
, similar to
(
35
, Lemma 8)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

(iii)

Next, we bound
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert
. It follows from the definition of the gain function
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}+\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}].

Invoking the regret guarantee of
\mathsf{ALG}
(
Lemma
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N)+\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N)}\right).

Moreover,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}(P_{t})=\mathcal{O}(1)
. For a fixed
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
, the sequence
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
is a martingale difference sequence. Repeating a similar analysis as that done for the sequence
\{X_{t}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}\right).

(iv)

Towards bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert
, we observe that the sequence
\{W_{t}\}
is not necessarily a martingale difference sequence, due to the indicator
\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}
. However, using the explicit form of
P_{t}
(thanks to our second modification to
31
) and
\rho
-Lipschitzness of
V(.,Y_{t})
, we argue that
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
. Thereafter, we consider the sequence
\{W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\}
, which is clearly a martingale difference sequence, and bound
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert
in an exactly same manner as
Step
(ii)
. Since
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

(v)

Finally, we relate
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
with
n_{i}
by bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})-\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}\right\rvert
. To achieve so, we consider the sequence
\left\{\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})-\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}\right\}
, which is again a martingale difference sequence. Proceeding similarly as
Step
(ii)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}-\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}),\beta=\log\frac{1}{\delta}
, and
\gamma>0
be the constant (including logarithmic terms) hidden in the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(.)
notation. Then the equation in the display above can be expressed as
\left\lvert n_{i}-\alpha\right\rvert\leq\gamma(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}+\beta)
. Solving for
\alpha
, we obtain
\alpha\leq\gamma\sqrt{\beta}+\sqrt{n_{i}}+\sqrt{\gamma\beta}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\sqrt{n_{i}}\right)
.

Combining everything and taking a union bound over all
i\in[N],f\in\mathcal{F}
, we obtain

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\rho n_{i}}{N}+\sqrt{n_{i}\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. The result of
Theorem
2
then follows by directly plugging the above bound in the definition of
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})
(
4
).

2.3
An oracle efficient algorithm

Finally, in
Subsection
4.2
, we propose an oracle-efficient algorithm (
Algorithm
2
) by reducing to online agnostic learning. Our oracle-efficient algorithm builds on
Algorithm
1
and achieves efficiency by using
\mathsf{OAL}

.
We remark that while the idea of making

1
oracle-efficient is adopted from
40

As mentioned briefly in
Subsection

, instead of enumerating over each

,

instantiates

output by

where

. Thereafter, we obtain a distribution

satisfying

and make a prediction

in a similar way as before. After observing

\ell_{t,i,\sigma}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}[\ell_{t,i,\sigma}(p,y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
for each

to
\mathsf{ALG}
and a feedback outcome

to
\mathsf{OAL}_{i_{t},\sigma}
for each

.

Next, we provide a proof sketch of the swap multicalibration guarantee (

1
) of
Algorithm
2
. Let
\kappa_{t,i,\sigma}\coloneqq\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}V(p_{t},y_{t})
.
Fix a

and failure probability
\delta
. We begin by realizing that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\kappa_{t,i,\sigma}f(x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\kappa_{t,i,\sigma}q_{t,i,\sigma}(x_{t})+{\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{F},n_{i})},

where the last inequality follows from
Definition
1
.
For the next step, we bound
\sup_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\kappa_{t,i,\sigma}q_{t,i,\sigma}(x_{t})
by proceeding similar to the proof sketch in

2.2
, which eventually leads to

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\rho n_{i}}{N}+\sqrt{n_{i}\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}+{\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{F},n_{i})}\right)

for all

with probability at least
1-\delta

then follows by plugging the above bound to the definition of
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})
(
4
).

Remark 2

(Oracle comparison)
.

Note that compared to

, which assumes an online squared error regression oracle for

and

, which assumes an offline optimization (multicalibration evaluation) oracle, we assume an online agnostic learner for

Since multicalibration is generally considered a stronger notion compared to omniprediction

)

Remark 3

(Lower Bounds)

While our results imply an efficient algorithm that achieves

3.1
Property Elicitation

Let the label space be
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[0,1]
and
\mathcal{P}
be a family of probability distributions supported over
\mathcal{Y}
. We view a property
\Gamma:\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
as a mapping from a distribution to a scalar value.
A loss function
\ell:[0,1]\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
takes as input a prediction
\gamma
about the property value, a label
y
, and maps it to a real number. We say that a property
\Gamma
is
elicitable
if there exists a loss function that is minimized at the true property value. This is formalized in the following definition, which is central to our work.

Definition 2

(Strictly consistent loss function, Property elicitation)
.

Fix a property
\Gamma
, a family of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}
. A loss function
\ell
is strictly
\mathcal{P}
-consistent if
\Gamma(P)\in\argmin_{\gamma\in[0,1]}\ell(\gamma,P)

for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\ell(\gamma,P)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[\ell(\gamma,y)]
. We also say that
\ell
elicits
\Gamma

and that
\Gamma
is elicitable if there exists a strictly
\mathcal{P}
-consistent loss function.

In other words,
\ell
is strictly
\mathcal{P}
-consistent if for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the true property value
\Gamma(P)
can be obtained by minimizing the expected loss of
\ell
over samples drawn from
P
. We provide several examples of elicitable properties:

•

The squared loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-y)^{2}
elicits the mean; more generally,
\ell(\gamma,y)=(\gamma-y^{k})^{2}
elicits the
raw moment

\mathbb{E}[y^{k}]
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

The
\ell_{1}
-loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert\gamma-y\right\rvert
elicits the median; more generally, the pinball loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=(\gamma-y)\left((1-q)\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-q\mathbb{I}{\left[y>\gamma\right]}\right)
elicits the
q
-th quantile for
q\in[0,1]
.

•

For a discrete distribution with finite support, the
0-1
loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\mathbb{I}{\left[\gamma\neq y\right]}
elicits the
mode
.

•

For a fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
, the loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=\frac{1}{2}(y-\gamma)^{2}\left\lvert\tau-\mathbb{I}{\left[\gamma-y\geq 0\right]}\right\rvert
elicits the
expectile
,
which corresponds to a
t
that satisfies
\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left\lvert t-z\right\rvert d\mu(z)=\tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lvert t-z\right\rvert d\mu(z)
, where
\mu
is the cumulative distribution function of
y
.

•

Let
P
be a distribution supported over
\{0,1\}
and
\Gamma(P)=\mathbb{P}(y=1)
. A loss
\ell:[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
proper
if
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\gamma}[\ell(\gamma,y)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\gamma}[\ell(\gamma^{\prime},y)]
for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in[0,1]
, e.g., squared loss, log loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=-y\log\gamma-(1-y)\log(1-\gamma)
, spherical loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=-\frac{\gamma y+(1-\gamma)(1-y)}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+(1-\gamma)^{2}}}
, Tsallis entropy
\ell(\gamma,y)=(\alpha-1)\gamma^{\alpha}-\alpha\gamma^{\alpha-1}y
for
\alpha>1
, etc. More generally, given any concave function
f(\gamma)
, the loss
\ell(\gamma,y)=f(\gamma)+\left\langle\partial f(\gamma),y-\gamma\right\rangle
is proper
20
, where
\partial f
represents a supergradient of
f
. Clearly, any proper loss
\ell
elicits
\Gamma
by definition.

Next, we define the notion of an identification function for a property, which intuitively measures the overestimate/underestimate of the true property value.

Definition 3

(Identification function)
.

Fix a property
\Gamma
and a space of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}
. A function
V:[0,1]\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
\mathcal{P}
-identification function for
\Gamma
if
V(\gamma,P)=0\iff\gamma=\Gamma(P)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, where
V(\gamma,P)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[V(\gamma,y)]
. We say that
\Gamma
is identifiable if there exists a
\mathcal{P}
-identification function for
\Gamma
.

As can be concluded from the definition, for any distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the true property value
\Gamma(P)
is a root of the expected identification function over samples drawn from
P
. For example,
V(\gamma,y)=\gamma-y
is an identification function for mean, whereas
V(\gamma,y)=\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-0.5
is an identification function for median; more generally,
V(\gamma,y)=\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq\gamma\right]}-q
is an identification function for the
q
-th quantile.

A seminal result due to
46
is that under mild technical assumptions on the family
\mathcal{P}
and the property
\Gamma
, the following conditions are equivalent: (a)
\Gamma
is elicitable; (b) there exists a strictly consistent loss function for
\Gamma
; (c) there exists an identification function for
\Gamma
; (d) the level sets
\Gamma_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{P};\Gamma(P)=\alpha\}
of
\Gamma
are convex. An immediate consequence of the characterization is that it allows us to identify several properties that are not elicitable. For example, consider the conditional value at risk (also known as expected shortfall), which for a target quantile
q\in[0,1]
is defined as
\text{CVaR}_{q}(P)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}[y|y>f_{q}(P)]
, where
f_{q}(P)
represents the
q
-th quantile of
P
. CVaR is of central significance in financial risk assesment, however, is not elicitable since its level sets are not convex
21
. Similarly, when
y
is a binary random variable and
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all Bernoulli distributions with mean
p
for
p\in[0,1]
, the variance has non-convex level sets, therefore is not elicitable.

3.2
Problem Setting

Let
\Gamma
be an elicitable property. Following
38
, we consider the following online learning protocol: at each time
t=1,\dots,T
, (a) the adversary selects a feature vector
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
and a distribution
Y_{t}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
over the label space
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\{0,1\}
, and reveals
x_{t}
; (b) the forecaster randomly predicts
p_{t}\in[0,1]
; (c) the adversary reveals
y_{t}\sim Y_{t}
. Throughout, (a) we assume that the adversary is adaptive, i.e.,
x_{t},Y_{t}
are chosen with complete knowledge about the forecaster’s algorithm and
p_{1},\dots,p_{t-1}
, and (b) the forecaster predicts
p_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}\subset[0,1]
for all
t\in[T]
, where
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
is a finite discretization of
[0,1]
and
z_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
for all
i\in[N]
. We let
\mathbb{E}_{t}[.],\mathbb{P}_{t}[.]
represent the conditional expectation, probability respectively, where the conditioning is over all randomness till time
t-1
(inclusive). Formally, letting
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
be the filtration generated by the random variables
p_{1},\dots,p_{t-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{t-1}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{t}[.]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[.|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}],\mathbb{P}_{t}[.]\coloneqq\mathbb{P}[.|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
respectively.

For a property
\Gamma
, an identification function
V:[0,1]\times\mathcal{Y}\to[-1,1]

takes as input the forecaster’s prediction
p
, the adversary’s chosen outcome
y
and maps it to a real number in
[-1,1]
(assumed without any loss of generality). Similar to
38
, we assume that the marginal
V(p,Y_{t})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y_{t}}[V(p,y)|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
is
\rho
-Lipschitz in
p
.

Assumption 1
.

The function
V(p,Y_{t})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lvert V(p_{2},Y_{t})-V(p_{1},Y_{t})\right\rvert\leq\rho\left\lvert p_{2}-p_{1}\right\rvert\text{ for all }0\leq p_{1},p_{2}\leq 1.

(7)

Furthermore,
V(0,Y)\leq 0
and
V(1,Y)\geq 0
for all
Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
.

For specific instantiations of (
7
), observe that for means
V(p,Y_{t})=p-\mathbb{E}_{t}[y]
is trivially
1
-Lipschitz in
p
, whereas for quantiles
V(p,Y_{t})=\mathbb{P}_{t}(y\leq p)-q
and (
7
) corresponds to the assumption that the CDF of
Y_{t}
is
\rho
-Lipschitz, which is a standard assumption invoked in the relevant literature on quantile calibration
(
33
;
17
)
. The second condition in
Assumption
1
is without any loss of generality; it is clearly true for means and quantiles and more generally can be interpreted as an underestimate/overestimate of the true property value. For instance, for expectiles,
V(p,y)=(p-y)(1-\tau)
if
p\geq y
and
V(p,y)=(p-y)\tau
if
p\leq y
. Clearly,
V(p,Y)
is Lipschitz in
p
and satisfies
V(0,Y)\leq 0
and
V(1,Y)\geq 0
for all
Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. Similarly,
Assumption
1
can be verified to be true for raw moments,
\Gamma(P)=\mathbb{P}(y=1)
under specific proper losses such as Tsallis entropy with
\alpha\geq 3
, spherical loss, etc.

For a bounded hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}\subset[-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}
,
\ell_{r}
-multicalibration (
\mathsf{MCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})
) and
\ell_{r}
-swap multicalibration (
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})
) errors
(r\geq 1)
are given by (
3
) and (
4
) respectively.
The goal of the forecaster is to make predictions
p_{1},\dots,p_{T}
such that
\mathsf{SMCal}_{\Gamma,r}(\mathcal{F})
is minimized.

3.3
Regret Minimization

As mentioned, regret minimization algorithms are important subroutines of our final algorithm.
Consider the following interaction between an algorithm and adversary over
T
rounds: at each time
t\in[T]
, the algorithm takes an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
and simultaneously the adversary reveals an outcome
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
; the algorithm observes
y_{t}
and incurs loss
\ell(a_{t},y_{t})
for a known loss function
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. The
external regret

\mathsf{Reg}\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(a,y_{t})
compares the cumulative loss incurred by the algorithm with that incurred by the best fixed action chosen in hindsight. Compared to external regret,
swap regret
is a stronger benchmark that compares the total loss incurred by the algorithm with that of the best swap (or modification) function
\nu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
chosen in hindsight, i.e.,
\mathsf{SReg}\coloneqq\sup_{\nu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(\nu(a_{t}),y_{t})
.

Expert problem.

The
K
-expert problem is a canonical problem in online learning, where at each time
t\in[T]
, an online algorithm outputs a probability distribution
w_{t}\in\Delta([K])

over the experts and subsequently the adversary reveals a gain (or reward) vector
\ell_{t}\in[-1,1]^{K}
, where
\ell_{t}(i)
is the gain incurred by expert
i
. The regret
\mathsf{Reg}\coloneqq\max_{i^{\star}\in[K]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(i^{\star})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle w_{t},\ell_{t}\right\rangle
measures the difference between the gain of the best fixed expert chosen in hindsight and the expected gain of the algorithm.

Sequential Rademacher Complexity.

A
\mathcal{X}
-valued binary tree is one in which all nodes have values that lie in
\mathcal{X}
. For a
\mathcal{X}
-valued tree
\boldsymbol{x}
with depth
n
, a path from the root to a leaf can be represented as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})
, where
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
and
-1,+1
correspond to traversing left, right respectively (
\epsilon_{n}
is clearly irrelevant). Moreover,
\boldsymbol{x}
can be represented by a sequence of mappings
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}:\{-1,1\}^{i-1}\to\mathcal{X}
represents the value of a node at the
i
-th level of
\boldsymbol{x}
. For notational convenience, we represent
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}({\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{i-1}})
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
, with the understanding that
\epsilon_{i},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
are irrelevant when describing a node at the
i
-th level.

Definition 4

(Sequential Rademacher Complexity)
.

For a hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
and a
\mathcal{X}
-valued tree
\boldsymbol{x}
with depth
n
, the conditional sequential Rademacher complexity
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n;\boldsymbol{x})
is defined as

\displaystyle\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n;\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))\right],

where the expectation is taken over the uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}\times\dots\times\{-1,1\}
.
The (unconditional) sequential Rademacher complexity
\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)
is then defined as

\displaystyle\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n)\coloneqq\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}}\Re^{\text{seq}}(\mathcal{F},n;\boldsymbol{x}),

where the supremum is taken over all
\mathcal{X}
-valued trees of depth
n
.

Similar to the role of Rademacher complexity in describing the learnability of a hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
in the distributional setting, the sequential Rademacher complexity characterizes learnability in the online setting.

4
Algorithm

In this section, we propose an algorithm for multicalibrating an elicitable property
\Gamma
with an identification function
V
that satisfies
Assumption
1
. We first propose an inefficient algorithm and subsequently propose an efficient algorithm by reducing to online agnostic learning.

4.1
Achieving Multicalibration Inefficiently

As mentioned in
Section
1
, we build upon the algorithm proposed by
31
in a different context. For each
i\in[N-1]
, let
\mathcal{I}_{i}=\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)
and
\mathcal{I}_{N}=\left[\frac{N-1}{N},1\right]
so that
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{N}
represents a partition of
[0,1]
. We consider a
2N\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert
-expert problem
where for each
f\in\mathcal{F},i\in[N]
, and
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
, we define the gain function
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[-1,1]

corresponding to the expert characterized by the tuple
(f,i,\sigma)
as
{\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x,y)\coloneqq\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x)V(p,y)}
. At each time
t
,
Algorithm
1
obtains a probability distribution
\{w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\}
over the
2\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N
experts via an expert problem subroutine

\mathsf{ALG}
, where
w_{t,f,i,\sigma}
is the probability allotted to the expert corresponding to
(f,i,\sigma)
. As shall be shown in
Proposition
1
, in
Line
4
we obtain a distribution
P_{t}
satisfying
h_{t}(P_{t})\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
, where the function
h_{t}:\Delta([0,1])\to[-1,1]
is defined as

{h_{t}(P)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P}\left[\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x_{t},y)|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]}.

Subsequently,
Algorithm
1
samples
\tilde{p}_{t}\sim P_{t}
and predicts
p_{t}=\frac{i_{t}}{N}
, where
i_{t}\in[N]
is such that
\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{t}}
. Finally, on observing
y_{t}
, we feed the gain
{\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}\left[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,y_{t},x_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]}
for each
(f,i,\sigma)
to
\mathsf{ALG}
. A subtle difference between
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}
and
\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}
is that the latter represents the actual gain fed to
\mathsf{ALG}
, while the former is an auxiliary function used to define the latter.

Algorithm 1

Multicalibration for an Elicitable Property (Computationally Inefficient Version)

Initialize:
An expert problem subroutine
\mathsf{ALG}
;

1:

for

t=1,\dots,T,

2:

Receive context
x_{t}
;

3:

Obtain weights
\{w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\}
from
\mathsf{ALG}
;

4:

Define the function
\Phi_{t}:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
as

{\Phi_{t}(p)\coloneqq\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\sigma\mathbb{I}{\left[p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})}.

If
\Phi_{t}(0)>0
, choose
P_{t}
that is only supported on
0
; else if
\Phi_{t}(1)\leq 0
, choose
P_{t}
that is only supported on
1
; else choose
i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
such that
\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\leq 0
and
P_{t}
such that

\displaystyle P_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)=\frac{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert},\quad P_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)=\frac{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}.

5:

Sample
\tilde{p}_{t}\sim P_{t}
and predict
p_{t}=z_{i_{t}}=\frac{i_{t}}{N}
, where
i_{t}\in[N]
is such that
\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{t}}
;

6:

Observe
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
;

7:

For each
(f,i,\sigma)
, feed
{\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t}}\left[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(p,x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]}
to
\mathsf{ALG}
;

Proposition 1
.

Algorithm
1
satisfies
h_{t}(P_{t})\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
for all
t\in[T]
.

Proof.

When
\Phi_{t}(0)>0
, we have

h_{t}(P_{t})=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}\left[\Phi_{t}(0)V(0,y)\right]=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\Phi_{t}(0)V(0,Y)\leq 0,

where the inequality follows from
Assumption
1
. Similarly, when
\Phi_{t}(1)\leq 0
, we have

h_{t}(P_{t})=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[\Phi_{t}(1)V(1,y)]=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\Phi_{t}(1)V(1,Y)\leq 0.

For the last case, we have

\displaystyle h_{t}(P_{t})

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{E}_{p\sim P_{t},y\sim Y}\left[\Phi_{t}(p)V(p,y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}\left[\frac{\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert V\left(\frac{i}{T},y\right)}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}+\frac{\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert V\left(\frac{i+1}{T},y\right)}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{Y\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\left[\frac{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i}{T}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{i+1}{T}\right)\right\rvert}\cdot\left\lvert V\left(\frac{i}{T},Y\right)-V\left(\frac{i+1}{T},Y\right)\right\rvert\right]\leq\frac{\rho}{T},

where the last inequality follows since due to the Lipschitzness of
V(.,Y)
(
7
) and that
\Phi_{t}
takes value in
[-1,1]
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Instantiating
\mathsf{ALG}
with the McMwC algorithm
(
8
)
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The McMwC algorithm
(
8
)
ensures the following regret bound

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(f^{\prime},i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}w_{t,f^{\prime},i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\ell_{t,f^{\prime},i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert NT)+\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert NT)}\right)

against each expert
(f,i,\sigma)
simultaneously.

Note that a canonical expert minimization algorithm such as Multiplicative Weights Update would incur
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret against each expert, which is not sufficient to achieve the sub-
\sqrt{T}
rates that we seek for in this paper. Instead, the McMwC algorithm ensures a regret bound that depends on
\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}
, which can be potentially much smaller than
\sqrt{T}
.
Moreover, compared to
(
31
, Lemma 5.4)
which incurs a factor proportional to
\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}}
, we incur a factor of
\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}
instead, since McMwC actually ensures a regret bound
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}^{2}}\right)
(ignoring additive constants) against the expert
(f,i,\sigma)
. For our choice of
\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}
, it follows that
\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}^{2}\leq\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
, thereby introducing a different factor.

Lemma 2
.

Algorithm
1
ensures that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\rho n_{i}}{N}+\sqrt{n_{i}\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}\right)

for all
i\in[N]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Recall that
n_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}
is the number of time instants the prediction made is
p_{t}=z_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
. Fixing a
i\in[N],f\in\mathcal{F}
, it follows
from the triangle inequality that

\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=z_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(p_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert,

where the sequences
\{X_{t}\},\{U_{t}\},\{Z_{t}\}
, and
\{W_{t}\}
are defined as

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot\left(V(z_{i},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(z_{i},y_{t})]\right),

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot(\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(z_{i},y_{t})]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})]),

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t}),

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\cdot(\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})]-V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})).

Note that conditioned on
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
, the random variables
p_{t}
(or
\tilde{p}_{t}
) and
y_{t}
are independent. In the next steps, we bound the sum of each sequence individually.

Bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_{t}\right\rvert
.

Since
V(.,Y_{t})
is
\rho
-Lipschitz and
\left\lvert p_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{N}
, we immediately obtain
\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{N}
.

Bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert
.

Clearly,
\left\lvert X_{t}\right\rvert\leq 2
and
\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t}]=0
; thus,
\{X_{t}\}
is a bounded martingale difference sequence. Furthermore, the cumulative variance
V_{X}\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]
can be bounded as
V_{X}\leq 4\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
. Therefore, for a fixed
\mu\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, it follows from
Lemma
6
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert\leq 4\mu\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+\frac{1}{\mu}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

(8)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
For the subsequent steps, we assume that
m\in\mathbb{N}
is such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{m}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{T}}<\frac{1}{2^{m-1}},

(9)

and partition the interval
\mathcal{J}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{T}},\frac{1}{2}\right]
into dyadic intervals as
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}_{m}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{J}_{1}\cup\mathcal{J}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{J}_{k}=\left[\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right)
for all
k\in[m-1],\mathcal{J}_{0}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\right\}
, and
\mathcal{J}_{m}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{T}},\frac{1}{2^{m}}\right)
. For each interval
\mathcal{J}_{k}
, we associate a parameter
\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}
. Taking a union bound over all
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert\leq 4\mu_{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}

with probability at least
1-\delta
(simultaneously for all
k
).
Each interval corresponds to a condition on
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
for which the optimal

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{2}\min\left(1,\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}}\right)

falls within that interval. In particular, for each
k\in[m-1]
, the interval
\mathcal{J}_{k}
shall correspond to the condition that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})}}<\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\equiv 4^{k-1}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}<\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\leq 4^{k}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}.

(10)

Additionally,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
shall represent the condition that
0\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\leq\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}
. Finally,
\mathcal{J}_{m}
shall correspond to
4^{m-1}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}<\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\leq T
.
Next, we prove an uniform upper bound on
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert
by analyzing the bound for each interval. Towards this end, let
k\in[m-1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+2^{k+1}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}\leq 6\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}},

where the second inequality follows from (
10
).
Similarly, for
k=0
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert\leq 2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+2\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}\leq 4\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}.

Finally, for
k=m
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+2^{m+1}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})+2^{m+1}\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq 6\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}},

where the first inequality follows from (
9
), while the second inequality follows from the condition characterizing
\mathcal{J}_{m}
. Combining the three bounds above, we have shown that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 6\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}}+4\log\frac{2(m+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

where the equality follows since
m=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\log T\right)
. Taking a union bound over all
f\in\mathcal{F},i\in[N]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})\left(V(z_{i},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{t}[V(z_{i},y_{t})]\right)\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{N\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert}{\delta}}+\log\frac{N\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert}{\delta}\right)

for all
i\in[N]
simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert
.

It follows from the definition of the gain function
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}+\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}].

Invoking the regret guarantee of
\mathsf{ALG}
(
Lemma
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N)+\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log(\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N)}\right).

Moreover,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(f,i,\sigma)}w_{t,f,i,\sigma}\ell_{t,f,i,\sigma}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}(P_{t})=\mathcal{O}(1)
. For a fixed
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
, the sequence
\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[\ell_{f,i,\sigma}(\tilde{p}_{t},x_{t},y_{t})|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}]
is a martingale difference sequence. Repeating a similar analysis as that done for the sequence
\{X_{t}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}\right).

Taking a union bound over all
f\in\mathcal{F},i\in[N]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}f(x_{t})V(\tilde{p}_{t},y_{t})\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}}+\log\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert N}{\delta}\right).

holds for all
i\in[N]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Bounding
\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\right\rvert
.

Observe that
\{W_{t}\}
is not necessarily a martingale difference sequence. However, since the distribution
P_{t}
randomizes between at most 2 points and
V(.,Y)
is Lipschitz,
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
. This is because,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]=f(x_{t})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}\left[\mathbb{I}{\left[\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\right]}(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},Y_{t})]-V(\tilde{p}_{t},Y_{t}))\right],}

where we have simplified the expression by not mentioning the filtration
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
, which is still implicit in
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[.]
. Subsequently, if
P_{t}
picks
0
or
1
deterministically,
\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]=0
. In the remaining case, let
P_{t}
randomize between 2 points
\frac{j}{T}
and
\frac{j+1}{T}
at time
t
. Then, if the sampled
\tilde{p}_{t}
is
\frac{j}{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}}[V(\tilde{p}_{t},Y_{t})]-V(\tilde{p}_{t},Y_{t})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert P_{t}\left(\frac{j}{T}\right)V\left(\frac{j}{T},Y_{t}\right)+P_{t}\left(\frac{j+1}{T}\right)V\left(\frac{j+1}{T},Y_{t}\right)-V\left(\frac{j}{T},Y_{t}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert P_{t}\left(\frac{j+1}{T}\right)\left(V\left(\frac{j+1}{T},Y_{t}\right)-V\left(\frac{j}{T},Y_{t}\right)\right)\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{T},

where the inequality follows due to the Lipschitzness of
V(.,Y)
(
7
). The above bound also holds if the sampled
\tilde{p}_{t}=\frac{j+1}{T}
. Therefore,
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert\leq\frac{\rho}{T}
. Equipped with this, we can bound
\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}
by considering the martingale difference sequence
W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]
instead and repeating a similar analysis using Freedman’s inequality. Particularly,
\left\lvert W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert\leq 4
since
\left\lvert W_{t}\right\rvert\leq 2
and the conditional variance can be bounded as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[(W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[{W_{t}}])^{2}]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}])^{2}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}^{2}]\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})
. Repeating a similar analysis as that done for the sequence
\{X_{t}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[W_{t}]\right\rvert=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{i})\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Since

Relating

(11)

holds for all

Lemma 6

[

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a martingale difference sequence where

Lemma 7
.

There does not exist a function
f_{q}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in[0,1]}n_{p}\left(q-\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}{\left[p_{t}=p,y_{t}\leq p\right]}\right)^{2}\leq\zeta\left(\sup_{\nu:[0,1]\to[0,1]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{q}(p_{t},y_{t})-f_{q}(\nu(p_{t}),y_{t})\right)+C

(15)

holds for every
T\in\mathbb{N},q\in[0,1]
, and sequences
\{y_{t}\},\{p_{t}\}
for some strictly increasing invertible function
\zeta
with
\zeta(0)=0
, and an absolute constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary, that there exists a function
f_{q}
such that (
15
) holds for all
q\in[0,1]
and sequences
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{p_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Fix a
q\in(0,1),p\in[0,1]
,
y\in(0,1)
, and let
p_{t}=p,y_{t}=y
for all
t\in[T]
. Then, (
15
) simplifies to

\displaystyle T\left(q-\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq p\right]}\right)^{2}\leq\zeta(T(f_{q}(p,y)-\inf_{p^{\star}\in[0,1]}f_{q}(p^{\star},y)))+C.

Since
\zeta
is strictly increasing and invertible, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle f_{q}(p,y)-\inf_{p^{\star}\in[0,1]}f_{q}(p^{\star},y)\geq\frac{1}{T}\zeta^{-1}\left(T\left(q-\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq p\right]}^{2}\right)-C\right).

Define the function
\psi(y)\coloneqq\inf_{p^{\star}\in[0,1]}f_{q}(p^{\star},y)
. Then, the inequality above implies that

\displaystyle\psi(y)

\displaystyle\geq\psi(y)+\frac{1}{T}\inf_{p^{\star}\in[0,1]}\zeta^{-1}\left(T\left(q-\mathbb{I}{\left[y\leq p^{\star}\right]}^{2}\right)-C\right)

\displaystyle=\psi(y)+\frac{1}{T}\min\left(\zeta^{-1}\left(Tq^{2}-C\right),\zeta^{-1}\left(T(1-q)^{2}-C\right)\right).

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
Tq^{2}\geq C
and
T(1-q)^{2}\geq C
, so that both terms
\zeta^{-1}\left(Tq^{2}-C\right)
and
\zeta^{-1}\left(T(1-q)^{2}-C\right)
are non-negative. It follows from the inequality above that either
\zeta^{-1}(Tq^{2}-C)=0
or
\zeta^{-1}(T(1-q)^{2}-C)=0
. However, since the only solution to
\zeta^{-1}(x)=0
is
x=0
, either
C=Tq^{2}
or
C=T(1-q)^{2}
. Clearly, this is a contradiction since
C
is an absolute constant, therefore cannot depend on
T
. This completes the proof.
∎
