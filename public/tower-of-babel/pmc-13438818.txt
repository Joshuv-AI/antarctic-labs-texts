Enhancing nursing students’ moral sensitivity through the World Cafe and ethical decision-making simulation to address diverse learning styles
Authors: Hsiao Lu Lee, Chiu-Yueh Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTeachers’ instructional strategies and students’ learning styles are key determinants of learning effectiveness. This study examined whether the World Cafe teaching strategy and the Four-Topic Approach to ethical decision-making could enhance outcomes in nursing ethics education. A total of 250 nursing students participated in this study. Two instructional strategies were implemented during the semester: (1) the World Cafe teaching strategy and (2) the Four-Topic Approach. Pretests and post-tests were administered. Learning-style classifications included visual (n = 106), auditory (n = 96), and kinesthetic (n = 124). Additionally, 50 students demonstrated a single learning style, 54 demonstrated two learning styles (eg, visual–auditory, auditory–kinesthetic, or kinesthetic–visual), and 56 demonstrated all three learning styles (visual, auditory, kinesthetic). Eighty-eight students could not be assigned to any learning-style category. Overall, 110 students (44%) exhibited two learning styles. Significant correlations were observed between the use of multiple teaching strategies and nursing students’ learning styles (r = 0.352, p = .01), as well as between teaching strategies and moral sensitivity (r = 0.329, p = .01). Both learning styles and instructional strategies were associated with improved moral sensitivity scores. The findings suggest that integrating diverse teaching strategies — such as the World Cafe and structured ethical decision-making — can effectively enhance nursing ethics education. Tailoring instruction to accommodate students’ learning styles may contribute to improved moral sensitivity among nursing students.

INTRODUCTION

Nurses with moral sensitivity can improve the quality of care and enhance interpersonal interactions.1 Therefore, it is very important for school nursing ethics education to cultivate moral sensitivity in nursing students. If nursing students with moral sensitivity were more able to consider the cases from the patients’ perspectives,2 spokesperson for them, express their concerns for the patients’ welfare, and promote and maintain the well-being of patients, moral sensitivity would be one of the fundamentals in cultivating nursing students’ ethical decision-making skills.3 The teachers of nursing ethics encouraged students to develop such skills at school because their sensitivity to ethical issues and the welfare of cases would directly influence how they make their ethical decisions after they become nurses in the future.

Moral sensitivity is a fundamental competency in nursing practice and can be enhanced through structured ethics education.4 For example, the research by Lee et al2 showed that ethics education intervention significantly improved nursing students’ moral sensitivity in the patient-centered nursing dimension, highlighting the potential of ethics education in cultivating ethical awareness. Building on this, Ertuğrul et al5 implemented an “ethics lab” project and observed significant improvements in both nursing students’ professional values and moral sensitivity, indicating that interactive and context-rich teaching strategies are particularly effective. These findings support a model that tailored teaching strategies (how) combined with students’ learning preferences (object/content) can promote deeper engagement and cognitive processing, thereby enhancing moral sensitivity (outcome) and ultimately helping to improve ethical decision-making in clinical nursing practice. However, there is a lack of empirical evidence linking learning styles to teaching strategies and whether learning through the four-square ethics framework can enhance moral sensitivity.

Learning styles

Dunn and Dunn’s learning style theory6 encompassed 25 elements across environmental, emotional, sociological, physiological, and psychological domains. This study specifically focuses on the physiological domain, examining visual, auditory, and kinesthetic (VAK) sensory preferences because these modalities represent the primary channels through which learners perceive and internalize information. Grounded in experiential learning theory7 and constructivist perspectives, the study posits that sensory preferences shape how students attend to ethical cues, interpret value-laden situations, and engage in moral reasoning.

This study intends to adopt two instructional strategies — (1) group discussion about ethical issues based on the World Cafe model and (2) practice of ethical decision-making — to investigate the application of these two non-traditional instructional strategies on nursing students with different learning styles and assess their respective effectiveness. Prior evidence suggests that aligning pedagogical approaches with sensory learning modalities enhances motivation, comprehension and ethical sensitivity.8 Accordingly, the study examines whether instructional alignment with VAK preferences contributes to improvements in nursing students’ moral sensitivity.

The strategy of the World Cafe

This study introduces the strategy of the World Cafe9 to our current curriculum. The teaching strategy of the World Cafe can show personal perspectives, promote interaction and engaging conversations with others, and aim to generate collaborative and innovative ideas and thinking.7 In this study, the students simulated discussions of ethical issues with each other in a relaxing environment so that their discussions would not be limited to their own group members. The World Cafe combines small group discussion; it is a teaching method where five students gather to engage in interactive, learner-centered, and brainstorming activities.10 Unlike traditional passive, one-sided teaching, small group discussions create an environment where students actively participate in the learning process. Chou and Chuang11 listed the benefits of adopting the World Cafe model: (1) to foster the students’ ability to listen with equal respect; (2) nursing students speak rationally, thus helping them understand the significance of rational arguments in a democratic system; (3) to help the students build a “collective” identity from their “individual” identity; (4) to train the students to think systematically, reflect on themselves, and construct a new mode of thinking; and (5) to provide diverse choices of communication, thus helping the students develop a teamwork spirit and the abilities to communicate and coordinate with one another during discussion, students accept this strategy to brainstorm and discuss with each other will increase their moral sensitivity.12 We considered that the World Cafe strategy is suitable for these three learning styles: (1) visual — worksheets for completing discussions in writing; (2) auditory — listening to others’ opinions and discussions; and (3) kinesthetic — moving around the room and physically interacting with others to complete their worksheets.

Ethical decision making practice using the Four Topic Approach

The second instructional strategy focused on ethical decision-making practice, an essential component of nursing ethics education that prepares students to handle real-world clinical dilemmas. This approach provided opportunities for learners to engage with ethical cases, recognize moral conflicts, make informed decisions, and reflect on their reasoning and consequences. Such experiential learning fosters critical thinking, moral sensitivity, and reflective judgment, which are crucial for ethical nursing care.13

In this study, ethical decision-making practice was implemented through the Four-Topic Approach,14 a structured model for analyzing clinical ethics cases across four domains: 1. medical indications (hospital indicators) — clinical data, nursing care, and treatment goals; 2. patient preferences (patient wishes) — autonomy, consent, and values; 3. quality of life — evaluation of the patient’s present and projected well-being; and 4. contextual features — social, legal, and institutional considerations. Pedagogically, the Four-Topic Approach supported students in organizing ethical reasoning within a clear framework while promoting active, reflective, and collaborative learning. It was integrated with the World Cafe strategy, where students rotated through four discussion rounds, each corresponding to one topic of the framework. This design helped learners explore multiple ethical perspectives and synthesize moral decisions collectively.

Incorporating the VAK learning model, students analyzed cases visually (eg, charts, diagrams), discussed ideas verbally, and engaged kinesthetically through simulation-based exercises. This multi-modal, inclusive design aligns with Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles,15 enhancing motivation, moral reasoning, and ethical sensitivity.

Overall, this integrated method — World Cafe dialogues combined with the Four-Topic Approach — transformed ethics education into an interactive, learner-centered process that strengthens students’ ability to apply ethical reasoning in nursing practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODOLOGY

Design and sample

This study is a quasi-experiment: it was a single group pretest and post-test. To explore the students’ learning styles, the nursing students took a test in the first week of their nursing ethics course. Two instructional strategies — (1) the World Cafe discussion model and (2) ethical decision-making practice — were introduced in Week 7 and Week 11 (Table 1). These two instructional strategies were designed to match three learning styles, so all nursing students were recruited with informed consent to participate and improve their ethical literacy. The students will be graded based on their group discussions on ethical issues and their proposed solutions. Meanwhile, their learning results are measured by their moral sensitivity (Figure 1). The students were tested in Week 1 and Week 17, with moral sensitivity and learning styles as the parameters. The subjects are around 250 third-year nursing students enrolled in the nursing ethics course at a 5-year junior nursing college. The students who missed over six classes or who have suffered major traumas in life were excluded. Two hundred fifty participants participated in this study: 235 (94.0%) participants are female, and 15 (6.0%) participants are male; 98 (39.2%) participants are the first child in their family; 173 (69.2%) participants have religious beliefs; 155 (62.0%) participants live in a nuclear family; 192 (77.1%) participants reside in urban areas; 132 (53.4%) of their parents received a high school education; 57 (30.0%) of the parents work as technicians.

Educational program

This 16-week nursing ethics course aims to cultivate students’ moral sensitivity and enhance their ethical decision-making abilities. The course incorporates two main teaching interventions: the World Cafe and ethical decision-making practice, implemented from weeks 7 to 11, to improve students’ moral sensitivity.

Purpose of the interventions. These interventions aim (1) to promote students’ moral sensitivity through World Cafe strategy; and (2) to integrate the Four-Topic Approach into nursing decision-making in real clinical situations.

Learning activities—World Cafe. Students discuss a simulated ethical scenario involving confidentiality and information disclosure in a sexually transmitted disease case (Mr Wang’s syphilis diagnosis). Guided by the “Four-Topic Approach” (medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, and contextual feature), students take turns discussing at four tables, each chaired by a faculty member or senior student. This activity aims to help students identify ethical issues (non-harm principle, benevolent principle, and informed consent) and requires students to submit a reflective worksheet after completing all discussions.

Simulation-based ethical decision-making practice assignments. Students practice ethical decision-making through clinical simulations. They need to decide whether and how to inform the patient’s spouse, explain their reasoning, and design appropriate communication strategies. These simulations are followed by group discussions and peer feedback to reinforce ethical reflection and professional responsibility.

The instructional framework is based on Dunn and Dunn’s learning style theory, emphasizing individualized learning methods to maximize student engagement and knowledge retention. The design incorporates VAK learning modes to cater to different learner preferences and promote deep understanding. Rationale for choosing instructional methods for different learning styles includes the following:

Visual learners — actively participate in learning by understanding ethical conflicts and decision-making steps through case scripts, diagrams, and ethical flowcharts.

Audiovisual learners — benefit from World Cafe discussions and group reflections after simulations, including oral discussions and role-playing dialogues.

Kinesthetic learners — their ethical competence was enhanced through rotating discussion tables, conducting simulated tasks, and completing hands-on exercises combining text and visuals.

Measuring tools

Learning style assessment. A 12-item Learning Style Scale was developed to identify participants’ preferred learning modalities. The items were created with reference to Fleming and Mills8 and relevant literature, reflecting behaviors associated with the three learning styles. The initial version was reviewed by three experts in nursing education and educational psychology to ensure clarity and content relevance. After expert review and revision, the scale demonstrated a content validity index (CVI) of 0.89 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.85, indicating good validity and reliability. There are three types of learning styles: (1) visual — through reading, looking at images, proofreading, and writing assignments; (2) auditory — through listening to audiotapes and lectures; and (3) kinesthetic — through experiencing with hands, feet, and body. The third style can involve various activities, such as field trips, in-class role-plays or bodily interaction, laboratory activities that require hands-on practice, making learning materials and diagrams, or writing reports. The assessment consists of 12 questions compiled from literature.

To determine participants’ dominant learning style, the mean score for each style was calculated. Students scoring higher than the sample mean on a particular dimension were classified as having a preference for that learning style. This mean-based classification was applied because no standardized cut-off points exist for this newly developed scale, and similar classification methods have been used in previous exploratory studies.16

Nursing Students’ Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire in Taiwan. Lee and Huang17 developed a “Nursing Students’ Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire in Taiwan.” Five categories were considered and discussed: the importance of morality, ethical principles (autonomy, informed consent, and fairness), patient-centered care, and professional responsibility. Based on a total of 18 questions, the scale measures the subjects’ agreement to moral sensitivity according to a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 7. The sum of each item is added up to indicate the level of the subject’s moral sensitivity. The reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) is 0.9 and the expert validity (CVI) is 0.885.

Data collection and analysis

All data were analyzed by SPSS 23.0 (IBM) and data collection was from March to June in 2018: 1. use descriptive statistics to describe the personal attributes of the research subjects, their frequency distribution, percentage, standard deviation, and mean value; 2. use Pearson’s product-moment correlation to test the correlation between family gender, ranking, living area, etc, and learning style and moral sensitivity; 3. use independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the differences in learning styles and moral sensitivity based on family gender, rank, living area, etc; 4. use paired t test whether there are differences in learning styles and moral sensitivity between pretests and post-tests; 5. Pearson Correlation Analysis (Pearson Correlation). Is there a correlation between learning styles and moral sensitivity?

Ethical approval

This research plan was reviewed and approved by Human Experimentation Board (institutional review board) of National Cheng Kung University Hospital, no. 105026. The subjects recruited for this study were explained the purpose of the study and asked for consent. After the subjects filled out the consent form, they then filled out the questionnaire. As for research assistants (non-course teachers), they must first protect the basic human rights of subjects and prevent subjects from being harmed or having their privacy rights violated. The ethical issues that this research pays attention to include the following: (1) commitment and reciprocity — avoid causing harm to subjects after achieving the research purpose; (2) confidentiality — the entire questionnaire information should be kept confidential and will only be referred to by code name; (3) the questionnaire information will be arranged according to the serial number of the participants, and no names will be left until all cases are closed and published; (4) participants can withdraw from the study at any time based on personal factors without any harm (including grades) or penalty.

RESULTS

Students’ learning styles

Students whose scores for a specific learning style exceeded the overall mean were classified as preferring that learning style: visual (M ± SD = 20.19 ± 3.98), auditory (M ± SD = 13.16 ± 3.33), or kinesthetic (M ± SD = 19.73 ± 3.82). The students with multiple learning style demonstrated more than one preferred style. Those with multiple learning styles exhibited significantly higher moral sensitivity than students with either a single or no dominant style, confirming the effectiveness of the combined instructional strategies in enhancing moral awareness among learners with diverse preferences (Table 2). Significant correlations were observed among the three learning styles: visual–kinesthetic (r = 0.440**), auditory–visual (r = 0.451**), and auditory–kinesthetic (r = 0.430**), indicating overlapping tendencies in perceptual learning modes. A significant gender difference was also found, with male students scoring higher in learning-style preferences than females (t = −1.31, p < .05). No significant differences were associated with other demographic variables such as birth order, religion, family structure, residence, reasons for choosing nursing, parental education, or post-graduation plans.

Moral sensitivity

After one semester of nursing ethics education that integrated two innovative teaching strategies — the World Cafe approach and the Four-Topic Ethical Decision-Making Practice — students’ moral sensitivity improved significantly across all five dimensions: patient-centered care, moral conflict, ethical principles, professional responsibility, and maintaining privacy. The overall post-test mean score (114.78 ± 10.09) was significantly higher than the pretest mean score (108.43 ± 11.82) (t = −7.95, p < .001). All subscales score also increased significantly (p < .001; Table 3). No significant differences in moral sensitivity were found based on gender, birth order, religion, family structure, residence, reasons for choosing nursing, or parental education levels (p > .05).

Relationship between learning styles and moral sensitivity

Both visual and non-visual learners demonstrated significant improvements in moral sensitivity after participating in the two instructional strategies. Across both assessments, visual learners scored significantly higher than non-visual learners (pretest, t = −4.439, p < .001; post-test, t = −5.25, p < .001). Similar patterns were observed among auditory and kinesthetic learners. These results suggest that students with dominant learning styles had higher initial moral awareness and achieved greater progress after the intervention. Significant correlations were found between learning-style scores and moral-sensitivity scores at both pretest and post-test (r = 0.387***, r = 0.442***), and between post-test moral sensitivity and pretest learning-style scores (r = 0.212**). Additional correlations were observed among the three styles — VAK — with moral sensitivity (r = 0.140, p < .05; r = 0.178, p < .01; r = 0.181, p < .01). These findings indicate that higher moral sensitivity is associated with more effective learning engagement and outcomes across diverse learning styles.

DISCUSSION

Moral sensitivity is a central construct in nursing ethics and constitutes the foundation of moral competence in clinical practice. Theoretical background was according to Rest’s Four-Topic Model,18 moral sensitivity represents the initial stage of ethical decision-making, allowing individuals to recognize moral issues and perceive the consequences of their actions. Within this framework, nursing ethics education aims to strengthen students’ ability to identify and interpret ethical dilemmas in patient care. Learning style theory, as described by Dunn and Dunn6 and Fleming and Mills,8 highlights that learners differ in how they process, retain, and apply information. The three dominant learning styles — VAK — reflect individual cognitive preferences that influence academic engagement and performance. Integrating these styles into moral education supports both cognitive and affective learning processes, encouraging students to connect ethical concepts with experiential understanding.

The study revealed significant improvements in nursing students’ moral sensitivity across all five dimensions — patient-centered care, moral conflict, ethical principles, professional responsibility, and maintaining privacy — after one semester of nursing ethics education. Educational interpretation of findings suggest that instructional designs incorporating multiple learning styles enhance students’ ethical perception and reflective ability.

In this study, 88 nursing students did not exhibit a dominant learning-style preference. Prior research indicates that a sizable proportion of nursing students can be classified as having no dominant style or as multi-modal learners, and recommends that educators purposefully adopt multi-modal and inclusive approaches so that all learners can access content effectively.19 Consistent with this, recent nursing education literature advocates for the UDL framework and inclusive pedagogy to proactively provide multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression — such as combining lectures with visuals, guided discussions, hands-on simulations, and reflective activities — to support learners without a single prevailing style.15,20 Empirical studies also show that many students in health professions prefer multi-modal learning, reinforcing the rationale for adopting mixed instructional methods rather than style-matched teaching alone.21 Accordingly, instructors in this study implemented varied modalities across sessions (eg, narrated slides, diagrams, short simulations, and structured debriefs) to accommodate students lacking a dominant style.

The significant correlations between learning style scores and moral sensitivity in both pretest and post-test conditions further indicate that a multi-modal learning environment facilitates moral development. Students who engaged with two or more learning styles achieved higher levels of moral sensitivity, supporting the concept that flexible cognitive engagement promotes ethical reasoning. Furthermore, the positive correlations between visual and auditory learning preferences and moral sensitivity imply that interactive and discussion-based learning activities stimulate moral awareness and empathetic understanding — key components in developing professional ethics in nursing education.

The present findings align with previous research demonstrating that ethics education interventions effectively enhance moral sensitivity among nursing students.22,23 Similar to Cannaerts et al24 found that participatory and reflective teaching approaches — such as simulation, group discussion, and case-based analysis — produce stronger moral learning outcomes than traditional lecture-based instruction. However, this study extends earlier findings by revealing that students with multiple learning styles displayed higher moral sensitivity than those with a single or no dominant style. This suggests that cognitive flexibility, or the ability to process information through different sensory modalities, may enhance ethical reasoning and adaptability. The integration of multi-modal and inclusive pedagogical frameworks, such as UDL, further supports equitable engagement for students with varied learning preferences.15,20

Limitations and future directions

Two limitations should be noted. First, the study was conducted at a single nursing school, which restricts the generalization of the findings. Second, the intervention lasted only one semester, limiting insights into long-term moral development. Future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-method designs to explore how enhanced moral sensitivity translates into ethical behavior in clinical practice. Including qualitative approaches, such as interviews and reflective journals, would provide deeper understanding of how students internalize moral reasoning through different learning modalities. Moreover, future studies could examine the integration of technology-enhanced strategies — such as digital simulations, virtual case studies, and interactive ethics modules — to foster moral growth in contemporary nursing education.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that the integration of innovative teaching strategies and consideration of students’ learning styles can significantly enhance moral sensitivity in nursing education. The results confirmed that moral sensitivity, as an essential component of professional ethical competence, can be effectively cultivated through multi-modal, student-centered instruction. Nursing students who engaged in diverse learning modalities — particularly visual and auditory — showed greater improvements in ethical awareness and reflective judgment.

These findings reinforce the importance of designing inclusive ethics curricula that address individual learning differences and promote active participation. By aligning teaching approaches with the principles of UDL and experiential education, nursing programs can foster deeper ethical understanding, empathy, and professional accountability among students.

Furthermore, the relationship between learning styles and moral sensitivity underscores the need for educators to continuously adapt instructional strategies to optimize moral learning outcomes. Future educational innovations should continue integrating technology-assisted, reflective, and interactive learning experiences to prepare nursing students for the ethical complexities of modern healthcare environments.

In summary, moral sensitivity is not a fixed attribute but a dynamic capability that can be cultivated through thoughtful pedagogy. When nursing ethics education embraces diversity in learning preferences and emphasizes active engagement, it not only enhances students’ moral awareness but also strengthens the ethical integrity of the nursing profession as a whole.
