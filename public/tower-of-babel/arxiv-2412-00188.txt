Evolution of the star formation rate surface density main sequence.
Jakub Nadolny, Michał J. Michałowski, Massimiliano Parente, Martín Solar, Przemysław Nowaczyk, Oleh Ryzhov, Aleksandra Leśniewska

Abstract
Context. Recent high-redshift ( z > 4 z>4 ) spatially resolved observations with the James Webb Space Telescope have shown the evolution of the star formation rate (SFR) surface density ( Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} ) and its main sequence in the Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} - M ∗ M_{*} diagram ( Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} ). The Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} is already observed at cosmic morning ( z ∼ 7.5 z\sim 7.5 ). The use of Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} is physically motivated because it is normalized by the area in which the star formation occurs, and this indirectly considers the gas density. The Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} - M ∗ M_{*} diagram has been shown to complement the widely used (specific) SFR- M ∗ M_{*} , particularly when selecting passive galaxies. Aims. We establish the Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} evolution since z = 12 z=12 in the framework of the L-Galaxies2020 semi-analytical model (SAM), and we interpret recent observations. Methods. We estimated Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} (- M ∗ M_{*} ) and the cosmic star formation rate density (CSFRD) for the simulated galaxy population and for the subsamples, which were divided into stellar mass bins in the given redshift. Results. The simulated Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} decreases by ∼ 3.5 \sim 3.5 dex from z = 12 z=12 to z = 0 z=0 . We show that galaxies with different stellar masses have different paths of Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} evolution. We find that Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} is already observed at z ∼ 11 z\sim 11 . The simulated Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} agrees with the observed one at z = 0 , 1 , 2 , 5 z=0,1,2,5 , and 7.5 7.5 and with individual galaxies at z > 10 z>10 . We show that the highest Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} slope of 0.709 ± 0.005 0.709\pm 0.005 is at z ∼ 3 z\sim 3 and decreases to ∼ 0.085 ± 0.003 \sim 0.085\pm 0.003 at z = 0 z=0 . This is mostly driven by a rapid decrease in SFR with an additional size increase for the most massive galaxies in this redshift range. This coincides with the dominance of the most massive galaxies in the CSFRD from the SAM. Observations show the same picture, in which the Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} evolutionary path depends on the stellar mass, that is, more massive galaxies have higher Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} at all redshifts. Finally, using the slope and normalization evolution, we derived the simulated Σ SFR ​ MS \Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS} as a function of stellar mass and redshift.

Introduction

The rate at which a galaxy forms stars is one of the most fundamental parameters providing insight into the assembly history of cosmic mass. The Schmidt-Kennicutt law
(
Schmidt, 1959
;
Kennicutt, 1998
)
shows a correlation of the star formation rate (SFR) with the density of the gas that is used to form stars, from which the SFR-stellar mass
M_{*}
correlation follows.
Many studies showed a tight sequence in the SFR-
M_{*}
diagram that is called star formation main sequence (SFMS) in optically selected star-forming galaxies
(
Brinchmann et al., 2004
;
Noeske et al., 2007
;
Speagle et al., 2014
;
Nadolny et al., 2023
, SFGs; )
, in very low-mass SFGs
(
Nadolny et al., 2020
)
, or in infrared selected SFGs
(
Michałowski et al., 2012
;
Michałowski et al., 2017
;
Koprowski et al., 2014
;
Koprowski et al., 2016
;
González-Otero et al., 2024
)
. Another way of expressing the star formation activity is via the specific SFR (sSFR), which is the SFR normalized by the stellar mass. The sSFR-
M_{*}
relation indicates the recent level of SF (including quenched SF), and similarly to the SFMS, it also evolves with cosmic time. The overall cosmic SFR density
(
Madau & Dickinson, 2014
;
Zavala et al., 2021
, CSFRD;)
has also been shown to evolve and to reach its maximum at
z\sim 2
, with a steady decay with increasing redshift
(
Bouwens et al., 2022
;
Donnan et al., 2024
)
.

In recent years, another more physically motivated parameter related to SF emerged
(
Wuyts et al., 2011
;
Salim et al., 2023
;
Calabrò et al., 2024
)
. This is the SFR surface density, which is defined as
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}={\rm SFR}/(2\pi R_{e}^{2})
[
M_{\odot}
/yr/kpc
2
], where
R_{e}
is the radius of a galaxy (half-light radius in this work). This parameter is more fundamental than other SF tracers because it is normalized by the extent of the star-forming region.

Salim et al. (2023)
showed that
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
complements the sSFR, particularly when passive galaxies are selected (because a galaxy with a constant SFR decreases in sSFR by definition). Its relation to the molecular gas density and the effectiveness of stellar feedback
(
Salim et al., 2023
)
also has a much deeper physical justification. The
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
has been found to increase by almost four orders of magnitude between
z=0
and 10
(
Wuyts et al., 2011
;
Calabrò et al., 2024
)
. Similar to the SFMS, a tight relation (or main sequences) in the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
-
M_{*}
diagram was found for SFGs (
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
). Several works
(
Wuyts et al., 2011
;
Salim et al., 2023
;
Calabrò et al., 2024
)
showed the increasing normalization of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
.
Salim et al. (2023)
reported an increasing slope at
z=1
and
2
caused by the fast bulge-mass build-up in the most massive galaxies, while
Calabrò et al. (2024)
observed the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z\sim 5
and
7.5
. The
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
measurement of high-
z
compact star-forming nitrogen-rich galaxy
(
Schaerer et al., 2024
)
suggests that it may be useful to select this rare galaxy population.

Recent works on magnetohydrodynamics simulations
(
Girma & Teyssier, 2024
, e.g.)
reproduced the observed
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
at the high spatial resolution (tens of parsecs) in striking exactitude. These simulations lacked the volume needed to provide a statistically significant evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
, however.

The successes of
L-Galaxies2020
semi-analytic models (SAMs) in predicting many observables
(
Henriques et al., 2020
;
Parente et al., 2023
;
Parente et al., 2024
;
Nadolny et al., 2024
;
Wang & Peng, 2024
)
showed that the combination of resolution and statistical completeness of these simulations are adequate for drawing important conclusions about the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
. This is the first study of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
evolution of galaxies divided by their stellar mass using an SAM.

We probed the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
since cosmic dawn from
z=12
to
z=0
using SAM
L-Galaxies2020
, which places low-, intermediate, and (very) high-
z
(up to 14) observations in perspective. In particular, we investigated the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
evolution of galaxies divided into stellar mass bins, following the analysis of the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
evolution. We explored the possible physical causes of the slope increase in
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
observed at
z
between 4 and 1. We show that the stellar mass and SFR are the fundamental parameters that define the different evolutionary paths of the simulated galaxy population.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
describes the data from the literature and the SAM. In Section
3
we present our results. Section
4
presents the discussion and conclusions of this work. Throughout this paper, we use a cosmological model with

h
= 0.673,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.685
,
\Omega_{m}=0.315

(
Planck Collaboration et al., 2014
)
, and the
Chabrier (2003)
initial mass function (IMF), as assumed in the applied SAM.

2
Data

2.1
L-Galaxies2020
semi-analytic model

We used the
L-Galaxies2020
SAM presented in
Parente et al. (2023)
, which is an extended version of the latest public release of the Munich SAM for galaxy evolution
(
Henriques et al., 2015
;
Henriques et al., 2020
)
. This SAM accounts for a detailed dust production and evolution, and it includes an updated treatment of disk instabilities.
The
L-Galaxies2020
model starts from the dark matter (DM) halo merger trees of the
Millennium
simulation
(
Springel et al., 2005
)
and populates them with baryons according to a number of important physical processes. Among these are gas infall into DM halos, gas cooling, disk and bulge formation, star formation, merger-driven starbursts, chemical enrichment, energetic feedback from stars and supermassive black holes (SMBHs), and environmental processes such as ram pressure and tidal stripping. We refer to the supplementary material of
Henriques et al. (2020)
and to
Parente et al. (2023)
for a detailed description of this SAM.

We used
StellarHalfLightRadius
as the effective radius
R_{e}
. The implemented star formation law is based on the H
2
surface density
(
Bigiel et al., 2011
;
Leroy et al., 2013
)
and a
Chabrier (2003)
IMF was adopted.

2.2
L-Galaxies2020
sample selection

To analyze
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
from
z\sim 12
to
z=0
, we used the whole simulated galaxy population with
M_{*}
>10^{8}
M_{\odot}
. This is a statistically significant sample at all redshifts (the sample size in each snapshot is given in Tab.
2
). Additionally, we divided the galaxies into stellar masses bins: between 8 and 9, 9 and 10, and above 10 in log(
M_{*}
/
M_{\odot}
).

2.3
Observational data

We used a compilation of the high-
z
JWST data from the CEERS
(
Finkelstein et al., 2023
)
and GLASS
(
Treu et al., 2022
)
surveys presented in
Calabrò et al. (2024)

(

)

M_{*}
>10^{11}
M_{\odot}

Gentile et al., 2024
)
from COSMOS-Web
(
Casey et al., 2023
)
; and the SFG
(
Lines et al., 2024

z
mean values from the GAMA survey
(
Smith et al., 2011
;
Driver et al., 2011
;
Driver et al., 2016
;
Baldry et al., 2018
)
, from the COSMOS survey
(
Scoville et al., 2007

)
, and from the low-mass [OII] emitters at
z=1.4

(
Cedrés et al., 2021
)
from the OTELO survey
(
Bongiovanni et al., 2019
)
. We also included observations of intermediate-
z
objects from
Williams et al. (2021)
and
Rupke et al. (2023)
. Finally, we also used the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
estimated by
Salim et al. (2023)
at
z=0,1
, and
2
. Regarding very high-
z
(
>10

(
Eisenstein et al., 2023
)
, in particular, GS-z14-0
(
Carniani et al., 2024
)
, GHZ2
(

)

(
Tacchella et al., 2023
)
at

, and

, respectively.

We estimated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
consistently (i.e., using the half-light radius
R_{e}
and the SFR based on the same IMF) in the SAM, the GAMA survey, and in the data from
Williams et al. (2021)
,
Gentile et al. (2024)
,
Carniani et al. (2024)
and
Tacchella et al. (2023)
. The remaining works give estimates of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
using the same IMF and size estimates (half-light radii
R_{e}
; see
Salim et al. 2023
for a discussion of the differences between the use of
R_{e}
and isophotal size). While the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
normalization changes for different size estimates and the scatter increases for
R_{e}
, the slopes of the obtained
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
are consistent in a given redshift
(
Salim et al., 2023
)
. This does not influence our conclusions, however, because we consistently used the same size estimates in the simulated data and observational results from the literature. Finally, we used the same stellar mass bins as for the simulated sample.

We focus below on the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
from
z=11
to
z=0
in the stellar mass range as shown in Figure
2
. The fit to the selected low-
z
simulated galaxies agrees with
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=0
and
1

(
Salim et al., 2023
)
. As shown in Figure
2
, our
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=0
and
1
are within the 0.5 dex scatter of the
Salim et al. (2023)

\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
with similar slopes. In the case of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=2
, the slope from
Salim et al. (2023)
is similar to the simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=2
, but with a lower normalization by
\sim 0.5
dex. Only 15% of the
z=2
sample from
Salim et al. (2023)
have spectroscopic redshifts, which may increase the uncertainties of the estimated parameters. At the same redshift, we show four massive (
>10^{11}
M_{\odot}
) dusty SFGs from the RUBIES survey
(
Cooper et al., 2024
, in green)
.
These are above the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
from
Salim et al. (2023)
, but agree with the extrapolation of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
derived in this work.
The observed
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=5
and
7.5
from
Calabrò et al. (2024)
both agree with the simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at the same redshifts, except for the low-mass end at
z=5
, where the observed
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
are
\sim 0.5
dex higher. As noted in
Calabrò et al. (2024)
, this may be caused by excluding the diffuse warm ionized gas and/or collisional extinction, which may contribute to the H
\alpha
emission and lead to overestimating the SF. The data in
Calabrò et al. (2024)
were moreover binned over a wide range of redshifts (4 to 6, and 6 to 10), which may also cause the observed difference. A smaller range would considerably reduce the number of sources in the narrower bins. Finally, we show individual galaxies at redshift
\penalty\ 4.5
and above
10
. These are found around (within 0.4 dex) the simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at a given redshift.

The general trend of an increasing normalization in
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
with redshift is visible in
L-Galaxies2020
(right panel in Fig.
2
). This increase in normalization at the most basic level is due to the overall decrease in the galaxy sizes for increasing SFRs with redshift (Fig.
3
, top right, and bottom left panels). Since the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
is proportional to SFR and inversely proportional to the square of the size of a galaxy, changes in these two parameters work toward increasing
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
with redshift.

The
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
slope rises rapidly between
z=1
and
3
. This rise was pointed out by
Salim et al. (2023)
. They suggested that the bulge build-up in more massive galaxies (or being more compact) caused this increase in the slope, up to
z=2
in their work. Here, the highest slope of 0.71 was found at
z=2.9
, which roughly corresponds to the peak of the CSFRD. As shown in Figure
4
, for
z<4.5
, the most massive galaxies begin to dominate the CSFRD (see Appendix
A
for more details and a comparison with observations). At the same redshift, we also find that the most massive galaxies show the fastest evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
(Fig.
1
). All these pieces of evidence indicate that the most massive galaxies (and in particular, the drastic drop in SFR) cause the change in the slope of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
. Stated differently, the star-forming activity in most massive galaxies has dropped more than in less massive galaxies since
z\sim 3
. We discuss the possible cause of this slope evolution in the next section.

Using the slope and normalization evolution with redshift (see the right panel of Fig.
2
), we derived the redshift-dependent
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
evolution. First, using the polynomial function, we estimated the evolution of the slope and the normalization with redshift. For the slope, we obtained a sixth-degree polynomial,

\begin{split}y_{\rm slope}=3.8972\times 10^{-5}\times z^{6}-0.001401\times z^{5}+0.01848\times z^{4}\\
-0.1024\times z^{3}+0.1688\times z^{2}+0.2272\times z+0.0877,\end{split}

(1)

and for the normalization [evaluated at log(
M_{*}
/
M_{\odot}
)=9], we obtained a third-degree polynomial,

\begin{split}y_{\rm norm.}=0.002315\times z^{3}-0.04812\times z^{2}+0.5879\times z-1.8406,\end{split}

(2)

where
z
is redshift. The slope and normalization evolution are shown in the right panel of Figure
2
.
Finally, using these, we derived the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
as a function of stellar mass and redshift in the form

\log(\Sigma_{SFR})=y_{slope}(z)\times[\log(M_{*}/\mbox{$M_{\odot}$})-9]+y_{norm.}(z),

(3)

where
y_{slope}
and
y_{norm.}
are the functions of redshift given in Equation
1
and
2
.

3.3
The cause of the slope evolution

We investigated what might cause the slope decrease between
z=4
and
1
. To verify whether the slope of the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
changes due to the bulge build-up in massive galaxies (as suggested by
Salim et al. 2023
), we considered the evolution of the bulge-to-total mass ratio (B/T) in stellar mass bins shown in the top left panel of Figure
3
. For massive and intermediate-mass galaxies, their B/T increases similarly between
z=4
and
2
. Later, below
z=2
, the most massive galaxies build their bulges in a pronounced manner to
z=0
. The low-mass galaxies have a low (
<20\%
) bulge contribution to the total mass.
The top right panel Fig.
3
shows a distinct
R_{e}
evolution for all three stellar mass bins in the same redshift range. The most massive galaxies increase their size by
\sim
200% from
z=4
to
z=0
, while the intermediate- and low-mass galaxies increase their sizes by
\sim
160% in the same
z
range.

We then considered the changes in SFR (bottom left panel Fig.
3
). In the same redshift range, there is a drop of
\sim 5000\%
(1.9 dex),
\sim 2000\%
(1.3 dex), and
\sim 1500\%
(1.2 dex) for massive, intermediate-, and low-mass galaxies. Thus, the change in
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
mostly depends on the changes in SFR, at least since
z\sim 4
, while the change in
R_{e}
also increases
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
. The slope of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z>4
is lower, and we can also observe a much slower SFR evolution in the most massive and intermediate-mass galaxies.

Thus, even if
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
is inversely proportional to the square of the size, the much greater changes in SFR (for the most massive galaxies in particular) drive the slope evolution with redshift between
z=4
and
1
. The bulge build-up alone is not sufficient to explain the slope evolution.

Considering the slope increase between
z=7.5
and
3
(Fig.
2
), we find that the appearance of the most massive highly star-forming galaxies seems to cause this. These massive galaxies with SFRs 1 dex higher than the lower-mass galaxies (at
z\sim 7
) drag up the high-mass end of the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
. This increase continues until the peak of the CSFRD at
z\sim 2.5
.

4
Discussion and conclusions

We showed the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and its main sequences
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
since
z=12
to
z=0
. This sets the evolutionary sequences for this physically motivated star formation indicator.

Considering the whole
L-Galaxies2020
population in snapshots from
z=12
to
z=0
, we find the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
with redshift by
\sim 3
dex (considering the mean values of the whole population; black squares in Fig.
1
).

The observational data from JWST at
4<z<10

(
Calabrò et al., 2024
)
agree with the simulated population with stellar masses below
10^{10}
M_{\odot}
. In particular, their fit runs parallel and between our low- and intermediate-mass bins with
M_{*}
<10^{10}
M_{\odot}
. A decrease in
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
from
\sim 25
to
\sim 1

M_{\odot}
/yr/kpc
2
is observed from
z=10
to
4
(see Fig.
1
). This also agrees with previous investigations of the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
evolution in this redshift range using simulated CEERS light cones
(
Yung et al., 2022
)
. The most massive galaxies have higher
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
at virtually all redshifts. Furthermore, these objects show the fastest evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
from
z=4
to
z=0
while dominating the CSFRD in the same redshift range (see Fig.
4
).

The simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
agree with the observed relations at
z\sim 0,1,7.5
. Considering the
z=2

\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
, the simulated MS have a similar slope but a 0.5 dex higher normalization than is observed. This difference may be due to a low number of objects with spectroscopic redshifts for the observed
z=2
sample
(
Salim et al., 2023
)
. However, massive dusty star-forming galaxies from the RUBIES survey at
z\sim 2
follow the simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at this redshift. For the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at
z=5
, the simulated MS is steeper, and the low-mass end lies below the observed relation.

In the context of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and its main sequences, we showed very distant (
z>10
) spectroscopically confirmed galaxies. These follow (roughly) the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
considering their stellar mass, and they are close to the predicted simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
at a similar redshift. This shows the SAM success in predicting such high-
z

\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
.

We described the evolution of simulated
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
from
z=12
to
z=0
(see Fig.
2
, and Tab.
2
).
The slope of the
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
decreases from 0.71 at
z=2.9
to 0.08 at
z=0
. We found that this decrease in the slope is due to a dramatic decrease (by
\sim 5000\%
) in SFR in this redshift range for the most massive galaxies. This is a dominant factor considering that the changes in
R_{e}
are much smaller.

Finally, using the slope and normalization evolution (Eq.
1
and
2
, and the right panel of Fig.
2
), we provided the evolution of
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}{\rm MS}
as a function of stellar mass and redshift within the SAM framework (Eq.
3
), up to
z=10.8
with the stellar mass ranges indicated in Table
2
.
