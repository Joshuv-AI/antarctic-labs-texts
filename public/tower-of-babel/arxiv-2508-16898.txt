Enhanced shape recovery in advection–diffusion problems via a novel ADMM-based CCBM optimization
Elmehdi Cherrat ⋅ \cdot Lekbir Afraites ⋅ \cdot Julius Fergy Tiongson Rabago

Abstract
This work proposes a novel shape optimization framework for geometric inverse problems governed by the advection–diffusion equation, based on the coupled complex boundary method (CCBM). Building on recent developments [ 2 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 51 ] , we aim to recover the shape of an unknown inclusion via shape optimization driven by a cost functional constructed from the imaginary part of the complex-valued state variable over the entire domain. We rigorously derive the associated shape derivative in variational form and provide explicit expressions for the gradient and second-order information. Optimization is carried out using a Sobolev gradient method within a finite element framework. To address difficulties in reconstructing obstacles with concave boundaries, particularly under measurement noise and the combined effects of advection and diffusion, we introduce a numerical scheme inspired by the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). In addition to implementing this non-conventional approach, we demonstrate how the adjoint method can be efficiently applied and utilize partial gradients to develop a more efficient CCBM–ADMM scheme. The accuracy and robustness of the proposed computational approach are validated through various numerical experiments. Keywords: Alternating direction method of multipliers, advection-diffusion model, coupled complex boundary method, inverse geometric problem, shape optimization, shape derivatives, adjoint method

Introduction

The advection–diffusion equation is a fundamental model in applied mathematics, describing the combined effects of transport and dispersion of substances within a medium
[
43
]
.
It arises in key environmental applications, such as the spread of pollutants in air and water and the transport of solutes in rivers and groundwater.
Accurate modeling of these processes is essential for pollution control, water resource management, and related environmental systems
[
57
]
.
Because of its central role in predicting and managing transport phenomena, the advection–diffusion framework has been widely studied in both theory and applications.

In recent years, significant attention has been devoted to inverse problems for advection–diffusion systems, particularly for identifying unknown shapes or sources from indirect measurements.
Many works have focused on point source identification
[
7
,
10
,
11
,
31
,
32
,
41
]
, while others have investigated shape reconstruction using adjoint-based methods
[

]
, domain derivative techniques

]
, and topological derivatives
[

]
.
More recently, we have studied inverse geometric problems for simplified advection–diffusion systems using conventional numerical methods
[
17
,
18
]
.
Building on this foundation, the present work considers a more general setting with spatially varying coefficients, and introduces a reconstruction strategy based on a non-conventional numerical shape optimization framework combined with an augmented Lagrangian method.

The objective of this paper is to recover both the solution
u
and an open, connected, bounded region
\varOmega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in an advection–diffusion problem.
The domain has an annular structure,
\varOmega=D\setminus\overline{\omega}
, where the outer boundary
\varSigma:=\partial\varOmega
is known and accessible for measurement, while the inner boundary
\varGamma:=\partial\omega
is unknown and must be reconstructed.
Here,

represents the flow domain and
\omega\subset D
is an inclusion modeling an obstacle or pollutant zone.
The reconstruction is based on a single pair of Cauchy data
(f,g)
prescribed on
\varSigma

\varGamma

The corresponding mathematical model is the following overdetermined boundary value problem:

-\operatorname{div}(\sigma\nabla{u})+\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla u=0\text{ in }\varOmega,\qquad u=0\text{ on }\varGamma,\qquad u=f,\;\;\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u}=g\text{ on }\varSigma,

(1)

where, in the context of pollutant transport in water,
\sigma:=\sigma(x),\ x\in D
, denotes the diffusion coefficient,

\bm{\mathbf{b}}:=\bm{\mathbf{b}}(x),\ x\in D

\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u}
denotes the outward normal derivative of

\varSigma

Consequently, we consider the inverse geometry problem:

\text{Find }\varGamma\text{ and }u\text{ that satisfy the overdetermined system }\eqref{eq:surdeterminee}.

(2)

We address this problem using shape optimization
[

For overdetermined systems such as (

[
17
,
18
]
.

Here, we propose a non-conventional shape optimization method based on the
coupled complex boundary method
(CCBM)
[

16
,

,

,
47
]
.
The CCBM encodes Neumann data as the real part and Dirichlet data as the imaginary part of a Robin-type complex boundary condition.
The inverse problem is then solved by minimizing the

norm of the imaginary part of the solution over the domain, which distinguishes the CCBM from conventional methods that impose misfit penalties directly on the boundary
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,

The main novelty of this study lies in extending the CCBM framework to advection–diffusion systems with spatially varying coefficients
\sigma

\bm{\mathbf{b}}

This setting provides a richer and more realistic description of transport phenomena compared to constant-coefficient models.
In addition, we consider complex geometries, including nonconvex shapes, beyond the smooth convex cases commonly addressed in earlier works
[
54
,
55
]
.
Our formulation therefore broadens the applicability of these non-conventional shape reconstruction techniques to more general scenarios relevant to practical modeling.

To improve the recovery of complex-shaped obstacles under noisy data, we embed the CCBM within an augmented Lagrangian framework based on the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
17
,
50
]
.
This provides an alternative to conventional regularization techniques such as perimeter or surface penalization.
Note that existing ADMM-based shape optimization methods use real-valued auxiliary variables.
In our case, the state variable is complex-valued, so the auxiliary variable must be redefined consistently and adapted to the CCBM structure.
This results in a state-of-the-art ADMM algorithm specifically designed for CCBM.
In addition, we propose a partial gradient scheme that further improves reconstruction accuracy while reducing computational cost.
Altogether, the resulting CCBM–ADMM framework provides a unified and efficient optimization strategy, whose effectiveness is demonstrated through numerical experiments, with particular emphasis on three-dimensional configurations.

2.2
The coupled complex boundary value problem

To address the inverse geometry problem (
4
), we adopt a non-conventional shape optimization approach based on the
coupled complex boundary method
(CCBM)
[
16
]
.
Unlike traditional least-squares methods that fit boundary data directly and may suffer from numerical instabilities, CCBM relocates the data fitting to the domain interior.
This naturally leads to a cost functional defined as a domain integral, which improves stability and robustness in the reconstruction process, in a manner comparable to the Kohn–Vogelius method
[
38
]
.

The main idea of CCBM is to encode the measured Dirichlet and Neumann data into a complex-valued Robin boundary condition, with the real and imaginary parts representing these measurements.
This formulation regularizes the inverse problem, providing stable and accurate reconstructions even in the presence of noise, while increasing the size of the resulting system.
CCBM has been successfully applied to various inverse problems, including conductivity reconstruction, parameter identification in elliptic equations, free boundary problems, and tumor localization
[
2
,
16
,
30
,
44
,
45
,
47
,
51
,
46
,
56
]
.

To implement CCBM, system (
1

-\operatorname{div}(\sigma\nabla{u})+\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla{u}=0\quad\text{in $\varOmega$},\qquad u=0\quad\text{on $\varGamma$},\qquad\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u}+iu=g+if\quad\text{on $\varSigma$},

(5)

where
u:\varOmega\to\mathbb{C}
and
i=\sqrt{-1}
.
Hereinafter, unless otherwise specified,

will always denote the solution of (
5

Let the sesquilinear form

and the linear form

\displaystyle a(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{\sigma\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\overline{\psi}+(\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla\varphi)\overline{\psi}}{\,{d}x}+i\int_{\Sigma}{\varphi\overline{\psi}}{\,{d}s},

(6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}{(g+if)\overline{\psi}}{\,{d}s},\quad\forall\varphi,\psi\in{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega).

Clearly, as a consequence of the assumptions,

is shape-differentiable and we can apply Hadamard’s differentiation formula (see
[
23
,
37
,
53
]
) to obtain

dJ(\varOmega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\left|{u_{2}}\right|^{2}}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\dot{u}_{2}}{\,{d}x}.

For
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
and sufficiently smooth
\psi
, the divergence theorem tells us that

-\int_{\Omega}{\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\psi}{\,{d}x}=\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\nabla\psi}{\,{d}x}-\int_{\Gamma}{\psi(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}})}{\,{d}s},

(22)

Putting
\psi=\left|{\Im\{u\}}\right|^{2}=\left|{u_{2}}\right|^{2}\in W^{1,1}(\varOmega)
we deduce that

dJ(\varOmega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]=-\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\nabla u_{2}}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\dot{u}_{2}}{\,{d}x}.

(23)

We rewrite the above expression with the goal of replacing
\dot{u}
with the adjoint variable
p
.
To start, let us take
\varphi=\dot{u}\in{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega)
in (
21
), to get

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}{\left\{\sigma\nabla p\cdot\nabla{\overline{\dot{u}}}+(\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\dot{u}})p\right\}}{\,{d}x}+i\int_{\Sigma}{p\overline{\dot{u}}}{\,{d}s}=\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\overline{\dot{u}}}{\,{d}x}.

(24)

Next, we choose
\psi=p\in{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega)
in (
18
) and note that
B={\operatorname{div}}_{\tau}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=0
on
\varSigma
, for any
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
, to obtain

a(\dot{u},p)=\mathcal{M}(u,p)
.
Comparing this equation with the sesquilinear form
a
given in (
6
), with
\varphi=\dot{u}
and
\psi=p
, we arrive at the equation
a(\dot{u},p)=\mathcal{M}(u,p)=\mathcal{M}
.

Now, conjugating Equation (
24
) gives (after computations detailed in Appendix
A
):

-\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\dot{u}}{\,{d}x}=\mathcal{M}=-\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\nabla{u}}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Gamma}{\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\overline{p}}\theta_{n}}{\,{d}s}.

(25)

Finally, by comparing the imaginary parts on both sides of this equation and returning to (
23
), we obtain the desired shape derivative:

dJ(\varOmega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]=-\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\nabla u_{2}}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{u_{2}\,\dot{u}_{2}}{\,{d}x}=\int_{\Gamma}{\left\{\sigma\left(\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{p_{2}}-\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{2}}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{p_{1}}\right)\right\}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\,{d}s}.

∎

Remark 1

(Necessary condition)
.

Assume
\varOmega^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}
solves Problem
1
, i.e.,
u_{2}=0
in
\varOmega^{\star}
; then
\varOmega^{\star}
is stationary for Problem
2
, hence
dJ(\varOmega^{\star})[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]=0
for all
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
.

From now on,
\varOmega^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}
is called a

of
J
if it satisfies Remark

.

3.3
Second-order shape derivative of the cost function at the critical shape

In this section, we derive the second-order shape derivative of the cost function using the material derivatives of
u
and
p
.
Direct computation via the shape derivatives of
u
and
p
requires
u,p\in\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{3}(\varOmega)\cap{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega)
, which holds if
\varOmega\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}\cap C^{2,1}
and
(f,g)\in H^{5/2}(\varOmega)\times H^{3/2}(\varOmega)

[
9
]
.
By contrast, expressing the second-order derivative in terms of material derivatives relaxes this requirement, allowing
\varOmega\in C^{1,1}
.

Proposition 6

(Shape Hessian of
J
)
.

Assume that
\varOmega
,
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}
, and the Cauchy pair
(f,g)
possess sufficient regularity and are admissible so that
J
is twice shape differentiable.
Then, its shape Hessian at a critical shape
\varOmega^{\star}
is given by

d^{2}J(\varOmega^{\star})[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]=\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}{h[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\,{d}s}=\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}{\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\,{d}s},

where
u_{2}=\Im\{u\}
,
u
solves (
5
) with
\varOmega=\varOmega^{\star}
, and
w=w_{1}+iw_{2}=\Re\{w\}+i\Im\{w\}\in\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})\cap{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega^{\star})
.
The adjoint variable
w
satisfies the following problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\operatorname{div}(\sigma\nabla w)+\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla w+(\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{b}}})w&=&\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}&\text{ in $\varOmega^{\star}$},\\
w&=&0&\text{on $\varGamma^{\star}$},\\
\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w}+w\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}-iw&=&0&\text{on $\varSigma$}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(26)

Remark 2
.

Notice that
w
is simply the derivative of
p
with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
while
\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}
is the derivative of
p
with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}
(cf.
[
48
,
45
]
).

Proof.

Our assumptions ensure the existence of
\ddot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\in H^{1}(\varOmega)
.
Then, differentiating
J
twice with respect to
\varOmega
, first along
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
and subsequently along
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}
, we obtain

d^{2}J(\varOmega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\left(\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\left|{u_{2}}\right|^{2}+2\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}u_{2}\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\right)}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{\left(\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}+u_{2}\ddot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\right)}{\,{d}x}.

At
\varOmega=\varOmega^{\star}
, we have
u_{2}=0
, so we get
d^{2}J(\varOmega^{\star})[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]=\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}}{\,{d}x}
.
Our objective is to rewrite this expression in terms of an appropriate adjoint variable, eliminating
\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
and
\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}
.
To this end, we multiply the first equation in (
26
) by
\overline{\dot{u}}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
, integrate over
\varOmega^{\star}
, and take the complex conjugate, yielding:

\displaystyle\hat{a}(\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w):=-\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\sigma\nabla\overline{w}\cdot\nabla{\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}{\,{d}x}-\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{(\bm{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})\overline{w}}{\,{d}x}-i\int_{\Sigma}{\overline{w}\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\,{d}s}=\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\,{d}x}.

(27)

Next, in (
18
), we set
\psi=w
and replace
\dot{u}
with
\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
, yielding

a(\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w)=\mathcal{M}^{\star}({u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w),

where
a
is the sesquilinear form defined in (
6
) with
\varphi=\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
,
\psi=w
, and
\varOmega=\varOmega^{\star}
. That is,

a(\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w)=-\hat{a}(\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w),

(28)

with
\hat{a}
given in (
27
).
Equation (
28
) then implies

-\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\,{d}x}=\mathcal{M}^{\star}({u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}},w)=-\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\nabla u_{1}}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}{\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\overline{w}}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\theta_{n}}{\,{d}s}.

Taking the imaginary parts on both sides, we finally obtain the desired expression:

d^{2}J(\varOmega^{\star})[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]=\int_{\Omega^{\star}}{\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\,{d}x}=\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}{\sigma\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\theta_{n}}{\,{d}s}.

∎

The preceding calculation shows that the shape Hessian at a critical shape can be obtained without computing the state’s shape derivative, though an appropriate adjoint must be introduced.
Higher regularity of the domain, deformation fields, and data is required only initially; in the final expression,
H^{2}
-regularity ensures the kernel is well-defined, specifically to interpret the traces of the normal derivatives of
u
and the adjoint
w
, for which it suffices that
u,w\in\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})\cap{\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{V}}}}(\varOmega^{\star})
.
Thus, the analysis can be restricted to
\varOmega^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}
, with deformation fields in
\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
and the data regularity of Assumption
2.1
.

3.4
Compactness of the Hessian at a critical shape

Having computed the shape Hessian at the stationary domain
\varOmega^{\star}
, we now analyze the stability of the shape optimization problem. In particular, we show its ill-posedness by demonstrating the lack of coercivity of the bilinear form associated with the Hessian in the energy space
H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
. For further discussion on well-posedness and ill-posedness in shape optimization, see
[
19
,
20
,
26
,
28
]
.

The main result of this subsection is:

Proposition 7

(Compactness at a critical shape)
.

The Riesz operator corresponding to the shape Hessian
d^{2}J(\varOmega^{\star})
defined from
H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
to
H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
is compact.

Proof.

For
\varOmega^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}
and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\subset C^{1,1}(\overline{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\theta_{n}=\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}
,
\tilde{\theta}_{n}=\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}\in H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
,
\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\in H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
, and
\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]\in H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
.

We introduce the following function mappings associated with
\varGamma^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})^{d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})^{d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sigma\,\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}],

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\,\theta_{n}.

Thus, we can write

d^{2}J(\varOmega^{\star})[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]=\langle\mathcal{S}(\theta_{n}),\mathcal{T}(\tilde{\theta}_{n})\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star}),\,H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})}=\int_{\Gamma^{\star}}{\sigma\,\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]\,\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{u_{1}}\,\theta_{n}}{\,{d}s}.

On the one hand, the operator
\mathcal{T}
is continuous since multiplication of

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u_{1}\in H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
by the
C^{0,1}
function
\theta_{n}

is a continuous operation on
H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
.
On the other hand, we claim that
\mathcal{S}
is a compact operator.
To prove this claim, we decompose
\mathcal{S}
as
\mathcal{S}={S}_{3}\circ{S}_{2}\circ{S}_{1}\circ{S}_{0}
, where

\displaystyle{S}_{0}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})^{d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{1}(\varOmega^{\star}),

\displaystyle{S}_{1}:H^{1}(\varOmega^{\star})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}},

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\longmapsto w,

\displaystyle{S}_{2}:\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star}),

\displaystyle{S}_{3}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star}),

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\longmapsto\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}},

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sigma\psi,

with the following properties:

•

{S}_{0}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})^{d}\to H^{1}(\varOmega^{\star})
is given by
{S}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}})=\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}
, where
\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}=\Im\{\dot{u}_{\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\}
denotes the material derivative of
u_{2}=\Im\{u\}
in direction
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}
. This operator is continuous.

•

{S}_{1}:H^{1}(\varOmega^{\star})\to\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})
is defined by
{S}_{1}(\psi)=w
, where
w
solves the adjoint problem (
26
).
Given
\psi=\dot{u}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}}\in H^{1}(\varOmega^{\star})
,
\sigma\in W^{1,\infty}(D)^{d\times d}
, and
\mathbf{b}\in W^{1,\infty}(D)^{d}
, elliptic regularity for
\varOmega^{\star}\in C^{1,1}
ensures
w\in\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})
and continuity of
{S}_{1}
.

•

{S}_{2}:\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\varOmega^{\star})\to H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
is the trace operator
{S}_{2}(w)=\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}
, where
w_{2}=\Im\{w\}
. This operator is also continuous.

•

{S}_{3}:H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})\to H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
is defined by
{S}_{3}(\psi)=\sigma\psi
, with
\sigma\in W^{1,\infty}(D)^{d\times d}
. Since
\sigma|_{\varGamma^{\star}}
is bounded, multiplication by
\sigma|_{\varGamma^{\star}}
is continuous from
H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
to
H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
.
The embedding
H^{1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})\hookrightarrow H^{-1/2}(\varGamma^{\star})
is compact by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem for the
C^{1,1}
boundary
\varGamma^{\star}

[
1
]
.

Therefore, the composition satisfies
\mathcal{S}(\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}})={S}_{3}\big({S}_{2}\big({S}_{1}\big({S}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}})\big)\big)\big)=\sigma\,\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{w_{2}}[\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{\theta}}}]
.
Since
{S}_{0}
,
{S}_{1}
, and
{S}_{2}
are continuous and
{S}_{3}
involves a compact embedding, it follows that
\mathcal{S}
is compact, as required.
∎

Remark 3
.

Proposition
7
reveals the inherent instability of the optimization problem
2
.
Specifically, near the critical shape
\varOmega^{\star}=D\setminus\overline{\omega}^{\star}
and for small perturbations
t>0
, the functional
J
behaves as its second-order expansion.
Therefore, it is not possible to guarantee an estimate like
Ct\sqrt{J(\varOmega_{t})}
with a constant
C
that is independent of the deformation vector field
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
.
This indicates that the shape gradient’s sensitivity varies significantly with the direction of deformation, becoming ineffective for highly oscillatory perturbations where
J
exhibits degeneracy.

Perimeter or surface-area regularization can stabilize shape optimization under noisy data
[
3
,
17
,
48
]
. However, its necessity depends on boundary conditions, noise level, and shape regularity.
For example, certain second-order generalized impedance conditions allow acceptable reconstructions without explicit regularization
[
3
,
14
,
13
]
. Alternatively, ADMM-based methods achieve stable reconstructions without classical regularization. In subsection
4.4
, we introduce a tailored ADMM approach for shape identification with complex PDE constraints, enabling improved reconstructions under noisy data and for geometries with pronounced concavities.

4
Numerical Algorithms and Examples

We implement the proposed method using a FEM-based descent algorithm driven by the shape gradient, following
[
17
,
18
]
. The first subsection summarizes the conventional numerical approach for clarity and self-containment.

4.1
Conventional numerical scheme for shape optimization

A natural descent direction for
J
is
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=-G\bm{\mathbf{n}}
with
G\in L^{2}(\varGamma)
and
G\not\equiv 0
.
However, this may lead to unstable reconstructions and a deterioration of mesh quality due to the formation of irregular or poorly shaped elements.
To address this issue, we adopt an
H^{1}
-Riesz representation of the shape gradient
[
25
,
8
]
; that is, we seek
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in H_{\varSigma,0}^{1}(\varOmega)^{d}
such that

c_{b}\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}:\nabla\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}x}+(1-c_{b})\int_{\Gamma}{\nabla_{\varGamma}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}:\nabla_{\varGamma}\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}s}=-\int_{\Gamma}{G\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}s},\quad\forall\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\in H_{\varSigma,0}^{1}(\varOmega)^{d},

(29)

with
c_{b}\in(0,1]
.
In this work,
c_{b}
is chosen as
0.7

The resulting Sobolev gradient
[
42
]
smoothly extends
-G\bm{\mathbf{n}}
into
\varOmega
, with the tangential term improving regularity.
For a discussion on (discrete) shape gradient flows, we refer readers to
[
25
]
.

To compute the
k
th domain approximation
\varOmega^{k}
, we follow these steps:

1.
Initialization

Select an initial shape
\varOmega^{0}
.

2.
Iteration

For
k=0,1,2,\ldots
:

2.1

Solve the state and adjoint state systems on the current domain
\varOmega^{k}
.

2.2

Select a step size
t^{k}>0
and compute the update vector
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{k}
in
\varOmega^{k}
.

2.3

Update the domain via
\varOmega^{k+1}=(\mathrm{id}+t^{k}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{k})\varOmega^{k}
.

3.
Stopping Test

Repeat the iteration until a convergence criterion is satisfied.

In Step 2.2, we compute
t^{k}=\mu J(\varOmega^{k})/|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{k}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\varOmega^{k})^{d}}
following
[
49
, p. 281]
, with
\mu>0
, reduced if necessary to avoid mesh inversion.
We set
\mu=0.1
in 2D and
0.01
in 3D.
The algorithm stops after a fixed number of iterations or if
t^{k}<10^{-8}
.

We test the method in 2D and 3D. Starting with a 2D reconstruction, we examine the effects of constant and spatially varying
\sigma
alongside spatially varying
\bm{\mathbf{b}}
, and introduce an ADMM modification to address observed limitations.
The resulting CCBM–ADMM scheme is then applied to 3D, demonstrating robustness against non-convex obstacles and noisy data.
All computations were performed on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 chip with 16GB RAM main memory, via
FreeFem++

[
36
]
.

4.4
Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers in shape optimization setting

In the previous discussion, we highlighted the difficulty of recovering concavities in non-convex obstacles due to noise and spatially varying diffusion and drift. To address this, we introduce an ADMM-based modification of the algorithm. This modification is applied to the 3D setting in subsection
4.6
and discussed in subsection

2

Problem 3
.

Given constants
a
and
b
with
b\geqslant a
, find the shape
\omega^{\ast}
in the admissible set

\mathcal{A}=\Bigl\{\omega\in\mathcal{A}\mid a\leqslant u_{1}\leqslant b\ \text{a.e. in }\varOmega\Bigr\}

5
Conclusion

We have introduced a novel shape optimization method for inverse advection–diffusion problems.
It embeds the coupled complex boundary method (CCBM) within an ADMM-inspired augmented Lagrangian framework.
The scheme uses a Sobolev gradient method that naturally achieves mesh smoothing.
As a result, it eliminates the need for explicit perimeter or surface-area regularization, which is typically required to stabilize reconstructions from noisy measurements.

Numerical experiments in two and three dimensions demonstrate accurate recovery of complex inclusion shapes, with marked improvement for concave or complex features where conventional methods struggle.
By employing a carefully designed adjoint formulation alongside partial gradients, the CCBM–ADMM strategy further enhances reconstruction and consistently attains lower cost values, even when the smoothness assumptions required in the analysis are not fully satisfied.

Overall, these results highlight the effectiveness of the CCBM–ADMM framework, supporting recent evidence
[
17
,
50
]
that ADMM-based methods effectively handle noisy and geometrically complex inverse shape problems.
