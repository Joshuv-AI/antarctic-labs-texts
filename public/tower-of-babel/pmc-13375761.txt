Bioinformatics-driven dissection of PANoptosis-related molecular subtypes and tumor immune microenvironment heterogeneity in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Wen-ting Pei, Chun-lei Liu, Xiao-ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PANoptosis, an integrated programmed cell death modality encompassing apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, exerts pleiotropic effects on tumor immune surveillance and immune escape. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying PANoptosis in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remain poorly elucidated. Here, we performed bioinformatic analyses of AML bone marrow transcriptomic data from the TARGET database, aiming to define PANoptosis-associated molecular subtypes, characterize tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) heterogeneity, and identify candidate regulatory genes and small-molecule binders. We identified 68 PANoptosis-related genes and used unsupervised clustering to resolve four molecular subtypes (C1–C4) with distinct clinical outcomes and TIME profiles. The C2 subtype exhibited robust CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell and natural killer (NK) cell activity linked to favorable prognosis, whereas the C1 subtype displayed immune suppression and poor survival. We identified an IGF1–CCL2–CCL4 gene set correlated with PANoptosis regulation and enriched in Toll-like receptor signaling. In silico molecular docking suggested SB216763 as a candidate ligand for these targets, though this requires experimental validation. Our findings outline PANoptosis-associated heterogeneity in pediatric AML and provide a stratification framework for future mechanistic and translational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07082-1.

Introduction

Pediatric acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematopoietic malignancy characterized by uncontrolled proliferation and differentiation arrest of myeloid precursors. It accounts for approximately 20% of childhood leukemias, with a 5-year overall survival rate below 50% [1]. Despite advances in risk-stratified therapy, conventional chemotherapy remains limited by severe toxicity and variable treatment responses [1]. Pediatric AML exhibits unique genetic and immunological features distinct from adult AML, including a high frequency of core-binding factor translocations and specific epigenetic alterations [2]. Biological heterogeneity directly contributes to divergent clinical outcomes and underscores the urgent need for molecularly guided individualized strategies beyond conventional mutation‑centric classification [2].

Among emerging molecular pathways, PANoptosis —an integrated programmed cell death modality that coordinates apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis — has been shown to modulate tumor immune surveillance and immune escape in solid tumors. [3, 4]. In adult AML, a PANoptosis-based prognostic model has recently demonstrated potential in predicting immunotherapy responses [5]. However, the distribution, regulatory logic, and clinical impact of PANoptosis in pediatric AML remain uncharacterized.

Molecular subtype heterogeneity and tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) are major determinants of AML treatment outcomes [6, 7]. Transcriptome-based subtyping has refined pediatric AML classification, but the links between PANoptosis, molecular subtypes, and TIME heterogeneity remain unclear [8, 12]. The TIME in AML exerts dual roles: it can mediate anti-leukemic effects via CD8⁺T cells and NK cells, but also fosters an immunosuppressive niche through regulatory cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [9, 11]. Single-cell studies have revealed distinct immune profiles in pediatric AML bone marrow [10, 12]. We hypothesized that PANoptosis-related gene expression defines robust molecular subtypes and underlies TIME diversity in pediatric AML.

In this study, we therefore asked: can PANoptosis-related gene expression profiles stratify pediatric AML into molecular subtypes with distinct immune microenvironment characteristics? To address this, we systematically curated PANoptosis-related genes, constructed regulatory networks, identified PANoptosis-associated molecular subtypes, and quantified TIME heterogeneity using multiple immune deconvolution tools. We further derived a candidate regulatory gene set and performed in silico docking to identify potential small-molecule ligands. These results provide a transcriptomic stratification framework for PANoptosis in pediatric AML and support future functional validation and precision therapy development [13].

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Transcriptomic RNA-seq data from pediatric AML bone marrow samples were obtained from the Therapeutically Applied Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) database (https://ocg.cancer.gov/programs/target). Sample selection: All available pediatric AML bone marrow samples with complete transcriptomic data and survival information were included. This resulted in 184 pediatric AML samples and 412 normal bone marrow controls (Supplementary Table S1). Raw count data were downloaded as HTSeq-counts.

Preprocessing pipeline: Quality control: the tinyarray R package was used to remove genes with zero expression in > 25% of samples. Normalization: trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization was applied using edgeR to adjust for library size and composition effects. Annotation: rrocessed counts were mapped to the human reference genome hg38 (GRCh38) using rtracklayer for gene symbol assignment. Filtering: genes with low expression (counts per million < 1 in fewer than 10 samples) were excluded. No additional batch correction was applied as all samples originated from a single TARGET study arm.

Identification of PANoptosis-related genes

PANoptosis-related genes were curated through a systematic literature search in PubMed (January 2010–December 2024) using the following search strategy:

("PANoptosis" [MeSH] OR "PANoptosome" [MeSH] OR "pyroptosis" [MeSH] OR "necroptosis" [MeSH] OR "apoptosis" [MeSH]) AND ("gene" [Title/Abstract] OR "regulator" [Title/Abstract] OR "marker" [Title/Abstract]).

Inclusion criteria: (1) validated roles in two or more cell death modalities; (2) reported in leukemia or immune-inflammatory pathways; (3) supported by STRING and GeneCards. The final 68 genes (including RIPK1, RIPK3, CASP1, NLRP3, AIM2, GSDMD, MLKL, etc.) are listed in Supplementary Table S2, along with the PubMed ID of the evidence source and the specific PANoptosis pathway(s) each gene participates in.

Construction of PANoptosis regulatory network

Pearson correlation analysis of PANoptosis-related gene expression was performed using the corrplot R package. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) and visualized with Cytoscape. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (EnrichGO and enrichKEGG functions). Association networks between genes and functional/pathway sets were plotted using the cnetplot function (Enrichplot R package). Jaccard correlation coefficients (JC) between functional/pathway sets were calculated using the pairwise_termsim algorithm, and correlation networks were visualized with the applyplot function. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed using the survival R package to evaluate the association between PANoptosis-related gene expression and overall survival (OS). Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant; none met this threshold.

Classification of PANoptosis subtypes

Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) was applied to the expression matrix of 68 PANoptosis-related genes using the NMF R package (version 0.24). The optimal rank (k) was determined by evaluating k = 2 to 7 based on three criteria: (1) cophenetic correlation coefficient (highest at k = 4, value 0.98), (2) residual sum of squares (elbow point at k = 4), and (3) average silhouette width (maximum at k = 4, average silhouette = 0.72). Clustering stability was assessed using 100 bootstrap resampling iterations; consensus clustering scores > 0.85 for all four subtypes were considered stable. PCA, tSNE, and UMAP were used solely for visualization of the NMF-derived clusters, not as standalone validation of biological subtypes.

TIME analysis

Nine computational algorithms (Cibersort, IPS, ConsensusTME, xCell, Estimate, MCP-counter, EPIC, quanTIseq, ABIS) were integrated to provide a consensus view of TIME. Rationale for method selection: No single algorithm is optimized for pediatric AML bone marrow; each has different strengths (e.g., CIBERSORT for lymphocyte subsets, EPIC for solid-tumor-derived markers, quanTIseq for myeloid cell deconvolution). Using nine algorithms allowed cross-validation and reduced algorithm-specific bias.

Handling of conflicting outputs: For each immune cell type, if different algorithms gave divergent abundance rankings, we adopted a majority-vote approach: a cell type was considered "relatively enriched" in a given subtype if at least 5 of the 9 algorithms agreed. For algorithms originally designed for solid tumors (e.g., EPIC), we restricted interpretation to well-validated markers known to be expressed in bone marrow (e.g., CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, monocytes, macrophages). All algorithms were run with default parameters as implemented in their respective R packages.

Identification and functional annotation of core regulatory genes

Differential expression analysis between PANoptosis subtypes was performed using the edgeR package with the glmQLFTest function. Common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across all pairwise subtype comparisons were visualized using Venn diagrams. LASSO regression models (glmnet R package) were applied to the common DEGs plus the 68 PANoptosis genes to select core genes with non-zero coefficients. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. A PANoptosis risk score model was constructed via LASSO-Cox regression, and its prognostic value was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. No external validation cohort was available, so reported AUC values reflect internal performance only and may overestimate real-world accuracy.

Small-molecule docking

Target structure selection: The protein structures of IGF1 (PDB ID: 1BQT, resolution 2.0 Å), CCL2 (PDB ID: 1DOL, resolution 1.8 Å), and CCL4 (PDB ID: 1JE4, resolution 1.9 Å) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). These structures were selected because they represent the full-length or ligand-binding domain with experimentally resolved active sites.

Structure preparation: Using AutoDockTools (version 1.5.6), we: (1) removed all water molecules, (2) added polar hydrogens, (3) assigned Gasteiger charges, and (4) merged non-polar hydrogens. Binding pocket definition: The binding site was defined as the region occupied by the native ligand in each crystal structure. For IGF1 (no native ligand in 1BQT), the pocket was defined based on the known IGF1 receptor binding interface (residues 24–37). Grid boxes of 20 × 20 × 20 Å3 were centered on the respective binding pockets.

Docking parameters: AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0) was used with exhaustiveness = 16 and num_modes = 9. The small-molecule library (198 compounds) was sourced from the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene. org/). SB216763 was identified as the top hit based on binding energy < −7.0 kcal/mol for all three targets. Crucially, these are in silico binding predictions only; no functional assays (e.g., SPR, cellular viability, target engagement) were performed. Therefore, all statements about "inhibition" or "therapeutic potential" are strictly hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as demonstrated biological activity.

Univariate Cox regression: After Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing, no individual PANoptosis-related gene reached statistical significance (all FDR > 0.05). This indicates that single-gene analyses have limited prognostic value in this pediatric cohort, supporting the need for multi-gene or pathway-level models. PANoptosis subtype classification

NMF clustering identified four optimal PANoptosis subtypes (C1-C4) with stable cluster assignments (bootstrap consensus > 0.85). Heatmap analysis revealed distinct PANoptosis-related gene expression patterns across subtypes. PCA, tSNE, and UMAP visually confirmed separation of the four NMF-derived clusters. KM survival analysis demonstrated significant prognostic differences: the C2 subtype exhibited the most favorable OS, and the C1 subtype the poorest (log-rank p < 0.01, Fig. 2).Fig. 2Analysis of PANoptosis subtypes. Tumor immune microenvironment heterogeneity across subtypes

Using the majority-vote consensus from nine algorithms, the C2 subtype was characterized by relatively higher CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell and NK cell signatures compared to other subtypes, suggesting a potentially more immune-active TIME. The C4 subtype showed lower tumor purity (by ESTIMATE) and relatively higher dendritic cell and M1 macrophage signatures. In contrast, the C1 subtype exhibited higher tumor purity and lower CD8⁺ T-cell/NK cell signatures (Fig. 3). Core regulatory genes and regulatory axis identification

Venn diagram analysis identified 10 common DEGs across all pairwise subtype comparisons, including CCL2 and CCL4. LASSO regression of common DEGs plus the 68 PANoptosis genes selected seven core genes with non-zero coefficients, including IGF1. GO and KEGG enrichment of these core genes highlighted the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (adjusted p = 0.003). PPI network analysis revealed a connected subnetwork involving IGF1, CCL2, and CCL4.

Important clarification: We refer to this as a correlative gene set or hypothesized regulatory axis, not a validated "critical mediator." The co-expression and PPI connections are consistent with potential regulatory relationships, but causality (e.g., IGF1 directly controlling CCL2/CCL4 expression) has not been demonstrated in this study. The LASSO-Cox-derived PANoptosis risk score model showed internal AUC values of 0.78 (3-year) and 0.75 (5-year) in the TARGET cohort. Discussion

PANoptosis integrates multiple programmed cell death pathways and regulates inflammatory and immune responses in cancer [3, 14]. Our study systematically defines PANoptosis-related molecular subtypes and TIME heterogeneity in pediatric AML. We identified 68 PANoptosis-associated genes forming a core network centered on cell death and immune signaling. Individual genes showed modest prognostic value, supporting multi-gene stratification [5, 15]. Meanwhile, our findings demonstrated that pediatric AML could be stratified into four molecular subtypes based on PANoptosis-related genes, each associated with distinct TIME features and clinical outcomes. However, several important limitations and interpretive cautions must be emphasized.

Subtype interpretation: The C2 subtype’s favorable prognosis was associated with computational estimates of higher CD8⁺ T-cell and NK cell infiltration. This raises the hypothesis that C2 patients might be more sensitive to immunotherapies (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors), but this hypothesis was not tested and requires direct investigation. Conversely, the C1 subtype showed features of an immunosuppressive TIME and poor prognosis. One speculative explanation is that C1 tumors may exhibit relative PANoptosis resistance, possibly involving anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members or reduced RIPK3 expression; however, none of these mechanisms were measured in this study, and alternative explanations are equally plausible.

IGF1-CCL2-CCL4 correlative set: We identified a co-expressed gene set involving IGF1, CCL2, and CCL4 that was enriched in the TLR signaling pathway. IGF1 inhibits apoptosis via the PI3K/Akt pathway [16, 17], while CCL2 and CCL4 mediate immune cell recruitment [18, 19]. This axis may provide a mechanistic link between growth factor signaling, chemokine-driven immune recruitment, and PANoptosis regulation. While the PPI and co-expression data are consistent with a potential regulatory relationship, this remains correlational. The phrase "regulatory axis" is used descriptively to indicate a set of interconnected genes in our network analysis, not as a claim of demonstrated causal regulation. Future studies using gain/loss-of-function experiments are required to test whether IGF1 functionally regulates CCL2/CCL4 expression in pediatric AML cells.

SB216763 docking prediction: In silico docking identified SB216763, a GSK-3 inhibitor, as a candidate ligand for IGF1, CCL2, and CCL4. GSK-3 modulates NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling and regulates cell death and inflammation [19, 20]. While these results support further preclinical testing, they are computational predictions and do not constitute evidence of therapeutic efficacy in pediatric AML. We therefore present SB216763 as a computational lead candidate for future experimental screening, not as a validated inhibitor or therapeutic strategy.

Risk score model: The PANoptosis risk score model showed promising internal performance (AUC 0.78 at 3 years, 0.75 at 5 years) in the TARGET cohort. However, without independent external validation in a separate pediatric AML cohort, these values may overestimate true prognostic accuracy. Model performance should be re-evaluated in future multi-center studies.

Overall study limitations: (1) All findings are derived from bulk transcriptomic data; single-cell resolution is needed to dissect cell-type-specific PANoptosis gene expression. (2) Functional validation (in vitro and in vivo) is absent for all proposed molecular interactions. (3) The subtype classification and risk score require external validation. (4) Immune cell estimates are computational and may not fully reflect true biological abundances.

Conclusion

We constructed a PANoptosis-centered correlational network, classified pediatric AML into four molecular subtypes with distinct TIME characteristics, identified the IGF1-CCL2-CCL4 gene set as a hypothesis-generating candidate axis, and performed preliminary in silico docking that nominates SB216763 as a computational lead for future testing. These findings provide a framework for further investigation of PANoptosis in pediatric AML but should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, not conclusive.
