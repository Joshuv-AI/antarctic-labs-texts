Abstract
. The closed unit bidisc 𝔻 ¯ 2 \overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2} is known to be a spectral set for any pair ( T 1 , T 2 ) (T_{1},T_{2}) of commuting contractions. When each T i T_{i} is pure and has finite defect, the pair admits a much smaller spectral set: the closure of a distinguished variety V V inside the bidisc 𝔻 2 {\mathbb{D}}^{2} . We find conditions on ( T 1 , T 2 ) (T_{1},T_{2}) that guarantee that the closure of V V is a minimal spectral set. In addition, we examine the relationship between V V and the annihilating ideal Ann ⁡ ( T 1 , T 2 ) \operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}) in H ∞ ​ ( 𝔻 2 ) H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}) . While V V is typically strictly larger than the zero set of Ann ⁡ ( T 1 , T 2 ) \operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}) , we isolate a natural constrained isometric co-extension ( S 1 , S 2 ) (S_{1},S_{2}) of ( T 1 , T 2 ) (T_{1},T_{2}) whose Taylor spectrum is contained in V V and is closely linked to the so-called support of Ann ⁡ ( T 1 , T 2 ) \operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}) . We also characterize when Ann ⁡ ( T 1 , T 2 ) \operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}) is the ideal of functions vanishing on the joint point spectrum of ( S 1 ∗ , S 2 ∗ ) (S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*}) .

1.
Introduction

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be commuting bounded linear operators on some Hilbert space
H
. We denote the Taylor spectrum of the pair by
\sigma(T_{1},T_{2})
.
Let
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a compact subset containing
\sigma(T_{1},T_{2})
, and let
\mathrm{C}(X)
denote the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra of continuous complex-valued functions on
X
. Let
{\mathcal{R}}(X)\subset\mathrm{C}(X)
denote the subalgebra of restrictions to
X
of rational functions with poles outside of
X
. We also let
{\mathcal{O}}(X)
denote the space of functions that are holomorphic on a neighbourhood of
X
. The Taylor functional calculus
[
21
, Theorem 30.7]
(see also the survey
[
12
]
) then gives a well-defined homomorphism

{\mathcal{O}}(X)\to B(H)

f\mapsto f(T_{1},T_{2}),\quad f\in{\mathcal{O}}(X).

We say that
X
is a
spectral set
for
(T_{1},T_{2})
if this homomorphism is contractive on
{\mathcal{R}}(X)
. When this homomorphism is completely contractive on
{\mathcal{R}}(X)
(i.e. contractive on every matrix level
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}({\mathcal{R}}(X))
) then we say that
X
is a
complete spectral set
for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

Because they lie at the interface of function theory and operator theory, spectral sets have long been of interest; see
[
23
]
and
[
19
, Chapter 37]
and the references therein. They still remain relevant to this day, being at the heart of the Crouzeix conjecture for instance
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
.

It is easy to show that any spectral set contains another spectral set that is minimal with respect to inclusion. The question of identifying such a minimal spectral set, however, is more delicate. In the case of a jointly subnormal pair, the Taylor spectrum is a spectral set
[
24
]
contained in every other spectral set, so it is clearly minimal. Beyond the subnormal case, fairly little is known regarding minimal spectral sets. In the single-variable case, the most definitive result in this direction was obtained a long time ago by Williams
[
30
]
(the notion of a spectral set for a single operator is defined completely analogously to the notion defined above for pairs). On the other hand, the multivariate case does not seem to have garnered much attention.

Suppose now that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
both have norm at most
1
. Let
{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
denote the open unit disc. It is well known that
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}^{2}}
is a spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
, by virtue of the classical inequality of Ando: rational functions with poles off
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}^{2}}
are uniform limits of polynomials. Interestingly, in many cases a much smaller set than
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}^{2}}
suffices.

Let
E
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and let
\Psi:{\mathbb{D}}\to B(E)
be a contractive holomorphic function. We put

V_{\Psi}=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}.

We say that
V_{\Psi}
is an
Agler–McCarthy variety
for the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
if

(1)

\|p(T_{1},T_{2})\|\leq\sup_{V_{\Psi}}|p|\quad\text{ for every polynomial }p.

When both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are pure contractions with finite defects, there is a rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi
such that
V_{\Psi}
is an Agler–McCarthy variety for
(T_{1},T_{2})
. This was first proved for pairs of matrices in a seminal paper of Agler–McCarthy
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, and later extended to the infinite-dimensional setting in
[
13
, Theorem 4.3]
. Some of these details appear at the beginning of Section
3
. Importantly, it follows from
[
2
, Theorem 1.12]
(see also
[
6
,
20
]
) that
\Psi
is a rational matrix-valued pure inner function precisely when
V_{\Psi}
is a so-called
distinguished
variety, in the sense that
V_{\Psi}\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
is non-empty and
\overline{V_{\Psi}}
exits
{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
through its distinguished boundary.

The study of distinguished varieties goes back at least to Rudin’s 1969 work
[
25
]
, and was followed by the landmark contributions of Agler–McCarthy
[
2
]
. Since then, these varieties have garnered significant interest, due to their deep connections with several branches of mathematics. For example, distinguished varieties arise naturally as uniqueness sets for solvable Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation problems on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, and they play an important role in strengthening Ando’s inequality, as discussed above. In addition, building on Fedorov’s work on unramified point-separating pairs of inner functions, Vegulla
[
29
, Theorem 3.4.4]
proved that every planar domain bounded by piecewise analytic curves is conformally equivalent to a distinguished variety, thereby providing a unified framework for understanding the function theory of multi-connected domains. More recently,
[
17
]
established a connection between distinguished varieties and the theory of essentially normal quotient modules, particularly in relation to the Arveson–Douglas conjecture. Furthermore, these varieties also appear in generalizations of Wermer’s maximality theorem on the bidisc
[
26
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Although it is not immediately clear from (
1
), we shall see below in Theorem
2.6
that if
V_{\Psi}
is an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for a pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
, then
\overline{V_{\Psi}}
is in fact a spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
. In particular, this means that
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}^{2}}
is
not
a minimal spectral set for such pairs. The first goal of the paper is to find conditions on
(T_{1},T_{2})
under which Agler–McCarthy varieties are in fact minimal spectral sets. We undertake this task in Section
2
.

As a first step, we take another look at the univariate setting. Given a nice domain
G\subset{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\overline{G}
is a spectral set for a single operator, we extend results from
[
30
]
and find a sufficient condition on the functional calculus for minimality of
\overline{G}
as a spectral set, provided that the spectrum of the operator does not meet the boundary of
G
(see Theorem
2.2
). In Theorem
2.3
, we show how this spectral assumption can be recast in
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebraic terms, using ideas from noncommutative Choquet theory. As an application, we show in Corollary
2.4
that there is a contraction
T
generating the Cuntz
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
(for
d\geq 2
) for which
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a minimal spectral set.

Turning back to the bivariate case, we obtain our first main result (Theorem
2.7
), which we summarize below. We denote by
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
the Banach algebra of bounded holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
.

Theorem A
.

Let
T_{1},T_{2}\in B(H)
be commuting contractions. Let
V\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
be an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.
Assume that

(i)

the spectrum of
T_{1}
is contained in
{\mathbb{D}}
, and

(ii)

there are non-constant functions
\varphi_{1}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
and
\varphi_{2}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
such that
1=\|\varphi_{1}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=\|\varphi_{2}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|
.

Then
\overline{V}
is a minimal spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

As far as we know, outside the subnormal setting this is the first instance of a result providing sufficient conditions for a spectral set to be minimal for a
pair
of commuting operators. In Corollary
2.8
, we exhibit a situation where condition (ii) above can be easily verified.

Our next objective is to identify an Agler–McCarthy variety (or a portion thereof) in terms of data uniquely determined by
T
. Henceforth,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
will be assumed to be pure and to have finite defects.
In particular, both
T_{1}
and the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
admit a weak-
*
continuous
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (see (
9
) below). We let

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})=\{f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}):f(T)=0\}\quad\text{and}\quad

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})=\{f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}):f(T_{1},T_{2})=0\}.

We let
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
be the zero set of
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. Following
[
2
]
and
[
13
]
, in Section
3
we recall the construction of a rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi
such that
V_{\Psi}
is an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

We begin our investigation with the observation that

Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset V_{\Psi}

(see (
11
)). Easy examples reveal that the inclusion is strict in general. It follows from the construction of
\Psi
that
V_{\Psi}
is the point spectrum of some isometric co-extension of the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
. This suggests that there may be a more suitable isometric co-extension that more accurately reflects the algebraic properties of
(T_{1},T_{2})
. Indeed, we construct the
constrained isometric co-extension

(S_{1}^{\Psi},S_{2}^{\Psi})
of
(T_{1},T_{2})
, acting on the space
K_{\Psi}
(see (
13
)). We then show that the set
\Omega_{\Psi}
, defined as the complex conjugate of the joint point spectrum of the pair
(S_{1}^{\Psi*},S_{2}^{\Psi*})
, coincides with
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
(Theorem
3.7
). We refine this result in Theorem
3.11
, which includes additional boundary information and describes the so-called support of
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})

[
7
]
in terms of
V_{\Psi}
and the full Taylor spectrum of
(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
. We summarize these findings in the following.

Theorem B
.

We have

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset\overline{V_{\Psi}}

and

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})).

This result extends to the bidisc some relationships between the support of the annihilating ideal and the spectrum that are known to hold in the disc and the ball
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
.

The next natural question is to relate
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
with the ideal
I(\Omega_{\Psi})
of functions in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
vanishing on
\Omega_{\Psi}
. Our second main result gives a complete answer under a mild assumption on the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
(see Theorem
3.12
).

Theorem C
.

Assume that
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is non-zero. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

The joint eigenvectors of
S_{1}^{\Psi*}
and
S_{2}^{\Psi*}
span a dense subspace in
K_{\Psi}
.

(ii)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})=I(Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))=I(\Omega_{\Psi})
.

(iii)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is a radical ideal in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.

(iv)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is generated by a Blaschke product with simple roots.

Notably, this result connects the spectral synthesis problem for a pair of commuting operators (see
[
4
]
and the references therein) to the algebraic structure of the annihilators.

2.
Minimal spectral sets in one and two variables

2.1.
The univariate case

Here and throughout,
B(H)
denotes the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
H
. We start by recordings some well-known properties regarding the Riesz–Dunford holomorphic functional calculus and spectral sets.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T\in B(H)
and let
A=\{f(T):f\in{\mathcal{O}}(\sigma(T))\}\subset B(H)
. Let
\pi:A\to B(K)
be a unital contractive homomorphism. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

Let
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(\sigma(T))
. Then,
\pi(f(T))=f(\pi(T))
.

(ii)

A spectral set for
T
is also a spectral set for
\pi(T)
.

Proof.

(i) The equality is easily verified when
f\in{\mathcal{R}}(\sigma(T))
. The general case follows from Runge’s approximation theorem along with the continuity property of the Riesz–Dunford functional calculus
[
21
, Theorem I.37]
.

(ii) Let
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a closed set that is spectral for
T
. For
f\in{\mathcal{R}}(X)
, we may apply (i) to see that

\|f(\pi(T))\|=\|\pi(f(T))\|\leq\|f(T)\|\leq\|f\|_{X}.

∎

Next, we give a criterion for minimality of spectral sets. This extends a result of Williams
[
30
, Theorem 9]
past the setting where
H
is finite-dimensional (or where
T
is compact). Our argument largely follows the scheme of proof thereof.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a bounded, connected, open set with boundary consisting of finitely many rectifiable simple closed curves. Let
T\in B(H)
be an operator admitting
\overline{G}
as spectral set.
Assume that

(i)

\sigma(T)
is contained in
G
, and

(ii)

there is a non-constant
\varphi\in H^{\infty}(G)
such that
\|\varphi(T)\|=\|\varphi\|_{G}=1
.

If
\|\varphi(T)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{X}
for some proper closed subset
X\subset\overline{G}
, then
X
is not a spectral set for
T
. In particular,
\overline{G}
is a minimal spectral set for
T
.

Proof.

By basic
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra theory, there is an irreducible
*
-representation
\pi:B(H)\to B(K)
along with unit vectors
\xi,\eta\in K
such that

(2)

1=\|\varphi\|_{G}=\|\varphi(T)\|=\langle\pi(\varphi(T))\xi,\eta\rangle=\langle\varphi(\pi(T))\xi,\eta\rangle

where the last equality follows from Lemma
2.1
.

Let
X\subset\overline{G}
be a proper closed subset for which
\|\varphi(T)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{X}
. Since
\pi
is contractive, we find

1=\|\varphi(\pi(T))\|=\|\varphi\|_{X}
. By another application of Lemma
2.1
, we see that it suffices to show that
X
cannot be a spectral set for
\pi(T)
. We may assume that
\sigma(\pi(T))\subset X
, for otherwise
X
plainly cannot be a spectral set for
\pi(T)
. Furthermore, note that
X
does not contain
G
, so without loss of generality, we may assume that
X=\overline{G}\setminus B
, where
B\subset G
is an open ball with closure disjoint from
\sigma(\pi(T))
.

Define the function
\omega:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(\pi(T))\to{\mathbb{C}}
as

\omega(z)=\langle\pi(T-zI)^{-1}\xi,\eta\rangle,\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(T).

Let
X^{\circ}
denote the interior of
X
, and consider the Hardy space
E^{1}(X^{\circ})
(see
[
15
, Chapter 10]
).
For
g\in H^{\infty}(X^{\circ})
, note that

\int_{\partial X}g(z)\omega(z)dz=\langle g(\pi(T))\xi,\eta\rangle

by properties of the Riesz–Dunford functional calculus. Assuming towards a contradiction that
X
is a spectral set for
\pi(T)
, this implies that

\left|\int_{\partial X}g(z)\omega(z)dz\right|\leq 1,\quad g\in H^{\infty}(X^{\circ})\quad\text{with }\|g\|=1

so by (
2
) the function
\varphi
is a solution to the extremal problem

\sup\left\{\int_{\partial X}g(z)\omega(z)dz:g\in H^{\infty}(X^{\circ}),\|g\|=1\right\}.

By a duality result of Havinson
[
18
]
analogous to
[
15
, Section 8.1]
, there is
h\in E^{1}(X^{\circ})
such that

\int_{\partial X}\varphi(z)(\omega(z)-h(z))dz=1=\int_{\partial X}|\omega(z)-h(z)|d|z|.

Therefore
\int_{\partial X}|\varphi(z)(\omega(z)-h(z))|d|z|=1
and

(3)

(1-|\varphi|)|\omega-h|=0\quad\text{ almost everywhere on }\partial X

with respect to arc length measure (see
[
30
, Theorem 5]
). Observe that
|\varphi|<1
on
\partial B
by the maximum modulus principle, since
\varphi
is non-constant on
G
. Thus,
\omega|_{\partial B}=h|_{\partial B}
almost everywhere by (
3
).

Next, by compactness of
\sigma(T)
, there is a set
U\subset G\setminus\overline{B}
containing
\sigma(\pi(T))
such that
U
is a finite union of open balls. Consider
Y=X\setminus U
. Then, both
\omega|_{Y^{\circ}}
and
h|_{Y^{\circ}}
belong to
E^{1}(Y^{\circ})
. Since these functions agree almost everywhere on
\partial B
, the Riesz theorem (see
[
15
, Theorem 10.3]
along with the discussion at the bottom of page 182) implies that they must agree almost everywhere on
\partial U
as well. But this is impossible: on one hand we have

\int_{\partial U}\varphi(z)h(z)dz=0
since
h\varphi
is holomorphic in
U
, while on the other hand

\int_{\partial U}\varphi(z)\omega(z)dz=\langle\varphi(\pi(T))\xi,\eta\rangle=1

because
\sigma(\pi(T))\subset U
.
∎

Next, we will show how condition (i) above can be encoded as a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebraic property of
T
. For this purpose, we require some machinery from Arveson’s theory of the noncommutative Choquet boundary
[
3
]
.

Let
A\subset B(H)
be a unital subalgebra. A unital
*
-representation
\chi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(A)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be a
boundary character
for
A
if, given a unital contractive linear map
\psi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(A)\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\chi|_{A}=\psi|_{A}
, we must have
\chi=\psi
on
\mathrm{C}^{*}(A)
. Recall also that the
numerical range
of an operator
T\in B(H)
is the set consisting of complex numbers of the form
\lambda(T)
where
\lambda:B(H)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a unital contractive linear map.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T\in B(H)
and let
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a spectral set for
T
. Let
A\subset B(H)
denote the unital subalgebra generated by
T
, and let
A_{X}=\{r(T):r\in{\mathcal{R}}(X)\}\subset B(H)
.
Then, the spectrum of
T
is contained in the interior of
X
provided that either of the following sets of conditions holds.

(a)

X
contains the numerical range of
T
and
A
admits no boundary character.

(b)

X
is a complete spectral set for
T
and
A_{X}
admits no boundary character.

Proof.

First note that since
X
is a spectral set for
T
, it must contain
\sigma(T)
. Thus, it suffices to show that
\sigma(T)
is disjoint from the boundary
\partial X
. Let
\zeta\in\partial X\cap\sigma(T)
.

(a) If
X
contains the numerical range
W
of
T
, then we must have
\zeta\in\sigma(T)\cap\partial W
, since
W
always contains
\sigma(T)
. Then, there is a boundary character
\chi
for
A
such that
\chi(T)=\zeta
by
[
3
, Theorem 3.1.2]
.

(b) If
X
is a complete spectral set for
T
, then the unital homomorphism
\rho_{0}:{\mathcal{R}}(X)\to B(H)
defined as

\rho_{0}(r)=r(T),\quad r\in{\mathcal{R}}(X)

is completely contractive. By Arveson’s extension theorem, there is a unital completely positive map
\rho:\mathrm{C}(X)\to B(H)
extending
\rho_{0}
.

Now,
T-\zeta I
is not invertible in the commutative Banach algebra
\overline{A_{X}}
, so there is a character
\chi_{0}:A_{X}\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\chi_{0}(T)=\zeta
. Let
\chi:B(H)\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a unital contractive linear map extending
\chi_{0}
. We will show that
\chi
is necessarily a
*
-homomorphism on
\mathrm{C}^{*}(A_{X})
, which immediately implies that
\chi|_{\mathrm{C}^{*}(A_{X})}
is a boundary character for
A_{X}
.

Note that

(4)

(\chi\circ\rho)(r)=\chi_{0}(r(T))=r(\zeta),\quad r\in{\mathcal{R}}(X).

By
[
16
, Theorem II.1.3]
, we see that
\zeta
lies in the Shilov boundary of
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(X)}
. In turn, by
[
16
, Theorem II.11.3]
, it follows that
\zeta
admis a unique representing measure, so (
4
) implies that
\chi\circ\rho
is the character on
\mathrm{C}(X)
of evaluation at
\zeta
. In particular,
\chi\circ\rho
is a
*
-homomorphism. For
r\in{\mathcal{R}}(X)
, we find by the Schwarz inequality
[
23
, Proposition 3.3]
that

\displaystyle\chi(r(T)^{*}r(T))

\displaystyle=\chi(\rho(r)^{*}\rho(r))\leq(\chi\circ\rho)(r^{*}r)

\displaystyle=|(\chi\circ\rho)(r)|^{2}=|\chi(r(T))|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\chi(r(T)^{*}r(T))

so
\chi(r(T)^{*}r(T))=|\chi(r(T))|^{2}
. Similarly
\chi(r(T)r(T)^{*})=|\chi(r(T))|^{2}
, so that
\chi
is a
*
-homomorphism by
[
23
, Theorem 3.18]
.
∎

We can now give an application of Theorem
2.2
. Recall that the Cuntz algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is the universal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra generated by isometries
s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}
satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}s_{j}^{*}=1
. A key property of this algebra is that, for
d\geq 2
, it is infinite-dimensional and simple
[
11
, Theorem 1.13]
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Then,
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is generated by a single contraction for which the closed unit disc is a minimal spectral set.

Proof.

There is
T\in{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
with
\|T\|=1
such that
\mathrm{C}^{*}(T)={\mathcal{O}}_{d}

[
22
]
. Note that
{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
coincides with the norm closure of the polynomials, by Runge’s approximation theorem. Thus, it follows from von Neumann’s inequality that
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a complete spectral set for
T
. Since
\|T\|=\|z\|
, to see that
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a minimal spectral set for
T
it suffices, by virtue of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
, to insure that
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
has no one-dimensional unital
*
-representations. But this is an immediate consequence of the fact that
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is simple and infinite-dimensional.
∎

2.2.
Minimal spectral sets in two variables

We now wish to apply our findings on minimality of spectral sets to Agler–McCarthy varieties. First, we prove something that was alluded to in the introduction, namely that (
1
) does indeed imply the formally stronger statement that a distinguished Agler–McCarthy variety for a commuting pair of contractions is a spectral set for the pair. We need a preliminary statement, which is likely well known to experts (see for instance
[
6
, Lemma 7.4]
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Psi
be a rational matrix-valued pure inner function on
{\mathbb{D}}
and let

V=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}.

Then, there is a polynomial
\psi
in two variables such that

\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:\psi(z,w)=0\}

and moreover this set coincides with
\overline{V}
. In particular,
\overline{V}
is polynomially convex.

Proof.

Consider the set

V^{\prime}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}

which is clearly closed since
\Psi
is continuous on
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Then,
\overline{V}\subset V^{\prime}
. Conversely, let
(\lambda,\mu)\in V^{\prime}
, so that
\det(\Psi(\lambda)-\mu I)=0
. Choose a sequence
(\lambda_{n})
in
{\mathbb{D}}
converging to
\lambda
. It follows that the sequence
(\det(\Psi(\lambda_{n})-\mu I))_{n}
converges to
0
. Hence, for each
n
there is
\mu_{n}\in\sigma(\Psi(\lambda_{n}))
such that
(\mu_{n})
converges to
\mu
. Since
V
is a distinguished variety
[
2
, Theorem 1.12]
, each
\mu_{n}
lies in the open disc
{\mathbb{D}}
. Hence,
((\lambda_{n},\mu_{n}))_{n}
is a sequence in
V
converging to
(\lambda,\mu)
. We conclude that
V^{\prime}=\overline{V}
.

Next, since
\Psi
is rational, so is the function

(z,w)\mapsto\det(\Psi(z)-wI)

so there is a polynomial
\psi
in two variables such that

\overline{V}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:\psi(z,w)=0\}.

The polynomial convexity statement now follows from a standard argument.
Given
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that

|p(\lambda,\mu)|\leq\max_{\overline{V}}|p|

for every polynomial
p
, it readily follows that that
\psi(\lambda,\mu)=0
. In addition,

|\lambda|\leq\|z_{1}\|_{\overline{V}}\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad|\mu|\leq\|z_{2}\|_{\overline{V}}\leq 1

so that
(\lambda,\mu)\in\overline{V}
. We conclude that
\overline{V}
is polynomially convex.
∎

We can now show that the closure of Agler–McCarthy distinguished varieties are spectral sets.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
T_{1},T_{2}\in B(H)
be commuting contractions. Assume that there is an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety
V\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
for
(T_{1},T_{2})
. Then,
\overline{V}
is a spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
, there is a polynomial
\psi
such that

\overline{V}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:\psi(z,w)=0\}.

We first claim that
\overline{V}
contains the Taylor spectrum of
(T_{1},T_{2})
. To see this, note that
\psi(T_{1},T_{2})=0
by (
1
). It then follows from the spectral mapping theorem
[
21
, Theorem 7.7]
that
\sigma(T_{1},T_{2})
is contained in the zero set of
\psi
inside of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}
, that is
\overline{V}
.

Next, let
r\in{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{V})
and choose polynomials
p,q
such that
r=p/q
, where
q
vanishes only outside of
\overline{V}
. Then,
q(T)
is invertible by
[
21
, Theorem 7.7]
again, so that
r(T)=p(T)q(T)^{-1}=\rho(r)
by
[
21
, Theorem 30.7]
. Thus

\|r(T)\|=\|\rho(r)\|\leq\|r\|_{\overline{V}}.

∎

Next, we apply Theorem
2.2
to examine the minimality of Agler–McCarthy varieties as spectral sets.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
T_{1},T_{2}\in B(H)
be commuting contractions. Let
V\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
be an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.
Assume that

(i)

the spectrum of
T_{1}
is contained in
{\mathbb{D}}
, and

(ii)

there are non-constant functions
\varphi_{1}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
and
\varphi_{2}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
such that
1=\|\varphi_{1}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=\|\varphi_{2}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|
.

Then
\overline{V}
is a minimal spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.6
that
\overline{V}
is a spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
, so we need only show that it is minimal. There is a rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi
on
{\mathbb{D}}
with the property that

V=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}.

Assume that there is a proper closed subset
X\subset\overline{V}
that is a spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
X=\overline{V}\setminus D
where
D\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
is some polydisc of radius
\delta>0
centred at some
(\alpha,\beta)\in V
.

Next,
\varphi_{1}
can be approximated uniformly on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{D}}
by a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})
. Since the spectrum of
T_{1}
is contained in
{\mathbb{D}}
, it follows that
\varphi_{1}(T_{1})
is the norm-limit of the sequence
(p_{n}(T_{1}))
, so that

\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p_{n}(T_{1})\|\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=\|\varphi_{1}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=1

as
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a spectral set for
T_{1}
by von Neumann’s inequality. Now,
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a spectral set for
T_{2}
as well, so we find

\|\varphi_{2}(T)\|\leq\|\varphi_{2}\|_{{\mathbb{D}}}=1.

The condition
\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|=1
then forces
\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\|=1=\|\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|
.

Using that
X
is spectral for
T
, there is
(\lambda,\mu)\in X
such that

1=\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|\leq|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)\varphi_{2}(\mu)|.

This implies

1=\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\|=|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|\quad\text{and}\quad 1=\|\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|=|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|.

Because
X
is a spectral set for
T
, it also follows that the set
X_{1}=\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\setminus B
is a spectral set for
T_{1}
, where
B=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z-\alpha|<\delta\}
. By virtue of (i) we may apply Theorem
2.2
to see that
\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\|>\|\varphi\|_{X_{1}}
, so
\lambda\notin X_{1}
and
\lambda\in B\subset{\mathbb{D}}
. Using that
V
is distinguished and
(\lambda,\mu)\in\overline{V}
while
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we conclude that
\mu\in{\mathbb{D}}
as well, so that
|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|<1
by the maximum modulus principle. This is a contradiction.
∎

We note here that condition (ii) above imposes strong restrictions on the nature of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
; see for instance
[
30
, Theorem 2]
. We end this section with a simple special case where the condition is satisfied.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
T_{1}\in B(H)
be an operator of norm
1
that commutes with an isometry
T_{2}\in B(H)
. Let
V\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
be an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for
(T_{1},T_{2})
. If the spectrum of
T_{1}
is contained in the open unit disc, then
\overline{V}
is a minimal spectral set for
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{1}(z)=\varphi_{2}(z)=z
. Then,

\|\varphi_{1}(T_{1})\varphi_{2}(T_{2})\|=\|T_{1}T_{2}\|=\|T_{2}T_{1}\|=1

since
T_{2}
is an isometry. We can thus apply Theorem
2.7
.
∎

3.
Agler–McCarthy varieties and the annihilator

Assume that we are given a pair of commuting contractions admits an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety. In this section, we aim to describe the variety using data determined by the pair. For this purpose, we start by recalling how Agler–McCarthy varieties arise in terms of dilation theory, following
[
2
]
and
[
13
]
.

3.1.
Hardy spaces preliminaries

Let
H^{2}
denote the Hardy space on the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
. For
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we denote the associated reproducing kernel vector by
k_{\lambda}\in H^{2}
, where
k_{\lambda}(z)=(1-\overline{\lambda}z)^{-1}
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
.
Recall that
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
denote the Banach algebras of bounded holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
and
{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
(respectively), equipped with the supremum norm. It is known that both
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
are dual spaces, where weak-
*
convergence of a sequence is characterized by bounded pointwise convergence.

We often will require vector-valued versions of these spaces. Let
E
be a Hilbert space. Let
H^{2}(E)
denote the Hilbert space of
E
-valued
H^{2}
functions, which we identify with
H^{2}\otimes E
. The set
\{k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi:\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}},\xi\in E\}
spans a dense subspace of
H^{2}\otimes E
.

Given another Hilbert space
E^{\prime}
, we denote by
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime},E))
the Banach space of
B(E^{\prime},E)
-valued bounded holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
. For

\Phi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime},E)),

we let
M_{\Phi}:H^{2}\otimes E^{\prime}\to H^{2}\otimes E
denote the corresponding multiplication operator acting as

(M_{\Phi}f)(z)=\Phi(z)f(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},f\in H^{2}(E^{\prime}).

Then,

\|\Phi\|_{H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime},E))}=\|M_{\varphi}\|_{B(H^{2}\otimes E^{\prime},H^{2}\otimes E)}.

We say that
\Phi
is
inner
if
M_{\Phi}
is an isometry. We have

(5)

M_{\Phi}^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)=k_{\lambda}\otimes\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\xi

for each
\xi\in E
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
. We need a well-known elementary fact about eigenvectors of multipliers.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Phi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E)),\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
f\in H^{2}(E)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

f
is a non-zero element of
\ker((M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}-\overline{\lambda}I)
.

(ii)

We have
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and there is a non-zero
\xi\in E
such that
f=k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi
.

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): This follows directly from (
5
).

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Assume that
f
is a non-zero element of
\ker((M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}-\overline{\lambda}I)
. Write
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\otimes\xi_{n}
for some
\xi_{n}\in E
satisfying
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|\xi_{n}\|^{2}=1
. Using the equality
(M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}f=\overline{\lambda}f
, a straighforward comparison of Fourier coefficients reveals that
\xi_{n}=\overline{\lambda}\xi_{n-1}
for each
n\geq 1
. Hence,
|\lambda|<1
and

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{\lambda}^{n}z^{n}\otimes\xi_{0}=k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi_{0}.

∎

Next, we record a crucial factorization property. Let
E,E^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime\prime}
be Hilbert spaces. Let
\Phi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime},E))
and
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime\prime},E))
. It follows from
[
1
, Theorem 8.57]
that

(6)

\operatorname{ran}M_{\Phi}\subset\operatorname{ran}M_{\Psi}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad M_{\Phi}=M_{\Psi}M_{\Gamma}

for some
\Gamma\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E^{\prime},E^{\prime\prime}))
. This will be used repeatedly below.

3.2.
An explicit isometric co-extension and functional calculus

Unless indicated otherwise, for the rest of this section,
T_{1},T_{2}\in B(H)
will be commuting contractions with finite defects, i.e. the ranges of
I-T_{1}T_{1}^{*}
and
I-T_{2}T_{2}^{*}
are both finite-dimensional. Let
d=\dim\operatorname{ran}(I-T_{1}T_{1}^{*})
. Assume in addition that both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are pure (or of class
C_{\cdot 0}
), namely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|T_{i}^{*n}h\|=0,\quad h\in H,\quad i=1,2.

By classical dilation theory
[
28
]
, there is an isometry
J:H\to H^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
with the property that

JT^{*}_{1}=(M^{*}_{z}\otimes 1)J.

This is the usual minimal isometric co-extension of
T
.

Next, it follows from
[
13
, Theorems 3.1 and 4.3]
(see also
[
14
, Theorem 1.5]
and
[
27
, Theorem 5.2]
) that there is a rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that

JT_{2}^{*}=M_{\Psi}^{*}J.

Here, the fact that
\Psi
be pure means that
\Psi
has no unimodular eigenvalues in the interior of the disc. Thus

(7)

\sigma(\Psi(\lambda))\subset{\mathbb{D}},\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}.

If
f
is a polynomial in two variables, then

(8)

Jf(T_{1},T_{2})^{*}=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}J.

We require an extension of this polynomial functional calculus. Given
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
, let
\Delta_{\Psi}:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}\times B({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
be defined as

\Delta_{\Psi}(z)=(z,\Psi(z)),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

If
f
is a polynomial in two variables, then
f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
and

f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=M_{f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}.

(ii)

If
(f_{n})
is a sequence in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
converging to
0
in the weak-
*
topology, then the sequence
(M_{f_{n}\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}^{*})
converges to
0
in the strong operator topology of
B(H^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
.

Proof.

(i) Let
f
be a polynomial in two variables. It is clear that
f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
. Moreover, for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, we have by virtue of (
5
) that

\displaystyle f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=k_{\lambda}\otimes f(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}\xi=k_{\lambda}\otimes(f\circ\Delta_{\Psi})(\lambda)^{*}\xi

\displaystyle=M_{f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi).

By density, we conclude that
f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=M_{f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}.

(ii) The sequence
(f_{n})
is bounded in norm, and thus so is
(M_{f_{n}\circ\Delta_{\Psi}})
. Another density argument then reveals that it suffices to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|M^{*}_{f_{n}\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)\|=0

for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
. By the calculation in the previous paragraph, this is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}\xi\|=0

for each
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Fix
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and consider
g_{n}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
defined as

g_{n}(z)=f_{n}(\lambda,z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

By assumption on
(f_{n})
, the sequence
(g_{n})
converges to
0
in the weak-
*
topology of
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
. Furthermore, the spectrum of the matrix
\Psi(\lambda)
is contained in
{\mathbb{D}}
by (
7
). Hence, the sequence of matrices
(g_{n}(\Psi(\lambda)))
converges to
0
in norm, and we find

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}\xi\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|g_{n}(\Psi(\lambda))^{*}\xi\|=0

as required.
∎

In light of this lemma, we will use the notation
f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=M_{f\circ\Delta_{\Psi}}
for
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
.
We may define a unital, completely contractive, weak-
*
continuous homomorphism
\rho:H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})\to B(H)
as

(9)

\rho(f)=J^{*}f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})J,\quad f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}).

We typically write
\rho(f)=f(T_{1},T_{2})
for each
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
. Note that this agrees with the usual definition when
f
is a polynomial, by virtue of (
8
). By approximation, it is readily seen that

(10)

Jf(T_{1},T_{2})^{*}=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}J,\quad f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}).

3.3.
The distinguished variety

We now arrive at a crucial step in our investigation. In the following statement, we retain the foregoing notation and assumptions.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

V_{\Psi}=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:\det(\Psi(z)-wI)=0\}.

Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

V_{\Psi}
is an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for the pairs
(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
and
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

(ii)

Let
\delta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
be defined as

\delta(z,w)=\det(\Psi(z)-wI),\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}.

Then,
\delta(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=0
and
\delta(T_{1},T_{2})=0
.

(iii)

There is a polynomial
p
of two variables such that

\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:p(z,w)=0\},

p(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=0
and
p(T_{1},T_{2})=0
.

(iv)

We have that
\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
.

Proof.

(i) The fact that
V_{\Psi}
is an Agler–McCarthy distinguished variety for
(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
follows from standard estimates; see the proofs of
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
13
, Theorem 4.3]
. The corresponding statement for
(T_{1},T_{2})
is then a consequence (
8
).

(ii) There are functions
\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{d}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
such that

\delta(z,w)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}\psi_{n}(z)w^{d},\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}.

For each fixed
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem implies that

\sum_{n=0}^{d}\psi_{n}(z)\Psi(z)^{d}=0

so that
\delta(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=0
. By (
9
) we also obtain
\delta(T_{1},T_{2})=0
.

(iii) By Lemma
2.5
, there is a polynomial
p
of two variables such that

V_{\Psi}=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:p(z,w)=0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:p(z,w)=0\}.

Combining (i) with (
1
), we see that

\|p(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})\|\leq\|p\|_{V_{\Psi}}=0

so that
p(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=0
. The fact that
p(T_{1},T_{2})=0
then follows immediately from (
9
).

(iv) By (iii), we know that
p(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})=0
. It then follows from the spectral mapping theorem
[
21
, Theorem 7.7]
that
\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
is contained in the zero set of
p
inside of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}
, that is
\overline{V_{\Psi}}
. Conversely, let
(\lambda,\mu)\in V_{\Psi}
. This means that there is a unit vector
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi
is a joint eigenvector of
((M_{z}\otimes I)^{*},M^{*}_{\Psi})
with joint eigenvalue
(\overline{\lambda},\overline{\mu})
. By
[
21
, Corollary 9.14 and Remark 25.2]
, we conclude that
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
. Hence,
V_{\Psi}\subset\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
so
\overline{V_{\Psi}}\subset\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
as well.
∎

Although we do not require it for our work below, we emphasize here that there is a somewhat explicit procedure for constructing
\Psi
from
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Hence, it is reasonable to ask if the variety
V_{\Psi}
, or perhaps some part thereof, can be identified as some canonical set associated to the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
.

3.4.
Zero sets and annihilators

The
annihilator
of the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
is defined as

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})=\{f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}):f(T_{1},T_{2})=0\}.

This is easily seen to be a weak-
*
closed ideal in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
. We seek to determine the precise relationship between
V_{\Psi}
and
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
, where for a subset
F\subset H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
, we write
Z(F)=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}:f(z,w)=0\text{ for every }f\in F\}
.

The starting point of our investigation is the following immediate consequence of Theorem
3.3
(ii).

(11)

Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset V_{\Psi}.

Typically,
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
is much smaller than any spectral set, as can be seen upon considering the pair
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
. We will show below that, under appropriate assumptions,
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
coincides with a certain natural subset of
V_{\Psi}
, defined by spectral conditions.

Retaining the notation used in Subsection
3.2
, consider

K_{\Psi}=\bigcap_{f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})}\ker f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}.

Note that for each
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
, we have by virtue of (
10
) that

f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}Jh=Jf(T_{1},T_{2})^{*}h=0,\quad h\in H

which shows that

(12)

JH\subset K_{\Psi}.

Moreover, it is clear by construction that
K_{\Psi}
is co-invariant for
M_{z}\otimes I
and
M_{\Psi}
.

Define

(13)

S^{\Psi}_{1}=P_{K_{\Psi}}(M_{z}\otimes I)|_{K_{\Psi}}\quad\text{and}\quad S^{\Psi}_{2}=P_{K_{\Psi}}M_{\Psi}|_{K_{\Psi}}

so that

(14)

S^{\Psi*}_{1}=(M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}|_{K_{\Psi}}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}^{\Psi*}=M^{*}_{\Psi}|_{K_{\Psi}}.

In particular, we conclude that
S_{\Psi}=(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
is a pair of commuting contractions. For ease of reference, we call
(S_{\Psi},K_{\Psi})
the
constrained isometric co-extension of
T
corresponding to
\Psi
.

Much like we did in (
10
), we can define an
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
-functional calculus for the pair
S_{\Psi}
, by setting

(15)

f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}|_{K_{\Psi}},\quad f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}).

We now want to establish some useful properties of
K_{\Psi}
, using the following general fact.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
be a subspace and let
\Theta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E,{\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
be an inner function. Put
L=(\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta})^{\perp}
and let
S=P_{L}(M_{z}\otimes I)|_{L}
. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

If
\operatorname{Ann}(S)
is non-zero, then
E={\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

(ii)

If
E={\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, then the function
\det\Theta
is non-zero and it generates
\operatorname{Ann}(S)
.

Proof.

(i) Let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Ann}(S)
be non-zero. Then

0=\varphi(S)^{*}=(M_{\varphi}\otimes I)^{*}|_{L}

so that
L\subset\ker(M_{\varphi}\otimes I)^{*}
or
\operatorname{ran}(M_{\varphi}\otimes I)\subset\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta}
(note that
M_{\Theta}
has closed range since it is an isometry). By (
6
), there is
\Gamma\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d},E))
such that
\varphi\otimes I=\Theta\Gamma
on
{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
\varphi
is non-zero, there is
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
such that
\varphi(z)\neq 0
. The fact that
\varphi(z)\otimes I=\Theta(z)\Gamma(z)
implies in particular that
\Theta(z)
is surjective, so that
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}=E
as desired.

(ii) By assumption,
\Theta(z)
is a square matrix for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, so we may define
\alpha\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
as
\alpha(z)=\det\Theta(z)
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
\Theta
is inner, there is
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\Theta(\zeta)
is a unitary matrix
[
28
, Proposition V.2.2]
, so that
\alpha
is not the zero function. Next, let
\varphi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
such that
\varphi(S)=0
. As above, this is equivalent to the equality
\varphi\otimes I=\Theta\Gamma
for some
\Gamma\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
. Taking determinants we find
\varphi\in\alpha H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.

Conversely, let
\Gamma:{\mathbb{D}}\to B({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
be such that
\Gamma(z)
is the algebraic adjoint of
\Theta(z)
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Then,
\Gamma\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
and
\alpha\otimes I=\Theta\Gamma
. This shows that

\operatorname{ran}\alpha(M_{z}\otimes I)\subset\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta}=L^{\perp},

so that
\alpha(S)=0
. We have thus shown that
\operatorname{Ann}(S)=\alpha H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.
∎

We can now record the required properties of
K_{\Psi}
, by specializing the previous result.

Corollary 3.5
.

There is a subspace
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and an inner function

\Theta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E,{\mathbb{C}}^{d}))

with the following properties.

(i)

K_{\Psi}=(\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta})^{\perp}
.

(ii)

Given
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the vector
k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi
belongs to
K_{\Psi}
if and only if
\Theta(\lambda)^{*}\xi=0
.

(iii)

If
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})
is non-zero, then
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}=E
and
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})
is generated by
\det\Theta
.

Proof.

Since
K_{\Psi}
is co-invariant for
M_{z}\otimes I
, by the Beurling–Lax–Halmos theorem there is a subspace
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and an inner function
\Theta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E,{\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that
K_{\Psi}=\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}=(\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta})^{\perp}
, which gives (i). Property (ii) then follows immediately from (
5
), while (iii) follows from Proposition
3.4
.
∎

Another key property of
(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
is the following.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
and
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
.

Proof.

For
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
, by (
10
) and (
15
) we have

Jf(T_{1},T_{2})^{*}=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}J=f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}J

where we used that
JH\subset K_{\Psi}
by (
12
). In particular, this shows that
f(T_{1},T_{2})=0
if
f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})=0
, so that
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. Conversely, let
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. Then,
K_{\Psi}\subset\ker f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}
by construction, so that

f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}|_{K_{\Psi}}=0

by (
15
). We conclude that
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
. We have shown that
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
.

Finally, let
g\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
. Define
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
as

f(z,w)=g(z),\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}.

Then
g\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is equivalent to
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
, and likewise
g\in\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})
is equivalent to
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
. By the first part of the proof, we conclude that
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
.
∎

Next, we define
\Omega_{\Psi}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
to be the set of points
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
(\overline{\lambda},\overline{\mu})
is a joint eigenvalue of
(S_{1}^{\Psi*},S_{2}^{\Psi*})
. This set has the following remarkable property, which is one of the main results of this section.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(T_{1},T_{2})
be a pair of pure commuting contractions on some Hilbert space, both with finite defect. Let
(S_{\Psi},K_{\Psi})
be the constrained isometric co-extension of
(T_{1},T_{2})
corresponding to some rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. Then, we have
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))=\Omega_{\Psi}
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Omega_{\Psi}
. By definition, this means that there is a unit vector
\xi\in K_{\Psi}
such that

S_{1}^{\Psi*}\xi=(M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}\xi=\overline{\lambda}\xi\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}^{\Psi*}\xi=M_{\Psi}^{*}\xi=\overline{\mu}\xi.

Let
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. Then,
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
by Lemma
3.6
so

0=f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}\xi=\overline{f(\lambda,\mu)}\xi

which implies that
f(\lambda,\mu)=0
. We conclude that
\Omega_{\Psi}\subset Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
.

Conversely, let
(\lambda,\mu)\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
. Define
\delta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
as

\delta(z,w)=\det(\Psi(z)-wI),\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}.

Then,
\delta\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
by Theorem
3.3
. We conclude that
\delta(\lambda,\mu)=0
, so
\overline{\mu}
is an eigenvalue of
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}
. Hence, there is a unit vector
\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}\eta=\overline{\mu}\eta
. In turn, this implies that
k_{\lambda}\otimes\eta
is a joint eigenvector of
M_{z}^{*}\otimes I
and
M_{\Psi}^{*}
, with corresponding joint eigenvalue
(\overline{\lambda},\overline{\mu})
. To finish the proof, it thus suffices to verify that
k_{\lambda}\otimes\eta\in K_{\Psi}
.
To see this, let
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. Then,

f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\eta)=\overline{f(\lambda,\mu)}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\eta)=0

since
(\lambda,\mu)\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
. Hence,
k_{\lambda}\otimes\eta
belongs to

\bigcap_{f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})}\ker f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}=K_{\Psi}.\qed

For future technical use, we now describe the projection of
\Omega_{\Psi}
onto the first component of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
T=(T_{1},T_{2})
be a pair of pure commuting contractions on some Hilbert space, both with finite defect. Let
(S_{\Psi},K_{\Psi})
be the constrained isometric co-extension of
(T_{1},T_{2})
corresponding to some rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
. Let
\Omega_{1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
denote the projection of
\Omega_{\Psi}
onto the first component of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. If
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is non-zero, then
\Omega_{1}=Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1}))
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
and
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Omega_{\Psi}
. Let
F(z,w)=f(z)
for
(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
. Then
F\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
so that
f(\lambda)=F(\lambda,\mu)=0
by Theorem
3.7
. This shows that
\Omega_{1}\subset Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1}))
.

Conversely, let
\lambda\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1}))
. By Lemma
3.6
, we see that
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is non-zero, so Corollary
3.5
implies that there is an inner function
\Theta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that
K_{\Psi}=\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}
and
\det\Theta
generates
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
. Hence
\det\Theta(\lambda)=0
and there is a non-zero vector
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
\Theta(\lambda)^{*}\xi=0
. In particular,
k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi\in K_{\Psi}
.

Next, let
G\subset\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
be a finite subset. By definition, we then have

K_{\Psi}\subset\bigcap_{\omega\in G}\ker\omega(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}

so

0=\omega(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)=k_{\lambda}\otimes\omega(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}\xi

for each
\omega\in G
.
Therefore
0
is a joint eigenvalue of the matrices

\{\omega(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}:\omega\in G\}.

For each
\omega\in G
, define
\tilde{\omega}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
as

\tilde{\omega}(z,w)=\overline{\omega(\overline{z},\overline{w})},\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}

Then, it is easily verified that

\omega(\lambda,\Psi(\lambda))^{*}=\tilde{\omega}(\overline{\lambda},\Psi(\lambda)^{*}),\quad\omega\in G.

By (
7
), the spectrum of
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}
is contained in
{\mathbb{D}}
, so each function
\tilde{\omega}(\overline{\lambda},\cdot)
is holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the spectrum. Thus we may apply
[
21
, Theorem 7.7]
to find
\mu_{G}\in{\mathbb{D}}
such that
\overline{\mu_{G}}
is an eigenvalue of
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}
and

\overline{\omega(\lambda,\mu_{G})}=\tilde{\omega}(\overline{\lambda},\overline{\mu_{G}})=0

for each
\omega\in G
.

Observe finally that the net
(\mu_{G})
lies in the spectrum of
\Psi(\lambda)
, which is compact. Hence, upon passing to a cofinal subnet if necessary, we may assume that
(\mu_{G})
converges to some
\mu\in{\mathbb{D}}
in the spectrum of
\Psi(\lambda)
. By construction, we find that
\omega(\lambda,\mu)=0
for each
\omega\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
, or
(\lambda,\mu)\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
, so we conclude that
\lambda\in\Omega_{1}
by Theorem
3.7
, and the proof is complete.
∎

3.5.
Boundary behaviour and the support

Note that by virtue of
[
21
, Corollary 9.14 and Remark 25.2]
, the set
\Omega_{\Psi}
is contained in the Taylor spectrum of
(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})
. In light of Theorem
3.7
, one may then wonder if there is a relationship between the full Taylor spectrum and
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
.

To address this issue, we require the following notion, inspired by
[
7
]
. Let
F\subset H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
be an ideal. The
support
of
F
, denoted by
\operatorname{supp}(F)
, is the subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that consists of all points
(\lambda,\mu)
such that

1\notin F+(z-\lambda)H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})+(w-\mu)H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2}).

We require a few basic properties, adapting arguments from
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
F\subset H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
be an ideal. Then,
\operatorname{supp}(F)\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}^{2}}
and

\operatorname{supp}(F)\cap\mathbb{D}^{2}=Z(F)
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
lie outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}^{2}}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
|\lambda|>1
, as the case where
|\mu|>1
is symmetric. Consider the function
g\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
defined as

g(z,w)=1/(z-\lambda),\quad(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}.

Then,
1=(z-\lambda)g
so that
\lambda\notin\operatorname{supp}(F)
by definition. This shows that
\operatorname{supp}(F)\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}^{2}}
.

Next, let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
lie outside of
Z(F)
. Then, there is
f\in F
such that
f(\lambda,\mu)\neq 0
. Define
f_{1},f_{2}:{\mathbb{D}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
as

f_{1}(z,w)=\frac{f(z,\mu)-f(\lambda,\mu)}{z-\lambda}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2}(z,w)=\frac{f(z,w)-f(z,\mu)}{w-\mu}

for each
z,w\in{\mathbb{D}}
. It is straightforward to verify that
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
and that

f(\lambda,\mu)=f-(z-\lambda)f_{1}-(w-\mu)f_{2}.

Since
f(\lambda,\mu)
is non-zero, it follows that
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\operatorname{supp}(F)
. Conversely, assume that
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
lies outside of
\operatorname{supp}(F)
. By the definition, there are two functions
f_{1},f_{2}
in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
such that

1+(z-\lambda)f_{1}+(w-\mu)f_{2}\in F.

In particular, this shows that
(\lambda,\mu)\notin Z(F)
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(T_{1},T_{2})
be a pair of commuting contractions on some Hilbert space admitting an
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
-functional calculus. Then,
\sigma(T_{1},T_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a point outside
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
. Then, there are
\varphi\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
and
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
such that

1=\varphi+(z_{1}-\lambda)f_{1}+(w-\mu)f_{2}.

Applying the functional calculus we find

I=(T_{1}-\lambda I)f_{1}(T_{1},T_{2})+(T_{2}-\mu I)f_{2}(T_{1},T_{2})

so that
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(T_{1},T_{2})
by
[
21
, Proposition 25.3]
.
∎

We can now give a refinement of Theorem
3.7
which contains further information about the boundary behaviour of the Taylor spectrum of the constrained isometric co-extension.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
(T_{1},T_{2})
be a pair of pure commuting contractions on some Hilbert space, both with finite defect. Let
(S_{\Psi},K_{\Psi})
be the constrained isometric co-extension of
(T_{1},T_{2})
corresponding to some rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
. Then, we have

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi}))

and

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, there is a polynomial
p\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})\cap\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
such that

\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\{(z,w)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}:p(z,w)=0\}.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}\setminus\overline{V_{\Psi}}
. Then
p(\lambda,\mu)\neq 0
. Observe that there are polynomials
q_{1},q_{2}
such that

p-p(\lambda,\mu)=(z-\lambda)q_{1}+(w-\mu)q_{2}

so that
(\lambda,\mu)
lies outside
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
and
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi}))
. Since we know that the support always lies in
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}^{2}
by Lemma
3.9
, we conclude that
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset\overline{V_{\Psi}}
and
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi}))\subset\overline{V_{\Psi}}
.

Next, we apply Lemma
3.10
to see that

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2}))

and

\sigma(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})).

Applying Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
3.6
, these become

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))

and

\overline{V_{\Psi}}\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})).

Hence, we have shown

\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\subset\overline{V_{\Psi}}=\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi}))

Finally, it follows from Lemma
3.9
that

\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})).

In turn, Theorem
3.7
implies that

\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}=\Omega_{\Psi}

which is contained in
\sigma(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})\cap{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
as explained at the beginning of this subsection. This completes the proof.
∎

Notably, when combined with Theorem
3.3
, the previous result implies that for the pair
(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})
, the Taylor spectrum coincides with the support of the annihilating ideal.

3.6.
The annihilator as a vanishing ideal

Motivated by Theorem
3.7
, another natural question is whether
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
can be described in terms of functions vanishing on
\Omega_{\Psi}
. Given a subset
X\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
, we let
I(X)\subset H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}}^{2})
consist of those functions
f
vanishing on
X
.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
(T_{1},T_{2})
be a pair of pure commuting contractions on some Hilbert space, both with finite defect. Let
(S_{\Psi},K_{\Psi})
be the constrained isometric co-extension of
(T_{1},T_{2})
corresponding to some rational matrix-valued pure inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
. Assume that
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is non-zero. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

The joint eigenvectors of
S_{1}^{\Psi*}
and
S_{2}^{\Psi*}
span a dense subspace in
K_{\Psi}
.

(ii)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})=I(Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))=I(\Omega_{\Psi})
.

(iii)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is a radical ideal in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.

(iv)

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is generated by a Blaschke product with simple roots.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): By Theorem
3.7
, we have
Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))=\Omega_{\Psi}
so certainly

I(Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))=I(\Omega_{\Psi}).

Plainly, it always holds that

\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})\subset I(Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})).

Let
f\in I(\Omega_{\Psi})
. We claim that
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
. By Lemma
3.6
and our assumption, to establish this claim it suffices to fix a joint eigenvector
h\in K_{\Psi}
for
S_{1}^{\Psi*}
and
S_{2}^{\Psi*}
, and to show that

f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}h=0.

Now,
h
must be an eigenvector for
(M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}
, so Lemma
3.1
implies there are
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and a non-zero
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
h=k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi
. Since
h
is also an eigenvector for
M_{\Psi}^{*}
, there is
\mu
in the spectrum of
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}
such that
\Psi(\lambda)^{*}\xi=\overline{\mu}\xi
. We see that
\mu\in{\mathbb{D}}
by (
7
).

Next, the fact that
h\in K_{\Psi}
means, by definition of
K_{\Psi}
, that for
g\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
we have

0=g(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)=\overline{g(\lambda,\mu)}(k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi)

so that
(\lambda,\mu)\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2}))
. By Theorem
3.7
, we infer
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Omega_{\Psi}
so
f(\lambda,\mu)=0
by choice of
f
. This means that

f(S^{\Psi}_{1},S^{\Psi}_{2})^{*}h=f(M_{z}\otimes I,M_{\Psi})^{*}h=\overline{f(\lambda,\mu)}h=0

and the claim follows.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): This is elementary. Let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
with the property that
f^{n}\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
for some
n\geq 1
. Define
F(z,w)=f(z)
for each
(z,w)\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2}
. Then,

F^{n}\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})=I(\Omega_{\Psi}).

This shows that
F^{n}
vanishes on
\Omega_{\Psi}
, and thus so does
F
. Hence,
F\in I(\Omega_{\Psi})=\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1},T_{2})
, so indeed
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
.

(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv): The ideal
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
is closed in the weak-
*
topology of
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
, so there is an inner function
\omega\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
with
\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})=\omega H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})

[
5
]
. The desired statement then follows from
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
L\subset K_{\Psi}
denote the closed subspace spanned by the joint eigenvectors of
S_{1}^{\Psi*}
and
S_{2}^{\Psi*}
. Then,
L
is co-invariant for
M_{z}\otimes I
, so by the Beurling–Lax–Halmos theorem there is a subspace
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
along with an inner function
\Gamma\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B(E,{\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that
L=(\operatorname{ran}M_{\Gamma})^{\perp}
. By assumption, there is a non-zero
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
. Then
f\in\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})
by Lemma
3.6
so in particular
f
annihilates

P_{L}S_{1}^{\Psi}|_{L}=P_{L}(M_{z}\otimes I)|_{L}.

Applying Proposition
3.4
, we see that
E={\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

Next, we see from Corollary
3.5
that there is an inner function
\Theta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that
K_{\Psi}=(\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta})^{\perp}
and
\det\Theta
generates
\operatorname{Ann}(S^{\Psi}_{1})
. By Lemma
3.6
, it follows that
\det\Theta\in\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1})
. Let
\lambda
be a root of
\det\Theta
. Then,
\lambda\in Z(\operatorname{Ann}(T_{1}))
, so we may apply Theorem
3.8
to infer that there is
\mu\in{\mathbb{D}}
with the property that
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Omega_{\Psi}
. In particular, there is
h\in L=\ker M_{\Gamma}^{*}
such that
(M_{z}\otimes I)^{*}h=\overline{\lambda}h.
We conclude from Lemma
3.1
that there is a non-zero
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
h=k_{\lambda}\otimes\xi
. Then,

0=M_{\Gamma}^{*}h=k_{\lambda}\otimes\Gamma(\lambda)^{*}\xi

so that
\Gamma(\lambda)
is not invertible and
\det\Gamma(\lambda)=0
. We conclude that
\det\Gamma
vanishes at all the roots of the simple Blaschke product
\det\Theta
, so we must have
\det\Gamma=(\det\Theta)\varphi
for some
\varphi\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.

Conversely, the inclusion
L\subset K_{\Psi}
implies
\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta}\subset\operatorname{ran}M_{\Gamma}
, so by (
6
) there is
\Delta\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}};B({\mathbb{C}}^{d}))
such that
\Theta=\Gamma\Delta
. In particular,
\det\Theta=(\det\Gamma)(\det\Delta)
. Combined with the conclusion of the previous paragraph, we infer that
\det\Delta
is invertible in
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
, so that
M_{\Delta}
is invertible by the adjoint formula for the inverse of a matrix. Thus,

L^{\perp}=\operatorname{ran}M_{\Gamma}=\operatorname{ran}M_{\Theta}=K_{\Psi}^{\perp}

and therefore
L=K_{\Psi}
.
∎
