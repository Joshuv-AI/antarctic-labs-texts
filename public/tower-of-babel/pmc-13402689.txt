Langmuir-Hinshelwood pathway enables 1000-h stable nitrate-to-ammonia electroreduction at 1 A cm−2
Authors: Yu Tang, Jiale Li, Yanfang Li, Pan Ran, Weixin Zou, Shicheng Yan, Lin Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving stable and selective electrocatalytic nitrate reduction (NO3RR) at high current densities demands a fundamental understanding of the hydrogenation mechanism. We demonstrate that alloying cobalt with ruthenium (RuCo) switches the dominant hydrogenation pathway from the Eley-Rideal (E-R) mechanism, involving solvated protons, to the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) mechanism, utilizing adsorbed protons. Microkinetic modeling, in situ spectroscopy and density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the competitive E-R pathway on pure Co causes sluggish hydrogenation kinetics and low Faradaic efficiency. In contrast, the L-H pathway on RuCo sustains high hydrogen coverage (θH ≈ 0.45), enabling efficient hydrogen-atom transfer for high-rate and deep hydrogenation. Leveraging this mechanistic insight, we achieve a satisfactory ammonia yield rate of 135.53 ± 1.18 mg h−1 cm−2 with 100% Faradaic efficiency and robust 1000-h stability at 1 A cm⁻². This work provides critical understanding of electrochemical hydrogenation pathways for designing efficient catalysts operating under industrially relevant conditions.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Electrochemical hydrogenation using water as the proton source enables efficient synthesis of chemicals and fuels from various feedstocks like CO2, N2, and NOx1–5. The electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR) is particularly promising for sustainable ammonia (NH3) production, which is regarded as one of the most important industrial chemicals6–11. Although thermodynamically favorable (theoretical potential: 0.69 V vs. RHE, pH = 14)12–14, NO3RR kinetics are hindered by its complex eight-electron, nine-proton transfer mechanism15. Understanding the fundamental hydrogenation steps is therefore crucial for achieving practical ampere-level current densities at low potentials. Hydrogenation pathways during NO3RR at the cathode are primarily governed by two competing mechanisms14,16: (1) Eley–Rideal (E–R), an adsorbed intermediate (*M) reacts with a solvated proton (H+) from the anodic water oxidation via proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET): *M + H+ + e− → *MH; (2) Langmuir–Hinshelwood (L–H), an adsorbed intermediate (*M) reacts with a surface-adsorbed hydrogen atom (*H is from Volmer-step of water dissociation at the cathode: H2O + e− → *H + OH−) via hydrogen atom transfer (HAT): *M + *H → *MH. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the E–R mechanism involves an indirect influence of the surface electric field on hydrogenation by H2O, mediated through the first-layer adsorbed *M species. This pathway implies a relatively slow protonation step occurring at the outer Helmholtz plane17,18, with reaction kinetics primarily governed by the intrinsic properties of the *M. Crucially, the alignment between the frontier orbital energy of *M and the electrode Fermi level plays a key role. Conversely, the L–H mechanism (Fig. 1b) is characterized by a strong, direct electric field effect on the co-adsorbed reactants (*M and *H) in close proximity. This facilitates synergistic activation at the inner Helmholtz plane19,20, where the effective redox levels of the interacting species can shift substantially from their isolated states. Consequently, the L–H pathway is expected to exhibit faster kinetics than the E–R pathway, owing to the direct interaction between these activated surface species.

Schematic illustration of hydrogenation mechanisms for NO3RR on Co and RuCo.

a, b A depiction of outer Helmholtz plane reaction for E–R hydrogenation mechanism (a) and inner Helmholtz plane reaction for L–H hydrogenation mechanism (b). c The E–R pathway on Co with low ammonia production rate and Faradaic efficiency. d Introduction of auxiliary component Ru to accelerate H2O dissociation and convert the NO3RR from E–R to L–H-dominated mechanism.

Establishing coexisting adsorption sites for NO3− and H2O dissociation is critical to enabling L–H reactions21–23. To experimentally validate this, we employed a RuCo bimetallic alloy model (Fig. 1c, d). Results show that single Co metal follows an E–R-dominated NO3RR mechanism. In situ spectroscopy and density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal this arises from unfavorable H2O dissociation energetics and intrinsically slow L–H hydrogenation kinetics on Co, leading to low adsorbed H coverage (θH) and competitive E–R pathway dominance. The E–R pathway exhibits sluggish kinetics and incomplete hydrogenation, caused by non-directional protonation and delayed electron transfer from random H2O distribution in the solvent layer. Consequently, Co catalysts show poor NO3RR performance (Faradaic efficiency ~50%), which further declines at high nitrate concentrations. Ru introduction reconfigures the reaction pathway (Fig. 1d), shifting from E–R mechanism to L–H dominance. This accelerates hydrogenation by generating abundant *H on Ru sites, enabling continuous hydrogenation and a high-efficiency L–H pathway. Since *NO3−-H coupling is non-faradaic, L–H rate directly depends on NO3− (θNO3) and H (θH) coverages. Microkinetic analysis identifies an optimal θH ≈ 0.45 for this pathway. With this optimized mechanism, the RuCo catalyst achieves an ammonia yield rate of 135.53 ± 1.18 mg h−1 cm−2 and 100% FE in a flow cell. Its rapid, continuous hydrogenation prevents intermediate accumulation, showing negligible degradation over 1000 h of electrolysis at 1 A cm−2. This work clarifies NO3RR pathway mechanisms and advances understanding of electrochemical hydrogenation kinetics.

Results

Catalyst design and characterization

To construct coexisting adsorption sites for NO3− and H2O dissociation on bimetallic catalysts, we first evaluated various foam substrates (Co, Cu, Fe, Zn, Ni, and Ti). This screening identified Co foam as the optimal primary component, as it inherently possesses high NO3RR activity (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c). Concurrently, Ru was selected as an ideal secondary component owing to its moderate *H adsorption energy, which is critical for promoting water dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Leveraging these insights, we synthesized RuCo nanosheets via electroreduction of a CoRuOx precursor. This precursor was prepared by first forming Co(OH)2 through galvanic corrosion of Co foam in NaHCO3 solution, followed by Ru incorporation via heating to yield CoRuOx (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). For comparison, Co nanosheets were synthesized using the same protocol but without Ru addition. After electroreduction of CoRuOx, the precursor’s characteristic Co–O vibrational Raman peaks at 481 cm−1 (Eg mode), 530 cm−1 (F2g mode), and 687 cm−1 (A1g mode) vanished24,25 (Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming the reduction of CoRuOx to metallic species. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks corresponding to the (100), (002), and (101) planes shifted significantly to lower 2θ angles (Fig. 2b), indicating the incorporation of larger Ru atoms into the Co lattice, which induced lattice expansion. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed the ultrathin nanosheet morphology of both RuCo and Co (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) of RuCo revealed a distinct d-spacing of 0.205 nm (Fig. 2d), corresponding to the (002) lattice plane of Co. This d-spacing was slightly larger than that of Co (0.203 nm; Supplementary Fig. 4b), which was attributed to the larger atomic radius of Ru compared to Co (Co: 0.152 nm; Ru: 0.173 nm)26, providing direct evidence for Ru incorporation into the Co lattice. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping additionally confirmed the uniform distribution of Ru across the Co matrix (Fig. 2e). For the Ru 3d5/2 core-level X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 6b), a binding energy of 280.43 eV was observed, confirming the presence of Ru0 species in RuCo14,21. Notably, the Co0 and Cox+ species in RuCo exhibited a binding energy of 778.4 and 779.64 eV, which was 0.4 and 0.99 eV lower than that in Co (Supplementary Fig. 6a), respectively14. This lower binding energy suggested that the higher work function of Co (5 eV) relative to Ru (4.71 eV) facilitated electron donation from Ru to Co and made Co species in RuCo maintain a lower valence state27,28. Moreover, an approximate Ru atomic ratio of 16.6% in RuCo was quantitatively determined based on XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7).

X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy was utilized to investigate the atomic chemical states and coordination environments of RuCo and Co. Figure 2f, j displayed the normalized Ru and Co K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra of RuCo, Co, and references (i.e., Ru foil, Co foil, RuO2, and CoO). The absorption edges of all RuCo and Co were close to Ru foil and Co foil, but with only slight shifts towards higher energy, indicating the metallic characteristics of RuCo and Co after electroreduction. Figure 2g, k demonstrated the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy of RuCo, Co, and references to investigate the local atomic configurations. The Ru K-edge Fourier transformed EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) spectra of RuCo only showed Ru–Co metallic bond at 2.32 Å without Ru-Ru metallic bond according to Ru foil (2.37 Å) (Fig. 2g). This implied that the doped Ru species did not undergo agglomeration, but were atomically dispersed within the alloy lattice. The weak Ru–O bond observed at 1.48 Å could be attributed to slight oxidation upon exposure to ambient air. Similarly, the Co K-edge FT-EXAFS spectra only showed Co–Co/Ru bond at 2.17 Å and Co–O bond at 1.62 Å (Fig. 2k). The coordination number of Ru–Co path (4.72 ± 0.33) and Co–Ru path (0.91 ± 0.53) in RuCo implied that the Ru/Co atomic ratio is ~1:5 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), similar to the conclusion of XPS analysis. Combined with the results of wavelet-transformed EXAFS (WT-EXAFS) contour plots in Fig. 2h, l, we can give a clear catalyst model of RuCo, where Ru atoms were atomically dispersed within the crystalline lattice of metallic Co, affording a uniform alloy structure, as illustrated in Fig. 2i.

L–H mechanism on RuCo and E–R mechanism on Co

Next, we aimed to clarify whether distinct NO3RR pathways exist between RuCo and Co catalysts. Hydrogen radicals (H·) play a crucial role, as they dictate how H2O participates in NO3RR. To investigate the involvement of H· in the hydrogenation process, we first employed electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as the spin trap. As shown in Fig. 3a, RuCo in 1.0 M KOH solution at −200 mA cm−2 for 10 min exhibited nine distinct EPR signals with an intensity ratio approximating 1:1:2:1:2:1:2:1:1, which was attributed to the DMPO-H spin adduct as the direct evidence of H· generation6,14. In contrast, only weak peaks were detected for Co, indicating insufficient H· supply on its surface. This suggests that Ru incorporation significantly promotes H· generation. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and pulse voltammetry were used to evaluate the catalysts’ capacity for H· generation and enrichment (Fig. 3b, c). CV scanning revealed a characteristic peak at 0.2–0.3 V, corresponding to H· accumulation/desorption6,29. Notably, RuCo exhibited a drastically stronger peak intensity (≈sevenfold higher than Co after ECSA normalization, Fig. 3b), confirming its improved H· formation proficiency. When NO3− was added, the H· peak of RuCo vanished immediately, whereas a distinct residual peak remained for Co (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). This stark contrast indicates that H· on RuCo is rapidly consumed by NO3−, signifying a fast L–H pathway. In contrast, the scarce H· on Co undergoes slower reduction; thermodynamic and kinetic constraints likely suppress the L–H pathway here, enabling the dominance of the alternative E–R pathway. Consistent with these findings, pulse voltammetry further demonstrated that RuCo possesses a much stronger ability to produce and retain H· than Co (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 13). Together, these results collectively indicate that Ru incorporation enhances H· generation and utilization, favoring the L–H pathway in RuCo, while Co relies on the E–R pathway due to limited H· availability.

To directly verify the hydrogenation mechanisms operating on RuCo and Co, we conducted electrochemical experiments using H/D isotope-substituted solvents (Fig. 3d). At high current densities, Co exhibited a pronounced isotope effect (KIE = 5.1) due to sluggish D2O dissociation and perturbed H-bond networks in the E–R pathway. Conversely, RuCo retains substantial *H generation in D2O (KIE = 3.4), confirming the robustness of its surface L–H mechanism. To further confirm the critical role of *H, we performed radical-quenching experiments using tert-butanol (TBA), yielding results consistent with the isotope experiments (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 14). Considering the competitive adsorption in the L–H pathway, there theoretically exists an optimal matching between NO3− and *H. As shown in Fig. 3f, we increased the concentration of KOH to reduce *H coverage, which led to better performance at lower concentrations of NO3− (500 mM @ 1 M KOH; 400 mM @ 1.3 M KOH; 300 mM @ 1.7 M KOH). However, such observation was not found on Co (Supplementary Fig. 15). This phenomenon also strongly supports that the hydrogenation step on RuCo follows the L–H mechanism.

The microkinetic analysis of NO3RR pathways (Fig. 3g–i and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17) revealed that RuCo and Co follow a general hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) mechanism and a proton-coupled electron transfer-HAT (PCET-HAT) hybrid mechanism, respectively30,31. First, the kinetic curve of RuCo (Fig. 3g) exhibits a distinct trend of initial increase followed by a decrease, with the reaction rate peaking at a specific NO3− concentration. This aligns with our proposed bimolecular L–H mechanism for RuCo, where excessively high coverage of either *NO3− or *H alone disrupts reaction equilibrium. In contrast, this descending trend is absent in Co’s kinetic curve, clearly indicating that Co favors a hybrid mechanism rather than an exclusive HAT pathway. The kinetic data further quantify the electrohydrogenation rate as a function of surface coverage (θ) and rate constants (Fig. 3g, i), while simulations provide a theoretical framework to evaluate and quantify key surface parameters for both substrates and active hydrogen intermediates. As shown in Fig. 3i, the initial *H coverage on RuCo (θ*H = 0.76) is substantially higher than that on Co (θ*H = 0.24) at low NO3− concentrations, consistent with its more favorable H2O dissociation and L–H hydrogenation kinetics (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Notably, increasing NO3− concentration leads to a rapid rise in *NO3− coverage and a marked decrease in θ*H due to competitive adsorption. Even under high NO3− loading, RuCo maintains a significantly higher θ*H than Co. For Co, the high *NO3− coverage (θ*NO3− ≈ 0.92) arises from its limited ability to form *H. Assuming minimal interference from other adsorbed species in aqueous electrolyte, the sum of θ*NO3− and θ*H on RuCo approximates 1. Theoretically, the reaction rate should peak when θ*NO3− and θ*H are balanced (≈0.5; r = k·θ*NO3−·θ*H), which corresponds to 500 mM NO3− (Fig. 3g, i) with θ*NO3− = 0.42 and θ*H = 0.45. This observation provides direct guidance for optimizing electrolyte conditions. To further clarify the contribution of dual hydrogenation pathways on Co, we quantified the two components of the rate equation (Supplementary Fig. 18, Eq. 2.16). At 10 mM NO3−, the L–H and E–R pathways contribute equally to Co’s activity, confirming its hybrid mechanism. As NO3− concentration increases, the E–R pathway dominates (>95%). As shown in Fig. 3h, Co achieves NH3 Faradaic efficiency (FE) > 90% at 10–100 mM NO3− (comparable to RuCo), but FE drops sharply to ~50% at higher concentrations, directly linked to the rising dominance of the E–R pathway. In contrast, RuCo maintains NH3 FE > 90% across all concentrations due to its exclusive reliance on the L–H mechanism, highlighting that the L–H pathway enables rapid, complete hydrogenation and dictates selectivity even when accounting for only 40% of the total reaction flux. Conversely, the gradual hydrogenation mode associated with the E–R mechanism was unfavorable for NH3 production.

Microenvironment study for L–H and E–R mechanism from in situ characterizations

In situ spectroscopic investigations into the atomistic level are of fundamental significance for probing electrochemical interfacial processes32,33 and distinguish the hydrogenation pathways. As *H derives exclusively from interfacial H2O under alkaline conditions, tailoring the interfacial H2O structure is vital for facilitating H–OH bond cleavage and sustaining an ample supply of *H intermediate34,35. In situ Raman spectroscopy was initially employed to elucidate the interfacial H2O structure governed by Ru sites. The broad band extending from 3000–3800 cm−1 was attributed to the O–H stretching mode of H2O36, which could be resolved into three distinct types based on Gaussian fitting: 4-coordinated hydrogen-bonded H2O (4 HB·H2O, ~3200 cm−1), 2-coordinated hydrogen-bonded H2O (2 HB·H2O, ~3400 cm−1) and free H2O (K+·H2O, ~3600 cm−1), as illustrated in Fig. 4a. Among all, free H2O is liberated from the extensive hydrogen-bonding network, allowing it to transform into the H-down structure and dissociate more readily to produce the *H34,37–39, which was essential for the L–H pathway. Compared with that of Co, the larger proportion of free H2O was instantly observed at 0.4 V vs. RHE (28.6% vs. 9.7%), as shown in Fig. 4b. This demonstrated that atomic-level Ru doping modified the interfacial H2O structure and disrupted the native hydrogen-bonding network. With progressively increasing applied potential, the proportion of free H2O on Co also rose gradually, yet remained substantially lower than that on RuCo (30.6% vs. 18.9% at −0.2 V vs. RHE). The subsequent decrease of free H2O on Co could be ascribed to the competing HER process favoring alternative H2O structures40, whereas that on RuCo remained nearly unaffected, validating the stability of its L–H pathway. Collectively, Ru incorporation markedly enhanced the proportion of free H2O, which accelerated H2O dissociation and served as the primary driving force for the switch from E–R to the L–H mechanism (Fig. 4c). To gain further direct mechanistic evidence for the L–H and E–R pathways, we carried out in situ Raman experiments of the HER process on RuCo and Co. The Raman bands around 1826 and 1960 cm−1 could be attributed to the vibrations of the Ru-*H bond on Ru0 and Ruδ+ species33 (Fig. 4d), implying a dual-adsorbed hydrogenation process (*H + *NOx). Notably, no evident Co-*H vibrational signal could be identified (2019 cm−1, Fig. 4e)41, revealing that Co followed a solvent-assisted hydrogenation process (H2O + *NOx).

NO3RR kinetics on RuCo and Co

Based on kinetic simulation and in situ characterization results, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was employed to evaluate the NO3RR activities of RuCo, Co, and commercial Ru/C catalysts in an H-type cell. Measurements were conducted in an electrolyte optimized to contain 0.5 M NO3− (θ*NO3− ≈ 0.5). To minimize mass transfer limitations (Supplementary Fig. 19), the electrolyte was stirred at 1400 rpm using a magnetic stirrer. Product yields (NO2− and NH3) were quantified by ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometry (Supplementary Fig. 20). The LSV curves (Fig. 5a) reveal that RuCo exhibits enhanced activity: it achieves a significantly lower onset potential (+0.4 V) and requires only 0.05 V to reach a current density of 300 mA cm−2. This performance markedly exceeds that of both Co (onset potential: 0 V; 300 mA cm−2 at −0.19 V) and Ru/C (onset potential: 0.1 V; 300 mA cm−2 at −0.4 V). The dominance of the L–H pathway on RuCo, relative to the E–R pathway on Co and Ru/C, is also evidenced by its high NH3 yield and near-unity Faradaic efficiency (~100%) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 21–26). Reaction kinetics analysis, reflected in the Tafel slope (inset, Fig. 5a), further supports RuCo’s better catalytic performance. RuCo exhibits the lowest Tafel slope (30 mV dec−1), compared to Co (192 mV dec−1) and Ru/C (196 mV dec−1), indicating faster reaction kinetics. This is corroborated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests, which show more favorable electron transfer kinetics for RuCo (Supplementary Fig. 27). Besides, RuCo demonstrates much higher NH3 yield rate than Co (70.57 ± 4.63 vs. 17.98 ± 2.43 mg h−1 cm−2) with nearly 100% NH3 faradaic efficiency (FE) at −0.13 V vs. RHE (Fig. 5b), confirming its intrinsic advantage associated with the L–H pathway.

To validate the industrial viability of the L–H pathway on RuCo, we conducted NO3RR in a continuous flow-cell system under industry-relevant conditions (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 28). Strikingly, RuCo delivered a current density of −1 A cm−2 at 0 V, reaching −2 A cm−2 at just −0.12 V (Fig. 5c). Critically, it sustained near-unity NH3 Faradaic efficiency (>99%) while achieving a linear increase in NH3 yield from 71.66 ± 0.66 to 135.53 ± 1.18 mg h−1 cm−2 as current density rose from −0.9 to −1.7 A cm−2 (Fig. 5d). The isotope-labeled 15NO3− coupled with 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) analysis was conducted to validate source of NH3 (Fig. 5e). Four H atoms in NH4+ reside in an identical symmetric tetrahedral environment, exhibiting no mutual spin–spin coupling but solely interacting with the central N nucleus42. With a nuclear spin quantum number l = 1 for 14N, the number of peaks (2 l + 1) appears as a triplet (JN-H = 52 Hz). In contrast, 15N has a nuclear spin quantum number l = 1/2, resulting in double peaks (JN-H = 72 Hz)43. The results confirmed that all ammonia output came from electrocatalytic NO3RR44. The capability of RuCo seems competitive relative to high-performance catalysts documented in prior works6,14,21–23,45–49 (Supplementary Fig. 25a and Supplementary Table 3), enabling an optimal energy conversion efficiency of 45.7% for NH3 production at 0.3 V (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 25b). Leveraging the rapid hydrogenation of L–H mechanism on RuCo to prevent intermediates accumulation, we conducted long-term electrolysis for 1000 h in flow-cell at −1 A cm−2 with negligible decay and morphology change (Fig. 5g). The intermittent potential drop indicated that most nitrate has been consumed. The NH3 FE remained above 80%, and we collected 0.5 L of cathodic electrolyte every 24 h NO3RR reaction to obtain a total volume of ~20.5 L (NH3 yield: 69 g). After adjusting the pH = 2 by HCl, H3PO4 and MgCl2·6H2O were added as the phosphorus source and magnesium source, respectively. Finally, the resulting 672.1 g of struvite product (NH4MgPO4·6H2O) was precipitated and extracted from electrolyte as illustrated in Fig. 5h. The NH3 fixation rate is 71.45% (49.3 g NH3 in NH4MgPO4·6H2O product). To assess practical utility, we simulated remediation of dilute nitrate wastewater streams (Supplementary Fig. 29). RuCo achieved >90% nitrate removal across 1–50 mM NO3–N concentrations while maintaining > 90% NH3 Faradaic efficiency. It further demonstrated robust tolerance to complex interfering ions (e.g., SO42−, Cl−, PO43−, Ca2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Fe3+, and Zn2+) prevalent in real wastewater (Supplementary Fig. 30), confirming its potential for industrial deployment.

Identification of intermediates and theoretical calculations

We first conducted electrochemical in situ ATR–FTIR spectra and online differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) experiments for the identification of possible intermediates. In the in situ ATR–FTIR spectra presented in absorbance mode, positive-going bands correspond to the formation or increase of species, whereas negative-going bands indicate consumption or decrease. As shown in Fig. 6a, during the NO3RR process over RuCo, positive-going characteristic peaks of *NH2 (1113, 3034 cm−1), *NO (1617 cm−1), and *NO2 (1275 cm−1) were detected (Fig. 6a, left panel), and similar results could be found on Co (Fig. 6a, right panel), suggesting that the NO3− reduction is a hydrogeneration process. As shown in Fig. 6a (left panel), the negative-going signal of the H2O peak at 3391 cm−1 on RuCo with decreasing potential reflects rapid water consumption at the RuCo/electrolyte interface. This efficient water dissociation supports the proposition that NO3RR on RuCo follows the L–H mechanism. In contrast, the same H2O peak on the Co catalyst shows a positive-going increase (Fig. 6a, right panel), suggesting water accumulation on the surface due to its comparatively weaker water dissociation ability. This behavior implies that the NO3RR on Co proceeds via an E–R mechanism. When the potential is swept more negative than −0.8 V, the surface H2O peak on Co decreases, indicating that the water dissociation capability of Co improves under strongly negative potentials. This trend agrees well with the kinetic simulation results, which suggest a mixed PCET-HAT mechanism under high-coverage conditions (Fig. 3g). Together, these observations confirm two distinct reaction mechanisms operating over RuCo and Co catalysts. Electrochemical online DEMS tests show the following m/z signals: N (14), NH (15), NH2 (16), NH3 (17), NO (30) and NO2 (46) both for RuCo and Co during NO3RR process (Fig. 6b). Based on above results, we calculated the Gibbs free energies during NO3− evolution following a pathway of NO3− → *NO3 → *NO3H → *NO2 → *NO2H → *NO → *NOH → *N → *NH → *NH2 → *NH3 → NH3 (where “*” denotes adsorbed species). Corresponding models for NO3RR, HER and hydrogenation transition state (TS) were depicted in Supplementary Figs. 32–37. Given that the established model cannot fully replicate the realistic catalytic environment, the theoretical calculation results were mainly used for mutual verification with experimental findings. Consistent with previous conclusions, initial NO3− adsorption steps on RuCo and Co were both spontaneous23, and *NO3 → *NO3H was universally identified as the rate-determining step (RDS) in NO3RR14. Obviously, the highest ΔG uphill was 0.66 eV for Co, and the activation barrier for this step was reduced to 0.49 eV upon Ru doping, attributed to the optimized electronic structure (Fig. 6c). This demonstrated the kinetics advantage of RuCo for NO3RR. To further verify the mentioned mechanisms of surface pathway, we explored active hydrogen formation by calculating the free energy of water dissociation on both catalyst surfaces. RuCo surface exhibited more favorable adsorption of H₂O and *H compared to Co, while both processes remained exothermic. In the subsequent step, where *H + *OH → *H + OH− served as the RDS, RuCo and Co exhibited diametrically opposed downhill (−0.11 eV) vs. uphill (0.08 eV) energetic trends (Fig. 6d). This divergence directly dictated that *H formation on RuCo was easier than on Co, which facilitated the progression of the L–H pathway. Moreover, the sharply increasing free energy for the coupling of *H into H2 on RuCo exhibited a poor HER competition. We further calculated the L–H and E–R hydrogenation pathways and the corresponding TS free energies for *NO3 → *NO3H on two catalysts during NO3RR11,14 (Fig. 6e, f). Notably, the TS barrier of L–H pathway on RuCo (2.03 eV) was substantially lower than that of Co (2.59 eV), suggesting the thermodynamic advantage of HAT mechanism on RuCo. The energy barrier of the E–R pathway on RuCo exceeded that of the L–H pathway by 0.45 eV, while on Co, the difference was only 0.28 eV. This indicated that the inherently slow L–H pathway on Co was more susceptible to competition from the E–R pathway, thereby corroborating the conclusion that RuCo and Co followed HAT and PCET-dominated mechanisms, respectively. This was consistent with microkinetic simulations, where the PCET-HAT mixed mechanism was essentially PCET-dominated owing to the negligible L–H participation on Co (Fig. 3g). Integrated experimental analysis and DFT calculations collectively validated the advantages of the L–H mechanism on RuCo, which offered guidance for material design and theoretical insights.

Preliminary techno-economic analysis for wastewater treatment

Building on the reliable performance of the NO3RR electrolyzer, a nitrate-containing wastewater treatment pathway for generating high-value-added products was proposed as an economical process. Process simulation models for the production of high-purity ammonia and ammonium chloride were developed using Aspen Plus to evaluate technical feasibility. Furthermore, techno-economic analyses (TEA) were conducted for two process routes and benchmarked against current industrial wastewater treatment costs (Fig. 7 and Supplementary note 1). It is noteworthy that the TEA was based on a 0.5 M NO3− wastewater model, with a treatment capacity assumed at 1 million m3 per year. Figure 7a illustrates the wastewater ammonia recovery system (WARS) targeting high-purity ammonia. The low-concentration ammonia solution produced by the electrolyzer is first enriched by steam stripping, with the liquid at the bottom of the column (mainly KOH solution) being recycled back to the electrolyzer. The ammonia-water mixture is further pressurized and then enters a distillation column for ammonia separation. A purity of 99.96 wt.% liquid ammonia is collected from the top of the column and condensed for storage. Furthermore, Fig. 7b shows the system targeting value-added ammonium chloride (called WARVS). Unlike WARS, the low-concentration ammonia solution from the electrolyzer is first enriched by air stripping, and the gas then enters an acid washing tower. After mixing with a controlled amount of HCl, ammonium chloride is obtained at the bottom of the tower.

As shown in Fig. 7c, the proposed WARS and WARVS systems achieve lower treatment costs than conventional wastewater treatment (WT) processes ($65 per kmol-N for NO3− removal, equivalent to $32.5/m3 wastewater)50,51. Economic analysis shows a treatment cost of $17.74/m3 for WARS, with electricity and electrolyzer capital accounting for 45.8% and 23.8%, respectively. WARVS exhibits a lower cost of $16.70/m3, with electricity and electrolyzer capital contributing 48.7% and 25.3%. Notably, WARVS has clear energy advantages over WARS. To prevent alkaline corrosion of separation units, steam stripping is applied prior to ammonia recovery, with steam generation contributing 17.6% of WARS cost. Both systems benefit from product revenues: assuming $800/t for green ammonia52, WARS cost drops to $10.89/m3; with ammonium chloride priced at $300/t53, WARVS cost decreases to $8.71/m3. These results highlight the strong application potential of both processes. To comprehensively analyze the impact of key factors on cost, we further explore the relationship between product price, electricity cost, and unit cost. As illustrated in Fig. 7d, despite market fluctuations in product revenue, both processes maintain significant economic competitiveness compared to conventional wastewater treatment. Furthermore, with the continued decrease in renewable electricity costs, the cost of both processes will further decline.

Discussion

In summary, we employed a RuCo alloy as a model catalyst and demonstrated that Ru incorporation shifts the hydrogenation pathway by enabling a mechanistic switch from a mixed PCET-HAT regime to a dominant HAT mechanism. Microkinetic modeling, in situ spectroscopy, and DFT calculations further revealed that E–R kinetics on Co contribute to sluggish hydrogenation rates and low Faradaic efficiency, while the L–H pathway on RuCo sustains high *H coverage (θ*H) for deep hydrogenation via hydrogen-atom transfer. Optimal performance occurs at θ*H = 0.45 and θ*NO3 = 0.42. Guided by this mechanism, we achieved an ammonia yield of 135.53 ± 1.18 mg h−1 cm−2 with 100% Faradaic efficiency and 1000-h stability at −1 A cm−2, demonstrating practical scalability. Our work conclusively establishes the effectiveness of the L–H mechanism in electrochemical hydrogenation. This pathway facilitates synergistic molecular reduction and hydrogenation within the inner Helmholtz plane, effectively lowering activation energy. In contrast, the E–R mechanism operates in the outer Helmholtz plane, resembling a single-molecule reaction where high-energy barrier activation precedes hydrogenation, inherently requiring greater activation energy. These findings provide fundamental insights into electrochemical hydrogenation mechanisms.

Catalyst synthesis

RuCo and Co catalysts were synthesized using a modified cation exchange method21. For RuCo synthesis, a piece of cobalt foam (2 cm × 2 cm) was sequentially washed with isopropyl alcohol, 1 M HCl, and deionized water (10 min each) to remove surface contaminants and oxide layers. The cleaned foam was then immersed in 0.25 M NaHCO3 solution under ambient conditions with mild stirring for 4 h to form Co(OH)2 nanosheets. After thorough rinsing with deionized water, the Co(OH)2 nanosheets were soaked in 10 mM RuCl3 solution for 12 h to facilitate cation exchange. The resulting material was dried at 80 °C for 2 h and subsequently annealed under flowing N2 at 300 °C for 2 h, yielding CoRuOx. Finally, an in situ electroreduction step was performed via chronopotentiometry at 100 mA cm−2 for 12 h, followed by 700 mA cm−2 for 1 h, to obtain the RuCo alloy catalyst. The mass ratio of Ru was approximately 1.2% according to the results of ICP-MS. For comparison, Co nanosheets were synthesized following the identical procedure but omitting the RuCl3 ion exchange step. Ru/C commercial catalysts were purchased from Macklin.

Material characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on a XRDynamic 500 diffractometer (40 kV, 40 mA) using a Co Kα radiation source (λ = 0.17902 nm). Raman spectroscopy measurements employed a Thermo ScientificTMDXRTM2xi instrument, scanning the range of 100–3400 cm−1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted using a Thermo ScientificTM K-AlphaTM+ spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV, 100 W). All analyses occurred under ultra-high vacuum (P < 10−8 mbar). Survey scans used a pass energy of 150 eV, while high-resolution scans used 50 eV. Peak binding energies were referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV to correct for adventitious carbon. XAFS experiments were performed at the BL13ssw and BL11B beamlines of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF, Shanghai, China). The signal of standard samples (Ru foil, Co foil, RuO2, CoO) was recorded in transmission mode. The spectra were processed and analyzed by the software codes Athena and Artemis. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) were performed using an FEI Talos F200X instrument operating at 200 kV. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping utilized the integrated Super-XTM detection system. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker NEO spectrometer operating at 400 MHz, following the sample preparation method described previously54. Specifically, 175 μL electrolyte was collected and acidified by adding 175 μL 3 M H2SO4. Thereafter, 650 μL DMSO-d6 was incorporated into the prepared mixture and served as an internal standard for subsequent 1H NMR characterization. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry utilized an SP-756P instrument (Shanghai Spectrum Instruments Co., Ltd.).

Determination of the concentration of N-containing species

NH3: The amount of NH3 in solution was determined by colorimetry using the indophenol blue method56. Firstly, the post-electrolysis solution was diluted to one-fiftieth. And then, 2 mL of diluted electrolyte was pipetted and mixed with 2 mL of NaOH solution (1 M) containing 5 wt.% salicylic acid and 5 wt.% sodium citrates. Sequentially, 1 mL NaClO solution (50 mM) and 0.2 ml sodium nitroferricyanide solution (1 wt.%) were added into the mixture. After 2 h of light avoidance, the mixed solution was tested by UV–vis spectrophotometry to obtain the absorption spectra, the peak value of which was then used with the corresponding standard curve for the quantification of NH3 production. The peak of the resulting indophenol blue was at ca. λ = 655 nm.

NO2−: The amount of NO2− in solution was analyzed using the Griess test57. The Griess reagent was prepared by dissolving 0.1 g of N-(1-naphthyl)ethyldiamine dihydrochloride, 1.0 g of sulfonamide, and 2.94 ml of H3PO4 in 50 mL of deionized water. In a typical colorimetric assay, 2 mL of diluted electrolyte was mixed with 2 mL Griess reagent for 10 min, where the sulfonamide reacted with NO2− for form a diazonium salt and then further reacted with the amine to form an azodye (magenta). The absorbance at ca. λ = 540 nm was measured and NO2− concentration was calculated.

NO3−: The amount of NO3− in solution was measured according to the standard method. Typically, a 40 mM potassium nitrate solution was diluted within the range of 0.01–0.1 mM. Then 4 mL of diluted electrolyte was mixed with 1 ml of 1 M HCl and 0.1 mL of 0.8 wt.% sulfamic acid. After 20 min of reaction, the absorbance A = A220nm–A275nm was recorded, and NO3− concentration was then calculated based on the standard calibration curve and the tested absorbance.

1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) analysis

In order to confirm the origin of ammonia, the 15N isotope-labeled experiments were operated based on the produced ammonia in the 15N or 14N-labeled KNO3 electrolyte quantitatively determined by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance. Detailed methods were referred to the previous literature58. Specifically, the catholyte was first electrolyzed with 0.5 M 14NO3− or 15NO3− (99.99 at%) at 0 V vs RHE. And then, 175 μL of the catholyte was taken out and acidified by 175 μL 3 M H2SO4. Afterward, the above solution was mixed with 650 μL DMSO-d6 as an internal standard for 1H NMR experiments.

X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis

Ru and Co K-edge analysis was performed at the BL13ssw and BL11B beamlines of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF, Shanghai, China), respectively, using Si (311) and Si (111) crystal monochromators. Before the analysis at the beamline, foam samples were pressed into thin sheets with 1 cm in diameter and sealed using Kapton tape film. The XAFS spectra were recorded at room temperature (25 °C) using a 4-channel Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) Bruker 5040. Ru and Co K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra were recorded in fluorescence and transmission mode, respectively. Negligible changes in the line shape and peak position of Ru and Co K-edge XANES spectra were observed between two scans taken for a specific sample.

Electrochemical in situ Raman tests

In situ Raman measurements were conducted using the Raman microscopy system (HORIBA, LabRAM HR Evolution) with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm and a laser power set at 50% of the maximum output. The working electrode was fabricated by immobilizing the catalyst with conductive adhesive, which was subsequently assembled into an in situ electrochemical cell; electrochemical tests were performed concomitantly with Raman spectral acquisition to monitor the reaction process in real time, with electrochemical data acquired on a CHI760E electrochemical workstation. A Pt foil and an Hg/HgO electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. In situ Raman measurements for monitoring H2O peaks were carried out in 1 M KOH + 0.5 M KNO3 solution over a potential range of +0.4 V to −1.0 V vs. RHE. For *H species detection, the measurements were performed in 1.0 M KOH solution within a potential window of 0 V to −1.0 V vs. RHE. All spectra were collected after the initial Raman scan verified the stability of the sample.

Electrochemical in situ ATR–FTIR tests

A gold thin film was first chemically deposited onto the reflective surface of a silicon prism (silicon crystal substrate) to serve as the working electrode. The catalyst was then immobilized on the surface of this working electrode, followed by the assembly of the in situ reaction cell. Subsequently, the reaction cell was mounted onto a vacuum-type infrared spectroscopy measurement system. Measurements were carried out on a Bruker VERTEX 70 V platform equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector, with electrochemical data acquired on a CHI760E electrochemical workstation. A Pt foil and an Hg/HgO electrode were employed as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. In situ ATR–FTIR measurements for monitoring N-containing intermediates were carried out in 1 M KOH + 0.5 M KNO3 solution over a potential range of +0.4 V to −1.0 V vs. RHE. All measurements were initiated after the initial infrared spectrum verified the stability of the catalyst.

Electrochemical online DEMS tests

The DEMS (PM-DEMS) was applied to explore the intermediates and products during the NO3RR. The electrolyte (1 M KOH + 0.5 M KNO3) was pumped into the electrochemical cell using a peristaltic pump. A Pt foil and an Hg/HgO electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The hydrophobic gas-permeable membrane was purchased from Cobetter with a pore size of 0.02 μm. The data was collected at −0.2 V vs. RHE, during which the potential was applied at regular intervals, and the mass signal was gathered seven times.

Methods of struvite products extraction

A similar method was used according to a previous report54. Briefly, 1 M HCl was added to the collected catholyte, and the pH was first adjusted to about 2. The equivalent molar amount of H3PO4 and MgCl2·6H2O was then added to the catholyte, after which the pH was stable at 1–2. Subsequently, the pH went back to 9 adjusted by 1 M KOH. After thorough homogenization and aged for 2 h, the obtained white products were eventually centrifuged, washed, and dried overnight.

EPR experiments

The 5,5-dimethyl−1-pyrrolineN-oxide (DMPO) powders (1 g) were re dissolved in acetonitrile (5 mL). At the initial reaction, the KOH solution (1 M, 40 mL) was added to both the anode and cathode, with an additional DMPO solution (1 mL) added to the cathode. The catalyst was placed on the cathode, and constant potential electrolysis was performed at −0.3 V vs. RHE for 1 h. At the last 10 min of the reaction, an additional DMPO solution (1 mL) was added again. The entire reaction was stirred at 600 rpm. After the reaction, the catholyte was collected for EPR spectroscopy analysis.

Computational methods

All the spin-polarized DFT calculations were conducted based on the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)59,60. The electron-ion interactions were accounted for by the projector augmented wave (PAW)62.
