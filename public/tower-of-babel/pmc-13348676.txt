Impact of nutritional status on 30-days postoperative morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis: a prospective observational study
Authors: Nishit Jain, Navin Kumar, Chandni Patel, Naman Pratap Singh, R. S. Prafulla Mahathi, Anila Vasudev, Dipendra Singh, Sudhir Kumar Singh, Farhanul Huda, Somprakas Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition is an under-assessed risk factor for poor surgical outcomes in emergency settings. Emergency laparotomy (EL) for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis presents a unique challenge due to its acute nature and associated sepsis, which often compromise preoperative nutritional optimization. This study evaluates the impact of preoperative nutritional status on 30-days postoperative morbidity and mortality following EL.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 105 adult patients undergoing EL for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis were assessed for nutritional status using CONUT and MUST scores. Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo Classification (CDC). Patients were stratified into high and low risk based on nutritional score thresholds, and complications were grouped as minor (CDC Grades I–II) or major (CDC Grades III–V).ResultsA significant proportion of patients were found to be moderate to severely malnourished (66.67% per CONUT, 40% per MUST). Major complications were observed in 38.1% of patients. All recorded deaths occurred in those with moderate to severe malnutrition. Strong correlations were found between higher CONUT/MUST scores and complication severity (ρ = 0.631 and 0.539, respectively; p < 0.001). ROC analysis demonstrated that CONUT had superior predictive ability for 30-day mortality (AUC = 0.949) compared to MUST (AUC = 0.843). Length of hospital stay (LOHS) was also significantly prolonged in patients with high nutritional risk.ConclusionsBoth CONUT and MUST demonstrated good predictive performance, with no statistically significant difference on DeLong comparison. The implementation of nutritional assessment may enable timely interventions, reduce complications, and improve survival outcomes in patients undergoing EL.

Background

Gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis represents a critical emergency surgical condition with high morbidity and mortality. Emergency laparotomy (EL) remains the primary therapeutic intervention, but pre-existing physiological derangements often influence outcomes [1]. Among these, malnutrition is a significant yet under-assessed determinant of postoperative complications and mortality. The global prevalence of malnutrition among hospitalized patients ranges from 20% to 40%, and this burden is particularly amplified in surgical emergencies, where the physiological stress of sepsis and acute inflammation worsens nutritional depletion [1–4].

Traditional surgical risk stratification tools such as APACHE II and P-POSSUM often overlook nutritional status, despite strong evidence linking malnutrition with delayed wound healing, increased infection rates, prolonged hospital stay, and higher mortality [5–7]. Validated tools such as the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score and the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) provide objective means to identify at-risk patients preoperatively. The CONUT score, derived from serum albumin, cholesterol levels, and lymphocyte count, offers a biochemical snapshot of immune-nutritional reserves. The MUST score includes BMI, weight loss, and acute disease impact [8–11].

This study aims to evaluate the association between preoperative nutritional status, using CONUT and MUST scores, and postoperative outcomes, including disease complications (assessed using Clavien Dindo Scoring System), length of hospital stay (LOHS), and 30-day mortality, in patients undergoing EL [12–18]. The study also seeks to determine the predictive validity of these tools in the context of an Indian tertiary care emergency surgical setting [19, 20].

Methods

This was a single-center, prospective observational cohort study conducted in the Department of General Surgery at a tertiary care academic hospital. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [21]. After obtaining approval from the institutional ethics committee (approval number: AIIMS/IEC/23/356), 105 patients were enrolled in this prospective observational study between October 2023 to April 2025. The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) of both sexes who provided consent and undergoing required emergency laparotomy for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis including gastric, duodenal, ileal, colonic, or appendicular perforations. Patients undergoing elective procedures or with incomplete clinical records, pregnant women, malignancy-induced perforation, trauma or history of previous chemo-radio-therapy, or patients undergoing re-exploration after initial surgery were excluded.

The primary outcome was 30-day postoperative mortality. Secondary outcomes included postoperative morbidity, graded using the Clavien–Dindo Classification (CDC), and length of hospital stay (LOHS). Thirty-day mortality was defined as death occurring within 30 days of surgery, irrespective of discharge status, while postoperative morbidity was defined as any deviation from the normal postoperative course within 30 days.

Upon admission, demographic and clinical details were recorded. Preoperative nutritional status was assessed using two validated tools: the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score—derived from serum albumin, total cholesterol, and lymphocyte count—and the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), based on BMI, recent weight loss, and acute disease effect. Patients were categorized into low (0–4) and high (5–12) CONUT groups [2], and low (0–1) and high (≥ 2) MUST risk groups [3].

Postoperative complications were monitored for 30 days and graded using the Clavien-Dindo Classification (CDC), further grouped into low-grade (I–II) and high-grade (III–V) complications [5, 19]. Length of hospital stay (LOHS) was stratified as short (1–7 days), moderate (8–14 days), prolonged (15–30 days), and very prolonged (> 30 days) [6, 18].

The study was conducted over an 18-month period. The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi software considering 30-day postoperative mortality as the primary outcome. In the absence of robust prior data, a conservative anticipated mortality proportion of 50% was assumed. With a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of ± 5%, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 105 patients. Based on institutional surgical volume, approximately 144 eligible patients were expected during the study period, allowing for exclusions and incomplete data [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Correlation between nutritional scores and postoperative outcomes was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) with corresponding p-values. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of nutritional scores, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs) for CONUT and MUST scores were compared using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 105 participants, 74 were male (70.5%) and 31 female (29.5%). The age distribution indicated that nearly half (44.7%) of the participants were aged between 20 and 39 years, followed by 38.09% in the 40–59 age group. The older age groups (60–79 and > 80 years) comprised a smaller portion of the sample, accounting for 12.3% and 4.76%, respectively. The mean age of participants was 43.20 years (SD = 15.13), reflecting a predominantly middle-aged population. Regarding body weight, most participants (47.61%) fell within the 50–60 kg range, with a mean weight of 53.08 kg (SD = 7.27), indicating that the cohort is relatively lean. Height distribution showed that 90.47% of the individuals were between 151 and 172 cm tall, with an average height of 159.59 cm (SD = 10.94). When assessing BMI, 82.85% of participants had a normal BMI (18.5–24.9). In comparison, smaller proportions were classified as mildly (12.3%), moderately (1.9%), or severely (2.85%) underweight, indicating that undernutrition was present in only a minority of cases. (Table 1). Ileal perforation was the most common etiology, particularly among younger patients (42 cases, 40%), while gastric and duodenal perforations were more frequently observed in older age groups (33 cases, 31.42%).

Based on the CONUT score, 66.67% of patients were categorized as moderately to severely malnourished (score > 5), while 40% were classified as high-risk per MUST score. Postoperative complications occurred in 80 patients (76.2%), with 38.1% experiencing major complications (CDC Grades III–V). All mortality cases (n = 16; 15.2%) occurred in patients with moderate to severe malnutrition as per CONUT.

Statistically significant associations were found between higher CONUT/MUST scores and increased complication severity (p < 0.001). Spearman’s correlation demonstrated strong positive relationships between CONUT and CDC (Spearman’s ρ = 0.631, 95% CI- 0.50–0.73, p < 0.001), and MUST and CDC (Spearman’s ρ = 0.572, 95% CI- 0.43–0.69, p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis demonstrated good predictive performance of both CONUT and MUST for mortality and prolonged length of stay. The AUC for CONUT was 0.961 (95% CI: 0.927–0.994), compared to 0.892 (95% CI: 0.83–0.953) for MUST. However, comparison of AUCs using the DeLong test showed no statistically significant difference between the two scores. (Fig. 3)

LOHS was also significantly prolonged in patients with high nutritional risk, particularly in those with high CONUT scores. A significant relationship was found between nutritional status and the duration of hospital stays. According to the CONUT scores, the chi-square value was χ² = 37.68 (p < 0.001), and for the MUST scores, it was χ² = 17.56 (p < 0.007). Participants classified as severely malnourished by the CONUT assessment tended to have more extended hospital stays, especially in the “very prolonged” category (stays exceeding 30 days).

Discussion

The prospective observational study evaluated the impact of preoperative nutritional status on 30-days postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis (GPP). The results demonstrated that malnutrition was highly prevalent in this cohort, with 66.7% of patients classified as moderately to severely malnourished using the CONUT score and 40% identified as high risk using the MUST score. Importantly, higher nutritional risk was strongly associated with increased postoperative morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay.

The predominance of ileal perforation among younger patients likely reflects regional disease patterns in low- and middle-income countries, where infectious etiologies such as enteric fever and tuberculosis are more prevalent in younger populations. In contrast to Western populations, where perforation peritonitis commonly affects older patients due to diverticular disease, ischemia, or malignancy, studies from the Indian subcontinent consistently report younger age predominance, often attributed to enteric infections such as typhoid fever and tuberculosis. Ileal perforations, a frequent manifestation of these infections, have been shown to occur most commonly in patients in their second and third decades of life, with male predominance, supporting the age distribution observed in the present study [21–25].

Among all 17 deaths recorded (16.2%), every case occurred in patients categorized as moderate to severe malnutrition on CONUT, highlighting the prognostic value of this tool in acute surgical emergencies. The present study found that 76.2% of patients developed complications postoperatively, with 38.1% experiencing major complications (CDC Grade III–V). Both CONUT and MUST scores showed statistically significant correlations with complication severity, though CONUT demonstrated a stronger correlation (Spearman’s ρ = 0.631, 95% CI- 0.50–0.73 vs. Spearman’s ρ = 0.572, 95% CI- 0.43–0.69 for MUST). These results align with the observations of Tokunaga et al. (2017), who reported that higher CONUT scores independently predicted severe postoperative complications and mortality in colorectal cancer patients undergoing curative resection [26]. Similarly, Liu et al. (2021) found CONUT to be a reliable predictor of in-hospital mortality in older surgical patients, further confirming the role of immune-nutritional assessment in outcome prediction [27].

From a clinical perspective, early identification of malnutrition in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy may aid in perioperative risk stratification, timely escalation of care, and prioritization for intensive monitoring and nutritional support. Although immediate surgery cannot be delayed in perforation peritonitis, nutritional assessment provides valuable prognostic information and may influence postoperative decision-making and resource allocation. Incorporating nutritional risk stratification into emergency surgical pathways may facilitate early ICU triage, heightened postoperative surveillance, and prompt initiation of nutritional and immunological support, even when definitive preoperative optimization is not feasible, as preoperative nutritional status has been shown to significantly affect postoperative outcomes including complications, length of stay, and mortality in surgical patients [28, 29].

All mortalities and the majority of severe complications occurred in patients with moderate or severe malnutrition as per CONUT. Similar results were observed by Vester et al. (2014) and Sorensen et al. (2008) in the EuroOOPS trial, which demonstrated that patients at nutritional risk were more likely to develop infectious complications and wound breakdown, translating into poorer outcomes [12, 13]. The study observed a 30-day mortality rate of 16.2%, consistent with reported mortality rates of 10–20% in emergency laparotomy cohorts in India and similar low- and middle-income countries [1, 11, 14, 15]. Mortality was confined to malnourished patients, with 59.2% of severe CONUT cases and 64% of high-risk MUST cases resulting in death. In ROC curve analysis, both CONUT and MUST demonstrated strong predictive performance for mortality and prolonged hospital stay. Although CONUT showed a numerically higher AUC compared to MUST, comparison using the DeLong test did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the two models. These findings suggest that both tools have comparable discriminatory ability in this clinical setting. Therefore, while CONUT may offer additional insight into immune-nutritional status, it should be interpreted as complementary rather than superior to MUST. In a meta-analysis Ma et al. (2022), where in a meta-analysis of pancreatic cancer patients, reported that CONUT had greater discriminatory power than other tools, including MUST, for predicting survival also showcased similar findings [16, 26, 30]. Similarly, Miano et al. (2023) observed that CONUT is an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality, sepsis, and prolonged hospital stay in internal medicine patients, reporting mortality escalation from 2.2% in low CONUT to 15.3% in markedly high CONUT groups [27]. Our findings suggest that both CONUT and MUST are valuable tools for risk stratification, with no significant difference in predictive performance. Both CONUT and MUST were significantly associated with morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay. CONUT may additionally reflect the immunological vulnerability of patients, which is particularly relevant in HVPP where systemic sepsis is a predominant feature. The present study also revealed a significant association between malnutrition and prolonged hospitalization. Ten out of 27 patients with severe CONUT scores required hospitalization beyond 30 days, whereas none of the patients with normal nutritional status experienced such prolonged stays. Similar associations were shown by Schiesser et al. (2009), that malnourished patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery had significantly longer hospital stays, more complications, and higher mortality [19, 20]. Similarly, Miano et al. (2023) reported an increase in LOS from 9.5 days in well-nourished patients to 13.1 days in those with high CONUT scores [17, 18]. Prolonged hospitalization in malnourished patients reflects delayed wound healing, impaired infection control, and reduced physiological reserves to withstand postoperative stress. This not only increases healthcare costs but also delays recovery and adjuvant therapies, further compounding patient morbidity [4, 27].

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the broader generalizability of the results to different healthcare settings or patient populations. However, the prospective design and standardized data collection strengthen the internal validity of the study.

Second, although statistically significant associations were observed, the number of mortality events (n = 16) was relatively limited. While sufficient to demonstrate meaningful trends, larger multicentric studies may further enhance the precision of predictive estimates and allow more robust subgroup analyses.

Third, postoperative morbidity was assessed using the Clavien–Dindo Classification, which categorizes complications according to the most severe event experienced. Although this system is widely accepted and facilitates standardized reporting, it does not quantify the cumulative burden of multiple complications in an individual patient. The Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), which integrates all postoperative events into a continuous score, may offer additional granularity in future research. Nevertheless, the Clavien–Dindo system remains an established and validated method for outcome assessment in surgical studies.

Finally, as an observational study, causal relationships cannot be definitively established, and residual confounding factors may persist despite prospective data collection.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that preoperative nutritional status is a key determinant of postoperative outcomes in emergency laparotomy for gastrointestinal perforation peritonitis. Both CONUT and MUST were significantly associated with morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay, with no significant difference in predictive performance. Thus, incorporation of nutritional assessment tools into emergency surgical workflows and the potential of CONUT as a rapid and reliable screening method in resource-constrained environments can be useful. By addressing malnutrition as a modifiable risk factor, we can improve perioperative care, optimize outcomes, and reduce the burden of postoperative complications in this high-risk population.
