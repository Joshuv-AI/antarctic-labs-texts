Circular Reasoning: Understanding Self-Reinforcing Loops in Large Reasoning Models
Zenghao Duan, Liang Pang, Zihao Wei, Wenbin Duan, Yuxin Tian, Shicheng Xu, Jingcheng Deng, Zhiyi Yin, Xueqi Cheng

Abstract
Despite the success of test-time scaling, Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) frequently encounter repetitive loops that lead to computational waste and inference failure. In this paper, we identify a distinct failure mode termed Circular Reasoning . Unlike traditional model degeneration, this phenomenon manifests as a self-reinforcing trap where generated content acts as a logical premise for its own recurrence, compelling the reiteration of preceding text. To systematically analyze this phenomenon, we introduce LoopBench, a dataset designed to capture two distinct loop typologies: numerical loops and statement loops . Mechanistically, we characterize circular reasoning as a state collapse exhibiting distinct boundaries, where semantic repetition precedes textual repetition. We reveal that reasoning impasses trigger the loop onset, which subsequently persists as an inescapable cycle driven by a self-reinforcing V-shaped attention mechanism. Guided by these findings, we employ the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) algorithm to capture these precursors for early loop prediction. Experiments across diverse LRMs validate its accuracy and elucidate the stability of long-chain reasoning.

Introduction

In recent years, Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have made significant progress in complex reasoning tasks through Test-time Scaling strategies
OpenAI 2024
;
Snell et al. 2024
.
However, relying on long chains of thought has a downside.
As sequences grow, models often generate unnecessary or even repetitive steps
Fan et al. 2025
;
Deng et al. 2025b
, leading to reduced accuracy and wasted computational resources
Xie et al. 2025a
.

Figure 1:
The Trap of Circular Reasoning.
A logical deadlock that blocks step-by-step inference. Analogous to a software deadlock (a), this mode entraps the model in a self-reinforcing cycle (c),preventing the flexible backtracking seen in normal reasoning (b).

In this paper, we identify a distinct failure mode termed
Circular Reasoning
. Unlike traditional model degeneration (e.g., collapse or simple repetition), this phenomenon manifests as a self-reinforcing trap where generated content acts as a logical premise for its own recurrence.
As shown in Figure
1
, unlike normal reasoning that navigates diverse paths towards a solution, this pathology locks the inference into an endless loop, blocking access to a valid conclusion.

Despite its severity, systematic research on this failure mode remains limited.
We observe that this pathology prevails in high-precision arithmetic and complex recursive reasoning tasks.
To study it, we construct
LoopBench
with 700 samples covering seven sub-tasks (§
2.1
).
Our experiments show that most LRMs, including open-source and closed-source models, suffer from severe repetition, manifesting as
numerical loops
or
statement loops
.
Notably, while numerical loops are equally prevalent across model types, statement loops are significantly more common in LRMs compared to base or instruction-tuned models (§
2.2
).

Next, we provide an in-depth mechanistic analysis. By dissecting the behavior, triggers, and persistence factors, we uncover the pathology of reasoning loops:

Behavior (§
3.1
):
Entering a loop marks a internal state collapse with clear boundaries. Through topological analysis using reasoning graphs
Minegishi et al. 2025
, we also reveal that semantic circularity consistently precedes explicit textual repetition, serving as a latent structural precursor.

Trigger (§
3.2
):
While reasoning impasses generally precipitate the loop onset, the specific drivers vary by type.
Statement loops
are driven by dense high-entropy minority tokens (e.g., ‘But’, ‘Wait’) within the semantic circularity that disrupt attention distribution. In contrast,
numerical loops
are typically induced by context saturation from tasks requiring long-form expansions.

Persistence (§
3.3
):
The ‘V-shaped’ attention pattern sustains loop cycles. Predominantly focusing on attention sinks and recent tokens
Fu 2024
, this bias compels the model to self-reinforce repetition once the immediate context becomes repetitive.

Building on above insights, we propose an early prediction strategy for
statement loops
.
We exploit the finding that repetition loops are typically preceded by a surge of reflection sentence, which triggers a distinct shift in hidden states.
By monitoring this latent warning signal via the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) algorithm
Tartakovsky et al. 2014
, we can proactively identify loop risks. Empirical validation across diverse models confirms the efficacy of this approach (§
4
), providing a new perspective on ensuring the stability of long-chain reasoning.

The main contributions of our work are summarized as follows:

•

We identify the “Circular Reasoning” phenomenon and introduce LoopBench to systematically quantify loop behavior in LRMs.

•

We conduct an in-depth analysis of loop mechanisms, systematically investigating both the model’s internal state during repetition and the factors that trigger and sustain these cycles.

•

We propose an early prediction strategy based on hidden states targeting
statement loops
, which effectively anticipates loop formation across diverse models.

2.2
Key Observations on LoopBench

We conducted extensive experiments to investigate loop behavior across diverse models.
Our selection covers open- and closed-source models of varying sizes, as well as different training stages including base, instruction-tuned, and reasoning models.
To validate the challenge level of LoopBench, we compared it against established datasets such as AIME2025
Zhang and Math-AI 2025
and a stratified subset of SuperGPQA
Team et al. 2025
.
For evaluation, we quantified loop using two specific metrics: Numerical Loop Rate (NLR) and Statement Loop Rate (SLR). These metrics correspond to the two distinct granularities observed in our analysis.
We evaluate the models across various decoding configurations.
Detailed implementation settings are provided in Appendix
B.1
.

Tables
1
,
6
, and
7
present the main results, highlighting the following key findings:

LoopBench triggers significantly higher repetition than other benchmarks.

The propensity to induce loop generation is substantially higher on LoopBench compared to baselines like AIME2025 and SuperGPQA, despite their recognized difficulty.
Specifically, under the balanced setting, NLR peaks at 29.90% and SLR reaches 44.86%.
This confirms that LoopBench effectively exposes model fragility under specific reasoning patterns often overlooked by standard evaluations.

Loop patterns exhibit distinct granular behaviors.

Repetition manifests at two granularities with divergent characteristics.
Numerical loops are a universal phenomenon across all training paradigms, typically appearing as structured cyclical digits rather than random noise. Conversely, statement loops are predominantly observed in reasoning models; while negligible in base and instruction-tuned models, they are prevalent in LRMs. Detailed cases are provided in Appendix
B.2
.
Crucially, we observe no significant correlation between repetition susceptibility and model scale or distillation status (e.g., DS-Qwen3-8B exhibits robust stability). This suggests that the issue stems from intrinsic generation mechanisms rather than capacity limitations.

Closed-source models exhibit superior yet imperfect robustness.

While generally demonstrating lower repetition rates than their open-source counterparts, they remain vulnerable. Numerical loops, in particular, persist as a significant bottleneck; with the exception of the gemini-2.5-pro and claude-Opus-4.1 series, most proprietary models still exhibit a DLR exceeding 15%, underscoring that even state-of-the-art systems are susceptible to the high-precision arithmetic traps in LoopBench.

Decoding strategies mitigate but do not eliminate repetition.

While increasing randomness (e.g., higher temperature and sampling settings) reduces repetition frequency, it offers no complete cure. Notably, numerical loops persist even at
T=1.0
(Table
7
), indicating a strong intrinsic bias towards these patterns despite high stochasticity.
Furthermore, relying on high temperature creates a dilemma. While lower temperatures are typically preferred for rigorous reasoning to ensure precision
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
, they increase the risk of repetition. Conversely, raising temperature improves diversity but often lowers reasoning accuracy and leads to hallucinations
Huang et al. 2023
. This indicates an inherent trade-off between loop and accuracy, suggesting that parameter tuning alone cannot fix the “Circular Reasoning” trap.

We investigate the mechanisms of circular reasoning using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen2.5-14B (hereafter DS-Qwen-14B) and Qwen3-8B as primary case studies. We examine this phenomenon from three perspectives by the lifecycle of a loop:

•

Behavior:
How does the model’s internal state evolve during the transition to loop?

•

Trigger:
What specific factors trigger the transition from normal reasoning to a loop?

•

Persistence:
Why is the model unable to self-correct and exit the cycle once initiated?

3.1
Loop Behavior Analysis

The transition into a loop state exhibits clear boundaries, irrespective of the repetition type. This indicates a fundamental shift to a cyclically constrained pattern in internal dynamics, distinct from the exploratory nature of normal reasoning.

Determinism Surge and State Collapse.

We first quantify the determinism surge via logit distributions and entropy. As shown in Figure
3
, the onset of a loop triggers a sharp rise in maximum logit values and a corresponding drop in entropy toward zero. This sudden shift to rigid determinism stands in sharp contrast to the dynamic fluctuations of normal generation.
To investigate the mechanism behind this surge, we analyze the accompanying state collapse in internal activations. Using statement loops in DS-Qwen-14B as a case study, we compute the cosine similarity and
L_{2}
norm differences between activation vectors of identical tokens (e.g., “
\n\nBut
”) across repetition cycles.
As illustrated in Figure
4
, the representation space contracts significantly. Unlike normal segments, repetitive loops exhibit a rapid increase in similarity across layers. Crucially, in deep cycles, cosine similarity saturates to nearly 1.0, while vector norm differences vanish. This state collapse confirms that the loop constitutes a distinct internal state, fundamentally different from normal reasoning.

Reasoning Graphs in Statement Loops.

Following
(
Minegishi et al. 2025
)
, we construct a reasoning graph to analyze the model’s reasoning path from a topological perspective.
Specifically, within each statement loop case, we represent each generated sentence as a data point using its mean final-layer hidden state.
We employ K-Means to group these states into
K=200
clusters, where each cluster serves as a graph node.
Consequently, the sequential transitions between clusters form directed edges, effectively transforming the text generation process into a trajectory on a graph.

We visualize these reasoning graph trajectories in 3D space by applying dimensionality reduction techniques (PCA and t-SNE) to the cluster nodes. This visualization reveals two critical topological characteristics.
(1) First, upon entering the loop, the model’s internal trajectory strictly traverses a fixed subgraph structure. As shown in Figures
5(a)
and
5(b)
, the trajectory collapses into a cycle, confirming that external statement repetition is underpinned by an internal topological lock-in.
(2)
More critically, we observe that semantic circularity precedes statement repetition.
As shown in Figure
5(c)
, the node transitions converge into a periodic oscillation (shaded region) before the explicit loop onset (dashed line).
While the generated sentences in this phase are lexically distinct, they exhibit high semantic redundancy, causing them to fall into recurrent cluster labels
. This indicates that the model is trapped in a “semantic attractor” prior to generating verbatim repetitive text.

3.2
Loop Triggering Mechanisms

We investigate the triggers of loop formation.
Our analysis identifies reasoning impasses as the key factors driving the transition to repetition, although the specific triggering dynamics differ fundamentally between statement and numerical loops.

Triggers of Statement Loops.

Our observations reveal that statement loops are frequently precipitated by reflective phrases acting as semantic pivots.
These pivots signal an
impasse where the model, unable to derive a solution yet unwilling to terminate, resorts to recursive retrials
, as illustrated in Figure
41
.
These semantic shifts, which manifest prior to the onset of explicit textual repetition, are composed primarily of dense high-entropy tokens (e.g.,
But”
,
Wait”
)
Wang et al. 2025
. They function as volatile decision junctions that inject significant uncertainty into the reasoning path, destabilizing the subsequent attention distribution.

By comparing sentences with and without repetition across five representative high-entropy tokens, we find that the model pays significantly more attention to these high-entropy tokens immediately before a loop begins.
As shown in Figure
6
, both the density of these tokens and their accumulated attention weights remain consistently high in repeating cases. This confirms that such an attention imbalance is a key driver of statement-level loops.

To verify this quantitatively, we compared 50 loop and 50 non-loop samples across different reasoning phases.
Following
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, we curated a list of high-entropy tokens that typically appear at the start of sentences. Specifically, we examined the “Repetition Onset” (a fixed window preceding repetition) and “Stable Repetition” (a fixed window following repetition) phases against the pre-repetition history and non-repeating baselines.
As shown in Figure
7
, both the density and attention share of these high-entropy tokens peak during the Repetition Onset phase. This confirms that a sudden shift in attention toward these tokens acts as a key precursor to repetition, signaling the transition from normal reasoning to a loop state.

Therefore, we conclude that statement loops are driven by
self-reinforcing attention
, where a high density of high-entropy tokens monopolizes the model’s limited attentional capacity. Consequently, the model is entrapped in a recursive cycle of redundant self-reflections, thereby irreversibly derailing the progressive reasoning path.

Triggers of Numerical Loops.

In contrast to statement loops, numerical loops originate from distinct triggering mechanisms.
They are primarily induced by arithmetic tasks necessitating long-form expansions with inherent periodicity.
For instance (Figure
38
), calculating square roots requires the model to repeatedly generate decimal periods (e.g.,
0.0000\dots
).
Such repetitive patterns rapidly saturate the recent context window and dominate the immediate history, effectively trapping the generation process in a self-reinforcing loop. Notably, this failure persists across both base and instruction-tuned models, suggesting a fundamental limitation in handling periodic sequences.

3.3
Loop Persistence Mechanisms

We investigate the mechanism of loop persistence by analyzing the attention dynamics in large reasoning models.
Our analysis reveals a consistent “V-shaped” attention pattern (Figure
8(a)
).
Specifically, during next-token prediction, attention weights are heavily concentrated on two distinct areas: the initial
attention sinks
and the most recent tokens.
This observation aligns with prior findings
Fu 2024
;
Xiao et al. 2023
.
However, while efficient for normal generation, this mechanism becomes detrimental once repetition initiates.
As repetitive sequences saturate the recent context window, the attention allocated to these local tokens rises abnormally (Figure
8(b)
).
This creates a self-reinforcing feedback loop: the model focuses excessively on its own recent repetitions, which in turn boosts the confidence of generating the same tokens again.
Consequently, this dynamic traps the model in a closed attractor state, confining generation to a recursive trajectory of identical content, thereby making the loop nearly inescapable.

4
Early Prediction of Loop Onset

Predicting repetition prior to its manifestation is crucial for enhancing model reliability and ensuring computational efficiency.
Since
numerical loops
is often an intrinsic requirement of calculation, our method specifically targets
statement loops
. We leveraging the observation that they are frequently precipitated by a surge of high-entropy reflection tokens.
This semantic shift induces a distinct state transition in the hidden states, which we utilize as a warning signal to forecast loop risks before the onset of explicit textual repetition.

To detect this transition, we apply the linear classifier from Section
3.1
to monitor the model’s internal states. Specifically, for each sentence
i
, we take the average of its hidden states at the last layer (denoted as
h_{i}
) and compute a prediction score:

x_{i}=w^{\top}h_{i}+b

(1)

As shown in Figure
9
, a predicted score
x_{i}>0
indicates that the model is likely entering a loop state. We observe that this probability rises sharply just before repetition starts, a trend that is absent in normal reasoning. This confirms that hidden states provide a warning signal before the model fails, allowing us to catch the transition from normal reasoning to loops by monitoring their trajectory.

Figure 9:
Prediction score for a loop case.
The red area indicates repetition onset; the gray area highlights the sharp probability rise preceding repetition.

Real-time Detection via Cumulative Sum.

We formulate repetition detection as a sequential change-point detection problem employing the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) algorithm
Tartakovsky et al. 2014
. Specifically, we track the cumulative deviation
S_{i}
based on the score
x_{i}
via the update:

S_{i}=\max(0,\ S_{i-1}+(x_{i}-r))

(2)

where
r
is a reference value calibrated to the mean of non-repetitive cases. This formulation ensures that
S_{i}
accumulates only systematic deviations exceeding
r
, effectively filtering out transient noise.

To further exclude momentary spikes, intervention is triggered only when the condition
S_{i}>h
persists for
p
consecutive steps. To minimize false positives, we calibrate
h=\alpha\cdot S_{max}
on a held-out normal set, where
S_{max}
denotes the maximum observed statistic.

Experimental Setup.

We evaluate our method on the LoopBench dataset using greedy decoding to ensure reproducibility. For CUSUM calibration, we sample 50 non-loop instances per model. From the remaining data, we construct a balanced test set for each model, comprising at least 50 repetitive and 50 non-repetitive cases. For models that do not meet the minimum requirement of 50 loop cases in LoopBench, we exclude them from this specific analysis to maintain statistical reliability.
Performance is measured using four metrics: Early Detection Rate (EDR), False Positive Rate (FPR), Average Sentence Earliness (ASE), and Average Token Earliness (ATE). These metrics assess the ability to anticipate repetition while quantifying the risk of misclassifying normal sequences. Further details on metric definitions and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix
D
.

presents the performance of our prediction strategy across diverse model architectures. We summarize the key findings as follows:
(1)
High Predictability with Low False Alarms.

(2)

Related Work

Neural Text Degeneration.

Although prominent in LRMs, looping originates from classic neural text degeneration, where models devolve into “repeaters”
Holtzman et al. 2020
. This pathology has been addressed across the pipeline:
(1)
Data Perspective.

Hernandez et al. 2022
identified corpus duplication as a primary driver via the “Repetition In, Repetition Out” principle. Thus, strict deduplication, from exact matching to semantic pruning
Lee et al. 2022
;
Abbas et al. 2023
, is critical for prevention.
(2)
Training Objectives.
To mitigate the degeneration induced by standard MLE
Welleck et al. 2019
, objectives evolved to explicitly penalize repetition (e.g., Unlikelihood Training) or improve isotropy
Su et al. 2022
. Methods like “Straight to the Gradient”
Lin et al. 2021
further reduce exposure bias by optimizing evaluation metrics directly.
(3)
Decoding Strategies.
To escape the “likelihood trap,” solutions include stochastic methods like Nucleus Sampling
Holtzman et al. 2020
for diversity, and deterministic approaches like Contrastive Search
Su et al. 2022
or explicit penalties
Keskar et al. 2019
that balance coherence without randomness.

Despite existing solutions for LLMs, the issue re-emerges in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) as
circular reasoning
. Driven by the reflective and “trial-and-error” nature of CoT
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2025b
;
Wei et al. 2025
, these meaningful logic loops evade standard deduplication methods effective for simple n-gram repetition.

Mechanistic Interpretability.

The goal of mechanistic interpretability is to reverse-engineer model behaviors
(
Elhage et al. 2021
;
Pang et al. 2025
)
by mapping distinct functional properties
(
Duan et al. 2025b
;
Todd et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2025a
;
Wei et al. 2025
)
to identifiable components within LLMs.
These components span various granularities, including neurons
(
Dai et al. 2022
)
, multi-headed self-attention (MHSA)
(
Ge et al. 2025a
;
Ge et al. 2025b
)
, feed-forward networks (FFN)
(
Deng et al. 2024
;
Duan et al. 2025a
)
, layers
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
, and circuits
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
.
Specifically regarding repetition, recent works attribute this phenomenon to distinct internal mechanisms, such as Induction Heads
(
Olsson et al. 2022
)
in attention layers and specialized repetition neurons within MLPs
(
Hiraoka and Inui 2025
)
.
Aligning with this direction, we primarily analyze the loop behavior of LRMs through the lens of internal hidden states and attention mechanisms.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we characterize
Circular Reasoning
via LoopBench, identifying loops as deterministic phase transitions where semantic circularity precedes textual repetition. Mechanistically, these are triggered by specific task demands and sustained by ‘V-shaped’ attention. Finally, we validate a CUSUM-based strategy to anticipate loop formation and ensure reasoning stability.

Limitations

We acknowledge three main limitations in this paper. First, our investigation concentrates on high-precision arithmetic and recursive reasoning tasks; consequently, loop dynamics in other domains remain to be fully explored. Second, as our mechanistic analysis primarily relies on the DeepSeek and Qwen families, the architectural universality of these findings across other model types requires further verification. Finally, our current intervention serves as an inference-time mitigation specifically optimized for statement loops, rather than a fundamental training-stage resolution that systematically eradicates the underlying pathology at its source.

Ethical Considerations

This work primarily contributes to the efficiency and sustainability of Large Reasoning Models. By enabling the early detection and termination of infinite reasoning loops, our approach significantly mitigates the computational waste and energy consumption associated with invalid recursive generation, aligning with Green AI principles.
While we characterize specific loop triggers that could theoretically be exploited for resource-exhaustion attacks (e.g., denial-of-service), we posit that exposing these mechanistic vulnerabilities is a prerequisite for developing robust defense mechanisms. Furthermore, our LoopBench is derived exclusively from public benchmarks, involving no personally identifiable information or safety concerns.

Evaluation Metrics.

Based on our observations, repetitive outputs in LRMs primarily manifest in two distinct forms:
numerical loops
and
statement loops
. To systematically quantify these behaviors, we introduce two corresponding metrics:
Numerical Loop Rate (NLR)
and
Statement Loop Rate (SLR)
. The detailed criteria for identifying these loops are defined as follows:

•

Numerical Loop
: We identify a numerical loop based on the total accumulated length of the repetitive sequence. Let
l
denote the length (in tokens) of the minimal repeating unit (comprising one or more digits), and
k
denote the number of consecutive repetitions. We classify an output as a numerical loop if the total length satisfies:

k\times l>500

This threshold is established based on the constraints of
LoopBench
, where no problem requires a numerical precision exceeding 500 digits. Consequently, any repetitive numerical sequence surpassing this length is considered an invalid infinite loop.

•

Statement Loop
: We identify a statement loop based on the recurrence of sentence-level units. Let
l
denote the number of sentences in the minimal repeating unit, and
k
denote the number of repetitions. We classify an output as a statement loop if the repetition count satisfies:

k>3

This threshold was empirically selected to robustly detect recursive reasoning traps where the model regurgitates previous thoughts without making new progress.

D.1
Evaluation Metrics

To comprehensively assess the effectiveness and timeliness of loop prediction, we employ four quantitative metrics: Early Detection Rate (EDR), False Positive Rate (FPR), Average Sentence Earliness (ASE), and Average Token Earliness (ATE). These metrics balance the trade-off between detection sensitivity and the risk of false alarms.

Early Detection Rate (EDR).

EDR quantifies the proportion of loops detected prior to their actual onset. Let
t_{alert}
and
t_{onset}
denote the detection step and the ground-truth start of repetition, respectively. A detection is deemed successful if
t_{alert}<t_{onset}
. EDR is defined as:

\text{EDR}=\frac{N_{\text{early}}}{N_{\text{loop}}}

(3)

where
N_{\text{early}}
is the number of successfully anticipated loops and
N_{\text{loop}}
is the total count of looping samples.

False Positive Rate (FPR).

FPR assesses the detector’s robustness on non-repetitive sequences. It is defined as the proportion of normal samples that are erroneously flagged as loops:

\text{FPR}=\frac{N_{\text{fp}}}{N_{\text{normal}}}

(4)

where
N_{\text{fp}}
denotes the number of false alarms (normal samples flagged as loops), and
N_{\text{normal}}
is the total count of non-repetitive samples.

Average Sentence Earliness (ASE).

Computed over successfully detected samples, ASE quantifies the average lead time between the model’s alert and the actual loop onset. It is defined as:

\text{ASE}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{early}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{early}}}(S^{(i)}_{\text{onset}}-S^{(i)}_{\text{alert}})

(5)

where
S^{(i)}_{\text{onset}}
and
S^{(i)}_{\text{alert}}
denote the sentence indices of the ground-truth onset and the detection trigger for the
i
-th sample, respectively. Higher ASE values indicate earlier warnings.

Average Token Earliness (ATE).

Analogous to ASE, ATE provides a more granular assessment of timeliness at the token level. It measures the average number of tokens generated between the alert and the loop onset:

\text{ATE}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{early}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{early}}}(T^{(i)}_{\text{onset}}-T^{(i)}_{\text{alert}})

(6)

where
T^{(i)}
represents the token index. This metric captures the precise anticipation margin independent of sentence boundaries.

Details of Intervention Strategy

Intervention Strategy

Unlike abrupt truncation, which indiscriminately cuts off reasoning and compromises integrity, we employ a “soft” intervention strategy via prompt guiding. Upon detecting the onset of a numerical or statement loop at step
t
, we immediately inject a conclusion-inducing token sequence
\mathcal{I}
(e.g.,
“Conclude now and provide the answer.”
) directly at the breakpoint.

This operation serves as an external perturbation that disrupts the self-reinforcing attention mechanism responsible for the loop. By forcibly altering the local context, we steer the model’s transition probability towards a concluding state. Formally, this shifts the next-token prediction as follows:

P(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\xrightarrow{\text{Intervention}}P(x_{t}\mid x_{<t}\oplus\mathcal{I})

(7)

where
\oplus
denotes string concatenation. This redistribution effectively breaks the deterministic deadlock, guiding the model to finalize the inference based on the augmented context rather than continuing the repetitive cycle.

LoopBench
. To distinguish between valid calculation steps and pathological loops, we set intervention breakpoints at 20 repetitions for numerical patterns and 3 repetitions for sentences. Inference utilizes standard sampling settings (non-greedy). We report the
Completion Rate
—defined as the proportion of instances successfully generating the end-of-thought token
</think>
—tracked across output lengths ranging from 1K to 4K tokens.
