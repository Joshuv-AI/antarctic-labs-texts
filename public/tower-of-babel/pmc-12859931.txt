Admission neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and the risk of preoperative early rebleed in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage
Authors: Huangcheng Shangguan, Ye Xu, Yi Wu, Darong Chen, Peisen Yao, Yibin Zhang, Yuanxiang Lin, Wenhua Fang, Shufa Zheng, Dezhi Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEarly rebleed after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a catastrophic complication associated with high mortality and poor neurological outcomes. While inflammatory dysregulation and vascular instability have been implicated in aneurysmal rupture, early biomarkers capable of identifying patients at heightened risk for rebleed remain limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between the neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) upon admission and the risk of preoperative early rebleed in patients with aSAH.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 752 patients diagnosed with aSAH who met the inclusion criteria from a single tertiary center. NHR was calculated from peripheral blood samples obtained within 6 h of admission (< 24 h from ictus). Preoperative early rebleed was defined as new clinical deterioration with radiological confirmation of hemorrhage occurring within 72 h of aSAH ictus before aneurysm repair. The association between NHR and early rebleed was assessed using multivariable logistic regression, propensity score matching (PSM), and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate consistency across clinical strata.ResultsAmong the included patients, 36 (4.79%) experienced preoperative early rebleed. Elevated admission NHR was independently associated with increasing preoperative early rebleed risk after adjustment for Hunt–Hess grade, modified Fisher grade, and activated partial thromboplastin time (adjusted odds ratio per unit increase in NHR: 1.12, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.05–1.20; P = 0.0005). Patients in the highest NHR tertile had a 3.25-fold increased risk compared to those in the lowest tertile. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed moderate discrimination for preoperative rebleed (the area under the curve = 0.69, 95% CI 0.655–0.723, P < 0.001). This association remained robust across PSM-adjusted cohorts and clinical subgroups. RCS analysis revealed a linear dose–response relationship between high NHR and early rebleed risk. These associations remained consistent in PSM-adjusted cohorts and across clinical subgroups.ConclusionsA higher NHR at admission is positively correlated with the risk of preoperative early rebleed in patients with aSAH. By reflecting systemic inflammation and reduced vascular protection, NHR complements established clinical and radiological predictors and may assist in prioritizing treatment timing in acute aSAH management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03719-5.Keywords: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Preoperative early rebleed, Neutrophil-to-HDL ratio, Inflammatory biomarkers, Risk stratification

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a devastating cerebrovascular disorder that accounts for approximately 5–10% of all strokes and remains associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality despite advances in diagnosis and treatment strategies [1–3]. The fatality rate approaches 40%, with up to 50% of survivors experiencing long-term neurological deficits [4, 5]. Among the early complications of aSAH, rebleeding is particularly catastrophic, often leading to abrupt neurological deterioration, increased intracranial pressure, and mortality rates exceeding 70% [6–8]. Preventing rebleed through timely aneurysm repair is therefore a cornerstone of acute management. However, logistical barriers, patient instability, and diagnostic uncertainties often delay definitive treatment, leaving a critical time window during which rebleed may occur. Therefore, identifying patients at high risk for rebleed is essential for guiding early triage, intensive monitoring, and prioritization for emergent treatment.

Several clinical and radiological parameters, such as Hunt–Hess (HH) grade, modified Fisher (mFisher) grade, aneurysm size and location, and systolic blood pressure, have been reported as predictors of rebleed [6, 9–11]. Although these markers provide useful information, they predominantly reflect aneurysm instability or clinical severity and may not fully capture the underlying biological processes driving aneurysm instability and rebleed (or re-rupture). Increasing evidence implicates systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction as central drivers of aneurysm formation, progression, and rupture [12–14]. Identifying biomarkers that integrate inflammatory and vascular homeostasis pathways may therefore improve risk stratification for aSAH.

The neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) has recently emerged as a promising integrated biomarker of inflammation and lipid-related vascular protection [15–17]. Neutrophil count (N) has been widely recognized as a marker of systemic inflammation, while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) reflects vascular protection and repair capacity [18–22]. The NHR therefore provides a composite index that integrates both heightened inflammatory activity (neutrophils) and diminished vascular protection (HDL-C), potentially offering a more comprehensive reflection of vascular vulnerability in aSAH. Recent studies have reported that elevated NHR levels were associated with adverse outcomes in ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, and aortic dissection [15–17, 23]. Yet, its importance in hemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease, specifically aSAH, is not yet fully understood.

We hypothesized that elevated NHR upon admission may be associated with an increased risk of preoperative rebleed in patients with aSAH. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained database of aSAH patients employing multivariate logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling, propensity score matching (PSM), and subgroup analyses. Our objective was to evaluate the association between NHR and early preoperative rebleed and to assess its potential integration into risk stratification strategies for acute aSAH management.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was a secondary analysis of a prospectively maintained database of patients diagnosed with aSAH and admitted to our tertiary neurosurgical center between January 2014 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of aSAH based on computed tomography (CT) and computed tomography angiography (CTA) or digital subtraction angiography (DSA); (3) aneurysm verified and subsequently treated via microsurgical clipping or endovascular coiling; (4) availability of complete admission laboratory tests, including neutrophil count and HDL-C within 6 h of admission; (5) the time from ictus to admission was less than 24 h. Exclusion criteria included: (1) traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH); (2) patients with recent active infection; (3) patients who exhibited preadmission neurological deterioration or suspected rebleed as reported by family members; (4) immunosuppressive or lipid-lowering treatment use within one month before admission; and (5) incomplete laboratory or clinical data. In our cohort, there were no cases meeting criteria (1–3), as such patients were screened out during initial record review and therefore do not appear in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Management of aSAH patients after admission

In our medical system, most patients with suspected aSAH are initially evaluated at local hospitals by CT scan and then transferred to our tertiary neurosurgical center if aneurysmal SAH is confirmed or strongly suspected, unless their condition is too unstable for transfer. A minority of patients are admitted directly from the prehospital setting. In this study, the term “admission” refers specifically to arrival at the tertiary neurosurgical center, where all patients underwent confirmatory imaging, laboratory evaluation, and subsequent treatment planning.

Upon admission, patients received standardized management in accordance with institutional protocols. Initial stabilization included airway protection, hemodynamic and neurological monitoring, and general supportive measures such as maintenance of normovolemia, blood pressure control, and analgesia. Nimodipine was administered routinely to prevent delayed cerebral ischemia. For patients presenting with hydrocephalus or elevated intracranial pressure, external ventricular drainage (EVD) was considered. Aneurysm repair, either via microsurgical clipping or endovascular coiling, was scheduled as early as possible. The timing of intervention was determined by the patient’s clinical status, aneurysm morphology, and practical logistical factors, including operating room or hybrid suite availability, surgical or endovascular team scheduling, anesthesia team availability, and, in some cases, delays related to inter-hospital transfer or the need for urgent preoperative stabilization (e.g., correction of severe electrolyte disturbance or cardiopulmonary instability). Endovascular procedures were performed exclusively with coiling. In selected cases with wide-neck aneurysms, stent-assisted coiling was applied. No WEB devices or flow-diverters were used. In cases where definitive treatment was delayed, patients were closely monitored for signs of rebleed, including sudden clinical deterioration and radiological evidence of new hemorrhage. Blood samples were collected within 6 h of admission for routine laboratory testing, including a complete blood count, blood biochemistry, and coagulation parameters.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data were extracted from a prospective database. Collected variables included age, sex, history of hypertension (HP) or diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking, alcohol use, Hunt–Hess grade (HH), modified Fisher grade (mFisher), aneurysm characteristics, rebleed status, and admission laboratory parameters. Time variables records included: ictus to arrival at neurosurgery, arrival at neurosurgery to rebleed, arrival at neurosurgery to aneurysm repair, and ictus to aneurysm repair. Laboratory parameters included complete blood count and lipid profile [HDL and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)]. The NHR was calculated as: NHR = neutrophil count (103 cells/μL)/HDL-C (mmol/L) [23–27].

Although no standardized clinical reference interval for NHR exists, population-based studies report that values in healthy adults generally range from 0.8 to 3.0, with median levels of 1.5–2.0 [28, 29]. Slightly higher values are observed in men and older adults, largely due to lower HDL-C levels [28, 30]. These contextual distributions are provided for reference, though no consensus cut-off value has been established.

Clinical and radiological severity was assessed at admission using the HH (I–V) and mFisher (1–4) grades. HH grades were dichotomized as mild (I–III) vs. severe (IV–V) clinical conditions, and mFisher grades as mild (1–2) vs. severe (3–4) SAH radiological severity [31, 32]. Aneurysm-related variables such as location, size, and neck size were categorized according to established thresholds. Aneurysm locations were dichotomized as “anterior cerebral artery (ACA)”, “anterior communicating artery (AcomA)”, “middle cerebral artery (MCA)”, “internal carotid artery (ICA)”, “posterior communicating artery (PcomA)”, and “other locations” [33]. Aneurysm size categories (<5, 5–15, 15–25, >25 mm) follow commonly used thresholds in aSAH literature and practice [32, 34].

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was the occurrence of preoperative rebleed, defined as the combination of new clinical deterioration and radiological confirmation of new or increased hemorrhage on repeat non-contrast CT within 72 h after ictus and prior to aneurysm repair [35, 36]. Clinical worsening without CT evidence was not considered rebleed, and no cases of CT-documented rebleed without clinical deterioration were observed in this cohort. While repeat angiographic imaging (CTA/DSA) was not universally performed upon rebleed, the initial aneurysm diagnosis was confirmed by CTA or DSA in all patients, in accordance with the inclusion criteria.

The secondary outcome was the 3-month functional status, assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), and dichotomized as favorable (0–2) vs. poor (3–6) outcome.

Statistical analysis

Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) depending on distribution. Group comparisons used Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Univariate logistic regression analyses identified potential predictors of rebleed. Variables with P < 0.05 were entered into multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent risk factors. NHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable in tertiles, using the lowest tertile (T1) as reference. Model I was unadjusted. Model II: adjusted for covariates significant in univariate analysis.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with knots at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles were used to explore potential nonlinear associations between NHR and rebleed. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed to evaluate the discriminative ability of NHR for preoperative early rebleed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

To reduce confounding, 1:3 propensity score matching (PSM) with a nearest-neighbor algorithm was used to match patients with and without rebleed based on admission characteristics. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of associations across different strata, including age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex, HP, DM, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Interaction terms were included to assess potential effect modification.

All analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA), Empower version 2.0 (Waters, Milford, USA), SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY), and MedCalc version 18.2.1 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Admission characteristics of aSAH patients according to tertiles of NHR

Treatment timelines and rebleed distribution

A total of 67.82% of patients underwent neurosurgical clipping and 32.18% underwent endovascular coiling. Median time from ictus to arrival at neurosurgery was 16.00 h (IQR 11.00–19.00), from arrival at neurosurgery to aneurysm repair 13.00 h (IQR 6.0–30.00), and from ictus to aneurysm repair 30.00 h (IQR 21.00–47.88). Among patients with early preoperative rebleed, median time from ictus to rebleed was 20.50 h (IQR 15.75–27.25). Aneurysm repair within 24 h of arrival at the neurosurgery was achieved in 57.31% of patients, 28.19% between 24 and 48 h, and 14.49% between 48 and 72 h.

Among patients with early preoperative rebleed (n = 36), the distribution after ictus was 0–6 h: 0/36 (0.0%), 6–24 h: 25/36 (69.4%), 24–48 h: 9/36 (25.0%), and 48–72 h: 2/36 (5.6%). These data indicate that events cluster within the first 24 h after ictus, while a smaller proportion occurs between 24 and 72 h.

Association between NHR and rebleed

On univariate analysis, several factors were associated with preoperative rebleed (Table 1), including Hunt–Hess grade (P = 0.008), mFisher grade (P = 0.001), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT; P = 0.049), and admission NHR (P < 0.001). Moreover, patients with rebleed also had worse 3-month outcomes (mRS 3–6: 55.56% vs. 13.27%, P < 0.001).

To account for potential confounders, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using covariates that reached significance in univariate analysis. In the adjusted model, NHR (as a continuous variable) remained an independent predictor of rebleed (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.05–1.20, P < 0.001). However, Hunt–Hess grade (P = 0.567), mFisher grade (P = 0.053), and aPTT (P = 0.279) were not independently associated with rebleed after adjustment. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that admission NHR showed moderate discrimination for preoperative rebleed, with an AUC of 0.690 (95% CI 0.655–0.723; Fig. 2A).

The AUC was 0.69 (95% CI 0.655–0.723; P < 0.001), indicating moderate discriminative ability. B RCS analysis illustrating the association between NHR and preoperative rebleed risk. A linear dose–response relationship was observed (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity = 0.421), demonstrating that higher NHR values are proportionally associated with increased odds of rebleed

In further adjusted models (Table 2 and Table 3), higher NHR values were consistently associated with increased rebleed risk. Each unit increase in NHR was associated with a 12% increase in the odds of rebleed (adjusted OR = 1.12; 95% CI 1.05–1.20; P < 0.001). When analyzed categorically, patients in the highest NHR tertile (T3) had a significantly higher rebleed risk than those in the lowest tertile (T1), with an adjusted OR of 3.25 (95% CI 1.03–9.35; P = 0.023). A significant dose–response relationship was observed (P for trend < 0.01) in both unadjusted and adjusted models.

Nonlinear associations between NHR and the risk of rebleed

To assess the potential nonlinear relationship between NHR and rebleed, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed (Fig. 2B). The analysis demonstrated a linear association between NHR and rebleed risk (P for nonlinearity > 0.05), indicating that rebleed prevalence increased proportionally with rising NHR levels.

PSM analysis of the association between NHR and rebleed

To minimize the influence of confounding variables, we conducted a 1:3 nearest-neighbor PSM analysis. This procedure yielded a matched cohort comprising 34 patients who experienced rebleed and 96 patients who did not. Admission clinical characteristics post-matching are summarized in Table 4. Following PSM, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of Hunt–Hess grade, modified Fisher grade, or aPTT, indicating successful balancing of key covariates. Importantly, NHR remained significantly higher in the rebleed group compared to the non-rebleed group (P = 0.026), consistent with pre-matching findings. In addition, the functional outcomes of patients with rebleed were significantly poorer than those without rebleed (52.94% vs. 15.62% unfavorable outcome, P < 0.001), mirroring trends observed prior to matching.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that admission NHR is independently associated with early rebleed in patients with aSAH. This association remained significant after adjusting for clinical and radiological severity and was consistent across propensity-matched cohorts and clinical subgroups. ROC and RCS analyses confirmed both the association and an approximately linear relationship between NHR and preoperative rebleed risk. These findings suggest that a heightened inflammatory state combined with diminished vascular protection contributes to aneurysm fragility in the acute phase of aSAH.

Clinical severity at admission and the extent of intracranial blood burden are established determinants of rebleed risk. Compared with other inflammatory indices, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio or the glucose-to-potassium ratio in aSAH [10, 35], NHR may better capture the combined effects of systemic inflammation and diminished vascular protection. In our cohort, the median ictus-to-repair time was 30 h and two-thirds of patients underwent repair within 40 h after ictus, which likely contributed to the relatively low incidence of preoperative rebleed (4.79%). This aligns with prior evidence that shortening the ictus-to-repair interval, preferably within 24 h, remains the most effective strategy to reduce preoperative rebleed risk, whereas differences between clipping and coiling primarily influence post-treatment outcomes rather than preoperative rebleed [6, 36–39].

An unexpected finding in our cohort was the absence of documented preoperative rebleeds during the first 0–6 h after ictus, although previous studies have reported a peak rebleeding risk in this ultra-early window [36, 39, 40]. This discrepancy is likely related to the tertiary referral nature of our center, the relatively long median ictus-to-arrival time, and the exclusion of patients with preadmission deterioration or suspected prehospital rebleed, which together introduce survivor and referral bias. In addition, very early rebleeds during the prehospital or inter-hospital transfer phase may have been indistinguishable from the index hemorrhage. Thus, our data probably underestimate ultra-early (0–6 h) rebleeding events and should not be interpreted as contradicting the established high rebleeding risk during the first hours after ictus.

The clinical relevance of NHR is supported by Lu et al., who reported that rebleed independently worsens outcomes irrespective of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) [8, 9]. This underscores rebleed as a distinct and deleterious process likely mediated by cerebral edema, blood–brain barrier disruption, and exacerbated inflammation. Consistent with this, elevated NHR has been associated with an increased risk of early hematoma expansion in spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) [27] and with unfavorable outcomes in acute ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, and aortic dissection [16, 17, 23, 41–43]. These findings collectively suggest that NHR reflects systemic inflammation and vascular instability common to multiple cerebrovascular pathologies, thereby supporting its association with early rebleed in aSAH.

In our cohort, HDL-C values remained within sex-specific reference ranges across NHR strata, whereas neutrophil counts were higher in patients who rebled, indicating that the observed elevation in NHR was primarily neutrophil-driven rather than HDL-C–driven. Because NHR integrates both heightened inflammatory activity and diminished vascular protection, it can serve as a surrogate indicator of early vascular vulnerability and, in our study, was associated with preoperative rebleed and 3-month outcomes. Our findings complement the post hoc ULTRA trial analysis showing that tranexamic acid (TXA) failed to prevent rebleed within 6 h after ictus [40], suggesting that early rebleed is more strongly influenced by non-fibrinolytic mechanisms, particularly inflammation and vascular instability. Moreover, Lu et al. identified early rebleed as being associated with EVD placement and poor admission grade [9], further linking neurovascular instability to procedural and systemic inflammatory factors. Elevated NHR may therefore reflect this underlying inflammatory–vascular imbalance predisposing to aneurysmal re-rupture.

However, the relevance of NHR in hemorrhagic cerebrovascular pathology, particularly in aSAH, has remained unexplored until now. Several mechanisms may explain the observed association between NHR and aneurysmal rebleed (Fig. 4). A key contributor is neutrophil-mediated vascular injury [44]. Following aneurysm rupture, neutrophils infiltrate the subarachnoid space and release reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which disrupt the extracellular matrix and impair endothelial integrity, thereby weakening the aneurysmal wall and predisposing it to re-rupture [19, 45–50]. In parallel, HDL-C depletion compromises vascular protection. HDL-C normally promotes nitric oxide production and exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects; its reduction, reflected by an elevated NHR, diminishes endothelial resilience and increases susceptibility to hemodynamic stress [18, 51]. An activated inflammatory state may also impair cerebrovascular autoregulation and promote a prothrombotic milieu. Neutrophils enhance platelet activation and tissue factor release, while low HDL-C impairs fibrinolysis, together favoring microthrombi formation and local hypoxia within the aneurysm wall [12, 20, 22, 42, 44]. This inflammatory–thrombotic imbalance delineates a transient time window of vascular vulnerability, during which even minor physiological fluctuations may precipitate an early rebleed. Collectively, these mechanisms support the biological plausibility of NHR as a surrogate marker of aneurysmal fragility and reinforce its independent association with early rebleed observed in our analyses.

Possible mechanism of rebleed. Elevated NHR reflects increased neutrophil-driven inflammation and reduced HDL-C-mediated vascular protection. This imbalance may promote aneurysm instability through neutrophil-mediated vascular injury, endothelial dysfunction, impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation, and a prothrombotic microenvironment, ultimately increasing the risk of rebleed

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, referral patterns may have introduced selection bias, resulting in fewer high-grade Hunt–Hess and mFisher cases than reported elsewhere. Critically ill patients might not have survived transfer or were managed locally, potentially underrepresenting severe cases. Second, NHR was measured only at admission, and its dynamic changes during the acute phase were not assessed. In addition, the timing of ictus, rebleeding, and blood sampling was abstracted from clinical records, and very early rebleeds, particularly in the prehospital or inter-hospital transfer phase, may have been misclassified. Consequently, some samples drawn within 6 h of admission may have been obtained after a rebleed, so that the measured NHR reflects a post-rebleed rather than a strictly baseline state in a subset of patients. Third, early aneurysm repair in many patients likely lowered the overall rebleed incidence, introducing possible confounding related to treatment timing. Fourth, despite the large baseline cohort and adjustment for multiple confounders, the number of rebleed events was relatively small, which may limit statistical stability. Finally, given sex-related HDL-C differences and the small event count (n = 36), ROC-derived thresholds are exploratory and unstable. Accordingly, we report overall discrimination (AUC) only and refrain from proposing a clinical cut-off until externally validated.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the level of admission NHR is independently associated with preoperative rebleed in patients with aSAH and may serve as a clinically useful adjunct marker of aneurysm re-rupture risk. By reflecting both systemic inflammation and reduced vascular protection, NHR complements established clinical and radiological predictors. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating serial inflammatory profiling and validation of NHR-based thresholds are warranted to confirm its prognostic value and to explore its integration into individualized management in acute aSAH management.
