Enhancing Diversity in Large Language Models via Determinantal Point Processes
Yilei Chen, Souradip Chakraborty, Lorenz Wolf, Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis, Aldo Pacchiano

Abstract
Supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning are two popular methods for post-training large language models (LLMs). While improving the model’s performance on downstream tasks, they often reduce the model’s output diversity, leading to narrow, canonical responses. Existing methods to enhance diversity are limited, either by operating at inference time or by focusing on lexical differences. We propose a novel training method named DQO based on determinantal point processes (DPPs) to jointly optimize LLMs for quality and semantic diversity. Our approach samples and embeds a group of responses for each prompt, then uses the determinant of a kernel-based similarity matrix to measure diversity as the volume spanned by the embeddings of these responses. Experiments across instruction-following, summarization, story generation, and reasoning tasks demonstrate that our method substantially improves semantic diversity without sacrificing model quality.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are typically post-trained to better align with human intentions or to perform effectively on downstream tasks
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
. Among post-training methods, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) are two of the most widely used approaches
(
Ziegler et al. 2020
;
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
. These methods substantially improve the quality of model outputs for targeted tasks. However, a widely observed drawback is that post-training often leads to a sharp reduction in output diversity
(
Kirk et al. 2023
;
Murthy et al. 2024
;
Anderson et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Casper et al. 2023
)
. Models trained with SFT or RLHF tend to converge toward a narrow set of canonical responses
(
Kirk et al. 2023
;
Go et al. 2023
)
. This lack of diversity can be problematic. There are many scenarios where maintaining diversity in generated outputs is desirable and important. For example, first, in reasoning and personalization, diverse responses enable the model to solve problems through different approaches or to provide a range of answers from which users can choose their preferred option. Second, diversity directly benefits test-time applications: it improves best-of-n performance, mitigates the effects of distribution shift between training and test rewards, and ensures better coverage of multiple modes in the reward landscape. Finally, from the perspective of training dynamics and search, diverse outputs foster exploration and support the discovery of novel strategies.

Common practices to encourage diversity in LLM outputs are largely restricted to inference-time interventions, such as adjusting temperature
(
Ackley et al. 1985
)
, top-k sampling,
(
Holtzman et al. 2020
)
, or other refined sampling strategies
(
Nguyen et al. 2024
;
Franceschelli and Musolesi 2025
)
. These methods attempt to enhance diversity by spreading out the model’s output distribution. However, modifying the next-token distribution only superficially increases variability at the token level and often comes at the expense of response quality. More importantly, such techniques are fundamentally limited by the base model’s learned distribution: if the model has already collapsed to a narrow set of responses, no sampling strategy can recover the missing modes. A more principled solution is to directly optimize for diverse and high-quality outputs during training. However, this raises two core challenges. First, measuring and optimizing diversity in a way that is both computationally efficient and theoretically grounded is nontrivial. Second, there is an inherent tension between promoting diversity and preserving response quality, requiring careful balancing.

Several recent works have attempted to enhance diversity in LLMs through training, but their efforts largely remain confined to the lexical level of diversity. For instance,
Yao et al. 2025
introduce a token-level entropy regularizer to encourage diversity on positive samples. However, token-level entropy often fails to capture human-intended diversity, which typically arises at the semantic rather than lexical level.
Lanchantin et al. 2025
consider diversity in direct preference optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
by dividing responses into high- and low-reward groups, then selecting the most diverse candidate from the high-reward group as the preferred answer in pairwise comparisons. Yet, their definition of diversity is still surface-level, relying on measures such as generation probability or the number of unique words. Similarly,
Li et al. 2025
propose preserving diversity in supervised fine-tuning by carefully controlling probability transfer between tokens during updates, again focusing only on token-level variation. More broadly, existing approaches emphasize the promotion of local lexical differences rather than encouraging LLMs to produce a set of responses spans distinct and meaningful modes of the answer space. Most related to our work,
Chung et al. 2025
propose a variant of DPO that weights the loss by the average pairwise distance in cosine similarity after embedding responses, this however, is limited to DPO, considers only pairwise distances, and requires sampling
k\geq 3
responses per prompt in the training dataset.

In this work, we propose a principled training method based on determinantal point processes (DPPs)
(
Kulesza et al. 2012
)
to directly optimize LLMs for both quality and diversity in generated responses. Unlike token-level entropy or lexical perturbations, our approach operates at the semantic level. Specifically, for each prompt we sample a set of responses, map them into an embedding space using a pretrained encoder, and compute a similarity matrix via a kernel function. The diversity score is then defined as the determinant of this matrix, which corresponds to the volume spanned by the response embeddings. Optimizing this objective encourages the model to generate responses that span a subspace in the answers’ embedding space with the largest volume. The reward of each response can be regarded as a scaling factor of the corresponding embedding vector, providing an interpretable mechanism to balance quality against diversity. We name our algorithm as DQO (
D
iversity
Q
uality
O
ptimization). We evaluate DQO across instruction-following, summarization, story generation, and reasoning tasks, and demonstrate that it significantly enhances semantic diversity while maintaining high response quality.

Related works

Evaluating Diversity of LLMs.
Several works have focused on evaluating the diversity of LLM generated content
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Shaib et al. 2024
)
, also on investigating the impact of post-training on diversity metrics
(
Kirk et al. 2023
;
Shypula et al. 2025
)
. The lack of diversity in LLM generated content also affects text written by humans using LLMs
(
Padmakumar and He 2024
)
.

Improving Diversity of LLMs.
There are mainly two lines of works on promoting diversity in LLMs. One focuses on inference strategies.
Nguyen et al. 2024
proposed a decoding method to reallocate the next-token probabilities which they show can increase the entropy of the correct solutions. The DiffSampling strategy, proposed by
Franceschelli and Musolesi 2025
, considers the largest difference between consecutive probabilities of tokens in a sorted distribution to promote diversity while maintaining correctness.
Ahmed et al. 2025
proposed a two-stage inference strategy which consists of a high-temperature key words sampling process and a low-temperature expansion procedure.

Another line of works focus on the training strategy to best elicit diversity from LLMs.
Lanchantin et al. 2025
proposed diverse preference optimization. They selected the most diverse response from the high-reward group and the least diverse response from the low-reward group to form the preference pair. The selection is based on some diversity criteria.
Yao et al. 2025
shows that by adding an entropy term of correct answers to the reward-based objective, LLMs can improve the diversity while maintaining the quality. Different from those using reinforcement learning algorithms,
Li et al. 2025
instead study the supervised finetuning approach. They proposed carefully-designed update strategy to mitigate the distribution collapse in SFT, thus encourages diversity. Most related to our work,
Chung et al. 2025
propose a variant of DPO that weights the loss by the average pairwise distance in cosine similarity after embedding responses, this however, is limited to DPO, considers only pairwise distances, and requires sampling
k\geq 3
responses per prompt in the training dataset.

Determinantal Point Processes.

Determinantal point processes (DPPs)
(
Kulesza et al. 2012
)
, are a class of probabilistic models
that arise in quantum physics and random matrix theory for modeling repulsion. DPPs are well-suited for modeling diversity.
Parker-Holder et al. 2020
proposed a DPPs-based algorithm to train a population of diverse polices in
reinforcement learning for better exploration.

Preliminaries

3.1
Notations

For ease of readability, we summarize some frequently used notations here. We use
x
and
y
to represent a prompt and a response, respectively. We represent a group of
k
responses
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\}
by
y_{1:k}
and we denote
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{k}\}
by
y_{-i}
. We use
I_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
to represent the identity matrix with size
k
. And
\det(\cdot)
represents the determinant of a matrix.

3.2
Determinantal point processes (DPPs)

In this work, we quantify the diversity of LLMs generated outputs based on the concept from DPPs. We introduce the definiton of L-ensembles below which is a subclass of DPPs. For a comprehensive introduction to DPPs, please refer to
Kulesza et al. 2012
.

Definition 1
(
L
-ensemble)

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
be a ground set, and
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
be a random subset. Suppose
L\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix. We say
L
defines an
L
-ensemble, if for every
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\Pr(\mathbf{Y}=A)\propto\det(L_{A}),

where
L_{A}
is the submatrix of
L
indexed by
A
.

DPPs are well-suited for promoting diversity because their probability measure inherently discourages the selection of similar items. If we think of the entries of
L
as measurements of similarity between pairs of elements, such as the dot product of the feature vectors of items, the determinant
\det(L_{A})
corresponds to the squared volume spanned by the feature vectors of items in
A
, which increases when the vectors are diverse and decreases when they are redundant or highly correlated. Consider the following example. Suppose
A=\{i,j\}
is a two-element set, then, by the definition,

\displaystyle\Pr(A)\propto\begin{vmatrix}L_{ii}&L_{ij}\\
L_{ji}&L_{jj}\end{vmatrix}=L_{ii}L_{jj}-L_{ij}L_{ji}

If item
i,j
are super similar, then
L_{ij}
is large, and the above probability is low. Conversely, if
L
is diagonal, i.e.
L_{ij}=0
, there are no correlations and the elements appear independently.

4
Proposed method

4.1
Diversity via DPPs

Based on the above definition of DPPs, given a bunch of responses
y_{1:k}
, we can formulate the diversity score of this group of responses as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Div}(y_{1:k})=\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))

(1)

where
L_{\phi}(y_{1:k})[i,j]=f(\phi(y_{i}),\phi(y_{j}))
,
f
is a kernel function and
\phi(\cdot)
is a selected embedding model which can map a response into a high-dimensional semantic space. In this work, we select the kernel function as the dot product,
f(\phi(y_{i}),\phi(y_{j}))=\langle\phi(y_{i}),\phi(y_{j})\rangle
. For simplicity, when it is clear from the context, we will omit the subscript in
L_{\phi}
.

Our definition of diversity offers two main advantages. First, it operates in the embedding space of the responses, allowing it to capture semantic diversity which is typically what humans intend. Second, its determinant-based definition induces a notion of group diversity that overcomes the limitations of simple pairwise distance metrics. A key weakness of using pairwise distances, such as the average distance between all responses, is its susceptibility to the "clustering" phenomenon
(
Parker-Holder et al. 2020
)
. An algorithm optimizing for this metric might produce responses that form a few distinct, widely separated clusters, creating a misleading sense of diversity. In contrast, our determinant-based metric, which encourages the formation of a parallelepiped with a large volume in the embedding space, directly addresses this issue. The determinant is highly sensitive to the linear independence of the response vectors. If responses form tight clusters, the vectors within a cluster become nearly linearly dependent, causing the determinant of the similarity matrix to approach zero, regardless of the large distances between clusters. This correctly identifies a lack of true diversity. Furthermore, the determinant is a more robust measure because it recognizes when responses, despite having large pairwise distances, are confined to a lower-dimensional subspace. This forces the system to explore the full high-dimensional embedding space, ensuring genuine diversity that simple pairwise distances fail to capture.

4.2
Quality-Diversity objective

Reinforcement learning has been a popular method for post-training LLMs with either an existing reward function or the one inferred from a preference dataset (e.g., RLHF). With the reward function, the model is typically optimized by maximizing the following KL-regularized objective,

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\{J(\pi_{\theta})-\beta KL(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref})\}

(2)

where
J(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
is the expected return and
\beta
is a hyperparameter balancing the KL divergence penalty and rewards. It can be shown the optimal policy of the above objective satisfies,

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y|x)\propto\pi_{ref}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)

(3)

As we have pointed out in the introduction section, after alignment, the model tends to converge toward a narrow set of responses, leading to limited diversity. To solve this issue of diversity collapse, inspired by the concepts from DPPs, we propose the DQO algorithm which maximizes the following objective to directly optimize LLMs for both quality and diversity in generated responses,

\displaystyle J_{Div}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x,y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(x,y_{i})+\alpha\log\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))\right].

(4)

For each prompt, we sample
k
responses
y_{1:k}
from the model like what people do with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Instead of optimizing only the reward, we incorporate a diversity term into the objective, where
L_{\phi}(y_{1:k})
is defined in (
1
). The hyperparameter
\alpha
controls the trade-off between quality and diversity.

It can be shown by optimizing (
2
) with our
J_{Div}(\pi_{\theta})
, the optimal policy satisfies,

\displaystyle\pi_{div}(y_{1:k}|x)\propto\pi_{ref}(y_{1:k}|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(x,y_{i})+\alpha\log\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))\right)\right)

(5)

For simplicity of exposition, suppose
\beta=\alpha
. We can define a reward-augmented new embedding vector for the prompt-response pair
(x,y)
as

\psi(x,y)=\sqrt{exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\cdot\phi(y)
.
The reward plays a role as a scaling factor of the original semantic embedding. With the formulation of the new embeddings, we can show our optimal policy satisfies,

\displaystyle\pi_{div}(y_{1:k}|x)\propto\det(L_{\psi}(x,y_{1:k}))

(6)

The above expression tells us that our policy (
6
) learns to generate a group of responses with the probability proportional to the determinant of the gram matrix formed by the embedding vectors of these responses. From a geometric view, our policy can pick a group of vectors in the embedding space of responses according to the squared volume of the space spanned by these vectors.

The balance between quality and diversity also admits a clear geometric interpretation. The embedding vector
\psi
consists of two components: a semantic embedding vector, which determines its direction, and a reward, which determines its norm. To maximize the volume of the spanned space, one should select vectors that are well separated from each other (i.e., diverse responses) while also having large norms (i.e., high-quality responses). The overall trade-off is governed by the hyperparameter
\alpha
.

4.3
Algorithm

In the preceding section, we introduced our quality-diversity (QD) objective based on DPPs in a principled way, which aims to optimize LLMs for both response quality and diversity. However, implementing this objective in practice poses several challenges, such as the high variance of estimation of stochastic gradients and potential numerical explosion. In this section, we present a practical version of the algorithm designed to stabilize training. First, we can calculate the gradient of
J_{Div}(\pi_{\theta})
is as follows,

\displaystyle\nabla J_{Div}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x,y_{1:k}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x)(r(x,y_{i})+\alpha\log\det(L(y_{1:k})))\right]

(7)

The first issue is the determinant of
L(y_{1:k})
can be close to zero which leads to a super negative value of
\log(\det(L(y_{1:k})))
. The unboundedness of the diversity term makes the training process unstable and also makes the balance between quality and diversity difficult such that only a carefully selected
\alpha
is effective. To fix this issue, we propose to consider the determinant of the matrix
L(y_{1:k})+I_{k}
. It can be shown that by adding an identity matrix, we have
k\geq\log(\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k}))\geq 0
which is well-bounded. Briefly speaking, adding an identity matrix to our objective can be regarded as a regularization term, in Appendix
A
, we discuss how it affects the objective.

The second issue is that the gradients is the summation of the gradient of
k
responses. It has high variance especially when
k
is large. To mitigate the issue of inflating variance, we propose to use leave-one-out (
loo
) gradient estimators by subtracting the log-determinant of the gram matrix which leaves one response out,

\displaystyle\nabla^{loo}J_{Div}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x,y_{1:k}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(x,y_{i})\left(r(y_{i})+\lambda\log\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}\right)\right]

The
loo
estimator is unbiased and based on the following Eigenvalue Interlacing Theorem, it has a nice property on the boundedness of its value shown in Lemma
1
.

Theorem 1
(Eigenvalue Interlacing Theorem
(
Fisk 2005
)
)

Suppose
A\in R^{n\times n}
is symmetric. Let
B\in R^{m\times m}
with
m<n
be a principal submatrix (obtained by deleting both
i
-th
row and
i
-th column for some values of
i
). Suppose
A
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}

and
B
has eigenvalues
\beta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{m}
. Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\leq\beta_{k}\leq\lambda_{k+n-m},\text{ for }k=1,\cdots,m

And if
m=n-1
, one has,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq\beta_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{n-1}\leq\lambda_{n}

Lemma 1

Let us write the eigenvalues of
L(y_{1:k})
as
\lambda_{k}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1}
, then we have
1+\lambda_{k}\geq\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I)}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I)}\geq 1+\lambda_{1}
. And the eigenvalue of
L(y_{1:k})
is always in
[0,k]
since the embedding vectors are normalized, we have
1+k\geq\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I)}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I)}\geq 1
and
\log(1+k)\geq\log\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I)}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I)}\geq 0
.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we propose an algorithm DQO to directly optimize LLMs for both quality and diversity in generated responses. Based on determinantal point processes, DQO defines the diversity in a group of responses as the determinant of a kernel-based similarity matrix of the embeddings of those responses. This definition of diversity has a straightforward interpretation as the squared volume of the space spanned by the embeddings of the response. We conduct extensive experiments across different kinds of tasks, and show that DQO can optimize the model to generate significantly more diverse responses while maintaining high quality in the generated responses.

Besides the performance, we discuss the limitations of this work, which also suggest interesting directions for future research. First, in this work, we use existing reward models since we observed from our experiments that the model is vulnerable to reward hacking when only the outcome reward is provided. The outcome reward is widely used in most reasoning tasks. In this setting, the model tends to produce a correct answer to secure the outcome reward, followed by random or irrelevant content to artificially increase diversity. This phenomenon does not exist when we use reward models, since the reward model evaluates the quality of the entire response rather than just the final answer.

We explored a potential solution by computing the cosine similarity between the embeddings of the prompt and the response and multiplying this score with the diversity term. The intuition is that only responses semantically aligned with the prompt contribute to the diversity objective. However, we observed that the model can still exploit this mechanism by generating answers that are both correct and superficially related to the prompt, suggesting that outcome-based reward alone is inherently susceptible to reward hacking. This phenomenon presents an intriguing challenge for future study.

Another limitation of our approach lies in the use of embedding models to map responses into a semantic space. The performance of our algorithm depends on the quality of these embeddings. A more principled or adaptive method for measuring diversity could better capture the underlying semantic variation, potentially adjusting automatically to the specific requirements of different tasks, which may emphasize different aspects of diversity, thus achieves better performance.

The Gram matrix is

L=B^{\top}B.

Now suppose we scale each column
\phi(y_{i})
by a factor
a_{i}
, and denote

A=\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),\quad B^{\prime}=BA.

Then the new Gram matrix is

L^{\prime}=(B^{\prime})^{\top}B^{\prime}=(AB^{\top})(BA)=A(B^{\top}B)A=ALA.

Taking determinants,

\det(L^{\prime})=\det(ALA)=\det(A)\,\det(L)\,\det(A)=\big(\det(A)\big)^{2}\det(L).

Since
\det(A)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}
, we obtain

\det(L^{\prime})=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\right)^{2}\det(L).

Recall that
\pi_{div}(y_{1:k}|x)
is defined as when
\alpha=\beta
,

\displaystyle\pi_{div}(y_{1:k}|x)

\displaystyle\propto\pi_{ref}(y_{1:k}|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(x,y_{i})\right)+\log\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{ref}(y_{1:k}|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(x,y_{i})\right)\right)\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\pi_{ref}(y_{i}|x)\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y_{i})}{\beta}\right)\right)\det(L_{\phi}(y_{1:k}))

The second equality holds because
y_{1:k}
are sampled independently. Combined with the result above, we have
\pi_{div}(y_{1:k}|x)\propto\det(L_{\psi}(y_{1:k}))
.

Analysis of
\det(L(y_{1:k}))
and
\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})
.

Maximizing
\det(L)
is equivalent to maximizing the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the selected feature vectors, which enforces strict linear independence: any subset that induces a singular
L
receives zero score. In contrast, maximizing
\det(L+I)
introduces a ridge-like regularization. Indeed, if
L=BB^{\top}
for a feature matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, we have

\det(L+I)=\det(BB^{\top}+I)=\det(I+B^{\top}B).

This is precisely the determinant of a regularized scatter matrix, analogous to the role of
(B^{\top}B+\lambda I)
in ridge regression. From this viewpoint, adding
I
stabilizes the objective by preventing collapse along directions of near-linear dependence and avoiding the degeneracy of zero determinants.

A complementary interpretation arises from Bayesian linear models and Gaussian processes. In Bayesian linear regression with a Gaussian prior
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and unit-variance observation noise, the marginal likelihood normalization involves
\det(I+B^{\top}B)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
. Similarly, in Gaussian process regression, the log marginal likelihood includes
\log\det(L+\sigma^{2}I)
, with
\sigma^{2}
corresponding to the noise variance. Setting
\sigma^{2}=1
recovers the
\det(L+I)
objective. Hence,
\det(L+I)
can be viewed as the determinant under a model with a prior noise floor, which softens the diversity requirement and balances between variance explained by the selected items and a baseline level of uncertainty.

Eigenvalue Interlacing Theorem
[
Fisk 2005
]
.

Suppose
A\in R^{n\times n}
is symmetric. Let
B\in R^{m\times m}
with
m<n
be a principal submatrix (obtained by deleting both
i
-th
row and
i
-th column for some values of
i
). Suppose
A
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}

and
B
has eigenvalues
\beta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{m}
. Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\leq\beta_{k}\leq\lambda_{k+n-m},\text{ for }k=1,\cdots,m

And if
m=n-1
, one has,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq\beta_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{n-1}\leq\lambda_{n}

Proof.

We use the Courant–Fischer min–max theorem. For a symmetric matrix

A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
, the
k
-th eigenvalue can be characterized as

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\dim(S)=k\end{subarray}}\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in S\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{x^{\top}Ax}{x^{\top}x}.

Similarly, for the principal submatrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with eigenvalues

\beta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\\
\dim(T)=k\end{subarray}}\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in T\\
y\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{y^{\top}By}{y^{\top}y}.

Now observe that
B
is obtained by restricting
A
to a coordinate subspace
(corresponding to removing some rows and columns). Hence any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be
embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by padding with zeros. Under this embedding,
the Rayleigh quotient is preserved:

\frac{y^{\top}By}{y^{\top}y}=\frac{x^{\top}Ax}{x^{\top}x},\quad\text{where $x$ is $y$ padded with zeros}.

Therefore, the feasible subspaces for
B
are restrictions of those for
A
.
This leads to the inequalities

\lambda_{k}\leq\beta_{k}\leq\lambda_{k+n-m},\quad k=1,\dots,m.

In the special case
m=n-1
, the inequalities expand into the chain

\lambda_{1}\leq\beta_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{n-1}\leq\lambda_{n},

which is exactly the interlacing property.

Lemma.

Let’s write the eigenvalues of
L(y_{1:k})
as
\lambda_{k}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1}
, then we have
1+\lambda_{k}\geq\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}\geq 1+\lambda_{1}
. And the eigenvalue of
L(y_{1:k})
is always in
[0,k]
since the embedding vectors are normalized, we have
1+k\geq\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}\geq 1
and
\log(1+k)\geq\log\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let’s write the eigenvalues of
L(y_{-i})
as
\beta_{k-1}\geq\cdots\geq\beta_{1}
. Based on Eigenvalue Interlacing Theorem, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}=(1+\lambda_{1})\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1+\lambda_{i+1}}{1+\beta_{i}}\geq 1+\lambda_{1}

and,

\displaystyle\frac{\det(L(y_{1:k})+I_{k})}{\det(L(y_{-i})+I_{k-1})}=(1+\lambda_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1+\lambda_{i}}{1+\beta_{i}}\leq 1+\lambda_{k}

Since
L(y_{1:k})
is positive semidefinite, it holds
\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ \forall i
. And we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=\mathrm{tr}(L(y_{1:k}))=k
due to the normalization of the feature vectors. Hence, we have
k\geq\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq 0
.
