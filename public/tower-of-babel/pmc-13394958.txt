Blocking SETD2 Enhances the Therapeutic Efficiency of Menin Inhibitor in MLL ‐Fusion Leukemia
Authors: Anpei Li, Yuan Zhang, Ziwei Li, Anhao Liu, Li Zhang, Shubin Gao, Xiao Lin, Xiaoming Liu, Huan Li, Guanghui Jin, Bin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuring transcriptional elongation, the histone methyltransferase SETD2 binds to RNA polymerase II and deposits trimethylation marks at histone H3 lysine 36 (H3K36me3). However, the role of SETD2 in MLL‐fusion leukemia has remained controversial over the past decade. Here, through genotyping of loss‐of‐function SETD2 mutations, we define the genetic basis for SETD2 acting as a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor in this disease. Then, we demonstrate that gene amplification and high expression of SETD2 are associated with poor prognosis, supporting the double‐sided roles of SETD2 in this kind of acute leukemia. Next, we show that inhibitors of SETD2 (EZM0414) and menin (Revumenib) have synergistic efficacy against MLL‐fusion and NPM1‐mut leukemia and make prominent induction of cell cycle arrest, differentiation, and apoptosis. Moreover, SETD2 inhibition sensitizes leukemia cells to menin inhibitor through decreasing H3K36me3 marks, and combination therapy dramatically inhibits transcription of the canonical targeted genes, whose function may account for these phenotypic changes. The mode of action of this combined treatment is due to the attenuated both H3K36me3 at gene body regions and H3K4me3 at gene promoter regions. Finally, we clarify that the combined‐drug treatment delays MLL‐fusion leukemia progression in vivo. Taken together, these findings establish the simultaneously blocking of transcription elongation and initiation by epigenetic inhibitors as a promising therapeutic strategy for these aggressive leukemias.

Introduction

Distinct histone methylation events occur sequentially or concomitantly during transcriptional initiation and elongation [1, 2, 3]. SET domain containing 2 (SETD2)—the unique methyltransferase responsible for histone H3 lysine 36 trimethylation (H3K36me3)—interacts with RNA polymerase II (pol II) and couples H3K36me3 deposition with transcription elongation [4, 5, 6]. H3K36me3 in the gene body regions suppresses histone exchange to maintain the accuracy of transcription by pol II [7]. Cryo‐electron microscopy data revealed that SPT6 mediates the interaction between SETD2 and nucleosome [8]. Meanwhile, SETD2 exhibits methyltransferase activity toward non‐histone substrates [9, 10, 11].

SETD2 typically exerts a tumor‐suppressive function in solid tumors and acts as a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor in diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [9, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The haploinsufficiency of tumor suppressor frequently induces the malignant phenotypes of the cancer, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly defined. Moreover, EZM0414 is a first‐in‐class, potent, selectively and orally bioavailable inhibitor of SETD2 enzymatic activity [16]. The preclinical studies show that EZM0414 resulted in significantly impaired growth of DLBCL and multiple myeloma (MM) [17, 18]. These findings suggest that DLBCL with SETD2 haploinsufficiency can be treated by targeted inhibition of the remaining wild‐type allele.

However, the function of SETD2 in leukemia with MLL (Mixed Lineage Leukemia, also known as KMT2A) translocation mutation (MLL‐fusion) has remained controversial in the last decade. On the one hand, SETD2 was a tumor suppressor: loss‐of‐function mutations of SETD2 were recurrent in MLL‐fusion leukemia, and knockdown of SETD2 confers a proliferative advantage of MLL‐fusion leukemia cells [19, 20]; Heterozygous knockout of SETD2 decreased the latency of MLL‐AF9‐induced leukemia, whereas homozygous deletion of SETD2 delayed leukemia progression, supporting SETD2 is a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor, and the residual wild‐type allele could be a potential therapeutic target [21, 22]. On the other hand, SETD2 was oncogenic: SETD2 was identified as leukemia dependency during MLL‐fusion leukemogenesis [23, 24]; Dietary methionine starvation impaired AML development and H3K36me3 epigenetic marks; EZM0414 treatment phenocopied much of the cytotoxic effects of methionine deletion [25]. Accordingly, the rationale for targeted inhibition of SETD2 in MLL‐fusion leukemia needs to be further confirmed.

MLL‐rearrangements occur in > 70% infant acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and 5%–15% of children and adults with acute leukemia including myeloid, lymphoid, mixed lineage or biphenotypic phenotypes. The most common fusion partners are AF9 and AF4, which are predominantly found in AML and ALL respectively [26]. The MLL‐fusion leukemias are frequently associated with poor prognosis [27, 28, 29]. MLL‐fusion mutation resulted in aberrant expression of homeobox A (HOXA) cluster and their DNA‐binding cofactor MEIS1 [30, 31], which are determinants for leukemia transformation and maintenance [32, 33]. Menin, the protein of MEN1 (Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia 1) gene, is a critical oncogenic cofactor of MLL‐fusion leukemia [34]. During leukemogenesis, menin interacts with MLL or MLL‐fusion proteins and recruits these histone methyltransferase complexes to MEIS1 and HOXA promoter regions catalyzing H3K4me3 (written by MLL) or H3K79me2 (written by MLL‐fusion proteins/DOT1L) modification, and regulates transcription initiation of target genes [35, 36, 37]. Pharmacologic inhibition of menin‐MLL interaction blocked the progression of leukemia with MLL‐fusion and NPM1 gene mutation (NPM1‐mut) [38, 39]. NPM1‐mut is the most common genetic alteration in adult AML with high expression of HOXA cluster genes and MEIS1 [40, 41].

The menin inhibitor Revumenib was reported to be safe and effective in relapsed or refractory acute leukemia patients with MLL‐fusion or NPM1‐mut [42, 43], and it has recently received FDA approval for the treatment of these kinds of acute leukemia. However, nearly half of the patients failed to respond to this therapy, and a subset of patients acquired resistance to Revumenib treatment due to somatic mutations of the MEN1 gene [44]. Therefore, combination therapy will be a valuable treatment strategy in the future. Several small molecular inhibitors have demonstrated promising efficacy against MLL‐fusion leukemia in combination with menin inhibitors [45, 46, 47, 48]. During a phase 1/2 first‐in‐human study of the menin inhibitor Ziftomenib (KO‐539) in patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia, one complete remission patient was observed with a SETD2 mutation; however, the mutation pattern was not shown [49]. Therefore, the feasibility of combining inhibitors of SETD2 and menin to treat MLL‐fusion leukemia is urgently needed to be clarified.

In this study, we elucidate the dual role—haploinsufficient tumor suppressor and oncogene—of SETD2 in MLL‐fusion leukemia. Next, we clarify that combined SETD2 and menin inhibitors exert synergistic effects against MLL‐fusion and NPM1‐mut leukemia, and provide the mechanistic insights into this combination therapy.

Materials and Methods

SETD2 Mutation Analysis in Leukemia Patients From Literature Reports

SETD2 mutation analysis was conducted by curating SETD2 mutation data from AML and ALL patients across three key published studies [19, 50, 51]. The DNA sequencing methods included Sanger or Illumina sequencing of PCR amplicons about SETD2 coding regions, and Illumina sequencing following SETD2 exon‐hybrid capture. Nonsense, frameshift, and splice site mutations are labeled as loss‐of‐function mutations, and loss‐of‐function mutations of SETD2 were genotyped according to the results from original authors or allele frequencies (homozygous: > 75%, heterozygous: 30%–75%, point mutation with allele frequency 1%–30% (subclonal) was also referred to as heterozygous). The functional effects of point somatic mutations were predicted through PolyPhen‐2 (Polymorphism Phenotype v2, http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), PROBABLY DAMAGING is regarded as a loss‐of‐function mutation of SETD2 (File S1). The original materials of clinical characteristics of leukemia patients with SETD2 wild‐type, het‐loss, hom‐loss are in File S2.

Correlation of SETD2 Gene Copy Number With Patient Prognosis

The clinical data of AML and ALL patients were downloaded from cBioPortal for cancer genomics (http://www.cbioportal.org/). Cases with available SETD2 copy number and survival information were included for Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. At last, all the cases used for Kaplan–Meier analysis were from TARGET (Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments) and TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) (File S3).

SETD2 Expression and Prognosis Analysis

Comparative analysis of SETD2 expression between AML and normal samples was performed through GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis, http://gepia.cancer‐pku.cn/). Kaplan–Meier's survival curve of high‐expression and low‐expression cohorts was made through GEPIA2 and Kaplan–meier plotter (https://www.kmplot.com/analysis/).

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Human leukemia cell line THP‐1 was obtained from National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (NICR, http://www.cellresource.cn/index.aspx). MOLM13 and OCI‐AML3 were obtained from German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (DSMZ, https://www.dsmz.de/dsmz). MV4; 11 and K562 were gifts of Siming Chen from School of Pharmaceutical sciences, Xiamen University. MOLM13, MV4;11, THP‐1, OCI‐AML3 and K562 were cultured in RPMI‐1640 media (Basal Media); 293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Basal Media). All growth media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cegrogen), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (PS, Sigma). Cell lines were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Small Molecular Inhibitors

Revumenib (HY‐136175) and EZM0414‐TFA (HY136328) were purchased from MedChem Express LLC. JIB‐04 (S81423) was supplied by Shanghai Yuanye Bio‐techoogy Co. Ltd., China. JQ1 (S7110) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals. Drug stocks were diluted in DMSO, stored at −80°C.

In Vitro Cell Viability Assay and Analysis of Drug Synergy

1 × 105 cells were plated in 12‐well flat bottom microtiter plates with 1 mL of culture medium. Cells were split and replated in fresh media every 2–3 days. For IC50 determination of combination treatment, cells were treated with SETD2 inhibitor (EZM0414) and menin inhibitor (Revumenib) sequentially diluted 2 or 4 fold for a total of 10 concentrations plus vehicle control (DMSO). The ratios of EZM0414 and Revumenib were 1:1 or 16:1, that is, the highest concentration of EZM0414 was 5 μM and the highest of Revumenib was 5 or 0.32 μM. Cell viability was determined via the CCK‐8 assay. For the evaluation of drug synergy, the combination indexes (CI) were calculated by the Chou‐Talalay method via Compusyn software tool (https://www.combosyn.com).

Colony‐Formation Assay

Clonogenic potential was assessed through colony growth in methylcellulose‐based medium (MethoCult H4230, Stemcell Technologies) involving DMSO (vehicle), EZM0414, Revumenib or EZM0414 + Revumenib. The leukemia cell lines were plated in triplicate at a cell dose 1 × 104/well with 1 mL mehtycellulose‐based medium in 12‐well flat bottom microtiter plates. The concentration of EZM0414 is 5 μM, and the concentration of Revumenib is 5 μM (THP‐1) or 0.32 μM (MOLM13 and MV4;11). Cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 7–10 days, then colonies were counted under MoticamPro282B imaging system (Motic) at 20× magnification. A colony was defined as a cluster of more than 10 cells, and a blast‐like colony was defined as a cluster of more than 500 cells.

Cell Cycle Analysis by Flow Cytometry

At the endpoint of drug treatment, cells were harvested at 70%–80% confluence, washed twice with ice‐cold PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4°C for 24 h. Fixed cells were then treated with RNase A (#R1030, 100 μg/mL; Solarbio) to degrade RNA and stained with propidium iodide (#P8080, 50 μg/mL, Solarbio) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed using a Beckman CytoFlexS flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo Version 10 software.

RNA‐Seq

RNA‐seq assays were performed in MOLM13 after treatment with EZM0414, Revumenib, and Combo (EZM0414, 1.25 μM, 6 days; Revumenib, 0.08 μM, 1 day). Cell pellets were immediately lysed in 1 mL of Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The lysate samples were shipped to Seqhealth Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China) on dry ice to ensure RNA integrity, strictly complying with the company's sample submission guidelines. Subsequent experimental procedures, including cDNA library construction and high‐throughput sequencing, were performed by the company using an Illumina HiSeq platform. The consequent bioinformatics analysis was also completed by Seqhealth Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

ChIP‐Seq

ChIP‐seq assays are performed in MOLM13 after treatment with Combo (EZM0414, 1.25 μM, 6 days; Revumenib, 0.08 μM, 1 day). The cells were harvested for ChIP as described in the method details. The purified DNA fragments were delivered to Novogene Biotech Co. Ltd. in dry ice according to the sample delivery instructions. Subsequent experimental procedures, including DNA library construction and high‐throughput sequencing, were performed by the company using an Illumina HiSeq platform. The consequent bioinformatics analysis was also completed by Novogene Biotech Co. Ltd.

In Vivo Efficacy Analysis in Mice

6–8‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 MV4;11 cells in BD MatrigelTM Matrix (#354234). Treatments were started when the mean tumor volume reached about 100 mm3. Baded on the tumor volume, mice were randomly assigned to respective groups: vehicle (10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween‐80 and 45% saline), EZM0414 (5 mg/kg, twice daily, i.p.), Revumenib (1 mg/kg once daily, i.p.) and combination of the two drugs (n = 5 or 6 per group). Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every day. Tumor volume = 0.52 × a × b 2, where “a” and “b” are the long and short diameters of a tumor. Drug treatments were stopped when the tumor volumes of vehicle group reached approximately 1500 mm3. Then, mice were euthanized and tumor weights were assessed. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Xiamen University and conducted in strict compliance with animal welfare and ethical guidelines.

Cellular differentiation was assessed by flow cytometry using CD11b staining and Wright‐Giemsa staining. Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry. Lentiviral transduction was performed to knock down or overexpress SETD2. Protein expression was examined by western blotting. Gene expression was quantified by RT‐qPCR. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed to analyze histone modifications and transcription factor occupancy at target gene loci.

Detailed information can be found in Data S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistics were calculated using Prism, Version 9 software (GraphPad). Student t‐test was used to assess significance in vitro assays. p values < 0.05 were considered significant with *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001, ns, not significant. All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent experiments.

Results

SETD2 Is a Potential Therapeutic Target in MLL ‐Fusion Leukemia

Heterozygous or homozygous deletion of SETD2 was reported to have opposite effects in mice leukemia progression [19, 21]. To clarify whether SETD2 is a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor in MLL‐fusion leukemia, we collected the mutation information of SETD2 gene in leukemia patients from three most relevant literatures [19, 50, 51]. There is a higher frequency of SETD2 mutations in MLL‐fusion patients (20%, 11 out of 55) compared to MLL‐WT (wild type) patients (6.4%, 55 out of 860), p < 0.01 (Figure 1A). This result is consistent with previous report [19]. Nonsense, frameshift and splice site mutation are common loss of function mutation patterns. Consequently, we performed genotyping analysis of the SETD2 mutation according to the mutation pattern and allele frequency (Table S1). Furthermore, the functional effects of some point mutations are predicted by PolyPhen‐2, and we find a higher frequency of heterozygous SETD2 loss (66.7%, 6 out of 9, without loss of heterozygosity) compared to homozygous SETD2 loss (22.2%, 2 out of 9) in MLL‐fusion leukemia (Table 1). This result provides a genetic basis for SETD2 haploinsufficiency in MLL‐fusion leukemia. Similar to the haploinsufficiency of SETD2 in diffuse large B‐cell lymphomas (DLBCL) [15], the remaining wild‐type SETD2 allele will be a contributor to MLL‐fusion leukemia. Then we compare the clinical characteristics of leukemia patients with SETD2 wild‐type, het‐loss, hom‐loss, and no bias of distribution among these three groups, which is attributed to the limited number of the cases (Table S2).

Some studies reported that SETD2 was a co‐activator in MLL‐fusion leukemia [23, 24, 25]. Due to the limited number of MLL‐fusion leukemia patients with SETD2 copy number or expression information, we exploit the characteristics of SETD2 gene copy number in leukemia patients incuding AML and ALL from cBioPortal for cancer genomics. Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrate that both overall survival and event‐free survival are significantly lower in the SETD2 gain group, but higher in the SETD2 loss group (deep or shallow deletion) compared to diploid group (Figure 1B,C), suggesting that SETD2 copy number is associated with prognosis of leukemia patients. Next, compared to the normal control, high expression of SETD2 are detected in AML patients through GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) (Figure 1D), despite the difference of the Kaplan–Meier survival curves is not statistically significant (Figure S1). In addition, SETD2 high expression is associated with poor prognosis in another AML patient cohort through Kaplan–Meier Plotter, the median survival is 11.7 months compared to 17 months of SETD2 low expression group (Figure 1E).

Together, the above results provide insights into the haploinsufficient tumor suppressor or oncogene roles of SETD2 in a subset of MLL‐fusion leukemia respectively, and establish targeted inhibition of SETD2 as a promising epigenetic therapy for this aggressive leukemia.

Combined Inhibitors of SETD2 and Menin Make Synergistic Effects Against MLL ‐Fusion Leukemia

AF9 and AF4 are the most common MLL translocation partners [26]. Therefore, we firstly assess the effects of combining SETD2 and menin inhibitors (EZM0414, Revumenib) in MOLM13 (MLL‐AF9) and MV4;11 (MLL‐AF4) leukemia cell lines. Because SETD2 inhibitor EZM0414 attenuated MOLM13 proliferation at high concentration (50 or 100 μM) [25], we select the concentration gradient in a constant 1:4 ratio with low concentration of EZM0414 (maximum concentration: 5 μM) and pre‐treatment strategy in cell viability assay to minimize potential toxicity.

MOLM13 was pretreated with EZM0414 for 5 days, followed by co‐treatment with Revumenib for an additional 4 or 6 days. As expected, SETD2 and menin inhibitors dramatically enhance cell viability inhibition compared to single drug treatment, as reflected by a shift of dose–response curve toward high doses (Figure 2A; Figure S2A). Next, after 3 days pretreatment with EZM0414, MV4;11 was exposed to a combination of EZM0414 and Revumenib for 2 or 4 days. Combo group shows a similar enhanced cell viability inhibition as MOLM13 (Figure 2B; Figure S2B). In addition to the data with MOLM13 and MV4;11, we also confirmed the potent efficacy of this combination therapy in THP‐1 (MLL‐AF9) cells (Figure 2C; Figure S2C). THP‐1 is resistant to menin inhibitor [52]. Thus, higher dose and prolonged exposure to EZM0414 and Revumenib are required to make synergistic effects. As reported, menin inhibitors block the progression of leukemia with NPM1 mutation [40, 41]. OCI‐AML3 (NPM1‐mut) were pretreated with EZM0414 for 6 days, followed by co‐treatment with Revumenib for an additional 6 or 9 days, and we observed the drug synergism similar to MLL‐fusion leukemia cell lines (Figure 2D; Figure S2D). All drug synergism were confirmed by Chou‐Talalay algorithm with Combination Indices (CI) < 1 (Figure 2A–D: lower or right panels). Notably, combination therapy decreases the IC50 values of the two inhibitors (Figure 2E). Furthermore, long term culture of MLL‐fusion leukemia cell lines with EZM0414 and Revumenib show more efficacious results (Figure S2E,F). On the contrary, we don't find any obvious drug synergism in Jurkat and K562 cell lines, which lack MLL translocation and NPM1 mutation (Figure S2G,H), suggesting the genetic specificity of this combination strategy.

To extend these findings we tested the clonogenic potential of human leukemia cell lines in methylcellulose containing DMSO or drugs. Consistent with the prominent effects in liquid culture, the colony‐forming potential of MLL‐fusion‐driven leukemias is significantly inhibited by the combined two drugs, whereas modest decreases are induced by each one of the drugs (Figure 2F; Figure S2I,J). These data indicate that combined blocking of SETD2 and menin has synergistic efficacy against MLL‐fusion and NPM1‐mut leukemia.

Combined‐Drug Treatment Promotes Cell Cycle Arrest, Differentiation, and Apoptosis

Menin inhibitors selectively prevent MLL‐fusion and NPM1‐mut leukemia cell growth through cell cycle (G0/G1) arrest, induction of differentiation, and apoptosis [38]. We performed fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis to assess the three major cell phenotypes after drug treatment. Two‐drug treatment causes a more pronounced inhibition of the G0/G1 phase compared to a single drug alone, and the same results are also found in serum starvation and release assays (Figure 3A; Figure S3A,B). Compared to single‐drug treatment, an enhanced profile shift of CD11b positive cells is induced by two‐drug treatment (Figure 3B). Moreover, we observe more substantial morphology changes (nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio) in the combination therapy group (Figure 3C). The results also indicate a more profound increase in apoptotic cells after two‐drug treatment than either drug alone (Figure 3D). Ultimately, to confirm the above results, we detect some major biomarkers of these three phenotypes by immunoblotting. The results show that combined‐drug treatment causes even more reduction of the G0/G1 checkpoint associated kinase CDK6, and more up‐regulation of the myeloid differentiation associated marker CD11b and key executor of apoptosis cleaved‐caspase3 compared to single‐drug treatment (Figure 3E). Taken together, these results demonstrate that combined‐drug treatment synergistically inhibits cell viability of MLL‐fusion leukemia through blocking the cell cycle and promoting cell differentiation and apoptosis.

SETD2 Inhibition Sensitize Leukemia Cells to Menin‐Inhibitor Through H3K36me3

EZM0414 is a selective inhibitor for enzymatic activity of SETD2; it can reduce the H3K36me3 modification in vivo and in vitro [16]. Immunoblotting analysis shows that EZM0414 effectively causes reduction of H3K36me3 level in a dose‐dependent manner, while Revumenib has no effect on this modification in MLL‐fusion leukemia (Figure 4A). In addition, two drugs treatment makes a similar decrease of H3K36me3 to EZM0414 (Figure 4A; Figure S4B). Moreover, EZM0414 and/or Revumenib don't affect the total levels of H3K4me3, H3K79me2, and H3K27me3 (Figure S4A,B), which excludes the possibility that EZM0414 results in the cell phenotypes through reverse increase of H3K27me3 levels, just like SETD2 knockout in prostate cancers [11]. Thus, the reductions of H3K36me3 level induced by EZM0414 are correlated with the phenotypes of combination therapy. To exclude the potential off‐target of EZM0414, we knock down the SETD2 expression by shRNA in MOLM13 cells. Two shRNAs significantly downregulate the SETD2 expression and the total H3K36me3 levels (Figure 4B), which makes the leukemia cells more sensitive to menin inhibitor treatment (Figure 4C). Because ectopic expression of full‐length SETD2 was extremely difficult, we used the SETD2‐F2 fragment including the SET domain to promote the H3K36me3 modification in MOLM13. SETD2‐F2 over‐expression elevates the H3K36me3 levels modestly and partly blocks the sensitivity of MOLM13 cells to Revumenib (Figure 4D,E).

Next, we use the histone demethylase KDM4A inhibitor JIB‐04 to rescue the H3K36me3 in MOLM13 with SETD2 shRNAs [21]. Considering JIB‐04 can inhibit MOLM13 proliferation in a dose dependent manner, we choose the low concentration of JIB‐04 with mild effect on cell proliferation (Figure S4C). The rescue experiment results demonstrate that JIB‐04 restores the H3K36me3 levels modestly and partly blocks the sensitivity of MOLM13 cells to Revumenib (Figure 4F,G). Together, EZM0414 enhances the anti‐proliferation effect of Revumenib through decreasing H3K36me3 modification.

Combined‐Drug Treatment Enhance the Suppression of Target Gene Transcription

To identify the precise transcriptional pathways controlled by this combination therapy, RNA‐seq assays were performed in MOLM13 (MLL‐AF9) after treatment with EZM0414, Revumenib, and Combo. Compared to single‐drug treatment, two‐drug treatment induces significantly more genes to undergo changes in expression levels—4413 and 4498 genes are up‐ and down‐regulated respectively (Figure 5A–C). The top genes (logFC > 0, p < 0.05) in EZM0414, Revumenib, and Combo groups were subjected to cluster analysis. We observe that a subset of genes, including the HOXA cluster and MEIS1—the direct target genes of MLL‐fusion proteins—are moderately suppressed by single‐drug treatment and further reduced in expression levels by two‐drug treatment (Figure 5D).

In addition, gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that various inflammatory pathways are activated (Figure S5A), suggesting a stress response of the cells after combined‐drug treatment. Moreover, cell differentiation, apoptosis, and positive regulation of cell death are enriched terms for up‐regulated genes (Figure S5B), which are consistent with the phenotypic consequences of two‐drug treatment in Figure 3. Next, we further used quantitative RT‐PCR to validate expression changes for the HOXA cluster and MEIS1 in MOLM13; the results demonstrate that combined‐drug treatment causes even more pronounced reduction of HOXA7, HOXA9, HOXA10, and MEIS1 compared to single drug alone (Figure 5E). Furthermore, SETD2 knockdown with or without Revumenib phenocopies EZM0414 treatment at HOXA9 and MEIS1 transcriptional levels (Figure S4D,E). In contrast, combined‐drug treatment in MV4;11 (MLL‐AF4) makes a much more pronounced reduction of MEIS1, but not the HOXA cluster (Figure S5C). This result is consistent with the report that MEIS1 is the common target gene of MLL‐AF9 and MLL‐AF4, and the HOXA cluster is the specific target genes of MLL‐AF9 [53]. Taken together, two‐drug treatment enhances the transcription inhibition of a common set of canonical target genes, whose function may explain the profound efficacy against MLL‐fusion leukemia cell lines.

Combined Inhibitors of SETD2 and Menin Attenuate H3K36me3 and H3K4me3 at Target Genes Loci

In order to uncover the underlying epigenetic mechanism of this combined treatment to target genes, we performed GO enrichment analysis for the RNA‐seq data. Notably, the top 10 significantly functional annotation terms of down‐regulated genes in Combo group focus on RNA polymerase II related regulation of transcription (Figure S5D), suggesting the two inhibitors—EZM0414 and Revumenib probably inhibit transcription of canonical target genes through RNA polymerase IIrecruitment and elongation. SETD2 binds phosphorylated RNA pol II (serine 2) and establishes H3K36me3 modification, which plays an important role in the transcription elongation process [4, 5, 6]. According to the published ChIP‐seq data [25], H3K36me3 mainly locates in gene body regions, whereas H3K4me3 focuses on promoter and promoter‐proximal regions (Figure 6A). Therefore, we design four primer pairs for each target gene as shown by amplicons P1–P4 (P1‐promoter region; P2‐promoter‐proximal region; P3, P4‐gene body region) (Figure 6A). The ChIP‐PCR results in MOLM13 cells demonstrate that combined‐drug treatment dramatically reduces H3K36me3 and H3K4me3 levels of MEIS1 loci, where H3K36me3 mainly distributes at P3, P4 amplicons with low level at P1, P2 amplicons and H3K4me3 focuses on P1, P2 amplicons with no detection at P3, P4 amplicons. The similar decrease of H3K36me3 and H3K4me3 levels are observed in HOXA9 and HOXA11 regions by two‐drug treatment. Moreover, the peaks of H3K36me3 are around P3, P4 amplicons, and the peaks of H3K4me3 are around P2, P3 amplicons. The overlapping of H3K36me3 and H3K4me3 distribution at HOXA cluster loci is probably due to the short length of the gene body compared to MEIS1 (Figure 6B–D). Nevertheless, there are low levels of H3K36me3 at promoter (P1) and promoter‐proximal regions (P2) of target genes, suggesting the potential roles of H3K36me3 written by SETD2 on transcription initiation and pause‐release (Figure 6B–D).

In addition, the H3K79me2 modifications of MEIS1 loci are significantly attenuated by combined‐drug treatment without apparent peak region (Figure S6A). However, H3K79me2 modifications of HOXA9 and HOXA11 loci are not affected at P1, P2 amplicons and are modestly elevated at P3, P4 amplicons (Figure S6B,C), suggesting the complex functions of H3K79me2 on target gene transcription.

Next, the ChIP analyses show that two‐drug treatment decreases the recruitment of MLL, MLL‐AFs, and RNA polymerase II to both the transcriptional start sites and gene bodies of MEIS1, HOXA9, and HOXA11 (Figure S6D–I). At last, we profile these three positive histone methylation modifications at the whole genome by ChIP‐seq assay. H3K36me3 shows wide peaks extending from transcription start sites (TSSs) across gene bodies, H3K4me3 generally focuses in narrow peaks directly over TSSs, and H3K79me2 is between these two patterns (Figure S6H). Furthermore, we observe slight attenuations of H3K4me3 and H3K79me2, rather than a global decrease of H3K36me3 (Figure S6H), suggesting only a selective subset of direct target genes is essential to the effects of this combined‐drug treatment.

These data demonstrate that inhibitors of SETD2 and menin suppress target gene transcription by decreasing H3K36me3 levels of gene body and H3K4me3 levels of gene promoter, providing a molecular rationale for the simultaneous inhibition of transcription elongation and initiation to more effectively prevent MLL‐fusion leukemia. A graphical abstract summarizing the present study is provided in Figure 7. Interestingly, this finding is consistent with the reports that histone deacetylase (HDAC) complex is recruited to gene body by H3K36me3, and histone acetyltransferase (HAT) complex is recruited to promoters by H3K4me3, suggesting a balance of HDAC and HAT activity at transcribed genes is probably sustained through regulation of H3K36 and H3K4 methylation [7].

Combined‐Drug Treatment Delays MLL ‐Fusion Leukemia Progression In Vivo

We next sought to establish the therapeutic potential of combined‐drug treatment in vivo. Xenograft models of the MV4;11 cell line are established in BALB/c nude mice subcutaneously and treated with a single drug, two drugs, and vehicle. Consistent with the prominent effects in vitro (Figure 2B), the subcutaneous growth of MLL‐fusion leukemia is significantly inhibited by the combined two drugs, whereas modest decreases were induced by single drugs (Figure 8A–C). Drug treatment induces no toxicity in mice, as reflected by no alteration in body weight (Figure 8D). Moreover, immunoblotting analysis performed in samples isolated from the MV4;11 tumors shows obvious reduction of H3K36me3, but not H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, suggesting the nice specificity of EZM0414 in vivo (Figure 8E). Furthermore, the expression of MEIS1 decreases more obviously in the two drugs group compared to single drug groups, but not HOXA9 (Figure 8F), this result is similar to the effects in vitro (Figure S5A). Taken together, these data confirm that combined‐drug treatment is a synergistic approach to combat MLL‐fusion leukemia.

Discussion

SETD2 mutation is recurrent in MLL‐fusion leukemia, but the roles of SETD2 in this disease are controversial [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Here through genotyping analysis of the SETD2 mutation we first provide the genetic basis of SETD2 as a haplo‐insufficient tumor suppressor. Given that Ebert et al. found that heterozygous knockout of SETD2 decreased the latency of MLL‐AF9‐induced leukemia, whereas homozygous deletion of SETD2 delayed leukemia progression, we believe that SETD2 is a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor in a subset of patients with MLL‐fusion leukemia. Moreover, half of SETD2 mutations are heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in MLL‐WT leukemia, suggesting SETD2 haploinsufficiency may be a common phenomenon in acute leukemia. In addition, SETD2 interference by shRNA promoted self‐renewal potential of leukemia stem cells derived from MLL‐AF9 knock‐in mice [19], but induced myeloid differentiation of MLL‐AF9/NrasG12D AML cells [24]. These opposing effects of SETD2 knockdown on MLL‐fusion leukemia initiation and progression are possibly due to the different interference efficiency, that is, poor interfering efficiency mimics the effect of SETD2 haploinsufficiency. Meanwhile, there is a small portion of patients with MLL‐fusion leukemia who have biallelic loss‐of‐function mutation of SETD2, which might be due to the genetic interaction of SETD2 with other driver mutations during leukemogenesis.

In this study, we report that combined inhibitors of SETD2 and menin achieve synergistic effects against leukemia cell lines with different MLL‐fusions and NPM1‐mut. Consistent with the profound effects in liquid culture, colony‐formation potentials of leukemia with MLL‐fusion are almost completely abolished in combination treatment. Moreover, this combined therapy dramatically reduced the IC50 of EZM0414 and Revumenib, which will minimize the adverse drug reactions in patients. In addition, the two drugs are potent and selective inhibitors with good PK/PD in vivo, so both of them are undergoing Phase 1 clinical studies. Although these results show the synergistic effects of SETD2 and menin inhibition in the maintenance of MLL‐fusion leukemia, further investigation is necessary to confirm whether this combined treatment will prevent the MLL‐fusion‐mediated transformation of HSC.

SETD2 is also required to maintain high H3K79me2 level at critical target genes, and knockdown of SETD2 sensitizes MLL‐fusion AML cells to EPZ5676 [24]. Our results demonstrate that H3K79me2 widely distributed in promoter and gene body loci of MEIS1, and combined treatment decreases the modification of H3K79me2. A similar spread pattern is observed at HOXA9 and HOXA11 loci, but combined treatment does not change the H3K79me2 modification at P1, P2 amplicons and selectively increase the levels of H3K79me2 at P3, P4 amplicons, which might be a compensatory elevation. These findings reflect the specificity and complexity of H3K36me3, H3K4me3 and H3K79me2 on target gene transcription regulation, suggesting that EPZ‐5676 combined with EZM0414 and Revumenib will be more efficient in MLL‐fusion leukemia treatment. This raises the possibility to elucidate the crosstalk of these positive histone methylation marks on gene transcription regulation during leukemogenesis.
