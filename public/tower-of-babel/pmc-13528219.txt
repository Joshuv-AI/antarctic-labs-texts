Photon-counting detector CT vs energy-integrating detector CT for the diagnosis of arterial dissection in the cervicocerebral region: A single-center case series
Authors: Kazuhisa Matsumoto, Masahiro Nakashima, Tatsuya Kawai, Takatsune Kawaguchi, Misugi Urano, Nobuo Kitera, Seita Watanabe, Akio Hiwatashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared the imaging findings and diagnostic confidence of photon-counting detector CT (PCD-CT) and energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT) for identifying cervicocerebral arterial dissections. We retrospectively analyzed 22 cases (11 PCD-CT and 11 EID-CT). Imaging findings, diagnostic confidence, and radiation exposure were assessed, and all patients underwent MRI or digital subtraction angiography for confirmation. Dissection was identified in 9/11 patients (82%) using PCD-CT and in 4/11 patients (36%) using EID-CT. Two PCD-CT cases and 7 EID-CT cases required MRI or DSA because neither an intimal flap nor a contrast-enhanced false lumen could be identified on CTA. Delayed-phase imaging improved visualization in 2 PCD-CT cases. There were no significant differences in CT dose index volume (CTDIvol) or dose-length product (DLP) between the 2 groups. Under the imaging conditions used in this study, PCD-CT provided clearer visualization of cervicocerebral arterial dissection than EID-CT at a comparable radiation dose, particularly when delayed-phase imaging was combined with PCD-CT's high contrast resolution.Keywords: Cerebral arteries, Computed tomography angiography, Dissection, Photon-counting detector CT

Introduction

Cervicocerebral arterial dissections, including internal carotid and vertebral artery dissections, have become widely recognized as a major cause of ischemic stroke among young adults [1]. Diagnostic methods for arterial dissection have included examinations by ultrasonography, digital subtraction angiography (DSA), CT angiography (CTA), and MRI. Although DSA had historically been regarded as the gold standard based on its ability to visualize the vessel lumen, it is invasive and is often replaced by noninvasive alternatives such as MRI and CTA. MRI is particularly valuable for its ability to detect mural hematomas and assess vessel-wall abnormalities with high sensitivity, whereas CTA excels in spatial resolution, enabling the precise visualization of intimal flaps and pseudo-lumens [2,3]. Studies comparing MRI and CTA for diagnosing arterial dissection have highlighted the unique advantages of each modality, but no consensus has been reached regarding which is superior.

Photon-counting detector CT (PCD-CT) directly converts X-ray photons into electrical signals without requiring scintillators and reflecting septa, resulting in improved spatial resolution, reduced noise, and minimized beam-hardening artifacts [[4], [5], [6]]. PCD-CT also facilitates ultra-high resolution (UHR) imaging with 0.2-mm slice thickness [7,8]. Several research groups have demonstrated the utility of PCD-CT for visualizing blood vessels [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. We anticipated that PCD-CT would offer superior diagnostic performance over conventional energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT) for identifying the hallmark features of arterial dissection, including intimal flaps and contrast-enhanced pseudo-lumens. We conducted the present study to compare the diagnostic performance of PCD-CT and EID-CT for identifying cervicocerebral arterial dissection.

Case presentation

We retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients with clinically and radiologically diagnosed cervicocerebral arterial dissection who underwent head CTA with a PCD-CT system at our institution between March 1, 2023 and November 30, 2025, and another consecutive group who underwent head CTA with an EID-CT system between April 1, 2018 and February 28, 2023, selected in reverse chronological order. Patients without available MRI or DSA for diagnostic confirmation were excluded. The final diagnosis was established based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical findings and available imaging studies, including CTA, MRI, and DSA. CTA was performed using standard institutional protocols; in the PCD-CT group, ultra-high-resolution (UHR) mode was used and an additional delayed-phase scan was performed in selected cases with insufficient arterial enhancement. Arterial dissection was considered present on CTA when an intimal flap or a contrast-enhanced false lumen was identified. Cases without these definitive findings were considered inconclusive on CTA. Two radiologists independently assessed the diagnostic confidence for arterial dissection using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = definitely absent, 2 = probably absent, 3 = equivocal, 4 = probably present, and 5 = definitely present). Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

A total of 22 patients were included in this study, with 11 patients undergoing PCD-CT and 11 undergoing EID-CT. One potentially eligible patient in the EID-CT group was excluded because confirmatory MRI or DSA findings were not available at our institution after transfer to another institution for treatment of concomitant Stanford type A aortic dissection. There were no significant differences between the groups in median age (62 [IQR, 48-79] years in PCD-CT vs 53 [IQR, 47-69] years in EID-CT, P = .490), CTDIvol (24 ± 8 vs 32 ± 14 mGy, P = .129), or DLP (847 ± 308 vs 873 ± 121 mGy·cm, P = .796). Delayed-phase imaging was performed in 2 PCD-CT cases because of insufficient arterial enhancement, resulting in improved visualization of the dissected vessel in both cases. Consequently, arterial dissection was identified on CTA in 9 of 11 patients (82%) in the PCD-CT group, including 7 of 11 patients (64%) on the initial arterial-phase images, compared with 4 of 11 patients (36%) in the EID-CT group. The median diagnostic confidence score was significantly higher in the PCD-CT group than in the EID-CT group (5 [IQR, 4-5] vs 3 [IQR, 2-4], P = .014). MRI findings were completely concordant with CTA in all 9 CTA-positive PCD-CT cases and in 3 of the 4 CTA-positive EID-CT cases. In the remaining EID-CT case, MRI demonstrated more extensive involvement of the basilar artery (BA) extending to the left vertebral artery (VA) than CTA. The remaining CTA-inconclusive cases (2 PCD-CT and 7 EID-CT) were diagnosed by MRI or DSA. The dissection involved the VA or BA in 7 PCD-CT patients and 8 EID-CT patients, and the internal carotid artery (ICA) in 4 and 3 patients, respectively. Patient 1

A patient in their sixties was admitted for surgery to treat pulmonary metastases from transverse colon cancer. As part of the preoperative risk assessment with the patient under general anesthesia, a head CTA was performed with PCD-CT, which incidentally revealed left ICA dissection. Since the patient was asymptomatic and the true lumen was preserved, conservative management was applied. An intimal flap was identified on both axial and coronal images (Fig. 1A and B).

Patient 2

For the comparison with PCD-CT, we presented the case of a patient in their fifties in whom an intimal flap was detected by EID-CT. The patient presented with sudden-onset hemiplegia and dysarthria. MRI showed an acute infarction in the left temporal lobe. On CTA, an intimal flap of the left ICA was identified on both axial and coronal images, with indistinct margins (Fig. 2A and B).

Patient 3

A patient in their nineties presented with right lower-limb motor dysfunction and sought medical attention the following day. MRI revealed acute infarctions in the left pons and right occipital lobe, leading to hospital admission. PCD-CT performed the same day identified a distinct intimal flap in the BA (Fig. 3A and B). On 3D T1-weighted turbo spin echo (TSE) imaging, high signal intensity was observed in the BA; this finding was nonspecific and insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis (Fig. 3C and D). Given the patient's history of rectal cancer with a best supportive care approach and previous transfusions, anticoagulant therapy was avoided due to the associated risk of bleeding. Rehabilitation was initiated the day after admission, and the patient's motor function gradually improved.

CTA images were displayed using identical vascular window settings (window level: 140 HU; window width: 600 HU). CTA, CT angiography, PCD, photon-counting detector.

Patient 4

A patient in their forties presented with posterior neck pain that had begun 7 days prior. On CTA with PCD-CT, the left VA appeared narrowed and showed poor contrast enhancement in the early phase, and the intimal flap was not clearly visible (Fig. 4A). Based on the CTA findings, an additional delayed phase scan was also performed, and it revealed improved contrast enhancement of the left VA compared to the early phase. An intimal flap and contrast enhancement within the false lumen were observed (Fig. 4B). The patient was admitted the same day and underwent regular follow-ups with MRI.

PCD-CT shows poor visualization of the left vertebral artery in the early phase (A), whereas the delayed phase (B) reveals an intimal flap and contrast enhancement within the false lumen, supporting the diagnosis of dissection. Discussion

In the cases of Patients 1 and 2, PCD-CT provided clearer visualization of the intimal flap compared to EID-CT. This advantage was attributed to the inherent high spatial resolution and reduced electronic noise of PCD-CT, especially when PCD-CT is combined with the UHR mode. In Patient 3, MRI showed a high-intensity signal in the basilar artery on 3D T1-weighted TSE, but the differentiation between dissection and vascular wall plaque remained inconclusive. The examination by PCD-CT enabled the definitive diagnosis by directly visualizing the intimal flap. This was achieved mainly through the high spatial resolution of PCD-CT, which enabled clearer delineation of the thin intimal flap compared to MRI. In Patient 4, the affected vertebral artery showed poor contrast enhancement in the early phase, likely due to delayed opacification caused by luminal narrowing from the dissection. In the delayed phase, contrast enhancement was observed within the dissected lumen, allowing visualization of the lesion. Despite the relatively low degree of enhancement, the dissection was clearly identifiable. This was attributed to the high contrast resolution of PCD-CT for subtle attenuation differences, which enabled delineation of fine intraluminal structures.

PCD-CT has been demonstrated to provide higher spatial resolution than EID-CT and to improve the depiction of small vessels by reducing blooming and beam-hardening artifacts. Mergen et al. reported improved spatial resolution and plaque visualization in coronary arteries with the use of PCD-CT [9,10], and studies by Harvey et al. and Symons et al. demonstrated an enhanced depiction of small vessels and reduced artifacts in cervicocerebral vasculature [11,12]. Coronary CTA was evaluated by Si-Mohamed et al. [13], who showed that these benefits could be achieved even with lower contrast and radiation doses. Hennes et al. [15] confirmed similar findings in aortic imaging using intraindividual comparisons. These findings are consistent with those observed in Patients 1, 2, and 3. On the other hand, ultra-high-resolution imaging with PCD-CT is known to increase image noise, computational demands, and susceptibility to motion artifacts as a trade-off for its higher spatial resolution. Although increased background image noise was also observed in the present case series, PCD-CT nevertheless demonstrated improved depiction of arterial dissection compared with EID-CT while CTDIvol and DLP remained comparable between the 2 groups. Keser et al. [16] compared the utility of PCD-CT and EID-CT in a patient with cervical artery dissection. As in the present study, they focused on arterial dissection, and they reported that PCD-CT provided clearer visualization of the dissection flap and associated pseudoaneurysm. Their results further support the clinical value of PCD-CT in this setting.

PCD-CT has also been shown to enhance the detection of low-contrast structures. Rajagopal et al. [17] reported that PCD-CT improved the visualization of noncalcified and hypodense plaques in coronary arteries compared to conventional EID-CT. The added diagnostic value of delayed-phase imaging was demonstrated in investigations involving the cervicocerebral vasculature and spinal arteries, highlighting its role in evaluating vessel patency, collateral flow, and subtle delayed enhancement [[18], [19], [20]]. Although delayed-phase imaging can also be performed with EID-CT and is therefore not unique to PCD-CT, it becomes particularly useful in hemodynamic conditions such as high-grade stenosis or near-occlusive disease, where the arterial phase may be captured before sufficient contrast has traversed the affected vessel, limiting visualization of the true lumen or an intimal flap. This mechanism is consistent with Patient 4′s case, in which the dissection flap was clearly visualized on delayed-phase imaging, thus emphasizing the diagnostic benefit achieved through appropriate contrast timing in combination with the superior low-contrast resolution of PCD-CT.

One of the major limitations of the present study is that the PCD-CT and EID-CT cohorts were acquired during different time periods and using different scanner platforms. Consequently, a potential limitation of the present study is that inconsistencies in imaging parameters and reconstruction kernels, particularly for EID-CT, may have influenced the results. Because the EID-CT scans were obtained from past cases, the imaging conditions were not standardized across all examinations. The EID-CT images were reconstructed with a relatively soft kernel, whereas the PCD-CT images used a sharper kernel, indicating that part of the apparent difference in image sharpness may reflect reconstruction choices rather than detector performance alone. In addition, temporal and equipment-related differences may have affected image quality and diagnostic yield, making it difficult to attribute the observed differences solely to detector technology. This variability could have affected the observed differences between PCD-CT and EID-CT, including in our comparisons of diagnostic accuracy and radiation exposure. Another limitation is the unequal imaging strategy between the 2 groups regarding delayed-phase acquisition. Delayed-phase imaging was performed only in a subset of the PCD-CT group to compensate for insufficient arterial enhancement, whereas no equivalent delayed-phase imaging was obtained in the EID-CT group. Because delayed-phase imaging may improve the visualization of dissection findings, such as an intimal flap or false lumen, independent of detector technology, it may have contributed to the higher detection rate observed in the PCD-CT group. Therefore, part of the apparent advantage of PCD-CT may reflect the additional diagnostic opportunity provided by delayed-phase imaging rather than detector performance alone. It should also be acknowledged that the diagnostic yield of EID-CT might improve if a comparable delayed-phase protocol were applied. Despite these limitations, our present analyses demonstrate that PCD-CT provided clearer visualization of dissection findings than EID-CT, and in some cases, even than MRI. The improved visualization translated into higher diagnostic confidence and may contribute to more accurate and timely diagnosis of cervicocerebral arterial dissection, particularly when conventional imaging is inconclusive.

Conclusion

Under the sharp ultra-high-resolution reconstruction conditions at our institution, photon-counting detector CT outperformed previous conventional energy-integrating detector CT schemes for the depiction of cervicocerebral arterial dissections at a similar radiation dose level. The added value was particularly evident when delayed-phase imaging was combined with PCD-CT's high contrast resolution.
