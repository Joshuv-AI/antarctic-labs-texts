Evaluation of the accuracy of 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates in pelvic tumor resection and reconstruction
Authors: Lulu Zhao, Zhengjia Zhang, Xin Zhou, Xiaomin Li, Wen Wu, Songtao Ai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe treatment of pelvic tumors has been widely recognized as challenging. Patient-specific osteotomy templates were designed and generated for precise surgery, based on tumor fusion images and 3D printing technology. This study aimed to investigate the accuracy of patient-specific osteotomy templates for the resection of pelvic tumors.MethodsFrom April 2014 to August 2023, 27 patients with pelvic tumors at our hospital were enrolled in this study. All patients underwent CT and enhanced MRI before surgery to develop a 3D preoperative imaging plan model for pelvic tumors based on tumor fusion images. For patients in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, we designed and generated patient-specific osteotomy templates for intraoperative assisted tumor resection. For patients in the no use of osteotomy templates group, surgeons performed tumor resection according to conventional techniques. All patients were followed up to obtain postoperative CT images of the pelvis. Preoperative planning osteotomy surface and intraoperative actual osteotomy surface were obtained by means of the image registration technique. Then, the distance deviation and angle deviation between the two surfaces were compared to assess the accuracy of surgery.ResultsSurgery was successfully completed in 27 patients, and all specimens were obtained for tumor-free resection margins. The median of intraoperative bleeding was 2350(1425 ∼ 3000) ml in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group and 4500(3150 ∼ 5200)ml in the no use of osteotomy templates group. p-value was 0.016. The median angular deviation of the actual osteotomy surfaces from the planned osteotomy surfaces was 5.02 (2.84 ∼ 7.37)° in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, and 7.17 (4.49 ∼ 11.96)° in the no use of osteotomy templates group. p-value was 0.044. The absolute mean distance deviation between the two surfaces was 4.90 ± 3.01 mm in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, and 7.21 ± 3.89 mm in the no use of osteotomy templates group. p-value was 0.038.Conclusions3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates can be accurately customized based on preoperative tumor fusion image, which can help improve the precision of pelvic tumor surgery.Keywords: Pelvic tumor, Tumor fusion images, 3D printing, Patient-specific osteotomy templates, Accuracy evaluation

Introduction

Limb salvage surgery has become the preferred procedure for most patients with pelvic tumors [1]. Due to the complex geometry and anatomical relationships of the pelvis, the main challenge for surgeons is to ensure surgical accuracy, that is, resection of tumors with safe margins and preservation of sufficient normal tissues to reconstruct limb function [2]. However, high complication rates as well as a recurrence risk of up to 28–35% are frequently observed in complex pelvic tumor surgery [3, 4].

Studies have shown that the quality of margins is strongly associated with tumor recurrence risk [5, 6]. However, excessive resection surrounding the tumor to achieve negative margins may result in difficulty in postoperative functional reconstruction [7].

Conventional methods performed based on anatomical landmarks and the surgeon’s visual observation and measurement may lead to inaccurate intraoperative osteotomies, posing the risk of unsafe margins and even affecting prosthesis implantation [8–10]. Even for experienced surgeons, maintaining the safety of surgical margins during pelvic surgery remains challenging [4]. In a study in which 24 patients underwent wide resection of malignant pelvic tumors, the surgical resections, classified according to the system described by Enneking et al., were found to be wide in 19 cases, marginal in 6 cases and intralesional in 1 case [11].

Advances in medical imaging and computer technology have made tumor surgery more precise. Several procedures for improving surgical accuracy have been described, such as intraoperative navigation systems [12, 13], robot-assisted surgery [14, 15] and the use of patient-specific osteotomy templates [14, 16, 17].

The main function of the 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates is the use of personalized adaptation as a guide. Due to the complexity and challenges of pelvic tumor surgery, the application of osteotomy templates for precise tumor resection is an effective treatment strategy [18]. This technique requires precise preoperative planning to accurately determine tumor boundaries and appropriate osteotomy planes. The osteotomy templates can guide bone-cutting along the preoperatively planned path, reproducing preoperative planning quickly and accurately during the actual operation. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of osteotomy templates in pelvic tumor surgery in terms of tumor resection margins and some clinical parameters (e.g., local recurrence, complications, survival, age at surgery, duration of surgery, tumor pathology type, etc.) [2, 19]. No significant differences have been reported between senior and junior surgeons using osteotomy templates in terms of the positioning accuracy and surgical margins obtained (both without intralesional resection), and osteotomies have been achieved with an accuracy no less than that of the navigation system [20].

To our knowledge, most studies on the accuracy of osteotomy template-assisted surgery have focused on synthetic models of pelvic tumors as well as cadaveric pelvic osteotomy studies or evaluation by postoperative specimens. There are few reports on how the osteotomy accuracy can be evaluated by a patient’s postoperative CT images. However, further clinical studies on the accuracy of osteotomy template-assisted pelvic tumor surgery are still necessary.

This study reports a series of pelvic tumor cases where two surgical techniques were used to assess osteotomy accuracy through image registration between preoperative and postoperative pelvic models, as well as between prosthesis models. We hypothesize that the use of patient-specific osteotomy templates will significantly improve the accuracy of osteotomies, leading to safer surgical margins and better alignment with preoperative planning compared to surgeries without the use of templates.

Materials and methods

Study population

Between March 2014 and August2023, 27 patients underwent surgery for pelvic tumor resection. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with pelvic tumors who had surgical indications and could tolerate surgical trauma; (2) patients with no contraindications to implantation; and (3) patients with no contraindications to CT and MRI examinations. Exclusion criteria were: 1.no osteotomy surfaces; 2.the pelvic tumor surgical planning STL file missing; and 3. poor quality of imaging.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the surgical technique used: (1) the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, where 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates were employed to guide the resection; and (2) the no use of osteotomy templates group, where conventional techniques were used without the assistance of templates.

The decision regarding which surgical technique to use for each patient was based on a combination of factors, including the location and size of the pelvic tumor, its proximity to critical structures (such as nerves, blood vessels, or organs), as well as preoperative imaging assessments. The surgical team conducted a multidisciplinary discussion for each case, considering the tumor’s complexity and the potential benefits of each technique. In cases where the tumor was located near vital structures, more precise osteotomy techniques were selected to minimize the risk of damaging surrounding tissues. Conversely, in less complex cases, a conventional technique was considered sufficient. Additionally, patient-specific factors, such as overall health status and potential for functional recovery, were taken into account when selecting the surgical approach.

SH9H-2021-T67-2). Informed consent was obtained before inclusion.

Image acquisition and digital processing

The preoperative 3D imaging planning protocol for pelvic tumors and design of patient-specific osteotomy templates were based on MRI and CT images. All patients underwent routine preoperative CT scanning or bilateral lower extremity CT angiography (CTA, for evaluation of the relationship between the tumor and important blood vessels) and enhanced MRI. The CT and MRI images of all patients were evaluated by two senior radiologists with more than 10 years of experience in diagnostic imaging. The location, size, Enneking region and boundary of the tumor were recorded. If there was disagreement on the assessment, a discussion was held to reach a final decision. All imaging images were stored in DICOM format.

Preoperative planning and patient-specific osteotomy template design

Radiologists and surgeons were involved throughout the preoperative planning and templates design process. CT and MRI images were processed by Medraw software (Image Medraw Technology Co., Ltd., China) to perform tumor segmentation and image registration. The extent of tumor infiltration was determined according to the T1WI-enhanced cross-sectional images and DWI images in the MRI images, and the tumor boundaries were outlined layer by layer. The software reconstructed the bony structures of the pelvis from CT images. At least 3 bony marker points (iliac crest, anterior superior iliac spine, posterior superior iliac spine, etc.) were selected according to the anatomical morphology of the pelvic bones. Alignment and fusion between images were performed by selecting the CT and MRI image fusion module. (Fig. 1) The safe resection boundaries were observed and determined on the fused images. The safe resection border for pelvic malignancy is the true tumor border expanded by another 3 to 5 cm. The resection margins of 3–5 cm were selected based on existing clinical guidelines and literature, which suggest this range as optimal for achieving negative margins while preserving as much healthy tissue as possible during pelvic tumor resection [21–24]. Then, the pelvic tumor model was obtained and saved in STL format. The pelvic tumor STL was imported into E-3D x64 V3.10 software, which allows further editing. The E-3D x64 V3.10 software, used for image modeling and registration, was downloaded from the official website of the provider (www.e3d-med.com). Afterwards, patient-specific osteotomy templates and pelvic reconstruction prostheses were designed based on the desired surgical plans (Fig. 2). The plane of osteotomy is determined based on the extent of surgical resection and the surrounding vital tissues. The osteotomy template was designed along the preoperatively determined osteotomy plane. The bone surface exposed after osteotomy in the optimal position was the planned osteotomy surface, and the bone surface exposed after intraoperative tumor resection was the actual osteotomy surface. The templates were designed to attach directly to the bone surface. The locating section was designed with round holes that guided the fixing pins. The completed models of the designed template were exported in an STL format recognized by the 3D printer. According to the clinical needs, reasonable methods, materials and parameters were chosen to complete the printing of the templates. The templates were postprocessed according to the application scene.

Surgical procedure

A standardized surgical procedure was applied to each patient. For patients in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, osteotomy templates were used to assist the surgeon in localizing the bone-cutting surface. The osteotomy templates were placed freehand by the surgeon and fixed on the bone surface using K-wires. For patients in the no use of osteotomy templates group, surgeons performed tumor resection according to conventional methods based on preoperative imaging plan. A histopathological analysis of the resected tumor specimens was performed. Intraoperative bleeding and resection margins were recorded in both groups.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients received X-rays and CT examinations at follow-up. Clinical follow-up time was defined as the time period from surgery to the patient’s last visit to our hospital.

Evaluation of the accuracy of intraoperative osteotomy

Postoperative CT images were performed using Medraw software to reconstruct the model of the patient’s pelvis and prosthesis. Anatomical landmarks (anterior superior iliac spine, sciatic tuberosity, etc.) were manually selected to achieve registration between the pre- and postoperative pelvic models. Meanwhile, to reduce the influence of metal artefacts on the actual osteotomy surface in postoperative CT images, we imported the preoperative STL model of the prosthesis into Medraw software, and aligned the preoperative planned prosthesis STL model to the real prosthesis through the same method. The smooth surface of the preoperative planning prosthesis model was used to replace the rough surface of the actual prosthesis model. The contact surface of the prosthesis with the bone was used as the actual intraoperative osteotomy surface. The preoperative planning osteotomy surface and the actual intraoperative osteotomy surface were extracted separately by the extraction of smooth plane method (Fig. 3). The software then provided angular deviation of the actual osteotomy surface relative to the planned osteotomy surface, as well as the point-to-point offset between them [15, 25] (See Figs. 4 and 5).

The above results were statistically analysed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. Homogeneous variances are reported herein as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD), and Student’s t test was used for statistical analysis. If the variances were not homogeneous, they are presented as the median with the range, as determined by statistical analysis with the Wilcoxon test. P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Pathologic and clinical findings

Surgery was successfully completed in 27 patients, and all surgical specimens obtained for tumor-free resection margins. There were no statistically significant differences between the basic clinical characteristics of the patients in the two groups (all P > 0.05). The median intraoperative bleeding volume was 2350 (1425 ∼ 3000) ml in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group and 4500(3150 ∼ 5200)ml in the no use of osteotomy templates group. The Wilcoxon test was chosen, and the p value was 0.016. (Table 2 Clinicopathological information and post-operative results of patients)

The accuracy of pelvic osteotomy

A total of 44 osteotomy surfaces were obtained in all patients: 29 osteotomy surfaces using osteotomy templates and 15 osteotomy surfaces using conventional methods.

Angular deviation.

The median angular deviation of the actual osteotomy plane from the planned osteotomy plane was 5.02 (2.84 ∼ 7.37)° in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, and this value was 7.17 (4.49 ∼ 11.96)° in the no use of osteotomy templates group. The Wilcoxon test was chosen, and the p value obtained was 0.044.

Distance deviation.

The absolute mean distance deviation between the actual osteotomy plane and the planned osteotomy plane was 4.90 ± 3.01 mm in the intraoperative use of osteotomy templates group, and this value was 7.21 ± 3.89 mm in the no use of osteotomy templates group. Student’s t test was chosen, and the p value obtained was 0.038.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether the tumor boundaries determined by CT and MRI fusion images could be accurately achieved during pelvic tumor surgery. And the study focused on the accurate replication of the preoperative plan using 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates. While this contributes to improved precision in executing pelvic tumor surgery, the overall improvement in clinical outcomes still requires further investigation.

In this study, the accuracy of the osteotomy templates was attributed to the followings.

First, complete image acquisition and accurate imaging planning are critical steps for improving surgical accuracy. Accurate implementation of the osteotomy templates technique requires precise preoperative planning, accurate determination of tumor boundaries and appropriate osteotomy planes. CT images can display the bony structures of the pelvis and the extent of bone destruction, and MRI images can accurately determine the tumor margins and surrounding soft tissues [26]. All patients in our study had well-acquired imaging images preoperatively. The three-dimensional model of pelvic tumor can intuitively display the tumor morphology and adjacency, providing an accurate basis for determining the tumor boundary and resection range. We used the CT&MRI image alignment fusion software to retain the imaging advantages of both CT images of bone tissue and MRI of soft tissue [27]. The fusion alignment between the images fully utilizes the morphological features in the CT and MRI images of pelvic tumors, and the method is highly reproducible. The design of the templates was based on preoperative tumor fusion images, which is unique and definitive. This is a prerequisite for precise execution of pelvic tumor surgery. The intraoperative osteotomy guides are fitted to the desired osteotomy site in the pelvis. The unique anatomical structure of the patient and the individualized shape of the osteotomy template only allow the osteotomy template to be placed in the preoperatively planned position. The osteotomy guide template can assist the operator to accurately position the tumor, effectively determine the osteotomy plane and direction, accurately and quickly remove the tumor entity, simplify the surgical operation, and achieve tumor resection within the safety boundary. Our previous patient-specific puncture templates designed based on tumor fusion images have obtained satisfactory results in bone tumor puncture biopsy applications [28].

Second, we used postoperative CT of the patients to evaluate the osteotomy accuracy, which is a major difference from most studies evaluating the accuracy of osteotomy templates. Postoperative CT images are often affected by metal prosthesis artefacts, making it difficult to directly evaluate the accuracy of surgical planning. There are few reports on how the osteotomy accuracy can be evaluated by a patient’s postoperative CT. There are several clinical imaging methods to minimize the effects of metal artefacts, such as metal deartefacting techniques based on dual-energy CT [29]. However, the imaging of large metal pelvic prostheses is not satisfactory because the image quality of normal structures could be compromised. Although the threshold segmentation method can reconstruct the prosthesis model, the surface is not smooth, and there is some distortion. For these reasons, in this study, the smooth surface of the preoperative planning prosthesis model was used to replace the rough actual osteotomy surface by means of the image registration technique between preoperative and postoperative prosthesis models, which avoided the effect of metal artefacts. This study needed two model registrations, and we used a point-to-point registration method. In the initial practice of our group, a total of 13 osteotomy surfaces were evaluated using this method in eight patients who underwent pelvic tumor surgery in our hospital, and the accuracy of all osteotomy surfaces was successfully assessed [30].

Third, both groups of patients in this experiment were operated on by the same group of surgeons (Ph.D., with decades of surgical experience), eliminating error stemming from the experience of different surgeons, which is another factor affecting the accuracy of surgery.

It is important to note that even when osteotomies are performed with the assistance of osteotomy templates, some errors still occur. There may be several reasons for this. First, the anatomy of the pelvis is complex. The proximity of vital structures such as blood vessels, nerves and important organs often results in limited visibility and a restricted workspace. Higher intraoperative bleeding will cause more restrictions. Furthermore, precise tumor resection is challenging. Second, the placement of the osteotomy templates can cause errors. Osteotomy templates are often designed to fit perfectly over the corresponding bone surface, which is a unique advantage but also has a drawback. The osteotomy templates do not have the expected effect on soft tissue. Soft tissues (muscles, ligaments, cartilage, tendons, etc.) on the surface of the bone or relatively large osteophytes that form an obstruction block can lead to deviations in intraoperative template placement [31–33]. Unlike navigation systems, osteotomy templates do not provide accurate intraoperative positional feedback and rely only on the surgeon’s subjective sense of fitting the contours of the bone [34]. Third, the bone tissue surrounding the tumor has a certain thickness. The osteotomy templates may deform when sawing the bone, and the resulting oscillation of the saw blade may also cause errors [35]. Fourth, the timeliness of the osteotomy templates is also critical. The templates are highly patient-specific and anatomically matched. For quickly progressing tumors, the longer the time between template design and surgery, the greater the risk of potential morphologic mismatch. Alternatively, a pathologic fracture during preoperative preparation may result in the loss of a bony structure with which the templates can be fitted.

The accuracy of the osteotomy templates helps to optimize the pelvic tumor osteotomy protocol, further reducing the extent of tumor resection and bone defects, and providing more satisfactory customized and personalized prostheses and internal implants. Especially when the bone tumor occurs in a functionally important area such as the acetabulum, it involves the stability of the pelvic ring structure and the patient’s gait function recovery. Preserving as much normal bone tissue as possible in the acetabular region is very important for patient reconstruction and recovery. In addition, bleeding was significantly reduced in the template group in this study, suggesting that osteotomy templates help to decrease the difficulty of the procedure and contribute to the reduction of patient injuries.

The precision of osteotomy not only relates to the safety of the tumor resection margins but also has implications for pelvic reconstruction. One of the hazards of inaccurate osteotomies is reflected in the intraoperative prosthesis fitting and the biomechanical stability of the prosthesis. Studies have reported potential hazards resulting from poor matching between the prosthesis and host bone tissue, such as infection, bone atrophy or loosening of the implant, and periprosthetic fracture [19, 36]. We observed in our study that if the actual osteotomy plane deviates significantly from the preoperative planning scheme, some revision of the osteotomy plane or repair of the implant material may be necessary to ensure good prosthetic alignment and stabilization of the pelvic ring. This process makes the surgery more difficult and inevitably leads to longer surgical times and increased intraoperative bleeding. The assistance of osteotomy templates can make intraoperative prosthesis fitting easier by minimizing distance deviations and angular deviations of the actual osteotomy plane. The improved match of the prosthesis facilitates further extension of the service life of the prosthesis. Of course, in our study, this requires further observation.

The current findings are instructive for improving osteotomy template design. First, the soft tissue distribution of the tumor osteotomy site should be evaluated preoperatively to adjust the design of the osteotomy templates. Ensure an adequate match to the bone tissue while fitting around vital soft tissue structures to minimize placement error of the osteotomy templates [37]. In addition, ensure adequate contact stability and develop measures to validate intraoperative template placement, such as a stability score of the contact surface between the osteotomy templates and bone [38]. Second, a cutting guide block can be added to the osteotomy templates. It has a certain thickness that can allow more stable control of the direction of the saw blade. At the same time, the guide block should not have too much flexibility, to avoid unintended oscillation of the saw blade during bone cutting. The material used to print the osteotomy templates can be a stronger metal to prevent accidental deformation during surgery. Finally, the design and production time of the osteotomy templates should be shortened to ensure timeliness. Alternatively, an osteotomy guide with multiple guide slots can be designed to allow flexibility in selecting the most appropriate guide slot if the desired osteotomy site changes.

Several limitations must be considered when reviewing the present study. First, the evaluation method in this study required a postoperative CT examination of the patient, which makes CT a necessary examination rather than X-ray, which is much cheaper and simpler. This undoubtedly increases the complexity of the diagnostic process as well as the medical cost. Second, this study did not directly assess the difference between the actual osteotomy plane and the planned osteotomy plane but instead chose to replace the actual osteotomy plane with the contact plane between the prosthesis model and the bone. There are some differences between the two planes in some cases, but the correlation between the two is strong, so the results are still significant. Third, we used a point-to-point registration method in this study. While this method is expected to be highly reproducible and generalizable, this was not tested in our study, as all surgeries were performed by two experienced surgeons. Further research is needed to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of this method across a broader range of surgeons. Fourth, a further limitation is the small number of patients, which is related to the lower prevalence of pelvic tumors. It is planned to further increase the number of patients and acquire more clinical data in future studies to enhance the persuasiveness of the experimental results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, using 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy template is an effective method for accurate reproduction of preoperative plans. In clinical practice, fusion of CT and MRI images allows for essentially accurate determination of pelvic tumor imaging boundaries as well as accurate customization of 3D-printed patient-specific osteotomy templates, which contributes to improved precision in the surgical execution of pelvic tumors.
