Self-reported sexually transmitted infections among women in Indonesia: analysis of 2017 Indonesia demographic and health survey
Authors: Septa Meriana Lumbantoruan, Ni Gusti Ayu Eka, Agustina Saputri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexually transmitted infections (STIs) are increasing around the world. An estimated over one million people are infected with STIs every day. The prevalence of STIs in Indonesia is largely unknown today. The related factors of self-reported STIs are still unclear and need further assessment. This research aimed to identify the factors associated with self-reported STIs in Indonesia.MethodsA cross-sectional study design. The data was obtained from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), especially the DHS of Indonesia in 2017. The participants were 37,109 sexually active women in Indonesia. The outcome variable was self-reported STIs. Respondents were adults aged 15–49 years old, and the data were analyzed using percentages, chi-squared, and logistic regression. An adjusted OR with a 95% confidence interval was used to identify the strength of association. All tests had alpha levels of 0.05.ResultsSelf-reported STIs were high among women in Indonesia, with an overall prevalence of 14.32% across all regions. Notably, North Maluku, North Sumatera, Riau, North Kalimantan, and East Nusa Tenggara reported over 20%. Factors associated with higher odds of self-reported STIs included younger age, employment, younger age at sexual activity, and ever heard about STI/HIV, and had lower economic status.ConclusionsThis study’s findings highlight demographic, behavioral, and economic factors differences among Indonesian women in regarding the burden of STI, emphasizing the need for targeted prevention and education strategies in high-prevalence regions.Keywords: Self-reported, Sexually transmitted infection, Women, Demographic health survey

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization [34], sexually transmitted infections are becoming more prevalent globally. The WHO estimates that over one million people acquired sexually transmitted infections (STIs) daily, resulting in 374 million new cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis and trichomoniasis in 2020 [34]. Deng et al. [7] reported that STIs surged by 58.38% in 2021 compared to 1990. Low-middle-income countries have a higher prevalence of STIs, such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and trichomoniasis infections [7]. 90% of STIs cases occurred in low- and middle-income countries [33].

The prevalence of STIs in Indonesia remains underreported to this day. In 2023, a total of 52.111 cases of STIs were reported [11],however, this number likely underrepresents the actual prevalence due to underdiagnosis and underreporting. A prior study indicated that Indonesia possesses one of the highest rates of STIs globally [9]. Furthermore, based on previous research, Indonesia is the leading country for having STIs among Southeast Asia (12,3%) [35]. Syphilis is the most prevalent STIs in Indonesia, followed by gonorrhea [12]. In 2019, over 600.000 Indonesians had incurable STIs, with high prevalence among productive age groups [13].

Individuals, partners, and society can all contribute to the risk of STIs [25, 26]. Moreover, STIs transmission is linked to social, economic, and behavioral factors, including ethnic and economic status, access to health care, substance abuse, stigma, and sexual networks [26]. In Indonesia, the transmission of STIs is related to sociodemographic factors, especially low income and living in rural areas [24]. Nevertheless, this study was derived from only one region in Indonesia rather than national data. Importantly, several studies about STIs focus on people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), female sex workers and other high-risk groups [20, 28], leaving a gap in understanding the prevalence and the important factors of self-reported STIs among women in general population,

Women are more vulnerable to STIs than men, particularly between the ages of 15 and 44 years old) [9, 34]. According to Van Gerwen et al. [30], the high prevalence of STIs in women can be attributed to both male-to-female transmission and female anatomy. STIs can negatively affect both women and their newborns [30]. STIs affect health and social life, as well as increasing the risk of poor health in mothers and infants. STIs can negatively impact women’s reproductive health, leading to infertility, cancer, and pregnancy complications [20, 34].

Despite the physical burden, women with STIs face negative stigma. Malek et al. [17] found that the stigma associated with discourages women from seeking care, resulting in delayed treatment. Self-reported STIs data provides valuable information, especially in setting where stigma and inadequate healthcare facility hinder case identification. While self-reported STIs can capture underdiagnosed case, stigma also contribute to underreporting in Indonesia [32].

Therefore, this study aims are to estimate the prevalence of self-reported STIs and identify demographics, behavior, and socioeconomic determinants of self-reported STIs among women in Indonesia using nationally representative data.

Methods

Study design and setting

The Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data are used in this study. The DHS has gathered information from many countries, including Indonesia. The Indonesian data is available from 1987 to 2017; however, this research analyzed the recent data (2017 only). DHS used a cross-sectional study design to collect data. The data is available at https://dhsprogram.com/Data/ and can be freely accessed. Surveys are used to obtain Indonesian DHS data from any province in the country. To represent the population in each province, DHS used weighted samples. There were two methods of sampling in DHS, including s and random selection of households within the primary sampling unit (PSU). The DHS survey has a response rate of 97.5% [29].

Population and sample

The available data in the Indonesian DHS is from individuals, households, women, couples, children, and births. STIs data in women, however, can only be discovered in individual files in this study. Age 15–49 years old women who have ever had sexual intercourse are the inclusion criteria for the sampling. Missing data and people who have never had sexual intercourse are the exclusion criteria. The sample inclusion flowchart is described in Fig. 1. This ethical consideration was confirmed by the DHS organization, as it has previously examined the ethical issue when gathering data.

Study variables

Dependent variable

In this study, the outcome variable self-reported STIs-was defined broadly as participants report STIs through sexual contact in the past 12 months, reporting having a bad-smelling or abnormal genital discharge in the past 12 months, reporting they had a genital sore or ulcer in the past 12 months, and had STI or STIs symptoms in the past 12 months. This question is from question number 741–743 of the Indonesia-DHS questionnaire, where responses of “yes” to these questions were considered did self-reported STIs. Based on DHS guidelines, this data does not reflect the clinical diagnosis of STIs but reflects the participants knowledge of the diseases and the potential for having the disease.

Independent variables

The independent variables included demographics, age, education, marital status, place of residence (rural or urban), and occupation. Behavioral variables related to STIs include age at the first time of sex, recent actual activity, and having heard about STIs and HIV. Sociodemographic factors, including socioeconomic status and media access, were also gathered.

Regarding categorization of independent variables, we primarily used DHS criteria. The wealth index from DHS is classed into five groups (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest); however, for simplicity in analysis, we categorized them into 3 different categories including poor, middle, and rich. We also classified partners into three categories: single (never partnered), married and living with partner (currently partnered), and widowed, divorced, and separated (previously partnered). Media exposure is defined as any exposure to newspaper/magazine, radio, and television at least once a week, categorized as “yes” for exposure and “no” for non-exposure.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Software for Data Science (STATA) version 18. The analysis in this study followed DHS guidelines for sampling weight and complex survey design. The sample weight for individual responses in the women's dataset from DHS was calculated by dividing variable v005 by 1,000,000 to create a normalized weight for descriptive and bivariate analysis. The survey design was performed by svyset [pw = wt], psu(v021) strata(v023) and compromise with unequal probabilities, clustering, and stratification. Moreover, the svy prefix was used to obtain standard errors (SE), confidence intervals (CI), and significance testing. In multivariate logistic regression models, the original weight variable v005 was directly as a probability weight (pweight) in the survey design: svyset [pweight = v005], psu(v021) strata(v023). As well as other analysis, the svy prefix also applied in all regression models to produce weight adjusted odds ratio (AOR), SE and CI.

All variables were evaluated using descriptive analysis, percentages, and frequencies. For bivariate analysis, the chi-squared test was utilized. Logistic regression was employed to examine the important factors of self-reported STIs. All considered variables associated with self-reported STIs were entered into the regression model. Before entering the model, the variance inflation factor (VIF) of the independent variable was assessed, revealing no instances of VIF over10 [21]. The regression model was developed hierarchically, with variables entered in three blocks: demographics in block 1, behavioral factors in block 2, and socioeconomic factors in the final block [3, 5, 22, 31]. The alpha value for all tests is 0.05. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and confidence interval (CI) 95% were utilized to present the regression results. In the analysis of logistic regression, the goodness of fit for each model was evaluated trough likelihood ratio (LR) χ2 test, pseudo R2 statistics, Aike’s Information criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [2], and Pearson χ2. McFadden’s and McKelvey & Zavoina’s were used for the Pseudo R2 values.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or the public in its design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination.

In model 1, the included variables were age, educational background, partner status, occupation, and place of residence. The odds of self-reported STIs were decreasing with increasing age with 18% less likely in age 25–30, 23% less likely in age 31–35, 40% less likely in age 36–40, 42% less likely in age 41–45, and 55% less likely in age 46–49 years old. In model 1, those in secondary and lower education had higher odds of self-reported STIs compared to no education (AOR = 1.44, 95% CI [1.03, 2.01]. Having a job became a significant factor in self-reported STIs compared to unemployed women (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI [1.05, 1.25]). Partner status and place of residence were not associated with self-reported STIs (Table 4).

In model 2, age of first-time sex, sexual activity in last four weeks, and hearing about STIs and HIV were included as behavioral factors. The odds of self-reported STIs are also similar to model 1. Increasing age decreased the odds of self-reported STIs, while those who had work increased the odds. Individuals who engaged in sexual activity within marriage were 23% less likely to self-report STIs.

Those who had heard about STIs/HIV had higher odds of self-reported STIs compared to those who had never heard (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI [1.02, 1.27]. Educational background lost significance, and sexual activity in last four weeks is not associated with self-reported STIs. Marital status and place of residence remained nonsignificant (Table 4).

In model 3, the socioeconomic factor included wealth and media exposure to the regression model. Overall, the result was similar to models 1 and 2. All odds ratios of each variable are similar to those of the first and second models. Increasing age decreased the odds, working increased the odds, first sex at marriage decreasing the odds, and ever hearing about STIs and HIV increased the odds. Those in better economic conditions are 16% less likely to self-report STIs. However, media exposure was not associated with self-reported STIs (Table 4).

Discussion

This study's findings highlighted significant association of self-reported STIs among women. The related factors include sociodemographic, economic, and behavioral factors. After adjusting for all factors, age, occupation, sexual activity, ever heard about STI/HIV, and wealth were the key factors of self-reported STIs.

This study has revealed that in 2017, there were 5,316 (14.32%) cases among 37,109 Indonesian women reporting STIs. This number shows an increasing case compared with the combine DHS data from 2012 and 2017 (12.30%) in Indonesia [35]. This increasing number is correlated with persistent high group, particularly is younger women and had low economic backgrounds [35], as well as low condom used and sexual risky sexual behavior [23]. The five provinces exhibiting the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs in this current study are North Maluku, North Sumatera, North Kalimantan, Riau, and East Nusa Tenggara. When compared to data from Ministry of Health Indonesia in 2017, North Maluku and North Sumatera also two of the highest prevalence of HIV and STI [18]. However, the top provinces HIV prevalence Jakarta and East Java, were not among the top provinces in self-reported STIs [18]. This discrepancy reflects variations in data collection methods, with DHS utilizing self-reported, while official reports rely on confirmed cases. These findings pose a challenge to public health strategies in making targeted interventions. Efforts to increase public awareness, improve access to sexual health services, promote regular STI screenings, and implement health education in schools should be considered for this area. Moreover, the gap between self-reported and confirmed cases can guide surveillance strategies to ensure comprehensive intervention for populations that have limited access to healthcare facilities.

Age was associated with self-reported STIs. Found that lower age showed higher self-reported STIs, particularly in adolescents aged 15–24 years. The global burden also showed the same trend [37]. Many countries faced the same issue, such as in the United States, where the prevalence is higher in age 15–19 [16]. STIs increased in younger age (10–14) from 1990 to 2019 [36]. This finding highlighted early sex health education and prevention strategies should be considered among adolescents. A short- or long-term education by a professional trainer in school was effective in increasing sex health education [4]. For example, school-based sex education program should develop to reach younger population, while community-based initiatives should enhance awareness among this age group through primary health care facilities.

Employment status was associated with higher odds of self-reported STIs, with working women had greater odds compared to non-working woman. The odds persisted after adjusting demographics, sexual behavior, and socioeconomic factors. The occupation is working in a company and a non-company setting. There is no information about sexual workers, as was the case in Bangladesh, where working women also exhibited increased odds of reporting STIs [10]. This finding might be related to the autonomy of socializing and interacting with others in the workplace, increasing the high-risk exposure of STIs [1]. Conversely, working women may also have had higher health literacy due to their greater education, enabling them to recognize symptoms of STIs and utilize healthcare facilities. This dual possibility, higher exposure and greater reporting, highlights the importance of understanding STIs risk in broader gender and occupational groups.

Age at the first-time sex associated with self-reported STIs, with those women who first time had sex in marriage tends to have lower odds. Previous studies have shown that early age in engaging sexual activity increases the odds of having STIs [10, 27]. This study was supported by a previous study that STI was lower among marriage individuals [8]. The explanation for this may be related to married woman being less likely to have multiple partners and showed more stable relationships. This finding suggested that women who never married should receive education regarding the risk of STIs and the importance of using protective measures during premarital sexual activity.

In this study, women who had ever heard about STIs/HIV had higher odds of self-reported STIs compared to those who had never heard. This association reflects two possibilities. First, women with a previous STIs diagnosis, who had visited a healthcare facility, had a higher frequency of hearing about this information. Second, greater awareness of STIs may reflect individuals’ ability to recognize symptoms, seek diagnosis, and report diagnosis [6]. A previous study from Southeast Asia found that greater exposure to STIs and HIV information was associated with increased awareness of self-reported STIs [35]. Similar findings were also reported in different genders,men who had ever heard about STIs or HIV in Africa had higher odds of self-reporting STIs [19]. However, the cross-sectional study design is limited in its ability to provide the directionality of this relationship. Therefore, this finding suggests that STIs targeted education especially for those women with limited cost, facilities, stigma, and limited diagnostic test and resulting in early detection, true diagnosis, and timely treatment.

In this study, people with lower economic status, in the poor wealth category, had higher odds of self-reported STIs. This may reflect greater risk and increased exposure to conditions heightening STIs' vulnerability. Previous research in Haiti also revealed similar findings that poor women often face barriers to accessing health care facilities, increasing their risk for undiagnosed and undertreated STIs [14]. Despite that, economic difficulties lead women to engage in unsafe sexual behavior, resulting in higher STIs [15]. However, regarding self-reports of STIs, women of low economic status may be more likely to access the free STI screening, leading to greater reporting. Thus, higher self-reporting of STIs among poorer women may reflect the combination of greater exposure and healthcare engagement. These findings emphasize the need for intervention for economically vulnerable groups and stigma reduction across socioeconomic levels. It is essential for primary healthcare facilities that focus on STIs to be accessible in low-income setting to effectively reach women from lower economic background. Additionally, a new program from the Indonesian government, titled “Cek Kesehatan Gratis/Free Health Checkup” is currently being implemented. This program focuses on providing general health check-up services that encompass routine STIs screenings—not only for human papillomavirus (HPV), but also for curable STIs such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis. This would support early detection and reduce stigma associated with STIs testing for women.

Strengths and limitations

This study used data from Indonesia, the largest country in Southeast Asia, so that the results can be generalized to similar country characteristics. The findings provide valuable information to local and national policymakers about STIs. This study employed adjusted models to control confounding variables, strengthening the validity of the results. Additionally, variables were derived from reliable and widely used data, ensuring their reliability.

However, the study relied on self-reporting STIs, which can interfere with social stigma and lead to the underestimation of true prevalence of STI. The cross-sectional study design limits causal inference, therefore, the findings considered as association to self-reported STIs rather than causal relationships.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there was a rising prevalence of self-reported STIs in Indonesian women who lived on North Maluku, North Sumatera, and Riau, the younger age group, those who had a job, those with lower incomes, younger age at first sex, and those with prior knowledge about STIs/HIV, highlighting the importance of targeted intervention. Factors such as density, rural areas, low access to public health, and lower awareness contributed to higher risk and reporting of STIs. Giving a sexual education to those in vulnerable groups, women and adolescents, is of high importance. Additionally, the absence of male partner data in this study limited the understanding of partner behavioral and health status on women STIs risk. Future research should incorporate male partner information to provide comprehensive understanding of STIs in women.
