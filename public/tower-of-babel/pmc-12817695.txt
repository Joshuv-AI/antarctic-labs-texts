Introduction

Background

Cognitive disorders in the postoperative period include postoperative delirium (POD) and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) and were first described in association with surgery and general anaesthesia in 1955 by Bradford and colleagues [1].

POD is an acute dysfunction in cognition, which did not exist preoperatively. Attention deficit disorder is the main symptom of POD and refers to the inability to direct, focus, maintain, or shift attention. Memory impairment, disorientation, or perceptual disturbances may also be present. Changes in cognitive capacity in patients with POD develop and fluctuate in the first few postoperative days [2].

Unlike POD, there is no precise definition for POCD. Based on data from the existing literature, it is defined as newly diagnosed cognitive deterioration that occurs after surgery [3]. The diagnosis of POCD should be based on pre- and postoperative screening with appropriate psychometric tests. It is long-lasting, often subtle, and affects several cognitive domains: memory, executive function, attention, verbal fluency, and/or visuospatial performance. Unlike the acute nature of POD, POCD can develop within 7 days to 1 year after surgery. Some patients have only faint symptoms, such as mild, albeit lifelong, memory loss. Compared to more pronounced cognitive deterioration, such as the inability to process information or concentrate, gentle symptoms can be easily overlooked or mistaken for a normal ageing process [4].

The impact of POCD on the quality of recovery and life after surgery is not fully understood.

Risk factors for the development of POCD include those related to the surgical procedure, anaesthesia, or the patient. Compared to less invasive and shorter operations, there is a higher risk of developing POCD after major, invasive, and longer operations [5]. Due to the complexity of lung cancer surgery, its specific anaesthetic techniques, intraoperative complications, and patient factors, occurrence of postoperative cognitive deficits is considered common [6].

Older age is the main risk factor for the occurrence of POCD [7]. Also, older surgical patients have higher prevalence of frailty (up to 50–60%) depending on age and type of surgery [8]. Poor physical status and cognitive impairment clearly overlap, although they are not synonymous. A recent study in cardiac surgery patients found that frailty was associated with postoperative delirium and long-term cognitive dysfunction [9]. On the other hand, preoperative cognitive training, as one fragment of the preoperative optimization, has been shown feasible in older cardiac surgery patients, but with no clear evidence of efficacy [10].

Cognitive deficit after surgery is associated with prolonged recovery and reduced quality of life. Despite numerous studies, postoperative cognitive dysfunction remains poorly defined and insufficiently understood. The pathophysiological mechanism is not known, and there is no definitive perioperative or anaesthesia strategy that will reliably reduce the number of affected patients or the severity of the disorder. Also, effective preventive strategies are not defined yet. The focus is on prehabilitation, a widespread concept that aims to improve functional reserve of the patient and optimize modifiable risk factors before major surgery [11]. It is developing in the direction of a multimodal approach that includes interventions to reduce stress and anxiety. Psychological factors are increasingly recognized as important and are often added to prehabilitation programmes (trimodal or multimodal prehabilitation model) [12].

In a view of these facts, we designed the Effect of Preoperative Cognitive and Physical Optimization in Prevention of Postoperative Cognitive Deficit in Elderly Patients with Lung Resection (COGNITION) Trial. The main objective of this study is to investigate if preoperative cognitive optimization and physical training applied before lung resection surgery in patients older than 60 years may affect the incidence of postoperative cognitive disorders or further deterioration of the existing preoperative cognitive dysfunction. Aditionally, the objective is to investigate if preoperative cognitive optimization and physical exercise may improve the quality of postoperative recovery.

Methods

Objectives and design

This is a prospective, single-centre, clinical, controlled, randomized study taking place at the Clinic for Anesthesiology and Resuscitation of the Military Medical Academy (MMA) (anaesthesia management of patients before, during, and after surgery, intensive care unit), the Clinic for Thoracic Surgery of the MMA (preoperative assessment and patient perioperative management, monitoring the course of recovery), the Central Chemical Laboratory of the MMA (analysis of the blood samples), the Physical training Clinic of the MMA (physical training), and the Neurology Clinic of the MMA (neuropsychological testing of patients).

The study has been approved by the institutional committee reviewing human or animal investigations: Ethical Board of the Military Medical Academy (Eticki odbor Vojnomedicinske akademije, decision number: 79/2023. Date: 13.10.2023).

Ethical Board of the Military Medical Academy will be notified for any important protocol modifications. It is required that any important protocol modifications are subjected to new Ethical Board approval. This is a single-centre study protocol; no further notifications about the new decision (no other investigation centres) are required.

All facilities and equipment for surgical care of the patients already exist in the hospital and are available for the research team and patients included since the Ethical Board and Medical Faculty approved the study.

In total, 120 patients who meet the criteria for inclusion in the study, after signing the informed consent will be randomly assigned to one of two different study groups:

The Intervention group, where patients will receive preoperative cognitive training (CT) and physical training from the inclusion to the study until surgery (up to 1 month before surgical treatment)

We hypothesized that patients older than 60 years of age who have preoperative cognitive optimization and physical training applied before lung resection surgery will have fewer postoperative cognitive disorders, enhanced recovery and improved outcome compared to the standard of care. Also, we expect that preoperative cognitive optimization and physical training in this patient population will prevent further postoperative decline of the already existing cognitive dysfunction.

Interventions, outcomes, and study setting

Patients from the Intervention group will be subjected to psychological testing and preoperative training (exercise) to improve cognitive function. This technique, presurgical cognitive optimization, involves several standardized cognitive stimulation games. The Cognifit application (https://www.cognifit.com/) available on a smartphone or tablet will be used for the study. CogniFit is a mobile- and web-based cognitive training application that offers short, easy-to-learn games designed to enhance various cognitive functions, such as perception, memory, and hand–eye coordination. For the purposes of this study, a structured training programme—comprising a targeted combination of games—was developed by a neurologist to specifically stimulate functions including short-term memory, contextual memory, focus, spatial and visual perception, and working memory.

The games are brief, user-friendly, and visually engaging, making the training experience accessible and enjoyable for participants. An introductory session is provided for individuals in the intervention group, during which the application is installed on the participant’s smartphone or tablet, according to their preference. This session also includes guided practice with the initial training module, as well as the selection of games based on the neurologist’s recommendations.

The patients will exercise at least three times a week for 20 min (from inclusion in the study, up to 1 month before scheduled surgery). Through the Cognifit application for researchers, insight into the patient’s compliance with training will be available. After testing for frailty, mobility, and physical reserve and consultation with a physiatrist, individual preoperative physical training (breathing exercises, walking, climbing stairs) will be prescribed for patients in this group. In addition to the exercise programme to improve physical reserve, education on breathing and physical training aimed at the postoperative period until full mobilization is scheduled. Patients will keep a diary of preoperative activities to be overviewed by researchers.

Patients from the Control group will be tested perioperatively for cognitive function, frailty, and physical reserve as the intervention group, but without cognitive intervention and physical training referred to surgery.

After the thoracic surgeon schedules eligible patients for surgery during the preoperative visit, they refer the patients to an independent researcher, who is responsible for obtaining informed consent, as well as for randomization and allocation to the study groups. Informed consent documents provide a comprehensive overview of the study, including information on the study protocol, the data collected, the tests performed, and the laboratory parameters monitored, and ensure that the participants (or authorized surrogate) fully understand the study’s risks, benefits, rights, and responsibilities.

Patients are randomized using a simple randomization method, implemented through a computer programme. A list of numbers from 1 to 120 is randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. This allocation list is concealed and securely held by a doctor who is not involved in any further steps of the study protocol. As each patient is enrolled, they are assigned a sequential number based on the order of inclusion. After informed consent is obtained, the independent researcher contacts the doctor holding the allocation list by phone to reveal the group assignment corresponding to the patient’s sequential number. It is important to note that while the independent researcher performs the randomization, they do not have access to the allocation list.

The process of assigning individuals to their respective groups is kept confidential and is known only to an independent researcher who is allocating patients to the study groups. As a result, the thoracic surgeon, anaesthesiologist, neurologist, and physiatrist involved in patient care are not given access to the randomization list. This ensures that the research study is conducted in a fair and objective manner, without potential bias or influence from the medical professionals involved in surgical treatment.

The intervention in this study is a non-invasive method based on structured games of the Cognifit application on the patient’s smart phone or tablet. The intervention can only be interrupted if the patient’s condition deteriorates due to another concomitant disease, the patient voluntarily withdraws participation or decide not to fulfil the prescribed programme.

In terms of cognitive stimulation exercises and physical training interventions, there has been no documented evidence of any adverse effects on patients who engage in these activities as a part of their perioperative care. These interventions are generally considered to be safe and beneficial for patients recovering from surgery, as they can help to improve cognitive function, physical mobility, and overall quality of life. Overall, the implementation of these interventions as part of a comprehensive perioperative care plan can help to improve patient outcomes and facilitate a smoother recovery process. On the other hand, the non-invasiveness of the intervention makes it simple to adherence.

We do not anticipate any requirements for unblinding. The design of the study is open label with only outcome assessors being blinded so any need for unblinding has not been anticipated. Given the low-risk nature of the intervention and the absence of anticipated adverse effects, no interim analyses or formal stopping rules have been included in the study protocol. No Data Monitoring Committee has been considered for auditing the trial. Each of the clinics have a senior investigator that is responsible for overviewing the study advancement. Meetings are not firmly planned but scheduled if needed.

Perioperative patient management

Aside from intervention, which is mostly homebased and completed preoperatively by the patients in the intervention group, all patients have the same intraoperative and postoperative treatment protocol. Standard anaesthesia protocol in our hospital includes standard monitoring, monitoring of anaesthesia depth, invasive blood pressure monitoring, and antibiotic prophylaxis 30 min before induction. After intravenous induction (midazolam, fentanyl, propofol, rocuronium), double lumen tube or bronchial blocker (if difficult intubation is anticipated) is placed. For the study, Sevoflurane will be used for maintenance. Analgesia protocol includes intercostal block and sparing opioid use, non-steroid anti-inflammatory, and non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol or metamizole). During the operation, all hypoxia episodes defined as SpO2 < 88% will be evidenced (incidence, duration in minutes, and management), as well as one lung ventilation duration (minutes), hypotension (incidence, duration in minutes, treatment), blood loss, and intraoperative blood management.

Standard laboratory analysis will be performed before, after surgery, before discharge, and as indicated (full blood account, BUN, creatinine, electrolytes, CRP, procalcitonin, protein, albumin, and transaminase).

Given that cognitive deficits are common in patients over 60 years of age and after prolonged surgeries, along with the frequent occurrence of hypoxia and hypotension in thoracic surgery, anaesthesia is administered by an anaesthesiologist who is blinded to the patient’s group allocation and provides care as per standard clinical practice, without any modification based on the patient’s assigned group.

Following the operation, patients are transferred to the intensive care unit and monitored for standard laboratory parameters, such as numerical pain scale, diuresis, and haemostasis. The patients remain in the intensive care unit (ICU) until the next morning and are transferred to the ward after meeting a usual discharge criterion. On the ward, until discharge, all patients are subjected to the usual hospital standard of care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for this study is defined as follows:

Postoperative cognitive dysfunction

Incidence of newly diagnosed or worsening of the existing postoperative cognitive decline assessed by change in score of MMSE – Mini-Mental State Examination (an 11-question measure that tests five areas of cognitive function: orientation, registration, attention, and calculation, recall, and language). On a scale from 0 to 30 points: ≥ 25 points normal cognitive function 19–24 points minor cognitive dysfunction, 10–18 points medium cognitive dysfunction and ≤ 9 points major cognitive dysfunction. In this study, a decrease of 3 or more points in MMSE scores between preoperative and postoperative assessments will be considered a clinically significant decline, indicating worsening cognitive function in an individual patient. This threshold is based on established criteria used in previous cognitive research to identify meaningful changes in cognitive status. To evaluate the effect of the preoperative cognitive optimization intervention, the change in MMSE scores from baseline (preoperative) to postoperative assessment will be compared between the intervention and control groups. This comparison will allow us to determine whether the intervention reduces the incidence or severity of postoperative cognitive decline in the study population.

Time frame for the assessment is from 1 month preoperatively (scheduling patient for surgery) until 30 th postoperative day.

The secondary outcome measures for this study are defined as follows:

(1) Perioperative depression and anxiety.

Perioperative assessment of depression, anxiety, and stress: DASS—depression, anxiety and stress scale (42-item self-report scale measures the negative emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress). Scoring DASS: normal 0–9 0–7 0–14, mild 10–13 8–9 15–18, moderate 14–20 10–14 19–25, severe 21–27 15–19 26–33, and extremely severe 28 + 20 + 34 +.

Results are assessed between the intervention group and control, from 1 month preoperatively (scheduling patient for surgery) until 30 th postoperative day.

(2) Postoperative delirium.

Incidence of newly diagnosed postoperative delirium: CAM-ICU (Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU) using CAM-ICU Flowsheet:

Step 1. Acute change or fluctuating course of mental status: yes (delirium positive) or no (CAM-ICU negative).

Step 2. Inattention: 0–2 errors (CAM-ICU negative) or > 2 errors go to next step.

Step 3. Altered level of consciousness (the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale—RASS): from − 5 (unarousable—no response to voice or physical stimulation) to + 4 (combative). Anything other than 0 (alert and calm) goes to the next step.

Step 4. Disorganized thinking: 0–1 errors (not delirious) or > 1 error (delirious). Feature 1 plus 2 and either 3 or 4 present = CAM-ICU positive.

Patients will be assessed for the postoperative delirium on the first postoperative day.

(3) Functional status and postoperative recovery.

Assessment of the functional status and speed of recovery—Clinical Frailty Scale: from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill).

Only patients marked as less than 6 are included in the study.

Any change in the assessment score, from 1 month preoperatively (scheduling patient for surgery) until 30 th postoperative day is marked as significant.

(4) Functional status and postoperative recovery.

Assessment of the functional status and speed of recovery—the New Mobility Score is a composite score of the patient’s ability to perform:

• Indoor walking,

• Outdoor walking, and.

• Shopping,

Providing a score between 0 and 3 (0: not at all, 1: with a help from another person, 2: with an aid, 3: no difficulty)—for each function. The total score can be from 0 to 9, with 9 indicating a high functional level.

Time frame for the assessment is from 1 month preoperatively (scheduling patient for surgery) until 30 th postoperative day.

(5) Postoperative recovery.

Assessment of the speed of recovery—A test of functional recovery: 10 items for assessing basic activities by a six-level ordinal scale, from 0 (activity not achieved) to 5 (fully independent and secure). Total scores can range from 0 to 50 (from inability to perform any activity to complete independence.) Speed of the recovery will be assessed with a comparison of the scores during the first 5 postoperative days.

(6) Functional status and postoperative recovery.

Assessment of the functional status and speed of recovery—Timed Up and Go test is performed by measuring the time for the following sequence of actions:

1. Stand up from the chair.

2. Walk to the line on the floor at a normal pace.

3. Turn.

4. Walk back to the chair at a normal pace.

5. Sit down again.

Mobility is assessed based on time to complete the test:

< 10 s = normal; < 20 s = good mobility, can walk outside alone; does not require a walking aid; < 30 s = walking and balance problems, cannot walk outside alone, requires walking aid.

Patients will be assessed within the time frame of 1 month preoperatively (scheduling patient for surgery) until 30 th postoperative day.

(7) Overall outcome: Complication rate.

Complication rate will be presented as % of patients within the groups who develop until 30 th postoperative day:

• Respiratory failure.

• Pneumonia.

• Atelectasis.

• Bronchospasm.

• Bronchopleural fistula.

• Air leak.

• Pulmonary oedema.

(8) Overall outcome: Morbidity.

Morbidity will be presented as % of patients within the groups who develop any kind of complication or illness postoperatively during the period of follow-up (30 th postoperative day).

(9) Overall outcome: Mortality.

Mortality will be presented as overall and % of deaths in the study population and both groups until 30 th postoperative day.

(1) Measurement of cognitive functions and weakness syndrome.

Assessment of cognitive functions will be carried out in the first group immediately after randomization, then in all patients the day before the operation, the day after the operation and on the day of discharge from the hospital. The assessment will be carried out by trained doctors involved in the research. The battery of neuropsychological tests will include the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination), DASS (depression, anxiety and stress scale), confusion assessment method (CAM), and several physiatry tests: Clinical Frailty Scale, New Mobility Score, A test, Timed Up and Go test.

One limitation of the study is that only the intervention group’s preoperative activities are monitored via the app and diary, while the control group’s activities are not assessed. As a result, control participants who independently engage in cognitive or physical training may create an unrecognized cross-over effect. However, since the intervention involves a structured, neurologist-guided programme unlikely to be replicated informally, the potential impact of this overlap is considered minimal.

Sample size and statistical analyses

The main objective of this study is to investigate whether patients over 60 years of age who receive preoperative cognitive optimization and physical training before undergoing lung resection surgery experience fewer postoperative cognitive disorders. We hypothesize that maintaining or improving cognitive function preoperatively will enhance postoperative recovery and lead to better outcomes compared to standard care. Additionally, we expect that this intervention will help prevent further postoperative decline in patients who already present with cognitive impairment. Based on this hypothesis, the sample size was calculated to detect a significant change in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores.

The sample size was calculated to detect a statistically significant difference in cognitive function, measured by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score, between the intervention and control groups. The calculation was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.2, selecting a two-tailed independent samples t-test. The analysis assumed a Type I error (α) of 0.05 and a power (1 − β) of 0.80.

Based on data published by Saleh et al. [13], where the mean MMSE scores were 28.9 ± 1.5 and 28.1 ± 1.5 in comparable groups, the effect size (Cohen’s d) was estimated at 0.53. With these parameters, the calculated total sample size was 114 participants, requiring 57 subjects per group to detect a statistically significant difference.

Descriptive statistical methods, statistical hypothesis testing methods, association analysis methods, and methods for modelling the relationship of outcomes and potential predictors will be used: t-test, Mann–Whitney test, chi-square test, Fisher’s exact probability test, analysis of variance and Kruskal–Wallis test, Pearson’s linear correlation coefficient and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, and linear and logistic regression.

All data will be analysed as intention to treat, and the results will be presented as both absolute and relative percentages of outcome change.

Discussion

Postoperative cognitive deficit represents a risk of prolonged postoperative recovery and reduced quality of life, and it is assumed that early recognition and management of possible perioperative risk factors could prevent it. It is expected that preventive strategies of postoperative deterioration of cognitive function will include a multidisciplinary team, close cooperation between surgeons, anaesthesiologists, physiatrists, and psychologists who will address and manage patient’s individual needs.

The association between frailty and increased postoperative mortality is often mentioned in the literature [14]. Frailty, a state of reduced functional reserve usually described as a deficit in physical function as well as in nutrition, cognition, and mental health is often associated with the geriatric population [15]. These individuals, who are inherently vulnerable to stressors, face an increased risk of negative outcomes and require greater utilization of health care resources when exposed to the stress of surgery [16]. One study found that frailty was associated with longer hospitalizations, more nonroutine discharges, and higher total hospital costs after spine surgery [17]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests a strong association between clinical frailty and POD. In general, POD is a relatively common complication in frail patient populations, but one with serious consequences.

Cognitive training (CT) refers to patient training programmes that involve performing specific cognitive tasks (playing video games, reading books, practising writing, remembering spatial locations, remembering objects or words, and greater communication with the patient) to improve performance in one or more cognitive domains, such as memory, attention, or executive function [18–20]. Many studies have shown that CT can improve cognitive functions [21, 22]. However, the effects of CT on POCD and POD are controversial. Saleh et al. [13] showed that preoperative CT significantly reduces the incidence of POCD after gastrointestinal surgery. However, other studies found no significant difference in the incidence of POCD and POD between patients receiving perioperative CT and controls, and CT had limitations in terms of feasibility and patient compliance [23, 24].

Altogether, it remains unknown whether implementing preoperative strategies that include cognitive training and/or physical exercise may affect postoperative outcomes in the population at risk for prolonged recovery and decreased quality of life.

Prehabilitation is a process of enhancing physiological, nutritional, and psychological resilience to surgery and cancer therapy [25]. The main objective is to increase functional capacity and empower patients to reduce postoperative morbidity and improve short- and long-term recovery. It can be acknowledged as a part of preoperative optimization and assessment of the modifiable risk factors as a part of the Enhance Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols (26). It is still unknown what part of multimodal prehabilitation could be dedicated to cognitive optimization. Additionally, it is unknown if addressing cognitive function as a part of preoperative optimization can affect physical recovery after surgery. Altogether, data are scarce regarding geriatric population in thoracic surgery.

The COGNITION trial is a unique trial that will investigate the effects of cognitive and physical preoperative training in elderly patients scheduled for lung surgery. It can provide more data in clarifying efficient interventions for ERAS protocols in elderly patients.
