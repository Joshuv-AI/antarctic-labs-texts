Gender-specific relationships between hyperuricemia and idiopathic deep venous thrombosis in the Chinese population: a case‒control study
Authors: Xiaoyu Cheng, Fei Yan, Xue Xiaomei, Qin He, Tian Liu, Lidan Ma, Ming Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany studies have indicated that hyperuricemia is positively correlated with secondary deep venous thrombosis (DVT); however, the risk factors for idiopathic DVT based on gender differences, such as serum uric acid (SUA) and hyperuricemia, have not been fully examined.ObjectivesTo investigate the association between hyperuricemia and the occurrence of idiopathic lower extremity DVT based on gender differences.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of 4299 patients who were hospitalized at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University from January 2012 to October 2021 and who underwent ultrasound of the lower limbs. A total of 930 patients were diagnosed in the DVT group, and 3369 patients were diagnosed in the control group without DVT. The baseline SUA and other important baseline data were compared between the two groups, and sex was stratified. Multivariate logistic regression analysis models adjusted for potential confounders were used to investigate the associations between hyperuricemia and idiopathic lower extremity DVT.ResultsThe SUA level in patients with idiopathic DVT was significantly greater than that in patients without DVT (total: 6.00 ± 1.75 vs. 5.40 ± 1.56 mg/dL, respectively; male: 6.42 ± 1.60 vs. 5.87 ± 1.57 mg/dL, respectively; female: 5.58 ± 1.79 vs. 4.72 ± 1.27 mg/dL, respectively; all P < 0.001). The proportion of patients with hyperuricemia in the idiopathic DVT group was significantly greater than that in the control group (total: 29.03% vs. 16.10%, respectively; male: 35.26% vs. 23.19%, respectively; female: 22.73% vs. 5.74%, respectively; all P < 0.001). The incidence of DVT in patients with hyperuricemia was significantly greater than patients with normouricemia (33.29% vs. 18.92%, respectively), and this difference was particularly prominent among women (58.01%). According to the univariate model, hyperuricemia was significantly associated with a grester risk of idiopathic DVT. After adjustment for potential confounders, this association remained significant. The risk of idiopathic lower extremity DVT in patients with hyperuricemia was 2.643-fold greater than that in patients with normouricemia (Model 3: OR: 2.643, 95% CI: 2.165–3.228). After stratification by sex, the risk of idiopathic lower extremity DVT in female patients with hyperuricemia was 7.482-fold greater than that in patients with normouricemia (Model 3, OR: 7.482, 95% CI: 4.999–11.199).ConclusionIn the Chinese population, hyperuricemia is closely related to an increased risk of idiopathic lower extremity DVT, especially in female patients.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), is a common complication of surgery and internal medicine in critically ill patients and is one of the main causes of disability and death [1, 2]. The latest statistical results showed that approximately 3 and 6 million people in the United States experience DVT every year, with an incidence rate of approximately 1 to 2 in 1000 people. The global impact exceeds 10 million people [3, 4], and the death toll is as high as 100,000 [5]. One study showed a mortality risk of up to 12% for 8 years [6]. The actual incidence and related mortality rate of DVT are much greater. The diagnosis and treatment of VTE have significantly improved recently, however VTE-related deaths have become the third leading cause of cardiovascular disease death, which is second only to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and stroke [3, 7]. However the onset of idiopathic DVT can be subtle and difficult to detect. Patients with idiopathic DVT account for more than 30% of the total number of DVT events [8]. Compared with patients in the control group, patients with idiopathic DVT had a 2.23-fold increase in mortality [2], a greater recurrence rate after treatment than patients with secondary DVT [9, 10], and a greater incidence of arterial cardiovascular events [11]. Currently, considerable attention has been given to the prevention of DVT in clinical practice for patients undergoing major surgeries, those with cancer, and those with severe cases. However, there is a lack of awareness and vigilance regarding idiopathic DVT, and effective prevention of the disorder, as well as prevention of further recurrence have not been performed. Therefore, it is crucial for identify the risk factors for idiopathic DVT and to screen for high-risk populations, which is conducive to individualized treatment to effectively prevent and hinder recurrence.

Serum uric acid (SUA) is the final product of nucleic acid breakdown in purine metabolism. Dysfunction of purine metabolism leads to an increase of SUA and even hyperuricemia. In recent years, the incidence rate of hyperuricemia has increased annually, and hyperuricemia has also gradually become a social problem of increasing concern [12, 13]. An increasing number of studies have confirmed that SUA is a prooxidant that is closely related to metabolic syndrome and CVD [14, 15], and is even closely related to many thrombotic diseases [16]. Moreover, in DeLucchi’s study, it found that hyperuricemia was independently associated with an increased risk of DVT recurrence [17]. At present, there are many clinical articles reporting of the relationship between hyperuricemia and secondary DVT; however there are almost no reports on hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT. Only one article has mentioned that SUA was closely related to the increased risk of idiopathic VTE in a high high -density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) population, whereas the low HDL-C group did not demonstrate this correlation [18]. This article also did not further analyse gender differences. Nevertheless, the SUA is often greater in male subjects than in female subjects. The sex distribution of SUA is attributed to the effect of oestrogen [19], and oestrogen itself has an impact on thrombosis; therefore, it is important to conduct clinical research on the relationship between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT based on sex differences. Therefore, in order to address this issue, we conducted a retrospective case‒control study to evaluate the gender-specific correlation between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted via retrospective collection of the clinical medical records of patients who visited the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University from January 2012 to May 2021. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committees of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University. According to the regulation of not recording personal identifiers, the data in the medical records were approved for use only for retrospective study analysis. Therefore, no personal consent was obtained.

We retrospectively included 16687 patients with aged 18 to 80 years who underwent dual lower limb vascular ultrasound, had complete clinical baseline data, and had two SUA measurements with an interval of more than six months and less than one year. The study exclusion criteria were as follows: previous history of thrombosis or hemorrhagic disease; nutritional risk (serum albumin < 30 g/L); previous or current tumour disease; immobilization; organ failure; severe liver dysfunction (including acute and chronic viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, primary cholestasis, and cirrhosis); chronic kidney diseases or renal impairment with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; chronic heart disease (including malignant arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy, heart valve disease, congenital heart disease, heart failure, aortic coarctation, and any other heart disease that affects cardiac function in class III [NYHA] or above) and chronic lung disease (including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, bronchial asthma, interstitial pneumonia, and pulmonary tuberculosis); gastrointestinal bleeding; had undergone surgery, or experienced trauma or fractures in the past six months; pregnancy or delivery; inflammatory and febrile disease; acute onset of gouty arthritis; acute myocardial infarction and atrial fibrillation; blood system diseases; use of drugs that affect coagulation, such as aspirin, clopidogrel, indobufen, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), low-molecular-weight heparin, vitamin K1, phenol sulfonyl ethylamine, tranexamic acid, thrombin; use of contraceptives, oestrogen or diuretics; hospitalization exceeding 15 days in length or prolonged bed rest; autoimmune disease; endocrine crise (pituitary crisis, thyrotoxic crisis, myxedema coma, adrenal crisis, hypertensive crisis, hypercalcemic crisis. Diabetic ketoacidosis (hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state hypoglycemia; stress states; using hormones and immunosuppressants; transsexualism or mental disorders; or known hypercoagulable diseases (including protein C, protein S, and antithrombin deficiency, coagulation factor V and factor II mutations). Lower extremity DVT was confirmed and diagnosed by at least two ultrasonic physicians through colour doppler ultrasonography. Idiopathic DVT was defined as the absence of the risk factors of secondary DVT mentioned above.

In total, 4299 patients were included in this study. There were 930 individuals (468 males and 462 females) in the idiopathic DVT group and 3369 patients (1992 males and 1377 females) in the control group according to the presence or absence of newly diagnosed lower extremity DVT (Fig. 1).

Clinical data collection

This was a retrospective collection of inpatient medical records from electronic medical records. All of the records were collected by trained investigators who were previously certified by a senior investigator. The required records included age, sex, height, weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), medical history (smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipemia, gouty arthritis, CVD, current uses of antihypertensive, antidiabetic and antilipidemic drugs, uses of uric acid lowering drugs and anticoagulant drugs, and other diseases that patients had or were currently suffering from. Some laboratory data, such as SUA, total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), and D-dimer, were also retrieved from the medical records. The eGFR was calculated by using the creatinine-based Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Association (CKD-EPI) formula [20]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Hypertension was based on the 2020 International Society of Hypertension Global Hypertension Practice Guidelines [21], any self-reported history of hypertension, or use of antihypertensive medication in the past 2 weeks. Diabetes mellitus was defined according to the 2021 World Health Organization diabetes diagnostic criteria [22], or any self-reported history of diabetes mellitus, or use of antidiabetic medication in the past 2 weeks. The diagnostic criteria for hyperlipidemia were based on the 2017 American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists and American College of Endocrinology Guidelines for Management of Dyslipidemia [23], or any self-reported history of hyperlipidemia, or the use of antilipid medication in the past 2 weeks. Hyperuricemia was defined as SUA ≥ 7 mg/dL recorded twice [24] or any self-reported history hyperuricemia. The definition of gouty arthritis was based on the 2015 ACR/EULAR classification diagnostic criteria [25]. CVD referred to a history of coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, peripheral artery disease, or cerebrovascular disease that was currently in a stable phase. Smoking status and drinking status were classified as current or none, according to self-reported information.

Laboratory indicator measurements

Plasma samples were obtained from the antecubital vein in the morning after an overnight fast for more than 8 h on the first day of admission. The SUA (uric acid enzyme method), ALT, AST, BUN, TC, TG, LDL-C, FPG and creatinine parameters were tested by using a Roche cobas 8000 automatic biochemical immunoassay analyser (Roche, Beijing, China); The PT, APTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer variables were tested using a STAR Max fully automatic coagulation analyser (STAGO, Shanghai, China). SBP and DBP were measured in the patient’s right radial artery 3 times with the participants in the seated position by using a mercury sphygmomanometer after 10 min of rest. Afterwards, the average of the 3 readings was recorded.

Statistical analysis

All of the data analyses were performed by using SPSS software version 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). When considering the large gender difference in the distribution of SUA, the results were separately analysed. Continuous data were presented (expressed or described) as the mean ± standard deviation (normal distribution) and were compared by using Student’s t test. If variables were not normally distributed, data were expressed as the median with interquartile range, and the Mann-Whitney U test was performed for comparison. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies or percentages and were compared by using the chi-square test. Crude and adjusted logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the associations between hyperuricemia and SUA quartiles and the risk of idiopathic DVT by calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The potential confounders were adjusted gradually in three models. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex at baseline. Model 2 was further adjusted for current smoking status, current alcohol consumption status, BMI and eGFR. When considering that many chronic diseases are related to the development of DVT, we further adjusted for hypertension, CVD, hyperlipidemia, and gouty arthritis in the Model 3.

To explore whether the ORs differed between patients with different baseline characteristics, subgroup analyses were performed by stratified by age (< 50 vs. ≥50 years), current smoking status (yes vs. no), hypertension status (yes vs. no), diabetes status (yes vs. no), hyperlipidemia status (yes vs. no), CVD status (yes vs. no) and BMI (< 24 vs. ≥24 kg/m2) on male and female patients. All of the interactions were analysed by using logistic regression analysis.

All of the reported probability values were 2-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 4299 patients were enrolled in this study (median age 58 years, 2460 men, 57.22%). We classified participants into an idiopathic DVT group (n = 930) and a control group (n = 3369). The baseline characteristics were summarized in Table 1 and were further categorized according to sex in Table 2. The average SUA in the patients with idiopathic DVT was significantly greater than that in patients without DVT (total: 6.00 ± 1.75 vs. 5.40 ± 1.56 mg/dL, respectively; male: 6.42 ± 1.60 vs. 5.87 ± 1.57 mg/dL, respectively; female: 5.58 ± 1.79 vs. 4.72 ± 1.27 mg/dL, respectively; all P < 0.001). The proportion of patients with hyperuricemia in the DVT group was significantly greater than that in the control group (total: 29.03% vs. 16.10%, respectively; male: 35.26% vs. 23.19%, respectively; female: 22.73% vs. 5.74%, respectively; all P < 0.001). In addition, age; TG, fibrinogen and D-dimer levels; and the incidence of hypertension, CVD, and hyperlipidemia were greater in the DVT group than those in the control group (P < 0.05), and there were no gender differences. The BMI and eGFR of both the male and female patients in the idiopathic DVT group were significantly lower than those in the control group (P < 0.05). The proportions of female patients with idiopathic DVT who smoked, drank alcohol, and used antilipidemic drugs were significantly greater than those in the control group (P < 0.05); however, there was no such difference observed in males. The proportions of patients with gouty arthritis and who used uric acid lowering drugs in the male idiopathic DVT group were significantly greater than those in the control group (P < 0.05); however, there was no such difference observed in females. There were no statistically significant differences in SBP, DBP, FBG, TC, LDL-C, the incidence of diabetes, or the use rate of antihypertensive drugs or antidiabetic drugs between male and female patients (P > 0.05).

Comparison of the incidence of idiopathic DVT events by sex

We compared the incidence of idiopathic DVT events in subjects with hyperuricemia to that in subjects with normouricemia (Fig. 2A) and found that hyperuricemic subjects had a significantly greater incidence of DVT events than did the normouricemic subjects (33.29% vs. 18.92%, respectively; P < 0.001). We also compared the incidences of idiopathic DVT events between hyperuricemic and normouricemic patients according to sex. The proportion of female hyperuricemic patients with idiopathic DVT was as high as 58.01%, whereas it was 26.31% in male hyperuricemic patients and 21.57% in female normouricemic patients. Similarly, in this study, there were sex differences in terms of idiopathic DVT incidence based on different quartiles of SUA. We calculated the incidences of idiopathic DVT events according to sex for the quartile ranges of SUA, including < 4.35, 4.35–5.32, 5.32–6.45, and ≥ 6.45 mg/dL for all subjects; <4.84, 4.84–5.80, 5.80-7.00 and ≥ 7.00 mg/dL for all of the male subjects; and < 3.88, 3.88–4.73, 4.73–5.65, and ≥ 5.65 mg/dL for all of the female subjects (Fig. 2B, C and D, respectively). The incidences of DVT events for the above mentioned quartile ranges were 15.9%, 17.6%, 21.9%, and 31.1%, respectively, in the total sample; 11.7%, 16.9%, 21.0%, and 26.3%, respectively, in the male group; and 17.8%, 19.9%, 21.1%, and 40.3%, respectively, in the female group. The results showed that individuals with higher SUA levels had a greater incidence of idiopathic DVT events in both sexes.

Associations between hyperuricemia and quartiles of SUA and the risk of idiopathic DVT events

Table 3 summarized the odds ratios (ORs) of hyperuricemia and quartiles of SUA and idiopathic DVT events. This clear association between hyperuricemia and DVT was observed via logistic regression analysis with the nonadjusted model. Hyperuricemia was a risk factor for idiopathic DVT events in this study population (OR: 2.138, 95% CI: 1.806–2.532, P < 0.001). After adjusting for potential confounders, this association remained significant. The risk of idiopathic DVT events in patients with hyperuricemia was 2.643-fold greater than that in patients with normouricemia (Model 3, OR: 2.643, 95% CI: 2.165–3.228). After stratification by sex, hyperuricemia was associated with idiopathic DVT events in both male and female subjects; however, the effect was much stronger in the female subjects. The risk of idiopathic DVT events in female patients with hyperuricemia was 7.482-fold greater than that in patients with normouricemia (Model 3, OR: 7.482, 95% CI: 4.999–11.199). According to the crude analysis, the OR and 95% CI of the high quartile were 2.693 (1.989–3.648) and 3.128 (2.307–4.241) in men and women, respectively, compared to those of the low quartile. After adjustment for clinical covariates, the association remained independent in men (OR: 2.742, 95% CI: 1.942–3.872) and in women (OR: 3.504, 95% CI: 2.448–5.015).

Subgroup analyses

We also examined the effect of hyperuricemia on the risk of idiopathic DVT events in different subgroups. There was a significant interaction effect between hyperuricemia and sex (P value for interaction: <0.001) for idiopathic DVT; therefore, we conducted subgroup analysis by sex. The P values for tests of the 2-way interaction effects of hyperuricemia by age, BMI, smoking status, hypertension status, diabetes status, hyperlipidemia status and CVD status on DVT were all > 0.05 for both male and female participants, thus indicating that there was no significant effect on the association between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT. The results in these different subgroups were consistent with the overall results, which showed that hyperuricemia increased the risk of idiopathic DVT, especially among female patients (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This is the first study to demonstrate an association between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT based on gender differences in a Chinese population. After adjusting for potential confounders, hyperuricemia increased the risk of idiopathic lower extremity DVT. This association is more prominent among women. Additionally, these data clearly indicate that the association between SUA and the risk of idiopathic DVT events remains significant even for SUA values lower than those used in clinical practice for defining hyperuricemia.

Our findings were in conjunction with those of previous studies on high SUA levels and VTE. Kubota Y reported for the first time of the association between high SUA levels and an increased risk of VTE, as high SUA levels may increase the risk of VTE in the general population [26]. One study demonstrated an independent correlation between elevated SUA levels and an increased risk of DVT recurrence [17]. Another study indicated that SUA served as an independent predictor of short-term mortality in patients with pulmonary embolism and can predict the risk of VTE recurrence [27]. These studies confirmed the association between SUA and VTE/DVT. With increasing SUA, the risk of DVT increases. In patients with hyperuricemia, the risk of DVT is greater. Therefore, hyperuricemia is expected to become a potential risk factor for preventing the occurrence of idiopathic DVT.

Although the mechanisms of the plausible link between increased SUA levels and DVT are not completely understood, several theories have been postulated. (I) Increased SUA can lead to vascular endothelium damage (ED), which is one of the initial triggering factors involved in coagulation. As the concentration of SUA increases, it can be gradually absorbed into endothelial cells via urate transporters (GLUT9/URATv1), thus causing oxidative stress, inflammation, and dephosphorylation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, which leads to ED. In the context of hypercoagulability, endothelial injury may promote venous thrombosis [14, 28–30]. This may represent the dominant mechanism. (II) Inflammatory states are significant risk factors for and thrombus formation in the venous system [31]. High SUA can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome through oxidative stress, as well as promote the release of inflammatory factors, such as neutrophils, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1b (IL-1b), and tumour necrosis factor-a (TNF-a) [32, 33], thus promoting the formation of blood clots [34]. Uric acid should be considered as a link between proinflammatory and prothrombotic states in patients. (III) Increased SUA that exceeds the saturation level can crystallize into monosodium urate crystals, which can trigger the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [35] and a series of inflammatory responses [36, 37]. In animal models, the occurrence of NETosis has been linked to thrombotic events, including VTE [38]. (IV) Elevated SUA can promote platelet activation, increase adhesion and upregulate the expression of platelet-derived growth factor and monocyte chemotactic factor [39, 40]. (V) Increased SUA can induce phosphatidylserine exposure and microparticle release on RBCs in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, which consequently enhances the procoagulant activity of RBCs [41]. The specific mechanisms of these effects still need to be further verified through functional experiments.

There is controversy concerning the incidence of VTE in men and women [42–44]. A systematic review demonstrated that females were more likely to present with DVT than males [45]. This gender difference was more pronounced for idiopathic VTE and was not related to age [46]. Therefore, the evaluation of gender differences in the impact of hyperuricemia on DVT is highly important. This study is the first to demonstrate the role of gender differences in the correlation between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT. The proportion of female hyperuricemic patients with idiopathic DVT was as high as 58.01%, whereas it was 26.31% in male hyperuricemic patients and 21.57% in female normouricemic patients. After fully adjusting for potential confounders, patients with hyperuricemia had a 7.48-fold greater risk of idiopathic lower extremity DVT than did normouricemic patients. In short, the impact of hyperuricemia on DVT is particularly prominent in women. The possible reason for this difference is not only the effect of oestrogen on thrombosis and SUA [47], but also the significant correlation between fibrinogen and female SUA [48]. The following mechanisms may explain these results. (I) Sex chromosomes play an important role in vascular damage [49].Therefore, it is speculated that the X chromosome may also play an indelible role in the formation and development of venous thrombosis. (II) Women have a greater platelet count and receptor reactivity than men do [50, 51], which is linked to increased adhesiveness to the endothelium and increased aggregability. (III) Female blood vessels, including blood arteries and veins, tend to be smaller in diameter than male blood vessels. The smaller vessel size in women may also contribute to difficulties in revascularization [52]. Blood thrombosis and embolism are more likely to occur. Women also experience more lesions in smaller vessels and multilevel disease [53]. (IV) In the animal experiments, the ischemic limbs of female mice exhibited reduced endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) protein expression and reduced blood perfusion to the lower limbs [54]. (V) Women also have increased levels of CRP, fibrinogen and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), which are more likely to present with thrombosis [55, 56]. Nevertheless, the specific underlying mechanisms are currently unclear and are worthy of further in-depth study.

Limitations

Our study had the advantages of a large sample size of idiopathic DVT patients as well as gender stratification. Nonetheless, some limitations need to be addressed. First, this was a retrospective single-center case-control study that did not consider the changes in SUA over time in individuals or its subsequent effects; thus, we cannot conclude that there was a causal relationship between hyperuricemia and the occurrence of idiopathic DVT. However, hyperuricemia was significantly related to an increase in the risk of idiopathic DVT. A large prospective multicenter cohort study is still needed to further understand the relationship between hyperuricemia and idiopathic DVT. Furthermore, there was a certain bias in the selection of data, including many patients with chronic diseases rather than completely healthy individuals. For example, the FBG level and the incidence of diabetes in the enrolled subjects were high. However, it had little impact on the statistical analysis. Third, the participants in our study included premenopausal and postmenopausal women, which may also have affected the results. Finally, cancer patients were excluded from the study, but not all patients underwent tumour marker screening, and we did not fully investigate the presence of occult malignant tumours. Therefore, we may have misclassified some patients with secondary DVT as idiopathic DVT patients; however, the impact on our results may be minimal.

In conclusion, this study showed that the hyperuricemia could increase the risk of idiopathic DVT, which was more obvious in female patients. Therefore, patients with hyperuricemia, especially female patients, should be alert to the possibility of idiopathic DVT and attention should be given to regular examinations via lower limb vascular ultrasound. In the future, a more in-depth and large sample cohort study on uric acid-lowering therapy is needed to determine whether hyperuricemia is related to the development of idiopathic DVT and whether there is a causal relationship between these factors.
