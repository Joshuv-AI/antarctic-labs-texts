Transition threshold for the Navier-Stokes-Coriolis system at high Reynolds numbers
Minling Li, Changzhen Sun, Chao Wang, Dongyi Wei, Zhifei Zhang, [10pt] School of Mathematical Sciences, Peking UniversityBeijing 100871, P. R. China

Abstract
The transition mechanism from laminar flow to turbulent flow is a central problem in hydrodynamic stability theory. To shed light on this transition mechanism, Trefethen et al.( Science 1993 ) proposed the transition threshold problem, aiming to quantify the magnitude of perturbations required to trigger instability and determine their scaling with the Reynolds number. In this paper, we investigate the transition threshold of Couette flow for the three-dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes-Coriolis system in the high Reynolds number regime ( Re ≫ 1 \mathrm{Re}\gg 1 ). By exploiting the combined effects of rotation (dispersion) and mixing mechanisms, we derive an improved stability threshold scaling in Re . \mathrm{Re}. Precisely, we show that if the initial perturbation satisfies ‖ 𝒗 i ​ n − ( y , 0 , 0 ) ‖ H ~ ​ ( 𝕋 × 𝔻 ) ≤ ϵ 0 ​ Re − α , \|\bm{v}_{in}-(y,0,0)\|_{\tilde{H}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{D}})}\leq\epsilon_{0}\,\mathrm{Re}^{-\alpha}, with any α > 2 3 \alpha>\frac{2}{3} and H ~ = H 6 ∩ W 3 , 1 \tilde{H}=H^{6}\cap W^{3,1} for 𝔻 = ℝ 2 {\mathbb{D}}=\mathbb{R}^{2} , and with any α ≥ 5 6 \alpha\geq\frac{5}{6} and H ~ = H 6 \tilde{H}=H^{6} for 𝔻 = ℝ × 𝕋 {\mathbb{D}}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T} , the corresponding solution of the Navier-Stokes-Coriolis system exists globally in time and remains asymptotically close to the Couette flow. The main analytical challenge arises from the anisotropic nature of the estimates for the zero modes and from the interactions between zero and non-zero modes, which we address using an anisotropic Sobolev space directly tailored to the zero modes. Additionally, we introduce a new dispersive structure for the zero modes and derive suitable Strichartz-type estimates. These tools enable us to exploit both the nonlinear structure and the improved dispersive behavior of certain good components of the zero modes, which play a crucial role in achieving the improved stability threshold.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following Navier-Stokes-Coriolis system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{v}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{v}+\beta\vec{e}_{3}\times\bm{v}-\nu\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{v}+\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}p^{N}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{v}=0,\end{array}\right.

where
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
,
\bm{x}_{1}=(x_{1},y,z)\in{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{D}}
, the domain
{\mathbb{D}}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
or
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
.
The unknown vector field
\bm{v}=(v^{1},v^{2},v^{3})^{T}
describes the incompressible fluid velocity, while
p^{N}
denotes the scalar pressure field.
Our coordinate system is chosen such that the axis of rotation is
\vec{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
.
The parameter
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
characterizes the rotation rate, which reflects in the Coriolis force
\beta\vec{e}_{3}\times\bm{v}
, and
\nu=\mathrm{Re}^{-1}>0
represents the viscosity.
The system (
1
) is supplemented with initial data:

\displaystyle\bm{v}|_{t=0}=\bm{v}_{in}.

It is easily to verify that system (
1
) has the following Couette flow profile stationary solution

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{v}}=(y,0,0)^{T},\quad\partial_{y}\bar{p}^{N}=-\beta y,

Here, we focus on the nonlinear stability analysis around the Couette flow at high Reynolds number regime. Thus, we introduce the perturbation velocity field:

\displaystyle\bm{u}\coloneqq\bm{v}-\bar{\bm{v}},

which leads to the following perturbation system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+y\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{u}+((1-\beta)u^{2},\beta u^{1},0)^{T}-\nu\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u}+\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}p^{L}=-\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u}-\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}p^{NL},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u}=0,\\
\displaystyle\bm{u}|_{t=0}=\bm{u}_{in},\end{array}\right.

where the linear and nonlinear pressure terms are given respectively by:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}p^{L}=-2\partial_{x_{1}}u^{2}+\beta(\partial_{x_{1}}u^{2}-\partial_{y}u^{1}),\quad\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}p^{NL}=-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{\bm{x}_{1}}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u}).

The Coriolis effect acts as a stabilizing mechanism in the fluid systems. However, its influence can vary significantly due to changes in the parameter
\beta
. The physical quantity to quantify the effects of rotation is the so-called Bradshaw-Richardson number
[
9
,
23
]
:

\displaystyle B_{\beta}\coloneqq\beta(\beta-1)\in[-\frac{1}{4},+\infty),

which is also termed the Pedley number in some literature
[
26
,
31
]
. The value of the
B_{\beta}
will decide the stability or instability of system (
1
).

The dynamics of solutions in system (
1
) differ fundamentally from those that are independent of the periodic
x_{1}
-variable, see Theorems
1.1
-
1.2
below.
To account for this, we use the frequency decomposition, i.e., for any function
f
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{0}f\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}f(x_{1},y,z)\,dx_{1},\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\not=}f\coloneqq f-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}f,

which we respectively refer to as the zero and non-zero frequencies of
f
.
In the sequel, to simplify the notations, we sometimes use the
(f_{0},f_{\not=})
instead of
({\mathbb{P}}_{0}f,{\mathbb{P}}_{\not=}f)
.

Applying
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
to system (
1
), we have

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}^{1}+(1-\beta)u_{0}^{2}-\nu(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})u_{0}^{1}=-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{1}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}^{2}-\beta\mathcal{R}_{3}^{2}u_{0}^{1}-\nu(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})u_{0}^{2}=-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{2})-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\partial_{y}p^{NL},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}^{3}+\beta\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathcal{R}_{3}u_{0}^{1}-\nu(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})u_{0}^{3}=-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{3})-{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\partial_{z}p^{NL},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{y}u_{0}^{2}+\partial_{z}u_{0}^{3}=0,\end{array}\right.

where
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{T}}
, the operators
\mathcal{R}_{2}\coloneqq\partial_{y}(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{3}\coloneqq\partial_{z}(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

1.1
Background and known results

The stability of plane Couette flow has been studied extensively since the pioneering works of Rayleigh
[
33
]
and Kelvin
[
24
]
. It is well established that plane Couette flow is linearly stable for all Reynolds numbers, as demonstrated in
[
19
]
and
[
34
]
. However, the flow can exhibit nonlinear instability and transition to turbulence under small but finite perturbations at high Reynolds numbers—a phenomenon known as the Sommerfeld paradox. One approach to resolving this paradox involves studying the transition threshold problem, a concept first rigorously formulated by Trefethen et al.
[
35
]
. In
[
2
,
3
]
, Bedrossian, Germain, and Masmoudi established a comprehensive mathematical framework for this problem, which can be stated as follows:

Given a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, find a
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{1}(X)
so that

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{X}\leq\mathrm{Re}^{-\alpha_{1}}\Rightarrow\mathrm{stability},

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{X}\geq\mathrm{Re}^{-\alpha_{1}}\Rightarrow\mathrm{instability}.

The exponent
\alpha_{1}
is referred to as the transition threshold.

Over the past decade, significant progress has been made in obtaining rigorous mathematical results for this problem.
The threshold
\alpha_{1}
in the Navier-Stokes equations exhibits a strong dependence on dimension, domain, function space of the perturbation, and boundary conditions.
For the 2-D case on the domain
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, it is known that
\alpha_{1}\leq 0
for the perturbations in Gevrey class
[
7
,
27
]
and
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{1}{3}
for the perturbations in Sobolev class
[
8
,
29
,
37
]
.
On the bounded domain
{\mathbb{T}}\times[0,1]
, the results further depend on the boundary conditions: under slip boundary conditions,
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{1}{3}
for the perturbations in Sobolev class
[
38
]
, while
\alpha_{1}\leq 0
for the perturbations in Gevrey class
[
6
]
; under no-slip boundary conditions,
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for the perturbations in Sobolev class
[
12
]
. Additional results on the stability of other kinds of 2-D shear flow
(such as Poiseuille flow) can be found in
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
11
]
and the references therein.
The 3-D case (i.e., system (
1
) with
\beta=0
) is considerably more challenging due to the lift-up effect.
For this setting, the current best-known upper bound is
\alpha_{1}\leq 1
, which has been established for the perturbations in Gevrey class on domain
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
, see
[
4
,
5
]
, Sobolev class on
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
, see

[
2
,
36
]
and
{\mathbb{T}}\times[0,1]\times{\mathbb{T}}
with non-slip boundary, see
[
10
]
.
Here, the results from
[
10
,
36
]
confirm the transition threshold conjecture proposed by Trefethen et al. in
[
35
]
. For more results on the other model on the stability of Couette flow (for example MHD), see
[
28
,
32
]
and therein.

Determining the threshold
\alpha_{1}<1
for the 3-D Navier-Stokes equations remains a challenging problem. One of the primary difficulties arises from the zero-frequency modes of the velocity field. Specifically, the linearized system for the zero modes
\bm{u}_{0}
satisfies the following heat equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{0}-\nu\Delta\bm{u}_{0}+(u_{0}^{2},0,0)=0,

where the term
(u_{0}^{2},0,0)
is called the lift-up term as it brings linear growth.
To address the lift-up effect, the works of
[
2
,
10
,
36
]

establish the following key estimate:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\|u^{1}_{0}\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\min\{1+t,\nu^{-1}\}\|\bm{u}_{in}\|_{H^{3}},

which introduces either a
\nu^{-1}
loss or a
(1+t)
growth. This behavior significantly impacts the transition threshold
\alpha_{1}
, suggesting that stability for the 3-D Navier-Stokes equations requires at least
\alpha_{1}\geq 1
. To potentially lower the bound on
\alpha_{1}
, a natural starting point is the study of the equations governing the streak solutions.

Alternatively, improving the transition threshold could involve identifying new dynamical mechanisms. A pioneering result in this direction was achieved by Coti Zelati, Del Zotto, and Widmayer
[
13
,
14
]
, who demonstrated that the 3-D Boussinesq system
on
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}

exhibits a dispersive effect, capable of suppressing the lift-up effect. Their analysis establishes the following key dispersive estimate for the linear system:

\displaystyle\|u^{2}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|u_{0}^{3}-\bar{u}^{3}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\theta_{0}-\bar{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}e^{-\nu t}\|(\bm{u}_{in},\theta_{in})\|_{W^{6,1}},

where the double zero-frequency components are defined by
\bar{f}_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{0}\,dz
with
f\in\{u^{3},\theta\}
.
The additional
(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}
decay rate arises from a dispersive effect, a novel phenomenon absent from the 3-D Navier-Stokes equations.
This improved decay estimate provides enhanced control over the nonlinear terms in the system for simple zero-frequency modes. Building upon these estimates, the authors in
[
14
]
were able to establish an improved transition threshold of
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{11}{12}
.

In contrast to the standard Navier-Stokes equations, the Navier-Stokes-Coriolis system (
1
) not only represents a fundamental physical model, but also exhibits stabilizing effects induced by the Coriolis force. However, the nature of these effects depends critically on the parameter
\beta
. The work of Coti Zelati, Del Zotto, and Widmayer
[
15
]
provides a comprehensive analysis of the linearized system, revealing three distinct regimes: the system exhibits lift-up stability for
B_{\beta}=0
; exponential instabilities emerge for
B_{\beta}<0
; a dispersive structure develops for
B_{\beta}>0
.
Huang, Sun, and Xu
[
21
,
22
]
found that the dispersive structure exhibited by the zero-frequency components
\bm{u}_{0}
suppresses the lift-up effect, and they established the
enhanced decay estimate:

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{0}-\bar{\bm{u}}_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}e^{-\nu t}\|\bm{u}_{in}\|_{W^{6,1}},

which represents a notable improvement over the corresponding estimates for the 3-D Navier-Stokes equations (cf. (
1.10
)). This dispersive behavior ensures that the zero-frequency components remain within the same order of magnitude as the initial data, thereby preventing secondary flow instabilities in system (
1
). Based on this analysis, they get the transition threshold
\alpha_{1}\leq 1
when
B_{\beta}>0
in
[
21
]
and
\alpha_{1}\leq 2
when
\beta=1
in
[
22
]
.
Recent advancements by Coti Zelati, Del Zotto, and Widmayer
[
15
]
have further refined these thresholds:
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{8}{9}
for
B_{\beta}>0
and
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{5}{6}
for
B_{\beta}\gtrsim\nu^{-1}
when the initial double zero modes equal to zero.

1.2
Main results

For any
s\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, we say that
f(x_{1},y,z)\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{D}})
whenever

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{D}})}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{l=0}^{s}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{D}}}|\nabla^{l}f|^{2}\,dx_{1}dydz<+\infty,

where
\nabla\coloneqq(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{y}+t\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{z})
, which refers to as the “good derivative”. We also observe that

(1.11)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\nabla^{s}f={\mathbb{P}}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}^{s}f,\quad\|{\mathbb{P}}_{0}f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{D}})}^{2}=\|{\mathbb{P}}_{0}f\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{D}})}^{2}.

Firstly, we consider the domain
{\mathbb{D}}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and our main result can be stated as:

Theorem 1.1

(Nonlinear stability on
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
.

Let
0<\nu\leq 1
and
B_{\beta}>0
.
There exists a constant
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\alpha>\frac{2}{3}
and any divergence-free initial data
\bm{u}_{in}
satisfying

(1.12)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}\coloneqq\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{in}\|_{H^{5}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})\cap W^{2,1}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq\epsilon_{0}\nu^{\alpha},

then the system (
1
) admits a unique global-in-time solution
\bm{u}
satisfying, for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}}^{4}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}))}+\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}\|_{L_{t}^{2}({\mathcal{H}}^{4}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}))}+\nu^{\frac{1}{6}}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{\not=}\|_{L^{2}_{t}({\mathcal{H}}^{4}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}))}\leq C\varepsilon_{0},

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
t
and
\nu
.

A more detailed description of the long-time dynamics of the solution can be obtained.

Theorem 1.2

(Asymptotic behavior on
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
hold
and that
u_{0in}\in W^{3^{+},1}.
Then the zero-frequency component of the solution satisfies the following dispersive estimates:

(1.13)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{0}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{2^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha-\frac{1}{2}-\delta_{1}},

(1.14)

\displaystyle\|(u_{0}^{3},\nabla u_{0}^{3},\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0})(t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1}\|\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{3^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha-\delta_{1}},

where
\delta_{1}>0
is a small constant.

For the non-zero frequencies, we have the following inviscid damping and enhanced dissipation estimates:

(1.15)

\displaystyle\|(u_{\not=}^{1},u_{\not=}^{3})(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+(1+t)\|u_{\not=}^{2}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}e^{-\delta_{2}\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}t},

where
\delta_{2}>0
is a small constant.

Remark 1.3
.

In contrast to the results established in
[
15
]
, our analysis achieves comparable (or improved due to better dispersion) transition thresholds without requiring further constraints on the double zero modes, which are no longer appropriate in the current setting
\mathbb{T}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

The main observation to avoid such assumption is that we should try to propagate the regularity of zero modes (in
x_{1}
)
\bm{u}_{0}

To this end, we establish energy estimates of zero modes (in
x_{1}

See Section

for more details.

1.3
Obstacles & strategies

In this subsection, we present the general framework, highlight the main obstacles, and outline the strategies for closing the estimates with the smallest possible thresholds. Here, we only outline the main ideas for the case
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The case
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
can be addressed by employing a similar method.

1.3.1
Non-zero modes

We consider the non-zero modes system. One of main analytical challenge in the study of non-zero modes stems from the nonlinear interaction terms between zero and non-zero modes.
To formulate this precisely, we introduce the moving frame

\displaystyle t=t,\quad x\coloneqq x_{1}-ty,\quad y=y,\quad z=z

and denote
\bm{U}(t,x,y,z)=\bm{u}(t,x+ty,y,z).

We then consider the following coupled system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}Q+C_{\beta}\partial_{z}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-1}W-\nu\widetilde{\Delta}Q=-\tilde{\partial}_{y}\widetilde{\Delta}P^{NL}-\widetilde{\Delta}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{2}),\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}W+C_{\beta}\partial_{z}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-1}Q-\partial_{x}\tilde{\partial}_{y}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-2}W-\nu\widetilde{\Delta}W=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}|\widetilde{\nabla}|\big(\partial_{z}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{1})-\partial_{x}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{3})\big),\end{array}\right.

where
\tilde{\partial}_{y}=\partial_{y}-t\partial_{x},\,\tilde{\nabla}=(\partial_{x},\tilde{\partial}_{y},\partial_{z})^{t},\tilde{\Delta}=\partial_{x}^{2}+\tilde{\partial}_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}
and

\displaystyle Q\coloneqq\tilde{\Delta}U^{2},\quad W\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}|\tilde{\nabla}|(\partial_{z}U^{1}-\partial_{x}U^{3}).

We observe that the equation for
W
contains a stretching term. And the presence of rotation terms results in a coupled system of equations for
Q
and
W
.
This coupling leads to an growth in
Q
, which consequently introduces new difficulties for lowering the transition threshold.

In the process of propagating high Sobolev regularity norm (say
H^{4}
) of
(Q_{\neq},W_{\neq})
, we need to deal with the following nonlinear term:

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\nabla^{4}{\mathbb{P}}_{\not=}\mathcal{A}\tilde{\partial}_{y}\widetilde{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(U\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U)\cdot\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}(\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3})||\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}|\,dxdydzd\tau

(1.19)

\displaystyle+\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}\tilde{\partial}_{y}\nabla^{4}(\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{i})\,\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau\Big|+\cdots,

here,
\mathcal{A}
is a time dependent Fourier multiplier type weights that is defined in (
3.1
). And the notation
{}^{\prime}\cdots^{\prime}
denotes terms that can be readily handled, whereas the first two terms (which are called as the reaction terms) on the right-hand side of (
1.19
) require special attention.
We now shift our analysis to these two reaction terms.

\bullet
The first term in the right-hand side of (
1.19
) can be bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}(\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3})||\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}|\,dxdydzd\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla^{4}([\mathcal{A},\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}]\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3})||\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}|\,dxdydzd\tau+\cdots

(1.20)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\|\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\|_{L^{p}_{t}H^{5}}\|\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3}\|_{L^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}_{t}H^{4}}\|\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}\|_{L^{2}_{t}H^{4}},

where
p=2
or
+\infty
.

To close the energy estimates, we need to control zero modes
\|\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\|_{L^{p}_{t}H^{5}}
. Similarly, when dealing with the term like

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\partial_{4-j}(\bm{U}_{0}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{j}_{\not=})||\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}W_{\not=}|\,dxdydzd\tau\qquad\,(j=1,3),

we need to bound
\|\partial_{z}U_{0}^{1,3}\|_{L^{p}_{t}H^{5}}.

A natural approach is to first work on the the zero modes of
(Q,W)
and then recover the estimates of
\bm{U}_{0}
.
This relies on analyzing the boundedness of the operators

\partial_{z}^{-1}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-1}
and
{\partial_{y}\partial_{z}^{-1}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-2}}.

Such a strategy works well in the setting
(y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}},
with additional estimates on double zero (in
x
and
z
) frequencies of
U_{0}^{1,3}.
Moreover, one can obtain favorable estimates on
\partial_{y}U_{0}^{1,3}

by imposing suitable assumptions on the initial data of the double zero frequencies of
U_{0}^{1,3}.
Nevertheless, this strategy fails in the current setting
(y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
or the case
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
without any assumptions on the double zero modes, since
\partial_{z}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-2}
and

{\partial_{y}|\nabla_{y}|^{-1}\partial_{z}^{-1}}
exhibit singularities at low
z
frequencies and it is no longer appropriate to consider separately zero and non-zero frequencies in
z.

To resolve the problem, instead of working on the simple zero (zero in
x
but non-zero in
z
) modes of
(Q,W),
we will try to propagate the regularity of the zero modes
\bm{U}_{0}
(or
\bm{u}_{0}
) directly by performing the energy estimates on the system (
1
).
This is doable due to the symmetric structure in the system
(
1
).

Coming back to (
1.20
). A natural approach is to leverage the dissipation term to control
\|\partial_{z}\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L_{t}^{2}H^{5}}
, which leads to

\alpha\geq 1
. To relax the constraint,
we should instead put
p=+\infty.

The crucial observation that allows us to do so is

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{j}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{4}}\leq\|(Q,W)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{4}},~j\in\{1,3\},

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Delta}U_{\not=}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{4}}\leq\|Q\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{4}}

which suggests that one can propagate the
H^{5}
norm of
\partial_{z}\bm{u}_{0},
given the
H^{4}
regularity of
(Q,W)^{t}.

These motivate us to introduce the anisotropic energy functional for the zero modes
\bm{u}_{0},
see (
3.3
).
Thus, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}(\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3})||\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}|\,dxdydzd\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\|\partial_{z}U_{0}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{5}}\|\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}U_{\not=}^{3}\|_{L^{2}_{t}H^{4}}\|\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}\|_{L^{2}_{t}H^{4}}+\cdots

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\nu^{-\frac{2}{3}}|{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(t)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{\not=}(\tau)\,d\tau\lesssim\epsilon_{1}^{3}\nu^{3\alpha-\frac{2}{3}}.

\bullet
Next, we aim to deal with the second term in the right-hand side of (
1.19
).
A direct computation shows that it can be controlled by

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}\tilde{\partial}_{y}\nabla^{4}(\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{i})\,\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau\Big|\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\partial_{i}U_{\neq}^{2}\|_{L_{t}^{2}H^{4}}\|\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{i}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H^{4}}\|\tilde{\partial}_{y}\mathcal{A}Q_{\neq}\|_{L_{t}^{2}H^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\nu^{-\frac{5}{6}}|{\mathcal{E}}_{\not=}(t)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{\not=}(\tau)\,d\tau\lesssim\epsilon_{1}^{3}\nu^{3\alpha-\frac{5}{6}},

which limits the stability thresholds
\alpha
to be no less than
\frac{5}{6}
.
To further reduce the threshold, we have to take fully advantage of the inviscid damping of
U_{\neq}^{2}.

For this purpose, we split the second term in the right-hand side of (
1.19
) into three terms

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\partial}_{y}\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{i})\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}([\nabla^{4},\tilde{\partial}_{y}^{2}U_{\not=}^{i}]\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2})\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}(\nabla^{4}\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}^{2}U_{\not=}^{i})\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau

The first term can be handled easily by favorable estimates for
\tilde{\partial}_{y}\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}.
For the second term, we can make use of the inviscid damping estimate for
U_{\neq}^{2}:

(1.21)

\displaystyle\|U_{\neq}^{2}(t)\|_{H^{3}}\lesssim\||\tilde{\nabla}|^{-1}\mathcal{A}Q_{\neq}\|_{H^{3}}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1}\|\mathcal{A}Q_{\neq}\|_{H^{4}}.

The last term is the most involved, since a similar estimate to (
1.21
) cannot be applied due to the regularity issue. The idea to overcome this difficulty is to find a way to ‘transfer’ half derivative
|\tilde{\nabla}|^{-\frac{1}{2}},
which arises from the relation
U_{\neq}^{2}(t)\approx|\tilde{\nabla}|^{-1}\mathcal{A}Q_{\neq}

into other quantities such as
\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=},
thereby also gaining time integrability. To this end, and motivated by
[
25
]
, we introduce the Fourier multiplier
m_{3}
(defined in (
2.21
)) to construct the time-dependent weight
\varUpsilon
(given by (
3.1
)).
This weight will bring us a new damping
\|\varUpsilon\mathcal{A}(Q_{\not=},W_{\not=})(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}
,
which enables us to control the last term as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}(\nabla^{4}\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}^{2}U_{\not=}^{i})\nabla^{4}\mathcal{A}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu^{-\frac{2}{3}}|\ln(\nu^{-1})|^{1+a_{0}}\|\mathcal{A}(Q_{\not=},W_{\not=})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{4}}\big\|\sqrt{\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}}\mathcal{A}(Q_{\not=},W_{\not=})\big\|_{L^{2}_{t}H^{4}}^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu^{-\frac{2}{3}}|\ln(\nu^{-1})|^{1+a_{0}}||{\mathcal{E}}_{\not=}(t)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{\not=}(\tau)\,d\tau\lesssim\epsilon_{1}^{3}\nu^{3\alpha-\frac{2}{3}}|\ln(\nu^{-1})|^{1+a_{0}}

for some
a_{0}\in(0,\sqrt{2}-1).
To summary, in order to propagate high regularity norms for the non-zero modes, we need the threshold
\alpha>\frac{2}{3}.

Considering the previously obtained transition threshold of
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{1}{3}
for the 2-D Navier-Stokes equations
[
8
,
29
,
37
]
and the presence of a stretching term in the 3D system, it is natural to conjecture that
\alpha_{1}\leq\frac{2}{3}
in the current context.

1.3.2
Zero modes

We consider the zero modes system.

Dispersive structure and estimates.

We work in the stable regime

B_{\beta}>0
in which the system (
1
) exhibits an oscillatory structure that gives rise to dispersive properties.
More precisely, as in the previous works
[
21
,
22
,
15
]
, one can
symmetrize the coupled system satisfied by

(u_{0}^{1},u_{0}^{2})
in system (
1
),
and introduce the quantities

\displaystyle W^{\pm}\coloneqq u_{0}^{2}\pm i\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}u_{0}^{1}\,,\quad\text{ with }\,\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}=\sqrt{-\mathcal{R}^{2}_{3}}=\sqrt{\partial_{z}^{2}(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})^{-1}},

which satisfies that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W^{\pm}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}W^{\pm}+\textrm{nonlinear terms},

where the operators
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}
are defined by

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}\coloneqq\pm i\mathrm{sgn}(\beta)\sqrt{B_{\beta}}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}+\nu(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}).

An important feature of the linear operators
\mathcal{L}^{\pm}
is that they exhibit the dispersive property:

(1.22)

\displaystyle\|e^{t\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}}f\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{2}{q})}\|f\|_{W^{(2^{+})(1-\frac{2}{q}),q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})},\qquad\forall\,2\leq q\leq+\infty.

The above estimate indicates that not only is the lift-up effect suppressed when

B_{\beta}>0,
but the oscillatory nature also causes the amplitude of the zero modes to decay over time. This is the primary reason that one can expect the stability threshold to be lower than in the case without rotation, i.e.,
B_{\beta}=0
.
Nevertheless, by working on
W^{\pm}
and (
1.22
), there are still
some troubles in proving the stability of Couette flow with the smallest possible threshold. Indeed, from (
1.22
) and the definition of
W^{\pm},
we are only able to
obtain the following anisotropic estimates for
\bm{u}_{0}:

(1.23)

\displaystyle\|(\nabla u_{0}^{2},\partial_{z}\bm{u}_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{3^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\text{contribution of nonlinear terms.}

However, estimates for
(1+\partial_{y})(u_{0}^{1},u_{0}^{3})
are not available.
Whereas on the domain
(y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
, such estimates can be achieved by studying the simple zero (zero in
x_{1}
but non-zero in
z
) modes of
(u_{0}^{1},u_{0}^{3})
and using the divergence-free condition
\partial_{z}u_{0}^{3}=-\partial_{y}u_{0}^{2}
to control
u_{0}^{3}
by
u_{0}^{2}
, this method fails for
(y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
due to the singularities in the low frequencies.
With the unfavorable dispersive estimates (
1.23
), we are only able to obtain a mild stability threshold
\alpha>\frac{4}{5}
.

To obtain a improved threshold, we need to identify a new dispersive structure. For such a purpose, we introduce the new unknown

\displaystyle\upsilon_{0}={\frac{\beta-1}{\sqrt{B_{\beta}}}}(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{z}u_{0}^{2}-\partial_{y}u_{0}^{3}),

which, together with
u_{0}^{1},
solves a coupled system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u_{0}^{1}+\sqrt{B_{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{3}\upsilon_{0}-\nu\Delta u_{0}^{1}=N^{1},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\upsilon_{0}+\sqrt{B_{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{3}u_{0}^{1}-\nu\Delta\upsilon_{0}=N^{2},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{R}_{3}=\partial_{z}(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, and

N^{1},N^{2}
are some nonlinear terms.
Then the quantity
{\mathcal{W}}^{\pm}\coloneqq u_{0}^{1}\pm\upsilon_{0}

solves

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathcal{W}}^{\pm}=\mathcal{L}^{\pm}{\mathcal{W}}^{\pm}+(N^{1}\pm N^{2})

where
{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}\coloneqq\pm\sqrt{B_{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{3}+\nu(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}).
We remark that

e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\pm}}
enjoys same dispersive property as
e^{t\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}}.

The advantage of introducing

{\mathcal{W}}^{\pm}
is that it allows one to derive dispersive estimates for all components of

\bm{u}_{0}
through the following relations:

\displaystyle u_{0}^{1}=\frac{1}{2}\big({\mathcal{W}}^{+}+{\mathcal{W}}^{-}\big),\quad\upsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\big({\mathcal{W}}^{+}-{\mathcal{W}}^{-}\big),\quad u_{0}^{2}={-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{B_{\beta}}}}\mathcal{R}_{3}\upsilon_{0},\quad u_{0}^{3}={\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{B_{\beta}}}}\mathcal{R}_{2}\upsilon_{0}\,,

where
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\partial_{y}(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

From these identities, we readily get that

(1.24)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{2^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\text{contribution of nonlinear terms.}

Moreover, as proved in Lemma
A.3
, the following improved dispersive estimate holds

\displaystyle\|e^{t{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}}\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1}\|f\|_{W^{3^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}.

As a result, we find that the quantities
u_{0}^{3}
and
\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0}
, which we refer to as “good components”, enjoy the improved dispersive estimates:

(1.25)

\displaystyle\|(u_{0}^{3},\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1}\|\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{3^{+},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\text{contribution of nonlinear terms.}

Propagation of high Sobolev regularity for zero modes.
As mentioned in the previous section, in order to close the estimates for the nonzero modes, it is necessary to propagate the zero modes in an anisotropic Sobolev norm

\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H^{5}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}.
This can be achieved by establishing the following
L_{t}^{2}L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
estimates for
\bm{u}_{0}:

(1.26)

\displaystyle\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\frac{1}{2}},

under the
a priori
assumption

(1.27)

\displaystyle\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H^{5}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L_{t}^{2}H^{5}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\alpha}.

To show (
1.26
), we establish some useful Strichartz type estimates in Lemma
4.3
. Notably, it holds that

(1.28)

\displaystyle\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\lesssim\nu^{-\delta}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{W^{2,1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|(1+\partial_{z}){\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u})\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})},

where the second term in the right hand side represents the contribution of nonlinear terms. Let us remark that if we instead using directly the dispersive estimate (
1.24
), the second term is replaced by

\nu^{-\delta_{1}}\|(1+\partial_{z}){\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u})\|_{L^{1}_{t}W^{3,1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})},
which, by (
1.27
),
can only be bounded by
\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha-1-\delta_{1}}.

This requires the threshold
\alpha>\frac{3}{4}
to guarantee that estimate (
1.26
) remains valid.
The advantage of employing an
L_{t}^{1}H^{1}
type norm for the nonlinear term is that it allows us to exploit both the good structure of the transport term:

\bm{u}_{0}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}_{0}=u_{0}^{2}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0}+u_{0}^{3}\partial_{z}\bm{u}_{0}

and the improved dispersive estimate (
1.25
). Upon using (
1.28
), we find that

\displaystyle\nu^{\delta_{1}}\|(1+\partial_{z})u_{0}^{3}\|_{L^{2}_{t}W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\nu^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta_{1}}\|(1+\partial_{z})\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{t}W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0in}\|_{H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\|(1+\partial_{z}){\mathbb{P}}_{0}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}\bm{u})\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\varepsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}.

Employing the estimates above, one can bound

\displaystyle\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|(1+\partial_{z})(\bm{u}_{0}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}_{0})\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\big(\|(1+\partial_{z})u^{3}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\|(1+\partial_{z})\partial_{z}\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}

\displaystyle+\|(1+\partial_{z})u^{2}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\|(1+\partial_{z})\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{t}W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\alpha-\frac{3}{4}-\delta_{1}}(\varepsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}),

which is less than
\epsilon_{1}\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\|(1+\partial_{z})\bm{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\infty}}
as long as
\epsilon_{1}
small enough and
\alpha>\frac{5}{8}.
We refer to Lemma
4.5
for more details.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Good unknowns and change of frame

Motivated by the standard 3-D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, we introduce the following two auxiliary unknowns to analyze their dynamical evolution:

(2.1)

\displaystyle q\coloneqq\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{2},\quad\omega^{2}\coloneqq\partial_{z}u^{1}-\partial_{x_{1}}u^{3}.

It is derived from the original system (
1
) that
(q,\omega^{2})
satisfies the following coupled system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}q+y\partial_{x_{1}}q+\beta\partial_{z}\omega^{2}-\nu\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}q=-\partial_{y}{\Delta}p^{NL}-\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{2}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}\omega^{2}+y\partial_{x_{1}}\omega^{2}+(1-\beta)\partial_{z}\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}^{-1}q-\nu\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}\omega^{2}=-\partial_{z}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{1})+\partial_{x_{1}}(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}u^{3}).\end{array}\right.

A key distinction from the standard 3-D Navier-Stokes equations lies in the rotational coupling between
(q,\omega^{2})
, where their governing equations are linearly coupled through the Coriolis terms.
This inherent coupling suggest treating
(q,\omega^{2})
as a complete coupled system, whose individual components cannot be estimated separately in the energy analysis.

The inherent shear effects of Couette flow naturally motivate our introduction of moving frame coordinates:

(2.4)

\displaystyle t=t,\quad x\coloneqq x_{1}-ty,\quad y=y,\quad z=z.

In this frame, we set

(2.5)

\displaystyle Q(t,x,y,z)\coloneqq q(t,x+ty,y,z),\quad\Omega^{2}(t,x,y,z)\coloneqq\omega^{2}(t,x+ty,y,z).

More generally, for any function
f(t,x_{1},y,z)
(which may be
q,\omega^{2},\bm{u},p^{L},p^{NL}
), we define the corresponding quantity
F(t,x,y,z)\coloneqq f(t,x+ty,y,z)
(which may be
Q,\Omega^{2},\bm{U},P^{L},P^{NL}
) under which the differential operators transform as

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{y}F\coloneqq(\partial_{y}-t\partial_{x})F,\quad\nabla_{\bm{x}_{1}}f=\widetilde{\nabla}F\coloneqq(\partial_{x}F,\tilde{\partial}_{y}F,\partial_{z}F),

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{\bm{x}_{1}}f=\widetilde{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}F\coloneqq\partial_{x}F^{1}+\tilde{\partial}_{y}F^{2}+\partial_{z}F^{3},\quad\Delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}f=\widetilde{\Delta}F\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{2}F+\tilde{\partial}_{y}^{2}F+\partial_{z}^{2}F.

And we denote

\displaystyle\nabla F\coloneqq(\partial_{x}F,\partial_{y}F,\partial_{z}F),\quad\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits F\coloneqq\partial_{x}F^{1}+\partial_{y}F^{2}+\partial_{z}F^{3},\quad\Delta F\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{2}F+\partial_{y}^{2}F+\partial_{z}^{2}F.

We note that the operator
\nabla
is referred to as “good derivative”, whereas the operator
\widetilde{\nabla}
is referred to as“bad derivative”.

In the stable regime where
B_{\beta}>0
, similar to
[
15
]
, to maintain consistent regularity for
Q
, we also define the following unknown:

(2.6)

\displaystyle W\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}|\widetilde{\nabla}|\Omega^{2}.

The system (
2.1
) is then reduced to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q+C_{\beta}\partial_{z}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-1}W-\nu\widetilde{\Delta}Q=-\tilde{\partial}_{y}\widetilde{\Delta}P^{NL}-\widetilde{\Delta}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{2}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}W+C_{\beta}\partial_{z}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-1}Q-\partial_{x}\tilde{\partial}_{y}|\widetilde{\nabla}|^{-2}W-\nu\widetilde{\Delta}W=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}|\widetilde{\nabla}|\big(\partial_{z}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{1})-\partial_{x}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}U^{3})\big),\end{array}\right.

where
C_{\beta}\coloneqq\mathrm{sgn}(\beta)\sqrt{B_{\beta}}
, the nonlinear pressure term may be determined explicitly,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}P^{NL}=-\widetilde{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\bm{U}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}\bm{U}).

The analysis for estimating the non-zero frequencies relies on the pair
(Q,W)
, hereafter denoted as the “good unknowns”.

2.2
The Fourier multipliers

In this subsection, we present some Fourier multipliers that will be frequently used in our analysis.

We begin with the first Fourier multiplier
\varphi
, which characterizes the interplay between stretching and dissipation effects.
We focus on a fundamental model originally introduced in
[
2
]
, described by the following equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\partial_{x}(\partial_{y}-t\partial_{x})\widetilde{\Delta}^{-1}f-\nu\widetilde{\Delta}f=0,

where the operator
\widetilde{\Delta}\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{2}+(\partial_{y}-t\partial_{x})^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}
.
Taking the Fourier transform, this model becomes

(2.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{f}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\hat{f}-\nu p\hat{f},

here the symbol
p\coloneqq k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2}+\eta^{2}
.
Notably, the stretching term
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\hat{f}
in the right-hand side of equation (
2.9
) is known to induce growth effects and
this growth cannot be fully balanced by the dissipation
\nu p\hat{f}
.

For
t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}
, we observe that
\partial_{t}p=-2k(\xi-kt)\leq 0
, implying that the first term on the right-hand side acts as a damping term for
f
.
However, when
t>\frac{\xi}{k}
, the derivative becomes positive, i.e.,
\partial_{t}p>0
, and the corresponding term drives growth.
Near the critical time
t=\frac{\xi}{k}
the dissipation satisfies
\nu p\sim\nu(k^{2}+\eta^{2})
, which is insufficient to uniformly control the growth in
\nu
.
Nevertheless, sufficiently far from the critical layer, dissipation dominates: for any fixed
\beta_{0}>2
, the inequality

\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\leq\frac{1}{4}\nu p,

holds whenever
|t-\frac{\xi}{k}|\geq\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
.

To enable the dissipation term
\nu p\hat{f}
to balance the growth caused by
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\hat{f}
, we adopt the approach of
[
2
,
28
]
, and introduce the Fourier multiplier
\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\varphi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}0,\quad t\not\in[\frac{\xi}{k},\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}],\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\varphi,\quad t\in[\frac{\xi}{k},\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}],\\[4.30554pt]
\end{array}\right.\\
\varphi(0,k,\xi,\eta)=1,\end{array}\right.

where
\beta_{0}>2
is a constant to be determined later.
Note that
\varphi(t,0,\xi,\eta)=1
by construction.
In contrast to
\hat{f},

the transformed quantity
\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{f}
exhibits no growth as it is governed by the equation

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu p)(\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{f})=0.

Consequently, the decay of the multiplier
\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}
precisely balance the growth that
\hat{f}
.
Remarkably,
\varphi
admits a closed-form expression as:

(1)

If
k=0
, or if
k\not=0
and
\frac{\xi}{k}\leq-\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
:

\varphi(t,0,\xi,\eta)=1;

(2)

If
k\not=0
,
-\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}<\frac{\xi}{k}\leq 0
:

\displaystyle\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)=\begin{cases}\frac{k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2}+\eta^{2}}{k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}},\quad&\text{if}~~0<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{k^{2}+(\beta_{0}k\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}})^{2}+\eta^{2}}{k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}},\quad&\text{if}~~t>\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}};\end{cases}

(3)

If
k\not=0
,
\frac{\xi}{k}>0
:

\displaystyle\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{if}~~0<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{k^{2}+(\xi-kt)^{2}+\eta^{2}}{k^{2}+\eta^{2}},\quad&\text{if}~~\frac{\xi}{k}<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{k^{2}+(\beta_{0}k\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}})^{2}+\eta^{2}}{k^{2}+\eta^{2}},\quad&\text{if}~~t>\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}.\end{cases}

This leads to the following lemma, which shows that the growth is exactly balanced by dissipation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\varphi
be the Fourier multiplier defined in (
2.2
).
For any
t\geq 0
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}
and
(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle 1\leq\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)\leq\beta_{0}^{2}\nu^{-\frac{2}{3}},\quad\frac{\varphi}{p}\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}+\eta^{2}}.

Moreover, for any
(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the following product estimates hold that

(2.14)

\displaystyle\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)\leq C\varphi(t,k,\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime},\eta-\eta^{\prime}\rangle^{2},

(2.15)

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle^{-2}\varphi(t,k,\xi,\eta)\leq 3,

where
\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime},\eta-\eta^{\prime}\rangle\coloneqq\sqrt{1+(\xi-\xi^{\prime})^{2}+(\eta-\eta^{\prime})^{2}}
and
C>0
is a constant.

Proof.

The proofs of estimates (
2.13
)-(
2.14
) can be found in
[
2
]
and are omitted here.
Now, we turn to prove (
2.15
).
If
k=0
, or if
k\not=0
and
\frac{\xi}{k}\leq-\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, then
\varphi=1
.
In these cases, estimate (
2.15
) is trivially satisfied.
For the case that
k\not=0
and
-\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}<\frac{\xi}{k}\leq 0
, if
0<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle^{-2}\varphi\leq\frac{k^{2}+2\xi^{2}+2k^{2}t^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle t\rangle^{2}}\leq\frac{2\xi^{2}+2k^{2}\langle t\rangle^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle t\rangle^{2}}\leq 2.

If
t>\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle^{-2}\varphi\leq\frac{k^{2}+2\xi^{2}+2(\xi+\beta_{0}k\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}})^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}\rangle^{2}}\leq\frac{2\xi^{2}+2k^{2}\langle\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}\rangle^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}\rangle^{2}}\leq 2.

As for the case that
k\not=0
and
\frac{\xi}{k}>0
, if
0<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}
, we have
\varphi=1
, from which (
2.15
) follows trivially.
If
\frac{\xi}{k}<t\leq\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle^{-2}\varphi\leq\frac{k^{2}+k^{2}t^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle t\rangle^{2}}\leq 1.

If
t>\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, some direct calculations yield that

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle^{-2}\varphi\leq\frac{k^{2}\langle\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}\rangle^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(k^{2}+\eta^{2})\langle\frac{\xi}{k}+\beta_{0}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}\rangle^{2}}\leq 1.

The estimate (
2.15
) is thus proved.
∎

In our energy estimates, we employ three key Fourier multipliers to capture enhanced dissipation and inviscid damping effects.
These multipliers have been widely employed in previous works
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
39
]
.
The first crucial multiplier,
m_{1},
addresses the transient weakening of enhanced dissipation near critical times.
This multiplier is particularly significant as it enables us to: (1) quantify accelerated dissipation without regularity loss; (2) maintain regularity unlike methods requiring infinite regularity
[
4
,
7
]
.
In particular,
m_{1}
is defined by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{1}(t,k,\xi,\eta)=\frac{2\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}}{1+\nu^{\frac{2}{3}}(\frac{\xi}{k}-t)^{2}}m_{1}(t,k,\xi,\eta),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle m_{1}(0,k,\xi,\eta)=1.\end{array}\right.

It is easy to check that, for any
t\geq 0
and
(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the multiplier
m_{1}
admits the explicit solution:

\displaystyle m_{1}(t,k,\xi,\eta)=\begin{cases}\mathop{\rm exp}\nolimits\left\{2\arctan\left(\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(t-\frac{\xi}{k}\right)\right)+2\arctan\left(\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}\frac{\xi}{k}\right)\right\},\quad\text{for}~~k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\},\\
1,\quad\text{for}~~k=0.\end{cases}

We then introduce another multiplier
m_{2}
, designed to control growth arising from both linear pressure terms and leading order nonlinear terms.
In particular,
m_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{2}(t,k,\xi,\eta)=A\frac{k^{2}}{p}m_{2}(t,k,\xi,\eta),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle m_{2}(0,k,\xi,\eta)=1,\end{array}\right.

where

A>0
is a large, but fixed constant, which will be determined later.

The following Lemma
2.2
states key properties of the Fourier multipliers
\varphi
,
m_{i}

(i=1,2)
.
Its proof is standard and follows from
[
2
]
, hence we omit it.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\varphi
,
m_{i}

(i=1,2)
be three Fourier multipliers defined in (
2.2
), (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), respectively.
Then the multipliers
m_{i}

(i=1,2)
are bounded from above and below uniformly with respect to
0<\nu<1
and
(t,k,\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Specifically, they are bounded by

\displaystyle 1\leq m_{1}\leq e^{2\pi},\quad 1\leq m_{2}\leq e^{A\pi}.

Moreover, for any
\max\{2\beta_{0}^{-1}(\beta_{0}^{2}-1)^{-1},4\beta_{0}^{-1}\}<\delta_{\beta_{0}}\leq 1
with
\beta_{0}>2
, we also have

(2.20)

\displaystyle\delta_{\beta_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial_{t}m_{1}}{m_{1}}+\nu p\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial_{t}\varphi}{\varphi}-\frac{\partial_{t}p}{p}\right)\geq\delta_{\beta_{0}}\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}.

In order to bound the following reaction term appearing in the energy estimates of
(Q_{\neq},W_{\neq})

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathcal{A}(\nabla^{s}\partial_{i}U_{\not=}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{y}U_{\not=}^{i})\nabla^{s}\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\nabla}Q_{\not=}\,dxdydzd\tau,

we introduce, motivated by
[
25
]
, the Fourier multiplier
m_{3}
as:

(2.21)

\displaystyle m_{3}(t,k,\xi)\coloneqq\mathop{\rm exp}\nolimits\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}}f^{n}(\tau,k,\xi)\,d\tau\Big\},\qquad\forall\,t\geq 0,~~(k,\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

where the function sequences
f^{n}(\tau,k,\xi)
are defined by

\displaystyle f^{n}(t,k,\xi)\coloneqq\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{n}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{n}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}\langle k-n\rangle^{-1-a_{0}},\quad\text{for}~~0<a_{0}<1.

The precise value of the parameter
a_{0}
will be determined in subsequent sections.
By some direct calculations, we can verify that the Fourier multiplier
m_{3}(t,k,\xi)
satisfies the following property.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
m_{3}
be the Fourier multiplier defined in (
2.21
). Then the multiplier
m_{3}
is bounded from above and below uniformly with respect to
0<\nu<1
and
(t,k,\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Specifically, it is bounded by

(2.22)

\displaystyle 1\leq m_{3}\leq e^{Ca_{0}^{-2}}.

Moreover, for any
0<a_{0}<1
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}
,
k^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
t\geq 0
, we also have

(2.23)

\displaystyle\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}\leq\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k,\xi),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}\leq Ca_{0}^{-1-a_{0}}\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\langle k-k^{\prime},\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{2+a_{0}(2+a_{0})}.

And for any
0<a_{0}<1
,
(k,k^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
,
(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
t\geq 0
, we have

(2.25)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k,\xi)\leq Ca_{0}^{-1-a_{0}}\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\langle k-k^{\prime},\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{2+a_{0}(2+a_{0})},

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
a_{0}
.

Proof.

It is easy to observe that

\displaystyle f^{n}(t,k,\xi)\leq\langle k-n\rangle^{-1-a_{0}},\quad\int_{-\infty}^{t}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}}f^{n}(\tau,k,\xi)\,d\tau\leq Ca_{0}^{-1}\langle k-n\rangle^{-1-a_{0}}.

Since
0<a_{0}<1
, we can obtain (
2.22
) immediately.
Next, we turn to prove (
2.23
).
Since the function sequences
f^{n}(t,k,\xi)
are non-negative, it follows that for
k\not=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k,\xi)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}}f^{n}(t,k,\xi)\geq f^{k}(t,k,\xi)=\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}.

We now focus on proving (
2.24
).
To this end, we deduce that for
k\not=0
,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\geq f^{k}(t,k^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})=\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}\langle k^{\prime}-k\rangle^{-1-a_{0}}.

Some simple calculations give that for any
0<a_{0}<1
,
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\ln(e+\langle x\rangle)\lesssim a_{0}^{-1}\langle x\rangle^{a_{0}},\quad\ln(e+\langle x+y\rangle)\leq\ln(e+\langle x\rangle)+\ln(e+\langle y\rangle).

From which, it then follows that

(2.27)

\displaystyle\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1}\lesssim a_{0}^{-1}\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{a_{0}}.

We also recall that

\displaystyle\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle\leq\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle.

This, combined with (
2.27
), implies that

\displaystyle\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi^{\prime}}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{1+a_{0}}\lesssim a_{0}^{-(1+a_{0})}\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{1+a_{0}}\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{1+a_{0}(1+a_{0})}.

We substitute the estimate above into (
2.26
) to obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}(t,k^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\gtrsim a_{0}^{1+a_{0}}\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle^{-1}\big[\ln\big(e+\big\langle t-\frac{\xi}{k}\big\rangle\big)\big]^{-1-a_{0}}\langle\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{-1-a_{0}(1+a_{0})}\langle k^{\prime}-k\rangle^{-1-a_{0}},

from which the estimate (
2.24
) follows.
Finally, following the similar derivations of (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), one obtain (
2.25
) directly.
∎

For simplicity, the combined multiplier is then defined as:

\displaystyle m\coloneqq m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}.

Construction of the energy functionals

Our goal in this section is to construct the energy functionals and establish the
a priori
estimates for the local smooth solutions of system (
1
).
Firstly, we introduce some symbols for any
f(t,x,y,z)
as:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f\coloneqq{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}[\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}m^{-1}e^{\delta_{2}\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}t}\hat{f}],\quad\varUpsilon\mathcal{A}f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\big[\sqrt{\frac{\partial_{t}m_{3}}{m_{3}}}\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}m^{-1}e^{\delta_{2}\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}t}\hat{f}\big],

\varphi^{-\frac{1}{2}}m^{-1}(t,0,\xi,\eta)=m_{3}^{-1}(t,0,\xi,\eta)
.

-variable, we introduce distinct energy functionals accordingly.

\bullet
For the case
{\mathbb{D}}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:

For the zero modes, we introduce the energy functionals for the solution to system (

) as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(t)\coloneqq\|\bm{u}_{0}(t)\|_{H^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}^{2}+\|(1+\partial_{z})\nabla^{5}\mathcal{A}_{0}\bm{u}_{0}(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}^{2},

, corresponding to the operators

and

defined in (

) and (

).

For the case

:

The energy functionals for the zero modes of the solution to system (

)-(

) by replacing the domain

{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
.

Remark 3.1
.

On the domain
{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
, the energy functionals defined in (

) for the zero modes are defined without the Fourier multiplier

, while those for the non-zero modes retain the definitions in (

)-(

We will use the continuity argument to prove Theorems
1.1
-
1.2
.
Thus, we give the following proposition concerning
a priori

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
T>0.

Assume that
\bm{u}_{0}
and
(Q_{\not=},W_{\not=})

are the solutions for systems (
1
) and (
2.1
), respectively,
such that for any
\epsilon_{1}>0
and
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\big({\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\tau)+{\mathcal{E}}_{\not=}(\tau)\big)+\int_{0}^{t}\big({\mathcal{D}}_{0}(\tau)+{\mathcal{D}}_{\not=}(\tau)\big)\,d\tau\leq\epsilon_{1}^{2}\nu^{2\alpha},

Appendix A
Dispersive and Strichartz estimates

This appendix provides the proof of the dispersive and Strichartz estimates the zero modes. Here,
k^{+}
is a number greater than but arbitrarily close to
k
.

A.1
Dispersive estimates on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{3}=\partial_{z}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-1}
.
There exists
C>0
such that for any
\gamma>0,f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}),

(A.1)

\left\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}(D)}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C(1+|\gamma t|)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{W^{2^{+},1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the case of
\gamma=1.

In fact, to show (
A.1
), we will prove that

(A.2)

\left\|e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{W^{2^{+},1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\forall~|t|\geq 1,

which, combined with the Sobolev embeddings, gives (
A.1
).

Let
\chi_{j}=\chi_{j}(\xi,\eta)=\chi_{0}(2^{-j}(\xi,\eta))\,(j\in\mathbb{Z})
be radial, dyadic cut-off functions that are supported on
\{2^{j-1}\leq|(\xi,\eta)|\leq 2^{j+1}\}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{j}(\xi,\eta)=1,\quad\text{for}~(\xi,\eta)\neq 0\,.

Let us first claim that it suffices to show that

(A.3)

\displaystyle\left\|e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\chi_{0}(D)f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\chi_{0}(D)f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Indeed, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}\big[e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\chi_{j}(D)f\big](\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}(\xi,\eta)}\chi_{0}\big(2^{-j}(\xi,\eta)\big){\mathcal{F}}[f](\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})}\chi_{0}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}){\mathcal{F}}[f](2^{j}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}))

\displaystyle=e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})}\chi_{0}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}){\mathcal{F}}[g](\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}),

where we denoted
(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})=2^{-j}(\xi,\eta)
and
{\mathcal{F}}[g](\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})\coloneqq{\mathcal{F}}[f](2^{j}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}))
.
Consequently, it follows from (
A.3
) that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\chi_{j}(D)f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle=2^{2j}\left\|e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\chi_{0}(D)g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim 2^{2j}|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\chi_{0}(D)g\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

(A.4)

\displaystyle=|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\chi_{j}(D)f(2^{-j}(x,y))\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=2^{2j}|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\chi_{j}(D)f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\forall~j\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle(\chi_{0}(D)g)(x,y)=2^{-2j}(\chi_{j}(D)f)(2^{-j}(x,y)).

Summarizing
j
over
\mathbb{Z},
we obtain
(
A.2
).

We now focus on proving (
A.3
). By Inverse Fourier transform, it suffices to show that

K(t,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i(y\,\xi+z\,\eta)+t\mathcal{R}_{3}(\xi,\eta)}\tilde{\chi}_{0}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta

satisfies (here
\tilde{\chi}_{0}={\chi}_{0}+{\chi}_{1}+{\chi}_{-1}
)

\|K(t,y,z)\|_{L^{\infty}_{y,z}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\forall~t\geq 1.

It is convenient to write
K
in the polar coordinate

\xi=r\cos\theta,\quad\eta=r\sin\theta\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{3}(\xi,\eta)=i\,\sin\theta\,\,.

Let us also set
y=\rho\cos\varphi,\,z=\rho\sin\varphi
, then
K
can be written

\displaystyle K(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=K(t,\rho,\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\sin\theta}\,e^{ir\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi)}A(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{0}e^{it\sin\theta}\,e^{ir\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi)}A(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{it\sin\theta}\,e^{ir\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi)}A(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta

\displaystyle\coloneqq K_{1}(t,\rho,\varphi)+K_{2}(t,\rho,\varphi),

where

\tilde{\chi}_{0}(\xi,\eta)=A(r)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Define

B(z)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{irz}A(r)r\,dr\,=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(A(r)r)(z).

We will prove that, for any
(\rho,\varphi)\in(0,+\infty)\times[0,2\pi]

(A.5)

|K_{2}(t,\rho,\varphi)|=\bigg|\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{it\sin\theta}B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))\,d\theta\bigg|\lesssim|t|^{-1/2},

the proof of
K_{1}
is similar. Note that
B=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(A(r)r)
is a Schwartz function, in particular
B\in W^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}})
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))|\,d\theta=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}B(\rho\cos\theta)|=2\int_{0}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}B(\rho\cos\theta)|\,d\theta=2\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}|B^{\prime}(z)|dz\leq C,

We can apply the 1-d stationary phase arguments to show (
A.5
).
Indeed,
(\sin\theta)^{\prime}=\cos\theta
vanishes at

\theta_{0}=\pi/2,
but
(\sin\theta)^{\prime\prime}=-\sin\theta
does not vanish near
\theta=\theta_{0}.

We thus split
K_{2}=K_{21}+K_{22},
where

\displaystyle K_{21}(t,\rho,\varphi)=\int_{I_{\delta}}\,e^{it\sin\theta}B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))\,d\theta,~~\text{and}~~K_{22}(t,\rho,\varphi)=\int_{[0,\pi]\backslash I_{\delta}}\,e^{it\sin\theta}B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))\,d\theta,

where
I_{\delta}=[\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\,\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta]
with

0<\delta<\pi/2
being small and to be chosen.
On one hand, thanks to
B\in L^{\infty}
,

K_{21}
can be controlled simply by

(A.6)

\displaystyle|K_{21}(t,\cdot,\cdot)|\lesssim\delta,

On the other hand, it holds that on the interval
[0,\pi]\backslash I_{\delta},

|\cos\theta|\geq\sin\delta\geq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Consequently, using the identity
e^{it\sin\theta}=(\frac{\partial_{\theta}}{it\cos\theta})e^{it\sin\theta}
and integrating by parts in
\theta,
we obtain that

(A.7)

\sup_{\varphi\in[0,2\pi]}|K_{21}(t,\rho,\varphi)|\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta t}\sup_{\varphi\in[0,2\pi]}\big(\|B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))\|_{L_{\theta}^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{\theta}B(\rho\cos(\theta-\varphi))\|_{L_{\theta}^{1}}\big)\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta t}.

The estimate (
A.5
) follows from
(
A.6
) and (
A.7
) by choosing
\delta=Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
∎

Remark A.2
.

By density arguments, it is easy to see that (
A.1
) holds as long as

f
is such that the right-hand side is bounded.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{3}=\partial_{z}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-1}
.
It holds that for
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and
for
a=0,1,

(A.8)

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\partial_{y}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-a}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C(1+|\gamma t|)^{-1}\|f\|_{W^{(3-a)^{+},1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

Proof.

The proof follows from similar and easier arguments as in the previous lemma.
It suffices to show

(A.9)

\displaystyle\left\|e^{t\mathcal{R}_{3}}\partial_{y}|\nabla_{y,z}|^{-a}\chi_{j}(D)f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 2^{(3-a)j}|t|^{-1}\|\chi_{j}(D)f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\forall~j\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

which relies on the boundedness of

\displaystyle M(t,\rho,\varphi)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\sin\theta}\cos(\theta)\tilde{B}({\rho}\cos(\theta-\varphi))\,d\theta

where

Thanks to the identity
e^{it\sin\theta}\cos(\theta)=\frac{1}{it}\partial_{\theta}(e^{it\sin\theta}),
we integrate by parts in
\theta
to get

\displaystyle|M(t,\rho,\varphi)|

\displaystyle=\left|t^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{B}({\rho}\cos(\theta-\varphi))\,d\theta\right|\leq t^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}\tilde{B}({\rho}\cos(\theta-\varphi))|\,d\theta

\displaystyle=t^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}\tilde{B}({\rho}\cos\theta)|\,d\theta=2t^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}|\partial_{\theta}\tilde{B}({\rho}\cos\theta)|\,d\theta=2t^{-1}\int_{-{\rho}}^{{\rho}}|\tilde{B}^{\prime}(z)|\,dz\lesssim t^{-1}.

∎

A.3

For any

\displaystyle h=\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}h^{l}(y)e^{ilz},\quad\text{for}~~h^{l}(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{T}h(y,z)e^{-ilz}\,dz.

We also define the corresponding operator
\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}\coloneqq-il(l^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

The semigroup generated by
\mathcal{R}^{l}_{3}
exhibits the following decay estimate:

Lemma A.6
.

For any
l\not=0
, it holds that

(A.17)

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}_{y}({\mathbb{R}})}\leq C(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}_{y}({\mathbb{R}})}+|l|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|h^{l}\|_{W^{(\frac{3}{2})^{+},1}_{y}({\mathbb{R}})},

(A.18)

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(L_{y}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}))}+\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}|l|\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{1}(L_{y}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}))}\leq C\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}.

Proof.

We will use the notations and some results in the proof of [Prop. 3.1] in
[
14
]
. The first inequality was proved in
[
14
]
. Let
P_{j}h^{l}(y)
be the projections
associated to a standard Littlewood-Paley decomposition,
h^{l}=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}P_{j}h^{l}
,
\mathcal{F}(P_{j}h^{l})(\eta)=\varphi_{j}(\eta)\hat{h^{l}}(\eta)
,
\varphi_{j}(\eta)=\varphi(2^{-j}\eta)
.
Then by Young’s convolution inequality, we have

(A.19)

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}(e^{-it\gamma l/|\eta,l|}\varphi(2^{-j}\eta))*h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}(e^{-it\gamma l/|\eta,l|}\varphi(2^{-j}\eta))\|_{L^{\infty}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}}.

Without loss of generality, we assume
l>0
, the case
l<0
can be proved by taking conjugation.
By the change of variables
\eta=l\xi
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}(e^{-it\gamma l/|\eta,l|}\varphi(2^{-j}\eta))(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{iy\eta-it\gamma l/|\eta,l|}\varphi(2^{-j}\eta)d\eta=|l|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{iyl\xi-it\gamma/|\xi,l|}\varphi(2^{-j}l\xi)d\xi=:|l|I(t\gamma,l,j),

It was proved in
[
14
]
that (here
j_{0}:=\log_{2}l-5/2
)

\displaystyle|I(t\gamma,l,j)|\lesssim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\{2^{j}|l|^{-1},(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}&j<j_{0},\\
(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}&j_{0}\leq j\leq j_{0}+4,\\
(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{\frac{3}{2}j}|l|^{-\frac{3}{2}}&j>j_{0}+4.\end{array}\right.

Inserting this into (
A.19
), we find that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|l|\min\{2^{j}|l|^{-1},(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim|l|(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{3}}(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}},\quad j<j_{0},

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|l|(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\lesssim|l|(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{3}}(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}},\quad j_{0}\leq j\leq j_{0}+4,

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|l|(\gamma t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{\frac{3}{2}j}|l|^{-\frac{3}{2}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{1}},\quad j>j_{0}+4.

Then by the standard
TT^{\ast}
method, one can obtain that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{6}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim|l|^{\frac{1}{2}}(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j\leq j_{0}+4,

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{4}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim 2^{\frac{3}{4}j}|l|^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j>j_{0}+4.

These estimates lead to that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\leq\|e^{-\nu tl^{2}}\|_{L_{t}^{3}}\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{6}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim|\nu l^{2}|^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{\frac{1}{2}}(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j\leq j_{0}+4,

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim\|e^{-c\nu t2^{2j}}\|_{L_{t}^{4}}\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{4}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim|\nu 2^{2j}|^{-\frac{1}{4}}2^{\frac{3}{4}j}|l|^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j>j_{0}+4.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim 2^{j/2}\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim 2^{j/2}e^{-c\nu t2^{2j}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim\|2^{j/2}e^{-c\nu t2^{2j}}\|_{L_{t}^{2}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\lesssim\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}.

In summary, we achieve that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j\leq j_{0}+4,

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim\min\{\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}2^{\frac{j}{4}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{4}},\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad j>j_{0}+4.

These two estimates above yield that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}P_{j}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq j_{0}+4}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}(2^{j}|l|^{-1})^{\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\min\{\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}2^{\frac{j}{4}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{4}},\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\sum_{2^{j}\leq|l/\nu|^{1/3}}\nu^{-\frac{1}{4}}2^{\frac{j}{4}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\sum_{2^{j}\geq|l/\nu|^{1/3}}\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle~\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}.

Using the above inequality with
\nu
replaced by
\nu/2
, it is easy to observe that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{1}L_{y}^{\infty}}\leq\|e^{-\nu tl^{2}/2}\|_{L_{t}^{2}}\|e^{\gamma t\mathcal{R}_{3}^{l}+\nu t(\partial_{y}^{2}-l^{2})/2}h^{l}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim|\nu l^{2}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{-\frac{1}{6}}\|h^{l}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},

which completes the proof.
∎
