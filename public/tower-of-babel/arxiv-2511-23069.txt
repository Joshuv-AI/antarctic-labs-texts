Quantifying the Spatial and Demographic Scales of Segregation
Rohit Sahasrabuddhe, Renaud Lambiotte

Abstract
Cities around the world exhibit residential segregation along ethnic, religious, socioeconomic, and other social divides. As high-resolution demographic and spatial data become widely available, decomposable measures have become essential tools for understanding the multi-scale structure of segregation. In this paper, we introduce a framework that quantifies how much segregation is expressed at different spatial and demographic scales. Extending existing spatial decompositions, our approach also measures the internal segregation of broad demographic groups, enabling a joint assessment of geographic and demographic structure. We illustrate the usefulness of this framework with a case study of ethnic residential segregation in England and Wales. Our methods provide a flexible, general tool for identifying the scales at which segregation operates and for guiding multi-scale models of urban systems.

Segregation through information theory

We use
X
to denote the random variable that takes the value of an individual’s demographic label. The demographic composition of a population is
P_{X}
, a probability distribution over the set of demographic identities
\mathcal{X}
.
P_{X}(x)
is the probability that a randomly chosen individual belongs to demographic group
x\in\mathcal{X}
. How diverse is the population? Shannon entropy
47

H(P_{X})
is a measure of the uncertainty in
X
and can be interpreted as the amount of information required to transmit
P_{X}

H(P_{X})=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(x)\ln P_{X}(x)\in\left[\,0,\ln|\mathcal{X}|\,\right],

(1)

where we set
0\ln 0=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}x\ln x=0
. When all the individuals have the same demographic identity,
X
is deterministic and
H(P_{X})=0
. At the other extreme,
H(P_{X})
is maximised when the population is distributed evenly into all the demographic groups. We define diversity as
D(P_{X})=\exp(H(P_{X}))
, which can be interpreted as the effective number of demographic groups in the population
20
(see Methods).

How different are the demographic compositions of two populations? Relative entropy or Kullback-Leibler divergence
29

\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}}\right)
measures how dissimilar
P_{X_{1}}
is from reference distribution
P_{X_{2}}
. It quantifies how unexpected an outcome of
X_{1}
if we were expecting to observe
X_{2}
(see Methods).

\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}}\right)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X_{1}}(x)\ln\frac{P_{X_{1}}(x)}{P_{X_{2}}(x)}\in\left[\,0,\infty\right),

(2)

where we assume
P_{X_{2}}(x)>0
. Divergence is
0

4
Discussion

In this work, we develop a methodology to quantify and explore the interplay of spatial and demographic scales in segregation. Building on previous work on spatial decompositions
45
;
8
, our demographic decomposition separates within and between-group segregation for coarse-grained demographic groups. These methods allow us to identify regions and broad demographic groups that are internally segregated. With a case study on residential ethnic segregation in England and Wales, we demonstrate that our methods can distinguish between regions with similar diversity but different levels of segregation. Within the district of Croydon, our demographic decomposition identifies
Asians
and
Whites
as being more internally segregated than the
Black
group. This exploratory analysis suggests that the methodology is of practical use in characterising the spatial and demographic scales at which segregation operates.

A more detailed study of segregation within England and Wales and extending our analysis to other regions, demographic indicators, and contexts for segregation beyond residential are immediate avenues of further research. Additionally, our work suggests several other research directions. First, our results suggest that the choice of demographic scale depends on the region and vice-versa. The information captured (Eq.
5
) can be used as an objective function to construct bespoke scales from empirical data. Maximising the information of a spatial scale corresponds to identifying demographically homogenous regions with boundaries capturing demographic transitions. While finding optimal regions is not straightforward, the problem can be tackled with a wide array of clustering tools
8
. Maximising the information of a demographic scale corresponds to grouping demographic identities such that individuals within a group tend to be well-mixed. When dealing with multi-dimensional demography data, this can be used to identify which demographic divides are most closely associated with spatial segregation
13
;
25
.

Second, our methodology is applicable to a more general question in the context of clustering – How related are partitions of points to their metadata? For instance, whether the community structure of a network obtained from its topology is correlated with the metadata of its nodes is an important question in network science
41
. Our methods enable a comparison of hierarchical network communities to partitions induced by hierarchical node metadata. Examples of such networks include supply chain networks between firms annotated with Standard Industrial Classification codes and labour mobility networks between occupations annotated with Standard Occupational Classification codes. In the segregation context, this perspective allows us to quantify segregation within systems that are not explicitly spatial, such as labour markets, education, and healthcare.

Third, our methods rest on Shannon entropy and relative entropy, which have many generalisations. For instance, Shannon entropy generalises to Rényi
44
and Havrda-Charvát-Tsallis
19
;
51
entropies, and relative entropy to the notion of Bregman divergences
2
. Exploring the usefulness and physical interpretation of these generalisations is another promising direction of research.

The primary limitation of this study is that our measures are purely descriptive – we do not test for statistical significance. Compare empirical estimates with distributions generated by suitable null models is an important next step. Developing null models is not straightforward. Beyond the simplest formulations, they are system and context-specific and require extensive domain knowledge. A suite of such models would enable hypothesis testing and the statistical inference of bespoke scales.

\mathcal{X}
,
0\leq H(P_{X})\leq\ln|\mathcal{X}|
, with the extreme values corresponding to
P_{X}
being concentrated in one element and the uniform distribution respectively. A crucial advantage of Shannon entropy over other measures of spread is that it follows the chain rule
9
. Equivalently, Shannon entropy is consistent under grouping
7
7

7

More precisely, the chain rule is equivalent to consistency under grouping given symmetry in arguments.
. For a simple example, consider a probability distribution
P_{X}=(p_{1},\,p_{2},\,\dots,\,p_{n})
. If we split
p_{1}
into two outcomes with relative probabilities
q
and
1-q
for
q\in[0,1]
, the Shannon entropy can be written as follows.

H\left((qp_{1},\,(1-q)p_{1},\,p_{2},\,\dots,\,p_{n})\right)=H(P_{X})+p_{1}H\left((q,\,1-q)\right)

This consistency is key to decomposing information into between-group and within-group contributions. Shannon
47
shows that along with continuity and increasing with
|\mathcal{X}|
for a uniform distribution, consistency under grouping characterises Shannon entropy – up to a positive scaling, it is the only function that satisfies these three properties.

Diversity

The diversity
D(P_{X})
of
P_{X}
is the effective number of demographic groups in the population
20
;
31
. Considering
P_{X}(x)
to be the ‘ordinariness’ of demographic group
x
,
1/P_{X}(x)
is a measure of rarity.
D(P_{X})
is the weighted geometric mean of rarity.

D(P_{X})=\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{1}{P_{X}(x)}\right)^{P_{X}(x)}=\exp H(P_{X})\in\left[\,1,|\mathcal{X}|\,\right]

(8)

D(P_{X})=|\mathcal{X}|
for the uniform distribution, indicating that all demographic groups are equally represented. The effective number of groups is lower for imbalanced distributions.
D(P_{X})
is a member of a family of diversity measures called the Hill numbers
20
which are differently sensitive to rare groups.

Cross-diversity and entropy

Cross-diversity measures how diverse a population with demographic composition
P_{X_{1}}
is with respect to a reference distribution
P_{X_{2}}
.
D^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})
is the geometric mean rarity of the population with rarities defined via the reference distribution. Cross-entropy
H^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})
is defined similarly.

\displaystyle D^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{1}{P_{X_{2}}(x)}\right)^{P_{X_{1}}(x)}

(9)

\displaystyle H^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X_{1}}(x)\ln P_{X_{2}}(x)

(10)

\displaystyle=\ln D^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}}),

where we require
P_{X_{1}}(x)=0
if
P_{X_{2}}(x)=0
.

Relative diversity and entropy

Quantifying how much more diverse a population is from its own perspective than from the reference distribution’s perspective leads to the notions of relative diversity
D(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})
and relative entropy
\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}}\right)
(Eq.
2
).

\displaystyle D(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})}{D(P_{X_{1}})}

(11)

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=H^{\times}(P_{X_{1}}||P_{X_{2}})-H(P_{X_{1}})

(12)

In addition to providing its interpretation in the context of diversity, expressing relative entropy in this way shows us that it inherits Shannon entropy’s consistency under grouping. This is essential for our demographic coarse-graining.

5.2
Segregation as information

The information at scale
(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\tilde{\mathcal{S}})
(Eq.
5
) measures how different the regions’ demographic compositions are. When the regions are demographically identical,
I(\tilde{X},\tilde{S})=0
. To find its upper bound, we re-write
I(\tilde{X},\tilde{S})
as follows (see Appendix
B.2
).

I(\tilde{X},\tilde{S})=H(P_{\tilde{X}})-\sum_{\tilde{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}P_{\tilde{S}}(\tilde{s})H\left(P_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\right)

(13)

Since the first term is fixed,
I(\tilde{X},\tilde{S})
is maximised by minimising the diversities of the regions
1
. This corresponds to each region having a single demographic group and
I(\tilde{X},\tilde{S})=H(P_{\tilde{X}})
.

5.3
Spatial decomposition

For a spatial scale, the total information (Eq.
3
) can be decomposed into the amount explained by between-region differences and the residual within-region information (Eq.
6
). We use this decomposition to measure how much a region
\tilde{s}
contributes to explaining information –
P_{\tilde{S}}(\tilde{s})\textnormal{d}\left(P_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\,||\,P_{\tilde{X}}\right)
and how much information it contains –
I^{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}}_{\tilde{s}}
(Eq.
6
). This decomposability is not unique to our definition of information. Relative entropy belongs to a class of functions called Bregman divergences
2
that can be used to define decomposable notions of total information
1
;
8
. Our definition allows us to leverage Shannon entropy’s consistency under grouping to perform a demographic decomposition.

5.4
Demographic decomposition

A demographic scale
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
is a coarse-graining of the demographic identities in
\mathcal{X}
into broad demographic groups
\tilde{x}
.
P_{\tilde{X}}
is the population distribution over the broad demographic groups and
P_{X|\tilde{X}=\tilde{x}}\left(x\right)
is the distribution over identities within
\tilde{x}
.

\displaystyle P_{\tilde{X}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\tilde{x}}P_{X}(x)\textnormal{, and}

(14)

\displaystyle P_{X|\tilde{X}=\tilde{x}}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{P_{X}(x)}{P_{\tilde{X}}(\tilde{x})}&\textnormal{if }x\in\tilde{x}\\
0&\textnormal{otherwise}\end{cases}

(15)

We can decompose the divergence of
P_{X_{1}}
from reference distribution
P_{X_{2}}
into contributions of between-group divergence and the residual within-group divergence (see Appendix
B.1
).

\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}}\right)=\textnormal{d}\left(P_{\tilde{X_{1}}}\,||\,P_{\tilde{X_{2}}}\right)+\sum_{\tilde{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}}P_{\tilde{X_{1}}}(\tilde{x})\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X_{1}|\tilde{X_{1}}=\tilde{x}}\,||\,P_{X_{2}|\tilde{X_{2}}=\tilde{x}}\right)

(16)

Applying this decomposition to
\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\,||\,P_{X}\right)
using the location-specific distributions
P_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}
and
P_{X|\tilde{X}=\tilde{x},\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}
gives us the following.

\begin{split}\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\,||\,P_{X}\right)=&\textnormal{d}\left(P_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\,||\,P_{\tilde{X}}\right)\\
&+\sum_{\tilde{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}}P_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\left(\tilde{x}\right)\textnormal{d}\left(P_{X|\tilde{X}=\tilde{x},\tilde{S}=\tilde{s}}\,||\,P_{X|\tilde{X}=\tilde{x}}\right)\end{split}

(17)

The population-weighted sum over regions gives us the demographic decomposition in Eq.
7
.
