Elevated heart rate is associated with increased mortality and rejection in pediatric heart transplant recipients
Authors: Thomas M Walsh, Humera Ahmed, Ivor B Asztalos, Carley Boyle, Jonathan B Edelson, Jonathan J Edwards, Lynne Ha, Kimberly Y Lin, Katsuhide Maeda, Joseph W Rossano, Carol A Wittlieb-Weber, Matthew J O’Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeElevated heart rate is associated with adverse outcomes in patients with heart failure. Heart transplant recipients often have elevated heart rate; however, the long-term associations of elevated heart rate with clinical outcomes are unknown.MethodsThe records of all pediatric (≤21 years) heart transplant recipients at our institution were reviewed. Patients who died or lacked a clinic visit within 1 year following heart transplant were excluded, as were those with a permanent pacemaker. Heart rate was collected from outpatient electrocardiograms and converted to Z-scores using age-specific normative data. The cohort was divided into elevated and normal heart rate groups, with elevated heart rate defined as an average HR Z-score ≥95th percentile during the first post-transplant year. Outcomes, including survival, rejection, and cardiac allograft vasculopathy were compared between groups.ResultsA total of 110 pediatric heart transplants were included (median age 11.4 years; 62% male). Thirty-six (33%) had an elevated heart rate. The elevated heart rate group was older (15.8 vs 6.1 years, p<0.001) and had shorter follow-up (3.0 vs 4.7 years, p = 0.007). Elevated heart rate was independently associated with increased risk of death (HR 5.44, 95% CI 1.47-20.2, p = 0.011) and a composite of death or rejection (HR 3.06, 95% CI 1.30-7.20, p = 0.010), but not cardiac allograft vasculopathy.ConclusionAmong patients who survive the first year after pediatric heart transplantation, elevated heart rate during the first post-transplant year is independently associated with increased risk of mortality and rejection. Further work is necessary to determine whether pharmacologic modulation mitigates this risk.Keywords: Heart transplantation, children, heart rate, outcomes

There are over 5000 cardiac transplants performed worldwide each year.1 This includes more than 4300 adult transplants and over 600 pediatric transplants.1, 2 Heart transplantation (HT) serves as the established standard of care for end-stage heart disease, conferring improved survival and enhanced quality of life in both adult and pediatric patients. Unfortunately, transplanted hearts have limited longevity, with median graft survival of approximately 12 years in adults and 15 years in children.1, 2

Elevated heart rate (HR) is associated with poor prognosis in both pediatric and adult patients with heart disease.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Elevated HR is associated with increased myocardial oxygen demand,11, 12 reduced diastolic filling time,13, 14 and adverse neurohormonal activation.15, 16 In adult heart failure populations, pharmacologic interventions that reduce HR (e.g., beta-blockers, ivabradine) have demonstrated survival benefit.5, 6, 7, 17 Pharmacologic interventions to reduce HR in the pediatric heart failure population may improve outcomes, but data are sparse.18, 19 However, the transplanted heart has distinct autonomic physiology, and these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to HT recipients.

HT recipients frequently exhibit elevated resting HRs,20, 21 often attributed to surgical denervation of the donor heart.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 While denervation is the main cause, elevated resting HR following transplant can also be influenced by the age of the donor heart,25 incomplete or heterogeneous re‑innervation,26, 27 and cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) or rejection.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 The etiology of elevated HR in the pediatric HT population is likely similar to that of adults. Elevated HR has previously been identified as a predictor of both CAV and mortality in adult HT recipients.29, 30, 31, 32 However, the prognostic significance of elevated HR in pediatric patients remains poorly characterized.

To date, there has been limited investigation into whether elevated HR in pediatric HT recipients is independently associated with adverse post-transplant outcomes. Specifically, the extent to which age-adjusted pediatric HR elevation correlates with outcomes such as rejection, CAV, or mortality is unknown. Additionally, it is unclear whether HR can serve as a simple, noninvasive indicator for identifying higher-risk patients during routine follow-up care.

This study aims to assess whether persistently elevated heart rate during the first post-transplant year, adjusted for age, is associated with adverse long-term outcomes such as mortality, rejection, and CAV in pediatric HT recipients.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and approved as exempt by the Institutional Review Board (IRB #23–020975) with waiver of informed consent. Electronic medical records of all pediatric HT recipients (≤ 21 years of age) between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2023, were reviewed (n=157 records, corresponding to 155 unique patients). This includes multi-organ transplants and retransplants. Patients were excluded if they died within their first-year post-transplant (n=20), were not transplanted at our institution (n=17), did not have 1 year of outpatient follow-up data available at the time of data collection and analysis (n=5), had a permanent pacemaker placed within the first post-transplant year (n=3), or never had a clinic visit during the first post-transplant year (n=2). Patients who died within the first year were excluded to allow for consistent assessment of longitudinal heart rate patterns; however, this approach limits inference to 1-year survivors.

Data collection

Identified patient charts underwent data abstraction. Demographic characteristics, diagnosis leading to transplant, date of re-transplant, date of death, date of last visit, date(s) and type of rejection, date(s) of CAV, dates and types of rate-control medication use, ischemic time of heart, transplant technique, and presence of a permanent pacemaker were recorded on a standardized data form. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiographic data were extracted for all patients. Ventricular rates from all available ECGs were reviewed and restricted to routine outpatient studies performed within 1-year post-transplant. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) measurements from apical 4-chamber views were obtained from outpatient echocardiograms closest to 1 year following transplantation. Diagnoses leading to transplant were grouped as cardiomyopathy (including myocarditis), single ventricle congenital heart disease, biventricular congenital heart disease, re-transplantation, and other. Rejection was defined as the presence of either cellular or antibody-mediated rejection, identified on endomyocardial biopsy and classified according to the criteria established by the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT).33, 34 Episodes of rejection ≥ grade 2R or pAMR2 were included, along with biopsy-negative rejection supported by compelling clinical evidence (hemodynamic compromise). Early rejection was defined as the presence of rejection within the first year of transplant. CAV was considered present when angiographic findings met ISHLT criteria for CAV1 or higher.35 Rate-control medication use was defined as treatment with a beta-blocker, digoxin, or ivabradine after HT. For time-to-event analyses, rate-control exposure was defined as medication use occurring before the relevant event or censoring time to preserve temporal ordering between medication exposure and outcome. For logistic regression analyses, which do not incorporate event timing, rate-control use was defined as any post-transplant exposure during available follow-up. Presence of a pacemaker was defined as an implantable cardiac pacemaker placed for an extended period of time, excluding temporary pacing support in the immediate post-operative period after heart transplantation.

Heart rate measurement, age adjustment, and classification

At our institution, standard outpatient follow-up includes once or twice weekly visits for the first month after hospital discharge, followed by monthly visits for the remainder of the first post-transplant year, and then routine visits every 3 months thereafter. HR data were obtained from standard 12-lead ECGs during outpatient visits in the first post-transplant year (Supplemental Table 1). Given the wide age range of our cohort, HRs were converted to age-adjusted Z-scores using established pediatric and adult normative datasets, accounting for physiologic variation across ages.36, 37 (Supplemental Table 2). Because the reference dataset did not provide parameters describing skewness, central tendency, or variability, Z-scores were calculated by mapping each HR value to its nearest reported percentile and applying the inverse normal transformation, with age-specific SDs estimated from the spacing of adjacent centiles.36 Adult patients (>18 years) were standardized identically using an analogous adult reference dataset.36 These datasets were chosen as they describe highly granular month-to-month variation in HR, which is particularly important in the pediatric population. A mean HR Z-score was calculated for each patient by taking a mean of all the Z-scores calculated in the first-year post-transplant. As such, heart rate was treated as a summary measure over the first post-HT year and was not modeled as a time-varying exposure relative to clinical events. Duplicate HR measurements taken on the same day were identified and removed in the data cleaning process. Patients with an average Z-score greater than or equal to the 95th percentile (Z-score ≥ 1.645) were placed in the elevated HR group and all other patients (Z-score < 1.645) were placed in the normal HR group.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome measure was all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcomes were composites of rejection/death and CAV/death. Follow-up duration was calculated from date of transplant to most recent clinical contact, death, or date of loss of follow-up. At our institution, surveillance biopsy occurs at 2 weeks, 1 month, 2 months, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after transplant, followed by annual catheterizations thereafter. Because heart rate classification was derived from ECG data collected during the first post-HT year, time-to-event analyses were conditioned on survival through the first post-transplant year. Accordingly, Kaplan-Meier, Cox proportional hazards, and logistic regression analyses used the 1-year post-transplant timepoint as cohort entry for outcome analyses.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographics and clinical variables were compared between the elevated HR group and normal HR group using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for survival, freedom from rejection or death, and freedom from CAV or death. The log-rank test was used to compare event-free survival between elevated and normal HR groups. Candidate covariates were identified a priori based on clinical relevance (e.g., age at transplant, early rejection) and supplemented by empirical evaluation using stepwise selection procedures. For each outcome, covariates retained in the final Cox and logistic regression models were those identified as clinically important or selected by the stepwise process based on statistically significant contributions. P-values were obtained from Wald tests. For binary outcomes, logistic regression was used to assess associations with HR group. Cox proportional hazards models were used to account for time-to-event risk, whereas logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations without incorporating time dependence. All models were evaluated to ensure assumptions were met, including proportional hazards for Cox models and linearity of the logit for continuous predictors in logistic models. Analyses were performed using complete-case data; patients with missing values for a given variable were excluded from that specific analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.0 using RStudio version 2025.05.0, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 110 pediatric HTs in 109 patients were included, of which 36 patients (33%) had significantly elevated mean HR during the first post-transplant year and 74 (67%) had a normal HR, based on age-adjusted HR Z-score threshold ≥95th percentile. The median age at transplant was 11.4 years (IQR: 2.2-15.3) and 68 (62%) were male. The elevated HR group was significantly older at time of transplant (15.8 vs 6.1 years, p < 0.001, Table 1). The length of follow-up was shorter in the elevated HR group (3.0 vs 4.7 years, p = 0.007, Table 1), although this was at least partially due to a difference in survival between the elevated HR and normal HR groups. The incidence of retransplantation was also higher in the elevated HR group (8.3% vs 0%, p = 0.033, Table 1). The overall mean heart rate was 109.8 bpm, with similar absolute heart rate values observed between groups. However, HR group classification was based on age-adjusted Z-scores rather than raw HR values, accounting for physiologic differences in expected HR across age groups; mean HR Z-scores were significantly higher in the elevated HR group(Supplementary Table 1). No significant differences by sex, race/ethnicity, mortality, early rejection, CAV, LVEF (apical 4-chamber view) at 1 year, diagnosis leading to transplant, rate control, ischemic time, transplant technique, or presence of a pacemaker were found.

Kaplan-Meier survival models

Overall survival was significantly reduced in patients with elevated HR (log-rank p = 0.016, Fig. 1a). Freedom from death or rejection was also significantly lower in the elevated HR group (log-rank p = 0.0041, Fig. 1b). In contrast, there was no significant difference in freedom from death or CAV (log-rank p = 0.13, Fig. 1c). To assess the contribution of each component outcome, supplementary analyses were performed. Elevated HR was significantly associated with reduced freedom from rejection (log-rank p = 0.005, Supplemental Figure 1a), but not with freedom from CAV (log-rank p = 0.4, Supplemental Figure 1b).

Cox proportional hazards models

In multivariable Cox regression analyses, elevated HR was independently associated with increased hazard of death (HR 5.44, 95% CI 1.47-20.2, p = 0.011) and the composite endpoint of death or rejection (HR 3.06, 95% CI 1.30-7.20, p = 0.010). A sensitivity analysis excluding retransplant and multi-organ transplant recipients demonstrated materially unchanged results, with elevated heart rate remaining significantly associated with mortality and the composite outcome of death or rejection, suggesting that these subgroups were not driving the primary findings. Age at transplant was included as a covariate in all models to account for its potential influence on post-transplant outcomes. In the survival model, rate-control therapy was also associated with increased hazard of death (HR 4.12, 95% CI 1.24-13.8, p = 0.021), while early rejection was not statistically significant (p = 0.2). In the composite model of death or rejection, early rejection was associated with increased hazard of the outcome (HR 2.29, 95% CI 1.07-4.90, p = 0.032), whereas rate-control therapy was not statistically significant (HR 1.57, 95% CI 0.75-3.30, p = 0.2). For the outcome of death or CAV, elevated HR was not significantly associated with increased hazard (HR 1.73, 95% CI 0.62-4.85, p = 0.3). However, both rejection (HR 3.42, 95% CI 1.44-8.11, p = 0.005) and rate control (HR 2.89, 95% CI 1.19-7.01, p = 0.019) were significant predictors. These findings are all summarized in Table 2. When the individual outcome components were analyzed separately, elevated HR remained independently associated with a higher hazard of rejection (HR 2.85, 95% CI 1.13-7.20, p = 0.027), but not with CAV (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.29-3.50, p = 0.9, Supplemental Table 3).

Logistic regression models

To further explore the complex relationships between heart rate group and adverse outcomes, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. Consistent with the Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models, elevated HR was associated with increased odds of mortality (OR 4.58, 95% CI 1.09-22.0, p = 0.043) and death or rejection (OR 2.76, 95% CI 1.01-7.88, p = 0.050).Similarly, age at transplant was included as a covariate in all models to account for its potential influence on post-transplant outcomes. Rate-control therapy was associated with higher odds of mortality (OR 4.69, 95% CI 1.31-18.9, p = 0.021), but not death or rejection (OR 1.95, 95% CI 0.78-5.00, p = 0.2). Early rejection demonstrated a trend toward association with death or rejection (OR 2.62, 95% CI 0.93-7.44, p = 0.067). For the death or CAV model, rejection (OR 14.6, 95% CI 4.47-59.4, p < 0.001) and rate-control therapy (OR 7.70, 95% CI 2.34-31.2, p = 0.002) were significant predictors, while elevated HR was not (OR 1.34, 95% CI 0.37-4.79, p = 0.7). The discriminatory capacity of the models was moderate to low, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.716 for mortality, 0.661 for death or rejection, and 0.856 for death or CAV (Fig. 2). In analyses of the individual component outcomes, elevated HR demonstrated a trend toward association with increased odds of rejection (OR 2.34, 95% CI 0.85-6.64, p = 0.1, Supplemental Table 4), but not CAV (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.16-3.27, p = 0.7, Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of elevated HR after pediatric heart transplantation. We found that persistently elevated HR in the first year after HT was significantly associated with reduced long-term survival and greater risk of death or rejection. These associations remained significant after adjusting for age at transplant and use of rate-control medications, although rate-control therapy itself demonstrated a trend toward association with adverse outcomes. Elevated HR was not a predictor for CAV, suggesting that its prognostic significance is more closely linked to rejection risk rather than coronary vasculopathy. These findings should be interpreted in the context of a 1-year survivor population, as our analysis only included patients who survived the first post-transplant year. The association between rate-control medication use and adverse outcomes may reflect underlying disease severity or arrhythmia burden; however, in our models, this relationship persisted even after adjustment for elevated HR. These findings suggest that persistently elevated age-adjusted resting HR may serve as an early, noninvasive physiologic marker associated with adverse post-transplant outcomes in pediatric patients, similar to previous findings in adult populations. However, given the observational design of this study, elevated HR should be interpreted primarily as a prognostic marker rather than a confirmed modifiable risk factor.

Previous studies in adult HT recipients have demonstrated that elevated resting HR as an independent predictor of both mortality and CAV, even after accounting for conventional risk factors and graft function.29, 30, 31, 32 Elevated HR following transplant can also be influenced by the age of the donor heart,25 incomplete or heterogeneous re‑innervation,26, 27 and CAV or rejection.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 However, the prognostic significance of elevated HR in the pediatric population is limited, likely due to smaller cohort sizes and greater variability in age and developmental physiology. This study represents the first investigation of elevated HR and adverse post-transplant outcomes in the pediatric population. Our results parallel those of the adult population, demonstrating a strong association between elevated HR and increased mortality, while further extending this relationship to encompass an elevated risk of rejection. Although these trends persisted after adjusting for age at transplant and use of rate-controlling medications, the complex interplay between heart rate, medication initiation, and subsequent clinical trajectory remains difficult to fully disentangle, particularly because patients may have started therapy at varying times during the first year. Thus, our findings highlight the potential importance of elevated HR as a physiologic marker; however, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

The observed association between elevated HR and adverse outcomes may reflect several underlying processes rather than a direct causal effect and is likely multifactorial. Persistent sympathetic dominance and loss of vagal modulation following transplantation may contribute to higher resting HR and reduced hemodynamic reserve.20 Chronic immune activation, infection, volume status abnormalities, graft dysfunction, or low-grade graft injury could result in subtle myocardial dysfunction that manifests as sustained tachycardia before overt clinical deterioration.38, 39 Finally, elevated HR itself may promote adverse remodeling and increased myocardial oxygen demand, further exacerbating graft injury over time.28 However, because HR was averaged across the first post-HT year, it is not possible to determine whether elevated HR consistently precedes all subsequent outcomes, and it may reflect underlying physiologic or subclinical processes occurring during the first year following HT.

Importantly, the association between elevated HR and mortality extends beyond transplantation. Numerous studies have demonstrated that higher resting HR predicts adverse outcomes in the general population and in nearly all major cardiovascular diseases.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 This pattern is remarkably consistent across mammalian species, suggesting that the deleterious impact of tachycardia reflects a fundamental biologic principle rather than a disease-specific phenomenon.40

These findings have important implications for post-transplant risk stratification and long-term management. Although elevated HR during the first post-transplant year is often attributed to transient factors such as sedation weans, diastolic dysfunction, or physiologic stress, our results suggest that it may also reflect a persistent risk signal with prognostic significance. Because HR is measured in standard outpatient follow-up, the use of age-adjusted HR Z-scores into routine clinical practice may provide a simple, noninvasive measure to identify pediatric HT recipients at increased risk of mortality or rejection. However, whether heart rate represents a modifiable therapeutic target in this population remains unknown. Use of age-adjusted HR Z-scores allowed us to account for the wide physiologic range in HR across our heterogeneous cohort, ensuring that observed associations with adverse outcomes were not driven by age differences alone.

Rate-control therapy requires cautious interpretation in this cohort. Patients receiving beta-blockers, digoxin, or ivabradine may represent a clinically distinct subgroup with greater arrhythmia burden, graft dysfunction, or other markers of disease severity. In time-to-event models, rate-control exposure was restricted to medication use before the relevant event or censoring time to preserve temporality. Even after accounting for rate-control therapy, elevated HR remained associated with mortality and death or rejection. These findings suggest that elevated HR may capture prognostic information beyond documented rate-control use, but treatment effects cannot be inferred from this retrospective analysis. Our study was not designed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of specific rate-control strategies, and further investigation is needed to determine whether targeted heart rate modulation can improve outcomes in pediatric heart transplant recipients, including whether one specific medication is superior to others.

Interestingly, elevated HR was not associated with an increased incidence of CAV in this study. This contrasts previous adult findings, where elevated HR has been linked to endothelial dysfunction and accelerated vasculopathy.28 However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as CAV often develops later in the post-transplant course. It is possible that our younger cohort and shorter follow-up time limited the ability to detect differences in CAV, which typically develops later in the post-transplant course.41 Alternatively, the mechanisms linking elevated HR and graft loss in pediatric patients may be related to immune or hemodynamic pathways than to structural vascular disease.42 Ongoing longitudinal follow-up will be essential to determine whether elevated HR predicts late CAV development beyond the early post-transplant period.

The strengths of this study include its relatively large single-center pediatric cohort, consistent longitudinal follow-up with availability of post-transition data, the inclusion of both cellular and antibody-mediated rejection, and objective quantification of HR using age-adjusted Z-scores derived from standardized ECG data. This study allows for meaningful comparison across a broad pediatric age range and provides a framework for future multicenter validation. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential confounding factors and limits causal inference. HR measurements were obtained during routine clinical visits and may not fully reflect ambulatory or diurnal variation. Specifically, because heart rate was summarized over the first post-transplant year rather than analyzed as a time-varying exposure, the temporal relationship between heart rate elevation and subsequent outcomes cannot be definitively established. While most patients demonstrated stable heart rate patterns over time, some variability was observed, and changes in heart rate after the first year may introduce residual time-varying confounding. Additionally, by excluding patients who died within the first post-transplant year, this study is subject to survivorship bias. As a result, our findings apply specifically to patients who survived long enough to contribute longitudinal outpatient heart rate data. Although we adjusted for age at transplant, rate-control therapy, and other clinical covariates, unmeasured factors such as graft function, autonomic recovery, or medication adherence may influence our observations. Elevated heart rate may also reflect subclinical rejection, infection, graft dysfunction, or other physiologic stressors occurring during the first post-transplant year rather than serving as a direct causal mediator of adverse outcomes. Additionally, because patients with elevated heart rate demonstrated reduced survival, shorter time at risk may have limited the ability to detect differences in the development of CAV, which typically occurs later in the post-transplant course. Lastly, statistical power may have been limited for detecting differences in infrequent outcomes such as CAV or late mortality.

Future studies should include prospective multicenter analyses incorporating continuous HR monitoring, autonomic testing, and biomarkers of inflammation or endothelial function to better define whether elevated HR primarily reflects underlying disease processes or contributes directly to adverse outcomes. Moreover, interventional trials assessing the safety and efficacy of HR-modulating therapies, such as ivabradine, could determine whether targeted HR control improves long-term graft and patient survival.
