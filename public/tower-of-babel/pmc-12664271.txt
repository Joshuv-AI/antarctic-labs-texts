A preliminary survey on the use of ART among blood donors in Shenzhen China and its implications to blood safety
Authors: Lilin Wang, He Xie, Fang Zhao, Lukun Zhang, Zhengrong Yang, Yizhong Liu, Tong Li, Tingting Chen, Xiaoxuan Xu, Yingnan Dang, Qi Zhang, Zhicheng Wang, Guochao Wei, Jujun Sun, Jun Xi, Chenze Xi, Jinfeng Zeng, Rong Xia, Limin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceThe recent introduction of the AIDS pre-exposure or post-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP/PEP) medication has sparked fear of the possible increase of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and Treponema pallidum (TP) transfusion-transmission risk due to the potential administration of antiretroviral therapy (ART) in blood donors. However, the extent to which ART drugs are currently being utilized by blood donors in China remains to be clarified.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of ART drug use in individuals who attempted to donate blood but were eventually rejected due to anti-HIV and/or anti-Treponema pallidum (anti-TP) reactive in Shenzhen, China and to elucidate the characteristics of these high-risk cohorts using anonymised individual-level data.DesignHigh pressure liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) was used to measure the concentrations of 8 ART drugs in plasma samples collected from the following 5 groups: Group-A (individuals with anti-HIV screened reactive who regularly take ART drugs, n = 3) and their gradient diluted samples, Group-B (repeated blood donors, n = 86), Group-C (anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP non-reactive screened individuals, n = 77), Group-D (anti-HIV non-reactive/anti-TP reactive screened individuals, n = 353) and Group-E (anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP reactive screened individuals, n = 23). And they were all identified from 440 000 screened individuals who came to donate in Shenzhen Blood Center from 2019 to 2023.ResultsNo ART drugs were detected in all the 86 plasma samples from repeated blood donors (Group-B) or in 353 plasma samples from anti-HIV non-reactive/anti-TP reactive screened individuals (Group-D). 3 positive ART drugs (Efavirenz, lamivudine, lopinavir, ritonavir, tenofovir and zidovudine) samples in Group-C (anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP non-reactive, 3 out of 77 screened), and 1 sample with efavirenz, lamivudine and tenofovir in Group-E (anti-HIV reactive/ anti-TP reactive, 1 out of 23 screened) were detected in 1:2 pooled plasma samples. The ART drugs could still be detected even after the pooled plasma was further diluted at 1:6. In addition, HIV RNA was not detectable in one of the 4 ART positive screened individuals.ConclusionART drugs were detected in 4(4/100) anti-HIV reactive plasma samples screened from 440 000 donations in Shenzhen, which indicated some HIV-infected people who take ART drugs donate blood without disclosing their health and medical histories and this may endanger blood safety because ART may compromise current screening strategies by suppressing HIV RNA replication below the detectable level.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12077-w.Keywords: Antiretroviral therapy (ART), Blood donor, Medication rate, Pooled testing

Introduction

Both HIV pre-exposure or post-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP/PEP) drugs are antiretroviral therapy (ART), which has been shown to be effective in blocking HIV infection in multiple trials [1–4]. Although ART cocktails can control HIV efficiently, poorly controlled HIV or breakthrough acute HIV infections do exist in immunocompromised individuals and in those who have poor medication adherence [5–7]. ART drugs have been shown to suppress HIV replication below the detection level of routine NAT assay, potentially leading to false negative results [8–11]. This is particularly alarming in blood donors because some of them still go to donate blood, which may endanger blood safety.

Several studies reported a small percentage of HIV-reactive individuals who take ART-based PrEP/PEP still donate their blood without disclosing their health and medical information. In the most recent report from US blood donors, 46 ART use (15.4%, 95% CI 11.5–20.0%) was detected in 299 HIV confirmed infected screened samples, and 9 ART use (0.6%, 95% CI 0.03–1.1%) was detected in 1,494 first-time male donations [12]. In another pilot study in England, 3 ART+ (6.5%) was detected from 46 confirmed reactive for syphilis screened individuals [13, 14]. Similarly, 122 (9.8%) samples were tested positive for ART screened from 1250 HIV-reactive screened individuals in South African [15].

China reported 1.215 million people infected with HIV, with 408,000 deaths by the end of September 2022, according to the National Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Meta analyses showed that the prevalence of HIV infection was 21.02 per 100,000 persons in blood donors during the period 2010–2017 in China [16]. The anti-HIV reactive rate was almost 2-times higher in first time blood donors (37.93/100,000) than that in repeated donors (20.55/100,000) [17]. Meanwhile, a systematic review highlighted that the prevalence of HIV infection in voluntary blood donors has been increasing in China, especially in those who are MSM (men who have sex with men) [18]. Although China has started to promote PrEP and PEP use for HIV prevention since 2019, and an estimated 36.2–75.6% of MSM are willing to take PrEP [19, 20], the extent to which ART drugs are being used by blood donors in China remains to be incompletely understood. Our current study focuses on surveying the prevalence of ART drug use among anti-HIV reactive individuals who were screened and eventually rejected from those who came to donate their blood in Shenzhen, China. Aims to provide background information for policy-makers to further ensure blood safety.

Materials and methods

Study design and plasma samples

This is a retrospective cohort study including 5 groups: Group-A included 3 anti-HIV reactive individuals (A-1, A-2 and A-3), who were identified when they came to donate blood and they were referred to start ART right after confirmation and regularly took ART (tenofovir, lamivudine, efavirenz) As an ART-positive control group, they have been consistently taking ART combination therapy for more than two years and HIV RNA is well controlled. Plasma samples from repeated blood donors were used to dilute A1, A2 and A3 uniformly at ratios of 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, 1:48, and 1:144 to optimize the appropriate dilution factor for individual testing. 86 repeated blood donors who were screened non-reactive for infectious diseases markers (HIV-1/2, HBV, HCV, Treponema pallidum) and who would not be expected to be reactive for ART drugs were named Group-B, 77 anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP non-reactive study participants were named Group-C, 353 anti-HIV non-reactive/anti-TP reactive screened individuals were named as Group-D, and 23 anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP reactive screened individuals were named Group-E. All these blood samples were collected after screening from 440 000 blood donations in Shenzhen Blood Center (Shenzhen, China) from January 2019 to September 2023. All plasma samples were separated and stored at − 80℃ until ART testing by HPLC–MS/MS.

Serological tests

ELISA-A kit HIV Ag-Ab assay (Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd.) and ELISA-B kit 4th generation Genscreen ULTRA HIV Ag-Ab assay (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) were used to screen for anti-HIV antibody, ELISA-C kit anti-TP assay (Lizhu Pharmaceutical Trading Co., Ltd.) and ELISA-D kit anti-TP assay (DiaSorin S.p.A UK Branch) were used to screen for TP infection. Reactive samples were retested in duplicate by ELISA. Samples tested by ELISA with a signal to cutoff (S/CO) ratio of ≥ 1.0 are reactive, and those with a S/CO ratio of < 1.0 are non-reactive. Reactive samples were retested in duplicate by the same ELISA kits and were considered seronegative if both retests were nonreactive, those with one or two reactive results were considered repeat reactive, and they were classified as the reactive groups. The titer of antibodies was indirectly assessed via the S/CO.

NAT tests

Donor samples were individually screened for HIV-1, HCV, and HBV on the Panther System using Procleix Ultrio Plus Assay (Grifols Diagnostic Solutions Inc., Emeryville, CA, USA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Confirmatory testing

Recombinant immune blot assay (RIBA, version 2.2, MP-Biomedical, Singapore) and Treponema pallidum particle agglutination (TPPA, Fuji Co., Ltd., Japan) are confirmatory test methods used for the confirmation of HIV and TP infection, respectively. For HIV initial ELISA reactive screened individuals, RIBA with antibody bands for at least 2 of 3 gene products of Env bands (gp160/gp41 or gp120), and the core protein (Gag, including p17, p24, p55), or the enzyme protein (Pol, including p31, p51, p66) were considered HIV-antibody confirmatory positive. The anti-TP initial reactive donations were sent to Shenzhen Chronic Disease Prevention for TPPA for confirmation (the result ≥ 1:80 was judged positive).

HPLC-MS/MS detection for ART drugs

Each group of plasma samples were sent to Calibra Scientific Inc. for detection of eight ART drugs by CalQuant-S LC-MS/MS system (Calibra Scientific, China). Plasma ART drugs’ concentrations were measured by using LC-MS/MS with an linear assay range of Abacavir (25.0-5000.0 ng/mL), Efavirenz (40.0-8000.0 ng/mL), Lamivudine (15.0-3000.0 ng/mL), Lopinavir (150.0-30000.0 ng/mL), Nevirapine (100.0-20000.0 ng/mL), Ritonavir (10.0-2000.0 ng/mL), Tenofovir (4.0-800.0 ng/mL), and Zidovudine (20.0-4000.0 ng/mL), respectively. The LC-MS/MS system consists of Applied Biosystems Sciex Triplequad 4500MD triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer, SciexExionLC controller, auto-diagnostics (AD) liquid phase pump, AD column heater and AD autosampler. The agent names of the internal standards used in the HPLC-MS/MS detection are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The details of low quality control (LQC), middle quality control (MQC), and high quality control (HQC) are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Liquid separation was performed using a Disigns Column 005 (Calibra Scientific, China). The correlation coefficients (r) of the calibration curves for each of the eight ART drugs is ≥ 0.995.

Briefly, a protein precipitant containing an isotopic internal standard was added to the plasma samples. The mass spectrometer was operated using a turbo ion-spray ionization source configured for electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive and negative switching ion modes, and the acquisition was conducted using multiple reaction monitoring. An ion-pair method was applied. The mobile phase was composed of A = 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.2% formic acid in water and B = 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.2% formic acid in MeOH at a flow rate of 400 µl/min. The column oven temperature is maintained at 40℃. Gradient elution was adopted with an elution time of 6 min. Data collection was performed with Analyst 1.6.3 software and processed using MultiQuant 3.0.2 software (SCIEX).

Statistical analyses

Data collected in this study were analyzed by SPSS22.0 software and presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and independent samples t tests were performed to compare the differences between groups, whereas differences between two groups were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t-test. The enumeration data were expressed as rate or percentage, and analyzed by chi-square test. Statistical significance was determined using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney test to compare two groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Among the various public health implications of HIV infection, the importance of understanding the use of ART drugs among individuals who intend to donate blood cannot be overstated. Individuals who are infected with HIV with/without taking ART drugs may donate blood without disclosing their HIV status and medication history due to stigma or fear of being discriminated. This situation raises critical blood safety concerns if they are not screened out and rejected for donation [15, 21, 22].

The objective of our current study is to preliminarily survey the prevalence of HIV ART drug use in individuals who came to donate blood but were eventually rejected due to anti-HIV reactive in Southern China. We first established a reliable and cost-effective method to detect the ART drugs in blood donations by HPLC-MS/MS. Plasma samples from three HIV + individuals who routinely took ART drugs (Group A) were used to validate the HPLC-MS/MS detection method. The HPLC-MS/MS detection system exhibits a wide linear detection range for eight ART drugs although the linear range varies considerably for different drugs. For example, the linear detection range for lopinavir is 150 − 30,000 ng/mL, while the linear range for tenofovir is 4.0–800 ng/mL. In addition, the sensitivity for detecting different drugs varies by nearly 40 times, and the difference between the maximum and minimum detection concentrations of the same drug is 200 times. Therefore, in order to balance the sensitivity and cost-effectiveness, it is necessary to determine an optimal sample mixing dilution ratio. By performing serial dilutions of Group A samples, we identified the optimal dilution factor (fold) that meets the minimum detection limit. All three ART drugs in the plasma samples from Group A were successfully detected when diluted between 1:4 and 1:8. Recent advancement in pooling methods for detecting ART drugs in plasma has emerged as a promising solution to the limitations posed by traditional techniques. Pooling methods involve combining samples from multiple patients into a single test, thereby reducing the overall cost and time required for analysis. This approach can be particularly beneficial in resource-limited settings or during public health emergencies, where rapid testing is essential [23]. Research has shown that pooling methods can maintain the same levels of sensitivity and specificity compared to traditional non-pooled methods when appropriately validated. For instance, it has been demonstrated that antiretroviral drugs like remdesivir and favipiravir could be accurately detected in pooled samples without compromising the quality of results, even at low concentrations [24]. Furthermore, appropriate pooling strategies can facilitate large-scale epidemiological studies and therapeutic drug monitoring, allowing for more efficient resource allocation and faster decision-making in clinical settings [25]. The ART detection method we reported here can be used in other regions to survey the prevalence of ART drug use in designated groups of individuals such as in blood donors in order to obtain a more in-depth understanding of this issue, especially its implication in blood safety.

In order to know the prevalence of HIV ART drug use in HIV + individuals who attempted to donate blood, we recruited 100 anti-HIV + blood donations screened from 440 000 blood donors who came to donate in Shenzhen Blood Center during 2019–2023. And these 100 anti-HIV + donations were allocated into two groups, with 77 in Group C (anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP non-reactive) and 23 in Group E (anti-HIV reactive/anti-TP reactive). We also included 86 donations from repeated blood donors (Group B) and 353 anti-HIV non-reactive/anti-TP reactive (Group D) as HIV- control groups (Table 1). At a more conservative dilution factor of 1:2, 3 donations (3/77) in Group-C (anti-HIV+/anti-TP-) and 1 (1/23) in Group-E (anti-HIV+/anti-TP+) were detected as ART positive. As shown in Table 5, these 4 individuals took different combinations of ART drugs and their concentrations vary significantly in their blood, therefore accurate detecting of all these 8 common ARTs is essential for rapid and accurate identification of ART + individuals who attempted to donate blood.

Our results indicated that some HIV + individuals who took ART indeed went to donate their blood in China although it is lower than the 15.4% detection rate of ART + among HIV + individuals who attempted to donate blood reported in the United States in 202012. Additionally, it is lower than the detection rates of 6.5% and 5.6% in TP + individuals who went to donate blood reported by UK teams in 2021 and 2023, respectively [13, 14]. This discrepancy may come from the relatively late adoption of HIV treatment by ART in China and the well-established voluntary, non-remunerated blood donation efforts in Shenzhen, as well as the differences in the size of the study population. A retrospective analysis of over 345,000 blood donations in Brazil found that 0.1% were tested positive for HIV, and 2.4% of those HIV + donations had detectable levels of the ART drug lamivudine, indicating unreported ART drug use among blood donors [26]. Similarly, a study conducted in South Africa reported that 9.8% of individuals with HIV + had undisclosed ART drug use [15]. These findings collectively demonstrated that some HIV + individuals who take ART drugs may donate their blood without disclosing their health and medical background, which may endanger the blood safety if not properly screened out and rejected. Group E stands for co-infected individuals with two distinct sexually transmitted pathogens, and risky sexual behaviors—specifically multiple sexual partners and condom non-adherence—propel syphilis transmission networks, potentially augmenting population-level ART uptake through amplified HIV exposure risk.

All these 4 ART + individuals presented with HIV antibody reactive in the initial screening (by ELISA-A and ELISA-B) and confirmed positive by WB (Table 6). It is of particular interest and alarming to note that one of these 4 individuals who took ART treatment has no HIV RNA detected (ART 1) although his antibody level is similar to that of HIV RNA + ART + individuals. Four ART drugs (Lamivudine, Lopinavir, Ritonavir, and Zidovudine) were detected in the blood of this RNA-ART + person while 2 or 3 drugs were detected in the remaining 3 RNA + ART + individuals. In addition, the concentration of Lamivudine in this RNA-ART + individual was 3.5–26.7 fold higher than that of others. These results may suggest that different combinations and/or high concentrations of ART drugs in the blood may suppress HIV replication to cause RNA undetectable in these individuals, leading to a potential threat to blood safety.

The use of ART drugs by the HIV + individuals who attempted to donate their blood without disclosing medical and medication history may endanger blood safety because these drugs may suppress HIV replication and antigen production below the detection levels of both NAT and ELISA, leading to false negative results. Our current study supports this hypothesis because no HIV RNA was detected in one of the 4 HIV + ART + donations (Table 4). It has been reported that even the third and fourth generation ELISAs, such as ELISA-A and ELISA-B used in this study, and confirmatory WB test failed to detect HIV seroconversion and serological reversion, the disappearance of previously detectable HIV antibodies in plasma [10, 11, 27]. The earlier ART is initiated, the more challenging it becomes to detect HIV RNA/protein and antibodies, which is particularly true for those individuals who use PrEP or PEP [28, 29]. If we could identify which WB bands (HIV proteins) are predominantly affected by the ART drugs, this could serve as a surrogate biomarker to identify those cases with negative results in routine antibody and nucleic acid tests but antiviral drugs are detected and the cases are diagnosed as infected through follow-up. Although a previous study reported the absence of antibody reactivity to pol and gag p39 antigens was associated with acute HIV infection and disease progression, respectively [30], we did not find any report on the disappearance of which WB bands (HIV proteins) were associated with ART drug use. An extensive follow-up study to identify those specific WB bands is necessary to clarify this question.

In addition, long-term ART use forces HIV mutation to benefit its survival and this may further compromise the screening performance of NAT. A sentinel surveillance study in Gabon revealed a 25% prevalence of drug resistance mutations among HIV + individuals who took ART [31]. Therefore, ongoing monitoring of HIV molecular diversity and drug resistant mutations is essential to guide the implementation of donor screening strategies and blood safety policies, particularly in regions with high HIV prevalence and extensive ART drug use [32].

Although the HIV ART drug detection method by HPLC-MS/MS we reported here has been confirmed to be able to identify those individuals who use ART before they attempted to donate blood, some potential limitations of this study should not be ignored. For the small sample size of HIV-reactive individuals and the geographical limitation of Shenzhen, the representativeness of our dataset may not be fully representative. We therefore propose the necessity for future multicenter collaborative research to comprehensively assess the overall prevalence of ART drug use in HIV + individuals who attempt to donate blood and its potential implication for blood safety in China. Although no internationally standardized donor health questionnaire (DHQ) has been established for assessing ART exposure factors, we plan to conduct a future questionnaire survey specifically designed to gather both qualitative and quantitative data on ART adherence and to elucidate the behavioral drivers behind undisclosed ART use, as part of our future research.

In summary, we identified some HIV + individuals who take ART drugs indeed go to donate their blood without disclosing their health and medication information. HIV RNA levels in these donations may be suppressed below the detection limit of NAT leading to false negative results which potentially endanger blood safety if these individuals, especially those who use PrEP/PEP are not properly screened out and rejected.
