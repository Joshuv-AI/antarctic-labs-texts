Combat readiness, alcohol abuse, and posttraumatic stress disorder among Uganda peoples’ defence forces soldiers
Authors: Elias A Baguma, Bives Mutume Nzanzu Vivalya, Patric O Ocen, Rosco Kasujja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis cross-sectional study assessed combat readiness, alcohol abuse and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among Uganda Peoples’ Defense Forces (UPDF) soldiers returning from Operation Lightning Thunder (OLT) in South Sudan and Central African Republic.MethodsOne hundred fifty participants recruited from UPDF soldiers who had recently withdrawn from OLT, and were based in Singo Peace Support Operation School were screened for combat readiness, alcohol abuse and PTSD. The Perceived Combat Readiness Questionnaire, Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test, and PTSD checklist military version were used to measure the variables. Inferential analyses were performed to determine the association between combat readiness, alcohol abuse, and PTSD, while controlling for sociodemographic factors.ResultsWe found that 16% of UPDF soldiers met positive screening results for PTSD, and 21% met the positive screening results for moderate substance use disorder. UPDF soldiers who benefited from combat readiness were less likely to develop PTSD or alcohol abuse. Our results showed a mediating effect of alcohol abuse on combat readiness and PTSD among soldiers with UPDF.ConclusionOur results highlight the high rates of PTSD and alcohol abuse among UPDF soldiers returning from military deployment. These findings provide insight into the role of combat readiness in reducing the risk of alcohol abuse and vulnerability to PTSD in the aftermath of military deployment.Keywords: Combat Readiness, Alcohol abuse, Posttraumatic stress disorder, Soldiers

Background

Combat readiness remains one of the major contributors to success during the deployment of soldiers [1]; it improves skills on how to survive in jungles, endure the heat of armed conflict, and travelling long distances [1–3]. Before deployment, all troops undergo physical training during combat readiness to maintain resilience during a mission [4–9]. Few countries have integrated mental health in pre-deployment preparations, with the exception of western countries like the United States that incorporated mental health services into regular army training, such as the introduction of the Comprehensive Soldier Fitness (CSF) program [5–7]. Little is known about how combat readiness is linked to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and alcohol use disorder in the aftermath of deployment. Studies have suggested that combat readiness reduces the risk of PTSD and maladaptive coping behaviors, including alcohol use disorder [1, 4].

Alcohol use disorder is commonly reported in soldiers who are coming from missions to self-medicate stressful events that occur during combat, while to some soldiers, alcohol is used to celebrate the victory of coming home alive from the mission. Insight is given by the famous Napoleon, who stated that in war, “there is always time for champagne, in victory one deserves it, and in defeat one needs it” [10].

A review of studies conducted among the military population returning from conflict zones revealed high prevalence rates of alcohol use disorder and PTSD compared to non-deployed soldiers and civilian communities [11–13]. Furthermore, high prevalence rates of alcohol use disorder have been reported in veterans in studies conducted in countries such as the US [11–16]. Also, PTSD is reported to be five times more likely in soldiers than in the general population [17, 18]. However, the prevalence rates of PTSD and alcohol use disorder have not been formally established in developing countries such as Uganda, which has been continuously involved in several national and international operations for over 20 years [20], such as Somalia, South Sudan, Central African Republic, and the Democratic Republic of Congo [19].

Given the fact that an adult Ugandan consumes 23.7 L of alcohol annually, and 2.5% of UPDF soldiers met the diagnosis of alcohol use disorder [20], it’s important to know the prevalence of alcohol use disorder and PTSD in active UPDF soldiers, which would inform policy-makers to implement strategies aimed at improving combat readiness, and reduce the likelihood of alcohol use disorder and development of PTSD.

Recent studies showed that the majority of UPDF soldiers returned from military operations with PTSD symptoms with longstanding use of substances including cigarettes and alcohol [21, 22]. However, there is insufficient data on the role of PTSD in alcohol use disorder among deployed UPDF soldiers. The results of the survey are intended to facilitate the design of targeted interventions to reduce the burden of alcohol use disorder and PTSD in UPDF soldiers during and in the aftermath of deployment, and highlight areas of improvement during combat preparations. This cross-sectional study assessed combat readiness, alcohol use disorder, and PTSD among UPDF soldiers returning from Operation Lightning Thunder (OLT) in South Sudan and the Central African Republic.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study used a quantitative approach involving 150 UPDF soldiers of 39 Battalion that had estimated 750 soldiers who had just returned from the OLT in South Sudan and the Central African Republic in January 2018 and were based in Singo Peace Support Operation (PSO), located in Uganda. The UPDF 39 Battalion was selected as the study population because it comprised UPDF soldiers returning from a military operation with reported high levels of combat exposure during this mission.

We enrolled all the UPDF soldiers aged 18 and over who belonged to the 39 Battalion and had been in OLT mission for more than one year, based in Singo Peace Support Operation (PSO), and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were soldiers with active symptoms of mental disorders that could affect their ability to consent and those who had been diagnosed with PTSD and/or alcohol use disorder before being deployed in the army mission.

Procedures

Two research assistants, supervised by the first author, collected data using a structured questionnaire administered by the research assistant through consecutive recruitment and face-to-face interviews. Participants who expressed willingness to participate were asked to provide written informed consent after the research assistants provided details about the study. These details are the reasons for conducting the study, with an emphasis on the study objectives, their rights, and the confidentiality of their responses.

After providing consent, participants were given a semi-structured questionnaire, and research assistants fluent in Luganda and English interpreted the questionnaire based on their preferences.

A pre-test involving five UPDF soldiers of 39 Battalion who had just returned from the OLT in South Sudan and the Central African Republic, who were not included in the analysis was conducted before the beginning of data collection. All data collection tools were written in English, translated into Luganda, and back-translated into English to ensure translation fidelity, accuracy, and reliability based on the feedback obtained from the data collection tool content. All the respondents were educated on their right to participate, confidentiality, and freedom from harm. Those who met the inclusion criteria were given questionnaires, and those who could not read and write were assisted by research assistants. Survey data were collected anonymously. Interviews were conducted in Luganda or English, depending on participants’ preferences.

Variables

The survey was designed for structured questionnaires, and they were based on previous similar studies in the same settings. We collected information on social demographics, combat readiness, levels of posttraumatic stress, and alcohol use disorder. The independent variables included information on age, sex, marital status, level of education, place of residence, rank, and position. Combat readiness was the intervening factor, whereas PTSD and alcohol use disorder were the outcome variables.

Measurements

Alcohol use disorder was assessed using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), developed by the WHO in 1982, with subsequent revisions. It provides an accurate measure of alcohol risk according to gender, age, and culture. The AUDIT had a high internal reliability of r = 0.86. It also had high validity, with a correction efficiency of r = 0.88. It has 10 questions that range from 0 (never) to 4 (daily or almost daily) drinking. The questions were rated as follows: 0 = never, 1 = monthly or less, 2 = weekly, 3 = weekly, 4 = daily, or almost daily. Points scored above 0 on questions 4–6 (especially weekly or daily symptoms) implied mild substance use disorder. Points scored on questions 7–10 indicated moderate alcohol use disorder, and a score of 15 or greater indicates severe alcohol use disorder.

Combat readiness was measured using the Perceived Combat Readiness Questionnaire (PCRQ) adapted by Nkewu [24] from the Combat Readiness Scale (CRS). The PCRQ measures the extent to which a soldier perceives himself as combat-ready’. The questionnaire consists of a five-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from 1 (do not agree at all) to 4 (completely agree) for scoring each item. The questionnaire consisted of 106 items distributed, among which six dimensions were relevant for this study: (i) confidence in oneself (10 items), (ii) confidence in team/unit (11 items), (iii) confidence in leaders (19 items), (iv) weapons and training (12 items), (v) morale and esprit de corps (06 items), (vi) horizontal cohesion (14 items), and (vii) vertical cohesion (34 items). The total questions were 106, and the cumulative PCRQ score ranged between 106 and 424, with a cut-off of 200 interpreted as expressing low levels of combat readiness, 300 as moderate and above 400 as high levels of combat readiness [25].

PTSD was measured using the PTSD Check-List Military version (PCL5-M), a 20-item self-report measure using a 4- Likert scale for all items and assessing the 20 DSM-IV symptoms of PTSD. The PLC-5 has strong psychometric properties, as measured by internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and convergent reliability. It has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 [26]. The total symptom severity score (ranging from 0 to 80) was obtained by summing the scores for each of the 20 items within a given cluster, that is, cluster B (items 1–5), cluster C (items 6–7), cluster D (items 8–14), and cluster E (items 15–20). A positive screening result for PTSD was made by treating each item rated as 2 = “Moderately” or higher as a symptom endorsed. A cut-off point of 36 can be used to indicate positive screening for PTSD, but other supporting assessments are needed to confirm the diagnosis [27].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were summarized as proportions and percentages for categorical variables, and means/standard deviations or median/interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Normality was assessed using statistical tests via graphical interpretation. Inferential analyses were performed to determine the association between combat readiness and PTSD, with alcohol use disorder as a mediating factor. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed combat readiness, alcohol abuse, and PTSD among UPDF soldiers returning from Operation Lightning Thunder (OLT) in South Sudan and the Central African Republic. We found that 16% of UPDF veterans met the positive screening results for PTSD, and 21% met positive screening results for substance use disorder. The prevalence of PTSD among UPDF soldiers ranges in the average of other studies, although this prevalence is higher than the findings of a study conducted in US Iraq War veterans [18] and UK [13, 29], due to methodological variations and cultural, contextual, and demographic differences. In addition, our findings indicate that the prevalence of alcohol abuse 21%, was higher than existing studies carried out in Afghanistan and Iraq and among US military veterans [30, 31]. Alcohol use disorder is commonly reported in soldiers before, during, or in the aftermath of deployment. The prevalence rate has been associated with the prevalence of combat-related PTSD.

Our results also revealed a negative relationship between combat readiness, alcohol abuse, and PTSD in UPDF soldiers. Combat readiness plays a vital role in preventing health threats during army missions by mitigating risks during deployment [32, 33]. Soldiers who were exposed to serious health threats are found to have features of PTSD in the aftermath of combat exposure [34]. Additionally, our findings indicating that alcohol abuse was negatively associated with combat readiness support other studies showing that combat readiness helps in the reduction of alcohol abuse in veterans [11, 13]. Soldiers who benefited from psychological preparedness are mostly likely to have positive coping mechanisms associated with resilience [14].

Similar to previous studies, we found a predictive relationship between alcohol abuse and PTSD [35, 36]. Veterans who were exposed to severe traumatic events during the deployment use alcohol to cope with distressful symptoms of PTSD and alcohol use disorder [19, 37]. Soldiers with longstanding use of alcohol who do not benefit from appropriate combat preparedness were reported to make inappropriate decisions during deployment exposing them to more injuries, death of unit members and PTSD symptoms in the aftermath of combat [29]. Failure to recognize likely ambush sites, and breaking concealment usually leads to unnecessary causalities and fatalities during military operations [38, 39]. We recommend further studies accessing the alcohol use habits in deployed soldiers in Uganda or similar settings, given that this study assessed for alcohol abuse in UPDF soldiers who are coming from a mission. Our results showed that alcohol had a partial mediation between combat readiness and PTSD among UPDF veterans using the indirect path. This finding aligns with other studies [40, 41]. However, the direct path of combat readiness to PTSD remained significant after the control of alcohol use disorder, showing partial mediation of alcohol use disorder between combat readiness and PTSD.

When interpreting our findings, the main limitations such as the cross-sectional design, and the lack of a healthy control group should be considered. Another limitation is that this study did not assess several factors able to influence combat readiness such as the presence of mental health problems or substance use disorder before the deployment. We assessed combat readiness, PTSD, and AUDIT at the same time, making difficult to determine the cause-effect between them. However, we assessed retrospectively combat readiness, while PTSD and AUDIT were assessed at the time of the survey.

Conclusion

We found that PTSD and alcohol use disorder were highly prevalent among UPDF soldiers coming from a mission. Our results also reveal a negative relationship between combat readiness and alcohol use disorder and PTSD, whereas this study showed a positive relationship between alcohol use disorder and PTSD. These findings provide insight into the role of combat readiness in reducing the risk of alcohol use disorder and vulnerability to PTSD in the aftermath of military deployment. Soldiers should not only be trained physically but also psychologically and should be equipped with skills for identifying and dealing with war-related stress and trauma.
