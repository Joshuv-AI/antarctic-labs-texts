A PI(3,5)P2/CHMP4B axis on lysosomes is essential for microautophagic degradation of STING
Authors: Tsumugi Shoji, Ayumi Shinojima, Takuma Kishimoto, Kanako Sato, Nana Ikegami, Eisuke Yumoto, Ruri Shindo, Yasunori Uchida, Satoshi Kusumi, Daisuke Koga, Eiji Yamamoto, Yoshinori Hirano, Ryo Ogino, Hirofumi Shibata, Kazushi Izawa, Takahiro Yasumi, Ryota Sato, Jun Nakayama, Shigeki Higashiyama, Junya Hasegawa, Hiroaki Kajiho, Takehiko Sasaki, Yoshihiko Kuchitsu, Tomohiko Taguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStimulator of interferon genes (STING) is critical for the type I interferon responses to pathogen- or self-derived cytosolic DNA. STING signalling is terminated by ESCRT-driven lysosomal microautophagy. How STING is directly encapsulated by lysosomes has not yet been understood. Here we show that two lysosomal components, a phosphoinositide PI(3,5)P2 and CHMP4B (a subunit of ESCRT-III subcomplex) are essential for STING encapsulation by lysosomes. Liposome sedimentation assay reveals that CHMP4B binds to PI(3,5)P2. The forced recruitment of the catalytic core of Pikfyve (a lipid kinase generating PI(3,5)P2) to early endosomes, recruits a fraction of CHMP4B to early endosomes. CHMP4B mutant, defective in the binding to PI(3,5)P2, cannot restore the microautophagic degradation of STING or the resolution of the STING signalling in cells depleted of Chmp4b. Our results reveal a molecular mechanism that terminates innate immune signalling at the lysosomal membrane.Subject terms: Autophagy, Pattern recognition receptors, Lysosomes

Introduction

Stimulator of interferon genes (STING)1, also known as MITA2/ERIS3/MPYS4/TMEM173, is an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized transmembrane protein that is essential for the control of DNA virus infections and tumor immune surveillance5–7. Upon binding to cyclic GMP-AMP8, which is produced by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase9 in the presence of cytosolic DNA, STING translocates to the trans-Golgi network (TGN), where it triggers type I interferon and proinflammatory responses through the activation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)10–14. STING further translocates to recycling endosomes (REs) and then to lysosomes, so that the STING-triggered signaling is terminated15. We have recently shown that STING degradation is executed by lysosomal microautophagy, in which the limiting membrane of LAMP1-positive compartments (hereinafter referred to as lysosomes) directly encapsulates degradative substrates in the cytosol15,16. We further revealed that this process is mediated by the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT). Mechanistically, encapsulation of STING by lysosomes requires the binding of TSG101 (a subunit of ESCRT-I subcomplex) to K63-polyubiquitinated STING in REs15. How TSG101-bound K63-polyubiquitinated STING is directly encapsulated by lysosomes has not yet been understood.

STING degradation is routinely evaluated by western blotting or fluorescence-activated cell sorter analysis15,17–24. Both methods have some disadvantages. First, as they are not high-throughput methods, chemical library screening cannot be performed. Second, as STING is subjected to K63-linked polyubiquitination and thus becomes smeared in western blotting, the western blot cannot accurately quantify the amount of STING. To circumvent these problems, we have recently established a quantitative assay to monitor STING degradation, exploiting two luciferases, firefly luciferase (FLuc) and Nanoluciferase (NLuc)25. The assay is not affected, in principle, with post-translational modifications of STING and can be performed on a 96-well/384-well plate, enabling high-throughput screening.

In the present study, we exploited the STING degradation assay to screen kinase inhibitors and ESCRT subunits/ESCRT-associated proteins, aiming at identifying lysosomal factors that regulate STING encapsulation by lysosomes.

Results

Kinase inhibitor screening identifies Pikfyve as a potent regulator of STING degradation

We have recently reported a high-throughput assay with two luciferases (NLuc and FLuc), which allows us to quantify STING degradation25. This assay exploits NLuc-tagged STING together with FLuc as an internal control, enabling high-throughput quantification of STING degradation (Fig. 1a). To identify factors other than TSG101 and VPS4A/B that regulate STING microautophagy15, we screened a library of 154 kinase inhibitors with this assay for their ability to interfere with stimulation-dependent STING degradation (Supplementary Data 1).

Cells were stimulated with DMXAA (a membrane-permeable mouse STING agonist) for 18 h in the presence of kinase inhibitors, and the NLuc/FLuc ratio was calculated. We found that 64 kinase inhibitors suppressed STING degradation more effectively than DMSO used as a vehicle (Fig. 1b, c). Among them, staurosporine and YM20163626 strongly inhibited STING degradation, similar to the vacuolar-type H⁺-ATPase inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Baf.A1), which blocks STING degradation19. YM201636 is a selective inhibitor of Pikfyve27, a lipid kinase that generates phosphatidylinositol 3,5-bisphosphate [PI(3,5)P2] from phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate [PI(3)P]. Staurosporine is a non-selective inhibitor of protein kinases; therefore, it was not considered for further analysis.

Apilimod is a highly selective Pikfyve inhibitor28, which was not included in the kinase inhibitor library. As shown (Fig. 1d), apilimod also suppressed STING degradation significantly, suggesting that the enzymatic activity of Pikfyve was required for STING degradation. Apilimod treatment decreased the cellular levels of PI(3,5)P2 by approximately 30%, while conversely increased PI(3)P levels (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1a). DMXAA stimulation did not alter PI(3,5)P₂ levels (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Of note, wortmannin and Vps34-IN1, inhibitors of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinases that generate PI(3)P, did not inhibit STING degradation (Fig. 1b, d). These results suggested that PI(3,5)P2, not its precursor phosphoinositide PI(3)P, is required for STING degradation. These results suggested that the constitutive Pikfyve-PI(3,5)P₂ axis was essential for stimulation-dependent STING degradation.

Pikfyve is required for termination of STING signaling

We next examined the effect of inhibition of Pikfyve on STING signaling. Immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblasts (iMEFs) were stimulated with DMXAA for the indicated times (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). As previously shown15, phosphorylated TBK1 (pTBK1)/phosphorylated STING (pSTING)/phosphorylated IRF3 (pIRF3), the hallmarks of STING activation, emerged 2 h after stimulation, and then diminished. In contrast, in cells treated with YM201636 or apilimod, pTBK1, pSTING, and pIRF3 lingered for up to 8 h after stimulation. In line with the sustained presence of pTBK1/pSTING/pIRF3, the treatment with apilimod resulted in the increased expression of Cxcl10 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3a). RNA-seq analysis showed the upregulation of various proinflammatory genes including Cxcl10 and Il6 (a gene regulated by NF-κB) with apilimod 12 h after stimulation (Fig. 2c). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) treated with apilimod showed the enhanced expression of the STING-downstream genes (Siglec1, Rsad2, and Cxcl10) in response to DNA stimulation (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Importantly, knockdown of Pikfyve in primary MEFs retarded the disappearance of pSTING and pTBK1 (Fig. 2e, f), and the enhanced expression of Cxcl10 and Ccl5 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3c). These results ruled out the possibility that the enhanced STING signaling observed with Pikfyve inhibitors was not due to their off-target effects, and demonstrated that enzymatic activity of Pikfyve was required for termination of STING signaling.

Pikfyve is required for encapsulation of STING-vesicle clusters by lysosomes

The sustained STING signaling in Pikfyve-knockdown cells may be due to impaired STING encapsulation of lysosomes. In this scenario, STING is expected to accumulate outside lysosomes. To examine this, Sting−/− MEFs stably expressing mRuby3-STING and EGFP-tagged LAMP1 (a lysosomal protein) were prepared. To facilitate visualization of STING within lysosomes, STING degradation was inhibited by the addition of lysosomal protease inhibitors (E64d/pepA). The cells were stimulated with DMXAA for 12 h in the presence of lysosomal protease inhibitors, and examined by Airyscan super-resolution microscopy. In line with previous publications15, mRuby3-STING accumulated in lysosomal lumen in vehicle (DMSO)-treated cells (Fig. 3a). In contrast, mRuby3-STING was hardly observed in lysosomal lumen in apilimod-treated cells. These results suggested a defect in lysosomal encapsulation of STING. As reported, apilimod treatment resulted in lysosomal dilation29.

Upon stimulation, STING moves to and is activated at the TGN. STING further translocates to REs, and is loaded into clathrin-coated vesicles22,30. Lysosomes then directly engulf a cluster of STING-positive clathrin-coated vesicles, leading to STING degradation15 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). To examine at which trafficking step STING is arrested under Pikfyve inhibition, we expressed three fluorescence proteins in Sting−/− MEFs [mRuby3-STING, an RE protein transferrin receptor (TfnR) tagged with EGFP, and emiRFP703-clathrin light chain (CLC)], and analyzed their co-localization. The signal of mRuby3-STING mostly diminished 12 h after stimulation in vehicle (DMSO)-treated cells (Fig. 3b). In contrast, the fluorescent signal of mRuby3-STING was largely restored in apilimod-treated cells (Fig. 3b, c), and co-localized with TfnR-EGFP or emiRFP703-CLC (Fig. 3b). The accumulation of STING at the REs/clathrin-positive compartments was also observed in Pikfyve-knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). STING undergoes K63-polyubiquitination at its Lys288 at post-Golgi compartments, most likely at REs15. By co-immunoprecipitation analysis, we found that STING underwent K63-polyubiquitination in apilimod-treated cells (Fig. 3d). The K63-polyubiquitination of STING appeared enhanced in apilimod-treated cells compared to vehicle (DMSO)-treated cells, possibly due to the prolonged residence time of STING in REs/clathrin-coated vesicles that may allow STING to be modified extensively by E3 ubiquitin ligases.

We then exploited three-dimensional correlative light and electron microscopy (3D- CLEM) to analyze the fine membrane structure of STING-positive compartments. The CLEM images of cells treated with apilimod revealed that a cluster of STING-positive compartments next to lysosomes comprised numerous vesicles (Fig. 3e–g and Supplementary Fig. 4a–f). Some clusters contained more than 300 vesicles. Together with the immunofluorescent images (Fig. 3b), these results suggested that Pikfyve inhibition impaired the lysosomal encapsulation process of the STING-vesicle clusters originating from REs.

Although apilimod interfered with lysosomal engulfment process of STING, apilimod did not interfere with amino acid starvation-induced Halo-p62 delivery into lysosomal lumen (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Lysosomes were positive with Magic Red in cells treated with apilimod (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results suggested that PI(3,5)P2 was not required for membrane fusion between lysosomes and autophagosomes, or between lysosomes and endosomes/TGN-derived vesicles that carry lysosomal hydrolases.

CHMP4B, the ESCRT-III component essential for STING degradation, localizes to lysosomes in a PI(3,5)P2-dependent manner

The ESCRT is the evolutionarily conserved multimeric protein complex that deforms various organelle membranes31. The ESCRT in mammals consists of four subcomplexes, ESCRT-0 (HRS, STAM1, and STAM2), ESCRT-I (TSG101, VPS28, VPS37A/B/C/D, MVB12A/B, and UBAP1), ESCRT-II (VPS22, VPS25, and VPS36), and ESCRT-III (CHMP1A/B, CHMP2A/B, CHMP3, CHMP4B/C, CHMP5, CHMP6, CHMP7, and IST1). Given that microautophagic degradation of STING requires the function of the ESCRT15, we hypothesized that some subunits of the ESCRT localize at lysosomes and act as a PI(3,5)P2 effector.

To test this hypothesis, we screened all the mammalian ESCRT and its associated proteins for STING degradation. Family genes, such as Vps37a/b/c/d, were knockdown simultaneously. As a result, we found that knockdown of 10 genes (Tsg101, Vps36, Chmp2a/b, Chmp4b/c, Ist1, Vps4a/b, and Lip5) significantly suppressed the stimulation-dependent STING degradation, compared to knockdown with control siRNA (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6).

We then focused on 5 ESCRT-III genes (Chmp2a/b, Chmp4b/c, and Ist1) out of these 10 genes, given that ESCRT-III forms a neck-like structure on the target membrane and facilitates membrane scission reaction31,32. As shown (Fig. 4b), mRuby3-tagged CHMP4B and CHMP4C co-localized with LAMP1-EGFP, whereas mRuby3-tagged CHMP2A, CHMP2B, and IST1 were diffusively distributed throughout the cytoplasm. Single knockdown of Chmp4b, not Chmp4c, significantly inhibited STING degradation (Fig. 4c), and resulted in the accumulation of mRuby3-STING at REs (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) between CHMP4B-mRuby3 and LAMP1-EGFP was higher than PCC between CHMP4B-mRuby3 and mNeonGreen (mNG)−2xFYVE [a probe for PI(3)P-enriched early endosomes], suggesting predominant localization of CHMP4B to lysosomes (Fig. 4d).

Most importantly, treatment with apilimod, not with Vps34-IN1 (a potent inhibitor of VPS34 that generates PI(3)P in endosomes), abolished the lysosomal localization of CHMP4B (Fig. 4e). The finding was corroborated by super-resolution time-lapse live-cell imaging. Up to 60 min after apilimod treatment, the membrane localization of CHMP4B-mRuby3 was mostly lost (Fig. 4f, g, Supplementary Fig. 8a, b, and 9). Similarly, siPikfyve treatment caused CHMP4B to disperse into the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. 8c), confirming that Pikfyve activity is essential for the lysosomal localization of CHMP4B. The lysosomal localization of CHMP4B-mRuby3 was not affected by knockdown of Tsg101, a subunit of ESCRT-I that is essential for STING encapsulation by lysosomes15 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The result suggested that the lysosomal localization of CHMP4B was independent of the upstream ESCRT complex. In contrast to CHMP4B, TSG101 was recruited to STING-positive structures in CHMP4B-depleted cells (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The lysosomal localization of CHMP4B remained unchanged during STING stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 7d). STING began to show partial co-localization with CHMP4B only around 4 h after stimulation, when STING underwent lysosomal engulfment and degradation (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

Lastly, to directly examine whether PI(3,5)P₂ is sufficient to recruit CHMP4B to cellular membranes, we performed the recruitment experiment of the catalytic core of Pikfyve (PikfyveCC) to early endosomes33. As shown in Fig. 5, rapamycin treatment for 10 min induced the recruitment of PikfyveCC to Rab5-positive early endosomes. CHMP4B-mNG did not localize to Rab5-positive early endosomes before rapamycin treatment (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Importantly, after rapamycin treatment, a fraction of CHMP4B-mNG became to co-localize with Rab5-positive early endosomes together with PikfyveCC (Fig. 5c, d, Supplementary Figs. 10b, 10c). These results provided strong cellular evidence that membrane recruitment of CHMP4B was sufficiently mediated by the interaction with PI(3,5)P2.

CHMP4B binds PI(3,5)P2 through its basic amino acid cluster

We sought to identify domains through which CHMP4B interacted with PI(3,5)P2. To begin with, we analyzed the interaction between CHMP4B and a lipid membrane using coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulations34–36. A three-dimensional structure of CHMP4B was predicted using homology modeling (Supplementary Fig. 11). Ten independent CG-MD simulations, each for 10 μs, were performed with CHMP4B initially positioned in a solution, away from the membrane, and in various orientations. The simulation showed time-dependent variations in the distance between a center of mass of CHMP4B and the membrane, as well as tilt angles of CHMP4B helix (Fig. 6a). In a bulk solution, CHMP4B freely rotated and diffused. In contrast, near the membrane, CHMP4B adopted either an upright orientation (state (i)) or a lying-down orientation (state (ii)) (Fig. 6b). A density map of CHMP4B orientation and distance from the membrane, averaged over the 10 × 10 μs simulations, revealed that the state (i) was a transient membrane-contacting configuration, whereas the state (ii) corresponded to a stable interaction, with CHMP4B adopting a specific orientation on the membrane surface (Fig. 6c).

The simulation (Fig. 6a) also showed time variations of interactions between individual residues of CHMP4B and PI(3,5)P2 (Fig. 6d). Approximately 0.5 μs after the initial membrane contact, a loop region between helices α1 and α2 of CHMP4B interacted with PI(3,5)P2, highlighting the importance of a positively charged residue cluster, including K70, R71, K72, K73, and R74 (Fig. 6b). The contact frequency analysis showed that K60 and K70 exhibited particularly high interaction frequencies with phosphate groups of PI(3,5)P2 (Fig. 6e), emphasizing their essential role in stabilizing CHMP4B on the membrane surface. Of note, this basic amino acid cluster is well conserved among CHMP4B orthologues in different species, such as SHRUB, SNF7, and VPS32 (Supplementary Fig. 12), supporting its evolutionary significance in the recognition of PI(3,5)P2.

Next, we examined the interaction between recombinant CHMP4B and PI(3,5)P2 by liposome co-sedimentation assay (Fig. 6f). Wild-type CHMP4B-mRuby3 bound to PI(3,5)P2 in its concentration-dependent manner: around half of CHMP4B-mRuby3 was recovered in the pellet fraction with liposomes containing 10% PI(3,5)P2. In contrast, a CHMP4B variant (ΔKRKKR) lacking the basic amino acid cluster (K70, R71, K72, K73, and R74) did not bind to PI(3,5)P2-containing liposomes. Wild-type CHMP4B did not bind to liposomes containing 10% PI(3)P (Fig. 6g), in line with the less colocalization of CHMP4B with early endosomes enriched in PI(3)P (Fig. 4d). Wild-type CHMP4B did not bind to liposomes containing 10% phosphatidylserine (PS) (Fig. 6g), an anionic phospholipid, further suggesting the specific affinity of CHMP4B to PI(3,5)P2.

Taken together, these results suggested that CHMP4B bound specifically to an anionic lipid PI(3,5)P₂ through its basic amino acid cluster.

The basic amino acid cluster is essential for CHMP4B function in lysosomal STING microautophagy

We then examined the subcellular localization of CHMP4B (ΔKRKKR). While wild-type CHMP4B localized to lysosomes, CHMP4B (ΔKRKKR) was diffusely distributed in the cytosol and showed significantly reduced co-localization with LAMP1 (Fig. 7a). These results suggested that the basic amino acid cluster in CHMP4B was essential for the lysosomal targeting of CHMP4B.

To assess the significance of the basic amino acid cluster in microautophagic degradation of STING, we performed knockdown-rescue experiments with a siRNA-resistant Chmp4b (WT) and Chmp4b (ΔKRKKR). Knockdown of Chmp4b impaired STING degradation (Fig. 7b–d) and enhanced the signaling (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 13). These phenotypes were rescued by expression of siRNA-resistant Chmp4b (WT), not that of siRNA-resistant Chmp4b (ΔKRKKR) (Fig. 7b–e, and Supplementary Fig. 13). These results suggested that the lysosomal localization of CHMP4B through the basic amino acid cluster was required for the STING encapsulation process by lysosomes.

Discussion

The ESCRT assembles into a multi-subunit machinery that performs a topologically unique membrane bending and scission reaction away from the cytoplasm31. Most notably, the ESCRT is engaged in the formation of multivesicular bodies (MVB) during a maturation process of early endosomes to late endosomes. The intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) of MVB, into which plasma membrane proteins are sorted, are eventually degraded in lysosomes. Mechanistically, PI(3)P in early endosomal membrane recruits HRS, a subunit of ESCRT-0 complex, enabling HRS to direct the downstream ESCRT components onto endosomal membrane. The ESCRT also contributes to lysosomal protein degradation by a process called endosomal microautophagy16. At steady state, TSG101 (a subunit of ESCRT-I) and VPS4 facilitate microautophagic encapsulation of specific cytosolic proteins, such as GAPDH, into late endosomal lumen37. By amino acid deprivation, CHMP4B (a subunit of ESCRT-III) and VPS4, not TSG101, regulate rapid degradation of autophagic receptors including p62/SQSTM138. The contribution of HRS into endosomal microautophagy in both cases has not been clear. Nonetheless, the observation by Mejlvang et al.38 raised a fundamental question as to how CHMP4B is recruited and functions on endosomal membrane without the upstream ESCRT subunit TSG101.

We have previously shown that degradation of an innate immune protein STING was regulated by lysosomal microautophagy15. Knockdown of mammalian orthologues of vacuolar protein sorting (Vps) genes in yeast showed that STING degradation required Vps23 (Tsg101), Vps32 (Chmp4b), Vps4, not Vps27 (Hrs). We also showed that TSG101 was recruited to REs, not to lysosomes/endosomes, through the binding of K63-polyubiquitin chain on K288 of STING15. Therefore, as is the case in endosomal microautophagy induced by amino acid deprivation38, the mechanism by which CHMP4B can function in lysosomal microautophagy of STING has not been clear.

In the present study, we found that CHMP4B localized on lysosomes and was essential for lysosomal encapsulation of STING. Its localization to lysosomes required PI(3,5)P2, not the upstream ESCRT complex or PI(3)P. Thus, these results demonstrated the PI(3,5)P₂-CHMP4B axis for lysosomal microautophagy, contrasting with the PI(3)P-HRS axis for the MVB pathway. How these two pathways (lysosomal microautophagic pathway and the MVB pathway) are selectively utilized for lysosomal degradation is a outstanding question. We assume that the pathway is mainly dictated by organelles where degradative substrate undergoes K63-linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 8). Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a representative cargo of the MVB pathway, undergoes ubiquitination at early endosomes39,40. HRS stays stably on early endosomes and binds to ubiquitinated EGFR, initiating the assembly of the downstream ESCRT complex and facilitating endosomal MVB formation. In contrast, STING moves directly from the Golgi to recycling endosomes without its transit through early endosomes. STING undergoes K63-polyubiquitination at recycling endosomes, where HRS does not localize. Instead of HRS, TSG101 (a cytosolic protein capable of binding to K63-linked ubiquitin), binds to STING. As suggested by the present study, CHMP4B on lysosomes functions in recognizing the STING-TSG101 complex for lysosomal microautophagy. Because CHMP4B does not stably localize on early endosomes, the STING-TSG101 complex may not be able to efficiently activate the downstream ESCRT-III machinery on early endosomes, preventing the complex from being engulfed by the endosomal MVB pathway.

CHMP4B stably localizes on lysosomes through the interaction with PI(3,5)P2, and is involved in lysosomal microautophagic degradation of STING. For comparison, the endosomal MVB pathway is shown. The image was created using Microsoft® PowerPoint for Mac ver.16.79.1.

Several recent reports indicated that mammalian lysosomal microautophagy functions in the degradation of a wide variety of organelles16,41, including the ones not associated with the secretory or endocytic pathways, such as lipid droplets42,43 and inner membrane of mitochondria44. Of note, the degradation of some of them requires TSG101 and VPS4A/B, as that of STING. The contribution of Pikfyve/CHMP4B to their degradation remains to be elucidated.

The size of the endosomal/MVB ILVs is rather small, ranging between 40 and 80 nm45, which highly contrasts with that of lysosomal STING-positive ILVs ranging between 200 and 300 nm15. In reconstitution experiments, yeast ESCRT-III proteins cooperate to form neck structures on liposomes. Intriguingly, the inclusion of SNF7 (CHMP4 in mammals) induces an extension of the neck structures into elongated tubes46. These findings suggested that specific combinations of ESCRT-III proteins can generate distinct types of neck structures. CHMP2A/B, CHMP4A/B, and IST1, the five components that are engaged in STING degradation, may cooperate to form a neck structure in the lysosomal membrane, distinct from that formed in the EE membrane. In addition, our siRNA screen identified Vps36 (ESCRT-II) as another factor required for STING degradation. Together with the results that CHMP4B is recruited to lysosomes independently of TSG101 (Supplementary Fig. 7b), these findings raise the possibility that ESCRT-II functions upstream by bridging the STING-TSG101 cluster to CHMP4B, thereby coordinating cargo recognition with the formation of a PI(3,5)P₂-dependent neck structure in the lysosomal membrane.

Although apilimod interfered with lysosomal engulfment process of STING, apilimod did not interfere with amino acid starvation-induced Halo-p62 delivery into lysosomal lumen (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Lysosomes were positive with Magic Red in cells treated with apilimod (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results suggested that PI(3,5)P2 was selectively required for ESCRT-dependent lysosomal membrane fission, not for membrane fusion between lysosomes and autophagosomes, or between lysosomes and endosomes/TGN-derived vesicles that carry lysosomal hydrolases.

PI(3,5)P₂ is essential for maintaining the morphology and intracellular location of endosomes/lysosomes, regulating the membrane trafficking through them, and cellular ion homeostasis47. These functions are achieved through several effector proteins that bind to PI(3,5)P₂, including a motor protein KIF1A48, ATG1849, an ion channel TRPML150,51. We assume that these PI(3,5)P₂ effectors, in addition to CHMP4B, facilitate microautophagy, for example, through the regulation of lysosomal location towards the perinuclear region where TSG101-bound STING mainly localizes. Several studies have demonstrated that STING can be released as extracellular vesicles (EVs)52,53. In some cell types in which KIF1A activity is dominant, STING-loaded lysosomes may be preferentially delivered to the cell periphery, at which STING is released to the extracellular space48.

Although PI(3,5)P₂ accounts for only ~0.1% of total phosphoinositide in various cell types, it exerts disproportionate biological effects47. In mice, Pikfyve knockout leads to early embryonic lethality54, and tissue-specific knockouts reveal critical roles in myeloid, oligodendrocytes, muscle, and intestine cells47,55. Pikfyve is thought to exist as a large multi-protein complex with two accessory proteins, VAC1456 and FIG457. Recent studies have revealed the involvement of Pikfyve/Vac14/Fig4 in human diseases47. Loss-of-function Fig4 mutations are involved in the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), primary lateral sclerosis, and Charcot-Marie-Tooth disorder. Loss-of-function mutations in Vac14 lead to a progressive early-onset neurological disease. Loss-of-function mutation in Pikfyve causes Francois-Mouchetee Fleck corneal dystrophy58–60 and congenital cataracts61. Given the diverse engagement of STING in inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases6,7, including TDP-43-62 or C9orf72-mediated ALS63, the prolonged STING signaling under Pikfyve inhibition may contribute to the pathogenesis of these Pikfyve-related diseases.

Cancer immunotherapy has transformed the treatment of cancer, with a success of immune checkpoint inhibitors of programmed cell death 1 and its ligand64. The cGAS-STING pathway has emerged as a critical intrinsic tumor-detecting mechanism5,65,66. Various small chemicals including cGAMP derivatives have been developed, and they show a significant anti-tumor activity in mice67. A very recent study showed that Pikfyve is essential for progression of precursor pancreatic intra-epithelial neoplasia lesions to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and that pharmacological inhibition of Pikfyve blocks pancreatic cancer progression in vivo68. As discussed above, the prolonged STING signaling under Pikfyve inhibition may also contribute, at least in part, to the enhancement of the anti-tumuor activity of immune cells. The present study suggested that Pikfyve inhibitors could be utilized to improve the efficacy of STING cancer immunotherapy by sustaining STING-mediated innate immune activation.

Methods

Ethical approval

MEFs from mice were collected according to ethics number PA17-84 approved by the Institute of Medical Sciences of the University of Tokyo. All experiments involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the ethics committee of Kyoto University Hospital (protocol number: G1233). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants before sampling. No compensation was provided.

Reagents

Antibodies used in this study are shown in Supplementary Data 2. The following reagents were purchased from the manufacturers as noted: DMXAA (14617, Cayman), bafilomycin A1 (11038, Cayman), brefeldin A (11861, Cayman), Custom Kinase Screening Library (9003376, Cayman), apilimod (HY-14644, MCH MedChemExpress Co.), Vps34- IN1(17392, Cayman), E64d (4321, Peptide Institute), pepstatin A (4397, Peptide Institute), anti-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (A2220, Sigma), HT-DNA (D6898, Sigma), and rapamycin (13346, Cayman). See also Supplementary Data 2.

Cell culture

MEFs were obtained from embryos of WT or Sting−/− mice at E13.5 and immortalized with SV40 Large T antigen. MEFs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine (PSG) in a 5% CO2 incubator. MEFs stably expressing the tagged proteins were established using retroviruses. Plat-E cells were transfected with pMXs vectors and the medium containing the retrovirus was collected. MEFs were incubated with the medium and then selected with puromycin (2 µg ml−1), blasticidin (4 µg ml−1), or hygromycin B (100 µg ml−1) for several days. RAW-Lucia ISG-KO-STING Cells (InvivoGen) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, normocin (100 µg ml−1), and PSG.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were seeded on coverslips (13 mm No.1 S, MATSUNAMI), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS at room temperature for 15 min, and permeabilized with digitonin (50 µg ml−1) in PBS or 0.1% TritonX-100 in PBS at room temperature for 5 min. After blocking with 3% BSA in PBS, cells were incubated with primary antibodies followed by secondary antibodies at room temperature for 45 min. When necessary, cells were stained with DAPI. Cells were then mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (P36984, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Confocal microscopy

Confocal microscopy was performed using LSM880 with Airyscan (Zeiss) with 20 × 0.8 Plan-Apochromat dry lens, 63 × 1.4 Plan-Apochromat oil immersion lens or 100 × 1.46 alpha-Plan-Apochromat oil immersion lens. Images were analyzed and processed with Zeiss ZEN 2.3 SP1 FP3 (black, 64 bit) (version 14.0.21.201) and Fiji (version 2.9.0/1.54 h). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was quantified by BIOP JACoP in Fiji plugin. Data shown are representative of three independent experiments.

Nano-Glo® Dual-Luciferase® Reporter assay

Cells in culture medium were lysed using ONE-GloTM EX Reagent, in a volume equal to the culture medium, at room temperature. After the measurement of the luminescence of FLuc, an equal volume of NanoDLRTM Stop & Glo® Reagent was added to quench the FLuc signal, and the luminescence of NLuc was measured with GloMax Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega, version 3.1.0). The luminescence value of NLuc was divided by that of FLuc to obtain the NLuc/FLuc ratio. In Fig. 1a, the NLuc/FLuc ratios obtained from cells treated with inhibitors were normalized to the averaged NLuc/FLuc ratio obtained from cells treated with DMSO. In Figs. 1c, 4a, c and 6b, the NLuc/FLuc ratios obtained from cells stimulated with DMXAA and the corresponding inhibitor or siRNA were normalized to the averaged NLuc/FLuc ratio obtained from unstimulated cells treated with the corresponding inhibitor or siRNA.

Plasmids

Complementary DNAs (cDNAs) encoding mouse Sting, mouse Chmp2a, mouse Chmp2b, mouse Chmp4b, mouse Chmp4c, and mouse Ist1 were amplified by PCR with mouse cDNA library. The amplified products were inserted into pMX-IPuro-NLuc-MCS-P2A- FLuc, pMX-IPuro-MCS-NLuc-P2A-FLuc, pMX-IPuro-FLuc-P2A-NLuc-MCS, pMX- IPuro-FLuc-P2A-MCS-NLuc, or pMXs-IBla-MCS-mRuby3. The plasmids encoding NLuc and FLuc were purchased from Promega. iRFP-FRB-Rab5 and mRFP-FKBP-PikfyveCC were kind gifts from Dr. Tamas Balla (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA).

Type I interferon bioassay

Cell culture supernatants of MEFs stimulated with DMXAA were added to Raw264.7- Lucia ISG-KO-STING Cells (Invivogen). Ten hours after incubation, the luciferase activity was measured by GloMax Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega, version 3.1.0).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using SuperPrep II (TOYOBO) or ISOGEN-II (Nippon gene), and reverse-transcribed using ReverTraAce qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using KOD SYBR qPCR (TOYOBO) and LightCycler 96 (Roche). Target gene expression was normalized to the expression of Gapdh.

Apilimod treatment and HT-DNA stimulation of human PBMCs

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy adult donors were prepared by density gradient centrifugation of whole blood samples. A total of 1.0 × 10⁶ cells were plated per well in a 24-well plate and treated with Apilimod (company name) at concentrations of 10 nM, 100 nM, and 1 µM. Two hours following the administration of Apilimod, the cells were stimulated with HT-DNA (2 µg ml−1) using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, the cells were harvested for analysis 24 h later. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ IV 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Mix (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the cDNA and the TaqManTM Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR was performed using the StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). TaqMan probes for Rsad2 (Hs01057264_m1), Siglec1 (Hs00988063_m1), and Gapdh (Hs99999905_m1) were used. The expression of the target genes (Siglec1 and Rsad2) was normalized on the basis of the Gapdh content.

Western blotting

Proteins were separated in 10% (Fig. 2f) or 12% (Figs. 2a, 3d, 7d, and Supplementary Fig. 2a) polyacrylamide gel and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). These membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by secondary antibodies conjugated to peroxidase. The proteins were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence using Fusion SOLO.7S.EDGE (Vilber- Lourmat). Data shown are representative of three independent experiments (Figs. 2a, f, 3d, 7d and Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and scraped in immunoprecipitation buffer composed of 50 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.2), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque, 25955, dilution 1:100) and phosphatase inhibitors (8 mM NaF, 12 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1.2 mM Na2MoO4, 5 mM cantharidin, and 2 mM imidazole). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 21,130g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resultant supernatants were pre-cleared with Ig- Accept Protein G (Nacalai Tesque) at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysates were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel. The beads were washed four times with immunoprecipitation wash buffer (50 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.2), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Triton X-100) and eluted with the Laemmli sample buffer.

RNA interference

siRNAs used in the present study were purchased from Dharmacon or Thermo Fisher Scientific. Cells were transfected with siRNAs (5 nM or 20 nM) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

3D-CLEM

Cells were cultured on coverslips with 150 μm grids (Matsunami Glass Ind.), and then fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 24 h at 4 °C, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (0.1 M PB) for 1 h at 4 °C, and rinsed with Milli-Q water. The cells were then stained with a 1% aqueous solution of uranyl acetate for 1 h, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, embedded in Epoxy resin, and incubated for 48 h at 60 °C. After resin polymerization, the coverslip was removed from the resin on a hot plate for 1 min at 100 °C. Cells previously imaged by light microscopy were identified by their position on the grid. A 0.5 mm2-area containing cells of interest was trimmed with a razor blade. 150 consecutive ultrathin sections (60 nm thickness) were cut with an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica) using a diamond knife (Diatome), mounted onto a glass slide, stained with a 1% aqueous solution of uranyl acetate for 10 min and with a 1% aqueous solution of lead citrate for 5 min. Backscattered electron images of these serial sections were obtained with a scanning electron microscope (SU-70, Hitachi). CLEM images were constructed using image processing software (Photoshop CC, Adobe Systems Inc.). 3D rendering of the region of interest was performed with Amira 3D (Thermo Fisher Scientific)69.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted by primary MEFs. RNA sequencing was performed using Novaseq 6000 (Illumina, Inc., CA, USA) by the NGS core facility at the Research Institute for Microbial Diseases of Osaka University. Estimation of expression counts was executed by kallisto (ver. 0.50.1)39 using “Mus_musculus.GRcm38.cdna.all.fa”. The expression counts were normalized as scaled transcript per million (TPM) by tximport package (ver. 1.26.1) in R (ver. 4.3.2). Heatmap was drawn using Morpheus (Broad Institute, https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/). Hierarchical clustering was performed by average methods with one minus Pearson correlation. Color scale was adjusted by the relative color scheme methods “Subtract row mean, divide by row standard deviation” in Morpheus.

Measurement of PI(3)P and PI(3,5)P2

Lipids extracted from immortalized MEFs were isolated using a modified Bligh-Dyer method. Phosphoinositides were pre-concentrated by open column chromatography using DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow (GE Healthcare). Briefly, the crude lipid extract was applied to the column, washed with chloroform/methanol (1/1) and chloroform/methanol/28% aqueous ammonia/glacial acetic acid (200/100/3/0.9), and eluted with chloroform/methanol/12 M hydrochloric acid/ultrapure water (12/12/1/1). The eluate was mixed with 120 mM NaCl, centrifuged at 1200g for 4 min at room temperature, and the lower phase was collected as the purified phosphoinositide fraction.

Purified phosphoinositides were derivatized by methylation with 150 µL of 0.6 M trimethylsilyl diazomethane at room temperature for 10 min. The reaction was quenched by adding 20 µL of glacial acetic acid. The samples were then mixed with 700 µL of methanol/ultrapure water/chloroform (48/47/3) and vortexed for 1 min. After centrifugation at 1200g for 4 min, the lower phase was dried under a stream of nitrogen and redissolved in 100 µL of acetonitrile.

Lipid samples were subjected to phosphoinositide regioisomer measurement by chiral column chromatography and mass spectrometry (PRMC-MS) using a QTRAP6500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (ABSciex) coupled to a Nexera HPLC and autosampler system (Shimadzu)70. 10 µL of each sample was injected, and lipids were separated on a CHIRALPAK IC-3 column (2.1 mm × 250 mm, 3 µm; DAICEL) at 22 °C. The mobile phases consisted of methanol containing 5 mM ammonium acetate (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 5 mM ammonium acetate (mobile phase B). Separation was achieved at a flow rate of 100 µL/min using the following gradient: the initial conditions of 40% A/60% B were maintained for 1 min, then mobile phase A was increased linearly to 85% over the next 2 min, and held at 85% A for an additional 11 min. Data acquisition and peak integration were performed using Analyst 1.6.3 and MultiQuant (both SCIEX), respectively. Concentrations of PI(3)P and PI(3,5)P2 were calculated based on the C37:4 species of the corresponding phosphoinositides (C37:4 PI(3)P and C37:4 PI(3,5)P2), which had been added to the cell pellets as internal standards prior to lipid extraction.

Molecular dynamics simulations

To construct the CHMP4B structure, we performed homology modeling using homology model module of MOE software (ver. 2022.02, Chemical Computing Group ULC, Montreal, Canada) based on PDB structures of CHMP1B and CHMP2A (PDB ID 6TZ9 and 7ZCH)71,72 (see Supplementary Fig. 6a). The sequence of CHMP4B was retrieved from UniProt database (UniProt ID: Q9H444). Coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulations of protein/membrane complex systems were performed using GROMACS 202173 with the Martini 2.2 force field74,75. The protein-protein Lenard Jones interaction strength was scaled down by 7%76.

To examine the conformation of the protein in solution, we first performed CG-MD simulations of the protein in a cubic box with a size of approximately 21 nm for 3 μs. The systems were solvated with ∼74, 000 CG water, and NaCl ions at 150 mM were added to neutralize the system. Supplementary Fig. 6d shows the time variation of the radius of gyration (Rg) for the protein modeled based on 6TZ9 or 7ZCH. In both CG-MD simulations, based on different structural models, Rg decreased, resulting in compact and stable structures. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6b, c, the charge distribution and hydrophobicity contribute to the formation of a coiled and compact protein conformation in solution.

To investigate protein-membrane interactions, we performed CG-MD simulations of CHMP4B with a lipid membrane system. The symmetric bilayer used in the simulations consisted of 450 POPC, 200 POPE, 20 POPS, 150 sphingomyelin (POSM), 100 POPI, 10 PI(3,5)P2, and 70 cholesterol molecules. The systems were solvated with ∼ 54,000 CG water, and NaCl ions at 150 mM were added to neutralize the system. The box size was Lx = Ly = 17 nm and Lz = 27 nm. A total of 10 × 10 μs simulations were conducted with a time step of 20 fs, starting with the protein displaced away from the membrane surface and in random orientations. Trajectories were saved every 0.1 ns. The results shown in Fig. 5 are based on the protein structure model derived from 6TZ9.

An elastic network was applied to backbone particles within the same helices, using a cutoff distance of 0.7 nm, to maintain the helical structure while allowing variations in inter-helix distances. Van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm using the Potential-shift-Verlet modifier. Coulombic interactions were calculated using the reaction-field method with a cutoff of 1.2 nm and a relative permittivity εr of 15. A pressure of 0.1 MPa and a temperature of 310 K were controlled using velocity-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat.

Protein purification

mRuby3-tagged WT or ΔKRKKR mutant of mCHMP4B cDNA was subcloned into pColdI (Takara Bio), and the N-terminal 6xHis-tagged and C-terminal mRuby3-tagged proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli solu BL21 (Genlantis). The bacterial culture was grown at 30 °C in LB medium (1 L) with 100 µg/ml of ampicillin until the optical density (OD600) reached 0.5-0.8. The expression of mCHMP4B(WT)-mRuby3 or mCHMP4B(ΔKRKKR)-mRuby3 was induced by the addition of isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.1 mM and shifting cells to 15 °C, followed by incubating overnight. The culture was harvested by centrifugation at 1902g for 15 min. Cell lysates were prepared by resuspending the pellet in buffer (20 mM of 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, pH 7.5), 500 mM of NaCl, 20 mM of imidazole, and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Nacalai Tesque) followed by sonication (TOMY). Insoluble protein and cell debris were removed by ultracentrifugation at 48,700g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was applied to TALON® Metal Affinity Resin (Takara Bio, #635502) and eluted with elution buffer (20 mM HEPES, 500 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole). Protein-containing fractions were desalted using PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva) with stock buffer (20 mM HEPES and 150 mM NaCl), and subsequently concentrated using VIVASPIN 20, MWCO, 3000 (Sartorius). After concentration, the protein was aliquoted into tubes and frozen with liquid nitrogen.

Liposome cosedimentation assay

Multilamellar liposomes were prepared by combining 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC, NOF Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with PI(3,5)P2 (Avanti), PI(3)P (Avanti), or 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (DOPS; NOF Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Lipid mixtures were prepared on a molar percentage (mol%) basis. The lipid mixtures were evaporated under a stream of nitrogen gas to form thin film, which was then hydrated with liposome buffer (0.1 M sucrose, 20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, and 1 mM EDTA) and vortexed to generate liposomes. Recombinant mCHMP4B(WT)-mRuby3 or mCHMP4B(ΔKRKKR)-mRuby3 protein was incubated with liposomes in HEPES-buffered saline (pH 7.5) for 30 min at 30 °C. The mixtures were then centrifuged at 20,000g for 30 min at 20 °C. The resulting pellets were washed once with HEPES-buffered saline. The pellets were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. For the quantification of the protein, the stained gel was scanned and analyzed using ImageJ software. Data shown are representative of three independent experiments (Fig. 6f, g).

Statistics and reproducibility

Error bars displayed in bar plots throughout the present study represent the standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise indicated. The SEM was calculated from at least triplicate samples. In box-and-whisker plots, the box bounds the interquartile range divided by the median, two-sided one-way ANOVA analysis of variance followed by Tukey-Kramer post hoc test for multiple comparisons (Figs. 2d, e, 3c, 4d, e, 7a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1b), or Dunnett’s test for multiple comparisons (Figs. 1d, 4c and 7c) with R (version 4.1.2) and KNIME (version 4.5.1); n.s., not significant (P > 0.05). No statistical method was used to pre-determine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
