Assessment of chemotherapy-associated sarcopenia using SARC-F and handgrip strength: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Nabil Almouaalamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Sarcopenia is a significant health concern among patients with cancer who receive chemotherapy due to its potential impacts on treatment tolerance and functional status.Objectives:To explore the association between chemotherapeutic agents and practical sarcopenia markers, including SARC-F scores and grip strength, among oncology outpatients with advanced cancer, and to explore the relationship between chemotherapy exposure (cycle number) and functional muscle decline.Methods:A secondary analysis of a primary cross-sectional dataset of 324 patients who attended outpatient clinics at Princess Noorah Oncology Center, Jeddah. Sarcopenia was assessed via the SARC-F questionnaire and digital grip strength measurement. Associations between chemotherapeutic agents and sarcopenia markers were evaluated using inferential analyses, and agents showing significant bivariate associations were further analyzed using multiple linear regression.Results:Among chemotherapeutic agents, paclitaxel was independently associated with higher SARC-F scores (β = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.26–2.12, P = 0.013) and significantly lower grip strength (median: 28.1 kg vs. 41.3 kg, P < 0.001). Vinblastine showed a negative association with SARC-F in unadjusted analysis (P = 0.025), but not after adjustment (P = 0.269). No significant correlations were found between the number of chemotherapy cycles and either grip strength (R = 0.099, P = 0.212) or SARC-F score (R = 0.066, P = 0.405).Conclusion:Paclitaxel use is an independent predictor of functional decline in cancer patients, highlighting the need for early sarcopenia screening and tailored supportive care during taxane-based chemotherapy.

Introduction

Cancer treatment using chemotherapeutic agents has witnessed significant advancements over the past years, improving survival rates across many malignancies[1]. These regimens have become indispensable for cancer management and inhibiting tumor recurrence. However, the chemotherapeutic approach is often associated with debilitating side effects and toxicities that can influence the quality of life of patients, particularly those with advanced malignancies[2]. One of these unfavorable outcomes is sarcopenia, defined as a reduction of muscle mass associated either with reduced muscle strength or low physical performance[3]. In clinical settings, functional decline owing to sarcopenia may be reliably quantified using tools like the SARC-F questionnaire and hand grip strength (HGS) measurement[4,5]. Physiologically, a decline in muscle mass occurs at a rate of 6% per decade from the age of 40[6], and such a rate accelerates to 25–40% per decade among individuals aged 70 years or more[7]. However, evidence from studies conducted on patients with gastric, esophageal, and pancreatic cancer showed that muscle loss had exceeded the physiological rates by a factor of 30–40[8,9]. These observations have raised concerns regarding the correlation between muscle loss and chemotherapy.

Interestingly, during chemotherapy, patients are at an increased risk of sarcopenia due to several possible reasons, including fatigue-related reduction in physical activity, impaired vitamin D intake, or mucositis-mediated malabsorption. However, experimental studies have revealed that prolonged administration of cisplatin-based chemotherapy is associated with accelerated weight loss without inducing anorexia[10]. As a consequence, it is plausible that there are direct myotoxic effects of specific chemotherapeutic agents[11]. Actually, existing literature highlights that cisplatin, doxorubicin, and paclitaxel may lead to muscle attenuation through systemic and myotoxic mechanisms[12,13]. Moreover, data suggest that chemotherapy dosage intensity may affect the severity and risk of sarcopenia through changes in drug pharmacokinetics[14]. On the other hand, accumulating evidence shows that sarcopenia is not only an indicator of frailty but also an independent predictor of chemotherapy toxicity, prolonged length of hospital stay, and reduced treatment adherence[15,16]. Furthermore, cancer patients with sarcopenia are more vulnerable to toxicities[17], accelerated tumor progression[18] and higher rates of overall mortality[19].

Despite the above-mentioned findings, sarcopenia quantification using simple measures among cancer patients on chemotherapy has not been adequately explored. Rather than imaging-based metrics of muscle quantity, validated sarcopenia screening measures using dedicated tools, such as the SARC-F questionnaire, may offer a practical advantage. Additionally, while previous evidence has shown increased susceptibility to chemotherapy-induced toxicity among patients with low muscle mass at baseline[20], little is known about how individual chemotherapeutic agents correlate with functional muscle decline during treatment. The current study aimed to assess the association between the administered chemotherapeutic agents and deterioration in muscle function using practical sarcopenia markers, particularly grip strength and SARC-F scores, by utilizing real-world clinical data from patients with advanced malignancies. We also explored the correlation between chemotherapy exposure levels (dosage intensity) and sarcopenia markers. The findings of the current study would help identify patients at heightened risk of chemotherapy-induced functional decline and support timely interventions and targeted therapies to preserve muscle integrity and improve patients’ outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

The current study is a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study carried out at princess noorah oncology center (PNOC) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia[21]. PNOC is a tertiary cancer institution located at King Abdulaziz Medical City, and it provides care through 11 adult outpatient clinics and 18 chemotherapy beds for patients with advanced malignancies. This article has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[22].

Study population

Data were retrieved from adult patients with confirmed advanced cancer who visited PNOC outpatient clinics. Eligible patients were those aged 18 years or more who visited the outpatient clinics with available complete records of chemotherapeutic agents received, number of chemotherapy cycles, SARC-F questionnaire scores, and hand grip strength measurements. Patients were excluded if they had a non-oncological diagnosis, missing or incomplete records, or data privacy exemptions.

Sample size and sampling

A convenience sampling approach was employed, where patients were recruited during their routine clinical visits to outpatient clinics. From an estimated population of 2000 outpatients, with 95% confidence intervals and a 5% margin of error, the required sample size was 324 patients. All study participants provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by king Abdullah international medical research centre ethics review board, study number SP20/061/J, on 1 June 2020.

Hand grip strength

HGS was measured in this study using the Jamar® PLUS + Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer (Patterson Medical, Cedarburg, WI, USA), set at the second handle position because of its suitability and reliability[23]. We adopted the American Society of Hand Therapists protocol[24]. Verbal instructions and demonstrations were given by the healthcare worker for each testing procedure before the test was performed. Three successive trials were performed with the dominant hand, with a 2-minute rest period between each.

A simple questionnaire to rapidly diagnose sarcopenia (SARC-F)

The English version of the SARC-F questionnaire was used, which is indicated for the screening of sarcopenia. It consists of five objective questions and is self-reported by the patient in a direct interview. A trained healthcare provider conducted interviews with the patients.

Thresholds and case-finding of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People Consensus 2 (EWGSOP2)[25]. The sex-standardized grip strength cut-off values were 70.5 pounds (32 kg) for men and 48.5 pounds (22 kg) for women, based on regional studies[24,26]. We used the SARC-F questionnaire to identify sarcopenia cases and applied HGS to identify probable sarcopenia cases, as per the EWGSOP2 case-finding algorithm.

Data collection and measurements

Data were readily available from the electronic health record (EHR) system, including: (1) patient-related characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, area of residency, body mass index, and dominant hand; (2) the SARC-F questionnaire score based on the screening of five items (strength, assistance with walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls); and (3) grip strength, measured three times using a digital hand dynamometer (in kg) with the dominant hand and the elbow fully extended. The mean value of the three grip strength readings was used for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). To assess the association between individual chemotherapeutic agents and sarcopenia markers (grip strength and SARC-F scores), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied, comparing median values between patients receiving and not receiving each chemotherapeutic agent. The relationship between the number of chemotherapy cycles and sarcopenia markers was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. For variables that showed significant bivariate associations (P < 0.05), multiple linear regression analysis was performed to determine whether individual chemotherapeutic agents were independently associated with higher SARC-F scores. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio software (version 2024.9.1.394, Boston, MA, USA), running R version 4.4.2. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Demographic characteristics of patients

A total of 324 oncology outpatients were included in the study. The majority of patients were aged ≥51 years (54.9%), followed by those aged 41–50 years (20.4%). Females slightly outnumbered males, comprising 53.1% of the sample. Most participants were ever married (84.0%), and the largest proportion resided in Jeddah (52.5%), followed by Almadina (17.3%) and Makkah (15.4%). In terms of BMI, 34.0% were classified as obese, and 30.9% as overweight. Discussion

The current study assessed the association between specific chemotherapeutic agents and the progression of sarcopenia, as indicated by SARC-F scores and grip strength among patients with advanced cancer. The results of our investigation revealed that PTX was significantly associated with reduced grip strength and higher SARC-F scores. The association with SARC-F scores remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders, indicating an independent relationship between PTX and sarcopenia progression. While there was a negative relationship between vinblastine and SARC-F scores in the univariable analysis, the association was no longer apparent in the multivariable model. Furthermore, the number of chemotherapy cycles was not significantly correlated with sarcopenia markers.

Importantly, the observed association between PTX and sarcopenia markers is supported by findings from the literature, which indicate significant muscle quality deterioration even if measurable muscle mass remains relatively stable. Rier et al[27] assessed body composition and muscle attenuation parameters among breast cancer patients who were undergoing taxane-based chemotherapy. Results showed that muscle attenuation significantly decreased during PTX treatment, while the amount of adipose tissue and muscle mass remained stable. These results were evident regardless of the number of completed treatment cycles[27]. This suggests that muscle quality has been impaired with PTX treatment, possibly leading to functional decline without apparent muscle mass loss. The observed muscle attenuation might be attributed to decreased physical activity, taxane-related neuropathy and myalgia, or previous exposure to adjuvant chemotherapy. Furthermore, the effect of co-administration of corticosteroids might have contributed to muscle attenuation changes in the study by Rier et al[27], since all patients had received concomitant dexamethasone. However, in our study, we did not collect data regarding corticosteroid administration.

Indeed, the above-mentioned findings indicate that PTX could influence muscle integrity at a cellular or structural level rather than disrupting muscle quantity. An experimental study on mice[3,28] has shown that four intraperitoneal injections of PTX, administered once daily every other day, have been associated with reduced grip strength, and these findings were ascribed to the collapse of the striated structure and muscle atrophy. Mechanistically, PTX causes a significant reduction in myosin content in a skeletal muscle model (C2C12 cells) and interferes with muscle cell fusion and differentiation[29]. At the molecular level in muscle cells, PTX leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and increased reactive oxygen species production[30,31]. Collectively, these observations suggest that PTX has myotoxic effects on the cellular, molecular, and structural levels, which would eventually influence muscle function and integrity.

Notably, although evidence showed a short overall survival among cancer patients with low muscle attenuation prior to starting chemotherapy[32–34], the study by Rier et al[27] emphasized no impact of the PTX treatment course on survival, indicating that having pre-established muscle attenuation has different prognostic consequences than the attenuation experienced during the treatment period. Moreover, studies highlighted that having low muscle mass upon treatment initiation is linked to an increased risk of PTX-induced peripheral neuropathy[15,20], which is related to increased maximum PTX plasma concentration[14]. A recent study[14] showed that longer PTX infusion times, from 1 to 2 or 3 hours, have limited the maximum concentration of the drug, thereby reducing neuropathy. Such findings establish the potential for performing effective PTX dosing regimens based on body composition metrics, particularly muscle mass indices, to reduce the likelihood of muscle-related toxicities while improving treatment outcomes. These dosing considerations should also be taken into account for those who are at risk of sarcopenia to avoid further functional decline during treatment.

In the present study, the negative association between vinblastine and SARC-F scores in the univariable analysis was not maintained in the multivariable analysis. Vinblastine has direct cytotoxic effects against cancer cells[35]; however, the relationship with sarcopenia markers has not been extensively studied in the literature. Our finding suggests that the association between vinblastine and low SARC-F scores might be overshadowed by other clinical factors.

Additionally, the lack of a significant correlation between chemotherapy cycles and sarcopenia markers in our study suggested that the cumulative exposure to chemotherapeutic agents was not exclusively associated with an increased risk of muscle attenuation. This highlights the complexity of muscle loss mechanisms, which might be associated with other individual patient characteristics, physical activity levels, nutritional statuses, and inflammatory responses. Sarcopenia-related parameters might also be influenced by chemotherapy dosages, cancer types, and supportive care interventions. This indicates the need for a multifaceted approach when assessing the risk of sarcopenia during cancer treatment.

In general, changes in skeletal muscle function have been reported due to chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, doxorubicin, and FOLFIRI, accounting for a significant reduction in muscle mass, reduced muscle strength, and physical performance indicators, such as chair-rise time and grip strength[36,37]. The symptomatic profile of these changes includes fatigue and joint dysfunction, occurring even before overt muscle atrophy. To counteract the negative effects on muscles, ghrelin and ghrelin receptor agonists have been used for cisplatin-induced muscle wasting[10,38]. The soluble activin receptor blocker ACVR2B/Fc has also shown promising results in preserving muscle mass in preclinical models treated with FOLFIRI or doxorubicin[39,40]. Such anabolic strategies can improve functional and potentially survival outcomes during chemotherapy. Accurate assessment of body composition, rather than reliance on body surface area parameters, is critical to personalize chemotherapy dosing, particularly for patients at risk of sarcopenia and those who would receive PTX-containing regimens. In this context, it is essential to implement pretherapeutic, imaging-based assessment of cancer patients, with targeted anabolic interventions that might provide a comprehensive, proactive approach to preserve muscle function and improve tolerance to chemotherapy.

The current study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The analysis has focused on individual chemotherapeutic agents rather than the effects of combination regimens. This is because specific combinations were scarcely administered to patients, and the analysis of these variables with small samples was not plausible. This limitation might have affected the accurate assessment of potential synergistic or antagonistic effects among agents on sarcopenia progression. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the dataset, along with the lack of time-to-event variables, precluded our ability to longitudinally assess the effect of chemotherapeutic agents on changes in muscular mass over time and overall survival. These variables would have supported the clinical significance of our study. Another important limitation is that we did not assess corticosteroid use among patients, a matter which might interfere with muscle metabolism and should be included in the multivariable model to exclude its effects on the outcomes of sarcopenia parameters. The lack of structured data on chemotherapy dosing and supportive care interventions might also have prominent effects on key variables related to treatment tolerance and muscle strength. Finally, the single-center design might have limited the generalizability of our findings to other institutions and oncology populations with diverse therapeutic protocols and care patterns. Future studies may benefit from the inclusion of a multi-center design, prospective follow-up, and detailed recording of treatment and supportive care in order to better assess the causal relationships between chemotherapy exposure and sarcopenia progression.

Conclusion

The current study assessed the association between chemotherapeutic agents and sarcopenia progression through the assessment of SARC-F and mean grip strength scores among oncology patients with advanced cancer. The findings showed that PTX was an independent predictor of impaired muscle function, as indicated by higher SARC-F scores and low grip strength. These results were evident even after adjustment for potential confounders. Such outcomes support existing evidence regarding the myotoxic effects of PTX, which influence muscle quality regardless of changes in muscle mass. The findings of the current study also showed that vinblastine use was inversely associated with SARC-F scores, but the correlation was no longer significant in the multivariable model. Notably, sarcopenia markers were not significantly affected by the number of chemotherapy cycles, highlighting that muscle deterioration might not be linked to cumulative exposure to chemotherapy.

The aforementioned findings emphasize that, given that muscle attenuation might take place without overt atrophy, early functional assessment using tools like SARC-F and grip strength is paramount to assess muscle deterioration before chemotherapy initiation, particularly among patients who receive taxane-based therapies. Patients may benefit from pharmacologic anabolic interventions, tailored physical exercise regimens, and individualized chemotherapy dosing. Such interventions should be guided by imaging-specific body composition metrics. Future research may incorporate detailed information regarding treatment regimens, supportive care, and corticosteroid exposure to better reveal the causal associations. Eventually, preserving skeletal muscle integrity would not only enhance the quality of life of patients but also improve long-term chemotherapeutic outcomes among patients with advanced malignancies.
