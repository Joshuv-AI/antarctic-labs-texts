Minority Representation in Network Rankings: Methods for Estimation, Testing, and Fairness
Hui Shen, Peter W. MacDonald, Eric D. Kolaczyk

Abstract
Networks, composed of nodes and their connections, are widely used to model complex relationships across various fields. Centrality metrics often inform decisions such as identifying key nodes or prioritizing resources. However, networks frequently suffer from missing or incorrect edges, which can systematically centrality-based decisions and distort the representation of certain protected groups. To address this issue, we introduce a formal definition of minority representation, measured as the proportion of minority nodes among the top-ranked nodes. We model systematic bias against minority groups by using group-dependent missing edge errors. We propose methods to estimate and detect systematic bias. Asymptotic limits of minority representation statistics are derived under canonical network models and used to correct representation of minority groups in node rankings. Simulation results demonstrate the effectiveness of our estimation, testing, and ranking correction procedures, and we apply our methods to a contact network, showcasing their practical applicability. Keywords: Contact networks; Graphon model; Noisy networks; Stochastic block model; Systematic bias.



1
Introduction

Networks are a powerful tool for analyzing complex data, where nodes represent entities and edges capture their interactions. Network analysis plays a crucial role in modern data science, enabling insights into both global network structures and individual node properties. One fundamental aspect of network analysis is node ranking, a topic studied extensively over the years. A well-known example is the PageRank algorithm
(
Bianchini et al. 2005
)
, originally developed at Google in the 1990s to score nodes by their importance in information diffusion. These scores can be used to algorithmically rank nodes—originally to select top hits for search queries.
More generally, network-based ranking algorithms take an input network of
n
nodes and produce a ranked ordering of these nodes.
Such rankings typically rely on node centrality metrics
(
Liao et al. 2017
)
, such as degree, closeness, betweenness, or eigenvector centrality
(
Rodrigues 2019
)
, which capture different aspects of influence.

In practical applications, algorithmic ranking can have major implications for opportunity and selection of nodes, for instance hiring of job applicants, or acceptance of students to university.
In network analyses, there can also be downstream effects on tasks like influence maximization: node rankings may be used as a simple proxy to select seeds for information spread, thus increasing their access to knowledge
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Mesner et al. 2023
)
.

Network data often includes node attributes, such as gender or age in social networks, and gene expression levels or cell types in biological networks.
Systematic bias occurs when the distributions of node centrality measures depend on protected group membership, resulting in node rankings that do not reflect the true network structure fairly
(
Zehlike et al. 2022
)
.

As a motivating example, consider citation networks, where nodes represent academic papers and edges represent citations. Research has shown that papers authored by women receive fewer citations, especially in male-dominated fields such as physics, mathematics, and engineering
(
Larivière et al. 2013
;
Tchilinguirova et al. 2024
)
. A key question is whether this under-citation stems from female authors’ minority status, or if systematic citation bias exists, where researchers preferentially cite papers by authors of the same gender. Understanding the causes and consequences of under-citation is vital, as citations affect career advancement, funding, and collaboration.

Systematic bias and mathematical fairness have been defined in various ways across statistics and machine learning. We adopt the framework of
Friedler et al. 2021
to formalize these concepts for network rankings. Each node
i
in the network has an
oracle score
S_{i}^{*}
, representing true importance. The set of oracle scores defines the
construct space
, where a fair ranking orders nodes strictly by
S_{i}^{*}
. In practice,
S_{i}^{*}
is unobserved; instead, we rely on an
observed score

S_{i}
, which may be biased or noisy, existing in the
observation space
.

Systematic bias
arises when the mapping from oracle scores to observed scores differs across groups, a phenomenon known as group skew. For a protected attribute
Z_{i}
(e.g., gender), if the conditional distributions of
S_{i}
given
S^{*}_{i}
vary across groups, i.e.,

p(S_{i}\mid S^{*}_{i},Z_{i}=0)\neq p(S_{i}\mid S^{*}_{i},Z_{i}=1),

(1)

then group-level disparities can emerge. In citation networks, for example, if female-authored papers (
Z_{i}=1
) receive fewer citations than male-authored ones (
Z_{i}=0
) with similar
S^{*}_{i}
, then
S_{i}
underrepresents their true importance. Rankings based only on
S_{i}
can therefore unfairly disadvantage female researchers, increasing disparities in visibility and advancement.

Friedler et al. 2021
highlight the issue of
non-identifiability
in the mappings between construct and observation spaces. This non-identifiability leads to different assumptions being made in practice, contributing to various interpretations and disagreements about what constitutes a fair ranking. They identify two primary worldviews or assumptions: “what you see is what you get” (WYSIWYG) and “we’re all equal” (WAE).

In fair algorithmic ranking, WYSIWYG assumes no group skew: observed scores accurately reflect true node importance. Under this assumption, directly ranking nodes based on their observed scores is considered fair because the observed scores are viewed as unbiased representations of the oracle scores. In contrast, WAE assumes that fairness in ranking is related to
proportional representation
among protected groups. Specifically, it requires that the proportion of nodes from each group in the top-
K
ranked positions is approximately equal to their proportion in the overall population of nodes. If the ranking fails to meet this condition, it is considered unfair, and correction is required.

Under WYSIWYG, a non-proportional ranking may still be fair if they reflect true importance. Under WAE, any systematic deviation from proportionality constitutes unfairness. An intermediate position, which does not fully adhere WYSIWYG or WAE, makes the question of fairness less clear-cut. In such cases, proportionality alone does not imply fairness.

We briefly review some key recent work on fairness and proportional representation in network-based rankings.
Karimi et al. 2018
focused on the effects of homophily on proportional representation, under a group-structured preferential attachment model.
They note that homophily or heterophily in the preferential attachment mechanism can lead to over- or underrepresentation of the majority group in the top degree-ranked nodes. Rather than addressing fairness directly, their empirical investigation focuses on the proportional representation of protected groups within node rankings.

Zhang et al. 2021
empirically studied a “chasm effect” in minority and majority representation in degree-based node rankings; they observed underrepresentation of minority group nodes among both the top and bottom ranked sets.

Neuhäuser et al. 2021
empirically studied how group-skewed edge errors affect networks with two protected groups. They found that missing within-group edges distort majority rankings, while missing between-group edges distort minority rankings. While not addressing fairness directly, their analysis lies between the WYSIWYG and WAE worldviews: they simulate networks lacking proportional rankings (violating WAE), then add group-dependent edge observation errors (violating WYSIWYG).
Due to this intrinsic non-identifiability of their model, the analysis in this paper is limited to empirical investigation.
This is consistent with formal statistical treatment of noisily observed networks in
(
Chang et al. 2022
)
.

A separate line of research in fairness-aware graph learning adopts a different notion of fairness. Here, fairness assumes nodes from different groups have similar within- and between-group connectivity. To estimate the graph, a fairness penalty term is incorporated into the graph estimation procedure. Some examples include
Navarro et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Navarro et al. 2024a
under various network models, including the Gaussian graphical model and Gaussian covariance graph model.

In this manuscript, we provide a formal statistical framework for assessing systematic bias in network rankings, including precise definitions of the statistics used to quantify minority group representation.
For degree-based rankings under some canonical network models, we derive the asymptotic behavior of minority representation statistics.
Finally, extending the empirical insights of
Neuhäuser et al. 2021
, we develop methods to formally detect and correct systematic bias in network models with observation error, producing node rankings which are fair on average.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of our theoretical and methodological contributions through comprehensive simulations and real data application.
To our knowledge, this is the first fairness-aware theoretical investigation of such node ranking statistics, and the first methodology for detecting and correcting systematic bias and mathematical unfairness in degree-based rankings.

2
Minority representation profiles and models

Throughout the paper, we primarily consider an undirected network represented as
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of
n
nodes and
E
is the set of edges, represented by a binary adjacency matrix
A^{(n)}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
and group labels
\bm{c}^{(n)}\in\{1,2\}^{n}
, jointly drawn from a labelled network model
denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Each node is independently assigned to the minority group (group 1) with probability
\kappa<1/2
and to the majority group (group 2) with probability
1-\kappa
. For simplicity, we omit the superscript
n
in the notation for
\bm{c}^{(n)}
,
A^{(n)}
throughout, and all limits should be interpreted as
n\to\infty
.

Nodes are ranked using a general
ranking criterion
, which maps from the adjacency matrix to a vector of node scores. We focus on
degree-based ranking
for its tractability and widespread use. Let
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}
denote the degree of node
i
. The top-
K
set, denoted
D_{K}(A)
, consists of the
K
nodes with the highest degrees, with ties broken at random.

2.1
Minority representation profiles

Our primary interest is in the sequence of statistics known as the
minority representation profile
, which tracks the proportion of minority (group 1) nodes in the top-
K
according to the degree ranking.
Specifically, define the random variable

R_{K}(\bm{c},A)=\frac{1}{K}\left\lvert\left\{i\in D_{K}(A):c_{i}=1\right\}\right\rvert\in[0,1],

for
K\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
To describe minority representation of a given network model, we define an expected analog of
R_{K}(\bm{c},A)
, as well as a limiting profile as
K
scales with
n
.

Definition 1
.

For a labelled network model
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, define the
expected minority representation profile
as

\rho_{K}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left\{R_{K}(\bm{c},A)\right\},

for
K\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Definition 2
.

For a sequence of labelled network models
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, define the
asymptotic minority representation profile
as

\rho^{*}(z)=\operatorname{plim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\{R_{\lfloor nz\rfloor}(\bm{c},A)\right\},

for
z\in(0,1]
if the limit exists, where
\operatorname{plim}
denotes the limit in probability of a sequence of random variables.

As we have noted in Section
1
, empirical assessments often rely on some form of proportionality. In both finite and limiting regimes, we formalize this notion as follows.

Definition 3
.

A labelled network model
\mathcal{P}_{n}
has
proportional representation
with respect to degree ranking if

\rho_{K}=\kappa,

for all
K\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Definition 4
.

A sequence of labelled network models
\mathcal{P}_{n}
has
asymptotic proportional representation
with respect to degree ranking if

\rho^{*}(z)=\kappa.

for all
z\in(0,1]
.

2.2
Canonical network models

In this section, we introduce two canonical generative models for labelled networks,
the stochastic block model
and a more general
labelled graphon model
, which we use to analyze minority representation in degree-based rankings.

2.2.1
Stochastic block model

The stochastic block model (SBM) is a widely used generative network model, first proposed to describe community phenomena in social networks
(
Holland et al. 1983
;
Abbe 2018
)
. In this model, nodes are partitioned into distinct communities, and edges are independently formed based on the communities to which the nodes belong.
We study a 2-group SBM, where each node is independently assigned to the minority group with probability
\kappa<1/2
, and to the majority group otherwise. Given the group label vector

\bm{c}
,
edges are formed independently with probabilities specified by a connectivity matrix
B\in[0,1]^{2\times 2}
, according to

\mathbb{P}(A_{ij}=1\mid c_{i},c_{j})=B_{c_{i}c_{j}}
for all
i<j
.
In an unbalanced SBM with
\kappa<1/2
, minority node degrees are influenced more by between-group connections due to group size disparity. When the model is assortative (within-group connections are more likely), this can lead to systematic underrepresentation of minority nodes in top-
K
degree rankings. Numerically, we will have
\rho_{K}<\kappa
for small values of
K
.

2.2.2
Labelled graphon

The graphon model generalizes the SBM by replacing discrete community labels with latent features. A general graphon model on
n
nodes is parameterized by a
latent position vector

\bm{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n})
, where
u_{i}\in[0,1]
represents the latent feature of node
i
, and a
graphon function

\omega:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to[0,1]
, where
\omega(u_{i},u_{j})
gives the probability of an edge between nodes
i
and
j
. The adjacency matrix
A
, representing the connections in the network, is generated as follows:

\mathbb{P}(A_{ij}=1\mid u_{i},u_{j})=\omega(u_{i},u_{j}),\quad\forall i<j.

(2)

To assess minority group representation, we introduce a labelled variant of the graphon. Nodes are assigned to group 1 with probability
\kappa<1/2
, and group 2 otherwise. Conditional on group label, we sample latent positions:

U_{i}|c_{i}=1\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\kappa),\quad U_{i}|c_{i}=2\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(\kappa,1),

independently. Then generate the adjacency matrix
A
as in (
2
).
This construction ensures the marginal distribution of
U_{i}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[0,1]
, so the resulting network remains a valid graphon model. This is similar to the so-called
stochastic block smooth graphon model
of
Sischka and Kauermann 2024
, although here we treat the community labels as observed.

In the graphon model, node degree is determined by the graphon function
\omega
and the latent features of other nodes.
Similar to the SBM, assortativity in
\omega
can lead to systematic underrepresentation of minority nodes; however this underrepresentation can arise in a broader range of network structures, and is not restricted to block-exchangeable patterns.

2.3
Network models with systematic bias

In network analyses, particularly those focused on minority representation in degree-based rankings, systematic inaccuracies can significantly distort conclusions
(
Neuhäuser et al. 2021
)
. These distortions often arise from biases embedded in the attributed structure of social networks, unfairly impacting minority node rankings. To model this bias, we suppose the true underlying network
A
is unobserved, and instead we observe a biased version
Y
, where the bias depends on the group labels of the nodes. Formally, we assume

\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=0\mid A_{ij}=1)=\beta_{c_{i}c_{j}}
,
independently over all
i<j
, for type II error rates
\beta_{11}
,
\beta_{22}
, and
\beta_{21}=\beta_{12}
(by symmetry).
Following the terminology in
Friedler et al. 2021
, we refer to the generative model for the underlying network
A
as the
construct model
, and the generative model for
Y
as the
observation model
.

These observation errors can systematically reduce the visibility of minority nodes in degree-based rankings. If the construct network
A
has proportionally representation (e.g., Erdős–Rényi model), but the observation errors favors within-group edges (e.g.,
\beta_{12}<\beta_{11}=\beta{22}
), minority nodes will underrepresented in top-
K
rankings.
If the majority group nodes are more likely to form within-group connections in the construct network
A
, additional observation bias with
\beta_{12}\leq\beta_{11}=\beta_{22}
will further exacerbate the underrepresentation of minority nodes in top-
K
rankings.

The addition of this observation error mechanism does not increase the class of models under study; it is easy to see that if the construct model comes from either the class of SBMs defined in Section
2.2.1
, or the class of the labelled graphons defined in Section
2.2.2
, the observation model will remain in the same class. For this reason, it is sufficient to study the probability limits of
\rho_{K}
under the SBM and labelled graphon models in Section
3
, without explicitly incorporating the observation bias.
We return to the systematic bias model in Section
4
, where we use it to develop a correction procedure when replicated network observations are available.

3
Quantifying minority representation

In this section, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the minority representation profile under the canonical network models introduced in Section
2.2
.

3.1
Stochastic block model

Consider the 2-group SBM (with minority and majority groups) with probability matrix

B=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}&q\\
q&p_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(3)

where
p_{g}
for
g=1,2
represent the within-group connection probabilities, and
q
represents the between-group connection probability.
We denote this model as SBM(
n,B,\kappa
).

In (
3
), we allow
p_{1},p_{2}
and
q
to depend on
n
.
The following proposition illustrates a phase transition in the detectability of group differences. Let
\delta_{g}:=p_{g}-q
denote the signal strength for group
g=1,2
.
When
\delta_{g}
is too small, the group differences are not detectable in a degree-based ranking, and the limiting ranking is proportionally representative. Conversely, when
\delta_{g}
is too large, the ranking becomes trivial: all majority group nodes dominate the top of the ranking in the limit. This idea is formalized in the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the SBM defined in (
3
) with an assortative structure, i.e.,
\delta_{g}>0
for
g=1,2
. Then:

(a)

If
\delta_{g}\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
for
g=1,2
and
K/n\to z
, then

R_{K}(\bm{c},A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\kappa.

(b)

If
(1-\kappa)\delta_{2}-\kappa\delta_{1}\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
,
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}
, and
K/n\to z
with
z\leq 1-\kappa
, then

R_{K}(\bm{c},A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(c)

If
\kappa\delta_{1}-(1-\kappa)\delta_{2}\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
,
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}
, and
K/n\to z
with
z\leq\kappa
, then

R_{K}(\bm{c},A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}1.

Therefore, we can focus on the parameter space where
\delta_{g}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
for
g=1,2
, in order to obtain nontrivial asymptotic behavior. To this end, we define
p_{g}=q+\frac{\mu_{g}}{\sqrt{n}}
for
\mu_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
, resulting in the following connection probability matrix:

B=\begin{pmatrix}q+\mu_{1}/\sqrt{n}&q\\
q&q+\mu_{2}/\sqrt{n}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

We can characterize the limiting representation profile.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a network
A
generated from an SBM
(n,B,\kappa)
, where
B
is defined in (
4
).
Suppose
K/n\to z
as
n\to\infty
.
Then we have

R_{K}(\bm{c},A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{\kappa}{z}\left\{1-F_{1}(c^{*})\right\}=:\rho^{*}(z;\kappa,q,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

where
c^{*}
is the
(1-z)
-th quantile of the following mixture of Gaussian distributions:

\kappa F_{1}(\cdot)+(1-\kappa)F_{2}(\cdot):=\kappa\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\kappa\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}},1\right)+(1-\kappa)\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)\mu_{2}}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}},1\right).

Remark 1
.

The conclusion remains valid if one or both signals vanish, i.e.,
\mu_{1}=o(1)
and/or
\mu_{2}=o(1)

\mu_{1}=o(1)

\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\mu_{2}

In Appendix
B

Labelled graphon

In this section, we derive asymptotic limits for minority representation in degree-based rankings under labelled graphon models, generalizing the results of Section

\omega_{n}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]

\{\omega_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

Assumption 1

There exists a constant
\epsilon>0

\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\in(\epsilon,1-\epsilon)

\sup_{u,v\in[0,1]}~\lvert\omega_{n}(u,v)-p_{n}\rvert=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(5)

Assumption 2

For

\sqrt{\frac{n}{p_{n}(1-p_{n})}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\omega_{n}(u,v)-p_{n}\right\}dv\rightarrow\mu(u).

4
Detecting and correcting for systematic bias

In this section, we develop methodology to detect for and correct systematic bias in rankings, under a 2-group SBM with observation errors, using the model defined in Section
2.3
.
In general, the full model is unidentifiable from a single observed network, however the two fairness worldviews – WAE and WYSIWYG – can be interpreted formally as identifying assumptions.

Under the identifying assumptions of WYSIWYG, we assume that there are no observation errors, and thus there is no need to correct the ranking of observed degrees.
Conversely, under the identifying assumptions of WAE, and assuming the construct model is ER,
any difference in the observation model edge densities is attributable to differences in the observation error rates.
Under WAE, the construct model is proportionally representative, making estimation of the construct model
\rho_{K}
a trivial task.
Moreover, there is a natural corrected degree ranking which takes the top nodes by degree in each group, so that the group proportions in any prefix of the ranking approximately match the overall minority proportion
\kappa

(
Zehlike et al. 2022
)
.

With neither worldview as an identifying assumption, our goal is not necessarily to make the ranking proportionally representative, but instead to correct it to match a general, (adaptive) target representation profile.
Formally, corrected rankings can be computed sequentially: initializing from the empty set, the next ranked node is either the top available minority group or top available majority group node, where ties are broken uniformly at random, and the group identity is chosen to minimize the discrepancy between the group proportion in the next prefix of the ranking and the corresponding value in the adaptive representation profile (see Algorithm
1
).
Any remaining discrepancy between the corrected and target representation profiles is due to the discreteness of the ranking problem.

Algorithm 1

In order to develop methodology without an identifying worldview, we extend the work of
Chang et al. 2022
, using independent network replicates to consistently estimate the group-dependent observation error rates.
By
Chang et al. 2022
, Theorem 1, replication is indeed a requirement here, it is impossible to consistently estimate the observation error rates without it.
Using the systematically biased model described above with only type II errors, two replicates are sufficient to estimate the block-wise observation error rates.

The availability of two independent replicates is common in various applications. For example, contact networks collected over different days
(
Fournet and Barrat 2014
)
and gene expression networks derived from repeated experiments under similar conditions
(
Faith et al. 2007
)
. In other applications, it may be possible to sample a single directed, noisy observation
Y
of a construct network
A
, characterized by group-dependent directed type II (recall) errors with

\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=0|A_{ij}=1)=\beta_{c_{i}c_{j}}

for any
i,j\in[n]

\beta_{12}\neq\beta_{21}
.

Directed noisy networks are often obtained via ego-centric sampling, where an individual (the “ego”) is asked to report their connections to a set of other individuals (the “alters”) within social or communication networks. Ego-centric networks are affected by recall or reporting biases, which can introduce systematic errors in the observed ties. A single directed noisy network provides similar information to the two replicate case, and the error rate parameters can be estimated analogously. In the following, we focus on the two replicate, undirected case, with the corresponding methodology for a single directed noisy network provided in Appendix
A.4
of the supplementary materials.

4.2
Detection

In this section, under our (undirected) 2-group SBM with observation errors, and assuming we have access to two independent network replicates, we develop an asymptotically valid test for the null hypothesis that no correction is needed for the degree ranking.

We develop a hypothesis test where the null hypothesis corresponds to a subset of the parameter space in which the corrected ranking is expected to be identical or nearly identical to the uncorrected ranking.
Formally, the null parameter space is made up of two subsets,

H_{0,\bar{\beta}}=H_{0,\bar{\beta}}^{(\beta)}\cup H_{0}^{(\mu)},

where

H_{0,\bar{\beta}}^{(\beta)}:\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0,\beta\in[0,\bar{\beta}],\quad H_{0}^{(\mu)}:\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0,\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0

for a tuning parameter
\bar{\beta}\in(0,1)

Under

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0

\beta\leq\bar{\beta}

We allow for small values of

In practice, we suggest setting

To develop a hypothesis test for the first subset of the null parameter space, we first consider the degenerate case

Real data analysis

In this section, we apply our approach to a real-world contact network.
Contact networks are a type of social network where individuals are represented as nodes and edges indicate physical or social interactions between them. Over the past decade, there has been growing interest in understanding contact patterns across different demographic or structural groups
(
Stehlé et al. 2011
;
Fournet and Barrat 2014
;
Mastrandrea et al. 2015
;
Kucharski et al. 2018
)
. Identifying the most connected individuals—those with the highest degree—can highlight key spreaders or control points within the network. Such insights are especially valuable for informing epidemic models and designing targeted control strategies, such as focusing interventions on high-risk groups to improve efficiency and impact.

There are various methods for collecting contact information among individuals, including self-reported diaries, surveys, and decentralized systems based on wearable sensors. Among these, contact diaries are a commonly used but imperfect source: they are prone to recall errors and reporting biases, as noted in
Smieszek et al. 2012
. Given their widespread use and inherent limitations, it is essential to account for potential systematic biases when making inferences from contact diary networks.

We analyze a directed contact network among 120 high school students at Lycée Thiers (Marseilles, France), based on diaries collected in December 2013
(
Mastrandrea et al. 2015
)
. The data include reported contacts, gender (male/female) and academic specializations - MP (mathematics and physics), PC (physics and chemistry), and BIO (biology), with each specialization comprising multiple classes (e.g., BIO1, BIO2, BIO3 for biology). In-degrees are used for ranking, as they better reflect influence than out-degrees.

For data preprocessing, we first excluded class MP2, which had only one student and no interactions. Figure

shows the estimated connection probability matrix across classes and genders. Most classes exhibit a relatively homogeneous connection pattern, with more interactions within the same gender, while BIO1 and BIO3 display more cross-gender connections.

6
Discussion and conclusions

Motivated by the challenge of fairness in ranking across different social groups, this paper introduces a mathematically rigorous framework to define and analyze systematic bias in nework degree-based rankings.
We characterize systematic bias as a disparity between construct and observed spaces and propose a statistically tractable measure, the minority representation profile, to evaluate systematic bias and proportional representation among top-
K
ranked nodes in labelled networks.
To model systematic bias, we employ a group-dependent observation error model. Due to non-identifiability, estimating bias parameters requires either two independent replicates or a directed network. We propose methods to detect and correct for systematic bias when it is significant, leveraging the derived asymptotic limits to adjust minority group representations and refine ranking results. Our approach provides a formal statistical foundation for identifying and mitigating bias in network rankings, contributing to broader efforts in algorithmic fairness and equitable representation in labelled networks.

There are several limitations and potential extensions of our work. First, our analysis focuses on degree centrality; it would be interesting to investigate whether the same framework can be extended to other centrality measures that assess node importance.
Second, hile our proposed label-dependent missing edge model falls within the class of systematic bias models, it would be valuable to explore alternative modelling approaches for systematic bias, as well as to develop new statistics for measuring fairness in network data.
For instance,
Braithwaite et al. 2020
identified underreporting by highly connected individuals in contact networks during the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting a model in which observation error rates are related to node degree in the construct network.
Lastly, if the labels contain more than two groups (
k>2
), designing trackable statistics to measure the fairness of the observed network and extending current detection and correction methods will significantly advance the statistical analysis of fairness in networks.

Let
Z
be a standard normally distributed random variable. Assuming (
8
), we have

F_{n}(Z)\xrightarrow{p}F^{*}(Z),

since

F_{n}(Z)-F(Z)=\mathbb{E}\left(F_{n}(Z)-F^{*}(Z)\mid Z\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{n}(x)-F^{*}(x)\right|\mid Z\right)\xrightarrow{p}0.

Thus,

\Phi\left(F_{n}^{-1}(t)\right)=\mathbb{P}_{Z}\left(F_{n}(Z)<t\right)\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{P}_{Z}\left(F^{*}(Z)<t\right)=\Phi\left((F^{*})^{-1}(t)\right),

for every
t
, since both
F^{*}
and
\Phi
are continuous. By the continuity of
\Phi^{-1}
, it follows from the continuous mapping theorem that

F_{n}^{-1}(t)\xrightarrow{p}(F^{*})^{-1}(t).

In particular, for quantiles where
K_{n}/n\to q
, and using the continuity of
F
, we have

F_{n}^{-1}\left(\frac{K_{n}}{n}\right)\xrightarrow{p}(F^{*})^{-1}(q).

Proof of Step 2
.

By the proof of basic Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem (Kosorok, 2008),
it should be sufficient to show pointwise convergence for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Following the proof of the the basic Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem, let
0<\delta<1
be arbitrary and take
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}
such that
F^{*}\left(x_{j}\right)-F^{*}\left(x_{j-1}\right)=\delta
. Note that
N(\delta)\leq 1+1/\delta
. If
x\in\left(x_{j-1},x_{j}\right]
, then clearly

\left(-\infty,x_{j-1}\right]\subset(-\infty,x]\subset\left(-\infty,x_{j}\right].

Now, fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Define
X_{i}:=\mathbb{I}\left(\dfrac{d_{i}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x\right)
,
and let

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\quad F^{(n)}_{\text{mix}}(x):=\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(x)].

We bound:

\mathbb{P}\left(|F_{n}(x)-F^{*}(x)|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|F_{n}(x)-F^{(n)}_{\text{mix}}(x)|\geq\epsilon/2\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|F^{(n)}_{\text{mix}}(x)-F^{*}(x)|\geq\epsilon/2\right).

(9)

We now control both terms on the right-hand side.

For the first term in (
9
), by Chebyshev’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}\left(|F_{n}(x)-F^{(n)}_{\text{mix}}(x)|\geq\epsilon/2\right)\leq\frac{4}{\epsilon^{2}}\operatorname{Var}(F_{n}(x)).

We control the variance via:

\operatorname{Var}(F_{n}(x))=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},X_{j}).

Since
X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, we have
\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{4}
, and so:

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})=O(n^{-1}).

Now consider the covariance term. For
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},X_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1,X_{j}=1)-\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)\mathbb{P}(X_{j}=1).

Write them as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1,X_{j}=1)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{d_{i}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x,\frac{d_{j}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x\right)=\mathbb{P}(d_{i}\leq\tau,d_{j}\leq\tau),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)\mathbb{P}(X_{j}=1)=\mathbb{P}(d_{i}\leq\tau)\mathbb{P}(d_{j}\leq\tau),

where
\tau:=np+\sqrt{np(1-p)}x
.

To isolate dependence, we condition on the edge
A_{ij}
:

\mathbb{P}(d_{i},d_{j}\leq\tau)=\mathbb{P}(d_{i},d_{j}\leq\tau\mid A_{ij}=1)\mathbb{P}(A_{ij}=1)+\mathbb{P}(d_{i},d_{j}\leq\tau\mid A_{ij}=0)\mathbb{P}(A_{ij}=0).

Recall that
d_{i}=d_{i}^{(-j)}+A_{ij}
, so:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d_{i},d_{j}\leq\tau\mid A_{ij}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau-1,d_{j}^{(-i)}\leq\tau-1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d_{i},d_{j}\leq\tau\mid A_{ij}=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau,d_{j}^{(-i)}\leq\tau).

We now compute the conditional expectation and variance of degrees under the graphon model. For any node
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(d_{i}\mid U_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega(U_{i},U_{j})\right]=n\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv

\displaystyle=np+n\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\omega(U_{i},v)-p\right\}dv

\displaystyle=np+\sqrt{np(1-p)}\left\{\mu(U_{i})+o(1)\right\}

\displaystyle=np+\mu(U_{i})\sqrt{np(1-p)}+o(\sqrt{n}),

where the second last step uses the scaling assumption in Assumption
2
.

Similarly, the conditional variance is:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}|U_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j}\omega(U_{i},U_{j})\{1-\omega(U_{i},U_{j})\}\right)+\operatorname{Var}\left\{\sum_{j}\omega(U_{i},U_{j})\right\}

\displaystyle=n\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv-\int_{0}^{1}\omega^{2}(U_{i},v)dv\right\}+n\int_{0}^{1}\omega^{2}(U_{i},v)dv-n\left(\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=n\left(\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv\right)\left(1-\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv\right).

Then
\int_{0}^{1}\omega(U_{i},v)dv=p+O(1/\sqrt{n})
since
\omega(U_{i},v)=p+O(n^{-1/2})
uniformly in
v
by Assumption
1
.

As a result, for any node
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(d_{i}|U_{i})=np+\mu(U_{i})\sqrt{np(1-p)}+o(\sqrt{n}),\quad\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}|U_{i})=np(1-p)+O(\sqrt{n}).

Applying the Berry–Esseen theorem conditionally on
U_{i}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}(d_{i}\leq\tau\mid U_{i})=\Phi\left(\frac{\tau-\mathbb{E}(d_{i}\mid U_{i})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}\mid U_{i})}}\right)+o(1).

Now marginalizing over
U_{i}\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]
, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}(d_{i}\leq\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi\left(\frac{\tau-\mathbb{E}(d_{i}\mid u)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}\mid u)}}\right)du+o(1).

Using the expansion:

\mathbb{E}(d_{i}|U_{i})=np+\mu(U_{i})\sqrt{np(1-p)}+o(\sqrt{n}),\quad\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}|U_{i})=np(1-p)+O(\sqrt{n}),

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\tau-\mathbb{E}(d_{i}\mid u)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}\mid u)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{np+x\sqrt{np(1-p)}-\left[np+\mu(u)\sqrt{np(1-p)}+o(\sqrt{n})\right]}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}+O(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x-\mu(u))\sqrt{np(1-p)}+o(\sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}+O(1)}

\displaystyle=x-\mu(u)+o(1).

Therefore:

\mathbb{P}(d_{i}\leq\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(x-\mu(u))du+o(1).

Similarly, for the leave-one-out degrees
d_{i}^{(-j)}:=d_{i}-A_{ij}
, we apply the same Berry–Esseen argument. Since removing a single Bernoulli term changes the degree by at most one, we have

\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau-1\mid U_{i})=\Phi\left(x-\mu(U_{i})\right)+o(1),\quad\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau\mid U_{i})=\Phi\left(x-\mu(U_{i})\right)+o(1).

Marginalizing over
U_{i}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[0,1]
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau-1)=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(x-\mu(u))\,du+o(1),\quad\mathbb{P}(d_{i}^{(-j)}\leq\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(x-\mu(u))\,du+o(1).

Therefore,

\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},X_{j})=o(1).

Finally, summing over all
i\neq j
:

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},X_{j})=o(1),

which establishes the overall variance bound:

\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)=O(n^{-1})+o(1)=o(1).

For the second term in (
9
), note that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|F^{(n)}_{\operatorname{mix}}(x)-F^{*}(x)\right|\geq\epsilon/2\right)=0

for large
n
since
F^{(n)}_{\operatorname{mix}}(x)-F^{*}(x)=o\left(1\right)
by the central limit theorem.

Combining both components, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left(\frac{d_{i}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x\right)-F^{*}(x)\right|\geq\epsilon\right)=o(1).

It follows that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{n}(x)-F^{*}(x)\right|\leq\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}\left|F_{n}\left(x_{j}\right)-F^{*}\left(x_{j}\right)\right|+\delta,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{n}(x)-F^{*}(x)\right|\geq 2\delta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}\left|F_{n}\left(x_{j}\right)-F^{*}\left(x_{j}\right)\right|\geq\delta\right)\leq No(1).

In conclusion,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{n}(x)-F^{*}(x)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

This completes the entire proof.

∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{d_{i}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x\right)=\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{d_{i}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\leq x|U_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu(U_{i}),1)\leq x|U_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1)+\mu(U_{i})\leq x|U_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1)+\mu(U_{i})\leq x\right)

where the convergence is guaranteed by the dominanted convergence theorem.

Applying Lemma
1
with
F_{1}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)+\mu(U^{(1)})
and
F_{2}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)+\mu(U^{(2)})
with
U^{(1)}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[0,\kappa]
,
U^{(2)}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[\kappa,1]
gives the final result.

∎

A.4
Technical details for directed networks

Suppose the construct network
A
is undirected and we observe
Y
with directed type II (recall) errors, following a group structure

\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=1|A_{ij}=1)=1-\beta_{c_{i}c_{j}}

for 2 groups (
c_{i}\in\{1,2\}
). There are
4
error rate parameters in
(0,1)

Consider the unobserved network generated from SBM
(n,\kappa,B)
with

B=\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}\\
p_{21}&p_{22}\end{pmatrix}

with
p_{12}=p_{21}
.
Thefore, in total, we have seven parameters to estimated under the directed noisy network setting.

Under this directed noisy network setting, we aim to estimate seven unknown parameters. We define the following estimators:

1.

\hat{u}_{11,d}=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)}\sum_{c_{i}=c_{j}=1}Y_{ij}
— observed density within group 1.

2.

\hat{u}_{11,a}=\binom{n_{1}}{2}^{-1}\sum_{c_{i}=c_{j}=1,i<j}\lvert Y_{ij}-Y_{ji}\rvert
— asymmetry within group 1.

3.

\hat{u}_{22,d}=\frac{1}{n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}\sum_{c_{i}=c_{j}=2}Y_{ij}
— density within group 2.

4.

\hat{u}_{22,a}=\binom{n_{2}}{2}^{-1}\sum_{c_{i}=c_{j}=2,i<j}\lvert Y_{ij}-Y_{ji}\rvert
— asymmetry within group 2.

5.

\hat{u}_{12,d}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{c_{i}=1,c_{j}=2}Y_{ij}
— density from group 1 to group 2.

6.

\hat{u}_{21,d}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{c_{i}=2,c_{j}=1}Y_{ij}
— density from group 2 to group 1.

7.

\hat{u}_{12,a}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{c_{i}=1,c_{j}=2}\lvert Y_{ij}-Y_{ji}\rvert
— asymmetry between groups.

These statistics have expectations:

1.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{11,d}=(1-\beta_{11})p_{11}

2.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{11,a}=2\beta_{11}(1-\beta_{11})p_{11}

3.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{22,d}=(1-\beta_{22})p_{22}

4.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{22,a}=2\beta_{22}(1-\beta_{22})p_{22}

5.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{12,d}=(1-\beta_{12})p_{12}

6.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{21,d}=(1-\beta_{21})p_{12}

7.

\mathbb{E}\hat{u}_{12,a}=[\beta_{12}(1-\beta_{21})+\beta_{21}(1-\beta_{12})]p_{12}

By method of moments, the estimators for the unknown parameters are:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{u}_{11,a}}{2\hat{u}_{11,d}},\quad\hat{p}_{11}=\frac{2\hat{u}_{11,d}^{2}}{2\hat{u}_{11,d}-\hat{u}_{11,a}},

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{u}_{22,a}}{2\hat{u}_{22,d}},\quad\hat{p}_{22}=\frac{2\hat{u}_{22,d}^{2}}{2\hat{u}_{22,d}-\hat{u}_{22,a}},

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{u}_{21,d}-\hat{u}_{12,d}+\hat{u}_{12,a}}{2\hat{u}_{21,d}},\quad\hat{\beta}_{21}=\frac{\hat{u}_{12,d}-\hat{u}_{21,d}+\hat{u}_{12,a}}{2\hat{u}_{12,d}},

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\hat{u}_{12,d}\hat{u}_{21,d}}{\hat{u}_{12,d}+\hat{u}_{21,d}-\hat{u}_{12,a}}.

We now consider testing for systematic bias in edge recall rates across groups. The hypotheses are:

H_{1,0}:\beta_{12}=\beta_{21};\qquad H_{1,1}:\beta_{12}<\beta_{21}.

The test statistic is based on the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\beta}_{12}-\hat{\beta}_{21}

In the proof, we establish a stronger version of the joint asymptotic normality for the empirical moments of

First, if the unobserved network
A
is generated from SBM
(n,B,\kappa)
with

p_{21}&p_{22}\end{pmatrix},

where
p_{12}=p_{21}

According to our model assumption, we observed a noisy directed network

with

\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=1|A_{ij}=1)=1-\beta_{c_{i}c_{j}}.

Marginally, we can think about
Y
generated from directed SBM
