Abstract

The (unbounded version of the) Lempert function l D l_{D} on a domain D ⊂ ℂ d D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d} does not usually satisfy the triangle inequality, but on bounded 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} -smooth strictly pseudoconvex domains, it satisfies a weaker version, with l D ​ ( a , c ) ≤ C ⁡ ( l D ​ ( a , b ) + l D ​ ( b , c ) ) l_{D}(a,c)\leq C(l_{D}(a,b)+l_{D}(b,c)) . 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Invariant functions and triangle inequalities.

The most commonly used invariant Finsler metric on the tangent space of a complex manifold
M

is the Kobayashi-Royden metric, defined as follows using “analytic discs”, i.e.
holomorphic maps from the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
to
M
:

\kappa_{M}(z;X):=\inf\{|\alpha|:\exists\varphi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},M),\varphi(0)=z,\alpha\varphi^{\prime}(0)=X\}.

It can be seen
[
14
]
as the infinitesimal version of the Lempert function,

l_{M}(z,w):=\tanh^{-1}l_{M}^{*}(z,w)
, where

l_{M}^{*}(z,w):=\inf\{|\alpha|:\exists\varphi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},M)\hbox{ with }\varphi(0)=z,\varphi(\alpha)=w\}.

Lempert
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
proved that
l_{M}
(and thus
l_{M}^{*}
) satisfies the triangle inequality and defines a distance
when
M
is a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This extends to bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth
\mathbb{C}
-convex domains,
as a consequence of the theorem by Jacquet

[
6
]
that they can be exhausted by strongly linearly convex domains with
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
boundaries,
see also
[
7
, Remark 7.1.21 (b)]
.

In general, as pointed out in
[
11
]
,
l_{M}
does not satisfy the triangle inequality and
the largest pseudodistance not exceeding
l_{M}
is obtained by taking an infimum over
chains of analytic discs and is called the Kobayashi (pseudo)distance
k_{M}
.
It turns out that
k_{M}
is the integrated form of
\kappa_{M},
that is,

(1)

k_{M}(z,w)=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{M}(\gamma(t);\gamma^{\prime}(t))dt,

where the infimum is taken over all absolutely continuous curves

\gamma:[0,1]\to M
such that
\gamma(0)=z
and
\gamma(1)=w.

Good general references about those topics are the monographs
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
.

From now on, we restrict attention to the case where
M
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, denoted
D
.
We say that a positive symmetric function
L
on
D\times D
satisfies a
Weak Triangle Inequality
(WTI) if there exists

C\geq 1
such that for any
x,y,z\in D
,

(2)

L(x,z)\leq C\left(L(x,y)+L(y,z)\right).

Notice that when
l_{D}
satisfies the Weak Triangle Inequality (resp. triangle inequality), then
l_{D}^{*}

satisfies the corresponding inequality.

Unlike the actual triangle inequality, the WTI is satisfied by
l_{D}^{*}
for large classes of domains.

Proposition 1
.

If
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth strictly pseudoconvex domain, then
l_{D}
,
and therefore
l_{D}^{*}
, satisfy a Weak Triangle Inequality.

This is a corollary of the main result in
[
13
]
: in a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth strictly pseudoconvex domain,
there is a constant
A\geq 1
such that
k_{D}\leq l_{D}\leq Ac_{D}\leq Ak_{D}
, where
c_{D}
stands for the
Carathéodory distance. It is however possible to prove the conclusion of the Proposition for
l_{D}^{*}
in a more elementary way, using
a natural localization lemma for the Lempert function which will be useful in the rest of the paper (Lemma
4
),
and we do so in Section
2
.

There are many examples of domains where
l_{D}
satisfies the WTI without satisfying the triangle inequality.
For instance, take an upper semicontinuous function
h:\mathbb{C}^{n}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)

such that
h(\lambda z)=|\lambda|h(z)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The balanced domain
D_{h}:=\{z:h(z)<1\}
is convex if and only
if
h(z+w)\leq h(z)+h(w)

[
7
, Remark 2.2.1 (i), p. 27]
.
For
z\in D_{h}
,
l_{D_{h}}^{*}(0,z)=h(z)

[
7
, Proposition 3.1.11, p. 108]
. Now take some
h
such that
D_{h}
is not convex,
but pseudoconvex; it can be approximated from the inside by smoothly bounded strictly pseudoconvex domains
(D_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, which cannot
be convex for
j
large enough, but
l_{D_{j}}^{*}
will satisfy
a WTI by Proposition
1
.

1.2.
Main result.

Theorem 2
.

If
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth domain, and
l_{D}^{*}
satisfies a Weak Triangle Inequality,
then
D
is pseudoconvex.

The scheme of the proof of Theorem
2
is as follows: if the domain is not pseudoconvex, we can find a point
p\in\partial D

which is, in a certain sense, strictly pseudoconcave. Near such a point we can find pairs of points
z,w\in D

such that
l_{D}^{(2)*}(z,w)\ll l_{D}(z,w)
as
z,w\to p
, where for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
m\geq 1
,

l_{D}^{(m)*}(z,w):=\inf\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}l_{D}^{*}(y_{j},y_{j+1}),y_{j}\in D,y_{0}=z,y_{m}=w\}.

This is done in Section
3
by comparing
upper estimates for
l_{D}^{(2)*}
near a pseudoconcave point (in
Proposition
5
, which does not depend on the smoothness
of
\partial D
) and lower estimates for
l_{D}
near
p
, which we first give in the easier case when
\partial D

is
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
-smooth for some
\alpha>0

(Proposition
6
), then when
\partial D
is merely

\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth (Proposition
8
).

To obtain the lower estimates, we study some model domains in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We thought it interesting to study more
completely the growth of the Lempert function along the normal to the boundary in those domains,
and this is done in Section
4
.

When the Lempert function
l_{D}^{*}
satisfies the triangle inequality,
it is clearly continuous (and thus
l_{D}
as well). The following shows that this is still the case when only a WTI is satisfied.

Corollary 3
.

If
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth domain, and
l_{D}^{*}
satisfies a Weak Triangle Inequality,
then for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
m\geq 1
,
l_{D}^{(m)*}
is continuous.

The Corollary follows from the Theorem because any
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth pseudoconvex domain will be taut
(even more is true: Lipschitz pseudoconvex domains are hyperconvex, thus taut,
[
7
, Remark 3.2.3 (b)]
,
[
8
]
);
in a taut domain, the Lempert function is continuous
[
7
, Proposition 3.2.9]
.

1.3.
Open Questions.

(1)

The above shows that continuity of the Lempert function can be seen as another relaxation of
the condition that
l_{D}
satisfy the triangle inequality. For a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth bounded
domain
D
, does the continuity of the Lempert function
l_{D}
imply pseudoconvexity of
D
? It would mean that among bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth domains, continuity of the Lempert function is equivalent
to pseudoconvexity (or tautness, or hyperconvexity, since they are
all equivalent in this case).

(2)

Our methods only work for domains with boundary of at least
\mathcal{C}^{1}
regularity. Does the
implication still hold for domains with Lipschitz boundary, or even Hölder?

If the boundary is very irregular, there are counterexamples. For instance, let
D=\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,
where
\mathbb{B}^{2}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then one can easily show that for all
z,w\in D
,

l_{D}(z,w)=l_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(z,w)
, and this last coincides with all the invariant distances in the ball,
thus satisfies the triangle inequality, while
D
is clearly not pseudoconvex. In Section
4
,
we mention some examples of the form
D\setminus E
, where
E
is an “exceptional” closed set;
but none of those examples has a boundary locally representable by the graph of a continuous function.

(3)

Finally, the main open question is whether the converse of our theorem holds for
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth
bounded domains: if
D
is pseudoconvex, does the Lempert function
l_{D}^{*}
satisfy a Weak Triangle Inequality?

2.
Sufficient conditions for the WTI

We first give a Lempert function version of a localization Lemma which goes back to Royden
[
15
]

for the case of the Kobayashi-Royden metric. For
A,B\subset D
, let
l_{D}^{*}:=\inf\{l_{D}^{*}(z,w):z\in A,w\in B\}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
V\Subset U
be two open sets such that
D\cap U
is nonempty and connected,
D\setminus U\neq\emptyset
;
let
z,w\in D\cap V
,
then

l_{D}^{*}(D\cap V,D\setminus U)l_{D\cap U}^{*}(z,w)\leq l_{D}^{*}(z,w).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and
\varphi
be any holomorphic map from
\mathbb{D}
to
D
such that
\varphi(0)=z
, and

\varphi(\alpha)=w
for some
\alpha<l_{D}^{*}(z,w)+\varepsilon
. If for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
r<1
,
\varphi(D(0,r))\subset D\cap U
,
then
l_{D}^{*}(z,w)=l_{D\cap U}^{*}(z,w)
and we are done. If not, let

r_{0}:=\sup\{r:\varphi(D(0,r))\subset D\cap U\}<1.

Since
\varphi(0)=z\in V
, we have
r_{0}\geq l_{D}^{*}(D\cap V,D\setminus U)
.
Let
\psi(\zeta):=\varphi(r_{0}\zeta)
for
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}
;

\psi(\mathbb{D})\subset D\cap U
, so
\frac{\alpha}{r_{0}}\geq l_{D\cap U}^{*}(z,w)
, thus

l_{D}^{*}(z,w)+\varepsilon>\alpha\geq l_{D\cap U}^{*}(z,w)l_{D}^{*}(D\cap V,D\setminus U).

Since this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we are done.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1
for
l_{D}^{*}
.

Suppose the result fails; then there exists for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
points
a_{k},b_{k},c_{k}\in D

such that

(3)

1\geq l_{D}^{*}(a_{k},c_{k})\geq k\left(l_{D}^{*}(a_{k},b_{k})+l_{D}^{*}(b_{k},c_{k})\right)\geq(\mbox{diam }D)^{-1}\left(\|a_{k}-b_{k}\|+\|b_{k}-c_{k}\|\right),

so passing to a subsequence all three points
a_{k},b_{k},c_{k}
tend to the same
p\in\overline{D}
.
If
p\in D
, then in a neighborhood of
p
,
l_{D}(z,w)\asymp\|z-w\|
, and (
3
) cannot hold
when
k
is large, so
p\in\partial D
.

Since
D
is strictly pseudoconvex, we may take neighborhoods
V\Subset U
of
p
such that
D\cap U
is biholomorphic to a convex domain. For
k
large enough,
a_{k},b_{k},c_{k}\in D\cap V
. By Lemma
4
,
l_{D}\asymp l_{D\cap U}
on

,
which contradicts (

) once again for possibly larger
k
.

3.
Necessary condition for the WTI

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We proceed by contradiction: supposing that
D
is not pseudoconvex, we will show that there are triples of points
which violate any WTI.

By
[
5
, Theorem 4.1.25]
, if
D
is not pseudoconvex, there exists
p\in\partial D
, a neighorhood
U
of
p
, and
an affine coordinate system in which
p=0
and
D\cap U\supset\mathbb{D}^{n}\cap\{\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}+q(z)<0\}
, where
q
is a
real valued polynomial, homogeneous of degree
2
, with
\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial z_{2}\partial\bar{z}_{2}}(0)=-1
.
Applying the Implicit Function Theorem, we can replace the inequation with
\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}<\Phi(z^{\prime})
,
z^{\prime}:=(z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

and
\nabla\Phi(0)=0
,
\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial z_{2}\partial\bar{z}_{2}}(0)=-1
. Then using the Taylor expansion
of
\Phi
we can find another real valued polynomial
q_{1}(z^{\prime})
, homogeneous of degree
2
, with

\frac{\partial^{2}q_{1}}{\partial z_{2}\partial\bar{z}_{2}}(0)<0
and
D\cap U\supset\mathbb{D}^{n}\cap\{\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}+q_{1}(z^{\prime})<0\}
.
Replacing
z_{1}
by
z_{1}-2\frac{\partial^{2}q_{1}}{\partial z_{2}^{2}}(0)z_{2}^{2}
, we keep only the term in
|z_{2}|^{2}

in the expansion of
q_{1}
.
Finally we can change the coordinates again to assume
\frac{\partial^{2}q_{1}}{\partial z_{2}\partial\bar{z}_{2}}(0)<-1
.

Altogether, we now have
G_{2}\times\{0\}\subset D\cap(\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\{0\})
, with
G_{2}:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}<|z_{2}|^{2}\}
.

Proposition 5
.

For
t\in(0,1)
, let
p_{t}:=(-t,0)
. Then for
0<\delta<\varepsilon
small enough,

l_{D}^{(2)*}(p_{\delta},p_{\varepsilon})\leq l_{G_{2}}^{(2)*}(p_{\delta},p_{\varepsilon})\leq 2\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}.

Proof.

Let
q_{\delta}:=(-\delta,\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\varepsilon^{1/2}})
. Considering the disc
\psi(\zeta)=(-\delta,\zeta)
,
we clearly have
l_{G_{2}}^{*}(p_{\delta},q_{\delta})\leq\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}
.

On the other hand, if we set
\zeta=x+iy
, the disc
\varphi(\zeta):=(-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/2}\zeta,\zeta)
satisfies

|\varphi_{2}(\zeta)|^{2}-\operatorname{\rm{Re}}\varphi_{1}(\zeta)=x^{2}+y^{2}+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{1/2}x\geq(x-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{1/2})^{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}>0,

so for
\varepsilon
small enough,
\varphi(\mathbb{D})\subset G_{2}
and since
\varphi(0)=p_{\varepsilon}
,

\varphi(\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\varepsilon^{1/2}})=q_{\delta}
,

l_{G_{2}}^{*}(p_{\varepsilon},p_{\delta})\leq 2\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}
.
∎

To prove the lower estimate for
l_{D}^{*}(p_{\delta},p_{\varepsilon})
, we will start with a special case where we can get
a more precise quantitative result.

Proposition 6
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
-smooth domain, for
0<\gamma\leq 1
, and
for
p\in\partial D
, let
p_{t}:=p+tn_{p}
, where
n_{p}
is the inner normal vector at
p
. Then for

0<\delta<\varepsilon
small enough,
l_{D}^{*}(p_{\varepsilon},p_{\delta})\gtrsim(\varepsilon-\delta)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2(1+\gamma)}-1}
.

This immediately implies the theorem for the special case of
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
-smooth boundary,
since for
\varepsilon
small enough
l_{D}^{*}(p_{\varepsilon},p_{\delta})\gg l_{D}^{(2)*}(p_{\varepsilon},p_{\delta})
.

Proof.

First take coordinates such that
p=0
,
n_{p}=(-1,0,\dots,0)
. Locally the domain can be
represented by
\{\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}<\Phi(\operatorname{\rm{Im}}z_{1},z^{\prime})\}
, with
z^{\prime}:=(z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

and
\nabla\Phi(0,0)=0
. The regularity of the boundary implies that
\Phi(\operatorname{\rm{Im}}z_{1},z^{\prime})\leq C(|\operatorname{\rm{Im}}z_{1}|^{1+\gamma}+\|z^{\prime}\|^{1+\gamma})
in a neighborhood of
(0,0)
. Finally, another change of variables allows us
to consider that
D\cap\mathbb{D}^{n}\subset\tilde{G}_{1+\gamma}
,
where
\tilde{G}_{\mu}:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{n}:\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z_{1}<|\operatorname{\rm{Im}}z_{1}|^{\mu}+\|z^{\prime}\|^{\mu}\}
.
We again denote
p_{t}:=(-t,0)
in those new coordinates (which changes the coordinates by a bounded multiple).

Using Lemma
4
, it will be enough to prove the conclusion of the Proposition for
D\cap\mathbb{D}^{n}
,
so by the contracting property of the Lempert function, the proof will conclude with the following Lemma.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mu\in(\frac{1}{2},2]
. Then
l_{\tilde{G}_{\mu}}^{*}(p_{\varepsilon},p_{\delta})\gtrsim(\varepsilon-\delta)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2\mu}-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}):\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow D
be a holomorphic map such that
\varphi(0)=p_{\varepsilon}
,
and there is
\alpha>0
such that
\varphi(\alpha)=p_{\delta}
. Write
B_{\alpha}(\zeta):=\zeta\frac{\zeta-\alpha}{1-\alpha\zeta}
.
For
j\geq 2
,
\varphi_{j}(\zeta)=h_{j}(\zeta)B_{\alpha}(\zeta)
where

is a holomorphic function with

.

Choose
\beta
such that
(2\beta^{2})^{\mu}=\varepsilon
.
We may assume
\alpha<\beta
,
since the conclusion holds otherwise. For

and
|\zeta|\leq\beta

. This implies that
\varphi_{1}(D(0,\beta))\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{\rm{Re}}z<C_{\mu}(\varepsilon+|\operatorname{\rm{Im}}z_{1}|^{\mu})\}=:P
.

Define a map
f

f(\xi):=-(-\xi+\varepsilon)^{1/2}
,
where the argument of the square root is taken in

We then have

∎

Remark 10
.

If
D:=\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus L
, where
L
is a real line,
D
is not pseudoconvex, but for any
z,w\in D
,
l_{D}^{*}(z,w)=l_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}^{*}(z,w)
,
and as a consequence
l_{D}
verifies the triangle inequality.

Proof.

Using automorphisms of the ball, we can reduce ourselves to the case where
L=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
. Considering discs of the form

\{|z_{1}|<\frac{1}{2},z_{2}=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{4}z_{1}^{2}\}
with
n\to\infty
shows that the Kontinuitätsatz fails for this domain.

Given any
z,w\in D
,
l_{D}^{*}(z,w)\geq l_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}^{*}(z,w)
by inclusion. If the complex line
\Lambda
through
z
and
w

does not intersect

(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})\cap\mathbb{B}^{2}
, it provides an extremal disc and we are done. Otherwise, if
\Lambda\neq\mathbb{C}\times\{0\}
,
let
\{(a,0)\}=\Lambda\cap(\mathbb{C}\times\{0\})
, with
a\in(-1;1)
, since the intersection point of the complex lines must be unique.
Choose
p=(a+i\varepsilon,0)
with
\varepsilon>0
. If
\zeta\mapsto\varphi(\zeta)
is a linear parametrization of
\Lambda
,
with
\zeta_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
\varphi(\zeta_{0})=(a,0)
; there exists
a vector valued polynomial interpolation map
P_{\varepsilon}
of degree
2
such that
P(\zeta)
when
\varphi(\zeta)\in\{z,w\}
,
and
P(\zeta_{0})=(i\varepsilon,0)
. When
\varepsilon
is small enough,
\|P\|_{\infty}
and
\|P^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
are small so that by the implicit function
theorem, there is a unique point
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
such that
(\varphi+P_{\varepsilon})(\zeta_{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{C}\times\{0\}
, so that

(\varphi+P_{\varepsilon})(\mathbb{D})
avoids
\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
. Restricting the map to a slightly smaller disc provides a candidate for

l_{D}^{*}(z,w)
, and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we see that
l_{D}^{*}(z,w)\leq l_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}^{*}(z,w)
.

If
z,w\in(\mathbb{B}^{2}\cap\mathbb{C}\times\{0\})\setminus(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})
, then we can perform the same reasoning with

\zeta\mapsto(\zeta,\varepsilon P(\zeta))
,
P
a polynomial of degree
2
vanishing exactly at
z
and
w
.
∎

On the other hand, if we consider
D:=\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus P
, where
P
is a totally real plane, then reasoning with the implicit function theorem,
we see that complex discs cannot avoid
P
. And of course if
D:=\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus(\mathbb{C}\times\{0\})
,
D
is pseudoconvex
(and we do not know whether a weak triangle inequality holds there).
