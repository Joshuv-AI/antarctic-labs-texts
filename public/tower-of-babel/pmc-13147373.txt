Integration of whole genome resequencing and transcriptomics reveals key candidate genes and pathway for fatty acid and amino acid compositions among duck breeds
Authors: Hengli Xie, Xinyue Hu, Shangmin Wang, Hehe Liu, Zongliang He, Hongjie Ji, Kunpeng Lv, Ting Zhu, Jiwen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe differences fatty acids and amino acids composition may be key factors influencing flavor variation among duck breeds. This study systematically analyzed the fatty acids and amino acids compositions in the pectoral muscle of 49 days old Cherry valley ducks (CV), Jinling white ducks (JL), and Liancheng white ducks (LC). Fatty acid analysis identified 27 fatty acids that exhibited significant differences across breeds (P < 0.05). The CV group had the highest total saturated fatty acid (SFAs) content, while total monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFAs) content ranked in the order of LC > JL > CV (P < 0.05). The total polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) content was significantly higher in both the JL and LC groups than in the CV group (P < 0.05), while the LC group showed a higher content than the JL group, though the difference was not significant. Amino acid analysis identified 20 amino acids that showed significant differences among breeds (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the total essential amino acids (EAAs) and non-essential amino acids (NEAAs) content ranked in the order of LC > JL > CV (P < 0.05). Whole-genome resequencing identified 2734 selected genes. The joint pathway analysis revealed co-enrichment of 9 fatty acids and 110 selected genes across 25 KEGG pathways, as well as 20 amino acids and 266 selected genes co-enriched in 78 KEGG pathways. RNA sequencing revealed 2539 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between JL and CV, 6574 DEGs between JL and LC, and 7072 DEGs between LC and CV. Venn analysis showed that 9 and 24 of these DEGs overlapped with selected genes associated with fatty acid and amino acid variation, respectively. Further analysis revealed that the DEGs MGLL, NDUFV1, NDUFS4, and GNG10 may cooperatively regulate retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, thereby jointly influencing the deposition levels of arachidonic acid, γ-Aminobutyric-Acid, and l-glutamate in duck pectoral muscle. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the genetic improvement of meat quality traits.Keywords: Amino acid, Duck, Fatty acid, Transcriptomics, Whole genome resequencing

Introduction

Duck meat has gained popularity among consumers due to its high content of digestible protein, polyunsaturated fatty acids, and vitamins, making it an important component of poultry products (Chen et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025, 2023; Zhu et al., 2022). With rising living standards, consumer preferences have shifted from an emphasis on quantity to a growing demand for quality. Significant differences in meat quality and flavor exist among different duck breeds, as well as among different strains within the same genetic background (Zhang et al., 2023a; Zhu et al., 2022). However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Studies have demonstrated that amino acids and fatty acid undergo a series of reactions to generate various flavor compounds, serving as the primary source of meat flavor (Xing and Yaylayan, 2022). In addition, essential amino acids (EAAs) positively contribute to the nutritional value of meat (Tang et al., 2021). Furthermore, the fatty acids content in muscle tissue influences meat quality traits such as color, juiciness, flavor, and nutritional value (Haraf et al., 2021). Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) can undergo oxidation to produce various volatile flavor compounds, thereby significantly enhancing meat quality (Wang et al., 2021). Different duck breeds or strains exhibit distinct meat quality and flavor characteristics. In conclusion, the composition of fatty acids and amino acids is closely related to duck meat quality and varies by breed. Therefore, the flavor differences among breeds are closely related to the contents of fatty acids and amino acids. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying the differences in fatty acids and amino acids are still not well understood.

In conclusion, understanding the differences in fatty acids and amino acids composition and their underlying molecular mechanisms is essential for improving duck meat nutritional value and flavor. With the development of sequencing technology, whole genome resequencing (WGRS) is considered to be of great significance for the breeding and selection of ducks (Zhu et al., 2024). Furthermore, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) is an efficient technique that provides extensive genetic data and has been widely used to study meat quality mechanisms in livestock and poultry (Liu et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022). Integrated analysis of WGRS and transcriptome data serves as an effective approach to identifying functional genes involved in biological processes. Differences in expression at the transcriptional level also serve to validate genomic selection signals. Cherry valley duck (CV) is a large meat breed characterized by fast growth and high meat production (Zhang et al., 2023b), whereas the Liancheng white duck (LC) is a rare native Chinese waterfowl, recognized for its unique meat flavor, texture, and medicinal value (Tang et al., 2023). Additionally, the Jinling white duck (JL) has a bloodline of CV and LC. Therefore, this study employed three duck breeds (CV, JL, and LC) to investigate differences in fatty acid and amino acid content. By integrating WGRS and transcriptomic analyses, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing fatty acid and amino acid composition in duck meat, thereby providing novel insights and strategies for meat quality improvement.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample preparation

Ducks of the three breeds (CV, JL, and LC) were hatched at the same time and raised under identical conditions with ad libitum access to water and feed until 49 days of age. At 49 days of age, a total of 39 female ducks (13 per breed) were randomly selected and subjected to an 8 hour fast. After fasting, the 39 ducks were weighed. The average body weights (mean ± SEM) of CV, JL, and LC ducks were 3950.83 ± 81.51 g, 2414.62 ± 27.82 g, and 844.62 ± 14.70 g, respectively. Blood was collected from the inferior wing vein into EDTA-containing tubes (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) and stored at −20 °C. After slaughter by exsanguination via the carotid artery, samples from the left pectoralis major muscle were collected, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China), and immediately stored at −80 °C. Among these, pectoral muscle samples from 5 ducks per breed were used for amino acid and fatty acid analysis, while pectoral muscle and blood samples from the remaining 8 ducks per breed were subjected to RNA-seq and whole genome resequencing, respectively. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Animal Protection and Utilization, Chengdu Campus, Sichuan, China; Permit Number: DKY20170913). The CV, JL, and LC ducks used in this study were sourced from the Nanjing Institute of Animal Husbandry and Poultry Science.

Determination of muscle fatty acids content

Fatty acids were analyzed based on the Chinese National standard for food safety - Determination of fatty acids in food (GB5009.168 - 2016). An appropriate amount of each sample was ground into powder under liquid nitrogen after removing fascia, tendons, and adipose tissue. The powder was added to 6 mL of 10% acetyl chloride-methanol solution (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) in a sealed tube under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was shaken thoroughly and incubated in a water bath at 80 °C for 2 h, with shaking every 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction solution was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The original tube was rinsed three times with 3 mL of sodium carbonate solution, and the washings were combined with the reaction mixture and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was subjected to gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Agilent, Palo Alto, USA) analysis.

Determination of muscle amino acids content

Amino acid content was determined according to the Chinese National standard for food safety–Determination of fatty acids in food (GB5009.124–2016). Briefly, an appropriate amount of pectoral muscle sample was frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powder after removing fascia, tendons, and adipose tissue. One gram of the sample was weighed into a hydrolysis tube, mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L HCl (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China), and then frozen for 3 - 5 min. The tube was evacuated, flushed with nitrogen, and sealed. Hydrolysis was performed at 110 °C for 22 h in a drying oven. After cooling to room temperature, the hydrolysate was transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask, diluted with ultrapure water, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. A 1.0 mL aliquot of the filtrate was transferred to a test tube and evaporated to dryness at 50 °C. The residue was redissolved in 1 - 2 mL of water and dried again under the same conditions. The dried sample was then dissolved in 2.0 mL of sodium citrate buffer (pH 2.2). Quantitative analysis was performed using an automatic amino acid analyzer equipped with a cation-exchange column (4.6 mm × 60 mm, 3 μm) and a post-column ninhydrin derivatization system. Amino acid concentrations were determined by external standard calibration based on chromatographic peak areas obtained at 570 nm and 440 nm.

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 24 pectoral muscle tissue samples using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen, Waltham, CA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). RNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China). After filtering low-quality reads through standard quality control using FastQC software (v0.12.1), clean reads were obtained. These clean reads were aligned to the Peking duck reference genome (GCF_015476345.1) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2019). Expression levels of transcripts were quantified using featureCounts (v1.6.0) (Liao et al., 2014). The FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript Per Million mapped reads) value for each gene was then calculated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package (v1.44.0) with P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.6) on the R platform (v4.5.1).

Whole genome resequencing

The genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples of 24 ducks using a Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit based on a modified phenol-chloroform method (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The quality of the extracted genomic DNA was verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Library construction and sequencing were performed on the Illumina platform at LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd (Zhejiang, China).

Selective sweep analysis and annotation

Raw sequencing data were processed using Trimmomatic v0.39 to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the Peking duck reference genome (GCF_015476345.1) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA v0.7.17) with the default parameters “mem -k 32 -M”. The resulting alignments were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools v1.15.1 (Li and Durbin, 2009). PCR duplicates were removed and base quality score recalibration (BQSR) was performed using the Sentieon toolkit. Variant calling was conducted across all samples with GATK v1.93.2 (McKenna, et al., 2010), and the resulting variants were filtered using PLINK v1.90 (Purcell, et al., 2007). SNP genotyping was performed using STITCH v1.68 (Davies, et al., 2016).

Nucleotide differentiation coefficient (Fst) and population nucleotide diversity ratio (π ratio) were calculated using PopGenome (Pfeifer et al., 2014). Mean Fst was estimated in 100 kb sliding windows with a 10 kb step size. The π ratio was log-transformed for normalization. The top 10% of Fst values and θπ ratios were used as thresholds for selective sweep detection. Genes located within genomic regions under selection were annotated and defined as selected genes. The selected genes annotation was performed using the GenomicRanges package (v1.54.0) on the R platform (v4.5.1). Functional enrichment analysis of the candidate genes was subsequently conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.6).

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA combined with Duncan's multiple range test was used for significance testing. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while P > 0.05 indicated no significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Fatty acid differential analysis

A total of 37 fatty acids were detected in the pectoral muscle of the three duck breeds, which were classified by saturation as 16 saturated fatty acids (SFAs), 8 monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and 13 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) (Table 1). Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in 27 fatty acids among the duck breeds. The total SFAs content in the CV group was significantly higher than that in the LC and JL groups (P < 0.05). The JL group showed a lower total SFAs content compared to LC, but the difference was not significant (Table 1). The total MUFAs content in the LC group was significantly higher than that in the CV and JL groups (P < 0.05), while the JL group had a significantly higher total MUFAs content than the CV group (P < 0.05). The total PUFAs content in the JL and LC groups was significantly higher than that in the CV group (P < 0.05). On the contrary, the total PUFAs content in the LC group was higher than that in the JL group, but the difference was not significant (Table 1).

Amino acid differential analysis

As shown in Table 2, a total of 23 amino acids were identified, comprising 8 essential (EAAs) and 11 non-essential amino acids (NEAAs), and 4 other amino acids. Statistical analysis revealed that 20 of these amino acids exhibited significant differences among the duck breeds (P < 0.05). The contents of total EAAs and NEAAs in the LC group were significantly higher than those in the JL and CV groups (P < 0.05). Similarly, the total EAAs and NEAAs content in the JL group showed significantly higher levels than the CV group (P < 0.05).

WGRS selective sweep signal analysis

WGRS was performed on a total of 24 ducks from three breeds. After quality control, 649.68 Gb of clean bases were obtained, with Q30 > 91.91%, Q20 > 96.30%, and an average GC content of 42.76%. When aligned to the Peking duck reference genome, the mapping rate exceeded 98.52%, and the average sequencing depth per individual reached 31 × (Supplementary Table S1). To enhance the detection of genome-wide selective signatures, Fst values and θπ ratios of SNP variants between JL and CV were calculated. The top 10% of Fst values and θπ ratios were used as thresholds for selective sweep analysis, identifying 1368 selected genes (Fig. 1A). Similarly, applying the same thresholds to the JL and LC comparison identified 1067 selected genes (Fig. 1B). For the LC and CV groups, the same thresholds were used, identifying 1122 selected genes (Fig. 1C). Therefore, a total of 2734 selected genes were obtained from the above three comparison groups.

Joint pathway analysis

To investigate the correlation between the selected genes (identified by WGRS) and fatty acid/amino acid profiles, joint pathway analysis was performed. This analysis revealed that 9 fatty acids (including C20:4n6, C18:1n9, C18:0, C16:1n7, C14:0, C12:0, C10:0, C16:0, and C4:0) and 110 selected genes were co-enriched in 25 KEGG pathways (Fig. 2A). These pathways are primarily associated with fatty acid metabolism, steroid metabolism, and protein digestion and absorption. In addition, 20 amino acids (including Val, Trp, Ile, His, Phe, Met, Leu, Lys, Tyr, Ser, Gly, Glu, Gln, Asp, Asn, Ala, Pro, γ-glutamate-cysteine, l-carnosine, γ-aminobutyric-acid) and 266 selected genes were co-enriched in 78 KEGG pathways (Fig. 2B). These pathways are primarily implicated in signal transduction, amino acid metabolism, and glutathione metabolism.

Transcriptome differential expression and functional analysis

RNA-seq quality information is presented in Table S2. Principal component analysis (PCA) of transcriptome sequencing data (Fig. 3A) revealed relatively clustered intra-group and dispersed inter-group distribution among the three duck breeds. Differential analysis revealed 2539 DEGs in the JL vs. CV comparison, including 1407 downregulated and 1132 upregulated genes (Fig. 3B). The JL vs. LC comparison yielded 6574 DEGs, with 3355 downregulated and 3219 upregulated (Fig. 3C), while the LC vs. CV comparison resulted in 7072 DEGs, comprising 3556 downregulated and 3516 upregulated genes (Fig. 3D). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that DEGs in the JL vs. CV comparison were significantly enriched in 41 pathways, including fatty acid metabolism, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, fatty acid degradation, pyruvate metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, apelin signaling pathway. These pathways are associated with fatty acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and cellular signal transduction (Fig. 4A). DEGs from JL vs. LC were significantly enriched in 90 pathways, several of which are associated with fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. Notable examples include fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, proteasome, propanoate metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, and the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (Fig. 4B). Similarly, LC vs. CV DEGs were enriched in 84 pathways. Several of these pathways are associated with fatty acid and amino acid metabolism function, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation; the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, fatty acid metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, and lysine degradation (Fig. 4C).

Screening of key regulatory genes and pathways

To further identify key genes and pathways influencing the variation in fatty acid and amino acid content in ducks, Venn analysis was performed between the 110 and 266 selected genes (co-enriched with fatty acids and amino acids, respectively) and the DEGs across the three breeds. The results revealed 9 key DEGs associated with inter-breed differences in fatty acid content (Fig. 5A) and 24 key DEGs associated with inter-breed differences in amino acid content (Fig. 5B). Notably, 8 genes (MGLL, CDC42, OXCT1, COL26A1, NDUFV1, VAV3, GNG10, NDUFS4) overlapped between these sets.

Discussion

China is the world's largest producer of duck meat, which holds a significant position in the poultry market (Bai et al., 2020; Zeng et al., 2016). Currently, there is an increasing consumer demand for higher-quality duck meat. Studies have demonstrated that the fatty acid composition in muscle influences poultry meat quality and flavor, with different fatty acids contributing distinctly. Higher SFAs levels negatively impact human health (Katan et al., 1994). In this study, CV exhibited significantly higher SFAs content than JL and LC, primarily attributed to its elevated C16:0 content, which has been reported as detrimental to human health (Temme et al., 1996). Furthermore, LC pectoral muscle contained higher MUFAs and PUFAs levels than both JL and CV, while JL surpassed CV in both MUFAs and PUFAs content. Compared to saturated fatty acids, unsaturated fatty acids are more prone to oxidation and have been shown to contribute more significantly to meat flavor (Bassam et al., 2022; Onk et al., 2019). These findings collectively indicate that LC possesses a superior fatty acid profile to JL and CV, consistent with previous reports describing slower-growing ducks as more conducive to the accumulation of flavor compounds in muscle (Huo et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023). Amino acids contribute to meat flavor and texture through Strecker degradation and Maillard reactions, making their composition and concentration crucial for nutritional value and sensory quality (Roobab et al., 2024). This study observed a descending gradient in both EAAs and NEAAs content across LC, JL, and CV pectoral muscle. Specifically, Val, Lys, Ser, Pro, His, and Asp followed this identical pattern. As Val, Lys, Ser, and Pro are associated with sweet notes, while His and Asp are linked to umami taste (Xu et al., 2022; Zhuang et al., 2016), the amino acid profile confirms the superiority of LC over JL and CV, with JL being superior to CV. This aligns with established findings that amino acid accumulation differs between slow- and fast-growing poultry breeds (Tran et al., 2021).

Research has demonstrated that genetic selection serves as the most effective tool for enhancing poultry meat quality (Mir et al., 2017). Consequently, a better understanding of the genetic background and molecular markers associated with duck meat quality traits is crucial for auxiliary breeding of high-quality ducks. Therefore, transcriptome sequencing and WGRS were performed on pectoral muscle tissue and blood from CV, LC, and JL. WGRS results revealed numerous SNPs and indels among breeds. Selective sweep analysis was performed using the top 10% of Fst values and θπ ratios of SNPs and indels among breeds as thresholds. A total of 2734 selected genes were obtained from the above three comparison groups. To further identify genetic variations associated with interbreed differences in fatty acid and amino acid composition, integrated pathway analysis was subsequently performed by combining the differential fatty acids/amino acids with the aforementioned 2734 selected genes. The results revealed that 9 differential fatty acids (including C20:4n6, C18:1n9, C18:0, C16:1n7, C14:0, C12:0, C10:0, C16:0, and C4:0) and 110 selected genes were co-enriched in 25 KEGG pathways, primarily involved in fatty acid metabolism, steroid metabolism, and protein digestion and absorption. Additionally, 20 amino acids with significant interbreed differences (including valine, tryptophan, isoleucine, histidine, phenylalanine, methionine, leucine, lysine, tyrosine, serine, glycine, l-glutamate, glutamine, aspartic acid, asparagine, alanine, proline, γ-glutamyl cysteine, l-carnitine, and γ-aminobutyric acid) and 266 selected genes were co-enriched in 78 KEGG pathways, many of which are related to signal transduction, amino acid metabolism, and glutathione metabolism.

Additionally, RNA-seq analysis revealed 2539 DEGs in the JL vs. CV comparison, 6574 DEGs in the JL vs. LC comparison, and 7072 DEGs in the LC vs. CV comparison. To further identify key genes and pathways influencing variations in duck fatty acid and amino acid content, Venn analysis was performed between the aforementioned 110 and 266 selected genes and DEGs across the three breeds. This identified 9 key DEGs associated with fatty acid variation and 24 key DEGs associated with amino acid variation. Furthermore, l-glutamate, γ-aminobutyric acid, arachidonic acid, and the DEGs (MGLL, NDUFV1, NDUFS4, GNG10) were found to be co-enriched in the retrograde endocannabinoid signaling pathway. This pathway is related to sugar metabolism and lipid metabolism (O'Hare et al., 2011). Within this pathway, 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) is synthesized as a downstream product of DAG and subsequently hydrolyzed by monoacylglycerol lipase (encoded by MGLL) to yield arachidonic acid (Syal et al., 2020). Furthermore, the 2-AG signaling system, mitochondrial energy metabolism governed by NDUFV1/NDUFS4, and GPCR signaling involving GNG10 collectively modulate cellular energy status, which in turn influences the synthesis of γ-aminobutyric acid and l-glutamate (Bahadur Patel et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2013). It has been reported that arachidonic acid, an important unsaturated fatty acid, can improve the flavor of meat (Takahashi, 2018). l-glutamate is a natural umami substance, and its content directly determines the umami taste of meat broth and meat itself. γ-aminobutyric acid is associated with food fermentation and is related to the prevention of certain diseases (Barido et al., 2022; Shin Yee, et al., 2024). In summary, our findings suggest that the MGLL, NDUFV1, NDUFS4, and GNG10 genes may function coordinately to regulate the retrograde endocannabinoid signaling pathway, thereby jointly modulating the deposition of key fatty acids and amino acids in duck pectoral muscle and ultimately shaping its flavor profile. These findings offer new candidate key genes for future related research.

Conclusions

This study systematically analyzed the differences in the composition of fatty acids and amino acids in the breast pectoral of LC, CV, and JL. LC ducks were superior to JL and CV in both the content and composition of these compounds, while JL ducks were superior to CV. By integrating WGRS and RNA-seq, the key genes (MGLL, NDUFV1, NDUFS4, GNG10) and pathway (retrograde endocannabinoid signaling pathway) underlying the differences in fatty acid (arachidonic acid) and amino acid (γ-aminobutyric-acid, l-glutamate) distribution among duck breeds were identified (Fig. 6). These findings provide new candidate key genes for future related research on duck meat quality improvement and the molecular breeding of high-quality meat duck varieties.
