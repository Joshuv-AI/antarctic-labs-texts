Prognostic performance of ICU scoring systems in patients with HIV/AIDS: a retrospective analysis in the western Brazilian Amazon
Authors: Viviane Lago de Oliveira, Taynná Vernalha Rocha Almeida, Heiner Borges da Silva Junior, Taysa Litaiff Isper Abrahim Alves, Antônio Luiz Boechat, Yury Oliveira Chaves, Guilherme Augusto Pivoto João, Fernando Fonseca de Almeida e Val, Monique Freire dos Reis, Luiz Carlos de Lima Ferreira, Roberta Lins Gonçalves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite advances in HIV treatment, low- and middle-income countries face challenges due to reduced funding for diagnostic and treatment programs. People living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) remain at high risk of severe complications and ICU admission. Prognostic scoring systems, an important benchmark of quality in ICU care, often lack validation for this population and specific clinical timeframes.ObjectiveTo evaluate the performance of seven prognostic scoring systems within the first 24 and 72 h of ICU admission among PLWHA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at Fundação de Medicina Tropical Doutor Heitor Vieira Dourado (Manaus, Brazil) and included 103 adult PLWHA. Prognostic models (APACHE II, SAPS III, ODIN, MPM II, SOFA, and MODS) were assessed using ROC curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test for calibration.ResultsThe study cohort had a median age of 34 years (IQR: 28–43), with 76.7% male patients. Most were non-adherent (54.4%) or irregularly adherent (22.3%) to antiretroviral therapy (ART). The median CD4+ count was 57 cells/µL (IQR: 18–179), and 83.1% had a detectable viral load (> 50 copies/mm³). In the first 24 h, five systems demonstrated modest discriminatory power (MPM II 24 h: AUC 0.665; ODIN: 0.649; APACHE II: 0.619). At 72 h, MPM II 72 h had the highest performance (AUC 0.802), followed by MODS (0.780) and SOFA (0.712). All models demonstrated good calibration, with the MPM II 72 h having higher sensitivity and accuracy.ConclusionThe systems demonstrated limited performance in the first 24 h, but MPM II 72 h showed better reliability and discrimination. These findings highlight the need for validated prognostic tools tailored to PLWHA in resource-limited settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have significantly reduced the progression to AIDS and related complications in many parts of the world, particularly in high-income countries. Nevertheless, healthcare systems in low and middle-income regions continue to face major challenges, including late diagnosis, inconsistent adherence to treatment, and limited access to specialized care. These barriers contribute to persistent risks of severe complications and admission to intensive care units (ICUs) among people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) [1, 2].

In Brazil, HIV care is universally provided through the public health system, which has ensured free access to ART since 1996. Despite this progress, gaps in adherence and delays in diagnosis remain critical drivers of advanced disease and ICU admission. For instance, from the beginning of the epidemic in 1980 until December 2023, 392,981 deaths due to HIV/AIDS were reported in the country, with 23,560 (6.0%) of these occurring in the northern region [3]. Since the onset of the HIV epidemic, ICU management has played an important role in the treatment of severe HIV/AIDS cases. Despite expanded access to ART globally, ICU admissions remain prevalent for patients with advanced-stage disease primarily due to inconsistent treatment adherence and delayed diagnosis [3, 4]. Prognostic scoring systems are commonly used in ICU settings to assess disease severity and predict outcomes. These include the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II), the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS III), and the Mortality Probability Model (MPM II) [5–7]. Additionally, certain scoring systems, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Organ Dysfunction and Infection (ODIN), and Multiple Organ Dysfunction Score (MODS), incorporate clinical data collected during the first 72 h of ICU admission to enhance prognostic accuracy [8–10].

However, the direct application of these models to PLWHA may not be straightforward. This population presents specific pathophysiological features that can influence prognostic accuracy. Immunosuppression due to low CD4+ counts alters host responses to infection and organ dysfunction. Opportunistic infections, such as pneumocystosis, cerebral toxoplasmosis, and tuberculosis, frequently drive critical illness and may not be adequately represented in models calibrated for general ICU populations [1, 2]. Moreover, PLWHA often carry multiple comorbidities, including cardiometabolic complications associated with chronic ART exposure and aging, which may confound outcome predictions. These distinct clinical patterns can lead to over or underestimation of mortality risk when general ICU prognostic systems are applied without prior validation in this group.

Despite their widespread use, limited research has assessed the validity of these models in specific populations such as PLWHA, who present with unique clinical challenges including immunosuppression, opportunistic infections, and comorbidities. Validating and adapting prognostic systems for this group is essential to improve clinical decision-making, optimize resource allocation, and ultimately reduce mortality [11, 12].

The selection of appropriate timeframes for patient evaluation is critical. The first 24 h of ICU admission represent a decisive window for initial treatment decisions, whilst the 72-hour mark provides further insight into disease progression and response to interventions. However, no consensus has been established regarding the most effective prognostic scoring system for this population at these time points, underscoring the need for further investigation.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study aimed to evaluate the performance of seven prognostic scoring systems—APACHE II, SAPS III, ODIN, MPM II, SOFA, and MODS—within the first 24 and 72 h of ICU admission in a cohort of PLWHA.

The study was conducted in a referral center for infectious diseases in Manaus, western Brazilian Amazon. This setting shares socioeconomic and healthcare challenges with other resource-limited regions, particularly within LMICs, such as constrained infrastructure and delays in access to care. However, specific local factors, including the unique epidemiology of the Amazon region and the profile of a specialized infectious disease ICU, should be considered when generalizing our findings to other settings.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study included consecutive adult PLWHA (≥ 18 years) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Fundação de Medicina Tropical Doutor Heitor Vieira Dourado (FMT-HVD) between September 2018 and December 2019. Patients were followed until hospital discharge or in-hospital death; therefore, the dataset constitutes a retrospective cohort of ICU admissions with prospective ascertainment of outcomes. We report the study in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [13].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of the Fundação de Medicina Tropical Doutor Heitor Vieira Dourado (CEP/FMT-HVD), under the following protocol numbers: CAAE: 98665118.6.0000.0005, Opinion No. 2.227.259, and retrospective approval No. 4.882.245. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the exclusive use of anonymized data extracted from medical records, obtaining individual informed consent from participants was not feasible. In accordance with Brazilian regulations — Resolutions No. 466/2012 and No. 510/2016 of the National Health Council — a formal waiver of informed consent was granted by the CEP/FMT-HVD. The research team also signed a Term of Commitment for Data Use (TCUD), ensuring confidentiality and ethical handling of all data collected. This waiver is formally documented and available upon request.

Settings

The study was conducted at Fundação de Medicina Tropical Doutor Heitor Vieira Dourado (FMT-HVD), a referral center for infectious diseases located in Manaus, Amazonas, Brazil. The hospital serves a diverse population from urban and rural remote areas of the Amazon region, frequently facing resource limitations similar to those seen in other low- and middle-income settings [3]. The ICU admits patients with advanced-stage HIV/AIDS and severe opportunistic infections.

Participants

The study included adult PLWHA (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU between September 2018 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria required complete clinical and laboratory data for both 24-hour and 72-hour time points. Patients were excluded if they were discharged or died within 72 h of admission, if they were pregnant or postpartum (30 days after childbirth), had insufficient medical records to calculate prognostic scores, were diagnosed with brain death, or were readmitted to the ICU during the study period.

It is important to note that advanced HIV disease (AHD), defined by a CD4 count < 200 cells/µL or an AIDS-defining event, was not an inclusion criterion. The high prevalence of AHD in this cohort reflects the natural clinical profile of PLWHA requiring ICU admission in this setting, rather than a selection bias.

As this was a retrospective analysis, a formal sample size calculation was not performed a priori. We included all consecutive eligible adult PLWHA admitted during the study period (n = 103). The observed AUCs and their 95% confidence intervals (Tables 6 and 8) provide post-hoc information about the precision of our estimates; for example, the MPM II 72 h AUC of 0.802 has a 95% CI of 0.700–0.904, indicating reasonable precision for exploratory validation.

Data collection

Data were collected from electronic and physical medical records. Variables included vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature), laboratory tests (arterial blood gas, hematocrit, leukocyte count, bilirubin, creatinine, CD4+ T lymphocyte count, HIV viral load), and clinical interventions (invasive mechanical ventilation use, vasopressors drugs use, presence of central venous lines). Primary reasons for ICU admission were recorded and are summarized in Table 1. In our cohort, respiratory failure was the most common indication (79.6%). Opportunistic infections, including pneumocystosis (12.6%), pulmonary tuberculosis (19.4%), and cerebral toxoplasmosis (13.6%), were frequent contributors to critical illness, indicating that many admissions were HIV-related. AIDS was defined according to CDC criteria, including CD4+ counts below 200 cells/mm³, opportunistic infections (e.g., pneumocystosis, cerebral toxoplasmosis), or AIDS-defining neoplasms (e.g., Kaposi’s sarcoma).

Prognostic scoring systems

The prognostic scoring systems evaluated in this study were selected for their widespread use in intensive care settings and their ability to predict mortality risk through various physiological and clinical parameters. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) system measures severity of illness by incorporating vital signs, laboratory results, and chronic health conditions [6]. Similarly, the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS III) is designed to predict mortality upon ICU admission and includes global data adjustments [7]. The Organ Dysfunction and Infection (ODIN) score evaluates the number and severity of organ dysfunctions, particularly in patients with infections [9]. The Mortality Probability Model (MPM II) assesses the probability of mortality at multiple time points, using non-physiological factors such as age and the reason for ICU admission [8]. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) focuses on the progression of organ dysfunction and is frequently used to monitor ICU patients with sepsis and other critical conditions [10]. Finally, the Multiple Organ Dysfunction Score (MODS) quantifies the degree of dysfunction in various organ systems over time to predict mortality risk [11]. Each score was calculated using standardized algorithms at 24 and 72 h after admission to assess both early and evolving clinical risks.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population. Categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality. Comparisons between survivors and non-survivors were performed using the Mann-Whitney test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The primary outcome for discrimination analyses was in-hospital mortality, defined as death during the index hospitalization versus survival to discharge. Each prognostic score was evaluated for its ability to discriminate this outcome using ROC/AUC analyses.

Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors of mortality, with variables having p-values < 0.25 selected for inclusion in a multivariate logistic regression model. The final model was developed using backward stepwise elimination, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to assess associations between predictors and mortality outcomes.

To evaluate the performance of prognostic scoring systems, discrimination and calibration analyses were conducted. Discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which plot sensitivity against 1-specificity across a range of cutoff values. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to quantify the model’s discriminatory ability. AUC values were categorized as poor (AUC < 0.690), acceptable (0.700–0.799), good (0.800–0.899), or excellent (≥ 0.900) [17]. To compare the AUCs between scoring systems at 24 and 72 h, the non-parametric method of de Long was used [14], while the algorithm of Sun and Xu provided additional verification of significance and statistical power [15].

Calibration, which measures the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. This test divides patients into deciles based on predicted mortality risk and compares observed and expected mortality rates within each decile. A p-value greater than 0.05 in this test indicated good calibration [16].

Comparisons of prognostic scores across subgroups were performed using the Mann-Whitney test for two-group analyses and the Kruskal-Wallis test for three or more groups. These analyses assessed whether differences in scoring system performance were statistically significant across various clinical and demographic variables.

Additional performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy, were calculated. Sensitivity measured the proportion of correctly identified deaths, while specificity quantified the proportion of correctly identified survivors. PPV and NPV indicated the likelihood that predicted outcomes (death or survival) were correct. Overall accuracy reflected the proportion of correctly classified patients in the entire sample.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.5) to ensure reproducibility and statistical reliability.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 103 adult PLWHA were included, admitted to the ICU between September 2018 and December 2019. The process of patient identification, selection, and inclusion is summarized in Fig. 1. The median age was 34 years (IQR: 28–43), with 76.7% being male. Most patients (76.7%) were either non-adherent or irregularly adherent to ART. Lower CD4 counts and detectable viral loads were prevalent among both survivors and non-survivors. Calibration and subgroup analyses

The Hosmer-Lemeshow test demonstrated good calibration (p > 0.05) across all models, indicating reliable alignment between predicted and observed mortality risks. Sensitivity and other predictive performance measures further support the MPM II 72 h as the best-performing model. Subgroup analyses revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) in prognostic scores among groups stratified by CD4 count, viral load, and organ dysfunction.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the prognostic performance of scoring systems in critically ill PLWHA improved significantly between 24 and 72 h of ICU admission. Within the first 24 h, discriminatory ability was modest, with the MPM II 24 h showing an AUC of 0.665. By 72 h, prognostic accuracy increased substantially, with MPM II 72 h achieving the highest performance (AUC 0.802), followed by MODS (AUC 0.780) and SOFA (AUC 0.712). All models demonstrated acceptable calibration, but MPM II 72 h showed superior sensitivity and overall accuracy. These results indicate that reassessment at 72 h provides more reliable prognostic information than admission scores alone. These findings underscore the importance of sequential patient evaluation, as prognostic accuracy improves after early stabilization when evolving organ dysfunction can be more accurately assessed [17].

Our findings align with prior studies showing that prognostic models may have limited performance at admission but improve after stabilization and reassessment of organ dysfunction. After 40 years of HIV pandemic, there has been a shift in the causes of hospitalization of PLWHA from opportunistics to cardiometabolic diseases (MACE), although in LMIC AIDS-related complications remain the leading cause of critical care admissions [18]. Although prognostic scores measured at admission to certain ICUs may represent variations in the quality of care, serving as a quality benchmark, specialized ICUs operating within national regulatory frameworks (such as our study ICU) tend to have mortality rates comparable to those observed in similar referral centers and national reports.

The challenge of applying general prognostic models to specific populations, such as PLWHA, has been extensively documented [19]. Prognostic models originally developed for the general ICU population may not fully account for the complex pathophysiology observed in PLWHA, including immune suppression, opportunistic infections, major cardiometabolic complications (MACE) associated with ART and aging, in addition to limited access to timely healthcare in LMIC. Our findings align with previous studies that have identified organ dysfunction, low CD4+ counts, and delays in treatment initiation as key contributors to increased mortality risk [20, 21]. In this study, patients with lower CD4+ counts and multiple organ dysfunctions had significantly worse outcomes, supporting the role of these variables as critical factors in predicting mortality [7].

The overall mortality rate of 56.3% reflects the severe clinical state of many patients at the time of admission. This rate is comparable to findings from other studies conducted in specific resource-limited settings, such as those in Korea and India, which report similar challenges including late-stage disease presentation and treatment delays [22, 23]. Our study highlights that many patients were non-adherent to ART (54.4%) and had detectable viral loads (83.1%), underscoring persistent barriers to long-term treatment adherence [24]. This suggests our setting may be representative of certain LMIC environments, but caution is warranted in extrapolating these results to all LMICs due to variations in health system organization, ICU capabilities, and patient demographics.

Despite these findings, the MPM II and MODS systems demonstrated good calibration across risk categories, suggesting that they may serve as reliable tools for early risk stratification in ICU patients. Previous studies have indicated that models like MPM II, which require fewer laboratory inputs, may be particularly advantageous in resource-limited environments [25]. In contrast, more complex models, such as APACHE II and SAPS III, may lose accuracy when data collection is inconsistent or incomplete due to infrastructure challenges [26]. Therefore, in settings with limited access to continuous monitoring and laboratory testing, simpler models that retain predictive power are preferable.

In this specific cohort, the MPM II score calculated at 72 h provided the best discrimination (AUC 0.802) and acceptable calibration for predicting in-hospital mortality. This suggests that a reassessment of prognosis at this later time point may be more informative than admission scores alone for PLWHA in ICUs with similar profiles. If validated externally in comparable settings, the MPM II-72 h could be considered as a tool to assist multidisciplinary teams in contexts such as resource prioritization (e.g., guiding decisions on intensity of monitoring or prompting earlier specialty consultations), informing nuanced goals-of-care discussions with families, and stratifying risk for research purposes. It is paramount to state that this score, or any prognostic model, must only serve as an adjunct to comprehensive clinical assessment and never as a sole determinant for limiting care. Before any clinical application is considered, rigorous local validation is essential to confirm its performance and determine appropriate, context-specific thresholds. The cutoff observed here (MPM II score = 18.90) is purely exploratory and is presented to facilitate future research, not for immediate clinical use.

Several limitations of this study must be considered. First, its retrospective, single-center design relies on the accuracy of medical records and missing or inconsistent data may have influenced the results [27]. The absence of a contemporaneous non-HIV comparator cohort also prevents direct estimation of HIV-specific effects. Second, although the center serves a diverse population from urban and remote areas, the findings may not be generalizable to other ICUs with different infrastructures, case mixes, or higher ART adherence and viral suppression rates [27, 28]. External validation in multicenter studies is therefore essential. Third, the scoring systems assessed were developed for general ICU populations and were not specifically designed for PLWHA; their performance can vary depending on underlying conditions [29]. Continued efforts to validate or adapt these models to HIV-specific contexts are needed. Finally, evaluating only two time points (24 and 72 h) may not capture rapid clinical changes, and therapeutic interventions during admission were not considered, which may have affected outcomes.

Conclusions

This study highlights the variability in prognostic performance of scoring systems for critically ill PLWHA on AIDS stage over time. While discrimination was modest within the first 24 h of ICU admission, it improved significantly at 72 h. The MPM II at 72 h emerged as the most reliable system, demonstrating the highest AUC, sensitivity, and calibration. This reinforces its potential as a valuable tool for early risk stratification and clinical decision-making in resource-limited settings. The findings underscore the importance of continuous assessment and the inclusion of dynamic markers, such as organ dysfunction, in predicting mortality outcomes. Patients with lower CD4+ counts and greater organ dysfunction at admission experienced higher mortality, reflecting the critical role of early interventions and comprehensive clinical evaluations. These results are consistent with studies from other resource-constrained regions, indicating similar challenges related to treatment delays and severe disease progression. To enhance clinical outcomes for PLWHA in the ICU, there is a need for further multi-center studies to validate these findings across diverse settings. Additionally, efforts to optimize healthcare infrastructure, improve adherence to ART, and develop prognostic tools adapted to HIV-specific contexts could contribute to reducing mortality and improving critical care practices.
