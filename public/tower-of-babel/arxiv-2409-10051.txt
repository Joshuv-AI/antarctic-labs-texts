1.
Introduction

A
Beurling generalized number system
is a pair
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
, where
\mathcal{P}=(p_{j})_{j}
, the sequence of
generalized primes
, consists of real numbers
p_{j}
with
1<p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\dotsb
and
p_{j}\to\infty
, and
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j}
, the sequence of
generalized integers
, consists of all possible products of the generalized primes, including
1
. One also orders this sequence in a non-decreasing fashion. We allow repetitions in both sequences; in the sequence
\mathcal{N}
, a real number occurs as many times as its number of distinct factorizations into generalized primes.

This notion was introduced by Beurling
[
1
]
, who wanted to investigate the minimal assumptions needed to show the
prime number theorem
. Denoting
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}1
and
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1
, his theorem states the asymptotic
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x(\log x)^{-\gamma})
where
A
is some positive constant and
\gamma>3/2
, yields the prime number theorem in the form

(1.1)

\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\sim\Li(x).

Here,
\Li(x)=\int_{2}^{x}(\log u)^{-1}\dif u
stands for the logarithmic integral.

We consider Beurling generalized number systems
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
for which the integers are
well-behaved
, meaning that there exists a number
\theta\in[0,1)
for which

(1.2)

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{\theta}),

for some constant
A>0
, the
density
of the integers. If we want to mention the exponent
\theta
, we will say the the integers are
\theta
-well-behaved. This assumption is quite natural, for instance it readily yields the analytic continuation of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)-\frac{A}{s-1}
to the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s>\theta
. Here
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
is the
Beurling zeta function
associated with the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
:

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{s}}=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-p_{j}^{-s}},\quad\operatorname{Re}s>1.

Besides being of independent interest, the class of Beurling zeta functions coming from well-behaved integers is also an interesting “testing ground” to analyze the strength of the analytic techniques used occurring in the study of the Riemann zeta function in the critical strip and the distribution of prime numbers. For example, the results of this paper can be regarded as a study on how far the classical zero-detection methods can be pushed in a general setting: our results apply to a very broad class of (general) Dirichlet series, which also includes many of the classical
L
-functions, see Theorem
1.3
and Remark
4.4
below.

Our main goal is to obtain
zero-density estimates
. These are bounds for the number of zeros of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
in rectangles: let
\alpha>\theta
,
T>0
, then we denote

N(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\alpha,T)=\#\{\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho)=0,\beta\geq\alpha,|\gamma|\leq T\}.

We often simply write
N(\alpha,T)
if the Beurling zeta function is clear from the context. Classically, one can use such zero-density estimates to study the distribution of prime numbers in short intervals. This application is also present in the Beurling case. We refer to
[
2
, Section 4]
for more details.

From the Euler product representation of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
, it is immediate that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has no zeros with
\beta>1
. Actually, the well-havedness of the integers (
1.2
) implies the zero-free region

\sigma>1-\frac{C(1-\theta)}{\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}},\quad\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\geq T_{0},

for some absolute constant
C
and some
T_{0}
depending on the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
. This was first shown by Landau
[
10
]
for Dedekind zeta functions attached to number fields, but his method readily applies to the Beurling case.

We are thus interested in bounds of
N(\alpha,T)
in the critical range
\theta<\alpha<1
.
The local number of zeros can be bounded by an application of Jensen’s formula, see e.g. Lemma
4.3
below, or
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
be the Beurling zeta function associated with a number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
with
\theta
-well-behaved integers. For each
T>2
and
\alpha>\theta
, the number of zeros
\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
with
\beta\geq\alpha
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\gamma\right\rvert}\in[T,T+1]
is bounded by
O_{\alpha}(\log T)
.

From this lemma we see that
N(\alpha,T)\ll_{\alpha}T\log T
. The goal of zero-density estimates is to improve upon this “trivial” estimate. More precisely, our goal is to find an abscissa
\sigma_{0}\in(\theta,1)
and a function
c(\alpha)<1
for which a bound of the form

N(\alpha,T)\ll T^{c(\alpha)}(\log T)^{O(1)}\quad\text{or}\quad N(\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{c(\alpha)+\varepsilon},\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0,

holds uniformly for
\alpha>\sigma_{0}
.

For zeta functions of well-behaved number systems, a zero-density estimate was first shown by Sz. Révész in
[
16
]
under the additional assumptions
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
(the so-called integrality assumption) and a sort of “Ramanujan condition” expressing that
\mathcal{N}
does not have too many repeated values. A little later, these restrictions could be removed, as found independently by Révész in
[
17
]
and by G. Debruyne and the author in
[
2
]
, where the admissible values

(1.3)

\sigma_{0}=\frac{11+\theta}{12},\quad c(\alpha)=\frac{12(1-\alpha)}{1-\theta},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{0}=\frac{2+\theta}{3},\quad c(\alpha)=\frac{c_{1}(\alpha)(1-\alpha)}{1-\theta},

respectively, with
c_{1}(\alpha)
an increasing function with
c_{1}(\frac{2+\theta}{3})=3
and
c_{1}(1)=4
, were established. With the integrality assumption and the Ramanujan condition, the value of
c(\alpha)
can be lowered further when
\alpha
is sufficiently close to
1
, as shown by J. Pintz and Révész in the recent preprint
[
14
]
. For example, they obtained
N(\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\frac{2(1-\alpha)}{\alpha-\theta}+\varepsilon}
when
\alpha
is sufficiently close to
1
.

In this paper, we keep the general setup of
\theta
-well-behaved integers without imposing additional assumptions. We obtain a zero-density estimate in the widest possible range
\alpha>\sigma_{0}=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
and also lower the value of the function
c(\alpha)
in (
1.3
).
Our main result is

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
be a Beurling number system satisfying (
1.2
) for some
\theta\in[0,1)
. Then we have uniformly for
\alpha\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}
and
T\geq 2
,

(1.4)

N(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\alpha,T)\ll T^{\frac{4(1-\alpha)}{3-2\alpha-\theta}}(\log T)^{9}.

Applying this to the system
(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{N})
of rational primes and integers, for which we can take
\theta=0
, we obtain the estimate
N(\zeta;\alpha,T)\ll T^{\frac{4(1-\alpha)}{3-2\alpha}}(\log T)^{9}
for the number of zeros of the Riemann zeta function. This estimate was first shown by Titchmarsh
[
18
]
(with the power of
\log T
replaced by
T^{\varepsilon}
), whose proof uses the approximate functional equation of the Riemann zeta function, which is in general not available for Beurling zeta functions.

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
does not require any (approximate) functional equation and actually applies to quite a broad class of general Dirichlet series
F(s)=\sum_{j}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
. An important aspect in the proof is the existence of the inverse of
F
,
G(s)=1/F(s)
as a general Dirichlet series, so it is sensible to assume that
(n_{j})_{j}
is a sequence of Beurling generalized integers. In fact, if it is not, we can without loss of generality consider
F
as a Dirichlet series over the multiplicative semigroup generated by
(n_{j})_{j}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
(with
n_{1}=1
) be a sequence of Beurling generalized integers satisfying
N(x+x^{\theta})-N(x-x^{\theta})\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
for some
\theta\in[0,1)
and for every
\varepsilon>0
. Let

F(s)=1+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}},\quad G(s)=\frac{1}{F(s)}=1+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{b_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}}

be (general) Dirichlet series satisfying
a_{j}\geq 0
and the “Ramanujan bound”
a_{j}\ll_{\varepsilon}n_{j}^{\varepsilon}
, and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rvert}\ll_{\varepsilon}n_{j}^{\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose further that

(1.5)

A(x)\coloneqq\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}a_{j}=xP(\log x)+O\bigl(x^{\theta}(\log x)^{q}\bigr)

for a certain polynomial
P
of degree
d
and some
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
F(s)
has meromorphic continuation to
\operatorname{Re}s>\theta
whose only singularity is a pole of order
d
at
s=1
, and

N(F;\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\frac{4(1-\alpha)}{3-2\alpha-\theta}+\varepsilon};\quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0,

uniformly for
\alpha\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}
.

It is an interesting question whether some specific structure of the Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
, e.g. the fact that
a_{j}\equiv 1
, or
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
having an Euler product, or simply having multiplicative coefficients, can be leveraged in some way to obtain a better zero-density estimate, or whether the number of zeros of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
can in general be as large (up to a factor
T^{\varepsilon}
) as the number of zeros of more general Dirichlet series
F(s)
.

If more analytic information of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
is available, then Theorem
1.2
can be improved. We investigate the case of higher moment estimates or pointwise bounds on the line
\sigma=\frac{1+\beta}{2}
, resulting in Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
, respectively.

The paper is organized as follows. First we discuss some preliminary results in Section
2
. The two main technical ingredients are a mean value theorem for general Dirichlet polynomials and a second moment estimate for Beurling zeta functions. Next, we prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
. We follow this up with Section
4
, where we discuss the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In Section
5
we prove the conditional improvements of Theorem
1.2
, namely Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
. We also discuss a hypothetical improved mean value theorem in the style of Montgomery’s conjecture for Dirichlet polynomials.
As alluded to, the range of validity
\alpha>\frac{1+\theta}{2}
of density estimates is optimal. When
\theta=0
, this follows simply from considering the Riemann zeta function. In the final section, we indicate why this range should be optimal for any value of
\theta
, and prove this when
\theta>1/2
. We also make some comments on the question of the optimal value of
c(\alpha)
.

2.
Preliminary results

The proof of Theorem
1.2
rests upon two main results: a mean value theorem for general Dirichlet polynomials, and a mean value theorem for Beurling zeta functions.

The classical mean value theorem (see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 6.1]
) for ordinary Dirichlet polynomials
\sum_{n}a_{n}n^{-\mathrm{i}t}
relies on the separation of the frequencies
\log n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
(see also
[
13
, Corollary 2]
). A generalization of this result was established in
[
2
]
, where the lack of separation of general frequencies
\log n_{j}
was remedied by introducing a factor
\chi
, which counts the size of “local clusters” of
n_{j}
’s. Theorem 2.1 of
[
2
]
states:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
and suppose that
1\leq n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\dotso\leq n_{J}\leq N
are real numbers. For
\lambda>0
denote by
\chi(x,\lambda)
the number of
n_{j}
’s within distance
\lambda
of
x
:

\chi(x,\lambda)=\#\{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|x-n_{j}|\leq\lambda\}.

Suppose
a_{j}

(j=1,\dotsc,J)
are complex numbers, and set

S(\mathrm{i}t)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{\mathrm{i}t}}.

Then for
T_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
T>0
, and
\eta>0
we have

\int_{T_{0}}^{T_{0}+T}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}^{2}\dif t\ll\biggl(T+\frac{1}{\eta}\biggr)\sum_{j=1}^{J}\chi(n_{j},\eta N)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}.

Estimating a sum over well-spaced points by an integral, one obtains the following discrete variant, see
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

With the same notation as in Theorem
2.1
, let
\delta>0
, and let
\mathcal{T}\subset[T_{0}+\delta/2,T_{0}+T-\delta/2]
be a set of
\delta
-well-spaced points, meaning that
|t-t^{\prime}|\geq\delta
whenever
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
with
t\neq t^{\prime}
. Then

\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}^{2}\ll\biggl(T+\frac{1}{\eta}\biggr)\biggl(\log N+\frac{1}{\delta}\biggr)\sum_{j=1}^{J}\chi(n_{j},\eta N)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}.

We shall also need an estimate for the sum over complex numbers
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
with well-spaced imaginary parts, but for which the real parts need not be the same.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\delta>0
, and
\mathcal{S}
a set of complex numbers
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
satisfying

\sigma\geq\alpha,\quad T_{0}+\delta/2\leq t\leq T_{0}+T-\delta/2,

and with the property that
\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
:
s\neq s^{\prime}\implies\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}s-\operatorname{Im}s^{\prime}\right\rvert}\geq\delta
.

Suppose that
N\geq 2
,
N\leq n_{1}\leq\dotso\leq n_{J}\leq 2N
, and let
a_{j}

(j=1,\dotsc,J)
be complex numbers. Write
S(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
. Then for every
\eta>0

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(s)\right\rvert}^{2}\ll\biggl(T+\frac{1}{\eta}\biggr)\biggl(\log N+\frac{1}{\delta}\biggr)\sum_{j=1}^{J}\chi(n_{j},\eta N)\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}}{N^{2\alpha}}.

Proof.

Write

S(s,x)=\sum_{N\leq n_{j}\leq x}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}}.

Integrating by parts we get for
s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle S(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{N-}^{2N}x^{\alpha-\sigma}\dif S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t,x)=(2N)^{\alpha-\sigma}S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t)+(\sigma-\alpha)\int_{N}^{2N}x^{\alpha-\sigma-1}S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t,x)\dif x

\displaystyle\ll\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}+\frac{1}{N}\int_{N}^{2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t,x)\right\rvert}\dif x.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(s)\right\rvert}^{2}\ll\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\int_{N}^{2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t,x)\right\rvert}^{2}\dif x.

We now apply Theorem
2.2
to the sums
S(\alpha+\mathrm{i}t,x)
with coefficients
b_{j}=a_{j}n_{j}^{-2\alpha}
if
N\leq n_{j}\leq x
, and
b_{j}=0
else.
∎

3.
Proof of the zero-density estimate

We now prove Theorem
1.2
, following the classical procedure from
[
12
, Chapter 12]
. In what follows, the implicit constants in the
O
-estimates may depend on the number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
, but are independent of
\alpha
.

3.1.
The setup

Let
\frac{1+\theta}{2}<\alpha<1
and
T\geq 2
. We let
2\leq X\leq Y\leq T^{O(1)}
be parameters to be determined, and we set

M_{X}(s)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq X}\frac{\mu(n_{j})}{n_{j}^{s}}.

Here
\mu
is the Möbius function associated with the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
:
\mu(n_{j})=(-1)^{\nu}
if
n_{j}
is the product of
\nu
distinct generalized primes, and
\mu(n_{j})=0
otherwise, i.e. if
n_{j}
is not squarefree
1
1

1

Strictly speaking
\mu
should be defined as a function of
j
instead of
n_{j}
, as unique factorization of Beurling integers need not hold. For example, we can have
\mu(p_{1}p_{3})=1
,
\mu(p_{2}^{2})=0
, even if
p_{1}p_{3}
and
p_{2}^{2}
happen to have the same numerical value. We will continue this slight abuse of notation, as sums over
\mu(n_{j})
are always well-defined.
. Then

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)M_{X}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}},

where
a_{1}=1
,
a_{j}=0
for
1<n_{j}\leq X
, and
|a_{j}|\leq d(n_{j})
for
n_{j}>X
. Here
d(n_{j})=\#\{(m,l)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n_{m}n_{l}=n_{j}\}
denotes the divisor function of the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
.

Using the inverse Mellin transform of the Gamma function

\mathrm{e}^{-1/x}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}\infty}\Gamma(w)x^{w}\dif w,\quad\kappa>0,

we obtain (with
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
)

(3.1)

\mathrm{e}^{-1/Y}+\sum_{n_{j}>X}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{s}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s+w)M_{X}(s+w)\Gamma(w)Y^{w}\dif w,\quad\kappa>1-\sigma.

We assume that
\alpha\leq\sigma<1
, and shift the contour of integration to the line
\operatorname{Re}w=\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\sigma
. As
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
is polynomially bounded, this is allowed in view of the exponential decay in vertical strips of the Gamma function. We pick up two residues at
w=1-s
and
w=0
to get

\mathrm{e}^{-1/Y}+\sum_{n_{j}>X}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{s}}=AM_{X}(1)\Gamma(1-s)Y^{1-s}+\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)M_{X}(s)\\
+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(t+v)\Bigr)M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(t+v)\Bigr)\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\sigma+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)Y^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\sigma+\mathrm{i}v}\dif v.

Let now
s=\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
be a zero of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
with
\beta\geq\alpha
and
|\gamma|\leq T
. Then

(3.2)

\mathrm{e}^{-1/Y}+\sum_{n_{j}>X}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{\rho}}=AM_{X}(1)\Gamma(1-\rho)Y^{1-\rho}\\
+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)Y^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v}\dif v.

The first term on the right hand side of (
3.2
) is in absolute value at most
1/6
, provided that
|\gamma|\geq C_{1}\log T
for a suitable constant
C_{1}
. Also, setting
Z=C_{2}\log T
for a sufficiently large constant
C_{2}
, we get

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|v|\geq Z}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)Y^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v}\dif v\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{6}.

Both estimates are due to the exponential decay of
\Gamma

Y
is sufficiently large, we have

\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}>Y^{2}}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{\rho}}\biggr\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{6}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{e}^{1/Y}\geq\frac{5}{6}.

We conclude that for a zero

\beta\geq\alpha

(3.3)

\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{X<n_{j}\leq Y^{2}}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{\rho}}\biggr\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{6},

or

(3.4)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{-Z}^{Z}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\gamma+v)\Bigr)\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)Y^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\beta+\mathrm{i}v}\dif v\right\rvert}\geq\frac{2\pi}{6}.

From the set of all such
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
-zeros, we select a subset

N(\alpha,T)\ll(\log T)^{2}+|\mathcal{R}|(\log T)^{2}.

This is possible in view of Lemma

. Let
\mathcal{R}_{1}

R_{1}
and
R_{2}
.

3.2.
The estimation of
R_{1}

To estimate
R_{1}
, we decompose each sum satisfying (
3.3
) into
O(\log Y)=O(\log T)
dyadic subsums. By the pigeonhole principle, there is a number
N
,
X\leq N\leq Y^{2}

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof of the more general Theorem
1.3
follows without much effort along the same lines as in Section
3
. We recall our setup:
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a sequence of Beurling integers satisfying
N(x+x^{\theta})-N(x-x^{\theta})\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
,

is a Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficients satisfying

,

, and

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rvert}\ll_{\varepsilon}n_{j}^{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 4.1
.

The sequence of Beurling integers
(n_{j})_{j}
is not necessarily strictly increasing and can have repeated values. In this situation, there is not a unique choice of coefficients
a_{j}
. For Theorem
1.3
it suffices that there is some choice of coefficients
a_{j}
satisfying
a_{j}\ll_{\varepsilon}n_{j}^{\varepsilon}
. The same remark applies to
G
. Hence, if the integer
n_{j_{0}}
has multiplicity
m_{j_{0}}
(which can be up to
n_{j_{0}}^{\theta+\varepsilon}
on our assumptions), we can allow following bound for the “total coefficient”:
\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n_{j}=n_{j_{0}}}a_{j}\ll_{\varepsilon}m_{j_{0}}n_{j_{0}}^{\varepsilon}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Note that
N(x+x^{\theta})-N(x-x^{\theta})\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
implies that
N(x)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{1+\varepsilon}
. In combination with the Ramanujan condition
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rvert}\ll_{\varepsilon}n_{j}^{\varepsilon}
, this implies that
G(s)
converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}s>1
and is analytic there. The absolute convergence of
F(s)
for
\operatorname{Re}s>1
already followed from (

it follows that

has no zeros for

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose

Writing

\sigma>1

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif\,\bigl(uP(\log u)\bigr)}{u^{s}}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif E(u)}{u^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}}+x^{1-s}\biggl(\frac{Q_{1}(\log x)}{1-s}+\dotsb+\frac{Q_{d+1}(\log x)}{(1-s)^{d+1}}\biggr)-\frac{E(x)}{x^{s}}+s\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{E(u)}{u^{s+1}}\dif u.

with the exception of

\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}}\biggr\rvert}\leq\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{\sigma}}\ll\frac{x^{1-\sigma}-1}{1-\sigma}(\log x)^{d},\quad E(x)\ll x^{\theta}(\log x)^{q}

∎

Lemma 4.3

With the same assumptions as in the previous lemma, we have the local bound

5.
Conditional improvements

For the Riemann zeta function, we currently have a fourth moment estimate on the
1/2
-line, and this stronger piece of information yields a better zero-density estimate. In general, better growth bounds on the Beurling zeta function will lead to improvements in the zero-density estimates. In this section we investigate such conditional improvements. We focus on two kinds of growth bounds:
higher order moment estimates
and
subconvexity bounds
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
be a Beurling number system satisfying (
1.2

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\dif t

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\dif t

\displaystyle\ll_{k}(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)},

for some (real)

(5.3)

\alpha\geq\frac{3k-1+(k-1)\theta}{4k-2}

In analogy with the Riemann zeta function, we refer to the statement “
N(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\frac{2(1-\alpha)}{1-\theta}+\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0
” as the
density hypothesis
for the Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
.

The proofs of (
5.4
) and (
5.7
) require another technical tool, the Halász–Montgomery inequality, which we adapt to the Beurling setting.

5.1.
The Halász–Montgomery inequality

This inequality is an alternative for bounding discrete mean values of Dirichlet polynomials. It is based on a Hilbert space inequality, and allows one to exploit good bounds for (sums of) zeta sums
\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}
(or smoothed version thereof), if available.
Higher order moment estimates and subconvexity bounds lead to such “good bounds” which are of sufficient quality to obtain an improvement of Theorem
2.2
in certain ranges of the involved parameters.

The basic inequality we shall use is
[
12
, Lemma 1.5]
, which states that

(5.8)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle\xi,\varphi_{r}\rangle\right\rvert}^{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert}^{2}\max_{r}\sum_{s=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle\varphi_{r},\varphi_{s}\rangle\right\rvert},

for vectors
\xi
,
\varphi_{r}
,
r=1,\dotsc,R
, in an inner product space.
We set
S(s)=\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
, where
a_{j}
are complex numbers and
n_{j}
are Beurling integers of a number system satisfying (
1.2
). Letting
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r=1,\dotsc,R\}\subseteq[0,T]
be a set of
1
-well-spaced points, we apply (
5.8
) with

\xi=\biggl(\frac{a_{j}\mathcal{I}_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}}}\biggr)_{j\geq 1},\quad\varphi_{r}=\Bigl(\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t_{r}}\Bigr)_{j\geq 1}.

Here
\mathcal{I}_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}=1
if
N<n_{j}\leq 2N
and
0
else.
Note that
\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}}\asymp 1
for
N<n_{j}\leq 2N
; this smoothing factor is introduced to obtain faster convergence in the Mellin transform. The inequality (
5.8
) thus yields

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll\biggl(\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}\biggr)\max_{r}\sum_{s=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}\bigr)n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})}\right\rvert}.

The inner-most sum can be written as

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{2-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{2+\mathrm{i}\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\bigl(w+\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})\bigr)\bigl((2N)^{w}-N^{w}\bigr)\Gamma(w)\dif w.

In this integral, we shift the contour to the line
\operatorname{Re}w=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
. We pick up a residue at
w=1-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})
, so that for each
r=1,\dotsc,R
,

(5.9)

\sum_{s=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}\bigr)n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})}\right\rvert}\\
\ll N\sum_{s=1}^{R}\Gamma\bigl(1-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})\bigr)+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{R}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(v+t_{r}-t_{s})\Bigr)\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)\right\rvert}\dif v.

At this point we insert a pointwise or moment bound of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
. If we assume (
5.5
), then it follows that

(5.10)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll_{B}\bigl(N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{B}R\bigr)\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2},

as the
1
-well-spacedness of
\mathcal{T}
ensures that
\sum_{s=1}^{R}\Gamma\bigl(1-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})\bigr)\ll 1
.

Suppose on the other hand that we have the moment bound (
5.1
) for some
k\geq 1/2
.
We truncate the integral in (
5.9
) to
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}w\right\rvert}\leq Z=C_{2}\log T
for some sufficiently large constant
C_{2}
.
Applying Hölder’s inequality twice and using the 1-well-spacedness of
\mathcal{T}
in (
5.9
), we obtain

\sum_{s=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/(2N)}-\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/N}\bigr)n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}(t_{r}-t_{s})}\right\rvert}\ll N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{R}\int_{-Z}^{Z}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}(v+t_{r}-t_{s})\Bigr)\right\rvert}\dif v\\
\ll N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}\biggl(\int_{-T-Z}^{T+Z}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)\right\rvert}^{2k}\dif v\biggr)^{\frac{1}{2k}}R^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}\log T.

Hence we get

(5.11)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll_{k}\bigl(N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{\frac{1}{2k}}R^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)}\bigr)\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}.

Remark 5.4
.

In any case, (
5.5
) always holds with
B=1/2
, while Theorem
2.4
yields (
5.1
) with
k=1
. Hence we obtain from (
5.11
)

(5.12)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll\bigl(N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{1/2}R^{1/2}(\log T)^{O(1)}\bigr)\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}.

This estimate is superior to the estimate

(5.13)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll(N+TN^{\theta})(\log N)\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}

obtained from Theorem
2.2
only if
R\ll TN^{\theta-1}
and
N^{1-\theta}=o(T)
(disregarding logarithmic factors). However, in this regime, both (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) follow from the trivial estimate by Cauchy–Schwarz

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(\mathrm{i}t_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll RN\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}.

Thus, without posing additional assumptions on the Beurling zeta function besides well-behavedness of the integers, the Halász–Montgomery inequality does not seem to supply new information.

We finally need the following modifications of (
5.10
) and (
5.11
). Let
\{s_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1\leq r\leq R\}
be a set of complex numbers satisfying
\operatorname{Re}s_{r}\geq\alpha
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}s_{r}-\operatorname{Im}s_{r^{\prime}}\right\rvert}\geq 1
whenever
r\neq r^{\prime}
. Then (
5.5
) and (
5.1
) imply

(5.14)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(s_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll_{B}\bigl(N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{B}R\bigr)\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}}{N^{2\alpha}}

and

(5.15)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(s_{r})\right\rvert}^{2}\ll_{k}\bigl(N+N^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{\frac{1}{2k}}R^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}\bigr)(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)}\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert}^{2}}{N^{2\alpha}},

respectively. This follows from (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) respectively, along the same lines as the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

5.2.
Proofs

We are now ready to prove Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

To prove (
5.3
), we go straight to the estimation of
R_{2}
. Raising (

) to the power
\frac{2k}{k+1}
, summing over
\mathcal{R}_{2}
, and applying Hölder’s inequality gives

(5.16)

R_{2} \ll Y^{\frac{2k}{k+1}(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\alpha)}\biggl(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\biggr)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\biggl(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2}\biggr)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}(\log T)^{O(1)}.

Applying Gallagher’s lemma (
[
12
, Lemma 1.2]
), Hölder’s inequality, and (
5.1
)-(
5.2
), we find

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\ll_{k}T(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)}.

Inserting this estimate and (
3.11
) into (
5.16
) gives, upon selecting once again
X=T^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
,

(5.17)

R_{2}\ll_{k}Y^{\frac{2k}{k+1}(\frac{1+\theta}{2}-\alpha)}T(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)}.

Comparing with the estimate (
3.6
) for
R_{1}
now yields the optimal choice
Y=T^{\frac{k+1}{2(1-\alpha)+k(1-\theta)}}
, from which (
5.3
) follows.

To prove (
5.4
), we assume that
\alpha\geq\frac{3k-1+(k-1)\theta}{4k-2}(>\frac{3+\theta}{4})
. We apply (
5.15
) to bound the right hand sides of (
3.5
) and (
3.10
), and insert the obtained upper bound in the latter case into (
5.16
). After some calculations, again assuming
\log X\leq\log Y\ll_{k}\log T
, we get

(5.18)

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\bigl(Y^{2-2\alpha}+TX^{-k(4\alpha-3-\theta)}\bigr)(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)},

(5.19)

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\bigl(X^{\frac{k(1-\theta)}{k+1}}Y^{-\frac{k(2\alpha-1-\theta)}{k+1}}T^{\frac{1}{k+1}}+X^{\frac{k(1-\theta)}{3}}Y^{-\frac{2k(2\alpha-1-\theta)}{3}}T\bigr)(\log T)^{O_{k}(1)},

which can be compared with (
3.6
) and (
5.17
) respectively. We now set

X=T^{\frac{2\alpha-1-\theta}{k(2\alpha-1-\theta)(4\alpha-3-\theta)+4(3\alpha-2-\theta)(1-\alpha)}}\quad\text{and}\quad Y=T^{\frac{2(3\alpha-2-\theta)}{k(2\alpha-1-\theta)(4\alpha-3-\theta)+4(3\alpha-2-\theta)(1-\alpha)}}.

Note that
X<Y
as
\alpha>\frac{3+\theta}{4}
, and
\log Y\ll_{k}\log T
. With these choices, both terms in the right hand side of (
5.18
) are equal, while the second term in the right hand side of (
5.19
) dominates the first (again since
\alpha>\frac{3+\theta}{4}
), and we obtain (
5.4
).

When
2k
is an integer, we may avoid Gallagher’s lemma, which requires information on the derivative. Instead, we will bound the sum of well-spaced
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}^{2k}
-values by a lemma of Ivić,
[
8
, Lemma 7.1]
:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
T
be large. Uniformly for
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq T
, and with
Z=C_{2}\log T
,
C_{2}
a sufficiently large constant (depending on
k
), we have

\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\frac{1}{\log T}+\mathrm{i}t\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\ll_{k}1+\log T\int_{-Z/2}^{Z/2}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t+\mathrm{i}v\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}^{2k}\mathrm{e}^{-|v|}\dif v.

The lemma follows upon writing, with
s=\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\frac{1}{\log T}+\mathrm{i}t
,

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{1-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{1+\mathrm{i}\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s+w)^{2k}\Gamma(w)\dif w=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}d_{2k}(n_{j})n_{j}^{-s}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}}\ll_{k}1,

and shifting the contour to
\operatorname{Re}w=-\frac{1}{\log T}
. Here
d_{2k}
is the
2k
-fold divisor function of the number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
. The switching of contours picks up two residues; for
w=0
the residu is
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)^{2k}
, and for
w=1-s
the residu is
O_{k}(1)
, in view of the exponential decay of the gamma function and its derivatives. For the same reason, we can truncate the remaining integral to the interval
[-Z/2,Z/2]
, and the lemma then follows from inserting the estimate
\Gamma(u+\mathrm{i}v)\ll\mathrm{e}^{-|v|}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u+\mathrm{i}v\right\rvert}^{-1}
, which holds uniformly for
v\in\mathbb{R}
and
u
in compacts.

In the proof of the zero-density estimate, we now assume that
\alpha\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\frac{2}{\log T}
and shift the contour in (
3.1
) to
\operatorname{Re}w=\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\frac{1}{\log T}-\sigma
. We similarly obtain the estimate (
5.16
), but with
\frac{1+\theta}{2}
replaced by
\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\frac{1}{\log T}
. Recalling that the
t_{\rho}
’s are
Z
-well-spaced, we may estimate the sum of the
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}^{2k}
-values by the
2k
-th moment of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
, in view of Ivić’ lemma, while we can keep the previously obtained bounds for the sum involving
M_{X}
in both cases. As
Y\leq T^{O_{k}(1)}
we have
Y^{\frac{1}{\log T}}\ll_{k}1
, so that we may complete the proof in the same way as before.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

To estimate
R_{2}
, we will use the subconvexity bound (

) instead of the second moment estimate to treat the contribution of

. For zeros

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\gamma-t_{\rho}\right\rvert}\leq Z

\mathinner{\!\bigl\lvert M_{X}\bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\bigr)\bigr\rvert}

. We recall that
Z=C_{2}\log T
for some large constant
C_{2}
. Employing the subconvexity bound (

) yields

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert M_{X}\Bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\Bigr)\right\rvert}\gg_{B}Y^{\alpha-\frac{1+\theta}{2}}T^{-B}(\log T)^{-1}.

To prove (
5.6
), we combine (
5.20
) with (

) to obtain

R_{2}\ll_{B}Y^{1+\theta-2\alpha}T^{2B}(T+X^{1-\theta})(\log T)^{5}.

Keeping the bound (

and setting

For

\displaystyle X^{2\alpha-\frac{3+\theta}{2}}

Remark 5.6
.

It could happen that one has a subconvexity bound, not on the line
\sigma=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
, but for some
\sigma
closer to
1
, maybe with
\sigma=\sigma(T)
even depending on

. It should be possible to obtain zero-density estimates in terms of the quantity

5.3.
A conjecture in the style of Montgomery

We shall later see in Subsection
6.2
that both the moment estimate (
5.1
) with
k>1
and the subconvexity bound (
5.5
) with
B<1/2
may fail. Another path to the density hypothesis is by assuming a stronger mean value theorem for (general) Dirichlet polynomials. Inspired by Montgomery’s conjecture
[
12
, Conjecture 9.2]
we may formulate:

Conjecture 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j}
be a sequence of
\theta
-well-behaved Beurling integers, and let
a_{j}
be complex numbers with
|a_{j}|\leq 1
. Then uniformly for
\nu\in[1,2]
and for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{T_{0}}^{T_{0}+T}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}\Bigr\rvert}^{2\nu}\dif t\ll_{\varepsilon}(TN^{\nu\theta}+N^{\nu})N^{\nu+\varepsilon}.

The idea is that we may treat
(\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t})^{\nu}
as if it were a Dirichlet polynomial of length
N^{\nu}
, even when
\nu
is not an integer.

From this one can again show that

(5.23)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}\Bigr\rvert}^{2\nu}\ll_{\varepsilon}(TN^{\nu\theta}+N^{\nu})N^{\nu(1-2\alpha)+\varepsilon},

whenever
\mathcal{S}
is a set of points
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
, with
\sigma\geq\alpha
and
t\in[-T,T]
well-spaced, in the sense that for
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
with
s\neq s^{\prime}
we have
|t-t^{\prime}|\geq 1
.

This generalization of Montgomery’s conjecture leads to the density hypothesis for Beurling zeta functions.
Indeed, following the proof of Section
3
, we now fix some
\varepsilon>0
and set
X=T^{\varepsilon}
. Then trivially
M_{X}(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho})\ll T^{\varepsilon}
. Inserting this bound in (
3.4
) and letting
t_{\rho}
now be such that
\mathinner{\!\bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\bigl(\frac{1+\theta}{2}+\mathrm{i}t_{\rho}\bigr)\bigr\rvert}
is maximal, we may use the second moment estimate of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
to see that

R_{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}Y^{1+\theta-2\alpha}T^{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}.

We again use the convention that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
denotes a (small) positive number, not necessarily the same at each occurrence, and which is at most a bounded multiple of the previously fixed
\varepsilon
.

Again we may find an
N
and
\gg R_{1}/\log T
zeros
\rho
for which

\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{\rho}}\biggr\rvert}\gg\frac{1}{\log T}.

We may in fact assume that
X\leq N\leq Y(\log Y)^{2}
(and could also have done so in Section
3
), as
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sum_{n_{j}>Y(\log Y)^{2}}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}n_{j}^{-\rho}\right\rvert}\leq 1/6
if
Y
is sufficiently large.
We first choose an integer
m
so that
N^{m}\leq Y(\log Y)^{2}<N^{m+1}
, and subsequently a number
\nu\in[1,2]
so that
N^{m\nu}=Y(\log Y)^{2}
. Note that
m\ll_{\varepsilon}1
. Applying then (
5.23
) to the Dirichlet polynomials

\biggl(\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}\frac{a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-n_{j}/Y}}{n_{j}^{\rho}}\biggr)^{m}=\sum_{N^{m}<n_{j}\leq(2N)^{m}}\frac{b_{j}}{n_{j}^{\rho}},

whose coefficients
b_{j}
satisfy
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rvert}\leq d_{2m}(n_{j})\ll_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}n_{j}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, yields

R_{1} \ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(TY^{\theta}+Y)Y^{1-2\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime}},

from which

N(\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\frac{2(1-\alpha)}{1-\theta}+\varepsilon^{\prime}},

upon choosing
Y=T^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
.

6.
Limitations for zero-density estimates

6.1.
Sharpness of the range of validity
\alpha\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}

First we indicate why it is not possible to obtain a zero-density estimate beyond the line
\sigma=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
. In the case
\theta=0
, this is evident from the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
, for which it is known that
N(\zeta;1/2,T)\sim\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}
.

In the case
\theta>0
, we consider a suitable rescaling of
\zeta(s)
. Set

(6.1)

\zeta_{\theta}(s)\coloneqq\zeta\Bigl(\frac{s-\theta}{1-\theta}\Bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}n^{-\frac{s}{1-\theta}}=\exp\biggl(\sum_{p}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{p^{\frac{m\theta}{1-\theta}}}{m}p^{-\frac{ms}{1-\theta}}\biggr),\quad\operatorname{Re}s>1.

We want to see this as the Beurling zeta function of a number system; such a system would have as its generalized integers the numbers
n^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with “multiplicity”
n^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}
. Unfortunately, there is not an actual sequence of primes
\mathcal{P}
which yields such integers: for starters the “multiplicities”
n^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}
are in general not integral.
However, the definition of Beurling number systems may be generalized as follows: a Beurling number system
in the extended sense
is a pair of non-decreasing right-continuous unbounded functions
(\Pi(x),N(x))
, satisfying
\Pi(1)=0
,
N(1)=1
, and linked via the identity

\zeta(s)\coloneqq\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif N(x)=\exp\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif\Pi(x)

whenever the integrals converge. Equivalently,

\dif N(x)=\exp^{\ast}(\dif\Pi(x))=\delta_{1}(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}(\dif\Pi(x))^{\ast m},

where
\delta_{y}(x)
denotes the Dirac delta measure concentrated at
y
, and
(\dif F)^{\ast m}
denotes the
m
-th convolution power with respect to the multiplicative convolution of measures
\ast
, which is defined as

\int_{E}\dif F\ast\dif G=\iint_{uv\in E}\dif F(u)\dif G(v).

This convolution naturally generalizes Dirichlet convolution.
We refer to
[
5
, Chapters 2–3]
for more background on this notion.

In our case, we set

\dif\Pi_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{p}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{p^{\frac{m\theta}{1-\theta}}}{m}\delta_{p^{\frac{m}{1-\theta}}}(x),\quad\dif N_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\delta_{n^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}}(x).

This system has associated zeta function
\zeta_{\theta}(s)
, given by (
6.1
). Note that

(6.2)

N_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{n^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\leq x}n^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}=(1-\theta)x+O(x^{\theta}),

and

N\Bigl(\zeta_{\theta};\frac{1+\theta}{2},T\Bigr)\sim\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi}\log\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi},

so that for this system in the extended sense, no zero density estimate beyond the “critical line”
\sigma=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
is possible.

We will now construct a sequence of Beurling primes
\mathcal{P}
for which
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has the same zeros as
\zeta_{\theta}(s)
, and for which the integers are also
\theta
-well-behaved. For this however, we will assume that
\theta\geq 1/2
.

First, we define a “prime-counting” function
\pi_{\theta}(x)
for which
\Pi_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(x^{1/m})}{m}
. By Möbius inversion we obtain, with
\mu
now denoting the classical Möbius function,

\dif\pi_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\dif\Pi_{\theta}(x^{1/k})=\sum_{p}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{p,m}\delta_{p^{\frac{m}{1-\theta}}}(x),

where

a_{p,m}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(k)\bigl(p^{\frac{m\theta}{1-\theta}}\bigr)^{1/k}.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
u>1
. Then

\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(k)u^{1/k}>0.

Proof.

The lemma is trivial in case that
m=1
, so we assume
m\geq 2
. Set
f_{0}(u)=\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(k)u^{1/k}
. As
f_{0}(1)=\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(m)=0
, it suffices to show that

f_{0}^{\prime}(u)>0
for
u>1
. Write

f_{0}^{\prime}(u)=\sum_{k\mid m}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}u^{1/k-1}\eqqcolon\frac{f_{1}(u)}{u}.

Now we indeed have
f_{1}(u)>0
for
u\geq 1
:

f_{1}(1)=\sum_{k\mid m}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}=\prod_{p\mid m}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)>0,

and

f_{1}^{\prime}(u)=\sum_{k\mid m}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^{2}}u^{1/k-1}\geq 1-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}>0.

∎

By the lemma, the coefficients
a_{p,m}
are positive and
\dif\pi_{\theta}(x)
is a positive measure. We now construct our sequence of generalized primes
\mathcal{P}
as the sequence of numbers
p^{\frac{m}{1-\theta}}
with multiplicity
\lfloor a_{p,m}\rfloor
. Hence

\dif\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{p}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lfloor a_{p,m}\rfloor\delta_{p^{\frac{m}{1-\theta}}}(x).

Note that
\pi_{R}(x)\coloneqq\pi_{\theta}(x)-\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\geq 0
. Setting
M=M_{x}=\lfloor(1-\theta)\frac{\log x}{\log 2}\rfloor
, we have the upper bound

\pi_{R}(x)\leq\sum_{p^{\frac{m}{1-\theta}}\leq x}1=\pi(x^{1-\theta})+\pi(x^{\frac{1-\theta}{2}})+\dotsb+\pi(x^{\frac{1-\theta}{M}})\leq\Li(x^{1-\theta})+O\Bigl(\frac{x^{1-\theta}}{(\log x)^{2}}\Bigr),

by a weak form of the prime number theorem with remainder. Consequently, we also have

(6.3)

\Pi_{R}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi_{R}(x^{1/m})}{m}\leq\Li(x^{1-\theta})+O\Bigl(\frac{x^{1-\theta}}{(\log x)^{2}}\Bigr).

We will now consider
\pi_{R}(x)
as the “prime-counting function” of a system in the extended sense. The system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
is then obtained by removing the “primes” of the system given by
\pi_{R}(x)
from the “primes” of the system given by
\pi_{\theta}
. Consequently, we may calculate the integer-counting function
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
of the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
as a convolution of
\dif N_{\theta}
with the “Möbius function” of
\pi_{R}
.

Let
N_{R}(x)
be the “integer-counting” function associated with
\Pi_{R}(x)
:
\dif N_{R}(x)=\exp^{\ast}(\dif\Pi_{R}(x))
, or equivalently,
N_{R}(x)
is the unique function with
N_{R}(1)=1
and for which

\zeta_{R}(s)\coloneqq\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif N_{R}(x)=\exp\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif\Pi_{R}(x).

Using a criterium for so-called “O-density” of integers by Diamond and Zhang, we show:

Lemma 6.2
.

We have the upper bound

N_{R}(x)\ll x^{1-\theta}.

Proof.

In order to apply the criterium of Diamond and Zhang, we first perform a rescaling and set

\tilde{\Pi}(x)\coloneqq\Pi_{R}(x^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}),\quad\dif\tilde{N}(x)\coloneqq\exp^{\ast}(\dif\tilde{\Pi}(x)).

Then
\tilde{N}(x)=N_{R}(x^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}})
. Setting
\tilde{\psi}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\log u\dif\tilde{\Pi}(u)
, the bound (
6.3
) yields

\tilde{\psi}(x)\leq x+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{\log x}\Bigr).

By
[
5
, Prop. 6.11]
, it follows that
\tilde{N}(x)\ll x
.
∎

Next, we set
\dif M_{R}(x)\coloneqq(\dif N_{R}(x))^{\ast-1}
, the convolution inverse of
\dif N_{R}(x)
. The function
M_{R}(x)
is the natural analogue of the summatory function of the Möbius function and we have

\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif M_{R}(x)=\frac{1}{\zeta_{R}(s)}.

As
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dif M_{R}(u)\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\exp^{\ast}(-\dif\Pi_{R}(u))\right\rvert}\leq\exp^{\ast}(\dif\Pi_{R}(u))=\dif N_{R}(u)
and
N_{R}(x)\ll x^{1-\theta}
, the above integral converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}s>1-\theta
.
The counting measure of the integers of our system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
is now given as the convolution
\dif N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\dif N_{\theta}(x)\ast\dif M_{R}(x)
. On the zeta-side this equation translates to
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\zeta_{\theta}(s)/\zeta_{R}(s)
. We now estimate this convolution:

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}\dif N_{\theta}\ast\dif M_{R}=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}N_{\theta}(x/u)\dif M_{R}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}\Bigl((1-\theta)x/u+O\bigr((x/u)^{\theta}\bigr)\Bigr)\dif M_{R}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\theta)x}{\zeta_{R}(1)}+O\biggl(x\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif N_{R}(u)}{u}+x^{\theta}\int_{1^{-}}^{x}u^{-\theta}\dif N_{R}(u)\biggr),

where in the second line we used (
6.2
), and in the third line we used
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dif M_{R}(u)\right\rvert}\leq\dif N_{R}(u)
. Integrating by parts, using Lemma
6.2
, and recalling that we assumed
\theta\geq 1/2
, we see that the above error term is

\ll x^{1-\theta}+x^{\theta}\int_{1}^{x}u^{-2\theta}\dif u,

which is
\ll x^{\theta}
if
\theta>1/2
, and
\ll x^{1/2}\log x
if
\theta=1/2
. Hence we obtain

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\frac{1-\theta}{\zeta_{R}(1)}x+O\bigl(x^{\theta}(1+\mathcal{I}_{\theta=1/2}\log x)\bigr),

where
\mathcal{I}_{\theta=1/2}=1
if
\theta=1/2
and
0
else.

Finally,

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\frac{\zeta_{\theta}(s)}{\zeta_{R}(s)}=\zeta_{\theta}(s)\exp\biggl(-\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif\Pi_{R}(x)\biggr).

From (
6.3
) we see that
\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif\Pi_{R}(x)
is analytic in
\operatorname{Re}s>1-\theta
, so that in particular,

N\Bigl(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\frac{1+\theta}{2},T\Bigr)=N\Bigl(\zeta_{\theta};\frac{1+\theta}{2},T\Bigr)\sim\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi}\log\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi}.

We have thus proved:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\theta\in[1/2,1)
. Then there exists a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
for which

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O\bigl(x^{\theta}(1+\mathcal{I}_{\theta=1/2}\log x)\bigr)

for some
A>0
, and whose zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
satisfies

N\Bigl(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\frac{1+\theta}{2},T\Bigr)\sim\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi}\log\frac{(1-\theta)T}{2\pi}.

6.2.
The extremal Diamond–Montgomery–Vorhauer-example

Consider a density estimate of the form

N(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\alpha,T)\ll_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}}T^{\frac{c_{1}(\alpha)(1-\alpha)}{1-\theta}+\varepsilon},\quad\frac{1+\theta}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1,

for every
\varepsilon>0
and every number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
with
\theta
-well-behaved integers. It is an interesting question what the optimal value for
c_{1}(\alpha)
is. Theorem
1.2
shows that
c_{1}(\alpha)=\frac{4(1-\theta)}{3-2\alpha-\theta}
, which increases from
2
to
4
as
\alpha
increases from
\frac{1+\theta}{2}
to
1
, is admissible.

Regarding lower bounds, Diamond, Montgomery, and Vorhauer
[
4
]
showed that
c_{1}(\alpha)\geq 1
, at least when
\theta>1/2
. In their paper, they constructed for every
\theta\in(1/2,1)
a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{DMV}},\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{DMV}})
with
N_{\mathrm{DMV}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{\theta})
for some
A>0
and for which
\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}(s)
has infinitely many zeros on the contour
\sigma=1-\frac{a}{\log(|t|+2)}
, for some constant
a>0
. They showed that, as a result, no better remainder than the de la Vallée-Poussin remainder
O\bigl(x\exp(-2\sqrt{a\log x})\bigr)
can be obtained in the prime number theorem (
1.1
). The authors also compute that for fixed
\alpha>\theta
,

N(\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}};\alpha,T)=\Omega(T^{2\lambda(1-\alpha)}).

Here
\lambda<\frac{1}{2(1-\theta)}
is a parameter occurring in the definition of the number system which can be chosen arbitrarily close to
\frac{1}{2(1-\theta)}
. (In
[
4
]
, this parameter is called
\alpha
.) Taking
\lambda=\frac{1}{2(1-\theta)}
yields

N(\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}};\alpha,T)=\Omega\bigl(T^{\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\theta}}\bigr),

at the cost of only having the slightly weaker
N_{\mathrm{DMV}}(x)=Ax+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{\theta+\varepsilon})
, for every
\varepsilon>0
. (Probably the error term
O\bigl(x^{\theta}\exp(c(\log x)^{2/3})\bigr)
with some
c>0
is possible, see
[
5
, Chapter 17]
for the case
\theta=1/2
.) It would be interesting to construct examples of Beurling zeta functions, or even general Dirichlet series as in the statement of Theorem
1.3
, which exhibit
c_{1}(\alpha)>1
or perhaps even
c_{1}(\alpha)\geq 2
.

One possible limiting factor to the amount of zeros of
\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}
is the fact that it is an approximation of a “continuous system”; more precisely,
\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}(s)=\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\mathrm{e}^{Z(s)}
, where
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)
is the Mellin–Stieltjes transform of an
absolutely continuous
measure
\dif N_{\mathrm{c}}
(and
Z(s)
satisfies
Z(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll_{\sigma}\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+2)}
for
\sigma>1/2
). One might expect that Mellin–Stieltjes transforms of the form
\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif f(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}f^{\prime}(x)\dif x
have in general fewer zeros than (discrete) Dirichlet series, for instance because of the fact that the mean values of
\int_{N}^{2N}x^{-s}f^{\prime}(x)\dif x
are better behaved (assuming
f
is sufficiently “nice”) than those of
\sum_{N<n_{j}\leq 2N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
.

The zeta function
\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}(s)
(with
\lambda=\frac{1}{2(1-\theta)}
) exhibits some other interesting extremal properties, which can be shown via routine calculations which we omit. For fixed
\sigma\in(\theta,1)
, it satisfies

\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)=\Omega_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\theta}-\varepsilon}\bigr),\quad\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0.

This shows that the classical convexity bound
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll_{\sigma}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\theta}}
,
\theta<\sigma<1
is essentially sharp for Beurling zeta functions
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
, and that the subconvexity bound (
5.5
) with some
B<1/2
may fail to hold.

Furthermore, letting
k>1/2
and
\theta<\sigma<1-\frac{1-\theta}{2k}=\frac{2k-1+\theta}{2k}
, one may verify that

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}^{2k}\dif t=\Omega_{\varepsilon}\bigl(T^{\frac{2k-1+\theta-2k\sigma}{1-\theta}-\varepsilon}\bigr),\quad\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0.

Setting
k=1
gives that the condition
\sigma\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}
for having at most logarithmic growth of the second moment
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\right\rvert}^{2}\dif t
in the class of
\theta
-well-behaved Beurling zeta functions
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
,
\theta>1/2
, is sharp. (In
[
3
]
, the range
\sigma\geq\frac{1+\theta}{2}
was already shown to be sharp in a broader class of general Dirichlet series and for any
0\leq\theta<1
.) The Diamond–Montgomery–Vorhauer example also shows that higher order moment estimates (
5.1
) with
k>1
are not available in general.

Even if one is able to show a logarithmic bound for a higher order moment for all Beurling zeta functions on a line to the right of
\sigma=\frac{1+\theta}{2}
, this example shows that this line should be so close to
1
that it most likely will not lead to an improvement in the zero-density estimate, at least not via the method of Section
3
. More precisely, say that one is able to prove that for some
\sigma_{1}\in(\theta,1)
and for some
k>1
, every Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
with
\theta
-well-behaved integers satisfies

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathinner{\!\bigl\lvert\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}\bigl(\sigma_{1}+\mathrm{i}t\bigr)\bigr\rvert}^{2k}\dif t\ll(\log T)^{O(1)}.

The zeta function
\zeta_{\mathrm{DMV}}
of Diamond, Montgomery, and Vorhauer then implies that
\sigma_{1}\geq 1-\frac{1-\theta}{2k}
. If one would like to exploit this
2k
-th moment, one would shift the contour in (
3.1
) to the line
\operatorname{Re}w=\sigma_{1}-\sigma
. Estimating
R_{2}
similarly as in the proof of Theorem
5.1
would now only give

R_{2} \ll Y^{\frac{2k}{k+1}(\sigma_{1}-\alpha)}T^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\bigl(T+X^{2-2\sigma_{1}}\bigr)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}(\log T)^{O(1)},

which is non-trivial only if
\alpha>\sigma_{1}
.
Choosing any
X\in[T^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}},T^{\frac{1}{2-2\sigma_{1}}}]
and comparing with the estimate (
3.6
) of
R_{1}
leads, in view of
\sigma_{1}\geq 1-\frac{1-\theta}{2k}
, to an estimate which is at best

N(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}};\alpha,T)\ll T^{\frac{2(k+1)(1-\alpha)}{3-2\alpha-\theta}}(\log T)^{O(1)},

and hence inferior to the one provided by Theorem
1.2
.
