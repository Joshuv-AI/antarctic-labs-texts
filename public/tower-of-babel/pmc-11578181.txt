A potent proresolving mediator 17R-resolvin D2 from human macrophages, monocytes, and saliva
Authors: Mélissa Simard, Robert Nshimiyimana, Nan Chiang, Ana R. Rodriguez, Bernd W. Spur, Charles N. Serhan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Production of specialized proresolving mediators (SPMs) during the resolution phase in the acute inflammatory response is key to orchestrating complete resolution. Here, we uncovered a trihydroxy resolvin in fresh human saliva. We identified and determined its complete stereochemistry as 7S,16R,17R-trihydroxy-4Z,8E,10Z,12E,14E,19Z-docosahexaenoic acid (17R-RvD2) using total organic synthesis and matching of physical properties. The 17R-RvD2 was produced by activated human M2-like macrophages, M1-like macrophages, and human peripheral blood monocytes. 17R-RvD2 displayed potent proresolving functions (picomolar to nanomolar). Topical application of 17R-RvD2 on mouse ear skin reduced neutrophilic infiltration (~50%). 17R-RvD2 increased M2 markers CD206 and CD163 on human monocyte–derived macrophages and enhanced efferocytosis of senescent red blood cells by M2-like macrophages (EC50 ~ 2.6 × 10−14 M). In addition, 17R-RvD2 activated the RvD2 receptor and was equipotent to its epimer RvD2. 17R-RvD2 also significantly increased phagocytosis of Escherichia coli by human neutrophils. Together, these results establish the complete stereochemistry and potent proresolving functions of the previously unknown 17R-RvD2.

INTRODUCTION

Excessive uncontrolled inflammation is well established today as playing a central role in many widely occurring diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (1–3) and neuroinflammatory diseases, notably Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases (4). Proinflammatory mediators such as cytokines and eicosanoids, e.g., prostaglandins and leukotrienes, have a prominent place in current anti-inflammatory treatment strategies (5). The resolution phase of the acute inflammatory response has emerged as a novel therapeutic approach with the discovery and elucidation of proresolving functions of lipoxins (LXs), resolvins (Rv), protectins (PDs), and maresins (MaRs), collectively termed the specialized proresolving mediators (SPMs) [reviewed in (6) and (3)]. Each SPM acts as an agonist to stimulate the endogenous resolution mechanisms in inflammation, infection and pain, rather than inhibiting the protective functions of the acute inflammatory response. This is in contrast with current anti-inflammatory treatments, which can lead to unwanted side effects and immunosuppression (7).

SPMs are biosynthesized from essential n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid precursors (PUFA) (8) and are present, along with RvD2, in human plasma demonstrated in randomized clinical trials, reviewed in (9). SPMs are identified in human blood (10), emotional tears (11), and human nasal lavages (11) using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Recent results show that human saliva also contains several lipid mediators including 17-hydroxy-4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,15E,19Z-docosahexaenoic acid (17-HDHA), PD1, and LXB4 (12). The presence of epithelial cells together with leukocytes in salivary glands, both having lipoxygenase (LOX) and cyclooxygenase (COX), is an ideal setting for transcellular biosynthesis of lipid mediators involved in inflammation (13, 14). Rvs, both E and D series, are products of transcellular biosynthesis (15). Therefore, saliva can be a potentially noninvasive method to assess SPMs in humans.

Here, we establish the stereochemistry of a 17R-epimer of RvD2 that was missing from the repertoire of validated LC-MS/MS functional standards of the SPMs, using total organic synthesis. We report identification of this novel 7S,16R,17R-trihydroxy-4Z,8E,10Z,12E,14E,19Z-docosahexaenoic acid (17R-RvD2) in human saliva. The 17R-RvD2 proved to possess potent functions in cellular mechanisms that are central in the resolution of inflammation. In the present report, we also found that the 17R-RvD2 activates the RvD2 receptor. RvD2 receptor axis has emerged as a potent therapeutic approach in controlling the resolution of inflammation in a wide range of human diseases, including lung inflammation, Duchenne muscular dystrophy (16, 17), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 cytokine eicosanoid storm (18) [recently reviewed in (19)].

RESULTS

A novel RvD2 in human saliva

Salivary glands are highly vascularized, allowing the exchange of compounds between blood and saliva by passive diffusion or active transport (13). Since lipid mediators, including several SPMs, are found in human saliva (12), we investigated whether SPMs are present in saliva of healthy individuals, using targeted LC-MS/MS with a panel of SPM and eicosanoid standards. A product with a different retention time (TR = 10.59 min) than RvD2 (TR = 9.73 min) was observed in saliva from healthy humans (Fig. 1A). This RvD2-like structure displayed similar MS/MS to RvD2 with parent ion mass/charge ratio (m/z) 375 = M-H and daughter ions m/z 357 = M-H-H2O, m/z (175) = M-H-CH2-(CH)2-(CH2)2-CO2-CH2-(CH)2-CH2-CH3-H2O-2H, and m/z 215 = 233-H2O (Fig. 1A). Also, this targeted approach confirmed the identification of both MaR2 and PDX in human saliva, as evidenced by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) chromatograms and characteristic ions present in their MS/MS spectra (fig. S1, A and B) (20). Identification included matching of retention times and key tandem mass spectral fragmentation ions to those also present in synthetic standards.

Total organic synthesis of 17R-RvD2

Given that both the 17S and 17R D-series Rvs were previously described (21, 22), this new RvD2-like structure was suspected to be the missing epimeric isomer of RvD2, namely, 17R-hydroxy-resolvin D2 (17R-RvD2). Thus, a convergent and stereocontrolled total synthesis was undertaken in order to determine its complete stereochemistry and biological actions (Fig. 1B and fig. S2). The synthesis of key intermediates I and II was carried out as illustrated in fig. S2. The vinyl iodide ester I was prepared from the known protected isopropylidene methyl 7(S),8-dihydroxy-4(Z)-octenoate (23) via conversion to the tert-butyldimethylsilyl ether (di-TBS) derivative followed by selective deprotection of the primary silyl group, Dess-Martin oxidation and Takai olefination. Intermediate II was prepared from the readily available isopropylidene derivative of 2-deoxy-l-ribose (24). Wittig olefinations, Dess Martin oxidation, and protodesilylation were among key reactions used to furnish the conjugated dienyne II (25). A base-catalyzed epimerization of the α-alkoxy center (26, 27) was the key step to establish the required chiral centers at C-16 and C-17 of 17-epi-RvD2. With these two key fragments at hand, the synthesis of optically pure 7(S),16(R),17(R)-RvD2 was accomplished by a Sonogashira coupling of the chiral vinyl iodide I with the chiral alkyne II in tert-butylamine to give the protected alkyne precursor of 17-epi-RvD2 (III) (Fig. 1B). Mild deprotection of the TBS and isopropylidene groups with 1N HCl produced IV. Next, selective cis-reduction of the triple bond in IV with the Boland Zn/Cu/Ag alloy at 40°C gave the methyl ester of 17-epi-RvD2 (V). Hydrolysis of the methyl ester V with 1N LiOH produced 17-epi-RvD2 (23, 28). The stereochemical structure was characterized and confirmed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), 13C NMR, and ultraviolet (UV; Fig. 1C and figs. S3 and S4). UV spectra showed that 17R-RvD2 have maximum absorbance λmaxMeOH = 301 nm (Fig. 1C).

Direct comparison of physical properties: 17R-RvD2 and RvD2

The new synthetic 17R-RvD2 was compared for its physical properties to RvD2. The chromatographic behaviors and fragmentation patterns of the two Rvs were assessed using LC-MS/MS. LC chromatogram showed a clear separation of almost 1 min between 17R-RvD2 (TR = 10.59) and RvD2 (TR = 9.73) (Fig. 2, A and B). MS/MS spectra of 17R-RvD2 and RvD2 both presented a parent ion at m/z 375 = M-H and daughter ions at m/z 357 = M-H-H2O, m/z 313 = M-H-H2O-CO2, m/z 295 = M-H-2H2O-CO2, m/z 259 = 277-H2O, m/z 247 = M-H-(CHOH)2-CH2-(CH)2-CH2-CH3 + H, m/z 233 = M-H-CHOH-CH2-(CH)2-(CH2)2-CO2, m/z 215 = 233-H2O, m/z 175 = M-H-CH2-(CH)2-(CH2)2-CO2-CH2-(CH)2-CH2-CH3-H2O-2H, and m/z 113 (Fig. 2, A and B). Thus, 17R-RvD2 and RvD2 are chromatographically distinct, while their MS/MS fragmentation and UV signatures are essentially identical.

Identification of 17R-RvD2 in human saliva

The RvD2-like structure identified in human saliva presented identical chromatographic behavior as the synthetic 17R-RvD2 (Fig. 2, B and C). In addition, MS/MS fragmentation spectra gave identical parent ions at m/z 375 = M-H and similar daughter ions of m/z 175 = M-H-CH2-(CH2)2-(CH2)2-CO2-CH2-(CH2)2-CH2-CH3-H2O-2H and m/z 215 = 233-H2O for both saliva and synthetic 17R-RvD2. The 17R-RvD2 was identified in the saliva of three healthy donors (table S1).

A new procedure for unambiguous identification of D-series Rvs was introduced. With N,N-dimethylethylenediamine (DMED) derivatization, Zhu et al. (29) observed that positive-ion electrospray ionization (ESI)–MS/MS of poly unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) containing allylic vicinal diols exhibit specific fragmentation patterns. When an allylic hydroxyl group of the vicinal diol is proximal to the terminal DMED moiety, as in RvD2 and 17R-RvD2, a carbene (─CH═CH─CH:) is formed. Zhu et al. (29) documented RvD2 in 20 μl of sera from women and the other SPMs using this powerful approach. Thus, we used this derivative approach to gain additional evidence regarding the identity of 17R-RvD2 in human saliva (Fig. 3). These results indicated the formation of the diagnostic carbene fragment at m/z 331, albeit low in intensity, in addition to other key ions including the parent molecular ion at m/z 447 = M + H and daughter ions at m/z 429 = M + H-H2O, m/z 411 = M + H-2H2O, m/z 393 = M + H-3H2O, and m/z 380, providing additional evidence to confirm endogenous 17R-RvD2 in saliva (Fig. 3). To evaluate instrument detection limits for 17R-RvD2-DMED, serial dilutions of the standard material were prepared, and the lower limit of detection was defined as a signal three times higher than the background, and was thus determined as approximately 0.5 pg/μl.

Production of 17R-RvD2 by human leukocytes

Saliva can contain squamous epithelial cells and leukocytes, mainly neutrophils as determined by flow cytometry using the CD15 surface marker for human neutrophils (Fig. 4A). In addition, salivary glands have epithelial cells and harbor a high number of macrophages (13). Thus, we next assayed which immune cell subtypes are involved in the biosynthesis of the 17R-RvD2 by incubating human leukocytes with DHA. Macrophage polarization into M1- and M2-like macrophages was verified by flow cytometry (fig. S5). In leukocytes, DHA is oxygenated to a 17R-hydroperoxy-intermediate by either aspirin-acetylated COX-2 or P450 (30) (Fig. 4B). Once formed, the hydroperoxy-containing product can undergo oxygenation by 5-LOX, leading to the biosynthesis of 17R-RvD2 (Fig. 4B). Conversion of DHA into 17R-RvD2 was observed with human M2-like macrophages (Fig. 4C), M1-like macrophages, and monocytes (Fig. 4D), but not with neutrophils (Fig. 4E). These results are consistent with the leukocyte 15-LOX stereochemical production of 30% R-alcohol chirality (31). DHA was converted to RvD2 by all leukocyte subtypes (Fig. 4E).

17R-RvD2 proresolving bioactions

During the acute inflammatory response, LTB4 induces neutrophil infiltration, while PGE2 increases vasodilatation (32, 33). Resolution of inflammation is defined by cessation of neutrophil recruitment and clearance of apoptotic cells by efferocytosis (34). Along these lines, we evaluated whether 17R-RvD2 has proresolving properties. LTB4 and PGE2 were used in mice as a pharmacological model to gauge neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 5, A to D, and fig. S6) (35). Topical application of 1 μg of LTB4 and 1 μg of PGE2 to the inner mouse ear induced higher percentage of neutrophils (Ly6G-positive cells) in the ear after 24 hours compared to vehicle alone (fig. S6). Topical treatment of the mouse ear with 17R-RvD2 before LTB4 and PGE2 blocked neutrophilic infiltration at 24 hours compared to LTB4 plus PGE2 with vehicle (Fig. 5, B to D).

M2-like macrophages play a key role in the resolution of inflammation by producing SPMs (36). Therefore, we next examined the actions of 17R-RvD2 on macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 5, E to G). 17R-RvD2 increased the percentage of CD206 and CD163 double-positive macrophages, showing enhanced polarization toward the M2-like phenotype (Fig. 5, F and G). Production of SPMs by M2-like macrophages was shown to increase efferocytosis of both apoptotic and senescent cells (37, 38). Next, we evaluated whether 17R-RvD2 stimulated the efferocytosis of carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)–labeled senescent red blood cells (RBCs) by M2-like macrophages (fig. S7). The 17R-RvD2 increased efferocytosis of senescent RBCs by M2 macrophages in a dose-dependent manner and statistically significant, with a 50% effective concentration (EC50) of 2.6 × 10−14 M (Fig. 5, H to I).

Activation of the DRV2/GPR18 receptor

The ability of 17R-RvD2 to activate the RvD2 receptor was examined using electrical cell substrate impedance sensing in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells overexpressing recombinant human GPR18 (Fig. 6 and fig. S8). Both 17R-RvD2 and RvD2 rapidly induced a decrease in impedance, thus indicating activation of the GPR18 receptor (Fig. 6, A and B). No statistically significant difference was obtained between RvD2 and 17R-RvD2. Ligand-receptor interactions were further confirmed by assessing cyclic adenosine 3′,5′-monophosphate (cAMP) levels, which has been documented as a second messenger downstream of GPR18 in human macrophages (39). Both 17R-RvD2 and RvD2 dose-dependently (0.1 to 100 nM) elicited increase in levels of cAMP (Fig. 6, C and D). The EC50 was determined for 17R-RvD2 ~ 1.0 × 10−10 M with Emax ~ 2.8-fold change, and RvD2 ~ 2.2 × 10−10 M with Emax ~ 2.0-fold change. These results indicate that RvD2 and 17R-RvD2 activate the DRV2/GPR18 receptor with similar potencies.

Phagocytosis of Escherichia coli by human PMNs

RvD2 stimulates neutrophil phagocytosis of E. coli via the activation of the GPR18 receptor (39). For direct comparisons, human neutrophils were incubated with 17R-RvD2, RvD2, the GPR18-specific agonist PSB-KD107 (1 and 10 nM) or vehicle [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.01% ethanol], 15 min before the addition of live, fluorescently labeled E. coli. Time-lapse single-cell imaging revealed that 17R-RvD2 increased neutrophil phagocytosis of E. coli in a time- and dose-dependent manner compared to vehicle alone (Fig. 7A and fig. S9). At 10 nM, all three GPR18 agonists increased E. coli phagocytosis with similar potencies (Fig. 7B). Once phagocytized, the pathogens are destroyed by the neutrophil’s arsenal, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Along these lines, we investigated whether increasing concentrations of 17R-RvD2 and RvD2 (0.1 to 10 nM) stimulate intracellular ROS generation in neutrophils incubated with E. coli. Both SPMs dose-dependently increased intracellular ROS by neutrophils (Fig. 7C). Along these lines, 17R-RvD2 increased E. coli killing in human whole blood, giving >50% reduction of E. coli colony-forming units at 2 hours (fig. S10). At the effective concentration of 17R-RvD2 (1 to 100 nM) for antimicrobial mechanisms, i.e., E. coli phagocytosis and clearance as well as intracellular ROS production, 17R-RvD2 did not exhibit direct antibacterial activity (fig. S11). Together, these results demonstrated potent actions of 17R-RvD2 with human neutrophil phagocytosis and bacterial killing.

DISCUSSION

Eicosanoids such as prostaglandin E2, I2, and F2α serve as the structural basis of mimetics that are successful therapeutic agents, e.g., misoprostol, iloprost, and latanoprost (7). The nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that block cyclooxygenases have adverse side effects that include immunosuppression (40). The development of new therapeutics targeting the resolution phase of inflammation has emerged as a promising avenue to treat uncontrolled inflammation (8). Along these lines, our present report highlights uncovering of a 17R D-series Rv, 17R-RvD2. Following structure-function elucidation, we report the total organic synthesis of 17R-RvD2, its complete stereochemistry 17R-RvD2, as well as its identification in human saliva, and cellular mechanisms that function in resolution of inflammation.

The D-series Rvs have proven to be conserved in evolution with the recent identification of nanogram amounts biosynthesized in fish such as Atlantic salmon (41). The SPMs are biosynthesized in humans and recently demonstrated in population studies to be associated with specific diseases with chronic inflammation (42, 43) and physiologic functions associated with cardiometabolic risk (44, 45). In the present report, we found that healthy human saliva is a source of SPMs, including MaR2, PDX, and 17R-RvD2 (Fig. 1). Other investigators have also reported the presence of SPMs in human saliva, including LXA4, LXB4, RvE1, MaR1, RvD1, and PD1 (12, 46–49). In the present study, cell type–specific production of 17R-RvD2 was obtained in human macrophages and monocytes, save neutrophils, using our incubation conditions. Neutrophils being the main leukocyte subtypes present in saliva suggest that biosynthesis of 17R-RvD2 might originate in the salivary glands. The submandibular salivary gland has the enzymatic machinery to biosynthesize D-series Rvs in their epithelium. These include both iPLA2, cPLA2, 15-LOX type I, and 5-LOX (50). Salivary glands can also have macrophages associated with the architectures of the tissue (13). Cell-cell interactions can thus function in the mature healthy salivary gland (51) to biosynthesize the D-series Rvs including the newly identified 17R-RvD2 that we found in the present results.

Of interest, production of SPMs in saliva is decreased in oral health status, such as periodontal diseases (46, 49). In addition, saliva values of mediators can reflect both salivary gland production and the peripheral blood compartment (52). Salivary lipid mediators correlate with cardiovascular disease (12, 48, 53, 54). For example, arterial stiffness is associated with higher salivary levels of proinflammatory mediators (53), whereas lower carotid intima media thickness is linked with significantly lower ratios of RvD1 to LTB4 (48). Patients with cardiovascular diseases displayed lower salivary levels of LXB4 (12) and PD1 (54) and higher salivary levels of MaR1 (54). Thus, saliva contains several SPMs that can be a promising noninvasive diagnostic tool for lipid mediators associated with human disease and health (55).

In general, proresolving mediators such as the Rvs, PDs, and MaRs that are SPMs carry defining specialized functions that are key to resolution of inflammation. These are (i) limiting neutrophil infiltration, (ii) enhanced efferocytosis, and (iii) increased clearance of microbial infection (6). RvD2 is a potent mediator in controlling sepsis (56, 57) and stimulates muscle stem cells, suggesting a novel therapeutic approach to address muscle wasting (16, 17). The epimers of RvD1, RvD3, and PD1, respectively, 17R-RvD1, 17R-RvD3, and 17R-PD1, have all been shown to exert proresolving functions (21, 22, 58). For instance, aspirin-triggered SPMs activate resolution of inflammation in colorectal cancer (59). In the present experiments, 17R-RvD2 exhibited potent proresolving functions, as it reduced neutrophil infiltration in a topical pharmacological model of inflammation induced by LTB4 plus PGE2, increased M2 macrophage differentiation and enhanced senescent RBC efferocytosis. 17R-RvD2 also increased E. coli phagocytosis and intracellular ROS generation by polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN; Fig. 7). This novel 17R-RvD2 activated the RvD2 receptor (Fig. 6) with similar potency shown for RvD2 (19). Activation of DRV2 receptor (GPR18) by 17R-RvD2 is thus likely to stimulate extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2, signal transducers and activators of transcription 3 (STAT3), and cAMP response element–binding protein as previously reported for RvD2 (60). Therefore, the results of the present experiments demonstrate that this new epimer 17R-RvD2 fulfills the established criteria (6) of a potent proresolving mediator.

The identification of 17R-RvD2 in human saliva and its proresolving functions suggest that this Rv could be involved in the resolution of inflammation in the oral cavity and along the gastrointestinal tract. Accordingly, other Rvs were showed to have proresolving function in oral cavity health. In the Sjogren’s syndrome, LXA4 blocks cytokine-induced adhesion molecule expression on human umbilical vein endothelial cells and on the human submandibular gland cell line (61). Of interest, the 17R epimer of resolvin D1 (aspirin triggered resolvin D1) restores secretion of saliva in vivo in a mouse Sjogren’s syndrome model (62).

Assignment of the complete stereochemistry of each SPM (6) and their availability has enabled other investigators to confirm and expand SPM identification via LC-MS/MS–based profiling and document their relationships to proinflammatory mediators in murine tissues and human immune cells (63, 64) and in human peripheral blood (65). The profile of Rvs and other SPMs has emerged as a useful means to assess the status of the resolution response in human and animal models. Along these lines, Zhu et al. (29) reported a new technique to confidently identify Rvs, LXs, and other SPMs using a DMED derivative that adds robust analytical precision. In the present experiments, we used this to monitor the 17R-RvD2 in saliva with substantially lower limits of detection with the LC-MS/MS operating in the positive mode (Fig. 3).

SPMs are produced by interferon-β and stimulate resolution of airway inflammation (66). SPMs such as 17R-RvD1 selectively reprogram leukocyte subsets to promote tissue regeneration (67). In view of these novel actions of the original SPMs elucidated in the resolution of inflammation and tissue regeneration (6), our results of the present studies uncover a new potent member of this superfamily of proresolving mediators that will likely have a role in resolution physiology, resolution pharmacology, and resolution medicine that will unfold in the years ahead.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Biosynthesis of Rvs and leukocyte subtypes in human saliva

Healthy humans took Algea DHA omega-3 (500 mg, Nordic Naturals, lot no. 222316, 11/23) for 7 days. On day 7, the volunteers took two low-dose aspirin (162 mg, Bayer, lot no. NAABXFN 10/2024). The subjects denied taking any other medications 2 weeks before donation of saliva. The submandibular glands were gently milked, and saliva was collected in accordance with protocol for discarded material as approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (protocol 1999P001279). Samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry

Three volumes of ice-cold LC/MS grade methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 500 pg of each of the following internal standards: d5-RvD2, d5-RvD3, d5-LXA4, d5-MaR1, d4-LTB4, d8-15S-HETE, and d8-5S-HETE (Cayman Chemical) were added to fresh human saliva. The lipid mediators were extracted from the samples using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) as described elsewhere (68). LC-MS/MS data were acquired on a triple-quadrupole 7500 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA) in low-mass mode coupled with an ExionLC system (SCIEX) and a Kinetex column 2.6-μm PS C18 100 Å (100 × 3.0 mm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA). Data were collected, analyzed, and are presented as screen captures using SCIEX OS-Q version 3.1.5.3945 (SCIEX). Synthetic materials were run with each experiment to obtain retention times for reference. Mediator identification criteria required matching of the retention time and at least five MS/MS fragments with the synthetic material and chromatographic signal to noise higher than 3. The default values of 0.00th for m/z are depicted in figures. However, the accuracy of this instrument is approximately ± m/z 0.1. See tables S2 and S3 for LC conditions, mobile phase composition, MS/MS acquisition parameters, mass transitions, and calibration curve coefficients for each targeted mediator.

Preparation of DMED-derivatized Rvs

Reagent grade N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), DMED, triethylamine, and 2-chloro-1-methylpyridinium iodide (Mukaiyama Reagent), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). DMED derivatization was carried out as reported in Zhu et al. (29). In brief, synthetic Rvs of interest (~5 μg each) were subjected to a gentle stream of N2 gas to remove ethanol and were redissolved in 200 μl of DMF.Next, 2 μl of each stock solution were mixed with 0.75 μl of DMED (300 mM), 0.75 μl of triethylamine (150 mM), and 0.75 μl of 2-chloro-1-methylpyridinium iodide (75 mM), followed by purging with N2 gas and vortexing for approximately 1 min. Each reaction mixture was then allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min, before LC-ESI-MS/MS data acquisition. Calibration curves showing good linearity and accuracy were obtained for each derivatized standards. DMED derivatization of Rvs from biological samples was similarly performed after solid phase extraction as indicated above.

Total organic synthesis of 17R-RvD2

The total synthesis of 17R-RvD2 was accomplished using a stereospecific and convergent strategy, from small commercially available building blocks. The structure of 17R-RvD2 was characterized by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and UV. For details, see Results.

Human cell isolation

Human PMN

Fresh human blood was collected with heparin (10 U/ml) from healthy volunteers, who denied taking medications at least 2 weeks before donation, by venipuncture in a heparinized syringe. All volunteers signed a consent form as approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (protocol 1999P001297). PMNs were isolated by density gradient using Ficoll-histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich, 10771) (69).

Human monocytes

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from deidentified leukopacks from Boston Children’s Hospital Blood Bank (Boston, MA) under protocol no. 1999-P-001279 approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll-Histopaque-1077 density gradient and seeded in six-well plates (30 × 106 PBMCs per well). The wells were then washed with PBS to concentrate monocytes by adhesion.

Human erythrocytes

Human peripheral blood erythrocytes from healthy human volunteers were isolated by centrifugation and aspiration of the platelet-rich plasma and buffy coat layer. Erythrocytes were purified by resuspension in PBS (Lonza, NJ) [10% hematocrit] and centrifugation (500g) followed by aspiration of the top 10% of the erythrocyte layer (70, 71). This purification procedure was carried out six times.

Differentiation of monocytes into M2-like and M1-like macrophages

Monocytes were differentiated for 7 days in RPMI 1640 (Lonza) containing 10% fetal calf serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 16000-044), 2 mM l-glutamine (Lonza, 17-605E), 2 mM penicillin-streptomycin (Lonza, 17-602E), and recombinant human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (20 ng/ml; hr-MCSF) (PeproTech, 300-25) at 37°C. For M2-like macrophages, monocyte-derived macrophages were then polarized into M2-like macrophages with interleukin-4 (IL-4; 20 ng/ml; PeproTech, 200-04) for 48 hours (69, 72). For the M0- and M1-like macrophages, monocytes were differentiated for 7 days in RPMI 1640 (Lonza) containing 10% fetal calf serum, 2 mM l-glutamine, 2 mM penicillin-streptomycin, and recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (20 ng/ml; hr-GMCSF) (PeproTech, 300-03) at 37°C. To get M1-like macrophages, monocyte-derived macrophages were further polarized with interferon-γ (20 ng/ml; PeproTech, 200-04), and lipopolysaccharide (100 ng/ml) for 48 hours.

Biosynthesis of 17R-RvD2 by human leukocytes

Neutrophils, monocytes, M2-like, and M1-like macrophages (5 × 106 cells/ml) were incubated in PBS+/+ containing 5 μg of DHA and zymosan (100 ng/ml; 1 ml per incubation) for 45 min. Incubations were stopped with the addition of 3 volumes of MeOH containing 500 pg of each of the internal standards. Of note, incubations were performed in six-well plates for the macrophages and in 15-ml tubes for the neutrophils and the monocytes. Samples were stored at −80°C (less than 2 weeks) until solid phase extraction and LC-MS/MS analyses.

Mouse ear inflammation

Animal experimental procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of Brigham and Women’s Hospital (protocol number 2016N000145). Male Friend leukemia virus B mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). The inner side of the right ear was treated with acetone, and the inner side of the left ear was topically treated with 1 μg of 17R-RvD2 in acetone (25 μl). After 5 to 7 min, a mixture containing 1 μg of LTB4 (Cayman Chemical, 20110) and 1 μg of PGE2 (Cayman Chemical, 14010) was applied on the inner side of both ears topically. At 24 hours, the ears were cut using forceps and a scalpel. Digestion of the tissues was performed by incubation with a solution of Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS) containing Ca2+ and Mg2+, collagenase (1 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, C5138) and deoxyribonuclease (100 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, DN25-100mg) for 45 min, followed by incubation with a solution of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200-056) for 20 min. The cells were counted using a hemocytometer. Cell suspensions were fixed with PBS containing 3% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 2% paraformaldehyde.

17R-RvD2 on macrophage polarization

Polarization of monocyte-derived macrophages was conducted with 1 nM 17R-RvD2 or corresponding amount of vehicle for 15 min before the addition of IL-4 (20 ng/ml). Addition of 17R-RvD2 was repeated after 24 and 48 hours. After 48 hours, the cells were washed and incubated for 20 min with PBS containing 5 mM EDTA. Cell suspensions were fixed with PBS containing 3% of FBS and 2% of paraformaldehyde.

Efferocytosis of senescent RBCs by M2-like macrophages

Purified erythrocytes were resuspended with PBS (20% hematocrit) and stored at 4°C for ~96 hours to induce senescence. Senescent erythrocytes were counted, washed twice with DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA), and then stained with Cell Trace CFSE at a concentration of 5 μM for 30 min at 37°C (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA). Monocyte-derived M2-like macrophages plated in six-well plates were incubated with 17R-RvD2 (10−16, 10−15, 10−14, 10−13, 10−12, and 10−11 M) or corresponding volume of vehicle (EtOH 0.01%) for 15 min at 37°C. M2-like macrophages were then incubated with senescent RBCs (ratio of 50 RBCs: 1 M2-like macrophage). After 3 hours at 37°C, the wells were washed six times. Cell suspensions were fixed with PBS containing 3% FBS and 2% paraformaldehyde and analyzed using flow cytometry.

hGPR18-CHO cells

CHO cells expressing recombinant human GPR18 (hGPR18) were prepared as in (39). In brief, wild-type CHO cells (CHO-WT) were transfected with human gpr18 (GPR18 [NM_005292] Human Untagged Clone, OriGene catalog no. SC321879) for 3 days. Positive hGPR18-expressing cells were selected using HAM F-12 media containing G418/geneticin (1 mg/ml; Life Technologies).

Impedance change

Ligand-receptor interactions were determined by measuring impedance across CHO cell monolayers using the Agilent xCELLigence real-time cell analysis system (Agilent RTCA S16; Santa Clara, CA). In brief, human GPR18 (hGPR18) or WT CHO cells were plated at 1 × 105 cells per well onto the 16-well E-plates (Agilent E-plate 16 PET, no. G092962). Test compounds were added to the chambers in HAM F-12 serum-free medium, and real-time impedance changes were monitored for 10 min at 37°C.

cAMP levels

CHO cells (WT or human GPR18 expressing cells) were plated onto 24-well plates (0.2 × 106 cells per well) and incubated with IBMX (50 μM; no. 13347, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) for 10 min, followed by RvD2, 17R-RvD2 (0.01 to 100 nM), or vehicle control [0.1% ethanol (v/v) in DPBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+] for 15 min and cAMP levels determined by using a cAMP select enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cayman Chemical, 501040). Emax (the maximum effect) and EC50 (the concentration that gives 50% of the maximum effect) were determined using nonlinear regression with log (agonist) versus response (three parameters) with GraphPad Prism.

Phagocytosis of E. coli

E. coli serotype O6:K2:H1 was cultured in Luria-Bertani broth, collected at mid-log phase (optical density at 600 nm = 0.5; 0.5 × 109 colony-forming units/ml), and labeled with BacLight Green (Molecular Probes, B35000). Neutrophils were plated onto eight-well chamber slides (1 × 105 cells per well) 15 min before the experiments. Neutrophils were incubated with 17R-RvD2, RvD2 (0.1, 1, or 10 nM) or vehicle control [containing 0.01% ethanol (v/v)] in DPBS containing Ca2+ and Mg2+ for 15 min at 37°C, followed by addition of E. coli (50 E. coli: 1 neutrophil). Fluorescent images were recorded every 10 min for 120 min (37°C) with Keyence BZ-9000 (BIOREVO) inverted fluorescence phase-contrast microscope (20× objective) equipped with a monochrome color switching camera using BZ-II Viewer software (Keyence). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using the BZ-II Analyzer.

Flow cytometry

Cell surface expression was analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer and files were analyzed using FlowJo version X (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA).

Saliva

Cell suspensions (1 ml of saliva) were fixed with PBS containing 3% FBS and 2% paraformaldehyde. Cells were stained with leukocytes markers PerCP anti-human CD45 (BioLegend, 304026), Alexa Fluor 488 anti-human CD11b (BioLegend, 301318), phycoerythrin (PE) anti-human CD11c (BioLegend, 301605), allophycocyanin (APC) anti-human CD16 (BioLegend, 302012), Pe/Cy7 anti-human CD15 (BioLegend, 301924), and APC/Cy7 anti-human CD14 (BioLegend, 325620) for 30 min.

Mouse ear inflammation

Cells were incubated with leukocytes markers APC/Cy7 anti-mouse CD45 (BD Pharmigen, 557659), BV605 anti-mouse CD68 (BioLegend, 137021), BV421 anti-mouse Ly6C (BioLegend, 128032), and AF700 anti-mouse Ly6G (BioLegend, 127622) for 30 min.

Macrophage surface marker

MCSF-differentiated macrophages with and without IL-4 were fixed in PBS containing 3% FBS and 2% paraformaldehyde. Cells were incubated with Human TruStain FcX solution (BioLegend) for 15 min at 4°C and then incubated with macrophage markers APC anti-human CD206 (BD Pharmingen, clone 19.2, 550889) and PE anti-human CD163 (BioLegend, clone GH1/61, 333606), CD54 (BD Pharmingen, clone HA58, 559771), and CD80 (BioLegend, clone 2D10, 305208) for 30 min (70).

GPR18 expression

CHO cells (WT or human GPR18 expressing cells) were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647–labeled rabbit anti-human GPR18 immunoglobulin G (IgG; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, Colorado, NBP2-24918AF647) or Alexa Fluor 647–labeled rabbit IgG isotype control (NBP2-36463AF647, Novus Biologicals) for 30 min at 4°C.

Software

Chemical structures were drawn using ChemDraw Level Professional v20.1.0.112 and Chem3D v21.0.0 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA).

Statistical analysis

Significance was determined with the use of one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Statistical significance was set at P values < 0.05.
