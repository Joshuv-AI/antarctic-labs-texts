Tardive Dystonia Described as Tardive Tremor
Authors: Sarah Gaber, Hikaru Kamo, Christopher W Hess
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the recent clinical image article discussing isolated neck tremor secondary to aripiprazole exposure [1]. We appreciate the authors bringing attention to the issue of tardive syndromes following neuroleptic exposure and specifically to the potential for both resolution of symptoms upon cessation of neuroleptic therapy and continued remission despite resumed neuroleptic use. We agree that the patient's symptoms indicate a tardive movement disorder; however, we would like to offer a few observations based on the accompanying video that suggest her symptoms are most consistent with tardive dystonia rather than tremor. These points may be of general interest to the broad readership and clinicians who evaluate similar cases.

Tardive cervical dystonia typically presents as focal cervical dystonic posturing characterized by sustained muscle contractions leading to abnormal head postures, most classically retrocollis, though postural abnormalities such as torticollis, laterocollis, shift, or a combination of these can occur [2]. Movements are patterned and typically sustained, but occasionally tardive dystonia can appear somewhat rhythmic, which complicates distinction from true tremor [2, 3]. In the present case, the patient has tardive dystonia characterized by dystonic retrocollis with repetitive pulling of the neck into extension, with phasic contractions producing a tremor‐mimicking quality. The movements appear isolated to the cervical region in the absence of other involuntary movements in the trunk or limbs, typical of focal cervical dystonic phenomenology [4]. Although described as oscillatory anterior and posterior motion, close examination suggests that the movements are not truly oscillatory around a neutral head position, but consist of phasic muscle contractions producing a slow, repetitive pulling backwards of the head producing the false impression of tremor due to dystonic contraction followed by passive relaxation [3, 5]. The movements observed in this video are quite low in frequency, variable in amplitude, and non‐rhythmic, which are features not observed with tremor [3]. The presence of intermittent laterocollis and torticollis can be observed as well.

From a phenomenological standpoint, additional bedside observations could be helpful in further characterizing similar presentations. For instance, it may be of use to observe whether positioning the patient's head opposite to the apparent direction of pull increases the amplitude of the movement, as positional modulation is often seen in dystonic patients [3]. Clarification regarding whether the neck pillow seen in the video serves as an alleviating maneuver would also be of interest, given the regular use of sensory tricks among patients with cervical dystonia [5]. These considerations are offered to support accurate diagnosis and characterization of phenomenology, and we appreciate the authors' contribution in bringing this tardive presentation to the literature.
