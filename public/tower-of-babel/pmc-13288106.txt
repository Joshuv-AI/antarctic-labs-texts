Annotated checklist of the butterflies (Lepidoptera: Papilionoidea) of Burkina Faso, West Africa—synthesis and new occurrences
Authors: Tom Jamonneau, Abubakar S Ringim, Michel Libert, Andrea C Reyes Camargo, Alain Coache, Jérémy Bouyer, Konrad Fiedler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsect biodiversity documentation remains strikingly uneven, with significant knowledge gaps persisting for various taxa and regions. Butterflies (Papilionoidea), recognized as reliable bioindicators and flagship species, face such gaps in some West African countries, particularly Burkina Faso (BF). This study presents the most comprehensive and up-to-date checklist of butterflies in BF, compiling records from 29 diverse sources, including published literature, online databases, citizen science platforms and private collections. Our analysis reveals 220-225 butterfly species for BF, the presence of 5 of them being uncertain. This represents a 40% increase over previous compilations, including 47 new records for the country. We highlight pronounced disparities in research effort, with for instance Lycaenidae and Hesperiidae consistently underrepresented compared to Nymphalidae and Pieridae, probably because of sampling biases and intrinsic species ecological characteristics. Spatial analysis revealed limited and uneven geographic coverage, with vast areas, particularly northern BF, remaining largely unexplored. We discuss challenges related to data accessibility, taxonomic inconsistencies, and the impact of national security on biodiversity research. This synthesis not only significantly expands BF’s known butterfly diversity but also underscores the deep need for building sustained and diversified research efforts, and local capacity to address remaining knowledge gaps in a country where entomological exploration still promises numerous discoveries.Keywords: Afrotropical region, biodiversity monitoring challenge, data compilation, geographic distribution, host plant

Introduction

Worldwide, efforts to document biodiversity are still strikingly unequal, with some areas remaining very poorly documented, especially in Africa. In parallel, human activities are deeply and increasingly modifying this biodiversity, often reducing it (Ruddiman 2013, Goudie 2018). Nonetheless, human activities frequently rely on biodiversity to ensure their own functioning, efficiency or resilience (Haines-Young and Potschin-Young 2010, Vanbergen and Insect Pollinators Initiative 2013); making biodiversity documentation a prerequisite for a sustainable management of ecosystems (e.g., Willis et al. 2007, Haines-Young and Potschin-Young 2010, Haase et al. 2018). Thus, areas where biodiversity remains poorly documented should be prioritized, especially as they are also frequently located in developing countries where rapid socioeconomic progress increases pressure on ecosystems (Marques et al. 2019, Zabel et al. 2019).

Butterflies (Papilionoidea) are recognized as a major component of biodiversity, with up to 19,000 species worldwide and more than 4,500 in Africa (Williams and Edge 2024). They hold a strategically important position in the management of ecosystems for at least 3 reasons. Firstly, they can act as umbrella and flagship species (e.g., Lindenmayer and Westgate 2020, Wang et al. 2020), being capable of engaging a broad audience, including policymakers and managers. Protecting butterflies can, in turn, contribute to the conservation of the ecosystems they inhabit (Caro 2010). Secondly, certain species are bioindicators, providing insights into the level of disturbance (or “health”) of the ecosystems in which they are present or absent (Bobo et al. 2006, Bouyer et al. 2007, Caro 2010). These species often rely on specific host plants for their larvae (caterpillars), which are generally stenotopic and require specific environmental conditions. In some cases, there are even more intricate associations such as interactions with ants and ant-nesting tree species described in many Afrotropical Epitolini (e.g., Larsen 2005, Sáfián and Larsen 2009). Ultimately, one may argue that said biodiversity components should be preserved for their intrinsic and nonutilitarian value, as art works (Bouyer et al. 2019). This assertion is supported by historical precedents demonstrating humanity’s willingness to risk personal safety for the preservation of cultural artefacts (Howard 2016).

Although Burkina Faso (BF) is not among the most butterfly-diverse countries in West Africa, such as Nigeria—which is probably home to more than 1,500 species—it is still an interesting field of research. In fact, Larsen (2005) documented only 161 species (a number obtained from a manual compilation of all listed species, rather than explicitly provided by the author), despite estimates suggesting that 200–250 may be present (Larsen, pers. comm. 2010). Following this, Larsen began studying BF's butterflies, intending to compile his findings into a dedicated book, after describing the country as one of the least explored countries in the region. Unfortunately, this work, along with several other projects, remained unfinished due to his passing in 2015 (Huertas 2015).

The present contribution compiles a substantial number of records from various sources of data, some of which Larsen had intended to use for his planned book, while many others are newly considered. Notably, an innovative aspect of our work is the inclusion of data from citizen science platforms. The compilation resulted in a list of 225 species, compared to the 2 previous compilations of 161 (Larsen 2005) and 154 (Sáfián et al. 2024, instead of 156, as 2 species of Anthene sp.–Triclema sp. appear twice because of synonymy). As a result, this study has increased BF’s list of butterflies by approximately 40%, notably including 47 yet unpublished occurrences. Ultimately, the continuing challenges in documenting the butterfly fauna of BF are also discussed in light of the recent history of the country.

For further illustration, the documents of the Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia and the BF list of the ABDB frequently relied on Larsen’s book. Records from the 2 citizen science platforms, iNaturalist and Observation.org, were carefully reviewed for identification errors or inaccuracies. Overall, our method assumed that increasing the number of sources helps ensure the records’ robustness and the checklist’s completeness.

In addition, when available, localities of species occurrences were retrieved, particularly to assess which areas of the country and taxonomic groups might be over- or underdocumented.

When judged relevant and possible, source authors were contacted to complete the information regarding species occurrences and/or associated localities.

The taxonomic classification used here follows the “Classification of Afrotropical butterflies to generic level” (Williams and Edge 2024). For subgenera and subspecies, it further relies on the more recent version of the genera documents of the Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia, except for 2 recently reviewed genera including African species, Eurema replacing Terias (Leong et al. 2025) and Capys (Afrix) replacing Deudorix (Virachola) (Zhang et al. 2025). Species common (i.e., vernacular) names were also retrieved from the Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia and eventually completed following Edge, pers. comm. (2025).

Results

General Remarks on Species Names, Taxonomy and BF Butterflies Occurrences

Inconsistencies were found within the analyzed sources regarding species and genera names. These inconsistencies fell into 2 main categories: the use of outdated taxonomy or an alternative taxonomic classification (e.g., Anthene marshalli of source 1 is now synonymized with Triclema phoenicis, or Acraea eponina in source 23 now being called Telchinia serena, Table 1), and, simple misspellings of taxa (e.g., Eriboea [ex. Charaxes] achaemenes atlantica and Charaxes varanes vologeses misspelled “C. achemenes atlentica” and “C. varanes vologedes” in source 19, Table 1). Despite being challenging, these inconsistencies did not significantly impact the results from the present study.

Going further, various analyzed sources raised important issues when documenting the presence of a species in BF and/or providing precise locality information as biogeographic descriptions tended to be imprecise. For instance, in d’Abrera’s book (source 13), accurately determining occurrences in BF was particularly challenging, as the biogeographic descriptions were of the order of “From Senegal to Central Africa” or “Open savanna areas in Africa”. Additionally, the author inconsistently referred to the country as Upper Volta and BF. Consequently, only confirmed occurrences were considered.

Minor errors were also found in other sources, such as the Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia. For example, Coeliades (Coeliades) aeschylus is reported in “Banforo Waterfall” within BF (Edge and Williams 2024b), whereas the locality name is “Banfora Waterfall.”

Generally, the accuracy of locality data varied, ranging from precise geolocation coordinates (e.g., for sources 4 and 5, Table 1) to broader regional descriptions within the country. To ensure data consistency, informative locality names (i.e., cities of a significant size or protected areas) were assigned to georeferenced occurrences reported within a maximum distance of 10 km from the locality (Table 2, Supplementary Table S1). In order to limit the size difference among localities, we divided the W National Park into 3 zones: the triple point zone (border with Benin and Niger), having the triple point—Kabougou road dividing the northern and the southern zones.

Moreover, in source 23, while 54 species were said to have been identified, we were able to extract only 25 (throughout the article sections) as no complete species list was given (Table 1). On the other hand, regarding the reliability of species identification, we were only able to verify part of all species occurrences, mainly from the studied collection of source 16, and iNaturalist and Observation.org (sources 4 and 6). These platforms often include photographs of organisms, which allowed us to have an accurate identification. During our prospect of butterfly records reported in BF through these platforms, several misidentifications were detected. Notably for Colotis auxo, Favonius quercus and Tarucus balkanicus individuals that in reality were C. evarne, Hypolycaena philippus and T. kiki, respectively. All these errors were reported to the Observation.org administrators and directly corrected on iNaturalist. In parallel, the only other inconsistency we could verify was Anthene kikuyu from the studied collection (source 16), which was attributed to A. talboti. Additionally, for the genus Leptotes, which is known to require genitalia dissection—particularly of male specimens—for reliable identification (Larsen 2005), such dissections were performed on specimens from the source 16 private collection. For all other sources, it should be noted that most have been either published in peer-reviewed journals and/or by specialists, and are therefore presumed to have a certain degree of confidence.

Which Taxa Are Known to Occur in BF and Where?

From the 29 data sources examined (Table 1), a total of 225 species were identified, spanning 96 genera, 18 subfamilies, and 5 families (Supplementary Table S1). Among these, 47 species represent new records for BF (Table 2), as they had not been previously reported in any scientific publication (for some in natura observations, see Fig. 1). Three genera were particularly speciose: Charaxes (11 spp.), Anthene (10 spp.) and Colotis (10 spp.), even though some of their species were reassigned to other genera (e.g., Eriboea, Triclema and Teracolus) in recent revisions. The number of species varied significantly across families (Fig. 2a; χ2 = 80.76, df = 4, p < 0.001); and the 2 most represented families (in terms of species count) were Lycaenidae (33.0%) and Nymphalidae (33.9%), followed by Hesperiidae (17.4%), Pieridae (12.5%), and Papilionidae (3.1%). The frequency of species mentions across data sources deviated significantly from the expected proportions based on the distribution of species among families (Fig. 2a and b; χ2 = 35.65, df = 4, p < 0.001), with an overrepresentation of Nymphalidae and, to a lesser extent, Pieridae, and an underrepresentation of Hesperiidae. Regarding locality information, the deviation from the same expected proportions was even more pronounced, with an additional underrepresentation of Lycaenidae (Fig. 2a and c; χ2 = 158.46, df = 4, p < 0.001). Similarly, the distribution of new country records among families differed significantly from the expected proportions based on the distribution of species among families, with a clear overrepresentation of Hesperiidae and underrepresentation across all other families, particularly Lycaenidae and Nymphalidae (Fig. 2a and d; χ2 = 19.97, df = 3, p < 0.001). Only a nonsignificant deviation from the same expected proportions trend was observed regarding species lacking locality information, with Lycaenidae being slightly more represented and Hesperiidae slightly less than expected (Fig. 2a and e; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.40).

Discussion

Using the most exhaustive and up-to-date compilation of butterfly records from 29 sources, the present study is an important step regarding butterfly knowledge in BF and, to a lesser extent, West Africa. A total of 225 butterfly species were recorded in the country, including 47 new records. This represents a 40% increase from Larsen’s book (2005), accounting for about 29% of the 336 known African butterfly genera (Williams and Edge 2024).

General Remarks on Examined Data Sources

Compilation and analysis of existing species records was a major challenge in this study since data access was not always granted, as already noted in previous studies (e.g., Vollmar et al. 2010, Gonier et al. 2024). This difficulty was particularly acute with data sources behind paywalls, no longer printed, lacking digital formats or those only available as images, which do not allow keyword searches. In some cases, such as for Gardiner et al. (2005, source 23), the recovery of a full species list was impossible despite extensive efforts, as no raw or summary data were provided and attempts to contact the authors were unsuccessful. We encourage future studies to publish digital versions alongside printed ones, ensure open access, share raw data (ideally in editable formats), and include reliable contact information. We acknowledge that these practices, overall open-access, can be challenging in developing countries due to financial and logistical constraints (Taubert et al. 2021, Kellner 2023). Another difficulty was due to taxonomic and nomenclatural inconsistencies which, even if it did not block the study, significantly slowed it. As previously emphasized, such errors can propagate and distort scientific knowledge, affecting methodologies, hypotheses and broader concepts (e.g., International Union of Biological Sciences 1999, Bortolus 2008). Responsibility for this lies not only on individual researchers but also on scientific journals and the broader research system, which often lacks funding to support proper taxonomic training and employment, and eventually push researchers to publish more. Based on the above, we encourage universities, research institutions and funding bodies to better support taxonomic expertise, and journals to uphold rigorous review standards. We further recommend authors to provide up-to-date taxonomy, relevant synonyms and vernacular names to clearly identify the organisms studied, which may render further advantages such as communicating with lay audiences or promoting inclusion in science (Potter 1984, Marinho and Scatigna 2022).

New Species Records for BF

At least 1 species of host plant was identified for 34 out of the 47 (72%) new BF species records, as documented in existing literature (Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia) (Arbonnier 2019, César et al. 2024, César, pers. comm. 2025); therefore the presence of these species in the country is consistent. This verification also highlighted numerous butterfly species with limited known host plants, including 9 without published host plant data (e.g., Abantis [Caprona] pseudonigeriana; Desloges and Williams 2024). This suggests that despite the current lack of documented host plant evidence in BF, it is more likely that the remaining 28% of new records are correct.

Among the 47 newly recorded butterfly species in BF, 20 belong to the Hesperiidae family, indicating a disproportionate overrepresentation. This finding reinforces the idea that the most significant discoveries, potentially including new species to science, often lie within smaller, more elusive and harder-to-identify butterfly groups. Despite this, several large and relatively easily recognizable species, particularly within Nymphalidae such as Euphaedra (Euphaedrana) themis, Pseudacraea lucretia, P. eurytus, and Hypolimnas anthedon, were also newly recorded in this study.

Among the species with at least one known host plant, Kallimoides rumia is the only one whose host plant has not yet been recorded in BF (Arbonnier 2019, César et al. 2024, César, pers. comm. 2025). Its caterpillars are known to feed on Brillantaisia, an Afrotropical genus of Acanthaceae (Williams 2020) that appears to be restricted to regions a few hundred kilometers south of BF. This suggests that Brillantaisia may occur locally in the Marre aux Hippopotames Classified Forest, where the species was observed, or that K. rumia relies on additional, as-yet-undescribed host plants in this locality.

Further evidence for this (previously) undocumented biodiversity comes from species whose presence in BF was hypothesized by Larsen (2005) based on biogeographical assumptions. Many of these, such as Eurema (Eurema) floricola or Colotis liagore, are now documented in this study. Furthermore, we concur with Larsen that the presence of other species, including Colotis calais (formerly amata) and Amauris (Amauris) niavius, is highly probable and will likely be confirmed by future studies. Effectively, biogeography and documented presence of host plants for these suspected species provide further support for their occurrence in BF. For instance, C. calais, found in the nearby arid savannas of Senegal, Mauritania, Niger and Mali, has documented host plants including Capparis spp., Salvadora australis and S. persica (Williams 2022a), which are now known to occur in BF (Arbonnier 2019, César et al. 2024, César, pers. comm. 2025). Similarly, A. (A.) niavius, documented in neighboring Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Togo and Bénin (in a large range of habitats including disturbed ones; Williams 2022b), utilizes host plants such as Glossonema spp. (syn. Cynanchum), Heliotropium indicum, Marsdenia spp., Secamone spp., and Tylophora spp., which are now known from BF (Arbonnier 2019, César et al. 2024, César, pers. comm. 2025).

These 47 new records expand the known ranges of occurrence for several species. For instance, E. (E.) themis and Bematistes (ex. Acraea) epaea, reliably documented at Bobo-Dioulasso and Nasso (at the periphery of the first locality), would now represent the northernmost recorded occurrences for these 2 species (Bernaud and Ducarme 2021, Bernaud 2023, Edge and Williams 2024a). Conversely, the presence of Teracolus eris in the BF section of the W National Park marks one of its southernmost records in West Africa (the species is otherwise known from several Central and South African countries; e.g., Larsen 2005).

Other Records Raising Particular Reflections

The documented presence of 5 species—Stempfferia (Cercenia) leonina, Capys (Afrix) lorisona, Anthene sylvanus, Leptosia medusa, and Papilio (Nieropapilio) horribilis—requires cautious consideration. For S. (C.) leonina and L. medusa, the sole primary source is Bivar de Sousa et al. (2016, source 20), a reference on which the Afrotropical Butterflies and Skippers Encyclopedia also relies for S. (C.) leonina (Williams 2021). However, Bivar de Sousa et al. (2016) provided no further specific details. Similarly, A. sylvanus is mentioned by Sáfián et al. (2024, source 1), Coache et al. (2017, source 17), and Larsen (2005, source 2), but we strongly suspect the first 2 sources rely solely on Larsen’s record(s). Crucially, Larsen’s book lacks further details, and we were unable to access or verify the BF specimen(s) he would have examined. Eventually, regarding P. (N.) horribilis, after contacting the authors of source 1, it was confirmed that their mention was not based on any actual record. Consequently, the species was removed from the official list in September 2025.

Furthermore, our understanding of these 5 species habitat preferences, based on personal experience and literature, indicates they are all forest-associated, ecosystems that are rare, even in the south of BF. Stempfferia (Cercenia) leonina thrives in moist semideciduous forests while being a priori less restricted than other Epitolini (Belcastro and Larsen 2006). Capys (Afrix) lorisona inhabits dense savannas and forests (Libert 2004, Edge and Williams 2024c). Anthene sylvanus is an uncommon species recorded in forests and dense Guinea savannas in West Africa (Larsen 2005, Libert 2010). Leptosia medusa prefers forests with complete canopy cover (Larsen 2005, Coache et al. 2017). And P. (N.) horribilis thrives in notably well-preserved wet forests (Larsen 2005).

Regarding their geographical context, Libert’s revision of the Epitola group does not mention any Stempfferia north of central Ghana (Libert 1999, 2020). However, Stempfferia caterpillars feed on algae on Crematogaster-infested trees and shrubs, and these ants are documented in riverine forest belts within BF’s savanna zone (Libert 1999, Larsen 2005, Sáfián, pers. comm. 2025). Undersampled areas in BF, such as the Ivorian frontier along the Leraba River as well as the Téré Classified Forest near Koundougou, present potential habitats for some Epitolini, including S. (C.) leonina.

Libert’s revision recorded C. (A.) lorisona in Bismarckburg, Togo, approximately 350 km south of the BF border, representing the nearest confirmed record (Libert 2004). Nevertheless, several of its known host plants are now documented in BF (César et al. 2024, Edge and Williams 2024c, César, pers. comm. 2025). Similarly, A. sylvanus was recorded by Libert in a genus-based revision as close as the Ghanaian Volta region, at least 400 km south of the BF frontier (Libert 2010). Even if C. (A.) lorisona and A. sylvanus habitats seem more prevalent in BF than those of L. medusa and P. (N.) horribilis, such significant geographical extensions, combined with the source limitations noted, reinforce our doubts.

For L. medusa, confusion with L. alcesta is probable, especially for nonexperts, given their morphological similarity (Larsen 2005, Vande Weghe 2010) and the fact that L. alcesta is confirmed by multiple sources, including the Kou Classified Forest, while previous L. medusa mentions are much further south. As a priori no host plant information is yet published for L. medusa, further discussion on this record is limited. Finally, P. (N.) horribilis is not easily confused with other butterflies, and its only supposed host plant, Beilschmiedia manni, is documented much further south and a priori absent from BF, which makes the species occurrence there particularly uncertain (e.g., Larsen 2005).

The presence of these 5 species in BF is possible, but their occurrence requires careful consideration and future verification. Given the current state of entomological knowledge in the country, and the likelihood of overlooked, poorly referenced, or hard-to-access historical data sources, as previously discussed, it would be inconsistent to completely dismiss their presence at this time.

Besides, we also encountered 2 species—Bebearia (Apectinaria) senegalensis and Euphaedra (Medoniana) medon—that were not new records per se, but whose presence in BF had previously been documented by César et al. (2010, source 8), a publication which has not been widely disseminated in subsequent entomological literature. One possible explanation for this limited visibility is that César et al.’s publication was written in French, despite including an English abstract and being freely available online. The absence of a comprehensive, up-to-date checklist for the butterflies of BF may have contributed to the neglect of such sources. This highlights not only the value of compiling thorough species inventories but also the persistent issue of unequal visibility in scientific literature—particularly for works published in languages other than English, as recently discussed by Serrano et al. (2025).

Unequal and Limited Research Efforts and Knowledge Upon BF Butterflies

Our findings reveal pronounced disparities in research attention and data availability upon butterfly families in BF. Although Lycaenidae and Nymphalidae accounted for the highest species richness (33.0% and 33.9%, respectively), this diversity is not proportionally reflected in the available data. Nymphalidae—and to a lesser extent, Pieridae—are consistently overrepresented across most data sources, whereas Hesperiidae are markedly underrepresented. Several nonexclusive factors likely contribute to this pattern. Species in the Nymphalidae and Pieridae families tend to be larger and more conspicuous, making them easier to detect, catch and identify, which in turn may lead to higher reporting rates. Moreover, the widespread use of fruit-baited traps in prior studies introduces a substantial sampling bias in favor of Nymphalidae, particularly the subfamily Charaxinae, which exhibits a notably high average number of records per species (x̄ ≈ 7.5). In contrast, Hesperiidae and Lycaenidae generally comprise smaller, more elusive species that are difficult to observe, capture and identify, resulting in lower levels of documentation here—in terms of both species and locality counts for the former, and locality counts for the latter. This pattern is consistent with findings from earlier studies (Vantieghem et al. 2017, Di Cecco et al. 2021, Goldstein et al. 2024, Gonier et al. 2024). Alternatively, one could argue that these disparities may (also) reflect true differences in abundance, with Nymphalidae and Pieridae being more numerous in natural environments. Indeed, several studies from other sub-Saharan countries have noted that, beyond local species richness, these 2 families may be locally more abundant than others (Gardiner et al. 2005, Nkwabi 2021, Wale and Abdella 2021, Munisi et al. 2024). As for the ecological mechanisms underlying such patterns—which warrant further investigation—it is plausible that differences in reproductive frequency and fecundity contribute to the observed trends.

On the other hand, the frequency of species mentions exhibited strong deviation from uniformity, with the majority of species documented in less than 6 mentions. This pattern reflects a stark limitation in taxonomic coverage across BF’s butterflies. Furthermore, the retrieval of “only” 29 data sources and an associated total number of mentions of n = 981, after a rather extensive search, underscores the relative scarcity of butterfly records for BF. And this even when compared to neighboring West African countries such as Ghana, Sierra Leone, Côte d’Ivoire, Benin or Nigeria; while this whole area is still known for its butterfly knowledge gap compared to other areas such as North, Central and even South America, Europe, Asia, or southern Africa. Notably, virtually no butterfly data from BF have been collected or published since 2020, further revealing a gap in recent monitoring. Still, BF is not the only sub-Saharan country in this situation; Mali, for instance, also appears to be largely unexplored (aauthors'personal observation).

There is little doubt that accessibility issues and insecurity contribute to the lack of data acknowledged in BF and Mali. In fact, this part of West Africa has long experienced instability linked to political unrest and terrorist threats (Afriyie 2019, Issaev et al. 2022). It is hoped that security restoration will allow new prospecting initiatives to be launched, which would help filling gaps in butterflies knowledge.

One possible way to fill these gaps would be to encourage widespread participation, for instance in schools and/or through citizen science platforms like iNaturalist (Prudic et al. 2018). Additionally, to obtain more standardized data, surveys such as Pollard walk (PW) variants would ensure a more balanced taxonomic coverage (but see Béguinot 2017 for PW critics). Ultimately, strengthening local capacity in species identification, preparation, and maintenance of entomological collections will be essential to support local research on butterflies and other insects.

Further Spatial Patterns in Research Effort and Butterfly Knowledge in BF

In the present compilation, only 69 localities in BF have documented records for at least 1 butterfly species, with 27 localities hosting 5 or more species. This limited and sparse spatial coverage aligns with the overall data limitations noted above. Notably, the northern region of the country remains almost completely undocumented. Furthermore, the number of locality records per species shows significant deviation from uniformity, with most species recorded from fewer than 5 localities. This spatial imbalance is particularly pronounced for underrepresented families (compared to their species richness) such as Hesperiidae and Lycaenidae. These findings suggest that certain butterfly families are underrepresented not only in terms of species records (as previously noted) but also in their documented geographic distribution. We contend that the explanations proposed in the previous section, including insecurity, equally apply here. Exploring new localities for butterflies, particularly in the northern region or in localities where high floristic richness has recently been documented (César et al. 2024, César, pers. comm. 2025), is promising and would clearly be beneficial. Still, the current security situation hinders such efforts and represents a broader challenge to the country’s development and to international research collaborations (e.g., Zoma 2024). Even if these issues fall outside the main scope of this article, we believe acknowledging them provides a more accurate and nuanced understanding of the system studied and its complex challenges, where butterfly research are not the main concern.

Conclusion

This study greatly advances the understanding of butterfly diversity in BF, and secondarily, in West Africa. By compiling and synthesizing data from a large array of sources, we have expanded the country’s known butterfly checklist by an impressive 40%, documenting 220-225 species, including 47 new country records. This comprehensive update addresses major knowledge gaps and establishes a valuable baseline for future research.

However, these advancements also highlight critical limitations. Several butterfly families remain severely understudied and research efforts are both limited and geographically uneven. These data gaps are further exacerbated by challenges related to data accessibility, taxonomic standardization and regional sociopolitical instability. The discovery of numerous new country records—some occurring far from previously known distributions—also suggests the possible presence of undescribed taxa.

Addressing these knowledge gaps is essential. Future research should prioritize long-term, collaborative monitoring programs that combine traditional field surveys with citizen science. Special attention must be given to underrepresented taxa and poorly explored regions. Strengthening local taxonomic expertise and investing in sustainable data management systems will be key to supporting biodiversity knowledge and conservation in the region.
