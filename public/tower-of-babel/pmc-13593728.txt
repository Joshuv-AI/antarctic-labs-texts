Orbitozygomatic-transsylvian approach to the insula: microsurgical anatomy and endoscopic correlation using the fiber-separation technique
Authors: Mustafa Eren Yuncu, Abdullah Bozoklar, Hayrettin Fidan, Erman Guler, Berra Bilgin, Ali Karadag, Necmettin Tanriover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsular surgery remains technically demanding because of the region’s deep location and intimate relationships with the middle cerebral artery complex and surrounding white matter pathways. Safe resection requires precise understanding of vascular landmarks and subcortical functional boundaries.MethodsEight formalin-fixed adult cadaveric heads (16 hemispheres) underwent bilateral two-piece orbitozygomatic (OZ) transsylvian (TS) dissections. Endoscopic assistance (0° and 30°) was used selectively to enhance visualization of deep and anatomically concealed regions. Following cortical exposure, approach-oriented subinsular fiber separation and dedicated hemispheric Klingler fiber dissections were performed to define operative surgical boundaries and characterize the three-dimensional organization of the peri-insular white matter.ResultsThe OZ–TS corridor provided wide anterior and anteroinferior exposure of the insula, allowing early identification of the M2 trunks, M3 opercular branches, and lenticulostriate arteries, thereby clarifying the medial vascular limit. Endoscopic assistance provided complementary visualization of the limen insulae, anterior perforated substance, and deep perforators while maintaining the established operative corridor. Layered subinsular dissection consistently revealed the extreme capsule, claustrum, external capsule with its uncinate fasciculus and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), followed by the putamen and internal capsule. The IFOF formed a critical medial boundary in anterior resections, whereas the posterior limb of the internal capsule and optic radiation defined posteromedial and posterior functional limits.ConclusionEndoscope-assisted OZ–TS exposure combined with a structured fiber-separation strategy provides an integrated anatomical perspective of the insular region. Early identification of vascular landmarks and systematic progression along intrinsic white matter planes facilitate anatomical definition of subcortical surgical boundaries.Keywords: Endoscopic-assisted microsurgery, Fiber-separation technique, Subinsular white matter, Transsylvian approach

Introduction

The insula is one of the most anatomically complex and surgically demanding regions of the cerebrum. Situated deep within the Sylvian fissure beneath the opercula, it forms a convergence point of cortical, subcortical, vascular, and white matter structures [1–3]. This convergence of eloquent structures defines both the potential and the limitations of insular surgery.

Morphologically, the insula is divided by the central insular sulcus into anterior short gyri and posterior long gyri, and it is circumscribed by the anterior, superior, and inferior limiting sulci [1, 4–6]. The limen insulae marks the anteroinferior transition toward the anterior perforated substance and represents a critical surgical landmark [1, 4, 7]. From a surgical perspective, the insula is characterized not only by its cortical anatomy but also by its close relationship with the middle cerebral artery (MCA) complex and surrounding white matter tracts [1, 5]. The M2 segments course directly over the insular surface, while lenticulostriate arteries (LSAs) arising from M1 penetrate medially toward the basal ganglia and internal capsule [8, 9]. These perforators define the medial vascular limit of resection, and even minor injury may result in irreversible motor deficits. Equally important are the layered white matter tracts that encircle the insula. Laterally, short association fibers of the extreme capsule connect the insula to the opercula [5, 10]. Medially, the external capsule contains the uncinate fasciculus (UF) and the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), the latter forming a critical functional boundary during resection [4, 5, 7]. The superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) – arcuate fasciculus (AF) complex surrounds the insula superiorly, whereas posteromedially the internal capsule (including the corticospinal tract (CST)) abuts the posterior insular cortex [1, 5]. These layered relationships underscore the need for a fiber-separation strategy instead of a solely topographic dissection.

Historically, insular lesions were considered high-risk or inoperable. Contemporary microsurgical principles, however, emphasize maximal safe resection guided by detailed anatomical knowledge and functional preservation. Two principal corridors are used to access the insula: the transsylvian (TS) and transcortical (TC) approaches [11–13]. The TS approach preserves the opercular cortex but demands precise sylvian fissure splitting under meticulous arachnoid dissection of the MCA branches [11, 12]. The TC approach provides a more direct trajectory but necessitates passage through the opercular cortex, with potential risk to eloquent regions [2, 14, 15]. Optimal approach selection depends on lesion location, hemispheric dominance, and anticipated subcortical involvement. To expand the TS working corridor and reduce brain retraction, skull base modifications such as the orbitozygomatic (OZ) craniotomy have been adopted [16–18]. By lowering the surgical trajectory and widening the angle of attack, the OZ approach improves visualization of deep perisylvian structures [16, 17]. In parallel, endoscopic assistance may complement microscopic inspection by providing angled visualization of anatomically concealed regions, particularly around the limen insulae and anterior perforated substance.

While each of these anatomical concepts has been described individually, their integration into a unified microsurgical, endoscopic, and fiber-separation framework has not been systematically illustrated. Therefore, in this cadaveric study, we combine endoscope-assisted OZ–TS dissection with systematic fiber-separation analysis to correlate vascular landmarks, operative perspectives, and layered white matter anatomy within a single three-dimensional anatomical roadmap.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Eight adult formalin-fixed human heads (16 hemispheres) were used in this study. In four specimens, colored silicone was injected into the arterial and venous systems to enhance visualization of vascular structures. The remaining specimens were prepared according to the Klingler fiber dissection technique to facilitate delineation of peri-insular white matter pathways. This dual methodology enabled integrated analysis of microvascular anatomy and white matter architecture. All dissections were performed under an operating microscope at ×6–×40 magnification using standard microsurgical instruments. A 0° and 30° rigid endoscope (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) was used as an adjunct for angled visualization of deep operative corridors. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national standards for anatomical research. High-resolution images were obtained using a digital camera system and processed with high dynamic range imaging software.

Surgical and fiber dissection technique

The OZ-TS approach was performed on four heads (eight hemispheres) to simulate operative exposure of the insular region. Dissections were conducted in the supine position with approximately 30° of head rotation. Stepwise microsurgical dissection of the Sylvian fissure was performed under microscopic visualization, with selective endoscopic assistance to inspect deep regions. The number and distribution of insular perforating arteries were not systematically recorded because the objective of the study was qualitative anatomical characterization rather than quantitative morphometric vascular analysis.

To analyze the subinsular white matter in its operative context, an approach-oriented subinsular fiber separation was performed on the silicone-injected specimens. Following layer-by-layer subpial removal of the insular cortex, subcortical structures were sequentially exposed from lateral to medial while preserving the major vascular structures. Rather than a complete Klingler hemispheric fiber dissection, this in situ technique was designed to demonstrate the anatomical relationship between the surgical corridor, vascular landmarks, and sequential subinsular white matter layers under operative conditions.

In contrast, complete hemispheric fiber dissections following the Klingler technique were performed on dedicated fiber-dissection specimens to demonstrate the full three-dimensional organization of the peri-insular white matter independent of the operative corridor. White matter tracts were identified according to their topographic relationships, fiber orientation, and anatomical landmarks, allowing correlation between the operative anatomy demonstrated by the approach-oriented subinsular fiber separation and the comprehensive hemispheric fiber architecture.

Under microscopic visualization, sharp arachnoid dissection of the Sylvian fissure was initiated from the sphenoidal segment, splitting the fissure into superior and inferior compartments (Fig. 2A). Progressive opening of the anterior horizontal and ascending rami provided access to the anterior insular surface. Retraction of the frontal and temporal opercula exposed the frontobasal region, opticocarotid cistern, and internal carotid artery (ICA) bifurcation (Fig. 2C). The insular cortex was exposed within its limiting sulci. The central insular sulcus clearly divided the anterior short gyri from the posterior long gyri. The anterior and posterior insular points (AIP and PIP), formed by the junctions of the limiting sulci, were consistently identified (Fig. 2E). The limen insulae was observed as a critical anteroinferior transition zone between the insular cortex and anterior perforated substance (Fig. 2C, E). At the level of the limen insulae, the MCA bifurcation, early M2 branches, and multiple perforating arteries were encountered in close proximity. LSAs arising from the M1 segment were observed penetrating medially through the anterior perforated substance toward the deep perforating territory (Fig. 2A, C, E). Insular perforators originating predominantly from the inferior M2 trunk entered the cortex near the central insular sulcus and limen insulae. The superior M2 trunk typically coursed over the anterior short gyri, whereas the inferior trunk followed the posterior long gyri. Distally, the M2 trunks continued as M3 opercular branches supplying the frontal and temporal opercula (Fig. 2E). The PIP corresponded approximately to the junction of the temporal and parietal opercula at the superior termination of the Sylvian fissure and aligned with the posteromedial aspect of Heschl’s gyrus. This region marked the superior boundary of insular exposure and the approximate lateral projection of the optic radiation. Using the OZ transsylvian approach, the anterior two-thirds of the insula and adjacent deep structures were visualized with minimal cortical manipulation. In contrast, exposure of the extreme posterior insula remained limited due to the depth and angulation of the operative corridor.

Endoscope-assisted visualization

The AIP, insular gyri, limiting sulci, limen insulae, MCA bifurcation, proximal M2 branches, and LSAs identified during microscopic dissection were also visualized endoscopically (Fig. 2B, D). Advancement of the endoscope beneath the frontal operculum enabled inspection of the anterior perforated substance inferior to the limen insulae while maintaining the established operative corridor (Fig. 2D). Endoscopic inspection also demonstrated the anatomical relationship of the limen insulae to the MCA bifurcation, proximal M2 branches, and LSAs from an inferior-oblique perspective (Fig. 2D). The superior and inferior M2 trunks together with their insular perforating branches were identified during endoscopic inspection, followed distally by their continuation as M3 opercular branches (Fig. 2B, F). Endoscopic observation further demonstrated the optico-carotid and carotid-oculomotor corridors, as well as the adjacent CN II, CN III, anterior cerebral artery (ACA), anterior communicating artery (ACoA), posterior communicating artery (PCoA), anterior choroidal artery (AChA), and ipsilateral basal cistern structures (Fig. 2D). Inspection of the posterior insular region demonstrated the PIP, posterior long gyrus, and their relationship to Heschl’s gyrus beneath the opercular margin (Fig. 2F). The anatomical relationships identified during microscopic dissection were confirmed during endoscopic inspection of the corresponding operative field.

First, an approach-oriented subinsular fiber dissection was performed through the OZ–TS corridor on silicone-injected specimens while preserving the vascular anatomy (Figs. 3 and 4). Second, complete hemispheric fiber dissections were carried out using Klingler-prepared specimens to delineate the three-dimensional organization of the peri-insular white matter (Figs. 5 and 6).

Peri-insular association layers.

Peri-insular association layers.

Removal of the insular cortex exposed the extreme capsule as the most superficial subinsular white matter layer (Fig. 3A-C). This layer consisted of short association fibers interconnecting the insular gyri and forming U-fiber bridges toward the surrounding opercula at the level of the limiting sulci. Deep to the extreme capsule, the claustrum was identified as a thin vertical gray matter lamina separating the extreme and external capsules (Figs. 3D and E and 5D–G). Removal of the claustrum exposed the external capsule as a distinct longitudinal (anteroposterior) white matter layer medial to the claustrum (Figs. 3D–F and 6A–D). Compared to the extreme capsule, fibers of the external capsule demonstrated a more uniform longitudinal orientation. Within the external capsule, two major association pathways were identified: the UF and the IFOF (Figs. 3D–F, 5E–H and 6A–D). The UF occupied the ventral portion of the external capsule and followed a hook-shaped course around the limen insulae toward the temporal pole (Figs. 3D–F and 6A–H). The IFOF coursed immediately dorsal to the UF, extending from the posterior temporal and occipital regions toward the frontal lobe along the deep insular plane (Figs. 5E and F and 6C and D). At the insular level, the IFOF formed a consistent lateral boundary to the putamen. Upon removal of IFOF fibers, putaminal gray matter and small perforating arteries became immediately visible medially (Fig. 3D–F).

2.Basal ganglia and projection fibers.

Basal ganglia and projection fibers.

Medial to the external capsule, the putamen was exposed as the lateral component of the lentiform nucleus (Figs. 3E and F, 4A, 5F and G and 6C and D). Windows created within the external capsule demonstrated the reddish-gray putaminal tissue beneath (Figs. 5F and 6B). Following removal of the putamen and globus pallidus, the internal capsule was identified as a compact superoinferior projection fiber bundle (Figs. 4A–C, 5H and 6E and F). Corona radiata fibers were seen to converge superiorly and condense at the level of the lentiform nucleus to form the internal capsule (Fig. 6E–H). The anterior commissure (AC) was visualized anteroinferior to the internal capsule, with the nucleus accumbens located lateral to it (Figs. 5H and 6E–H).

3.Dorsal association system, optic radiation and ventricular anatomy.

Dorsal association system, optic radiation and ventricular anatomy.

The SLF and AF formed a C-shaped complex arching around the insula (Figs. 5A and 6A). After removal of the opercula and extreme capsule, SLF fibers were clearly visible superior to the insular region (Fig. 5A, B). The AF occupied the superior-lateral margin of the insula and extended between the frontal operculum and temporal stem (Figs. 5A and D and 6A). The optic radiation was identified medial and posterior to the IFOF (Figs. 6C–G). Meyer’s loop, representing the anterior portion of the optic radiation, coursed along the roof and lateral wall of the temporal horn (Fig. 6F, G). These fibers were located medial to the UF–IFOF complex and lateral to the ventricular cavity. Opening of the temporal horn through a transinsular route (via the inferior limiting sulcus) demonstrated that the UF and IFOF were encountered first, whereas the optic radiation lay medial and posterior to these structures (Figs. 4A–F and 6F and G). Preservation of the tapetum permitted visualization of the thalamus, caudate nucleus, and choroid plexus within the lateral ventricle (Fig. 6G, H). The layered white matter organization surrounding the insula defined reproducible surgical boundaries in all specimens. These boundaries were consistently associated with specific anatomical landmarks and functional risks. Discussion

Insular surgery represents a convergence of cortical, vascular, and subcortical challenges. Although the cortical anatomy of the insula is well described, the true surgical complexity of this region lies in its intimate relationship with the MCA complex and the multilayered white matter architecture that surrounds it. This cadaveric study illustrates the anatomical relationships underlying endoscope-assisted OZ–TS exposure combined with a structured fiber-separation strategy for defining reproducible subcortical planes and functional boundaries. While previous anatomical studies have individually described TS approaches, OZ modifications, endoscopic visualization, or peri-insular white matter anatomy, the present study integrates these complementary perspectives within a single anatomical framework. Rather than proposing a new surgical approach, the present study correlates paired microscopic and endoscopic operative views with sequential fiber-separation dissection, linking surface vascular landmarks to the layered organization of the subinsular white matter. Accordingly, the principal contribution of the present study lies in integrating these complementary anatomical concepts rather than describing previously unrecognized white matter anatomy.

Orbitozygomatic modification and surgical corridor optimization

The OZ craniotomy was originally developed to expand the anterior and superior angle of attack to deep skull base structures [16, 19–21]. Quantitative anatomical studies have shown that the two-piece OZ technique allows greater removal of the orbital roof compared to the single-piece variant, thereby lowering the surgical trajectory and increasing the working angles [16, 22]. In the present anatomical study, the two-piece OZ modification was intentionally selected to provide broad anatomical exposure of the anterior insular region while simultaneously demonstrating the frontobasal neurovascular anatomy and its anatomical continuity with the limen insulae and anterior perforated substance within a single operative corridor. This corridor facilitated appreciation of these three-dimensional anatomical relationships rather than quantitative assessment of surgical exposure. Because no morphometric measurements were performed, quantitative comparisons with standard pterional approaches cannot be made and remain beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, consistent with previous morphometric analyses, exposure of the extreme posterior and posterosuperior insula remained limited due to the depth and angulation imposed by the parietal operculum [2, 11]. These anatomical constraints underscore that the OZ–TS approach is optimized for anterior and anteroinferior lesions [1, 11]. The purpose of selecting the OZ modification in the present study was not to advocate its routine use for all insular lesions. Standard or modified pterional and pretemporal TS approaches remain appropriate for many insular pathologies and continue to represent the most commonly used clinical corridors [1, 2, 11, 12]. Rather, the OZ modification was chosen as an anatomical model to maximize visualization of the anterior insular region, limen insulae, anterior perforated substance, and adjacent frontobasal neurovascular structures within a single operative exposure, thereby facilitating direct micro-endoscopic anatomical correlation [16–22]. The selection of the surgical approach in clinical practice should ultimately be individualized according to lesion location, surgical objectives, and surgeon preference [2, 11, 12]. Whether endoscope-assisted modified pterional or pretemporal approaches provide comparable surgical exposure for specific lesions requires dedicated comparative anatomical and clinical studies and was beyond the scope of the present investigation.

Endoscopic assistance: beyond an adjunct

One of the practical observations of this anatomical study is the complementary visualization provided by angled endoscopy during inspection of deep operative corridors. The endoscope did not merely enlarge the microscopic field; it altered the surgeon’s perception of hidden anatomical relationships. Regions that are obliquely oriented or concealed beneath opercular overhang—particularly the limen insulae and anterior perforated substance—were more clearly appreciated under 30° endoscopic inspection. Direct visualization of LSAs at their origin from the M1 segment may facilitate anatomical orientation during definition of the medial vascular boundary. LSAs define the medial vascular limit of insular resection, and even minimal injury may produce devastating internal capsule infarction [1, 8]. Under microscopic visualization, the origin of these perforators may be difficult to appreciate because of the viewing angle and their relationship to the overlying MCA branches. Endoscopic assistance enabled angled inspection beneath the frontal operculum while maintaining the established operative corridor, providing complementary visualization of small perforators and the regional vascular anatomy. Importantly, the endoscope also improved spatial understanding of the three-dimensional relationship between the MCA bifurcation, early M2 trunks, and the limen insulae. This enhanced perspective reinforces a principle central to fiber-separation surgery: vascular structures must be respected as fixed boundaries, and dissection should proceed along natural planes rather than across them.

An additional surgical consideration is the distinction between insular perforating arteries arising from the proximal M2 trunks and the lenticulostriate arteries originating from the M1 segment [1, 8, 9]. Although both vascular groups course toward the deep insular region, they differ in their origins and vascular territories [1, 8]. Intraoperatively, misidentification of proximal M2 perforators as lenticulostriate arteries may falsely suggest that the medial limit of safe resection has been reached, potentially leading to unnecessarily conservative tumor resection [8]. Careful recognition of the parent vessel together with the anatomical relationship of the limen insulae and anterior perforated substance may facilitate more accurate interpretation of the true medial vascular boundary [1, 8, 11]. Because the present study was designed as a qualitative anatomical investigation, the number and distribution of insular perforating arteries were not systematically analyzed and remain an important subject for future anatomical and morphometric studies.

Fiber-separation strategy and layered subinsular anatomy

The principal contribution of the fiber-separation component is not the description of individual peri-insular white matter layers, which have been comprehensively established in previous anatomical studies [5, 7, 10, 23, 24], but their integration into an operative sequence that correlates each anatomical layer with reproducible surgical boundaries encountered during the OZ–TS approach. Unlike conventional hemispheric Klingler dissections, the approach-oriented subinsular fiber separation performed in Figs. 3 and 4 preserves the operative orientation and vascular relationships, thereby allowing direct correlation between the surgical corridor, vascular landmarks, and sequential white matter planes encountered during insular exposure. From a fiber-separation perspective, this layered organization provides a stepwise surgical roadmap (insular cortex → extreme capsule → claustrum → external capsule [UF/IFOF] → putamen → internal capsule), allowing the surgeon to progress along intrinsic cleavage planes rather than relying solely on surface landmarks. Within this sequence, the IFOF represents a particularly important transition, serving as a reproducible functional and medial boundary before entry into the putamen and deeper projection systems [5, 7]. Respecting these anatomical planes may reduce inadvertent violation of eloquent subcortical pathways during progressive insular dissection.

The dorsal association system, consisting of the SLF–AF complex, arched superiorly around the insula. This C-shaped configuration reinforces the concept that superior extension of insular resection risks disruption of phonological and articulatory networks [1, 5]. In contrast, the posteromedial boundary was defined by the posterior limb of the internal capsule, containing the CST. In our dissections, the CST lay in close proximity to the posterior insular cortex, with only a thin basal ganglia interface separating them in some regions. This anatomical relationship explains the high incidence of motor deficits associated with overly medial or superior resection in the posterior insula [2, 5, 11]. The optic radiation, particularly Meyer’s loop, was identified medial and posterior to the UF–IFOF complex along the roof and lateral wall of the temporal horn. The transinsular trajectory via the inferior limiting sulcus encountered the UF and IFOF before reaching the ventricular cavity, while the optic radiation remained medial and posterior. This layered relationship supports clinical observations that a carefully executed transsylvian–transinsular approach may spare visual pathways when dissection respects the medial IFOF boundary [5, 7, 11]. Together, these findings validate the surgical relevance of Table 1, in which white matter tracts define distinct medial, anteroinferior, superior, and posteromedial limits. Each boundary corresponds not only to an anatomical landmark but also to a distinct functional risk profile.

Transsylvian versus transcortical considerations

The TS approach preserves the opercular cortex and utilizes the Sylvian fissure as a natural corridor but requires meticulous arachnoid dissection and careful handling of the MCA bifurcation and M2 branches [1, 11]. Extensive arachnoid dissection along the Sylvian fissure may increase the risk of vasospasm or damage to perforating arteries, particularly when normal vascular relationships are altered by tumor [1, 8]. In contrast, the TC route offers a shorter and more direct trajectory to the insular cortex, avoiding prolonged Sylvian fissure dissection. Nevertheless, it necessitates passage through the frontal, parietal, or temporal opercula, which may involve eloquent cortical regions and subcortical association fibers [2, 11].

Although the present study did not include transcortical dissections, the anatomical observations obtained through the OZ–TS corridor may be interpreted in the context of the established literature comparing TS and TC approaches. From this perspective, the sequence of white matter layers demonstrated during the present dissections provides an anatomical framework that may assist in understanding the different subcortical planes encountered through these operative corridors. Based on previous anatomical studies, the OZ–TS corridor may facilitate stepwise progression along the extreme capsule, external capsule, and IFOF before reaching the deep gray nuclei in anterior and anteroinferior lesions [5, 13, 16]. Conversely, previous anatomical and clinical studies have suggested that posterior-dorsal insular lesions may be more favorably approached through a tailored TC corridor because the TS trajectory becomes progressively deeper and narrower owing to the parietal operculum [2, 5, 11]. Because no TC dissections or quantitative comparisons were performed in the present study, these considerations should be regarded as an interpretation of the existing literature rather than conclusions derived directly from our anatomical dissections.

Regardless of the operative corridor selected, these anatomical observations should be interpreted together with awake language and motor mapping in dominant-hemisphere lesions, which remain the clinical standard for defining functional cortical and subcortical boundaries during insular surgery [2, 11, 12]. The present anatomical roadmap is therefore intended to complement, rather than replace, intraoperative functional mapping during surgical planning and insular resection.

Limitations

This study is anatomical and does not incorporate intraoperative functional mapping or tractography. Tumor infiltration may distort or infiltrate white matter tracts, obscuring the anatomical boundaries observed in cadaveric specimens. Additionally, endoscopic advantages demonstrated in cadaveric models require validation in clinical series to assess their impact on morbidity and extent of resection. Furthermore, no comparison between right- and left-sided dissections was performed because the objective of this study was to define reproducible microsurgical anatomy rather than hemispheric functional differences. Likewise, correlation of the present anatomical findings with representative operative cases and clinical outcomes would further strengthen their practical applicability and should be addressed in future clinical investigations.

Conclusion

By integrating paired microscopic–endoscopic anatomical correlation with systematic fiber-separation dissection, the present study provides a three-dimensional anatomical framework for understanding operative anatomy during insular surgery. Identification of vascular landmarks—particularly the LSAs—and stepwise progression along intrinsic white matter planes facilitate anatomical definition of the medial and subcortical boundaries encountered during insular exposure. Rather than relying solely on cortical landmarks, the present anatomical roadmap emphasizes the relationship between vascular landmarks and fiber-defined subcortical anatomy. Future morphometric and clinical studies will be necessary to validate the anatomical observations presented in this qualitative cadaveric investigation.
