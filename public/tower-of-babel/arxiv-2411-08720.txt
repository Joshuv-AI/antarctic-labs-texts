Hunter Ng

Abstract
I show that outsourced cryptocurrency wash trading is shaped by the objective assigned to the outside firm. When hired to manufacture volume, wash traders buy and sell nearly equal amounts, repeatedly turning over the same inventory. When also tasked with selling issuer holdings, trading becomes markedly more one-sided, with sales exceeding purchases. Using documented enforcement cases and transaction records, I trace these differences in net selling, two-way trading, and inventory replenishment across controlled wallets. The results show that similar reported trading volume can mask fundamentally different economic activity as contractual objectives shape whether manipulation recycles inventory or liquidates issuer holdings.

cases provide three advantages over existing settings. First, unlike most existing
studies, which infer wash trading from statistical patterns in observed transactions,
these cases provide documentary evidence of wash trading. Such evidence has
traditionally been difficult to obtain because wash trading can be difficult to
distinguish from legitimate trading based on transaction data alone.

Using the trading records of these documented wash-trading cases and their documented contracts, I find that the objectives assigned to the outside firm are reflected in how it trades. When the firm is hired mainly to generate volume, purchases and sales closely offset one another, allowing substantial trading activity to be generated with very little net selling. When the firm also receives an issuer-sales instruction, sales rise relative to purchases and net selling accounts for a larger share of gross activity. Trading composition and net selling differ across the two engagements in a manner consistent with their contractual objectives.

I first compare total purchases and sales over each trading episode. In the volume-oriented arrangement, purchases and sales are almost equal, and nearly all observed activity takes the form of two-way trading. In the arrangement that also includes an issuer-sales instruction, sales exceed purchases by substantially more. The volume-oriented firm consequently generates far more trading activity for each token sold on net. These patterns are consistent with the model’s prediction when the issuer-sales target binds and inventory and market-absorption constraints are slack.

I next examine whether this trading composition persists over shorter horizons. A firm may generate balanced purchases and sales over an entire episode through trading that is highly directional within shorter intervals. I therefore divide the CLS trading history into shorter intervals and measure how closely purchases and sales offset within each interval. The volume-oriented arrangement remains highly balanced at six-hour and daily horizons, and its observed balance exceeds the conditional upper bound implied by the reported HYDRO company-account purchases and sales.

Finally, I examine how this balance is generated across the wallets controlled by the outside firm. Individual wallets exhibit substantial directional trading, while these positions offset extensively once the wallets are aggregated under common control. I also find that most tokens sold by the volume-oriented firm are replenished through purchases in the controlled wallet cluster within a day. The trading network therefore generates high activity through offsetting purchases and sales across controlled accounts.

Taken together, these results show differences in trading composition across the two contractual objectives. The volume-generation assignment exhibits balanced, repetitive turnover, while the issuer-sales assignment exhibits substantially more net selling. The key implication is that the same reported trading volume can represent very different economic activity depending on the objective given to the wash trader.

II. Institutional Details

Wash trading in cryptocurrency markets is often studied using indirect evidence. Researchers typically infer manipulation from statistical irregularities in publicly observed transactions, including unusual trade-size distributions, deviations from Benford’s law, abnormal volume patterns, and structural breaks in trading activity. These approaches can identify patterns consistent with wash trading, but these patterns alone cannot conclusively establish that particular
transactions were wash trading or identify the parties controlling the accounts involved.
Gandal et al. (2018)
and
Aloosh and Li (2024)
provide a rare exception using leaked internal records from Mt. Gox that identify the traders on both sides of each transaction. The Mt. Gox data were unusual precisely because the exchange’s internal transaction records became public following its collapse, allowing wash trading to be identified directly rather than inferred from trading patterns. The SEC and DOJ enforcement actions announced between 2022 and 2024 provide a new set of independently documented cases. Although there could be many other instances of undocumented wash trading, these cases provide an authoritative basis for identifying the parties involved and the underlying contracts.

Cong et al. (2023)
examine trading records from 29 centralized
cryptocurrency exchanges and develop statistical tests for identifying
artificial trading. They find substantial abnormalities in the trading records
of unregulated exchanges and estimate that wash trading accounts for more than
70 percent of reported volume on these exchanges, on average. They further
show that fabricated volume improves exchange rankings and can temporarily
affect prices. The authors also report that their research was consulted by
the FBI and other U.S. authorities to assist federal investigations

(
Cong et al., 2023
)
.

Lehar and Parlour (2025)
examine whether decentralized exchange can perform
the economic functions of a conventional market. Combining a model of
liquidity provision with transaction-level evidence from Uniswap, they
document the absence of persistent arbitrage opportunities and identify
conditions under which automated market making outperforms a limit order
market. Their findings concern the efficiency of exchange, which is
distinct from whether observed trading reflects independent investor
demand. A market can execute transactions efficiently even when some
participants trade specifically to create the appearance of activity.
Indeed, SEC enforcement records describe arrangements in which token
promoters hire specialized firms to manufacture trading volume

(
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2024b
)
. In such arrangements, volume could also be determined by outsourced wash traders. This leaves
a gap in the literature in understanding how different objectives are implemented within outsourced wash trading.

Amiram et al. (2025)
study the incentives of cryptocurrency
exchanges to inflate trading volume. They find that greater competition among
exchanges is associated with greater volume inflation. Exchanges receive
short-run benefits from inflated activity but subsequently incur reputational
costs. Together with
Cong et al. (2023)
, these findings suggest that
manufactured trading volume should be viewed as an economic objective rather
than simply as a by-product of other manipulative activity.

Aloosh and Li (2024)
use internal transaction records from Mt. Gox
to identify more than 115,000 trades in which the same trader appears on
both sides. They use these trades to evaluate statistical methods for
detecting wash trading. They find support for tests based on Benford’s
law, trade-size clustering, lognormal distributions, and structural
breaks, whereas tests based on power-law tails can give misleading
conclusions. Their own index, based on unusually high trade counts and
small median trade sizes, performs as well as, or better than, the
alternative methods they compare for identifying short periods containing
wash trades. Their findings show the value of direct evidence for
evaluating statistical detection methods. Similar to their setting, this paper uses documented cases of wash trading and their documented wash-trading arrangements.

III. Model

I first review the available documented contractual arrangements for
outsourced wash trading in Supplemental Appendix Table B1. The most common structure combines a periodic fixed
service fee with trading resources supplied separately by the token issuer.
These resources typically include the issuer’s tokens and a counterasset such
as ETH, BTC, or USDT. The issuer also specifies the objectives of the
engagement, either generating trading volume, maintaining liquidity, or
selling issuer inventory. Thus, compensation, trading resources, and trading
objectives are separate components of the arrangement. This structure
motivates the model below and is consistent with models in which
agents are evaluated on multiple dimensions of performance

(
Feltham and Xie, 1994
;
Budde, 2007
)
.

I consider a target-based execution problem in which a sales requirement can
be met by changing either net selling or the amount of offsetting trading.
The token issuer hires a specialized market maker for a fixed fee
F
.
Let
\bar{V}>0
denote the required level of gross trading activity and

\bar{S}\geq 0
a minimum quantity of gross sales. Both targets are expressed
in token units over the assignment period. A volume-oriented benchmark
has no additional sales requirement,
\bar{S}=0
.
3
3

3

The gross-sales
constraint is a theoretical specification; HYDRO’s documented sales instruction
does not define whether repurchases are deducted. Both model targets use token
units, whereas CLS’s operating plan states dollar volume. An absence of a
documented sales requirement does not imply that sales were prohibited.

Let
B\geq 0
and
S\geq 0
denote the market maker’s aggregate purchases and
sales over the assignment period. Gross trading activity is

V=B+S,

(1)

and net selling is

L=S-B.

(2)

A given level of trading activity can therefore be generated with different
combinations of purchases and sales. When purchases and sales are equal,

L=0
. When sales exceed purchases,
L>0
and trading reduces the market
maker’s token position on net. The distinction between
S
and
L
matters:
repurchases can offset gross sales.

The outsourced wash trader, henceforth the market maker, incurs cost

C(V,L)=\frac{c_{V}}{2}V^{2}+\frac{c_{L}}{2}L^{2},\qquad c_{V},c_{L}>0.

(3)

The first term captures the costs of generating trading activity. The second
captures the additional cost of net selling. Repeated offsetting trades can
generate substantial volume without requiring the market to absorb a
persistent increase in token supply, whereas net sales must ultimately be
absorbed by other market participants. I use the quadratic specification to
capture increasing marginal execution costs. A price-impact interpretation
is provided in the Appendix.

Conditional on participation and compliance with the targets, the market maker chooses
V
and

L
to minimize

C(V,L)

(4)

subject to

V\geq\bar{V},\qquad\frac{V+L}{2}\geq\bar{S},\qquad 0\leq L\leq V.

(5)

The sales constraint follows from
S=(V+L)/2
. The last condition follows
from nonnegative purchases and the focus on net selling. Targets are taken
as given performance requirements. The benchmark assumes sufficient trading
capital and token inventory. I consider inventory and market-absorption
constraints in the Appendix.

Proposition 1.

Define
r=\min\{1,c_{V}/c_{L}\}
. Conditional on feasibility and participation,
the unique least-cost execution is

V^{*}=\max\left\{\bar{V},\frac{2\bar{S}}{1+r}\right\},\qquad L^{*}=\max\{0,2\bar{S}-V^{*}\}.

(6)

There are three regimes. If
\bar{S}\leq\bar{V}/2
, the volume requirement
already generates sufficient sales through balanced trading:

V^{*}=\bar{V}
and
L^{*}=0
. If

\bar{V}/2<\bar{S}\leq(1+r)\bar{V}/2
, gross activity remains at its target
and additional required sales are met by increasing net selling:

V^{*}=\bar{V}
and
L^{*}=2\bar{S}-\bar{V}
. Above this threshold, the market
maker expands gross activity, with
L^{*}/V^{*}=r
.

Purchases and sales satisfy

B^{*}=\frac{V^{*}-L^{*}}{2},\qquad S^{*}=\frac{V^{*}+L^{*}}{2}.

(7)

In the intermediate regime, purchases fall and sales rise by equal amounts
while gross activity remains unchanged. In the high-sales regime, net
selling is costly enough to justify additional turnover when
c_{L}>c_{V}
.
Both purchases and sales then increase with the sales requirement. If

c_{L}\leq c_{V}
, the high-sales solution instead consists entirely of sales.

Let
a=\bar{S}/\bar{V}
. The directional and two-way balanced shares are

D^{*}=\frac{L^{*}}{V^{*}}=\min\{r,\max(0,2a-1)\},\qquad R^{*}=1-D^{*}.

(8)

For
L^{*}>0
, gross activity per unit of net selling is

\frac{V^{*}}{L^{*}}=\frac{1}{D^{*}}.

(9)

Holding costs and the volume target fixed, a higher sales requirement weakly
increases directional trading until its share reaches
r
. A higher cost of
net selling relative to turnover reduces this limiting share and favors
more offsetting trading. A sales requirement can therefore coexist with
high two-way balance; it need not produce substantial net liquidation.
These predictions are conditional on additional inventory and
market-absorption constraints being slack. The proofs and extensions are
provided in the Appendix.

IV. Data

A. Enforcement Records

I first examine publicly available SEC and DOJ enforcement records and
collect blockchain transaction data through publicly available application
programming interfaces (APIs). The enforcement records identify the parties
involved, the period of manipulation, and the underlying trading
arrangements. The blockchain data provide a record of on-chain transactions,
including transaction timestamps, wallet addresses, and token quantities.
Because these sources are publicly recorded and can be independently
verified, the data are comprehensive and reliable. More
importantly, the enforcement records document what the outside market
maker was hired to accomplish, allowing me to identify contractual
objectives independently of the trading patterns that I subsequently
examine.

I begin with the case inventory in Supplemental Appendix Table B1, drawn from enforcement records and leaked internal records. The inventory distinguishes documented trading cases from related proposed engagements and preserves the evidentiary status of each record. I classify these cases according to whether the manipulation was conducted internally or outsourced and, for outsourced arrangements, according to the trading objectives documented in the underlying records. I then apply a common data requirement to the outsourced cases: the trading objective must be documented independently of the trading outcomes, and purchase and sale quantities must be observable for an explicitly reported trading scope associated with the outside firm’s engagement. These requirements leave two arrangements with different documented objectives, NexFundAI–CLS Global and HYDRO–Moonwalkers, for the main comparison.

For the NexFundAI–CLS Global case, the NexFundAI project team contracted CLS Global to generate artificial trading volume for a monthly fee of US$4,000, payable in Tether. NexFundAI was created at the FBI’s direction, and CLS was unaware of the undercover investigation. CLS’s trading therefore reflects its execution of an assignment it believed came from a token project. CLS’s operating plan estimated US$32,000 of trading volume per 24 hours using 30 wallets. CLS conducted the wash trading on Uniswap from August 23 to September 18, 2024, providing reconstructed purchases and sales over 27 active days
(
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2024a
)
.

C. Measures

I adapt measures of trading activity and order imbalance from

Chordia et al. (2002)
and their more recent applications in equity and
cryptocurrency markets

(
Makarov and Schoar, 2020
;
Boehmer et al., 2021
;
Brugler and Comerton-Forde, 2025
;
Liu et al., 2026
)
.
I separate gross trading activity from net selling because the two capture
different dimensions of the trading arrangement. Gross activity captures how
much trading is generated, while net selling captures the extent to which
sales exceed purchases. This distinction is central to my setting because
volume generation can be achieved through offsetting purchases and sales,
whereas net liquidation requires sales to exceed purchases.

Let
B_{t}
and
S_{t}
denote token purchases and sales within the reported
trading scope in interval
t
. The common comparison uses episode totals.
For the CLS interval analysis, the primary partition uses UTC calendar days,
with one-hour and six-hour intervals reported as robustness checks.

First, I measure gross trading activity as

G_{t}=B_{t}+S_{t}.

(10)

Trading volume is commonly used to measure the amount of trading activity

(
Chordia et al., 2002
;
Brugler and Comerton-Forde, 2025
)
. I measure activity
in token quantities because the contractual objectives in my setting concern
both trading activity and sales of issuer tokens, making token units directly
comparable across purchases and sales within an arrangement. This gross
token-flow measure is distinct from the dollar volume in CLS’s operating plan.

Second, I measure net selling as

N_{t}=S_{t}-B_{t}.

(11)

This follows the signed-volume and order-flow literature, which uses the
difference between purchases and sales to measure the direction of trading

(
Makarov and Schoar, 2020
;
Liu et al., 2026
)
. I orient the measure
so that positive values indicate net selling after purchases offset sales,
measuring the directional outcome of the issuer-sales assignment.

To compare net selling across intervals with different levels of trading
activity, I scale it by gross trading activity:

D_{t}=\frac{S_{t}-B_{t}}{S_{t}+B_{t}}.

(12)

Scaling buy–sell imbalance by total trading volume is common in recent
market-microstructure research

(
Boehmer et al., 2021
;
Chen et al., 2025
)
.
This normalization allows me to compare the direction of trading across
intervals with substantially different amounts of activity.

Finally, I express the same normalized imbalance as a measure of two-way
balance:

R_{t}=\frac{2\min(B_{t},S_{t})}{B_{t}+S_{t}}=1-|D_{t}|.

(13)

Recent work commonly uses absolute order imbalance to measure the degree of
directional trading
(
Brugler and Comerton-Forde, 2025
)
. I express its
complement as two-way balance because my empirical prediction concerns the
extent to which purchases and sales offset one another. Higher values of

R_{t}
therefore indicate more balanced two-way trading. Both
D_{t}
and

R_{t}
are defined only when
G_{t}>0
. Zero-activity intervals are omitted
from unweighted interval averages and contribute zero to gross-weighted totals.

For episode totals
B=\sum_{t}B_{t}
,
S=\sum_{t}S_{t}
,
G=B+S
, and
N=S-B
,
I report
N/G
,
R_{\mathrm{episode}}=1-|N|/G
, and
G/|N|
. The first
two extend the normalized order-imbalance measures to the full episode.
The final measure expresses gross trading activity per unit of net selling,
which maps directly into the model’s distinction between volume generation
and net liquidation.

V. Findings

A. Predicted Trading Composition

The model separates a requirement for gross sales from net liquidation.
A volume target already generates gross sales when purchases and sales
offset, so an additional sales instruction does not necessarily produce
a directional imbalance. Once the sales requirement binds, the market
maker can satisfy it through more net selling, more gross turnover, or
both. Their relative execution costs determine the composition.

B. Trading under the Two Contractual Objectives

I first compare how purchases and sales are combined over the full trading
episode. Because the two arrangements involve different tokens and very
different amounts of trading, the informative comparison is the composition
of each case’s own gross activity. I separate gross activity into the portion
that can be matched by purchases and sales and the portion that represents
net directional trading:

G=B+S=2\min(B,S)+|S-B|.

(14)

The first component is two-way balanced activity and the second is directional
imbalance. Dividing each component by gross activity puts the two arrangements
on a common scale.

The difference between the two arrangements is large. CLS purchases
469.156 billion NexFundAI tokens and sells 469.795 billion. As a result,
99.932 percent of its gross activity is two-way balanced and net selling
accounts for only 0.068 percent. The cluster generates 1,470.66 units of
gross activity for each unit of net selling.

The reported HYDRO quantities show substantially more directional trading.
Purchases total 3.141 billion HYDRO and sales total 3.618 billion. Net selling
therefore accounts for 7.056 percent of gross activity and the two-way balanced
share is 92.944 percent. HYDRO generates 14.17 units of gross activity for
each unit of net selling.

These patterns are consistent with the contractual objectives. The
volume-oriented arrangement generates almost entirely offsetting turnover,
whereas the arrangement with an issuer-sales instruction directs a much
larger share of trading toward net sales. Relative to gross activity, the
net-selling share in HYDRO is approximately 104 times that of the CLS cluster.

C. Two-Way Balance across Trading Intervals

The episode-level comparison could conceal substantial directional trading
over shorter periods. Purchases made early in an episode could offset sales
made much later, making the full episode appear balanced even when trading is
strongly one-sided from hour to hour or day to day. I therefore examine how
closely purchases and sales offset within shorter trading intervals.

For any partition of the episode, gross-weighted two-way balance satisfies

R_{w}=\frac{\sum_{t:G_{t}>0}G_{t}R_{t}}{\sum_{t}G_{t}}=1-\frac{\sum_{t}|N_{t}|}{G}\leq 1-\frac{|\sum_{t}N_{t}|}{G}=R_{\mathrm{episode}}.

(15)

The episode-level balance therefore provides an upper bound on the balance
that can be observed over shorter intervals. For HYDRO, the reported aggregate
purchases and sales conditionally imply an upper bound of 92.9436 percent for every
partition of the same trades.

97.57 percent over daily intervals. Both exceed the conditional upper bound implied
by the reported HYDRO company-account purchases and sales. Shifting the daily boundary by
12 hours produces essentially the same result, with balance of 97.59 percent.

purchases and sales accumulate over several hours. The near-perfect balance

short horizons.

D. Common Control and Sale Replenishment

For CLS, I treat the controlled wallet cluster as the relevant trading actor.
This matters because an outsourced wash trader can distribute purchases and
sales across many controlled wallets. Looking at wallets separately can make
coordinated two-way trading appear directional even when the firm’s aggregate
position changes very little.

Figure
4
shows that this distinction is economically large. Across
individual CLS wallets, gross-weighted absolute imbalance is 20.11 percent.
After aggregating the same wallets under common control, absolute imbalance
falls to 0.068 percent. Purchases in some controlled wallets therefore offset
sales made through others.

I next examine how quickly sold quantities are matched to subsequent
purchases. This measures the timing of offsetting execution flows within
the controlled cluster. Let
q_{j}
denote the quantity of sale
j
and
let
m_{j}(h)
denote the quantity matched to subsequent purchases within
horizon
h
. The quantity-weighted replenishment rate is

Q(h)=\frac{\sum_{j}m_{j}(h)}{\sum_{j}q_{j}},\qquad 0\leq m_{j}(h)\leq q_{j}.

(16)

Under the seven-day first-in-first-out allocation, CLS cluster purchases
match 76.03 percent of sold quantity within six hours, 98.86 percent within
24 hours, and 99.25 percent within seven days. Restricting matching to the
wallet making the original sale yields 3.55, 16.93, and 66.13 percent.
Allowing purchases across controlled wallets therefore produces substantially
more matched replenishment. Seven days is the maximum follow-up horizon.
Although the CLS trading episode ends on September 18, complete swap-event
coverage of the original NexFundAI pool extends through October 18, 2024,
providing complete seven-day follow-up for all 366 sales within that pool.

Fixed seven-day FIFO assigns purchases to the oldest outstanding sales.
Horizon-specific expiry instead removes sales older than each horizon before
allocating purchases, allowing purchases to match more recent sales. These
rules yield 0.33 versus 49.09 percent at one hour and 76.03 versus 93.85
percent at six hours. At 24 hours they yield 98.86 and 99.25 percent.
The daily replenishment finding is therefore stable across the two rules,
while the shorter-horizon estimates depend substantially on the allocation.

The wallet and replenishment results show how the volume-oriented arrangement
generates its trading activity. Individual wallets take directional positions,
but these trading flows offset across the controlled cluster, and subsequent
purchases match nearly all sold quantity within a day under both cluster rules.
This pattern is consistent with repeated turnover supported by replenishment.

VI. Conclusion

I show that trading composition differs across outside-firm assignments
with different contractual objectives. When the assignment is primarily to generate
trading volume, purchases and sales are highly balanced and sold quantities
are rapidly replenished through purchases across controlled accounts. When the
assignment also includes an issuer-sales instruction, trading contains a
substantially larger net-selling component.

The contribution of this paper is to show that outsourced wash trading cannot
be characterized by reported volume alone. Existing research has largely
focused on identifying artificial trading and measuring its prevalence. I show
that gross trading activity and net liquidation are distinct dimensions of
manipulation and that their observed composition differs across contractual
objectives. The same amount of reported activity can
therefore represent extensive recycling of controlled inventory or substantially
more net selling of issuer tokens.

Taken together, these results show that outsourced wash trading varies across
assignments with different documented objectives. Once manipulation is delegated to a specialized outside
firm, contractual objectives and relative execution costs jointly determine
least-cost execution in the model. A binding issuer-sales target can be met through
additional net selling or additional gross turnover. Relative execution costs
govern this choice when inventory and market-absorption constraints are slack. The contract therefore becomes an important part of the
economic organization of manipulation, linking the issuer’s objective to the
transaction patterns ultimately observed in the market.

1. Model Derivations

A. Execution Problem

The token issuer specifies an assignment
(F,\bar{V},\bar{S})
, where
F
is
the fixed service fee,
\bar{V}>0
is the required gross trading activity,
and
\bar{S}\geq 0
is a minimum gross-sales quantity.
Let aggregate purchases and sales be
B
and
S
. Define

V=B+S,\qquad L=S-B.

(17)

Because the analysis focuses on net selling,
L\geq 0
. Inverting these
relations gives

B=\frac{V-L}{2},\qquad S=\frac{V+L}{2}.

(18)

Nonnegative purchases require
L\leq V
.

Conditional on accepting the assignment, the market maker solves

\min_{V,L}\left\{\frac{c_{V}}{2}V^{2}+\frac{c_{L}}{2}L^{2}\right\}

(19)

subject to

V\geq\bar{V},\qquad V+L\geq 2\bar{S},\qquad 0\leq L\leq V.

(20)

Strict convexity gives a unique optimum. The fixed fee affects participation,
but does not affect the least-cost choice conditional on acceptance and
enforcement of the targets.

If
\bar{S}\leq\bar{V}/2
,
(V,L)=(\bar{V},0)
satisfies the sales
requirement and minimizes both costly quantities. Otherwise the sales
constraint binds. Substituting
V=2\bar{S}-L
reduces the problem to

\min_{0\leq L\leq\min\{\bar{S},\,2\bar{S}-\bar{V}\}}\left\{\frac{c_{V}}{2}(2\bar{S}-L)^{2}+\frac{c_{L}}{2}L^{2}\right\}.

(21)

The unconstrained minimizer is
2c_{V}\bar{S}/(c_{V}+c_{L})
. Projecting it onto
the feasible interval and defining
r=\min\{1,c_{V}/c_{L}\}
yields

V^{*}=\max\left\{\bar{V},\frac{2\bar{S}}{1+r}\right\},\qquad L^{*}=\max\{0,2\bar{S}-V^{*}\}.

(22)

Equivalently,

(V^{*},L^{*})=\begin{cases}(\bar{V},0),&0\leq\bar{S}\leq\bar{V}/2,\\
(\bar{V},2\bar{S}-\bar{V}),&\bar{V}/2<\bar{S}\leq(1+r)\bar{V}/2,\\
\left(2\bar{S}/(1+r),\,2r\bar{S}/(1+r)\right),&\bar{S}>(1+r)\bar{V}/2.\end{cases}

(23)

In the intermediate regime,

B^{*}=\bar{V}-\bar{S},\qquad S^{*}=\bar{S}.

(24)

Hence
\partial B^{*}/\partial\bar{S}=-1
and

\partial S^{*}/\partial\bar{S}=1
, holding
\bar{V}
fixed.
In the high-sales regime,

B^{*}=\frac{1-r}{1+r}\bar{S},\qquad S^{*}=\bar{S}.

(25)

Thus additional sales are met with additional purchases when
r<1
,
whereas
r=1
gives pure selling.

B. Trading Composition

For
L\geq 0
, the gross quantity that can be matched by opposite-direction
trading is
2B=V-L
. The two-way balanced share is

R=\frac{2B}{V}=1-\frac{L}{V}.

(26)

Let
a=\bar{S}/\bar{V}
. At the optimum,

D^{*}=\frac{L^{*}}{V^{*}}=\min\{r,\max(0,2a-1)\},\qquad R^{*}=1-D^{*}.

(27)

Directional share is zero in the balanced regime, increases at rate

2/\bar{V}
with
\bar{S}
in the intermediate regime, and is constant
at
r
in the high-sales regime. Two-way balance has the opposite
comparative statics. For
L^{*}>0
,

\frac{V^{*}}{L^{*}}=\frac{1}{D^{*}},

(28)

which decreases in the intermediate regime and equals
1/r
in the
high-sales regime. At
L^{*}=0
this ratio is undefined.

When
c_{L}>c_{V}
, the high-sales solution is

V^{*}=\frac{2c_{L}}{c_{V}+c_{L}}\bar{S},\qquad L^{*}=\frac{2c_{V}}{c_{V}+c_{L}}\bar{S}.

(29)

Within this regime, a higher
c_{L}
increases gross activity and decreases
net selling. More generally, increasing
c_{L}
weakly reduces
D^{*}
and
weakly increases
R^{*}
. These results allow gross sales to rise while
directional share remains small. Proposition 1 follows.

C. Why Net Selling Is Costly

The quadratic net-selling cost can be given a simple market-absorption
interpretation. Suppose the residual price available when placing tokens
with the outside market is

p(x)=p_{0}-\lambda x,\qquad\lambda>0,

(30)

where
x
is cumulative net quantity sold. Selling
L
tokens net generates

\int_{0}^{L}p(x)\,dx=p_{0}L-\frac{\lambda}{2}L^{2}.

(31)

Relative to executing all net sales at
p_{0}
, the loss is

p_{0}L-\int_{0}^{L}p(x)\,dx=\frac{\lambda}{2}L^{2}.

(32)

This motivates
c_{L}
as a reduced-form market-absorption cost; the costs of gross
turnover are captured separately by
c_{V}
. Gross sales and persistent
net supply are different quantities.

D. Price Constraint and Market Absorption

Some assignments require the market maker to limit price depreciation.
Suppose
p_{0}\geq\underline{p}
and the issuer requires

p(L)\geq\underline{p}.

(33)

The residual-demand specification implies

L\leq D_{\max}\equiv\frac{p_{0}-\underline{p}}{\lambda}.

(34)

This is a bound on net selling, not on gross sales. If the benchmark
optimum exceeds
D_{\max}
, the market maker can instead meet its
gross-sales requirement with more offsetting turnover. The price
constraint alone therefore does not make the assignment infeasible when
gross turnover is unrestricted.

For any net-selling cap
K\geq 0
, the constrained solution is

L_{K}^{*}=\min\{K,L^{*}\},\qquad V_{K}^{*}=\max\{\bar{V},2\bar{S}-L_{K}^{*}\}.

(35)

To verify this result, for each
L
the least feasible gross quantity is

\max\{\bar{V},L,2\bar{S}-L\}
. The resulting cost is convex in
L
,
and capping its unique minimizer at
K
gives the solution above.
When the cap binds,
L_{K}^{*}=K
and
V_{K}^{*}=2\bar{S}-K
.
Consequently, if
K>0
, further increases in the sales requirement
decrease directional share
K/(2\bar{S}-K)
. The uncapped monotonic
prediction therefore need not hold under an absorption constraint.

E. Token Inventory

Let
Q_{L}
denote inventory available for terminal net disposition.
This implies
L\leq Q_{L}
. Combining this terminal inventory restriction
with the price constraint gives

K=\min\{Q_{L},D_{\max}\}.

(36)

The solution is equation (
35
). If

K<L^{*}
, greater available net-selling capacity reduces the turnover
needed to meet the gross-sales requirement. Once
K\geq L^{*}
, the cap is
slack and further capacity does not change the optimum.

If gross activity also has a technological upper bound
U
, feasibility
requires and is equivalent to

\bar{V}\leq U,\qquad 2\bar{S}\leq U+\min\{K,U\}.

(37)

The conditions follow by using the largest feasible gross activity and
net selling. The benchmark assumes adequate
working inventory and counterassets to execute the required turnover.

F. Does the Market Maker Use All Available Tokens?

Gross trading volume is a flow, whereas token inventory is a stock.
Repeated offsetting trading can therefore generate activity much larger
than the inventory actively used. Define the two-way component

A=V-L=2B.

(38)

Let
q
denote token inventory actively used for this component and

\tau>0
the gross two-way turnover one unit of active inventory can
support during the assignment. Under this stylized technology,

A\leq\tau q,\qquad q\geq\frac{V-L}{\tau}.

(39)

These are operating-inventory requirements, distinct from the terminal
net-selling bound
Q_{L}
. In the intermediate sales regime, required
two-way activity is
2(\bar{V}-\bar{S})
and falls as the sales quota
increases. In the high-sales regime, it equals

2(1-r)\bar{S}/(1+r)
and rises with the quota when
r<1
.
If the net-selling cap binds, it instead equals
2(\bar{S}-K)
.

If more inventory than this minimum is supplied, the benchmark is
indifferent among feasible deployment choices. It does not predict
that every supplied token is placed in circulation.
A binding operating-inventory constraint would require solving the
execution problem with that additional restriction.

G. Participation

Let the market maker’s outside option be zero. Conditional on feasible
targets and their enforcement, participation requires

F\geq C(V^{*},L^{*}).

(40)

In the high-sales regime, the minimum fee is

F_{\min}=\frac{2\bar{S}^{2}}{(1+r)^{2}}(c_{V}+c_{L}r^{2}).

(41)

For an assignment with a binding net-selling cap, the same participation
condition uses
C(V_{K}^{*},L_{K}^{*})
. Additional gross turnover may therefore
restore technological feasibility without making a fixed fee sufficient.
The fixed fee changes willingness to accept the assignment; it does not
alter least-cost execution conditional on acceptance. Targets are given and enforced.
