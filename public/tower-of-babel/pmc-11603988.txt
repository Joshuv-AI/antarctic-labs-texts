Associations between folate intake and knee pain, inflammation mediators and comorbid conditions in patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Zhenhua Liu, Ze Chen, Zewei Wu, Mingze Tang, Yongcong Lin, Cuixi Wu, Zhaohua Zhu, Guangfeng Ruan, Changhai Ding, Weiyu Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the associations between folate intake and changes in knee pain, inflammation mediators and comorbid conditions over 2 years in patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis (OA).MethodsA post-hoc analysis was performed based on data from the VIDEO study, a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial aimed at assessing the impact of vitamin D supplementation on patients with knee OA who were also vitamin D deficient. The original trial’s design and inclusion and exclusion criteria were integrated into this subsequent post-hoc analysis. The average daily folate intake was evaluated using the Dietary Questionnaire for Epidemiological Studies version 2 over two years. The progression of knee symptoms was monitored at the baseline and then at months 3, 6, 12, and 24, utilizing the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Index alongside a 100-mm visual analog scale. Levels of serum inflammatory mediators were quantified using ELISA techniques. Assessments of knee joint structures, leg muscle strength, depressive symptoms, feet pain, and low back pain were treated at both baseline and follow-up intervals.ResultsFolate intake was correlated with reductions in overall knee pain, dysfunction, and stiffness, as well as decreased levels of Leptin and Apelin. Additionally, it was associated with enhanced leg muscle strength and diminished feet and low back pain. However, there is no association between folate intake and alterations in serum cytokine levels or knee joint structural changes. Within the subsets of overall knee pain, a significant relationship was identified between folate intake and the reduction of pain experienced when ascending or descending stairs and standing for two years.ConclusionsFolate intake was linked with reduced knee pain, lower levels of adipokines, and a decreased prevalence of comorbid conditions in individuals with knee OA, implying that folate consumption may be associated with an improvement in knee OA symptoms, but further research is needed to verify this association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-08095-5.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Folate intake, Knee pain, Adipokines, Comorbid conditions

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent type of chronic joint disease, marked by inflammation and significant structural modifications within the joints, leading to discomfort and a considerable economic impact globally [1]. Knee OA emerges as the foremost variant of this condition, affecting 6% of the adult populace and entailing changes in the articular cartilage, subchondral bone, synovial membrane, and surrounding musculature [2, 3]. It was projected that the fraction of individuals aged 45 and above diagnosed with knee OA by healthcare professionals will escalate from 13.8 to 15.7% by the year 2032 [2]. Those afflicted with knee OA were typically characterized by a higher body mass index (BMI) and intense pain [2, 4]. Individuals possessing a higher BMI, indicative of increased levels of inflammatory mediators and changes of adipokines, were at a greater risk of acquiring knee OA [5–12].

Pain emerges as the principal symptom for individuals diagnosed with knee OA, significantly disrupting daily routines. This ailment chiefly caused knee joint discomfort, severely limiting everyday activities such as walking, ascending stairs, and the motions involved in laying down or getting up from a chair [13]. Those afflicted with knee OA experience intermittent pain that varies in frequency, intensity, and duration, often necessitating consultations with primary healthcare providers [14]. Concurrently, knee OA is frequently associated with a variety of comorbid conditions, such as depression, reduced leg muscle strength, feet pain, and lower back pain [15–17]. Given the scarcity of disease-modifying medications for OA currently under clinical investigation [18], there is an urgent need to investigate and formulate efficacious therapies to alleviate the distress experienced by patients with OA.

Folate, a vital coenzyme in the one-carbon metabolism pathway, plays a vital role in nucleic acid metabolism, thereby impacting cellular growth and metabolism [19]. This compound, which the human body cannot synthesize independently, is fundamental for numerous biological processes. Studies have indicated that serum folate levels tend to decrease with age [20]. Recent findings from a case-control study have revealed that individuals with knee OA exhibit lower dietary folate intake in comparison to healthy controls [21]. Moreover, a contemporary Mendelian Randomization study highlighted folate’s protective effects against knee OA in women, though these benefits were not observed in men [22]. Additionally, the most recent research has identified a correlation between folate deficiency and a progression in the radiographic severity of OA within the knee joints [23, 24]. Therefore, folate supplementation could offer potential benefits for patients suffering from knee OA, and elucidating the link between folate intake and knee OA may pave the way for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies.

According to insights from the Framingham Osteoarthritis Cohort Study, folate intake did not diminish knee OA pain or the risk of OA progression [25]. In light of this perspective, this investigation aims to assess whether folate intake was associated with changes in multiple clinical parameters in patients with knee OA over two years, despite previous findings suggesting a limited role in pain reduction and disease progression.

Methods

Participants

A post-hoc analysis was performed based on the VIDEO study, a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial designed to assess vitamin D supplementation’s effect on patients with knee OA suffering from a deficiency in vitamin D. The methodology, including the criteria for inclusion and exclusion, has been meticulously outlined in the protocol previously published [26]. In brief, eligibility for participation was determined for individuals with vitamin D levels in the serum between 12.5 and 60 nmol/L, who exhibited symptoms of knee OA, as evaluated by the criteria set forth by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) for no less than six months, and who reported a pain intensity exceeding 20 mm on a visual analog scale (VAS) with a total length of 100 mm [27]. The exclusion criteria align with those outlined in a previously published article [26]. A total of 413 participants were incorporated into this analysis.

Folate intake calculation

The assessment of daily mean folate intake was conducted through the application of the Dietary Questionnaire for Epidemiological Studies (DQES) version 2, for two years [28]. This semi-quantitative dietary questionnaire, crafted by the Cancer Council Victoria, was designed to gauge the dietary habits of Australian adults meticulously. It encompasses a detailed inventory of seventy-four food items alongside six categories of alcoholic beverages, utilizing a ten-point scale to quantify consumption frequency. This scale elegantly spans from ‘never’ to ‘three or more times per day’ allowing for a nuanced capture of dietary patterns.

Knee symptoms assessment

Symptoms of the knee were meticulously evaluated at the baseline and subsequently at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months utilizing the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) alongside a 100-mm VAS [29]. This comprehensive tool disaggregates into three distinct domains, encompassing knee pain (with five queries), dysfunction (encompassing seventeen queries), and stiffness (addressed through two queries). Each query was appraised using the 100-mm VAS, culminating in an aggregate score ranging from 0 to 2400. This total encapsulates the dimensions of knee pain (0-500), dysfunction (0-1700), and stiffness (0-200), further delineating pain into specific activities: when on flat surface, when ascending/descending stairs, while in bed, when sitting and standing upright.

Serum inflammatory mediators assessment

Fasting blood specimens were procured at the initiation point and again 24 months subsequently. Adhering to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer, the concentrations of Leptin, Adiponectin, Resistin, Adipsin, and Apelin in the serum were quantified utilizing Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) techniques, courtesy of Phoenix Pharmaceuticals Inc or Millipore Inc. Concurrently, the serum levels of interleukins IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, IL-17 A, and IL-23 were meticulously evaluated using Bio-plex Luminex assay kits supplied by Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc. The precision of these assays was underscored by an inter-assay Coefficient of Variation (CV) maintained below 10% and an intra-assay CV falling below 15% [30].

Comorbid conditions assessment

Depressive symptoms. Depression was systematically evaluated at the initial assessment and then at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months utilizing the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a tool renowned for its efficiency and reliability as a self-report measure [31]. The PHQ-9 probes into nine distinct symptoms, with the occurrence rate of these symptoms among participants gauged over 2 weeks via a 4-point Likert scale. This scale is meticulously designed, ranging from 0 (indicating no occurrence of the symptom), 1 (symptom appearing on several days), 2 (symptom experienced more than half of the days), to 3 (symptom occurring almost every day). The aggregate score, which spans from 0 to 27, serves as a quantitative indicator of the severity of depressive symptoms, with higher scores denoting more pronounced symptoms.

Lower limb muscle strength. Dynamometry was employed to assess the muscle strength of the lower limbs at the commencement and again at 24 months, focusing on both legs concurrently with a particular emphasis on the quadriceps and hip flexors. All measurements were conducted by the same trained individual to ensure consistency and reduce variability. The calibration of the apparatus was meticulously conducted by suspending weights of known mass at prescribed intervals, ensuring precision and accuracy in the measurements obtained.

Feet and Low back pain. Feet and low back pain were quantitatively assessed through questionnaires at the initial assessment, and subsequently at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months for feet pain, and at 6, 12, and 24 months for low back pain, adhering to the previously outlined protocol [26]. Subsequently, a cumulative score was computed for each, wherein elevated scores were indicative of heightened pain severity.

Anthropometrics

Weight was precisely recorded to the closest 0.1 kg, with participants divested of footwear, hosiery, and heavy garments, utilizing one pair of electronic scales. Similarly, height was ascertained to the nearest 0.1 cm, with footwear, hosiery, and headgear removed. From these measurements, the BMI was calculated, adhering to the formula: weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters.

Knee structure changes measure

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) imaging of the right knee was conducted at the baseline and again at 24 months, capturing images using a 1.5-Tesla full-body magnetic resonance unit. A T1-weighted sagittal fat-saturated spoiled gradient echo sequence was employed for cartilage. Meanwhile, to elucidate the changes in other structures of the knee joint, a T2-weighted sagittal fat-saturated fast spin-echo sequence was utilized, as we have detailed in previous descriptions [28, 32].

Cartilage volume. Cartilage was meticulously evaluated using T1-weighted images by a singularly trained evaluator at the commencement and the 24-month mark, utilizing the Osiris software program, following previously outlined methodologies [33]. The image data were transmitted to a dedicated workstation. The volume of each cartilage plate—medial tibial, lateral tibial, and patellar—was delineated section by section. This process involved the manual drawing of disarticulation outlines along the cartilage peripheries, followed by resampling using bilinear and cubic interpolation to obtain a final 3D rendering. The overall cartilage volume was determined by summing the volumes of the medial tibial, lateral tibial, and patellar regions. At our institution, the coefficients of variation (CVs) were recorded between 2.1% and 2.6% [33].

Cartilage defects. Defects of cartilage were evaluated in the medial and lateral tibial, femoral, and patellar regions, following formerly established methodologies [34]. The highest score obtained at any local site within a specific compartment (or across the entire knee) was designated as the cartilage defect score for that particular area (or the entire knee). The assessments demonstrated robust reliability, with intra-observer reliability scores varying between 0.89 and 0.94 and inter-observer consistency scores between 0.85 and 0.93 [34].

Bone Marrow Lesions. The localized zones exhibiting elevated signal intensity near the subcortical bone, identified as Bone Marrow Lesions (BMLs), were evaluated in the medial and lateral tibial, femoral, and patellar regions utilizing an adapted version of the Whole-Organ Magnetic Resonance Imaging Score (WORMS) [35]. The peak score attained at any individual site within a specific area (or throughout the entire knee) was assigned as the BML score for that area (or the entire knee). The Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs) for inter-observer consistency ranged from 0.73 to 0.95, demonstrating a high degree of agreement among observers [34].

Effusion synovitis. Effusion synovitis was assessed using an adapted WORMS method, as previously detailed in our publications. The interrater reliability and intrareader reliability for this assessment across various subregions were reported to be 0.63–0.75 and 0.60–0.75, respectively [36]. The variation in effusion-synovitis scores was determined by deducting the initial score from the score obtained at month 24, thus quantifying the variation in effusion-synovitis in the study period.

Hoffa synovitis. Hoffa synovitis, which serves as an indirect indicator of genuine synovitis, was quantified based on a 0–3 scale reflecting the condition of the intercondylar area and the Hoffa’s fat pad, employing the WORMS [37]. Additionally, signal changes in Hoffa’s fat pad were scrutinized for evidence of high signal intensity [38]. This evaluation was performed on sagittal views of fat-suppressed T2-weighted images by a skilled professional using MATLAB, following the methodologies we have previously delineated. The procedures for calculating the standard deviation in the infrapatellar fat pad (sDev (IPFP)), the upper quartile value of high signal intensity region (UQ (H)), the ratio of the volume of high signal intensity region/whole IPFP volume (Percentage (H)), and the clustering factor of high signal intensity (Clustering factor (H)) remained consistent with our prior descriptions [32].

Other measurements

Vitamin D levels were determined employing direct competitive chemiluminescent immunoassays, facilitated by DiaSorin Inc. This method ensured precision in measurement, as reflected by the coefficients of variation for intra-assay and inter-assay, which were noted at 3.2% and 6.0% respectively [39].

Data analysis

Baseline characteristics were delineated using the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or percentage. The longitudinal relationship between folate intake and variations in the WOMAC score, serum adipokine levels, comorbid conditions, and serum cytokine levels over two years was examined through linear regression analysis. This analysis took into account adjustments for age, sex, BMI, initial vitamin D levels, energy intake, and treatment allocation. Statistical significance was attributed to a P value of < 0.05 (2-tailed) or a 95% confidence interval (CI) that did not encompass the null value. All statistical evaluations were conducted through Stata version 15.0 (Stata Corp).

Discussion

This pioneering epidemiological investigation unveils the longitudinal connections between folate consumption and variations in the WOMAC score, serum adipokines and cytokine levels, comorbid conditions, and knee joint structures among individuals with symptomatic knee OA who has vitamin D deficiency. Our findings revealed that folate intake correlates with reduced knee pain, lower levels of leptin and apelin, and a diminished prevalence of comorbid conditions in knee OA patients, underscoring folate’s protective impact on this demographic. These insights advocate for supplement folate in the daily diet of knee OA patients, positing potential ameliorative effects on their condition. Individuals suffering from symptomatic knee OA could experience significant relief in daily symptoms, such as knee pain and depression, which considerably impair their quality of life, following folate supplementation.

Pain is the principal symptom of OA, the most prevalent form of arthritis [40]. A study examining the impact of folate and cobalamin on hand OA reported that patients exhibited more tender joints when treated with folate compared to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or a combination of vitamin B12 and folate, along with higher self-reported mean right-hand pain post-grip under folate and placebo conditions than under NSAIDs or B12-folate [41]. Contrarily, our research uncovered a significantly negative association between folate intake and WOMAC scores for knee pain, dysfunction, and stiffness in knee OA patients. We consider that this discrepancy might stem from differences in folate administration methods or the distinct types of OA examined in the two studies. Further analysis revealed that a lower WOMAC knee pain score, particularly during stair ascent/descent or while standing, was significantly linked to folate consumption, which implies folate intake may bring benefits to patients with knee OA.

Adipokines play an essential role in the development of OA by contributing to synovial inflammation, production of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), cartilage degeneration, and bone remodeling [7, 11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that folate deficiency contributes to lipid accumulation and the production of adipokines in fat cells [42]. These molecules are also key regulators of inflammatory immune responses within cartilage. Numerous adipokines, such as leptin, adiponectin, resistin, adipsin, and apelin, were connected with knee OA. Adiponectin, in particular, was positively correlated with MMP-13 and might play a role in the matrix remodeling process during OA [43]. Morakinyo et al. have suggested that folate intake could reduce adiponectin expression in pregnant and lactating rats [44]. However, our findings indicated that folate intake did not improve adiponectin levels in knee OA patients. Despite significant increases in resistin and adipsin levels in the serum of OA patient, indicating their potential contribution to the disease’s pathological process [45, 46], our study found only a marginal association between folate intake and these levels. A Mendelian randomization study suggested that leptin may contribute to chondrocyte hypertrophy and degeneration, as well as increased ossification during OA [7, 47]. Leptin levels in the serum have also been associated with symptoms of OA, WOMAC-pain scores, and the radiographic severity of OA [48, 49]. Concurrently, Chan et al. declared that folate deficiency is correlated with increased serum leptin levels [50]. Furthermore, apelin levels in human OA synovial fibroblasts have been positively correlated with the severity of OA cartilage degradation [51]. Our exploratory study identified a negative connection between folate intake and serum levels of leptin and apelin, implying that folate consumption might offer benefits to patients with knee OA, but additional research is required to confirm these findings.

The musculature of the lower extremities is posited to function as both shock absorbers and stabilizers of the joints, playing a crucial role in the onset of knee OA [16, 52]. A diminution in leg muscle strength, ascribed to augmented joint loading, was believed to hasten the onset of knee OA, whereas an enhancement in muscular strength may mitigate pain and enhance functional capacity in individuals suffering from knee OA [52]. Notably, two cross-sectional analyses have suggested a favorable correlation between serum folate levels and the strength of lower limb muscles [53, 54]. Consequently, the intake of folate could potentially fortify muscle strength, thereby ameliorating the symptoms associated with knee OA. Moreover, a cross-sectional examination, utilizing a 3-day dietary record to assess nutrient intake, revealed that elevated consumption of folate is linked with greater muscle strength [55]. Corroborating these findings, our research also demonstrated folate consumption is good for the strength of leg muscles. In general, intake of folate may offer new insights for the prevention and treatment of knee OA.

The pervasive nature of OA-induced chronic pain exerted a profound detriment to the quality of life-related to health [17]. The occurrence of simultaneous feet pain not only exacerbated the intensity and severity of knee pain but also augmented the symptoms of depression in patients suffering from knee OA, in stark contrast to those afflicted by knee OA in isolation [56]. Furthermore, low back pain emerged as a notable comorbid condition of knee OA, wherein its coexistence markedly escalated the level of disability and intensified the experience of pain [57, 58]. Given the increased likelihood of patients with knee OA to concurrently suffer from feet and low back pain, it is imperative to prioritize the management of chronic pain in these patients. Studies have suggested that folate intake may exert a pain-relieving effect on chronic musculoskeletal pain, potentially through the inhibition of oxidative stress [59, 60]. According to our findings, folate intake markedly mitigated the symptoms of feet and low back pain in knee OA patients, potentially enhancing the quality of healthy life, though this warrants further investigation.

Depression, frequently precipitated by chronic pain and disability caused by OA, was closely linked with heightened pain and greater functional impairment [61, 62]. A prospective study has illuminated the potential of a low dietary folate intake as a contributing factor to the aggravation of depression [63]. Individuals afflicted by depression typically exhibited lower serum levels of folate and a reduced intake of this nutrient compared to those unburdened by depression [64]. Studies have demonstrated that folate supplementation may enhance neurotransmitter synthesis and alleviate depressive symptoms, offering a ray of hope for those grappling with knee OA-induced depression [65, 66]. Our investigation revealed a positive relationship between folate intake and a reduction in depressive symptoms, post adjustment for confounding variables, aligning with the findings of prior research.

Epidemiological studies consistently revealed a prevalent subclinical folate deficiency across various demographics [67]. In numerous European nations, folate consumption fell short of the recommended levels for most life-stage groups, notably among adults and the elderly, suggesting its potential benefit for individuals with knee OA [68]. The World Health Organization (WHO) advises a daily folate intake of 400 µg for adults, yet a mere 12% of the population meets this guideline, with 58% of adults and 68% of the elderly experiencing lower folate consumption [69]. Recent Mendelian Randomization research indicated folate’s protective properties against knee OA, while folate deficiency correlated with an escalated radiographic severity of the condition [22, 23]. Contrarily, earlier cohort studies have argued that folate intake neither alleviated knee OA pain nor curtailed the progression risk of OA [25]. In our investigation, despite the average daily folate intake being a mere 0.27 µg, we observed an improvement in clinical symptoms and an enhancement in the quality of healthy life among knee OA patients, aligning with prior findings. Hence, it is imperative for individuals, particularly the elderly and those diagnosed with knee OA, to prioritize dietary folate intake, a recommendation that necessitates further empirical scrutiny.

Several potential limitations should be taken into account in our study. Primarily, the conclusions drawn herein are provisional, emanating from a post-hoc analysis after a randomized controlled trial (RCT); thus, confirmatory research is requisite to substantiate these hypotheses. Moreover, the applicability of our findings to the wider knee OA population is potentially limited, given the adherence to the original RCT’s inclusion and exclusion criteria within our study. Our study population may not be fully representative of the broader knee OA patient population, particularly in terms of vitamin D status. More comprehensive studies are needed to further explore the role of folate in knee OA. Additionally, our analysis solely quantifies folate intake through dietary sources, omitting the assessment of serum folate and homocysteine levels in knee OA patients, which could attenuate the established correlation between folate intake and our evaluated indices. Fourthly, the study’s assertion that folate supplementation might reduce low back pain should be approached with caution, as it did not account for factors such as diabetes and undiagnosed spinal disorders that could contribute to low back pain. Finally, the observed folate consumption significantly trails the WHO-recommended daily intake. Consequently, not only do our findings necessitate validation through further experimental and clinical research, but they also ought to be approached with circumspection, considering the exploratory nature of this study after an RCT.

Conclusion

In this analysis, an association was observed between folate intake and reduced knee pain, lower levels of inflammation mediators, and fewer comorbid conditions in patients with knee OA. However, these findings should be explained cautiously, as this study was exploratory. Future research, more comprehensive and rigorously designed, is essential to validate our conclusions.
