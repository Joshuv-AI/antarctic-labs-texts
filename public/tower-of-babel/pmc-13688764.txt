Plasmodium falciparum histidine rich protein 2 plasma concentrations from uncomplicated and severe malaria patients estimated by rapid test band intensity
Authors: Achaporn Yipsirimetee, Chorpaka Tangjaroenpoonsuk, Patpannee Khanthagan, Amornrat Promsongsil, Noppadon Tangpukdee, Borimas Hanboonkunupakarn, Nicholas J White, Arjen M Dondorp, Kesinee Chotivanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnlike peripheral blood parasitaemia, plasma concentrations of PfHRP2 has been established as a measure of total body parasite burden in patients with falciparum malaria, which is prognostic for progression to severe malaria and case fatality. Plasma PfHRP2 concentrations are typically quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) methods. We assessed whether band intensity on a PfHRP2-based ultrasensitive rapid diagnostic test (RDT) can provide a semi-quantitative measure for plasma PfHRP2 concentrations.MethodsPlasma samples from 60 African patients with severe falciparum malaria and 20 patients with uncomplicated falciparum malaria were assessed for PfHRP2 by ELISA and compared with RDT band intensity, assessed both visually and by digital scanning.ResultsVisual readings of RDT band intensity correlated with PfHRP2 plasma concentrations assessed by ELISA in patients with both uncomplicated (ρ = 0.80) and severe falciparum malaria (ρ = 0.61). Digitally assessed RDT band intensity in patients with severe malaria was higher than in patients with uncomplicated falciparum malaria.ConclusionRDT band intensity correlates with plasma PfHRP2 concentration measured by ELISA and may offer a simple method for estimating total parasite burden in patients with falciparum malaria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14077-w.Keywords: Malaria, P. falciparum, Histidine rich protein-2, Rapid diagnostic test

Background

Malaria is the most important parasitic disease globally, with an estimated 282 million cases in 2024 resulting in 610,000 deaths, predominantly African children [1]. Histidine-rich protein2 (HRP2) is a Plasmodium falciparum species-specific protein encoded by the pfhrp2 gene located on chromosome 8, with a repetitive motif of histidine and alanine residues [2]. HRP2 is synthesized abundantly in the second half of the asexual stage of the intra-erythrocytic parasite and is the most widely used antigen in malaria rapid diagnostic tests, which have become the mainstay for diagnosing falciparum malaria [3].

A discrete quantity of around 5 fg HRP2 per parasite is released into the blood plasma at the moment of schizont rupture [4]. Because of this, the plasma concentration of HRP2 is a surrogate measure of the sequestered mature stage parasite biomass, which is not circulating in the peripheral blood and causes organ failure by obstructing the microcirculation [4–6]. Plasma HRP2 concentrations in malaria patients are prognostic for progression to severe malaria [7] and case fatality [5, 8] and have been proposed as a tool to distinguish patients with severe febrile illness with coincidental parasitaemia from patients with real severe falciparum malaria [9]. As a consequence, assessment of plasma HRP2 concentrations has practical implications for patient management. However, plasma HRP2 is quantified using an ELISA method, which prevents routine implementation of plasma HRP2 assessment in low-resource settings in malaria-endemic countries. We hypothesized that the band intensity of HRP2-based malaria RDTs after applying a plasma sample provides a semi-quantitative measure of the plasma HRP2 concentration. To evaluate this, we compared the HRP2 plasma concentration measured by ELISA with the band intensity on an HRP2-based RDT with increased sensitivity (Alere™ Abbott Malaria Ag Pf RDT, recently renamed NxTek™ Eliminate Malaria Ag Pf) [10]. We also assessed whether RDT band intensity is an indicator of falciparum malaria disease severity.

Methods

Patient samples

The study used stored frozen plasma samples from 60 patients with severe falciparum malaria included in the African Quinine Artesunate Malaria Trial (AQUAMAT), which were collected in the nine African countries during 2005–2010 [11] and 20 patients with uncomplicated falciparum malaria were included in the Tracking Resistance to Artemisinin Collaboration II (TRAC II) trial in 2015–2018 [12]. The blood samples were taken from the malaria patients on the day enrolled to the study. The plasma was obtained and shipped on dry ice to the Mahidol-Oxford Tropical Medicine Research Unit (Bangkok, Thailand). The samples were kept at -80 °C until used. In addition, 10 plasma samples of 10 healthy adult Thai individuals were used as controls. Parasitaemia was assessed during the clinical trial period using microscopic examination to count the number of asexual parasites. Ethical approval was obtained from the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 034 − 02) and the Thai Central Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 014/2015). Samples included from the AQUAMAT trial were from a selection of patients with severe falciparum malaria with low parasitaemia and mature-stage parasites in the peripheral blood slide, which has been identified as a prognosticator for disease severity previously [13]. Uncomplicated and severe falciparum malaria were defined by standard criteria from the World Health Organization [14].

Assessment of rapid diagnostic test band intensity

Plasma samples were applied to an ultra-sensitive P. falciparum HRP2-based RDT (Alere™ Abbott Malaria Ag P.f RDT, renamed NxTek™ Eliminate Malaria Ag P.f) [10, 15]. A single batch of RDTs was used, and the RDTs were stored at room temperature. All plasma samples were kept at -80 °C and thawed on ice for two hours before assessment. Test procedures followed the recommendations provided by the RDT manufacturer. In short, 5 µL of plasma sample was applied to the sample port, followed by 4 drops of test buffer to the buffer port. The test band intensity was read after 20 min by visual assessment and digital scanning. Visual assessment was performed by three independent observers who graded band intensity numerically as: 0 (no visible band), 1 (very faint line, just visible with the naked eye), 2 (faint band, but clearly visible), 3 (very clear band, but weaker than the control band), or 4 (clear band equivalent to or more intense than the positive control band) (see additional information Fig. 1). The level of agreement between the independent observers was calculated as the inter-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Band intensity by digital scanning was used BioRad Gel Doc™ XR+ imaging, analysed by Image Lab™ Software version 6.0. The maximum intensity of the control band, the test band, and the background area of the strip were measured by this method. Background intensity was subtracted from the control and test band, providing the net intensity reading of the test band.

Quantification of HRP2 by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Plasma HRP2 was quantified using CELLABS sandwich ELISA (Cat no. KM2). For this, frozen plasma samples were thawed on ice and spun down to remove debris. The samples were diluted at 1:25, 1:125, 1:625, and 1:3125 in PBS with 0.01%Tween-20 in a standard 96-well plate. The reference HRP2 standards containing 0.391 ng/mL to 25 ng/mL HRP2, were prepared by serial 2-fold dilution in PBS with 0.01%Tween-20. PBS without additions was used as a negative control. The samples and reference standard solutions were transferred to the assay plate and incubated for 1 h in a humid chamber at room temperature. Unbound HRP2 was washed out before adding the secondary antibody. After 1 h of incubation, the unbound secondary antibody was washed out, and the substrate was added to each well. Plates were incubated in the dark for 15 min, MASS solution was added to stop the reaction, and absorbance at λ = 450 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer. A standard curve of HRP2 concentration against the ELISA absorbance signal was used to determine the HRP2 concentration of plasma test samples, using Microsoft Excel LTSC MSO (16.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis used IBM SPSS Statistics software version 29.0.0.0. Histograms of parasitemia, PfHRP2 concentrations, visual score, and net reading after digital scanning were constructed, and patient groups were compared using nonparametric, Mann-Whitney U test with a threshold significance level of 0.05. The association between HRP2 level and parasitaemia, visual reading, and net reading of digital scanning was assessed by Spearman’s rank correlation.

Results

Plasma HRP2 concentrations assessed by ELISA

The plasma samples were collected from uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria patients (n = 20) from the TRAC II study in Thailand and from severe P. falciparum malaria patients (n = 60) from the AQUAMAT study in Tanzania (Table 1). Plasma PfHRP2 concentrations were measured in 20 samples from uncomplicated malaria patients and 60 severe malaria patients using the reference ELISA assay. The median (IQR) plasma PfHRP2 concentration was 11.5 (88.8) ng/mL in patients with uncomplicated malaria and 1,068.0 (1,954.7) ng/mL in severe malaria (p < 0.01; Table 1). In comparison, the median parasitaemia was 17,710 parasites/µL in uncomplicated malaria, compared to 780 parasites/µL in this selected group of patients with severe malaria with low parasitaemia (Table 1). There was no correlation between the plasma PfHRP2 concentration and peripheral blood parasitaemia in both plasma samples from patients with uncomplicated malaria (ρ = 0.34, 95% confidence interval (CI) -0.13 to 0.81, p-value = 0.14) or severe malaria (ρ = 0.0002, 95% CI -0.26 to 0.26, p-value = 0.99) (Fig. 1).

Correlation of HRP2 concentration and RDT band intensity

All plasma samples were tested using the ultra-sensitive RDT. None of the plasma samples from healthy volunteers showed a visible line on the RDT, whereas a test line was visible in 11 out of 20 (55%) of the samples from patients with uncomplicated malaria and 59 out of 60 (98%) with severe malaria (Table 2). Among the ultra-sensitive RDT-negative results in uncomplicated malaria, the plasma PfHRP2 concentrations ranged from 1.65 to 11.45 ng/mL. Visual assessment of line intensity in the RDT showed good agreement between observers in both samples from patients with uncomplicated malaria (ICC = 0.95, p-value < 0.01) and severe malaria (ICC = 0.92, p-value < 0.01). Visually scored band intensity correlated with plasma PfHRP2 concentration assessed by ELISA in both uncomplicated malaria (ρ = 0.80, 95%CI 0.61 to 1.01, p-value < 0.01) and severe malaria (ρ = 0.61, 95%CI 0.42 to 0.8, p-value < 0.01) (Fig. 2). There was no correlation between parasitaemia and visual reading of band intensity (Fig. 2).

Using digital scanning, the median (IQR) net reading of RDT line intensity was 7,181 (108,792.25) in plasma from patients with uncomplicated malaria and 503,471 (80,936) in severe malaria (Table 2). The net reading of band intensity in severe malaria was significantly higher than in uncomplicated malaria (p-value < 0.01). When categorized by the disease severity, the correlation remains significant in both uncomplicated malaria (ρ = 0.592, 95%CI 0.21 to 0.97, p-value = 0.01), and severe malaria (ρ = 0.43, 95%CI 0.18 to 0.69, p-value < 0.01). Discussion

In areas in Sub-Saharan Africa with high falciparum malaria transmission, partial immunity against the disease is acquired in childhood, so that a malaria infection in older children and adults will often not cause clinical symptoms, resulting in a high proportion of asymptomatic parasite carriage [16, 17]. As a result, the presence of peripheral blood parasitaemia in a patient with severe febrile illness caused by another pathogen can result in a misdiagnosis of severe malaria [18, 19]. It has been estimated that up to one-third of children < 14 years with a malaria slide-positive clinical diagnosis of severe falciparum malaria have an alternative diagnosis explaining their severe febrile illness [9, 20]. This includes bacterial sepsis and is in line with published evidence that a considerable proportion of children with a clinical diagnosis of severe malaria have concomitant bacteremia [21]. Clinical differentiation between severe malaria and sepsis is challenging [22, 23], and the presence of low blood platelet counts and high concentrations of plasma HRP2 has been shown useful tool to distinguish true severe falciparum malaria from other causes of severe febrile illness with coincidental parasitemia in high transmission settings [20]. The parasite protein HRP2 is released from the infected erythrocyte mainly at the moment of schizont rupture, and stays in the circulation for a few days, thus representing the total body parasite burden [6]. Plasma HRP2 concentrations correlate with malaria severity and prognosis in both African children and Asian adults and predict progression to cerebral malaria in a study in Malawian children [7]. In contrast, peripheral blood parasite densities only reflect the circulating ring stage parasites, because more mature parasitized red blood cells are sequestered effectively in the microcirculation. This is illustrated by the selected patients with slide-positive severe malaria used in the current study, with low peripheral blood parasitaemia, but high plasma PfHRP2 concentrations, reflecting the high burden of sequestered parasites. Assessing plasma PfHRP2 by ELISA is obviously not feasible in low-resourced field settings, and the current study shows that the band intensity of a PfHRP2-based rapid diagnostic test provides a practical surrogate measure that can be deployed in any field setting.

In the current study, the ultra-sensitive RDTs detected plasma HRP2 in 55% of uncomplicated and 98% of severe malaria cases. The ultra-sensitive RDT-positive results had the plasma HRP2 level ranging from 4.85 to 386.45 ng/mL in uncomplicated malaria and 2.43–20,625 ng/mL in severe malaria. Negative RDT results were associated with low or undetectable HRP2 levels which were measured by ELISA. Visual line intensity showed strong agreement between observers and correlated with PfHRP2 concentrations. Digital RDT readings were significantly higher in severe falciparum malaria and strongly correlated with plasma HRP2 levels, but not with parasitaemia. None of the samples with a strong band score of 4 were observed in samples from patients with uncomplicated malaria, which all had a low plasma PfHRP2 concentration assessed by ELISA. However, some samples with parasitaemia > 5,000 parasites/uL showed negative results on the ultrasensitive RDT. In addition, some uncomplicated malaria cases with parasite density 17,809 parasites/uL produced only a faint band. Furthermore, one severe malaria sample was found the be undetectable of plasma HRP2 in both ELISA and ultrasensitive RDT. These findings may be explained by the deletion of pfhrp2/3 gene deletion, which has been reported to cause weak positive or false negative HRP2-based RDT result [24].

The recent data in 2021, pfhrp2/3 deletions are rare in Tanzania, with an estimated 1.6% of pfhrp2 deletion samples and 50% of pfhrp3 deletion samples [25, 26]. However, no evidence of pfhrp2/3 deletions was reported in 11 samples from Tanzania in the AQUAMAT study [27] but this possible factor needs to be elucidated. Additionally, the ultra-sensitive RDT had tested the cross reactivity with PfHRP3 and was able to detect the positive parasite down to 98 parasite/µL [28]. One of the limitations for using PfHRP2 based RDT as a tool for the semi quantitative of plasma HRP2 is the variation between the commercial kit brand. The different test kit would possibly provide the different intensity range of the test line which might cause inconsistency among the different settings. Thus, it should be noted that the observed association in our results influenced by the choice of RDT platform. In addition, the band intensity may be affected by several factors, including; degree of hemolysis, blood viscosity, haematocrit and environmental conditions. The potential confounding factors should be taken into account when interpreting the results. It is also important to consider the persistence of the HRP2 in the circulation, as the HRP2 remain detectable for several weeks after the parasite clearance. This may contributed to the positive RDT result even when the infection has been successfully treated [29]. In conclusion, band intensity assessed by an ultra-sensitive HRP2-based RDT correlates well with plasma PfHRP2 assessed by ELISA. This RDT together with other clinical manifestations can be integrated and used as a useful tool to distinguish ‘true severe malaria’ from ‘severe febrile illness with coincidental peripheral blood parasitaemia’. Semi-quantitative assessment of plasma HRP2 by this RDT could also be used as a prognosticator in patients with severe falciparum malaria.
