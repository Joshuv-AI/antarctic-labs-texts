Quantitative assessment of efficacy and evaluation of feasibility of exercise training protocols in adult and pediatric primary mitochondrial disease
Authors: Isaac Martin, Jean Flickinger, Imon Rahaman, Tejas Sarna, Elizabeth B Ballance, Nicholas Ginelli, James T Peterson, Arkady Uryash, Jose A Adams, Rui Xiao, Zarazuela Zolkipli-Cunningham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise has been demonstrated to be effective in the general population and in a small number of Primary Mitochondrial Disease (PMD) human studies, raising the potential of exercise as standard of care in PMD. However, standardized exercise prescriptions have not been established in this heterogeneous patient population. This study aimed to identify effective and feasible exercise training protocols, to inform the design of rigorous exercise studies in adults and/or children with PMD. This was achieved through integration of a structured feasibility evaluation preceded by a quantitative review of published PMD studies, with specific focus on subgroup analyses by exercise type (aerobic, resistance, interval, and combination exercise).MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted to identify published exercise studies in PMD cohorts. Due to the limited number of PMD exercise studies, the search was expanded to include disorders with secondary mitochondrial dysfunction (SMD), with similar symptoms of muscle weakness, muscle fatigue and exercise intolerance, as occurs in PMD. In total, 77 studies comprising 109 exercise training protocols were identified. Of those, a quantitative analysis was performed on 37 PMD and SMD studies reporting key outcome measures to assess the effects of aerobic, resistance, combination or interval exercise training. All 109 exercise training protocols were then systematically evaluated for feasibility, as defined by the likelihood of successful implementation in future exercise studies of PMD, using a standardized scoring system.ResultsResults of our quantitative analysis demonstrate the significant effect of distinct exercise types including aerobic exercise training on relative peak oxygen consumption (VO2), peak work, and Short-Form 36 (SF-36) physical summary score; and combination (interval/resistance/inspiratory muscle training) exercise training on relative peak VO2 and SF-36 across PMD studies. Feasibility evaluation across PMD and SMD studies involving expert group consensus review identified high-intensity circuit training (HICT), low-intensity steady-state (LISS) aerobic exercise, low-intensity resistance training, and blood flow restriction resistance training (BFR-RT) as being potentially feasible and effective in PMD.ConclusionsResults of this study highlight specific exercise training protocols that merit assessment in future rigorously designed exercise studies to determine their feasibility and therapeutic efficacy in adults and/or children with PMD.Keywords: Primary Mitochondrial Disease, Mitochondrial Myopathy, Exercise intervention, Aerobic training, Resistance training, Outcome measures.

Background

Primary Mitochondrial Disease (PMD) is a clinically heterogeneous group of > 400 gene disorders [1] due to pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in either nuclear DNA (nDNA) or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). PMD collectively affects at least 1 in 4300 people across all ages [2]. The PMD phenotypic spectrum ranges from isolated organ system involvement to multi-systemic disease that may affect any organ, leading to broad variation of clinical phenotype [3]. The most frequent clinical feature is myopathy [4, 5]. ‘Mitochondrial Myopathy’ (MM) refers to a subset of individuals with PMD who predominantly express muscle weakness, muscle fatigue, exercise intolerance and/or imbalance [6]. These symptoms typically lead to a progressively sedentary lifestyle, which further contributes to a decline in muscle oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) capacity and quality of life (QOL) [7]. Overall, the physical capacity of PMD patients, MM patients in particular, is often significantly decreased; related to the predominance of muscle symptoms. Activities of daily living (ADLs) such as walking, cleaning, and cooking that are routine for most individuals in the general population can be challenging for individuals with PMD [8, 9]. Although PMD is among the most common of genetic disorders and is significantly burdensome with a mean of 16 symptoms [4], there are currently no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapies for PMD [10]. The FDA recently approved nucleoside therapy for a rare form of MM, TK2 deficiency [11]. PMD standard of care management includes mitochondrial supplementation and physical therapy (PT) [12], with definitive supplemental therapy in gene-specific PMD subtypes, e.g. Primary Coenzyme Q10 deficiency [13, 14]. Exercise has led to consistent benefits across a range of preclinical mitochondrial disease models, including a respiratory chain complex I deficiency Harlequin (AIF-deficient) mouse model [15], haploinsufficient mouse model of Opa1 (Opa1+/-) [16], mtDNA polymerase PolgA mutator mouse [17], homoplasmic mt-ND6P25L, mt-CO1V421A, and homozygous ANT1-/- mouse models of PMD [18]; as well as in wild-type (WT) mice and rats [19–21]. These findings are further supported by small clinical studies of PMD [7, 8, 22–27]. Collectively, this body of evidence has led to exercise being recognized as a potential standard of care therapeutic strategy in PMD [28].

However, consensus guidelines on standardized exercise prescriptions in PMD have not been established. By comparison, exercise is a component of cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) with standardized protocols that have long been established [29]. CPET has utility as a clinical diagnostic test in PMD [30], although results may be nonspecific [29]; and is complementary to standard of care biochemical and genetic diagnostic testing [31], the latter ultimately confirming a PMD diagnosis.

There have been two published systematic review papers of exercise in neuromuscular disorders (NMDs) that included PMD and/or MM studies. Voet et al., 2019, conducted a Cochrane review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that assessed the effects of exercise, either strength, aerobic or combined strength and aerobic exercise versus (vs.) no exercise training in NMDs [32]. Only one RCT of MM (Cejudo et al., 2005) was included [23]. Voet et al., 2019, concluded that the certainty of evidence was very low for the effect of exercise on all outcome measures in this study, including muscle strength and aerobic capacity, and recommended further study with a larger cohort and robust methodology [32]. Stefanetti et al., 2020, conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analyses of exercise as an intervention in NMDs, and included a metabolic myopathy sub-group that included Pompe, McArdle’s and MM studies [33]. The study objective was to evaluate the effect of exercise on clearly defined clinically relevant outcome measures and safety of exercise in NMDs. The authors conducted a meta-analysis comparing effects of exercise within groups (before vs. after exercise); and between groups (exercise training vs. usual care) [33]. Results revealed significant improvement of peak VO2, peak work, and Short-Form 36 health survey (SF-36) overall scores after exercise training in the metabolic myopathy subgroup. A subgroup analysis by exercise type across all NMD studies was also performed (results displayed in publication extended table) [33], however, similar to the Cochrane review [32], only aerobic, resistance or combined aerobic/resistance exercise had been evaluated in the published studies. This highlights the limitations of exercise studies published thus far in the PMD literature, as most have not evaluated beyond aerobic and resistance exercise protocols.

Indeed, most published studies of exercise in PMD and/or MM, have studied the effects of aerobic exercise training. However, sustained aerobic exercise is not consistently feasible in the majority of PMD patients who experience exercise intolerance and muscle fatigue, especially in children. Likewise, the American Heart Association guideline, 2nd edition, recommends at least 150 minutes (min)/week of moderate-intensity, or 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity in adults to improve cardiovascular and general health [34], which would not be achievable in most PMD patients. And yet, there are a growing number of narrative review papers underlining the beneficial effect of exercise in PMD [7, 8, 25, 26, 32, 33]. Thus, the gap remains that safe, feasible and effective standardized exercise protocols to be clinically implemented in PMD have not been established. There is an urgent need to develop feasible PMD-specific exercise training recommendations that are informed by rigorous studies, and are beyond traditional aerobic exercise training; to ensure that PMD individuals gain similar health-related benefits as compared to the general population. Ultimately, when approved drugs in PMD and MM are available, the effects of drug treatment will not likely replace the benefits and need for exercise.

Given the limited number of PMD exercise studies across the two systematic review papers [32, 33], it was not surprising that our independently conducted systematic literature search yielded only a small number of published PMD exercise studies (n = 11, Fig. 1) based on our study inclusion criteria. Across the published PMD studies identified in our search, the most recent publication was in 2018 [24], when newer exercise protocols such as high-intensity interval training (HIIT) were emerging in fitness trends [35]. Thus, although PMD was the primary population of interest, given i) the small pool of published PMD and/or MM exercise studies and ii) historically limited exercise types (aerobic, resistance, or aerobic/resistance combination exercise) evaluated in PMD [32, 33], it became apparent that an expanded literature search beyond PMD and NMD was required, in order to identify newer exercise types not previously studied in PMD. By comparison, a larger number of exercise studies have been conducted in broad disease cohorts with secondary mitochondrial dysfunction (SMD), defined in this study as genetic or non-genetic disorders with known associations to abnormal mitochondrial structure and/or function that likely contributes to the underlying disease pathophysiology. Further, our inclusion criteria for SMD studies was limited to cohorts with muscle weakness, muscle fatigue and/or exercise intolerance as occurs in PMD and/or MM [6], which includes metabolic diseases (type 1 and/or type 2 diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome) [36–40], aging-related populations (older adults with age-associated metabolic decline) [41–45], neurodegenerative conditions (Alzheimer’s and related disorders) [46–48], neuromuscular diseases (Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Spinal Muscular Atrophy, Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and other muscular dystrophies) [49–59] and cardiovascular disease cohorts [60–62]. Across diverse disorders with SMD, the shared downstream bioenergetic deficits may potentially provide biologically relevant insights in similarly exercise-intolerant populations. Hence, inclusion of SMD literature in this study serves only to generate hypotheses and is not intended for direct translation to PMD cohorts. Of note, SMD studies that incorporated exercise training protocols unlikely to be feasible in PMD, e.g., exercise to exhaustion, were excluded.

Literature review and screening for inclusion in quantitative analysis and the appraisal and scoring process. The overall study outline is demonstrated in this 2020 PRISMA Flow Diagram for Systematic Review (Source: Page MJ et al., BMJ 2021; 372:n71. doi: 10.1136/bmj.n71). A total of 77 published exercise studies were included. Across the 11 PMD and 26 SMD studies, there were a total of 11 and 39 exercise training protocols, respectively, as some SMD studies assessed more than 1 exercise training protocol in their study cohorts.

Thus, the overarching goal of this study was to i) conduct a quantitative analysis to evaluate the efficacy of various exercise types administered as distinct exercise training protocols previously studied in PMD and SMD cohorts and to ii) assess for overall feasibility of implementation in future PMD exercise studies. The feasibility assessment involved an appraisal of all exercise training protocols (n = 109) identified in our literature review, using a standardized scoring approach based upon a consensus of factors that represented clinically meaningful considerations and prioritized newer exercise training protocols evaluated in SMD cohorts that have not been previously evaluated in PMD. We hypothesized that short and high-intensity exercise training protocols may be feasible in PMD cohorts. Our results will inform the design of future exercise studies in adults and children with PMD, a critical effort that is necessary in order to achieve the ultimate goal to establish standardized exercise protocols as precision therapy in the standard of care management of PMD.

Methods

PRISMA statement for the quantitative review

This systematic review and evidence synthesis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. Random-effects quantitative analysis adhered to the PRISMA guidelines and the Cochrane Collaboration exercise training protocol. Analysis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) [Computer program] Version 7.2.0., The Cochrane Collaboration, 2024, available at https://revman.cochrane.org. This study was not registered in PROSPERO because of the incorporation of a systematic feasibility assessment and expert-consensus component that evolved during the literature appraisal, which is not accommodated by PROSPERO’s prespecified protocol framework. A PRISMA flow diagram summarizing the study selection process is found in Fig. 1 and PRISMA checklist is available in additional file materials (Additional File 1: PRISMA_ 2020 checklist.pdf).

Search process and eligibility criteria

A systematic search was conducted in online databases (PubMed, Google Scholar from 1994 to Dec 2024) to identify published exercise studies performed in PMD and SMD cohorts and exclude published meta-analyses and review articles of exercise in PMD, Fig. 1. PubMed was used to capture peer-reviewed biomedical literature indexed in MEDLINE, while Google Scholar was included to broaden retrieval across interdisciplinary and exercise science journals that may not be consistently indexed in MEDLINE. The search and initial screen were initially conducted by a single reviewer (IM) within a defined project timeline, necessitating a pragmatic balance between search comprehensiveness and feasibility; this approach is acknowledged as a limitation and is addressed through transparent reporting of search terms, eligibility criteria, and study selection processes. To mitigate this limitation, all included studies were manually screened, and study selection was reviewed in consultation with subject-matter experts (JF, JAA, ZZC) to ensure inclusion of key and representative literature. IM was blinded to the PMD studies included in prior published systematic reviews and meta-analyses [32, 33] to avoid bias in our literature search process. Exclusion criteria were prespecified to ensure that included SMD studies were clinically and mechanistically relevant to PMD, and that the exercise interventions represented structured exercise training protocols potentially translatable to future PMD exercise study design. Exclusion criteria also considered feasibility and safety considerations in exercise-intolerant populations, where exercise training protocols were required to be scalable, repeatable, and tolerable for PMD individuals.

Study eligibility and literature search strategy for PMD

The PMD literature review was exhaustive. Inclusion criteria included PMD cohorts with confirmation of nDNA or mtDNA genetic etiology and/or clinical, biochemical, or histopathologic evidence of PMD. Detailed cohort descriptions are shown in Additional File 2: Table S1. The literature search for PMD was conducted using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to PMD and exercise interventions and are included in Additional File 3: Table S2. This strategy was designed to capture exercise training studies across PMD phenotypes, and to identify various exercise types including aerobic, resistance, interval, and combination exercise interventions reporting clinically relevant outcome measures in PMD (as outlined in section 2.3 below), for quantitative synthesis and feasibility assessment.

Study eligibility and literature search strategy for SMD

In this study, SMD was defined as genetic or non-genetic disorders with known associations to abnormal mitochondrial structure and/or function that likely contributes to the underlying disease pathophysiology [63, 64]. Studies were eligible when SMD cohorts shared core clinical phenotype features of muscle weakness, muscle fatigue and exercise intolerance as observed in PMD and/or MM [6], including cohorts with type 2 diabetes [65, 66], obesity [67–71], older adults [72–78], heart failure [79, 80], multiple sclerosis (MS) [81]; and neuromuscular diseases including Duchenne muscular dystrophy [82], Becker muscular dystrophy [83], Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) [84, 85], myotonic dystrophy [86, 87], facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy [88, 89], Bethlem myopathy [90], and other non-metabolic myopathies [91]. Specifically, these SMD studies were incorporated in our quantitative analysis and/or feasibility assessment to provide contextual and translational insight into exercise responses in disease cohorts with mitochondrial impairment in order to generate hypotheses and were not intended for direct translation to PMD. These studies were weighted accordingly in the feasibility assessment.

The literature search for SMD studies was conducted using a combination of disease- and population-specific terms paired with exercise-related keywords. Searches incorporated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to SMD relevant disease states and displayed in Additional File 3: Table S2. Search terms were iteratively adapted to capture a broad range of exercise training modalities in intervention studies across diverse SMD cohorts in which mitochondrial dysfunction is recognized as a component of disease pathophysiology. The literature search in PubMed and Google Scholar resulted in a total count of 276,790 records. We excluded all records that did not contain exercise training (defined as multiple sessions of exercise over time) in PMD or SMD cohorts (n = 251,295). Next, all abstracts were screened for studies performed in PMD and/or SMD cohorts (n = 25,495). Exclusion criteria included i) study cohorts that did not align with PMD phenotype (e.g., non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) [92], ii) exercise training protocols that did not align with PMD phenotype and physical capabilities (e.g. high-intensity/explosive/high-speed power training [93–95], iii) exercise training protocols that were performed to exhaustion [96], iv) studies that included only a single experimental exercise session [97–102], and v) studies where drug intervention (e.g., chemotherapy) were included [103–106].

After the screening process shown in Fig. 1, a total of 77 published exercise studies comprising 109 distinct exercise training protocols were identified. Of those, 37/77 (48%) studies met criteria for inclusion in the evidence synthesis and quantitative analysis related to having defined outcome measure(s) of interest that were commonly reported across the published studies and considered to be clinically relevant in future PMD clinical trials, as described below. All 77 studies comprising 109 distinct exercise training protocols were scored for feasibility and subjected to expert scoring to achieve consensus on the top-ranked exercise training protocols worthy of consideration for a future exercise clinical trial in PMD. The entire identification, screening, and inclusion process for the selection of these studies is shown in Fig. 1.

Description of the quantitative analysis

The quantitative analysis was conducted using a predefined Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes (PICO) framework. The population (P) included individuals diagnosed with PMD or MM, confirmed by genetic, and/or clinical, biochemical, and histopathologic criteria. Due to the limited number of exercise studies conducted exclusively in PMD, exercise studies in SMD cohorts that shared core clinical phenotypic features with PMD, including exercise intolerance, muscle weakness, and fatigue, were included to assess newer exercise types not previously studied in PMD. Both pediatric and adult populations were eligible, although exercise studies in PMD were predominantly conducted in adults. The interventions (I) of interest were structured exercise training protocols consisting of repeated sessions over time and were categorized as aerobic, resistance, combination or interval (including high-intensity interval training) exercise protocols. The primary comparators (C) were within-subject pre-intervention vs. post-intervention measurements in PMD and/or MM cohorts and SMD cohorts. The outcomes (O) selected for quantitative synthesis were clinically relevant outcome measures commonly reported across exercise studies, including relative and absolute peak oxygen consumption (VO₂), peak work capacity, upper- and lower-body one-repetition maximum (1RM), peak blood lactate level, and health-related quality of life assessed by the SF-36 physical summary score, as these were considered clinically relevant in future exercise studies of PMD. This PICO framework guided study selection, data extraction, subgroup analyses, and interpretation of findings.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for each study using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials and the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized and pre–post intervention studies, Additional File 4: Table S3 and Additional File 5: Table S4. Two reviewers (JAA, AU) independently evaluated risk of bias across all prespecified outcome measures, including peak VO₂, peak work capacity, muscle strength, blood lactate, and health-related quality of life. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer when required.

Statistical analysis

Our quantitative analysis of published exercise studies across PMD and SMD cohorts was conducted to ascertain, i) the efficacy and effect size of distinct exercise training protocols including subgroup analyses by exercise type and ii) the utility of select outcome measures of interest to inform the design of future PMD exercise studies. Quantitative analysis (Figs. 2–14) included subgroup analysis by exercise type if there was a sufficient number of studies, including aerobic, resistance, interval training, and combination exercise. Interval exercise training protocols included intervals of high-intensity exercise alternating with periods of rest or active rest, which involved exercise at lower intensities. The combination exercise training protocols included two or more types of exercise, for example, both aerobic and resistance exercise within each exercise session or on different days throughout the study duration. All quantitative analyses and forest plots were generated in Revman Web (Figs. 2–14).

Only 11 studies in PMD and 26 in SMD disease cohorts, consisting of 50 distinct exercise training protocols utilizing one or more of the defined outcome measures met criteria for inclusion in the quantitative analysis (Fig. 1). Random-effects analysis was performed separately across PMD and SMD studies. A study-specific standardized mean difference (std. mean difference) was first calculated to compare the mean value between pre- and post-exercise for the relevant outcome measure(s) utilized in each study [107]. A std. mean difference was then calculated across all studies (total) and across sub-groups where relevant (subtotal). The std. mean difference accounts for variations in cohort size and the distribution of the outcome measure data in each study. The significance of the std. mean difference was assessed using the test for overall effect, the Z-statistic. In random-effects quantitative analysis, a larger absolute Z-value indicates a more pronounced overall effect and greater statistical significance [108]. Study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, which describes the percentage of the variability in effect estimates due to heterogeneity of the data and not due to chance or random sampling error [109]. Thresholds for interpreting the I² statistic were defined as i) low heterogeneity when the I² value was < 20%, ii) medium heterogeneity for I² values between 20 and 49%, and iii) high heterogeneity > 50% [110].

Definition of select outcome measures of interest

We selected outcome measures of interest based on i) review of outcome measures reported across published PMD studies, ii) outcome measures included in recent PMD clinical trials, iii) clinical judgement of the relevance of each outcome measure, and iv) frequency of outcome measure reporting across eligible studies, which enabled a quantitative synthesis. These included absolute and relative peak oxygen consumption (VO2), lower body one-repetition maximum (1RM), upper body 1RM, peak work capacity, peak blood lactate, and the SF-36 physical summary score.

Absolute peak VO2 is expressed in liters per minute (L/min) and represents the total amount of oxygen consumed regardless of body weight, while relative peak VO2 is expressed in milliliters per kilogram of body weight per minute (ml/kg/min). Peak VO2 was selected as a primary outcome measure of interest because maximal oxygen uptake (peak VO2) indicates aerobic fitness. Oxygen uptake from rest to maximal exercise is primarily attributed to skeletal muscle oxygen uptake [101, 111, 112]. One-repetition maximum (1RM) is a widely used measure of maximal muscle strength, which directly addresses the strength component of muscle function. 1RM has been demonstrated to be a reliable measure in healthy and disease cohorts [113, 114] and is considered the gold standard for assessment of muscle strength in real-world settings [115]. 1RM is described as the weight that can be lifted no more than once with acceptable form [76, 113–116], the latter defined as an exercise performed primarily by the specified muscle groups without the use of momentum or change in body position, unless directly resulting from the movement of the weight itself during the entire range of motion. When this criterion for acceptable form is not achieved in a repetition, the last successfully performed weight is noted as the 1RM. However, other papers more loosely define 1RM as the maximal amount of weight that can be lifted in one repetition [117, 118]. In our quantitative assessment, we incorporated both definitions and focused exclusively on leg press exercise as a measurement for lower body 1RM and free weight bench press for upper body 1RM. Work capacity is colloquially defined as the ability to perform real physical work [119]. Peak work capacity (W) is assessed as the maximal wattage generated on a cycle ergometer (e.g., MedGraphics 2000) [120]. Peak blood lactate (mmol/L) measurements were collected at a specified time point following maximal exercise and were typically drawn 3–8 min post-maximal exercise testing [121]. The SF-36 is a 36-item short, self-administered survey for persons 14 years of age and older, and is a well-established tool for assessing health-related QOL [122]. The SF-36 consists of 8 health concepts, which are tabulated and yield a single physical component summary score [122–125]. It was not consistently evident that the SF-36 physical component summary score was the score being presented across all PMD and SMD studies, therefore we included SF-36 scores from all studies using the following descriptions, “single physical component summary score”, “total physical component score”, and “physical functioning component score”.

Quantitative analysis exclusion criteria

Reasons for exclusion from the quantitative analysis included studies that utilized primary or secondary outcome measures that were mainly i) biochemical (e.g. lipid profile) [126], ii) histopathological (muscle biopsy) [127] or iii) anthropometric weight loss measures (BMI) [128]. Furthermore, outcome measures that were study cohort specific, but not relevant to PMD (e.g., frailty index [70], insulin sensitivity [129, 130] or raised uncertainties in specific outcome measure results interpretation [131] were excluded. In addition, studies that evaluated the efficacy of drug interventions alongside exercise were excluded [103–106], with the exception of one study performed in adult patients with Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) that enrolled subjects prior established on creatine supplementation with no changes made to the dosing regime and without its efficacy being evaluated [132].

Appraisal and feasibility scoring of all exercise protocols

A total of 109 exercise training protocols were appraised and initially scored for feasibility by one author (IM) (Additional File 6: Table S5). Feasibility was evaluated using eight prespecified categories, each scored on a three-point ordinal scale (1 = lowest feasibility; 3 = highest feasibility), as detailed in Table 1. Categories include study population, training period (weeks), exercise duration per session (minutes), reported compliance rate (%), exercise intensity (% of maximum heart rate, peak VO₂, peak work, or 1RM), exercise position, equipment requirements, and year of publication.

Scoring Template for Feasibility Assessment.

Each exercise training protocol was scored across 8 categories with a score ranging from 1 (lowest) to 3 (highest), with a maximum total score of 24. The categories consist of i) study population, ii) training period (weeks), iii) exercise duration (minutes), iv) compliance rate (%), v) exercise intensity [% of heart rate (HR), peak VO2, peak work, or 1RM], vi) exercise position, vii) equipment requirement, and viii) year of publication.

Exercise training protocols conducted in PMD cohorts were weighted highest (score = 3), followed by SMD disease cohorts (score = 2) and obese or elderly populations (score = 1). Training periods of ≥12 weeks received the highest score, reflecting greater potential for long-term sustainability; followed by 7–11 weeks (score = 2) and ≤6 weeks (score = 1). Exercise sessions of ≤30 min were scored highest, whereas durations of 30–45 min and >45 min were assigned progressively lower scores, given the high prevalence of exercise intolerance in PMD. Studies reporting compliance ≥85% received the highest score, those reporting <85% received an intermediate score, and studies not reporting compliance were scored lowest.

Exercise position, intensity, and equipment requirements were prioritized as key determinants of feasibility. Protocols performed in sitting or supported positions were scored higher than those requiring standing or running, reflecting common limitations in balance and ambulation among individuals with PMD. Exercise training protocols utilizing home-accessible equipment were weighted more favorably than those requiring specialized equipment or substantial space. Finally, lower-intensity protocols ( < 55% of maximum heart rate, peak VO₂, peak work, or 1RM) received the highest feasibility scores, consistent with the limited aerobic and muscular reserve characteristic of PMD populations. Descriptive narratives of distinct exercise training protocols of interest are included in the Results and Discussion sections.

Expert group ranking and consensus

Following initial feasibility scoring (IM), all 109 exercise training protocols were reviewed to identify those with the highest potential relevance for PMD. Protocols with the highest feasibility scores ≥ 18 were subjected to a first-tier expert group consensus review (JF, EB, MM, ZZC), with prioritization of novel or underexplored exercise modalities not previously evaluated in PMD cohorts, including high-intensity, low-intensity and interval/circuit-based exercise approaches. This resulted in the selection of the top nine exercise training protocols. These nine protocols were then independently ranked by two physical therapists (PTs) with expertise in PMD (JF, EB), with emphasis on feasibility, safety, and anticipated compliance in PMD cohorts.

Selection of the final top 4 exercise protocols was determined through a second-tier expert group consensus (JF, EB, MM, JA, ZZC). This multistep expert review process was designed to prioritize exercise training protocols most likely to be clinically implementable and scalable in future PMD exercise studies. Given the high prevalence of exercise intolerance and muscle fatigue in PMD [6], identifying exercise training protocols that were safe, tolerable, sustainable and would promote consistent participation in exercise training long-term was considered a necessary prerequisite to inclusion in the design of future exercise studies and ultimately, to establish standard of care exercise training protocols. This approach was intended to robustly inform the future design and implementation of rigorous PMD exercise studies.

Results

Risk of bias assessment for quantitative synthesis review of exercise in PMD studies

Risk of bias was assessed for all PMD studies using RoB 2 for randomized trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized intervention studies, Additional File 4: Table S3. There were two RCTs that were judged to have an overall risk of bias of “some concerns,” primarily due to incomplete reporting of allocation concealment, limited blinding inherent to exercise interventions, and absence of prespecified analysis plans. All non-randomized PMD exercise studies (n = 10, including one of the two RCT studies with representation in both the RCT and non-RCT group due to a dual-step study design initially involving non-randomization) were rated as having serious risk of bias, driven predominantly by confounding related to pre–post study designs without concurrent controls, small sample sizes, and selective participant inclusion. Across studies, objective physiological outcomes (e.g., peak VO₂, peak work capacity, and magnetic resonance–based measures) were generally at lower risk of measurement bias, whereas self-reported outcomes were more susceptible to bias. Results of risk of bias assessment is displayed in Additional File 4: Table S3.

Risk of bias assessment for quantitative synthesis review of exercise in SMD studies

Risk of bias in studies of SMD was assessed using RoB 2 for randomized trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies, Additional File 5: Table S4. All RCTs in SMD (n = 7) were judged to have some concerns overall, mainly due to limited reporting of randomization procedures and deviations from intended interventions, while measurement of objective physiological outcomes was generally at low risk of bias. All non-randomized SMD studies (n = 21) were rated as having serious risk of bias, driven primarily by confounding from pre–post designs and moderate risk related to participant selection and missing data. Objective outcomes including peak VO₂ and peak work capacity consistently showed lower measurement bias than self-reported outcomes, Additional File 5: Table S4.

Characterization of exercise studies

A total of 37/77 exercise studies identified in our systematic literature review comprising 50 distinct exercise training protocols met criteria for inclusion in the quantitative analysis (Fig. 1). The 37 exercise studies were conducted in PMD (n = 11) and SMD (n = 26) disease cohorts and reported one or more of the outcome measures of interest: absolute and relative peak VO2, lower and upper body 1RM, peak work capacity, peak blood lactate, and SF-36 physical summary scores. Across the 11 PMD and 26 SMD studies, there were a total of 11 and 39 exercise training protocols, respectively. Exercise intervention types in PMD included aerobic (n = 8), resistance (n = 1), and combination (n = 2) exercise training protocols, performed in adults only. Exercise intervention types in SMD included aerobic (n = 17), resistance (n = 7), interval training (n = 7), and combination (n = 8) exercise training protocols. Only two SMD studies included children and adolescents [65, 85]. Montes et al., 2015 [85], enrolled subjects aged 10–48 years and Naylor et al., 2016 [65] enrolled adolescents and young adults aged 13–21 years. Details of each exercise training protocol and study demographics are described in Additional File 6: Table S5.

PMD exercise study cohorts included adults with a single, large scale mtDNA deletion (SLSMD), nuclear DNA variants primarily involved in mtDNA maintenance (POLG, TWNK, TK2) and mitochondrial dynamics disorders (OPA1); and pathogenic mtDNA variants in various mt-tRNAs and respiratory chain subunits (MT-TE, MT-TL1, MT-TL2, MT-TK, MT-TN, MT-TW, MT-ND2, MT-ND4, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6) [22, 24, 91, 120, 131, 133–139]. Several studies also included adult subjects without identified genetic etiology who were clinically diagnosed based on clinical, biochemical and/or histopathologic features; or multiple mtDNA deletions in muscle [22–24, 91, 120, 137, 138, 140, 141]. Synthesis of quantitative analysis results (n = 50 exercise training protocols)

Relative peak VO2 (ml/kg/min)

PMD (n = 5 exercise training protocols)

Five exercise training protocols measuring relative peak VO2 (n = 5) in PMD were included in the analysis, Fig. 2. A total std. mean difference of 1.40 with 95% CI = [0.54, 2.27], (Z = 3.19, p = 0.001) was obtained across all 5 studies. Thus, it can be concluded that the overall effect of these exercise training protocols on relative peak VO2 was significant across PMD cohorts. High heterogeneity was observed, I2 = 74%. All five studies enrolled adult subjects with Mitochondrial Myopathy (MM), however there were differences in cohort age and sex. Cohort mean age was 39.3 ± 9.5 years (mean ± standard deviation, SD), 60% female, in Taivassalo et al., 2001 [120]; an older cohort of 51 ± 16 years, 33% female in Porcelli et al., 2016a [141]; a broad range of adults of 19–59 years, 33% female in Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 [24]; a middle-aged cohort of 42.4 ± 10.5 years, 40% female in Bates et al., 2013 [133]; and a range of adults of 26–57 years, 50% female in Jeppesen et al., 2006 [134].

Aerobic exercise training protocols (n = 4) [120, 133, 134, 141] showed a significant increase in relative peak VO2 (ml/kg/min) post aerobic exercise training with a std. mean difference of 1.26, 95% CI = [0.22, 2.31], (Z = 2.38, p = 0.02). The aerobic studies implemented similar exercise training protocols using cycle ergometry at 70–80% of maximum heart rate (Taivassalo et al., 2001) [120], 65–70% of maximum heart rate (Porcelli et al., 2016a) [141], target heart rate corresponding to 65–75% of peak VO2 (Jeppesen et al., 2006) [134], or a heart rate corresponding to 70–80% of symptom-limited maximum oxygen uptake with Borg rate of perceived exertion (RPE) score of 12–14 (Bates et al., 2013) [133]. Each study involved participation in exercise 3–4 times each week for 14 weeks (50 sessions) [120], 4 times each week for 12 weeks (48 sessions) [141], ~ 4 times each week for 12 weeks (50 sessions) [134], and 3 times per week for 16 weeks (48 sessions) [133], respectively, Additional File 6: Table S5.

The only combination exercise training protocol (Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018) [24] revealed the highest std. mean difference of 1.98 with 95% CI = [0.98, 2.99], (Z = 3.86, p = 0.0001), showing a significant effect of combination exercise on relative peak VO₂ post-exercise training, Fig. 2. Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 implemented an 8-week combination protocol which included 20 min of high-intensity interval training (starting at 65% peak power output and working up to 90–100%) on a cycle ergometer, followed by a resistance training circuit for arms, legs, and abdominal muscles using weight training equipment, 3 times per week (24 sessions), with sessions lasting 60–90 min [24]. In addition, the authors included inspiratory muscle training consisting of 30 inspirations through a pressure loaded device against 40% of maximal inspiratory pressure, implemented twice daily, 6 days each week throughout the 8-week protocol [24], Additional File 6: Table S5.

SMD (n = 19 exercise training protocols)

Nineteen exercise training protocols measuring relative peak VO₂ in SMD cohorts were included, Fig. 3. A total std. mean difference of 1.28, 95% CI = [0.88, 1.68], Z = 6.29, p < 0.00001, was obtained across all 19 studies. Thus, it can be concluded that the overall effect of these exercise training protocols on relative peak VO₂ was significant across the SMD cohorts. High heterogeneity was observed, I² = 73%. To better determine the source of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analysis by exercise type which included aerobic exercise, combination exercise, and interval training.

Across all protocols, a std. mean difference of 1.28, 95% CI = [0.88, 1.68], Z = 6.29, p < 0.00001 was obtained. The I² was high at 73%. Aerobic exercise training protocols, the largest subgroup (n = 10 protocols) [79, 80, 84, 86, 87, 90, 141–143], showed the greatest std. mean difference of 1.80 with 95% CI = [1.37, 2.24], Z = 8.12, p < 0.00001. Heterogeneity was medium with I² = 46%. Nine of these aerobic exercise training protocols involved a cycle ergometer [79, 84, 86, 87, 90, 141–143] at varying levels of low to moderate intensity (40–75% of peak VO₂ or maximum heart rate) for 30–45 min training sessions. One study involved uphill treadmill walking (Wisloff et al., 2007a) [80] at 70–75% of maximum heart rate for 47 min sessions. The exercise training period across the 10 exercise training protocols ranged from 8 to 12 weeks. All studies enrolled adult cohorts with a wide range of diagnoses including neuromuscular disorders consisting of SMA type III [84], myotonic dystrophy type 1 [86, 87], and males with Bethlem myopathy [90]; overweight and obese subjects with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [142], adults with chronic heart failure [79], adults with McArdle’s disease [141], adults with post-infarction heart failure [80], and elderly men [143]. The collective evidence from these 10 studies demonstrates a significant effect of aerobic exercise training on relative peak VO2.

Combination exercise training protocols (n = 5 protocols) consisted of aerobic and resistance training (Montes et al., 2015, Naylor et al., 2016, n = 2) [65, 85], resistance and interval training (Zaenker et al., 2018) [81], aerobic and interval training (Bankole et al., 2016) [88], and a home-based program of active range of motion, low-intensity resistive, stepping exercises, and posture/breathing exercises (Ercin et al., 2020c) [142]. Results showed a significant increase in relative peak VO2 post-combination exercise training protocols [65, 81, 85, 88, 142] with std. mean difference of 0.43, 95% CI = [0.09, 0.77], Z = 2.49, p = 0.01. There was no measurable heterogeneity across the subgroup (I2 = 0%). The duration of exercise training protocols ranged from 8 weeks to 6 months. Enrolled cohorts included adolescents with type 2 diabetes [65], ambulatory children and adults with SMA [85], adults with multiple sclerosis (MS) [81], adults with facioscapulohumeral dystrophy (FSHD) [88], and overweight/obese adults with COPD) [142].

Interval training exercise training protocols (n = 4) [72, 80, 142, 144], also showed a significant increase in relative peak VO2 (ml/kg/min) post interval exercise training with a std. mean difference of 1.3, 95% CI = [0.33, 2.28], Z = 2.62, p = 0.009. This subgroup had the highest heterogeneity, I2 = 79%. Exercise training protocols involved either treadmill walking or cycle ergometry with bouts of high-intensity [85–95% peak VO2 (Charifi et al., 2003) [72], 80–90% VO2 reserve (Bartlett et al., 2018) [144], 90–95% peak heart rate (Wisloff et al., 2007b) [80], or 100% peak work rate (Ercin et al., 2020a) [142]] exercise alternating with lower intensity [65–75% peak VO2 [72], 50–60% VO2 reserve [144], 50–70% peak heart rate [80], or fully resting [142]] intervals with sessions lasting 30–45 min each. Exercise training durations ranged from 8 to 14 weeks. Enrolled cohorts included older adults with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [144], healthy elderly males [72], overweight/obese adults with COPD [142], and older adults with heart failure [80]. Interval times of high versus low intensity varied across studies, likely contributing to the heterogeneity. The highest std. mean difference in this group, 3.06, 95% CI = [1.6, 4.52] involved walking uphill on a treadmill at 4 min intervals at 90–95% of maximum heart rate with 3 active “rest” periods at 50–70% maximum heart rate between intervals for a total exercise session of 38 min including a warm-up and cool down [80]. This was performed 3 times each week including 2 supervised sessions and 1 home session.

Cejudo et al., 2005, enrolled subjects with MM who were diagnosed on the basis of clinical and muscle biopsy results as well as symptoms of exercise intolerance [23].

Upper body 1RM (kg) in SMD (n = 4 exercise training protocols)

Four exercise training protocols measuring upper body 1RM in SMD cohorts [75, 76] were included, Fig. 8. Results showed a significant effect of exercise training interventions on upper body 1RM in these SMD cohorts (std. mean difference of 1.50 with 95% CI = [0.63, 2.37], Z = 3.37, p = 0.0008). These exercise training protocols studied similar cohorts of older men and women (Pyka et al., 1994 and Taaffe et al., 1999a-c) [75, 76]. All of the exercise training protocols included resistance exercise using a bench press, working at 60–65% of 1RM initially, then progressing to 75% [75] and 80% [76] of 1RM. The duration of exercise training ranged from 24 weeks [76] to 52 weeks [75]. The loads to achieve the target 1RM continued to be adjusted every three weeks and strength continued to improve throughout the year-long exercise training protocol. Also of note, the three exercise groups included in Taaffe et al., 1999 differed only by exercise frequency each week, with (Taaffe et al., 1999a) 1 time weekly yielding a smaller std. mean difference of 0.72 (95% CI = [−0.15, 1.59]) compared to (Taaffe et al., 1999b) 2 times weekly at 1.37 (95% CI = [0.46, 2.27]) and (Taaffe et al., 1999c) 3 times weekly at 1.26 (95% CI = [0.33, 2.20]) [76]. Although the authors reported that all three exercise groups experienced significantly increased muscle strength relative to the control group (p < 0.01), there was no significant difference in the exercise training effect on strength between the three exercise groups [76]. The differences in frequency and duration likely contributed to the high heterogeneity across all studies (n = 4), I2 = 64%.

SF-36 Physical Summary Score

SF-36 Physical Summary Score in PMD (n = 5 exercise training protocols)

Peak lactate (mmol/L)

PMD (n = 4 exercise training protocols)

Four exercise studies reported peak blood lactate post-exercise training (n = 4) [23, 24, 134, 141] as an outcome measure in PMD cohorts. Results showed no significant effect of exercise interventions on peak lactate levels (std. mean difference of 0.34, 95% CI = [−0.18, 0.85], Z = 1.27, p = 0.20, I2 = 31%, Fig. 13). The aerobic component of the exercise training protocols were similar across studies, with the exception of Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 [24], and focused on aerobic exercise on a cycle ergometer for 12 weeks, at a frequency of 3 times each week (Cejudo et al., 2005) [23] or 4 times each week (Jeppesen et al., 2006 and Porcelli et al., 2016a) [134, 141] at sub-maximal levels. In addition, Cejudo et al., 2005 combined aerobic exercise with upper extremity strengthening at 50% 1RM during each session [23]. Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 included a combination exercise training protocol consisting of high-intensity interval training (HIIT) on a cycle ergometer combined with resistance training 3 times each week (24 sessions) with sessions lasting 60–90 min [24]. This exercise training protocol also included twice daily inspiratory muscle training, 6 days each week throughout the 8-week exercise training protocol [24]. Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 did not report peak lactate values pre-training and post-training but did report resting lactate levels (obtained before aerobic power tests) and the acute change values (defined as the value after the maximal cycle-ergometer test minus the value before maximal cycle ergometer test) pre-training and post-training [24]. Therefore, to effectively compare results to other studies in our quantitative analysis, we combined their resting lactate levels with acute change levels to obtain the peak blood lactate levels post-training. Overall, there was no significant change in post-exercise training peak lactate levels across the 4 PMD studies.

Informed by results of our quantitative analysis, we next pursued a systematic evaluation and appraisal of all identified exercise studies (n = 77), consisting of 109 exercise training protocols, followed by expert scoring to identify and reach consensus on newer exercise protocols not previously studied in PMD to be considered for future pediatric and adult PMD exercise clinical trials. The impact of our study design to include SMD studies was the most evident hereafter, as newer exercise protocols were readily identified in SMD studies, in part due to the more recently published literature.

Literature appraisal and systematic assessment of feasibility to identify new exercise protocols for implementation in future PMD clinical trials

Demographic description across all exercise training protocols (n = 109)

After the initial literature search and screening process, a total of 109 unique exercise training protocols were identified. Characterization of each of the 109 exercise training protocols is described in Additional File 6: Table S5. All exercise training protocols were appraised and scored using a standardized scoring template developed by the expert group (Table 1). Exercise was categorized into four subtypes: aerobic exercise (n = 44 protocols), resistance exercise (n = 36 protocols), interval training (n = 9 protocols), and combination exercise (n = 20 protocols). Across the 109 exercise training protocols, 15 exercise training protocols were studied in PMD cohorts (in adults only) including aerobic (n = 11), resistance (n = 2), and combination exercise (n = 2); and 94 exercise training protocols in SMD cohorts including aerobic (n = 33), resistance (n = 34), interval (n = 9), and combination exercise (n = 18), where 81 were studied in adult subjects and 13 in children, Fig. 1, Additional File 6: Table S5.

In the SMD category, exercise training protocols included obese and elderly cohorts (n = 47), diabetes (n = 8) [65, 66, 147–150], cerebral palsy (n = 5) [151–153], and autism (n = 1) [154]. Additionally, studies in neuromuscular disease cohorts included Duchenne muscular dystrophy (n = 2) [82, 155], myotonic dystrophy type 1 (n = 2) [86, 87], Becker muscular dystrophy (n = 2) [83], facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (n = 3) [88, 89, 156], Bethlem myopathy (n = 1) [90], Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) (n = 5) [84, 85, 157–159], and Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (n = 2) [132, 160]. The remaining 16/94 studies are outlined in Additional File 6: Table S5.

Appraisal and systematic scoring across all identified exercise protocols (n = 109)

Results of the appraisal and initial feasibility assessment demonstrated that of the 109 exercise training protocols scored, the highest score was 22 of a possible 24 points, and the lowest score was 13 of 24 points. Overall, the mean score was 18.1 ± 1.88 (mean ± standard deviation, SD) and the mode was 19, with a range of 13–22 points (minimum–maximum) across all studies (Additional File 6: Table S5).

An initial first-tier expert group review of all highly scored exercise training protocols was conducted (JF, EB, MM, ZZC) to achieve consensus on the top 9 exercise training protocols considered of greatest interest for potential implementation in PMD. Expert group discussions highlighted the need to prioritize newer exercise protocols not previously studied in PMD cohorts. As a result, aerobic exercise protocols using cycle (lower extremity) ergometry and treadmills (n = 42) were not included. This was a pragmatic decision made to facilitate exploration of other exercise modalities less studied in PMD for future exercise protocols, in order to ensure optimized feasibility and participation of MM subjects in whom cycle ergometry and treadmill exercise training may be challenging to complete. Indeed, our clinical experience has been that approximately only 20% of our clinic cohort are able to complete cycle ergometry exercise for CPET, limited by a combination of muscle weakness, muscle fatigue, impaired distal sensation/proprioception, and imbalance with risk of falls, thereby raising safety concerns and the inability to reach maximal exercise effort. Indeed, the American Thoracic Society/ American College of Chest Physicians (ATS/ACCP) states that patients who are unable to exercise due to neurological or orthopedic problems should not be candidates for CPET [161]. In addition, the challenges of conducting CPET in patients with mobility disorders have been observed by other groups [162].

Exercise training protocols that could potentially be applied across the age span were favored over protocols deemed too difficult for children. Of note, we considered blood flow restriction resistance training (BFR-RT) to be potentially feasible for in-person exercise training in PMD adults as well as in adolescents, based on published studies in physically inactive young adults, elderly individuals, healthy adults and various adult disease cohorts [163–169]. BFR-RT has been shown to elicit meaningful strength adaptations at low external loads, thereby potentially reducing mechanical stress and sarcopenia [146, 165, 168] in older adult populations with limited strength and exercise tolerance.

A total of 13 pediatric ( ≤ 17 years old) exercise training protocols were reviewed with 8/13 scoring at or above a threshold of 18 out of a possible 24 points (n = 8) (Alemdaroglu et al., 2015; Burns et al., 2017; Cho et al., 2016b; Lewelt et al., 2015; Montes et al., 2015; Schranz et al., 2018a and 2018b; Toscano et al., 2018) [82, 85, 151, 152, 154, 158, 160], see “Expert Scoring” column in Additional File 6: Table S5. This threshold of ≥18 points was selected based on the range of scores across the limited number of pediatric exercise training protocols, as compared to a threshold of ≥19 points in the adult protocols, outlined below. Two of 8 pediatric exercise training protocols were excluded due to the use of aerobic training on a cycle ergometer (Montes et al., 2015)[85] or treadmill (Cho et al., 2016b) [152] as part of a combination or aerobic protocol, respectively. The remaining 6 pediatric exercise training protocols (Alemdaroglu et al., 2015; Burns et al., 2017; Lewelt et al., 2015; Schranz et al., 2018a and 2018b; Toscano et al., 2018) [82, 151, 154, 158, 160] were appraised by expert group review, reaching consensus on the 4 top pediatric exercise training protocols considered to be feasible in PMD, Table 3.

Expert group consensus review to identify the top 4 exercise training protocols

Across all pediatric and adult studies, a total of 9 exercise training protocols, including 4 pediatric studies [82, 151, 154, 160] and 5 adult studies [78, 132, 146, 173, 174] were selected by expert group consensus. These studies were subsequently subjected to a second-tier expert group review for feasibility and likelihood of compliance in PMD, Table 3. Consistent with prior methods, exercise training protocols that could logistically be implemented across the age span of both adults and children with PMD to facilitate a comparison of the effects of similar exercise training protocols across a PMD cohort were prioritized.

To generate an unbiased consensus on the most relevant exercise training protocols in PMD, all 9 studies were independently ranked by two content experts (JF, EB), followed by second-tier expert group review (JF, EB, MM, JA, ZZC). Results of the quantitative-analysis, literature appraisal scores (IM), and clinical experience informed our expert ranking and group consensus. The exercise training protocols were ranked (from highest to lowest) in order of feasibility and deemed most clinically relevant in PMD, with a ranking of 1 being the most highly regarded exercise training protocol. Studies conducted in children and adults were ranked separately (1–4 and 1–5 respectively). Any disputes in rank order were resolved through further discussions and group consensus.

Group consensus identified the top 4 exercise training protocols predicted to be feasible in future PMD exercise trials, which included 1) high-intensity circuit training (HICT) [151], 2) low-intensity steady-state (LISS) exercise [82], 3) low-intensity resistance training [132], and 4) blood flow restriction resistance training (BFR-RT) [146]. Of note, most of these studies could not be included in the quantitative analysis, as their outcome measures did not meet criteria for inclusion, except for Yasuda et al., 2014 [146] that reported 1RM as an outcome measure.

The high-intensity circuit training (HICT) exercise training protocol by Schranz et al., 2018b [151] scored 19/24 points and was independently ranked the top pediatric study by both experts (JF, EB). This study was conducted in children with cerebral palsy and compared an 8-week HICT exercise training protocol involving 5 functional strength exercises at maximal intensity in short intervals of 30 s each, with 30 s breaks performed in a circuit (n = 11); to a more traditional progressive resistance training (PRT) program for 8 weeks in a separate cohort (n = 11). The HICT cohort reported a significant increase in compliance, muscle power, and strength while each training session was shorter (by an average of 28 min) compared to the PRT group. The HICT group also showed improvement in ADLs as measured by the parent-reported Pediatric Outcome Data Collection Instrument (PODCI) in subscores of Basic Mobility and Transfer score and Happiness score [151]. Further, the HICT exercise training protocol did not require any equipment, used simple functional exercises using body weight, and could be performed at home which may be of particular interest to PMD patients who may prefer to avoid busy gyms due to concern for acquiring infections. These characteristics were considered during consensus discussion, resulting in a high feasibility score for future implementation in children and adults with PMD, particularly individuals with symptoms of exercise intolerance, as the shorter training sessions would encourage compliance. Overall, this HICT protocol could potentially be feasible and benefit PMD children and adults with exercise intolerance and preference for shorter in-home training sessions, who are able to fully weight bear in the standing position and have ability to transfer into and out of various positions that require minimal flexibility and balance. However, systematic study of the safety, tolerability and efficacy of HICT exercise training in PMD cohorts, including across broader PMD genotypes, would be imperative.

The low-intensity steady-state (LISS) exercise training protocol by Alemdaroglu et al., 2015 [82] scored 19/24 points and was also ranked highly. This study enrolled a cohort of boys ages 8–12 years old with Duchenne muscular dystrophy using an upper body ergometer for 8 weeks, 3 times each week, at 50% maximum difficulty level for 30 min of active exercise with 5 min passive warm-up and passive cool down. Though no significant strength benefits were identified, the arm ergometry training group showed significantly improved North Star Ambulatory Assessment (NSAA) functional scores. This study was ranked highly by group consensus because it has potential to be home-based and enables inclusion of non-ambulatory PMD individuals who have not been able to participate in exercise trials thus far. The arm ergometer (Motomed) used in this study may not be widely affordable and accessible, however, more affordable arm ergometers that include active and passive exercise modes are readily available. In summary, this exercise training protocol, performed while seated, may be feasible in PMD children and adults who are non-ambulatory with significant balance deficits, muscle weakness, and exercise intolerance in whom moderate-intensity exercise would be difficult. Systematic study of LISS exercise training in PMD cohorts is required.

The low-intensity resistance exercise training protocol by Chetlin et al., 2004 [132] was ranked highly, and scored 19/24 points. This was a double blind, placebo-controlled study in adults with Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) (n = 10) that incorporated a 12-week, home-based resistance exercise program for arms and legs at varying resistance levels starting at 20% of maximal voluntary isometric strength (MVIS) for arms and 40% MVIS for legs, using adjustable wrist and ankle weights and gradually increasing repetitions and resistance levels, performing 3 sets of each exercise, 3 days each week. Of note, this study was an extension of a prior trial involving randomization of subjects to creatine supplementation with no observed effect of creatine on strength or activities of daily living (ADL) variables [132]. The Chetlin et al., 2004 study reported an increase in strength (maximal voluntary isometric strength, MVIS) and a significant improvement in ADLs, p < 0.001, post-exercise training. Assessments of functional ability measured in this study included rising from a chair without use of arms, rising from a supine position, ascending and descending steps, and a lift-and-reach test involving moving various weights to shelves of varying heights [132]. This low-intensity resistance exercise training protocol was considered feasible by group consensus for implementation in PMD individuals, as the study cohort reported high compliance rates and tolerance to the lower initiation weights (20% 1RM elbow, 40% 1RM knee) and lower repetitions (3 sets of 4 repetitions) with a slow, gradual increase in weights (up to 30% 1RM elbow, 50% 1RM knee) and repetitions (up to 3 sets of 10 repetitions) over the course of the 12-week program. The low-intensity resistance exercise training and gradual increase in intensity was considered by group experts to be likely well tolerated by older children and adults with PMD who commonly experience muscle pain, fatigue, and exercise intolerance [6]. In addition, some individuals with PMD have peripheral neuropathy, similar to individuals with CMT. This exercise protocol also targeted some of the muscles known to be weak in MM patients including elbow flexors in children and adults, and knee flexors in children [6]. Further, improvement in ADLs is a desired outcome for individuals with PMD [9] and exercise training protocols yielding functional benefits could lead to enhanced motivation and consistency of participation in a sustainable exercise program. In summary, this low-intensity resistance exercise training protocol to improve upper and lower extremity strength and ADLs may be feasible in older children and adults with PMD who commonly experience muscle pain, weakness, fatigue, as well as peripheral neuropathy. However, systematic study to assess the feasibility and efficacy of low-intensity resistance exercise protocols in PMD cohorts is needed.

Finally, the blood flow restriction resistance training (BFR-RT) protocol by Yasuda et al., 2014 [146] scored 20/24 points and was ranked in the middle, as this type of exercise is restricted to implementation in centers with the expertise and relevant equipment. Indeed, expert group consensus agreed that this exercise protocol should not be considered for at-home assessments in future PMD clinical studies. Three of the top five scored adult exercise training protocols included BFR-RT exercise training protocols. Yasuda et al., 2014 [146] studied healthy older men and women (mean age ± SD, 70 ± 6 years, n = 8) and involved 12 weeks of exercise training for two days each week performing knee extension at 20% 1RM and leg press exercises at 30% 1RM on machines with blood flow restriction using elastic cuffs worn on upper legs at 120–270 mmHg for 75 repetitions each exercise session, followed by 24 weeks of detraining. The control group performed the same exercises at high intensity of 75% 1RM and 30 repetitions each. Results showed increased muscle strength following 12 weeks of training with strength gains being well preserved following 24 weeks of detraining in the BFR-RT group. The protocol was scored as highly feasible in individuals with PMD, despite the need for in-person study visits, related to the use of low weights of 20–30% 1RM and lower exercise training frequency of two days each week. Individuals with PMD often have muscle weakness and muscle fatigue that may preclude safe and successful participation in traditional high-intensity resistance training using heavy weights. Resistance exercise training using a BFR-RT exercise protocol would enable lower loads and training frequency while potentially achieving similar muscle strength benefits gained from high-intensity resistance training [175], which has been shown to be preserved longer through periods of detraining [146]. Strength training two days weekly with lower weights would also allow ample rest time between exercise sessions rendering this protocol of great interest with the potential to encourage compliance in PMD cohorts. Hence, BFR-RT was scored highly by consensus due to its mechanistic appeal, as it has been shown to elicit meaningful strength adaptations at low external loads, while reducing mechanical stress and muscle fatigue in disease cohorts with limited exercise tolerance [118, 146]. Although no pediatric studies of BFR-RT were identified in our literature search, there is evidence that BRF-RT was safely performed by healthy adolescents (15.4 ± 1.61 years, n = 29) after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) and was well-tolerated with minor side effects [167]. Overall, this exercise training protocol could potentially be feasible in older adolescents and adults with PMD with muscle weakness and fatigue, specifically in those subjects unable to tolerate frequent exercise sessions. However, systematic study to assess the safety, tolerability and efficacy of BFR-RT exercise training in PMD cohorts would be prudent.

Initial assessment of PMD patient interest

We conducted a preliminary assessment of PMD patient interest in three of the top four exercise training protocols [82, 132, 151] on two children and two adults with PMD in our clinic. The BFR-RT exercise training protocol was not assessed as we were not able to administer this in a clinic setting. Our preliminary assessment did not involve data collection and was focused on evaluation of the ease of administration of each exercise training protocol by seeking direct feedback from the patients. Following one session of exercise, each of the four patients reported no concerns with any of the 3 protocols and indicated their interest to participate in a future exercise study of the respective exercise protocols. Further systematic study is required of these four expert-ranked exercise training protocols in PMD children and adults.

Discussion

Discussion of PMD exercise training protocols

Results overview

Our systematic literature search identified a total of 15 PMD and 62 SMD exercise studies. Our quantitative analysis included 11 PMD and 26 SMD studies, comprising 11 PMD and 39 SMD exercise training protocols, reporting the outcome measures deemed to be of interest.

Results of quantitative analysis across PMD studies demonstrated the significant effects of i) aerobic [120, 133, 134, 141] and a combination of resistance/interval/inspiratory muscle [24] exercise training on relative peak VO2, Fig. 2, Table 2; ii) aerobic [120, 133, 136, 140, 141] exercise training on peak work capacity, Fig. 6; and iii) aerobic [22, 91, 136] and a combination of resistance/interval/inspiratory muscle exercise training [24] on SF-36 physical summary scores, Fig. 11, Table 2. Interval exercise training was shown to be efficacious and well tolerated as part of a combination protocol in a PMD cohort [24], indicating potential utility in a future PMD exercise clinical trial. However, further systematic study to assess the potential safety, feasibility and efficacy of interval/HIIT exercise training in PMD is needed, as this was only a single study.

By comparison, Stefanetti et al., 2020, reported the significant improvement of exercise training on peak VO2, peak work and total SF-36 scores in a subgroup of metabolic myopathies that included Pompe, McArdle’s and MM studies [33]. Across all NMD studies that included over 35 different types of disorders including peripheral neuropathies, motor neuron disease and metabolic myopathies, subgroup analysis by exercise type revealed significant effect of aerobic exercise on peak VO2 (ml/min/kg, n = 19 studies), peak power (watts, n = 12 studies), and SF-36 (0–100, n = 4) (Extended data, Table 9, p 66–67); while strength exercise training revealed significant effect only for the sit to stand (STS) outcome measure (Extended data, Table 9, p 66) [33]. Of note, our study team (IM) was blinded to the PMD studies included in prior published reviews and meta-analyses [32, 33] during our literature search. We subsequently confirmed overlap in our identified PMD studies as compared to prior reviews [32, 33]; with several variations related to our outcome measure inclusion criteria [24, 176–178].

Thus, our quantitative results across 11 PMD studies (a total of 117 subjects, Additional File 2: Table S1) extends prior knowledge by reporting i) the significant effect of specific exercise types, or combination exercise, on clinically relevant outcome measures; and ii) the significant effect of interval/HIIT exercise training tested in a combination protocol in one single PMD study [24]. Collectively, these results highlight the efficacy of these distinct exercise types, and support the utility of relative peak VO2, peak work, and SF-36 as clinically meaningful outcome measures in future PMD exercise trials. However, we advise caution in the translation of these quantitative results to all PMD patients given the small number of published PMD exercise studies thus far, including a single study involving interval/HIIT exercise training [24]; with enrollment in most studies targeted to MM patients with symptoms predominantly in muscle. The effect of exercise in other PMD clinical phenotypes (e.g. Leigh syndrome) has not been studied.

Of note, exercise training revealed limited effect of exercise on peak blood lactate levels post-exercise in PMD studies (n = 4), with no significant change following aerobic training [134, 141], p = 0.36, and combination (aerobic/resistance, [23]) and (resistance/interval/inspiratory muscle, [24]) training, p = 0.5, Fig. 13. Similarly, the SMD studies in Fig. 14 (4 exercise training protocols across 3 studies), showed no significant change in post-exercise training plasma lactate values at the end of the study period, p = 0.46 [80, 83, 141]. Although only a small number of PMD studies were included, these results are consistent with current knowledge [179] and emphasize the lack of utility of plasma lactate as a biomarker of PMD in exercise studies. Indeed, lactate metabolism is complex and varies widely across physiological states, diseases, and interventions. Clinical measurement of plasma lactate is often highly variable in PMD and thus limits its utility as a meaningful biomarker in PMD exercise studies.

Muscle mitochondrial adaptation

Although the outcome measures of interest in this study included relative and absolute peak VO2, peak work capacity, lower body and upper body 1 repetition maximum (1RM) and SF-36 physical summary score; the ultimate goal of exercise training is to achieve mitochondrial adaptation in skeletal muscle that underlies improvements in muscle strength, endurance and physical performance.

Evidence to support muscle mitochondrial adaptation changes following exercise training derives from multiple experimental domains. Indeed, skeletal muscle has been shown to be highly adaptive and sensitive to exercise training based on evidence across cell lines, animal models and human studies demonstrating exercise training-induced muscle mitochondrial adaptation changes. These include i) mitochondrial biogenesis as shown in studies of C2C12 skeletal muscle myoblasts [180], wild-type (WT) Wistar rats [181, 182], p53 KO mouse muscle [183], and healthy human muscle [184, 185]; ii) satellite cell activation in healthy human muscle [186, 187], iii) improved mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) capacity demonstrated in mouse myotube cell lines [188], WT Wistar rats [19], and healthy human muscle [189]; and iv) increased protein translation in exercised healthy individuals [190]. Collectively, these adaptations are underpinned by coordinated upregulation of key mitochondrial regulatory pathways including PGC-1α, AMPK, and p38 MAPK, as reported in Wistar rat [181] and healthy human studies [184, 191, 192]. Further, exercise training intensity and/or volume (the amount of work conducted in each exercise session) have been shown to be important determinants of muscle mitochondrial OXPHOS capacity and citrate synthase activity in healthy human studies [193–195].

Specifically by exercise type, endurance exercise training has been reported to i) induce angiogenesis in WT rodents and healthy human studies [196], ii) induce mitochondrial biogenesis and increased citrate synthase activity in WT rodents [19–21, 182] and animal models including interleukin-6 KO mice [197] and the Opa1 haploinsufficient mouse model of PMD (Opa1+/-) [16]; and iii) enhance peak VO2 in mt-ND6P25L and mt-CO1V421A PMD mouse models [18]. Similarly, in healthy human studies, endurance exercise training enhanced cardiac output, increased peak VO2, and mitochondrial biogenesis [184, 198, 199].

By contrast, resistance exercise training primarily promotes increased muscle size and strength in healthy individuals [75, 200, 201], but has also been associated with important mitochondrial adaptations. Converging evidence from comprehensive reviews of animal and human studies indicates that resistance exercise training may promote mitochondrial biogenesis [185, 202], mediated in part by transcriptional regulators such as NRF-1 [198], however, available evidence suggests greater adaptation effects to resistance exercise in untrained or older individuals [203]. In addition, studies in WT animals and healthy humans have shown that resistance exercise induces satellite cell activation and skeletal muscle remodeling, processes that contribute to both structural and metabolic adaptation [186, 187, 204]. In healthy sedentary males (n = 8), Crameri et al., 2004, demonstrated that a single bout of high-intensity resistance exercise incorporating eccentric contractions significantly increased satellite cell number, supporting the role of mechanical loading, particularly eccentric stimuli, as a potent activator of satellite cell proliferation [186]. Similarly, BFR-RT exercise with low loads has also been shown to elicit an increase in satellite cells along with substantial myofiber hypertrophy, following short-term, low-load resistance exercise with partial blood flow restriction in healthy adults (n = 7-10) [205, 206].

It is important to note that these collective studies were not conducted in PMD individuals. Thus, the mechanistic underpinning of the effects of exercise on mitochondrial adaptation in PMD skeletal muscle to inform exercise training protocol development needs further study. There is limited evidence of increased muscle mtDNA content or citrate synthase [33, 120, 134, 135] and increased muscle electron transport chain (ETC) enzymatic activity [120, 136] after aerobic exercise training across a small number of PMD exercise studies. Maximal oxygen uptake (peak VO2) and peak work (watts) represent mitochondrial aerobic capacity. Indeed, PMD studies included in our quantitative analysis utilized both aerobic and combination exercises to significantly increase relative peak VO2 [24, 120, 133, 134, 141] and aerobic exercise to significantly increase peak work capacity [120, 133, 136, 140, 141].

Only a limited number of resistance exercise training protocols have been studied in PMD adults (n = 2) [131, 139], and none have been studied in children with PMD. Both adult PMD resistance exercise training protocols involved high-intensity/low-repetition training, a common but strenuous mode of resistance training. The likelihood of successful implementation of high intensity resistance training in individuals with PMD, especially in children, requires further study. Initial PMD studies have suggested that resistance exercise training elicits an increase in satellite cells, along with decreased mutant mtDNA heteroplasmy load [7, 131]. Murphy et al., 2008, studied the utility of high-intensity resistance training in lower extremities using leg extension and leg press exercises at 80–85% 1RM in 8 adult female subjects with a single, large-scale mtDNA deletion [131], reporting the number of satellite cells, as determined by neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM) immunoreactivity, to increase from 18.9 ± 13.2% pre-training to 42.3 ± 12.3 post-training (p = 0.003) [131]. Taivassalo et al., 1999 utilized a resistance exercise training protocol in one male adult subject with Kearns-Sayre Syndrome showing mtDNA heteroplasmy levels > 90% in skeletal muscle and reported significant improvements following concentric resistance training, including a 43.5% reduction in COX-negative fibers and a 33.4% increase in wild-type mtDNA, however no significant change in NCAM-positive satellite cells was observed [139]. Ultimately, these were early studies, and further work is needed to elucidate mitochondrial adaptation changes following resistance exercise in PMD.

Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018, utilized a unique approach to resistance training as part of a combination exercise training protocol that was reported to be well tolerated by the adult PMD subjects [24]. The authors used the Borg 1–10 scale of perceived exertion to determine initial load and subsequent rate of load increases. Individuals worked with loads corresponding to Borg levels 6–7 during the resistance phase of this training protocol, and loads were increased when Borg scores were < 6. The authors did not examine exercise training effect on mtDNA mutation load or satellite cells in muscle tissue.

Of note, none of the PMD aerobic exercise studies in our quantitative analysis assessed satellite cell activation following exercise training, however there have been reports of aerobic exercise causing satellite cell activation in sedentary adults and inactive middle-aged women [207, 208]. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to characterize the response of muscle mitochondrial adaptation(s) to distinct types of exercise in individuals with PMD.

Discussion of SMD exercise training protocols and feasibility assessment

Results overview

Quantitative analysis across SMD studies demonstrated significant effect of i) aerobic [79, 80, 84, 86, 87, 90, 141–143], interval [72, 80, 142, 144], and combination (aerobic/interval [88], aerobic/resistance [65, 85, 142], and resistance/interval [81]) exercise training on relative peak VO2, Fig. 3; ii) aerobic [66–69, 74, 141, 142] and interval [66, 68, 69, 142] exercise training on absolute peak VO2, Fig. 5; iii) aerobic [68, 79, 86, 87, 90, 142, 143], interval [68, 142], and combination (aerobic/interval [88], aerobic/resistance [142], and resistance/interval [81, 145]) on peak work, Fig. 7; iv) resistance exercise training on upper [75, 76] and lower body [75, 76, 146] 1RM, Figs. 8 and 10; and v) combination (aerobic/resistance) [70] exercise training on SF-36 scores, Fig. 12. Collectively, these results from SMD studies highlight the efficacy of distinct exercise types and suggests the additional potential utility of 1RM as a clinically meaningful outcome measures in future PMD exercise trials. However systematic study in PMD cohorts is needed.

SMD studies included in our quantitative analysis utilized both aerobic and interval/HIIT to significantly increase absolute peak VO2 [66–69, 74, 141, 142], relative peak VO2 [72, 79, 80, 84, 86, 87, 90, 141–144], and peak work capacity [68, 79, 86, 87, 90, 142, 143]. On initiation of our feasibility assessment, we hypothesized that HIIT exercise protocols may be feasible, tolerable, and efficacious in PMD patients, the primary advantage being the shorter exercise duration. Recent published reviews have similarly highlighted the benefit of HIIT in studies of healthy individuals and various disease groups [209, 210]. Indeed, our structured feasibility assessment deemed a high-intensity circuit training (HICT) protocol [151] that incorporated functional resistance exercise performed in short 30 s intervals of maximal intensity with periods of 90 s rest in between circuits, as one of the top four exercise training protocols and ranked first among the pediatric studies on feasibility assessment, scoring 19/24 points. Schranz et al, 2018 [151] reported a significant increase in compliance, muscle power, and strength after 8 weeks of HICT training involving shorter training sessions, as compared to 8 weeks of a more traditional progressive resistance training (PRT) in a separate pediatric cohort. Moreover, Ercin et al., 2020, also scoring highly on our feasibility assessment at 19/24, reported significantly lower scores on a modified-Borg dyspnea and leg fatigue scale in the interval exercise training group as compared to continuous-type (CT) and home-based exercise (HE) training protocols, in overweight and obese patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, n = 24), p < 0.05 [142]. Short exercise durations would be of clinical relevance for implementation in disease cohorts that are exercise intolerant, in whom personalized HIIT exercise training protocols may present as a feasible exercise option. However, there is a critical need for systematic study of interval/HIIT and HICT exercise training in PMD cohorts, to understand the potential safety, tolerability and efficacy of these exercise training protocols.

Results of our quantitative analysis demonstrated that resistance exercise training protocols across the SMD studies were efficacious in improving 1RM. Most of the SMD exercise training protocols started at lower standard percentages of 1RM (60–65%) and gradually increased to higher loads (75–80%), while the 1RM continued to be adjusted throughout the study period. Pyka et al., 1994, with the longest training duration of 1 year, demonstrated the greatest change in upper (Fig. 8) and lower body (Fig. 10) 1RM post-exercise training [75]. One particular exercise training protocol, Yasuda et al., 2014, used a significantly lower percentage (30%) of 1RM in the lower body (leg press) along with blood flow restriction elastic cuffs during exercise but yielded significant change in strength post-exercise training [146]. Further, strength gains were preserved after 24 weeks of detraining [146]. Notably, physiological adaptations following exercise training have been shown to revert to baseline on detraining [136, 146], which emphasizes the need for an effective and feasible exercise training protocol that can be sustained long-term. Thus, we were intrigued by the Yasuda et al., 2014 exercise training protocol [146] that could potentially allow individuals with PMD, who have symptoms of muscle weakness and fatigue, to train with lower weights while improving strength and limiting detraining effects. This was one of the top-scoring exercise protocols [146] in our feasibility assessment, to be considered for implementation in individuals with PMD. Notably, the study with the longest exercise training period of 52 weeks [75] resulted in the largest std. mean difference in upper and lower body 1RM post-resistance training in an SMD cohort. Thus, duration of exercise training should be considered on designing exercise training protocols to improve 1RM in PMD.

A low-intensity, resistance training with slow movement and tonic force generation (LST) exercise training protocol consisting of a 3 s eccentric, 1 s isometric, and 3 s concentric phase at 30% 1RM has been shown to elicit significant strength and muscle cross-sectional area gains in elderly individuals [78]. This LST exercise training protocol by Watanabe et al., 2014 [78], one of our top 9 exercise training protocols, reported a 100% compliance rate and may be more feasible in PMD individuals, as compared to the standard high-intensity low-repetition training typically administered to elicit strength gains. The authors demonstrated that very low-intensity resistance training with slow movement (30% 1RM) led to significant increase in quadriceps muscle cross-sectional area and knee extensor strength over 12 weeks, whereas matched low-intensity training performed at normal speed did not induce muscle hypertrophy [78]. Watanabe et al., 2014, proposed that LST exercise training could potentially stimulate myofibrillar protein synthesis resulting in muscle hypertrophy in these older adults [78].

Unique challenges of studying pediatric patients in exercise studies

There have been limited exercise studies conducted in children with PMD as compared to adults with PMD. In our quantitative analysis, no exercise training protocols were conducted in children with PMD. Studies involving an arm ergometer for aerobic exercise may be more feasible and could be applied to a larger spectrum of PMD patients including non-ambulatory and pediatric patients. One study in children with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Alemdaroglu et al., 2015) implemented a low-intensity steady-state (LISS) exercise with an arm ergometer and demonstrated improved functional ambulatory scores after 8 weeks [82]. This study was included in our feasibility assessment and was ranked highly among the top four exercise training protocols predicted to be feasible in PMD, as upper body ergometry would allow participation of non-ambulatory children and adults with PMD as well as those with poor balance and incoordination. Resistance exercise using ankle and wrist weights are also anticipated to be feasible in children with PMD, as shown by Burns et al., 2017 in a pediatric cohort with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease [160] that scored among the top 9 exercise protocols on our feasibility assessment (Table 3).

Ensuring compliance and consistency in younger cohorts can be challenging. The use of virtual reality in tandem with an exercise protocol has been shown to elicit significant improvements in exercise compliance and exercise-related results in children with cerebral palsy [152, 211]. Gamifying exercise protocols to maximize compliance in young study cohorts is an important aspect to be considered. It is also important that exercise protocols have flexibility to allow for progression and regression of exercise intensity and volume to maximize compliance in the individual.

Limitations of this study

This quantitative review is subject to limitations related to the literature search and study inclusion strategy, including reliance on PubMed and Google Scholar and a pragmatic single-reviewer screening process, which despite mitigation efforts, may have introduced potential selection and reviewer bias. Further, inclusion of additional databases including Embase/Emcare or CINAHL may have identified additional relevant studies, particularly from allied health, rehabilitation, or nursing-focused journals. Although use of PubMed and Google Scholar may have excluded some studies, the search for PMD exercise trials can be considered largely exhaustive given the limited number of PMD-specific studies available to date and verified overlap in publications as identified by Stefanetti et al., 2020, an earlier published meta-analysis that included PMD exercise studies [33], and a Cochrane review by Voet et al., 2019 [32]. Publication bias is also likely, as exercise trials with non-significant findings are less frequently published, favoring studies reporting statistically significant results. Additional limitations include heterogeneity in study design, populations, and outcome reporting, particularly across PMD and SMD cohorts. Genetic etiologies of PMD cohorts in previously published exercise studies should be reviewed with caution as variant and gene classifications may change over time [212, 213]. A key example of this is noted with MT-TC m.5814 T > C, previously classified as pathogenic [137] and has since been downgraded to likely benign (ClinVar Variation ID: 3000) due to its high frequency in the general population. Furthermore, as patient cohort characterization reported in most of the published PMD studies was primarily real-world data, genetic diagnostic testing may not have been widely available at the time of publication. As a result, some PMD studies enrolled subjects who were determined to have a PMD diagnosis based on clinical, biochemical and/or histopathological findings, Additional File 2: Table S1.

A specific challenge of studying the effects of exercise in PMD is the well-known broad clinical heterogeneity across PMD cohorts where there is great variation in phenotypic manifestations including disease onset, severity, and trajectory over time, even within affected kindreds [214]. Where clinical descriptions were available in the PMD studies that were identified in our literature search, most subjects were reported to be symptomatic of muscle symptoms (Additional File 2: Table S1), however, complete clinical descriptions were not available in some studies. Further, on review of the genetic etiologies across the individuals included in the PMD studies (Additional File 2: Table S1), the most common etiologies were in mtDNA, including MT-TL1 and single large scale mtDNA deletion (SLSMD). Thus, given the known genetic and clinical heterogeneity of PMD, we caution against translation of the results of our quantitative analysis across 11 PMD studies comprising 117 PMD subjects to the broader PMD population.

Indeed, variable response to exercise and safety concerns have been proposed in published pre-clinical studies of mouse models of PMD, including mt-ND6P25L and mt-CO1V421A, with specific safety concerns related to the effects of endurance exercise on cardiomyopathy in ANT1-/- mice [18]; and exacerbation of muscle pathology by endurance swimming exercise in a mouse model with a single, large-scale mtDNA deletion and severe phenotype [215]. Importantly, the high prevalence of cardiac involvement and neurologic impairment including stroke-like episodes (SLEs) across PMD cohorts [133, 216–220] warrants specific safety precautions and personalized, multi-system risk assessment in each individual with PMD before consideration of exercise training.

Further, understanding of the physiologic adaptive responses to exercise training in PMD patients without predominant muscle symptoms is lacking. Future systematic studies of large PMD cohorts with broad clinical phenotypes and varying levels of severity, and representation across various genetic etiologies to enable subgroup comparisons would be critical. In addition, published PMD exercise studies may have been biased toward enrollment of individuals with muscle-predominant phenotypes with sufficient coordination, cognitive capacity, and strength to enable participation in structured exercise interventions; limiting generalizability and translation of the results of our quantitative analysis and feasibility assessment to the broader PMD patient population, including more severely affected PMD individuals with Leigh syndrome, and/or non-ambulatory PMD individuals.

In this study, we included published exercise studies in SMD cohorts to raise the possibility of observing similar findings in PMD cohorts. We caution against direct translation of findings from the SMD studies in our quantitative analyses and feasibility assessment to PMD patients without further systematic study. It is worth noting that our literature search in SMD disorders did not identify any yoga protocols, therefore was not included in our quantitative analysis and feasibility evaluation. Yoga has not been studied in PMD cohorts thus far, but would be of interest as an 8-week yoga program has been associated with improved health assessment questionnaire disability index (HAQ-DI), significant reduction in whole blood reactive oxygen species (ROS), significant increase in total antioxidant capacity (TAC), and reduced rheumatoid arthritis disease activity [221].

There was a lack of functional-based outcome measures, such as the 6 min walk test, utilized across most published studies. Indeed, a future exercise trial in PMD should include pertinent functional and disease specific outcome measures such as the Mitochondrial Myopathy Composite Assessment Tool (MM-COAST) that assesses muscle strength, muscle fatigue, exercise intolerance, balance and dexterity that has been validated in MM [6], with natural history data to support the observation of improvement following exercise training. Further, the use of more accessible outcome measures would facilitate broader participation of institutions that are lacking the expertise and resource infrastructure to incorporate the use of a cardiology lab required for CPET-VO2 measurements in clinical exercise trials. Alternatively, pooling expertise by centralizing CPET assessment analyses in a multi-center exercise clinical trial could be a potential solution.

The exclusion of cycle ergometry- and treadmill-based aerobic exercise training protocols (a total of 37, comprising 2 pediatric and 35 adult exercise protocols) in the feasibility assessment may be viewed as a limitation, since these modalities have been shown to be efficacious in prior PMD and SMD exercise studies as described in our quantitative analysis. In addition, these modalities are routinely utilized in both research and clinical settings. However, our clinical experience of cycle ergometry- and treadmill-based exercise in CPET is that feasibility is limited to approximately only 20% of our PMD clinic cohort. Muscle weakness, fatigue and imbalance, impaired hearing and vision, and short stature are among the common barriers to safe and effective completion of leg cycle ergometry- and treadmill-based exercise. Similar limitations of CPET related to the physical capacity of patients, such as mobility disorders; or intolerance of the mask used in the test have been described [162]. The ATS/ACCP Statement recommends that patients unable to exercise due to neurological or orthopedic problems should not undergo CPET [161]. Hence, in order to prioritize identification of newer exercise protocols not previously studied in PMD, we made a pragmatic decision to exclude cycle ergometry- and treadmill-based exercise protocols in our feasibility assessment.

Limitations were also encountered on creation of the forest plots (Figs. 2–14). Resistance exercise training protocols used different exercises or machines to test for 1RM, which presents a limitation in the ability to accurately compare 1RM across the studies. The resistance exercise training protocols included upper (bench press) and lower body (leg press) movements to provide some standardization. Plotting peak VO2 across studies also presented some limitations, as studies varied on their use of absolute peak VO2 (L/min) and relative peak VO2 (ml/kg/min). Some studies did not report the subjects’ average body weight, which did not allow for conversion between absolute and relative peak VO2 [136]. Variability in equipment (e.g., cycle ergometer, treadmill, and resistance machine) and exercise training protocols implemented in each study limited the ability to standardize the results and contributed to a high level of heterogeneity across all studies. Indeed, in a multi-center exercise clinical trial, it would be critical that equipment, expertise, and exercise training protocols are standardized across centers in multi-center studies.

PMD patient motivation to exercise

It is possible that subject motivation to participate in exercise studies may differ to the motivation to enroll in a therapeutic drug intervention trial due to perceived benefit of receiving drug treatment [222–224]. However, it is worth noting that we routinely observe our clinic PMD patients to be highly motivated to participate in exercise. This is reflected in a single-center qualitative study involving standardized semi-structured patient interviews [9]. Individuals with MM were asked to describe how symptoms impacted their daily life. One subject with exercise intolerance responded, “I am very disappointed I can’t exercise like normal people ….it really affects my quality of life that I can’t do normal exercise.” Another subject stated, “If I could go walk around the block outside instead of holding onto the treadmill, that would really be wonderful [9].”

Agreements and disagreements with other studies or reviews

Overall, there was broad agreement across existing literature [7, 8, 25, 26, 32, 33] of the benefits of exercise in PMD cohorts. Thus far, there remains a lack of consensus regarding optimal exercise training protocols for standard of care use in individuals with PMD. Our evidence synthesis and quantitative review adds to the current literature by highlighting the benefits of distinct exercise types in PMD, including raising the potential for future implementation of interval exercise training in PMD.

Considerations for safety extrapolated from the published literature

Recent studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of BFR-RT on muscle strength gains [169, 225–227]. BRF-RT allows for lower-intensity resistance training that is generally safe and well tolerated in healthy individuals, elderly, musculoskeletal disorders, and various disease groups [163–166], however exercise training volume and intensity, amount of restriction time, cuff pressure, and cuff width [163, 228, 229] should be considered in the exercise training protocol design. BFR-RT exercise with low load resistance training was highly scored for feasibility in our assessment as it provides a potentially effective alternative to high-intensity resistance exercise training in PMD. Nevertheless, BFR-RT has not been studied in PMD individuals, therefore we highlight the imperative need for systematic studies in order to assess the safety, tolerability and efficacy of BFR-RT in PMD. BFR-RT should be limited to closely supervised clinical or research settings following individualized risk assessment with gradual increases in cuff pressure and oversight by experienced clinicians in BFR-RT [163, 164, 166, 229].

A systematic review by Leite et al., 2023, reported that HIIT exercise training protocols were found to elevate markers of exercise-induced muscle damage, including creatine kinase (CK), myoglobin (Mb) and muscle circumference; immediately and 24 h after a HIIT session in healthy adult cohorts, most of whom were trained athletes [230]. However, in SMD cohorts including overweight/obese adults with COPD [142] and adult men with spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA) [157], HIIT was found to be more tolerable compared to continuous training (CT) since HIIT was associated with less dyspnea and leg fatigue [142] and no change in plasma CK levels or self-reported muscle pain or muscle fatigue after exercise training was reported [157]. The different responses may be explained in part by the lower level of exercise intensity achieved by individuals with SMD, which is likely much lower as compared to healthy individuals and athletes. Fiuza-Luces et al., 2018 utilized a gradual implementation of HIIT in a combination protocol in a PMD cohort where the intensity level started low and was gradually and individually increased to higher intensities and reported to be safe, with excellent compliance rates [24], demonstrating that safe implementation of HIIT protocols in PMD is possible. However, this is the first HIIT protocol to be evaluated in PMD subjects (n = 12). Individuals with PMD, who may be deconditioned at baseline likely need to gradually increase exercise intensity to achieve targeted higher intensities safely, with close clinical monitoring and guidance. Larger cohort systematic study of the safety, feasibility and efficacy of HIIT exercise training protocols in PMD is required including across broader PMD genotypes.

In PMD, cardiac involvement is commonly observed [133, 217, 218, 231, 232]. This underscores the importance of personalized exercise prescriptions, ideally initiated under supervised conditions following multisystemic clinical evaluations including longitudinal cardiology assessments to ensure safety and tolerability [28]. Of note, Bates et al., 2013, demonstrated that endurance exercise training involving 30 min of cycling on an upright ergometer 3x/week for 16 weeks in patients with m.3243 A > G-related PMD was beneficial based on significant improvement in peak VO2 (Fig. 2), and was deemed safe with no deleterious effects on cardiac structure and function, as evidenced by lack of significant effect on peak heart rate and blood pressure, cardiac volumes, global systolic or diastolic function, and myocardial strains or torsion on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) when compared before and after exercise training [133]. However, this was a small PMD cohort (n = 10) study that specifically excluded individuals with known cardiac involvement, hence the potential risks of exercise training in PMD individuals with cardiac involvement is not yet understood. Similar concerns apply to PMD individuals with moderate to severe neurologic involvement (e.g. SLEs, seizures), requiring careful consideration.

In their review of aerobic, resistance and combined aerobic/resistance exercise studies, Stefanetti et al., 2020, concluded that no safety concerns were reported across the NMD studies, including a small number of MM studies (n = 16) [33].

Public Health Implications of Modest Physical Activity and Lifestyle Improvements

Across large prospective adult cohorts and meta-analysis datasets, studies consistently demonstrate that small, achievable improvements in physical activity and related lifestyle behaviors confer substantial survival benefits. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Yu et al., 2025 examined associations between physical activity trajectories across adulthood and mortality outcomes [233]. Across 85 prospective cohort studies, individuals who remained active or increased physical activity over time had 20–40% lower all-cause and 30–40% lower cardiovascular mortality compared with inactive individuals [233]. Adhering to physical activity guidelines over time or increasing activity volumes to meet the guidelines was associated with reductions in mortality risks, but remaining active or increasing physical activity below the guidelines could also bring appreciable health benefits for individuals who were inactive [233]. In a meta-analysis, Ekelund et al., 2026 showed that under hypothetical high-risk and population-based intervention scenarios, even small increases ( + 5 min/day) in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity could prevent ~ 6 and 10% of deaths, respectively, when targeting the least active group; and reducing sedentary time by 30 min/day, could prevent a smaller but still meaningful (3–7%) proportion of deaths in the two risk scenarios [234]. Further, in a large UK Biobank adult cohort study, Koemel et al., 2025 showed that while individual sleep, physical activity and nutrition (SPAN) behaviors required substantial amounts to achieve improvements in lifespan and healthspan, when addressed in combinations, the overall dose needed for meaningful improvements was substantially lower [235]. Compared to the 5th percentile of all SPAN behaviors, a minimum combined dose of an additional 5 min/day of sleep, 1.9 min/day of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and 5 diet quality score items (DQS) (e.g., 1/2 serving of vegetables per day or additional 1.5 servings of whole grains per day) was associated with one additional year of lifespan [235]. Larger theoretical changes in SPAN behaviors were associated with statistically significant improvements in healthspan, i.e., an additional 24 min/day of sleep, 3.7 min/day of MVPA and an improvement of 23.0 DQS points (e.g., an additional one cup of vegetables per day, one serving of whole grains per day, and two servings of fish per week) were associated with 4 additional years of healthspan [235]. Notably, small, achievable changes in integrated lifestyle interventions were associated with benefits comparable to much larger changes in a single behavior [235].

Conclusions

Results of our appraisal and feasibility assessment across 109 clinical exercise training protocols revealed a total of four exercise training protocols evaluated in SMD cohorts that were deemed by expert group consensus to be of great interest and clinical relevance for future PMD exercise studies, including high-intensity circuit training [151], low-intensity steady-state training (LISS) [82], low-intensity resistance training [132], and blood flow restriction resistance training (BFR-RT) [146] exercise protocols. These specific exercise training protocols merit further systematic assessment in robust clinical studies to determine their broad feasibility and therapeutic efficacy in children and adults with PMD. Indeed, larger clinical studies of PMD to establish tolerable, safe, efficacious and sustainable exercise regimens need to be performed. Thus, caution must be used in recommending specific exercise regimens to PMD patients until further systematic studies are conducted.

In designing exercise interventions for PMD, it is essential to balance physiological response to exercise with safety, feasibility, and long-term adherence; with consideration of the heterogeneity of PMD clinical phenotype and severity. Personalized exercise prescriptions, ideally initiated under supervised conditions following multisystemic clinical evaluations including longitudinal cardiology assessments, and progressive transition to home-based implementation with slow progression in exercise intensity or frequency may enhance safety, promote confidence, and optimize participation. If effectively implemented and sustained, exercise may play a potential role as a disease modifying intervention and preserve functional capability and quality of life in individuals with PMD.
