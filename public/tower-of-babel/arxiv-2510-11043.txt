Zephyrus: Scaling Gateways Beyond the Petabit-Era with DPU-Augmented Hierarchical Co-Offloading
Yuemeng Xu, Haoran Chen, Jiarui Guo, Mingwei Cui, Qiuheng Yin, Cheng Dong, Daxiang Kang, Xian Wu, Chenmin Sun, Peng He, Yang Gao, Lirong Lai, Kai Wang, Hongyu Wu, Tong Yang, Xiyun Xu

Abstract
Operating at petabit-scale, ByteDance’s cloud gateways are deployed at critical aggregation points to orchestrate a wide array of business traffic. However, this massive scale imposes significant resource pressure on our previous-generation cloud gateways, rendering them unsustainable in the face of ever-growing cloud-network traffic. As the DPU market rapidly expands, we see a promising path to meet our escalating business traffic demands by integrating DPUs with our established Tofino-based gateways. DPUs augment these gateways with substantially larger table capacities and richer programmability without compromising previously low-latency and high-throughput forwarding. Despite compelling advantages, the practical integration of DPUs into cloud gateways remains unexplored, primarily due to underlying challenges. In this paper, we present Zephyrus, a production-scale gateway built upon a unified P4 pipeline spanning high-performance Tofino and feature-rich DPUs, which successfully overcomes these challenges. We further introduce a hierarchical co-offloading architecture (HLCO) to orchestrate traffic flow within this heterogeneous gateway, achieving > 99 % >99\% hardware offloading while retaining software fallback paths for complex operations. Zephyrus outperforms LuoShen (NSDI ’24) with 33% higher throughput and our evaluation further indicates 21% lower power consumption and 14% lower hardware cost. Against FPGA-based systems, Albatross (SIGCOMM ’25), it doubles the throughput at a substantially lower Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), showcasing its superior performance-per-dollar. Beyond these performance gains, we also share key lessons from several years of developing and operating Zephyrus at production scale. We believe these insights provide valuable references for researchers and practitioners designing performant cloud gateways.

Design goals of our cloud gateway.

Driven by these characteristics, we crystallize three fundamental design goals for our cloud gateway:

•

Low latency forwarding.
To directly satisfy latency-sensitive traffic demands, the cloud gateway must minimize end-to-end transmission delays to ensure that these applications consistently meet user experience requirements. This goal is critically challenging in cloud networks due to deep topologies inherent to hyperscale deployments, where the co-existence of high throughput and million-flow tables compounds latency-critical path conflicts.

•

High throughput and large table support.
To sustain such high throughput in ByteDance’s datacenters, the cloud gateway must deliver high-throughput packet forwarding and processing, while simultaneously maintaining massive routing and policy tables to support tens of thousands of tenants in a multi-tenant environment.
Meeting both requirements simultaneously is challenging: petabit-per-second aggreate traffic places significant demands on gateway bandwidth, whereas large tables impose heavy pressure on memory resources.

•

Cost efficiency.
At Petabit scale, even minor inefficiencies quickly translate into significant total cost. Cost efficiency necessitates co-optimizing procurement (CapEx), operational expenses (OpEx), and development velocity through judicious selection of forwarding components and architectures for minimal TCO. Because real-world traffic exhibits highly skewed flow size distributions and the required packet processing and forwarding logic is fundamentally simple,
hardware caching and offloading represent a strategically sound design choice for cost efficiency. However, realizing hardware caching and offloading while maintaining flexibility and sufficient resources remains a non-trivial challenge.

2.2
Evolution towards Zephyrus

The Volcengine cloud gateway has experienced two major production-scale iterations. The evolution of Zephyrus is a story of systematically identifying and overcoming architectural bottlenecks. We first moved from a flexible but slow software gateway to a performant switching ASIC-based design(with x86 software assistance). However, new bottlenecks soon emerged, particularly the limited on-chip resources of switching ASICs.
These constraints ultimately motivated our transition to a heterogeneous compositional architecture,
which fundamentally overcomes the limitations of previous generation gateways and simultaneously achieves low latency, high throughput, rapid development, and cost efficiency.

The first generation: Software-only cloud gateways.
Our initial cloud gateway adopted a fully software-based architecture, which is flexible and versatile but ultimately limited in performance and scalability

While a default per-attachment cap (e.g., 5 Gbps) effectively contained noisy neighbors, this rigid approach inherently conflicted with east-west traffic demands, resulting in non-negligible packet drops and application degradation. These challenges are evidence that general-purpose CPUs are ill-suited for the simple, repetitive work of line-rate packet processing. Although initially flexible, a software-only gateway presents a fundamental barrier to achieving scalable, high-performance operation.

Towards the third generation: hybrid programmable gateways based on heterogeneous hardware.

The limitations of previous generations motivate the need for a new class of hardware that bridges the gap between CPUs and Tofino ASICs.
Rather than replacing existing components, this hardware is designed to work in concert with Tofino and software, forming an integrated next-generation gateway. Such hardware must:

1.

Provide abundant on-chip resources for table entries.

2.

Maintain programmability and flexibility.

3.

Achieve competitive throughput and latency.

We conducted extensive analysis and experimentation to evaluate two potential options that could meet these requirements: FPGAs and DPUs.
We ultimately selected DPUs, as they offer several advantages over FPGAs in practice:

•

Lower power consumption and cost.
Cloud gateways with DPUs consume about 21% less power and reduce overall procurement cost by 14% compared to FPGA-integrated designs.
When scaled to large deployments, these savings translate directly into lower CapEx and OpEx through reduced hardware spend and energy usage.

•

Lower development complexity and faster deployment.

Compared to FPGAs, DPUs are significantly easier to develop with. DPU programming typically relies on high-level languages such as C/C++ and P4, supported by mature software ecosystems and toolchains

While DPUs provide compelling benefits, deploying them in coordination with switching ASICs in cloud gateways is a non-trivial task.
With no prior blueprint to follow, our design of Zephyrus required rethinking the gateway architecture from the ground up.
We systematically addressed these challenges by establishing guiding principles.

3
Zephyrus’s Architecture

This section first establishes the guiding principles for our design and then presents the architecture of Zephyrus. Detailed processing logic and optimization techniques are deferred to Section
4

3.1
Guiding Principles

To navigate the complexity of the system, we established a set of guiding principles before our design.

Execute each processing stage on the most suitable hardware.
The division of labor between switching ASICs and DPUs is informed by their intrinsic hardware characteristics. Switching ASICs are tasked with high-throughput, low-complexity processing; they can accommodate millions of simple table entries and process them with ultralow latency. Moreover, the relatively ample TCAM resources on switching ASICs motivate us to perform LPM matching on the ASICs, while delegating exact matches to DPUs.

Hierarchical offloading architecture.
Hierarchical offloading is crucial in heterogeneous systems, as components exhibit an inherent programmability–performance gradient: switching ASICs offer the least programmability but the highest performance, DPU pipelines sit in the middle, and software offers the most programmability but the lowest performance. This naturally extends the conventional two-tier fast/ slow path model to a three-tier processing hierarchy: the ASIC pipeline (Tier 1), the embedded pipeline of the DPU (Tier 2) and the general-purpose ARM cores of the DPU (Tier 3). This structure recursively applies the fast/slow path paradigm: Switching ASIC and DPU pipelines form layered, high-speed mechanisms, while ARM cores provide the ultimate policy decisions.

4
Compositional P4 Pipeline Design

Beyond fundamental cloud gateway functions (e.g., ACL, NAT, AZ affinity, mirroring, and metering), we developed targeted optimizations to improve performance. In this section, we first describe the detailed architecture of the unified P4 pipeline, followed by detailed packet processing, then present key mechanisms enabling efficient, consistent, and scalable packet processing in cloud gateways.

4.1
Detailed Pipeline Organization

Figure
4
shows the pipeline organization in detail; protocol and control packets are diverted at ingress pipe 3, as indicated by the red dashed arrows. Given the limited resources of a single Tofino pipeline, we evaluated the required memory footprint and fine-grained pipeline-stage count for the major tables (e.g., interface tables, metering tables, routing and policy-routing tables, and next-hop determination tables) and packet-processing logic, concluding that four independent Tofino pipelines are needed to complete the tasks. To this end, we apply pipeline folding to assemble the four independent pipelines into a single folded pipeline that traffic traverses. After folding, we quadruple the available Tofino ASIC resources at the cost of reducing throughput from 6.4 to 1.6 Tbps (4× reduction). We then employ four 400 Gbps embedded DPUs in parallel to match the 1.6 Tbps line rate of the folded pipeline, using ECMP to distribute traffic across the DPUs.

We also evaluated an alternative design where the DPUs are placed serially after the entire folded Tofino pipeline. Although this arrangement might appear to simplify management, it introduces two critical drawbacks. First and most fundamentally, it misaligns the roles of the hardware. This design forces DPUs to handle line-rate forwarding, a task for which they are not optimized. DPUs excel at offloading complex operational tasks (e.g., encryption, deep packet inspection), not replacing the raw forwarding power of a switching ASIC
[
51
]
.
Second, the alternative design suffers from a severe bandwidth penalty. This forces a hard partitioning of the front-panel interfaces that half of the ports to be consumed by the internal ASIC-DPU connection and the other half for external connectivity. Consequently, the bandwidth is halved from 1.6 Tbps to 800 Gbps. The key advantage of the original design is that traffic enters and exits the DPU from the same Tofino pipeline, achieving full-duplex communication without bandwidth loss, and it also places the DPU exactly where complex operations are needed. By comparison, LuoShen sacrifices a quarter of its external ports to connect with the CPU and FPGA
[
4
]
, while the current design avoids any bandwidth loss by allowing data to enter and exit the DPU from the same Tofino pipeline.
Therefore, we adopt the three-stage "sandwich" architecture for our design.

4.2
Packet Processing across Heterogeneous Hardware

Guided by our
Principles
discussed in Section 3, our design strategically maps different table types to the hardware components that are best suited for them. Given the scarcity of TCAM on DPUs but their abundant SRAM and DRAM, we place large-scale exact-match tables (e.g., for VM-destined traffic) on them. In contrast, Tofinos are equipped with TCAM, which is particularly well-suited for longest-prefix match (LPM). For protocol and control packets, DPU involvement is unnecessary. Allowing them to traverse the whole pipeline not only lengthens the processing path but also introduces potential risks to stability. To address this, we divert such traffic at the ingress pipe 3, bypassing the DPU and forwarding directly to the CPU. Prior to CPU handoff, essential protocol-specific handling (e.g., BGP processing) is performed at the ingress stage, ensuring correctness since certain BGP functions depend on table lookups. This selective diversion avoids unnecessary traversal of heterogeneous hardware, reduces latency, and guarantees that control-plane traffic follows the shortest and most stable path.

Table lookups in Tofino pipelines.
As illustrated in Figure
5
, Tofino executes packet processing as a sequence of table lookups. Incoming packets first look up a traffic classification table, which recognizes two categories of packets: VXLAN-encapsulated tenant traffic (identified by remote VTEP’s physical IP) and protocol/control packets. Tenant traffic passes through a tenant identification table that determines the policy routing table to look up. At this point, the system translates the VXLAN Network Identifier (VNI), a generic overlay identifier, into a policy routing identifier, which selects the appropriate virtual routing context. This translation is key to achieving resource isolation, since each tenant is associated with a dedicated policy routing table.

After the tenant context is established, packets proceed to the policy routing table, which supports multiple routing instances as commonly deployed in public cloud environments. Within a single tenant (i.e., the same VNI), multiple subnets or security domains may coexist. Consequently, the inner virtual source IP address is used to select the appropriate routing instance. Packets then enter the routing table, which performs a lookup on the inner destination IP address to obtain either a nexthop index or an ECMP group index. ECMP selects a nexthop based on the five-tuple hash, providing load balancing across multiple equal-cost paths. Finally, the nexthop table provides the precise forwarding information, including the output port and encapsulation.

Packet processing in DPUs.

Once a packet enters the DPU, it first traverses our P4-programmable fast path. The programmable pipelines, p4Ingress and p4Egress, implement flow and action logic across eight-stage match–action (MA) tables. These stages cover packet parsing, classification, flow lookup, and forwarding decisions. Fixed-function modules, including P4RxDMA and P4TxDMA, handle packet DMA operations and integrate vendor-customized lookup.

As illustrated in Figure 6, for cache miss or failed version check (expired packets, detailed in 4.3), we redirect them to the slow path, where the ARM cores running the DPDK-based gateway program reexecute policy enforcement, ACL checks, and nexthop computation. The results are then synchronized back into the fast path, ensuring that subsequent packets of the same flow can be processed entirely in hardware.

Table lookups in DPUs.

When a packet enters the DPU pipeline, it first performs a lookup in the Flow Table, which primarily returns the nexthop index (pointing to the precise nexthop entry in Tofino at the post-DPU stage) and the version number (detailed in 4.3). The VM–NC (VM-to-Node Control) table maintains the mapping between virtual machines and their hosting nodes, allowing the system to efficiently determine the physical host associated with a given VM. Since the VM–NC table is the largest lookup structure and previously caused localized hotspots in Tofino, it has been relocated to DPU DRAM. On the software slow path, the system leverages the routing table identifier embedded in the packet metadata (obtained during the Tofino pre-DPU stage) as an index to selectively retrieve nexthop and VM–NC entries as needed.

4.3
Mechanisms for Consistent and Efficient Heterogeneous P4 Pipeline

Hash-table coalescing on DPUs.
On DPUs, each additional hash table introduces extra processing logic, including key extraction, hash computation, memory lookup, and action execution. DPUs provide only limited TCAM capacity, which is insufficient for efficient longest-prefix matching (LPM). As a result, they primarily depend on SRAM/DRAM with hash indexing, favoring exact-match lookups but performing poorly on LPM. As the number of tables grows, the pipeline accumulates more control branches, forcing the DPU to schedule a larger set of action executions and then amplifying processing overhead, so that we need to try our best to coalesce our hash table. We observe that due to the specific traffic characteristics of cloud gateways, instances of multiple matches in longest-prefix matching (LPM) are rare.
When transforming LPM into exact-match lookups, each candidate prefix must be probed in sequence, and each probe requires a base hash table and an overflow hash structure to handle collisions. In order to mitigate overhead, we perform collision checks in the control plane prior to rule installation. If no conflicts are detected, the entries are consolidated into a single table. Furthermore, we can guide user configurations to better align with our hash-coalescing scheme. In the absence of multiple matches, hash-table coalescing substantially reduces both processing complexity and resource consumption.

Minimal yet expressive metadata for communication.
Since our DPU provides only eight P4 pipeline stages, we delegate the identification lookups to Tofino pipelines and carry the result to the DPU through metadata. We carefully design the metadata structure (illustrated in Figure
6
) so that it conveys only essential information. To simplify processing, we align the header length for packets sent from egress pipe 2 to DPUs and for packets returned from DPUs to ingress pipe 2, avoiding the need for DPDK to repeatedly insert or remove headers. For the private protocol design, our initial format was Ethernet + DPU key + user Ethernet, but this proved cumbersome for each heterogeneous component to process. We later switched to Ethernet + private key + user payload, reusing the original Ethernet header and restoring it only when the packet leaves the system.

Version-based flow update mechanism.
Flow updates are particularly challenging in heterogeneous hardware environments. Forwarding rules are distributed across Tofino ASICs, DPUs, and CPUs, each with distinct data structures and update mechanisms. Synchronizing these views consistently is non-trivial, especially given the mismatch in update timescales. Our solution associates each instance with a unique service ID, assigned in the Tofino ASIC and carried in the metadata to the DPU pipeline. Each service object is also tagged with a version number that reflects the latest configuration. Upon reconfiguration, the controller in control plane updates the version number. As illustrated in Figure
6
, the DPU pipeline validates each flow by comparing its version with the current service version; a mismatch redirects the packet to the slow path for recomputation. This design provides a lightweight yet effective mechanism for maintaining consistency during flow updates, requiring only a version check in the fast path.

In recent years, Data Processing Units (DPUs) have rapidly emerged as a key enabler for cloud and datacenter infrastructure. Recent work highlights their diverse capabilities, including storage offloading

[

In the context of cloud gateways, previous state-of-the-art works primarily focus on using FPGAs

[

[
3

In contrast, as a centralized gateway, Zephyrus adopts DPUs to augment the Tofino pipeline. This design significantly expands effective table capacity, overcoming the resource shortage limitations in architectures such as Sailfish and Luoshen. Meanwhile, Zephyrus’s three-stage "sandwich" architecture improves its throughput to 1.6 Tbps, a 33% improvement over Luoshen and a 100% improvement over Albatross. To overcome memory limitations, Albatross, which is based on FPGA, suffers a significant performance regression in throughput (4x), packet rate (18x) and latency (10x) compared to Tofino-based gateways. By comparison, Zephyrus uses a DPU-augmented architecture to overcome resource limitations; by hierarchically co-offloading over 99% of packets to hardware, it achieves only a slight performance loss compared to Tofino-based gateways.

Programming these disparate hardware targets requires specialized teams and fragmented toolchains, leading to long and costly development cycles. We introduce a unified P4 pipeline to accelerate development cycles

7
Experiences

7.1
Development and Deployment Experiences

Heterogeneous Hardware Development.

Managing a heterogeneous data plane presents significant operational challenges. To address these, first, we implemented a flexible packet distribution strategy. By default, hash-based routing balances traffic between the Tofino switch and the DPU processing nodes. However, recognizing that hashing can suffer from traffic skew, we provide a dynamic one-to-one mapping mode to deterministically route specific flows, mitigating hotspots and preventing node overload.
Second, to provide fine-grained visibility into this complex pipeline, we implemented a packet-level tracing mechanism. Each packet is marked with a unique identifier at ingress, enabling the precise tracing as it traverses the Tofino, the DPU, and other processing stages. This replaces what is typically a complex and time-consuming debugging task with a process of systematic analysis. As a result, operators can quickly identify the exact stage of failure, streamlining fault diagnosis in a heterogeneous environment.

System Upgrade.

We upgraded the system through multiple major releases using a staged deployment approach. To minimize downtime during transitions, we routed traffic to x86
-
based fallback paths, enabling rapid rollback when necessary.
The rollouts were carried out in phases: internal pilot groups, internal key operational teams, external users, and finally all users of the legacy system.
External rollouts followed the same progression (first pilot deployments, then expansion to key external partners).
Central to this strategy was continuous, real-time monitoring. This enabled our teams to detect and resolve any performance regressions or anomalies prior to each subsequent deployment phase, ensuring our service-level objectives (SLOs) for latency and availability were consistently met.

Business Expansion.

In some deployment scenarios, a single data center must carry aggregate bandwidth at the TB level.
During business expansion, additional capacity is typically provisioned before traffic is routed onto new resources.
However, when traffic is migrated in batches after expansion, newly added nodes may not align with the existing traffic-distribution layer or routing symmetry; consequently, individual links can transiently receive more than half of the load during the transition, risking node overload or failure.
To mitigate this, we conduct a capacity and topology assessment prior to expansions. If the assessment reveals a high risk of transient traffic spikes, we adopt a more cautious, two-step transition plan. First, traffic is temporarily redirected to underlying physical switches to ensure a more uniform load distribution. Then, once fully prepared, all new nodes are brought online simultaneously in a single, coordinated update.

Disaster Recovery.
Our disaster recovery strategy is designed for rapid fault detection and recovery across multiple failure domains. When a link fails, the underlying routing protocol automatically detects the status change and isolates the link, preventing fault propagation. For a device-level failure, traffic is immediately rerouted to a standby device based on pre-established connectivity, preserving service continuity.
Primary and backup clusters are deployed in separate data centers with segmented routing to flexibly schedule traffic, allowing rapid switching to backup sites in case of datacenter-level failures and thereby ensuring high availability.
These measures enable swift, appropriate responses to faults, minimizing business impact and maintaining system stability.

7.2
Lessons Learned

Upgrade the system selective.

During the upgrade process, we selectively upgraded only the necessary components. The previous Tofino-based cloud gateways remain well-suited for fixed-function, lightweight forwarding tasks, so we retained parts of this infrastructure (e.g., IGW, CEN) in Tofino-based gateways. This separation reduces Zephyrus’s resource footprint, lowers the upgrade burden, and, in turn, significantly reduces costs while enabling rapid deployment.

Choose the right programming language.
We find P4 to be the ideal language to describe gateway forwarding logic, as its declarative style naturally expresses the packet processing pipeline (parser, match action, deparser) and compiles directly to fixed ASIC stages with stable latency. Compared to Verilog/VHDL, which imposes a steep learning curve and long development cycles, P4 achieves much higher development efficiency and benefits from a mature ecosystem (e.g., bmv2, P4Runtime), making it a natural fit for cloud gateway development.

Conduct thorough evaluation before development.
We first evaluated our growth in production traffic and recognized the need to redesign the next generation gateway. Through a systematic evaluation of hardware options, we selected DPUs over FPGAs. While both provide hardware acceleration, DPUs offer significantly lower development complexity. Prior work shows that FPGA-based data plane designs often require tens of thousands of lines of Verilog or HLS code for packet parsing and control logic (e.g., FpgaNIC
[
59
]
, NetFPGA
[
60
]
), while DPU development leverages C/C++ with DPDK libraries or P4 for specific modules, typically implemented in only a few thousand lines of code
[
61
,
62
]
. Guided by workload analysis including traffic composition, frequency distributions, and resource demands, we adopted a hierarchical forwarding architecture. We also evaluated the expertise within our team and assigned responsibilities accordingly before starting implementation. This careful upfront evaluation directly contributed to reduced TCO, and substantially fewer man-months compared to a less structured development process.

Make the system future-proof.
Fueled by continuous breakthroughs in SerDes (Serializer/Deserializer)
[
63
]
technology, the aggregate throughput of switching ASICs has followed an exponential growth trajectory, doubling approximately every two years. We implemented and deployed Zephyrus using Intel Tofino switches, which were the state-of-the-art P4-programmable ASICs when our project began. As a long-running system, we must anticipate a future where our existing inventory of a specific ASIC model becomes depleted during large-scale deployment, and the broader hardware landscape will have evolved. Designing an abstract architecture decoupled from specific hardware, building on hardware types that constantly advance, and maintaining an experienced developing team enable us to rapidly migrate to more performant or cost-effective switching ASICs (e.g., Xsight Labs Unveils X1
[
64
]
, Broadcom Tomahawk
[
63
]
) with minor redevelopment. Meanwhile, in terms of DPUs, the Intel roadmap projects that its upcoming IPUs (a DPU equivalent design) will reach 800 Gbps in 2025
[
65
]
, offering strong evidence in favor of our design and showing that Zephyrus is well positioned for future evolution.

7.3
Broader Design Principles and Insights

The design, implementation, and deployment of Zephyrus have yielded insights that extend beyond our specific system. Here, we discuss the broader design principles and insights that have emerged from our work.

The principle of software-hardware asymmetry.
Our experiences with Zephyrus suggest that architectures can benefit from an intentional software-hardware asymmetry
[
66
]
. This experience indicates that hardware’s role is often best suited for low-latency, high-throughput, and repetitive data-plane tasks (e.g., packet parsing, encryption, and basic forwarding). In contrast, software appears well suited for managing complexity and evolving business logic (e.g., policy orchestration, cross-domain optimization, and anomaly detection
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
). Zephyrus’s architecture is a direct embodiment of this principle, combining the Tbps-scale throughput of its ASIC data plane with the rich flexibility of its software components.

An explicit line between standardization and customization.
Running a system like Zephyrus at scale forces us to confront a classic tension: how do we keep the system stable while constantly adding new features for different customers? Our answer was to draw an explicit line between standardization and customization. In our observation, core infrastructure components, such as routing protocols, hardware interfaces, and baseline control logic, benefit from conforming to industry standards to promote interoperability
[
70
]
and stability. Programmability, in turn, is leveraged at the service edge to enable fine-grained, tenant-specific features (e.g., differentiated QoS policies, dynamic filtering rules). This separation allows us to satisfy unique requirements while minimizing impact on the standardized core, thereby preserving system-wide consistency and robustness.

8
Conclusion

Zephyrus is ByteDance’s third-generation cloud gateway. DPU integration, together with the hierarchical co-offloading strategy, overcomes challenges and limitations of previous gateways, without compromising their core strengths. Built for the petabit era, Zephyrus scales to support massive traffic workloads while meeting emerging business needs. We are continuously optimizing this new architecture to deliver higher performance and meet emerging business demands. As we move forward with the system’s continuous upgrades and deployments, we are committed to sharing our operational experiences and insights with the cloud networking community.
