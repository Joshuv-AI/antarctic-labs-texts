Quantitative estimates for high-contrast random media
Peter Bella, Matteo Capoferri, Mikhail Cherdantsev, Igor Velčić

Abstract
This paper studies quantitative homogenization of elliptic equations with random, uniformly elliptic coefficients that vanish in a union of random holes. Assuming an upper bound on the size of the holes and a separation condition between them, we derive optimal bounds for the regularity radius r ∗ r_{*} and suboptimal growth estimates for the corrector. These results are key ingredients for error analysis in stochastic homogenization and serve as crucial input for recent developments in the double-porosity model, such as those by Bonhomme, Duerinckx, and Gloria ( https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02847 ). Keywords: high-contrast media, random media, stochastic homogenisation, quantitative estimates, corrector, convergence rates. 2020 MSC classes: primary 35J70, 60H25; secondary 35B27, 35B40, 74A40, 74Q05.

Introduction

Many materials found in nature exhibit material parameters that vary on very small scales, which presents significant challenges when it comes to simulating their behaviour. This difficulty arises from the pronounced separation of scales between the material heterogeneity and the overall size of the specimen being studied. The concept of homogenization offers a potential solution to this problem by transforming what might be perceived as a disadvantage into an advantage. By approximating highly oscillatory problems with simpler homogeneous models, one can facilitate more efficient treatment through various methods, such as numerical simulations.

In particular, while the mathematical theory of homogenization is considerably more straightforward for cases involving periodic microstructures, most real-world materials do not conform to this idealised structure. Instead, they often possess random characteristics or, at best, their structural properties are understood only in statistical terms. As a result, there is a growing need for an appropriate framework that addresses these complexities; hence, the corresponding theory of stochastic homogenization becomes essential. This theory aims to provide insights and methodologies for effectively analysing materials whose properties are inherently random or statistically defined, thereby enhancing our understanding and predictive capabilities regarding their behaviour under various conditions.

One of the most fundamental and accessible problems in the study of partial differential equations (PDEs) is the Poisson equation and its heterogeneous counterpart, expressed in the form of uniformly elliptic equations. A common formulation for such homogenization problem is given by

-\nabla\cdot(a(x/\varepsilon)\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f\,,

(1.1)

where
\varepsilon>0
represents a small parameter characterising the scale of heterogeneity. The matrix-valued function
a(x/\varepsilon)
encodes fine-scale material properties, such as electrical or thermal conductivity, which may vary rapidly at a microscopic level.

The study of such equations is motivated by numerous applications in physics, engineering, and materials science where multiscale phenomena naturally arise. As
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, corresponding to an increasingly finer microscopic structure, it has been rigorously shown that the solutions
(u_{\varepsilon})
to (
1.1
) converge to the solution
u_{\mathrm{hom}}
of a homogenised problem:

-\nabla\cdot(a_{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla u)=f\,.

(1.2)

Here
a_{\mathrm{hom}}
is a constant matrix encoding effective or “averaged” material properties at the macroscopic scale. These results hold under various assumptions on
a
, such as periodicity or stochastic ergodicity, different types of right-hand sides
f
, and in different functional norms.

Although these
qualitative
results — asserting that
u_{\varepsilon}\to u_{\mathrm{hom}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
— are theoretically important, they are insufficient for practical purposes. In real-world scenarios,
\varepsilon
is small but finite; thus,
quantitative
estimates become essential. Specifically, one seeks precise error bounds on
\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\mathrm{hom}}\|
that depend explicitly on
\varepsilon
. Such estimates provide valuable insights into how well the homogenized solution approximates its heterogeneous counterpart for given values of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, obtaining these bounds requires careful mathematical analysis that often depends on additional structural properties of
a(x)
.

An equally crucial aspect involves efficiently computing the homogenized coefficients
a_{\mathrm{hom}}
. While theoretical frameworks like periodic homogenization or stochastic homogenization provide formal definitions for
a_{\mathrm{hom}}
, their numerical computation can be challenging due to high-dimensional integrals or complex probabilistic structures. Developing robust algorithms to approximate
a_{\mathrm{hom}}
accurately remains an active area of research with significant implications for computational multiscale modeling.

To understand the behaviour of heterogeneous media as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, a common approach in stochastic homogenization is to instead fix
\varepsilon=1
and study the problem on increasingly larger scales. This perspective avoids directly shrinking
\varepsilon
and instead focuses on how solutions behave over growing domains — an approach we will also pursue here.

A central object in the quantitative theory of stochastic homogenization is the
corrector

\phi
, which serves as a correction to linear functions so that they become
a
-harmonic. Mathematically, the corrector is defined via
the equation

-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla(x_{i}+\phi_{i}))=0,

(1.3)

where
x_{i}
represents a coordinate direction. The corrector encodes key information about the microscopic oscillations of the coefficient field
a(x)
and plays a critical role in determining both homogenization errors and other properties of solutions, such as regularity.

One of its most important features is its growth — or, rather, its lack thereof — which reflects sublinear behavior at large scales. This sublinearity is pivotal for understanding quantitative error estimates in homogenization as well as for establishing regularity results for solutions to heterogeneous PDEs. In particular, quantifying this sublinearity forms the cornerstone of modern quantitative theories of stochastic homogenization (see, e.g., works by Gloria and Otto
[
32
]
).

More precisely, one studies the size (or decay) of
\phi/R
over balls of growing radii
R
. A common approach to measure the latter involves bounding quantities like

\inf_{c}\frac{1}{R^{2}}\fint_{B_{R}}|\phi-c|^{2}
,
which captures normalised deviations from constants within a ball
B_{R}
. The smallness of this quantity has profound implications: for example, it connects directly to large-scale regularity properties such as Schauder estimates or simpler but practically useful Lipschitz estimates. For instance, Lipschitz bounds take the form

\fint_{B_{r}}|\nabla u|^{2}\lesssim C\fint_{B_{R}}|\nabla u|^{2}
,
for all large enough radii
r\leq R
, where
u
is an
a
-harmonic function. Such estimates are fundamental tools in controlling oscillatory behaviors at different scales.

Interestingly, these Lipschitz-type bounds can also be leveraged through sensitivity analysis to gain control over the growth behavior of the corrector itself. By carefully analysing how changes propagate across scales, one can link these bounds back to sublinearity properties of
\phi
. Buckling is then used to make this “circular argument” between corrector growth and regularity of solutions mathematically rigorous.

The double porosity problem concerns elliptic equations with coefficients that are of order
\varepsilon^{2}
in soft inclusions. These inclusions have size
\varepsilon
and are spaced at a distance
\varepsilon
, leading to resonances between the microscopic structure and the governing partial differential equation. Such resonances give rise to intriguing properties of materials modeled by these equations, as the interplay between the geometry and the small-scale coefficients significantly influences their macroscopic behavior, most notably their spectral properties, see e.g.
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
and
[
18
]
for some recent results in this area.

The effective behaviour of such problem can be described using standard homogenization techniques. Specifically, it involves constructing a corrector for a degenerate elliptic problem where the coefficient vanishes inside the perforations (soft inclusions). This degeneracy reflects the physical reality of highly permeable or weakly resistive regions within the material. These PDEs can be equivalently formulated as uniformly elliptic equations in perforated domain with zero Neumann boundary conditions on the perforations. The resulting homogenised model captures both the influence of these soft inclusions and their interactions with the surrounding medium, providing insights into macroscopic transport properties such as diffusion or conductivity.

This framework is particularly useful for studying materials with hierarchical porous structures or composite media where soft inclusions play a dominant role in determining effective behaviour.

The study of homogenization in perforated domains has a long and rich history, primarily focusing on the case with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions imposed on the holes. This line of research was initiated by Tartar
[
36
]
, and further developed by Cioranescu and Murat, who introduced the oscillating test function method
[
22
]
. Using this approach, they analysed the homogenization of the Poisson equation in domains perforated with periodically arranged critically sized holes. In the limit, they derived an effective equation featuring an additional “strange” Brinkman zero-th order term, which is defined through the capacity of the holes.

A similar result was obtained independently by Papanicolaou and Varadhan for randomly placed critically sized holes under the assumption that their distribution obeys a stationary and ergodic law
[
34
]
. Their work demonstrated that stochastic methods can also be employed effectively to handle such problems in random settings.

Subsequent research revealed that these techniques are robust enough to address more complex equations in perforated domains. For instance, Allaire extended these ideas to study incompressible Navier-Stokes equations modeling fluid dynamics in perforated geometries
[
1
]
. More recently, progress has been made on compressible fluids models (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
for periodic as well as
[
13
]
for random configurations of holes) or effective viscosity problem where the obstacles move with the flow
[
27
]
.

Turning back to the elliptic equations, let us now briefly discuss the strategy to quantify the growth of the corrector, building on the theory of stochastic homogenization for uniformly elliptic equations developed over the past decade by Otto and coauthors. This framework provides powerful tools to analyse sublinear growth properties of correctors in random environments.

The (lack of) growth of the corrector stems from cancellations in its gradient. Thus, understanding how quickly the gradient decorrelates is key. Since the gradient of the corrector
\nabla\phi
solves an equation with coefficients given by
a
, this requires quantifying the ergodicity of randomness in
a
. In this context, coefficient fields
a
are treated as elements of a probability space
\Omega
. To achieve this quantification, functional inequalities come into play. These inequalities — extensions of classical Poincaré and Sobolev inequalities to infinite-dimensional probability spaces — are known as Spectral Gap and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities (log-Sobolev).

Unlike standard Poincaré inequalities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where gradients appear on the right-hand side (RHS), in Spectral Gap inequalities, these gradients are replaced by “vertical” derivatives. Vertical derivatives measure oscillations or sensitivity of random variables when values of
a
(as elements in the probability space
\Omega
) are altered locally within different parts of the domain. One significant advantage of this approach is that it decouples analytical arguments (e.g., PDE estimates) from stochastic considerations (e.g., ergodicity). Another advantage is its generality: such functional inequalities can be independently verified for many stochastic models
[
25
,
26
]
, making them widely applicable.

To control oscillations appearing on the RHS of these functional inequalities, we need to understand how much
\nabla\phi
changes if we modify
a
locally within a ball
B_{r}(x)
. Denoting this modified coefficient field by
a^{\prime}
, we study differences between
\nabla\phi
and
\nabla\phi^{\prime}
, where
\phi^{\prime}
is the corrector associated with
a^{\prime}
. Observing that this difference satisfies an elliptic equation with a RHS supported in
B_{r}(x)
allows us to efficiently apply energy estimates. Combined with large-scale Lipschitz bounds — which propagate control over
|\nabla u|^{2}
across scales — this leads to bounds on oscillations. These bounds then feed into functional inequalities to control variances of large scale averages of
\nabla\phi
.

When dealing with equations involving random coefficients, it is important to note that large-scale Lipschitz estimates do not hold uniformly across all scales but instead depend on a random scale
r_{*}(x)
. This scale
r_{*}(x)
, which varies spatially due to randomness, marks the threshold where large-scale regularity begins to hold around a point
x
. Crucially,
r_{*}(x)
is closely linked to the sublinearity properties of correctors over balls
B_{r}(x)
for
r\geq r_{*}(x)
— an observation highlighted in
[
31
]
. This connection plays a pivotal role in bridging sublinearity results for correctors with quantitative homogenization theory. Specifically, understanding how
r_{*}(x)
behaves provides insight into how solutions transition from microscopic oscillations to macroscopic regularity.

Another key idea involves the use of massive correctors, denoted by
\phi_{T}
, for
T\gg 1
. These are defined as whole-space solutions to
\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}-\nabla\cdot a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})=0
,
where
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents a fixed direction. Massive correctors serve as an effective proxy for the standard corrector
\phi
. On one hand, the massive term modifies the dependence of
\phi_{T}
on the coefficient field
a(x)
, effectively localising its sensitivity to regions of size approximately
O(\sqrt{T})
. On the other hand, any power-law growth observed in
|\phi_{T}|/R^{\alpha}
with
T=R^{2}
directly translates into analogous growth behavior for the original corrector
|\phi|/R^{\alpha}
.

Before the work of Otto and coauthors, Armstrong and Smart
[
8
]
developed quantitative estimates using a completely different technique, building on the foundational work of Avellaneda and Lin
[
9
]
. They also utilised the variational structure (initially for symmetric coefficient fields
a
, later extended to the general case in
[
5
]
), analysing the averaged energy over an increasing sequence of triadic cubes. By combining this with a convex dual quantity, they established quantitative convergence rates to the homogenised limit. In all these works the regularity theory plays a crucial role, also enabling a precise understanding of the behavior of solutions
[
6
]
. For further details on this framework and its applications, we refer to the comprehensive book by Armstrong, Kuusi, and Mourrat
[
7
]
.

More recently, homogenization techniques have been applied in settings where classical homogenization does not occur. For example, consider Brownian motion in two spatial dimensions with drift along the level lines of a Gaussian Free Field (GFF). Physicists predicted that such motion would exhibit superdiffusive behavior with logarithmic corrections—a phenomenon supported by stochastic methods (see e.g.,
[
37
]
). However, these earlier works did not identify the precise rate of superdiffusion.

While the presence of superlinear diffusion indicates a failure of homogenization, Chatzigeorgiou et al.
[
19
]
truncated correlations of the field on scale
L
and employed homogenization methods to analyse diffusion speed. By averaging over all random realisations of the field, they were able to let
L\to\infty
to obtain the correct superlinear rate, including identifying the appropriate prefactor in the limit of vanishing drift.
Using local quantities and homogenization techniques across multiple scales, combined with a careful analysis of interactions between these scales, Armstrong, Bou-Rabee, and Kuusi
[
2
]
achieved stronger results in several ways: their findings are quenched (i.e., valid for almost every realization of the Gaussian Free Field), identify the optimal constant without requiring weak drift, and are presented in the form of a scaling limit.
These advancements represent significant progress in understanding anomalous diffusion phenomena in random environments. Furthermore, it is believed that these techniques can be adapted to handle perturbatively supercritical fields. In such cases, instead of logarithmic corrections leading to superlinear growth rates, one would expect power-law behaviors—an exciting direction for future research.

A homogenization setting closely related to the present situation is that of non-uniformly elliptic equations. This area has been motivated by studies of the invariance principle for random walks in degenerate random environments
[
14
,
15
]
or on quantitative homogenization in supercritical percolation clusters
[
3
,
24
]
. Quantitative homogenization of degenerate equations was considered by the first author and Kniely
[
12
]
(see also
[
4
,
23
]
), building on earlier work concerning regularity theory in such degenerate setting
[
10
]
. These studies provide key tools to address challenges posed by degeneracy, where coefficients may be close to vanish or become unbounded.

Shortly before the release of this manuscript, an article by Bonhomme, Duerinckx, and Gloria
[
16
]
appeared online as a preprint.
At first glance, their work appears to tackle very similar issues; however, upon closer inspection, it becomes evident that while there is some overlap between their and the present work, their results are largely complementary to ours. Specifically, in
[
16
]
, the focus lies on establishing qualitative homogenization results as well as quantitative statements under
the assumption
that correctors are stationary objects in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
As discussed earlier in this introduction, controlling correctors is a fundamental prerequisite for achieving quantitative homogenization results. One of the main contributions of our work lies precisely in providing such control within this framework. Thus, while both studies address related problems, they do so from different perspectives and with distinct focal points.

v\in W^{1,p}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega}^{k}\setminus\omega^{k})
the extension
v^{\rm ext}:=E_{k}u
satisfies

(i)

(l+x_{i})^{\rm ext}=l+x_{i}
for every
l\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(ii)

\|v^{\rm ext}\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega}^{k})}\leq c\,\|v\|_{L^{p}\,(\mathcal{B}_{\omega}^{k}\setminus\omega^{k})}
,

(iii)

\|\nabla v^{\rm ext}\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega}^{k})}\leq c\,\|\nabla v\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{B}_{\omega}^{k}\setminus\omega^{k})}
,

where the constant
c
depends on
p
and the parameters of minimal smoothness (entering Assumption
2.4
), but is independent of
\omega
and
k
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let us point out that in the current paper we will only extend functions lying in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}
, namely, we will always be in the case
p=2
. Hence, for all practical purposes, we can suppress the dependence of
c
from

in Theorem
2.5
, and treat the constant as if it were uniform with respect to
p
.

Of course, Theorem
2.5
implies functions defined in

can be extended to functions defined on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Indeed, one can do this inclusion by inclusion, because extension domains can be constructed in such a way that they do not overlap — see Remark
2.3
. With slight abuse of notation, we will be denoting by

the extension of a function

from any collection of extension domains where it is defined into the corresponding collection of inclusions.

2.2
Mathematical model

Put
\mathcal{M}_{\omega}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}
. We refer to
\mathcal{M}_{\omega}
as the
matrix
, in that it models the “stiff” material supporting the random perforation described by
\omega
. Further on, for notational simplicity, we will often tacitly assume the dependence of
\mathcal{M}_{\omega}
on
\omega
and simply write
\mathcal{M}
, instead.

Given
\omega\in\Omega
, let us consider matrix-valued coefficients
a_{\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
which are symmetric and satisfy

a_{\omega}=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{\omega}}\,a_{\omega},

(2.3)

a_{-}|\xi|^{2}\leq\xi\cdot a_{\omega}(x)\xi\quad\textrm{and}\quad|a_{\omega}(x)\xi|\leq a_{+}|\xi|\qquad\text{for all}\ x\in\mathcal{M}_{\omega},\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.4)

Here
a_{-},a_{+}
are strictly positive constants.
In plain English,
a_{\omega}
is a random matrix coefficient field that vanishes inside the perforation
\omega
and is elliptic
in the matrix
\mathcal{M}
. We will identify
a_{-},a_{+}
with the functions that take constant value on
\mathcal{M}
and vanish on the complement of
\mathcal{M}
and often write
a
for
a_{\omega}
.

Given
\overline{f}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
f(y,\omega):=\overline{f}(T_{y}\omega)
its
stationary extension
(or
realisation
). If
\overline{f}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, the latter being the usual space of
p
-integrable functions on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, then
f\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};L^{p}(\Omega))

[
33
, Chapter 7]
.

Recall that a vector field
p\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is said to be

•

potential
if there exists
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
p=\nabla\varphi
;

•

solenoidal
if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p\cdot\nabla\varphi=0
for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

One defines a vector field
\overline{p}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to be potential (resp. solenoidal) if its stationary extension
y\mapsto f(y,\omega)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is potential (resp. solenoidal) almost surely.

Put

\mathcal{V}^{2}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\Omega):=\{\overline{p}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ |\ \overline{p}\ \text{is potential},\ \mathbb{E}[\overline{p}]=0\}\,.

(2.5)

Let
\overline{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}^{2}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\Omega)
be the solution of the problem

\mathbb{E}[a_{\omega}\,(\psi+\mathbf{e})\cdot\nabla f]=0\qquad\forall\bar{f}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\,.

(2.6)

The existence of
\overline{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}^{2}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\Omega)
is guaranteed
by
[
33
, § 8.1]
combined with the extension result
[
21
, Lemma D.3]
.

Definition 2.7
.

We define the
homogenisation corrector
(or
corrector
for short) in the direction
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the random variable
\phi=\phi_{\omega}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
whose gradient is the stationary extension of the solution
\overline{\psi}
to (
2.6
):

\nabla\phi(x)=\psi(x,\omega)\,.

The corrector
\phi
is defined uniquely up to an additive constant. Observe that while
\nabla\phi
is stationary by definition,
\phi
itself is not, in general, guaranteed to be stationary. In physical space, the corrector satisfies the equation

-\nabla\cdot a(\nabla\phi+\mathbf{e})=0\,,

(2.7)

understood in a weak sense with smooth compactly supported test functions. See also Lemma
4.8
for additional details on integrability properties of
\phi
.
We will denote by
\phi^{\rm ext}
the extension of
\phi|_{\mathcal{M}}
into
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 2.8
.

We define the
massive homogenisation corrector
(or
massive corrector
for short) on the scale
\sqrt{T}\geq 1
in the direction
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the stationary extension
\phi_{T}
of the unique solution
\overline{\phi}_{T}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
to the equation

\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}f+a_{\omega}\,(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\cdot\nabla f]=0\qquad\forall f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\,.

(2.8)

Realisations of the massive corrector are
H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
functions which satisfy almost surely the equation

\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}-\nabla\cdot a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})=0\,,

(2.9)

understood in a weak sense with smooth compactly supported test functions.
Additional integrability properties of
\phi_{T}
in physical space will be discussed in Lemma
4.6
. We will denote by
\phi_{T}^{\rm ext}
the extension of
\phi_{T}|_{\mathcal{M}}
into
\mathcal{M}
.

As customary in the subject, the flux
q
and the massive flux
q_{T}
are (random) vector fields are defined in terms of the corrector as

q:=a(\nabla\phi+\mathbf{e})

(2.10)

and

q_{T}:=a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\,,

(2.11)

respectively. One can associate to the flux (
2.10
) a tensor field called flux corrector.

Definition 2.9

(Flux corrector)
.

We define the
flux corrector
to be the unique, modulo an additive constant, tensor field
\sigma\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};L^{2}(\Omega))
whose components
\sigma_{ijk}
,
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, are the almost sure solutions of the equation

-\Delta\sigma_{ijk}=\partial_{j}(q_{i})_{k}-\partial_{k}(q_{i})_{j}\,,

(2.12)

understood in a weak sense with compactly supported test functions. Here
(q_{i})_{j}
is the
j
-th component of
q_{i}
, the flux (
2.10
) associated with
\mathbf{e}=\widehat{e}_{i}
, where
\widehat{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unit vector along the
i
-th coordinate axis.

By definition, the flux corrector is skew-symmetric in the last pair of indices,
\sigma_{ijk}=-\sigma_{ikj}
and it satisfies

\partial_{k}\sigma_{ijk}=(q_{i})_{j}\,.

(2.13)

As for the corrector, one has that
\nabla\sigma
is stationary, whereas
\sigma
itself is not guaranteed to be such. Moreover, observe that
\sigma
is defined through the Poisson equation in the whole space. Notably, the solution
\sigma
does not directly depend on the specific shape of the inclusions but incorporates their influence solely through the flux
q
. This observation significantly simplifies the analysis by decoupling geometric details from the governing equations. Consequently, the entire theoretical framework developed in
[
31
]
can be applied, provided that
q
satisfies the corresponding bounds required for consistency and regularity.

Next, we introduce two quantities that allow one to quantitatively describe the large-scale behaviour of the corrector in an averaged, yet sufficiently precise, sense.

The first is the
excess
, which measures the extent to which
u
deviates from the space of
a
-affines functions at the level of gradients:

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{r}):=\inf_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|^{2}\,.

(2.14)

The second — in a sense, the cornerstone of our whole technical analysis — is
r_{*}
. Given a constant
\mathcal{C}>0
, we denote by
r_{*}
the random variable

r_{*}=r_{*}(a_{\omega};\mathcal{C}):=\inf\left\{r\geq 1\ :\ \frac{1}{R^{2}}\fint_{B_{R}}\left|(\phi^{\rm ext},\sigma)-\fint(\phi^{\rm ext},\sigma)\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}}\quad\forall R\geq r_{*}\right\}\,.

(2.15)

If the RHS of (
2.15
) is empty, then it is understood that
r_{*}=+\infty
.

Finally, we will introduce an additional assumption on our probability space and present a few meaningful examples satisfying all our assumptions.

Given a random variable
F(a)
, one can measure its sensitivity to local perturbations of
a
by means of its
oscillation
, defined in accordance with

\left|\partial^{\rm osc}_{Q}F(a)\right|:=\\
\sup\left\{F(a_{1})-F(a_{2})\ \vline\ a_{1}=a_{2}=a\quad\text{in}\quad(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus Q)\cup\bigcup_{\omega^{k}(a)\cap\partial Q\neq\emptyset}\omega^{k}(a)\right\}\,.

(2.16)

That is,
\partial^{\rm osc}_{Q}F(a)
measures how much
F(a)
varies when one modifies
a
inside
Q
, but without touching those inclusions that intersect the boundary of
Q
. This is merely conventional for the sake of definiteness: and one could drop the latter requirement without affecting the analysis.

Assumption 2.10

(Spectral Gap)
.

There exists constants
\rho,M>0
such that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
satisfies the
Spectral Gap Inequality
with constants
\rho,M
: for every random variable
F=F(a)
we have

\operatorname{Var}_{L}[F]\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\,\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\partial^{\rm osc}_{B_{M}(x)}F(a)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right]\qquad\forall L\geq 1\,,

(2.17)

where
\mathrm{Var}_{L}
and
\mathbb{E}_{L}
are variance and expectation taken with respect to the ensemble scaled by
L
, for rescaled coefficient fields
a(L\,\cdot\,)
.

Assumption 2.11

(Multi-scale Spectral Gap)
.

There exists constants
c,\beta,M>0
such that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
satisfies the
Multi-scale Spectral Gap Inequality
with constants
c,\beta,M
: for every random variable
F=F(a)
we have

\mathrm{Var}_{L}[F]\leq\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\pi(l)(l+1)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\partial^{\rm osc}_{B_{(l+1)M}(x)}F(a)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}l\right]\qquad\forall L\geq 1\,,

(2.18)

where
\pi(l):=c\exp(\frac{l^{\beta}}{c})
.

Remark 2.12
.

(a)

It is possible to impose the Multi-scale Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.18
) for some integrable
\pi:\mathbb{R}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, more generally, obtaining different estimate for the exponential moment of the random variable
r_{*}
(we refer the reader to
[
31
]
for details). However, in the examples we shall present we will only consider
\pi
in the form above. Note that the “ordinary” Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.17
) is satisfied when (
2.18
) holds for a
\pi
with compact support.

(b)

We require the Spectral Gap and Multi-scale Spectral Gap Inequalities to hold for every
L\geq 1
rather than just for
L=1
. One can show that in all examples of interest such condition is satisfied. This is connected with the fact that mixing properties improve under scaling with
L\geq 1
, see also
[
26
, Remark 2.2]
.

(c)

Instead of imposing the (Multi-scale) Spectral Gap Inequality, one could impose a stronger (Multi-scale) Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality, see, e.g.,
[
25
]
. This would again imply a stronger estimate for the exponential moments of the random variable
r_{*}
. In Theorem
3.1
one could have
d/2
replaced with
d
, provided the weight
\pi
possesses exponential decay as imposed above.

2.2.1
Some examples

We will now discuss some prototypical examples to demonstrate that our set of assumptions is meaningful and covers a non-empty class of scenarios. These examples illustrate how the (Multi-Scale) Spectral Gap Inequality applies in various random settings.

Example 1

Let us consider a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
(V_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
taking values in
[0,1/3]
. Construct a random perforation by placing a hole
B_{V_{j}}(z)
at each
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In this case, the Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.17
) is satisfied with
M=2/3+\varepsilon
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, as shown in
[
26
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Example 2

Consider a Poisson point process
\mathcal{P}
with intensity
\mu>0
. Denoting the points of the process by
\{p_{k}\}_{k}
, construct a random perforation by placing a hole of radius

r_{k}:=\min\left\{R,\frac{1}{3}\inf_{j\,:\,j\neq k}|p_{k}-p_{j}|\right\}

at each
p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
R>0
acts as an upper bound on the hole radii. In view of
[
26
, Proposition 2.3]
, the Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.17
) is satisfied with
M=2R+\varepsilon
, for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Example 3

Consider a random parking measure as a point process. This process ensures that there exists
R>0
such that all points are at least distance
R
from one another. Furthermore, it is saturated: no additional point can be added to the configuration while maintaining this minimum distance constraint. Construct a perforation by placing a hole of radius
R/2
at every point of the process. Then, the Multi-Scale Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.18
) is satisfied with weights given by

\pi(l)=C\exp(-l/C),

where
C>0
depends on structural properties of the process.

Further examples can be found in
[
26
]
, including random point processes with randomly shaped holes and random radii. The corresponding formulae for the weight function
\pi(l)
are also discussed there. Finally, we emphasize that while these (Multi-Scale) Spectral Gap Inequalities hold under specific geometric or probabilistic conditions, they are not guaranteed if one only assumes finite correlation properties. Each scenario must be carefully examined based on its unique structure and assumptions.

3
Main results

The main result of our paper is the following bound on the exponential moments of the regularity radius
r_{*}
for perforated domains.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that the probability measure
P
satisfies the Multi-scale Spectral Gap Inequality (
2.18
).
Then there exists a positive constant
C=C(d,a_{\pm},\beta)
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{r_{*}^{\min\{d/2,\beta\}}/C}\right]\leq 2.

(3.1)

In the case when spectral gap is satisfied, we can take
\beta=\infty
in (
3.1
).

From the bounds of the exponential moments, it is well known that one can obtain bounds on the growth of the corrector and, in turn, estimate the homogenization error. This is the content of the upcoming statements. Note that Theorem
3.1
supplies an essentially optimal bound. The estimates below, however, are suboptimal, and can be improved by using more sophisticated approaches available in the literature, e.g., that from
[
32
]
.

u-(u_{\rm hom}+\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}\phi_{i}),&\mbox{ in }\mathcal{M}\cap B_{R^{\prime}},\\

(4.13)

Note that
v\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{R^{\prime}})
. In the above the superscript
\cdot^{\rm ext}
stands for the extension of the restriction of the function to the set
\mathcal{M}
into the perforations
\omega^{k}
.
Although the extension into the perforations does not appear in the next few calculations, it will play an important role at a later stage.

The following calculation is to be understood in the weak sense (i.e. it holds for any test function from
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
):

-\nabla\cdot a\nabla v=\nabla\cdot(1-\eta)a\nabla u_{\rm hom}+\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom})\cdot a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})+\nabla\cdot\phi_{i}a\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}).

(4.14)

In the above we used that
-\nabla\cdot a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})=0
.
Utilizing (
4.12
) we have

\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom})\cdot a_{\rm hom}e_{i}=-\nabla\cdot((1-\eta)a_{\rm hom}\nabla u_{\rm hom})

(4.15)

(here we have actual
u_{\rm hom}
everywhere rather than its harmonic extension).
Combining the last two identities yields

-\nabla\cdot a\nabla v=\nabla\cdot((1-\eta)(a-a_{\rm hom})\nabla u_{\rm hom}+\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom})\cdot(a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})-a_{\rm hom}e_{i})+\nabla\cdot\phi_{i}a\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}).

(4.16)

Next, recalling (
2.13
), we make a substitution

\nabla\cdot\sigma_{i}=q_{i}=a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})-a_{\rm hom}e_{i},

(4.17)

and use the skew-symmetry of
\sigma_{i}
to get

-\nabla\cdot a\nabla v=\nabla\cdot((1-\eta)(a-a_{\rm hom})\nabla u_{\rm hom}+(\phi_{i}a-\sigma_{i})\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom})).

(4.18)

Testing this identity with
v
and using Hölder’s inequality we have

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}}a\nabla v\cdot\nabla v=\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}}((1-\eta)(a-a_{\rm hom})\nabla u_{\rm hom}+(\phi_{i}a-\sigma_{i})\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}))\cdot\nabla v,

(4.19)

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla v|^{2}\lesssim\|(1-\eta)\nabla u_{\rm hom}\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\|\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}+\\
\|\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi\nabla(\eta\nabla u_{\rm hom})\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}\|\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}+\|\sigma\nabla(\eta\nabla u_{\rm hom})\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}\|\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}.

(4.20)

By construction of
v
and properties of the extension operator we have

\|\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-1}}\lesssim\|\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}}.

(4.21)

Then, using Young’s inequality we can absorb this term by the left hand side:

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla v|^{2}\lesssim\|(1-\eta)\nabla u_{\rm hom}\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}}^{2}+\|\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi\nabla(\eta\nabla u_{\rm hom})\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}^{2}\\
+\|\sigma\nabla(\eta\nabla u_{\rm hom})\|_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-1}}^{2}.

(4.22)

Then
\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\nabla v=\chi_{\mathcal{M}}(\nabla u-(\nabla u_{\rm hom}+\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}\nabla\phi_{i}+\phi_{i}\nabla(\eta\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom})))
yields

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-\partial_{i}u_{\rm hom}(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i})|^{2}\lesssim\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\\
+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}|(\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi,\sigma)|^{2}(|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\rho^{-2}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2})+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-1}}|\nabla v|^{2}.

(4.23)

For
r\leq\frac{R^{\prime}}{2}\leq R^{\prime}-2\rho
and setting
\xi=\nabla u_{\rm hom}(0)
we have

\int_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|^{2}\\
\lesssim\int_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|I+\nabla\phi|^{2}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}-\nabla u_{\rm hom}(0)|^{2}+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\\
+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}|(\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi,\sigma)|^{2}(|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\rho^{-2}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2})+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-1}}|\nabla u^{\rm ext}|^{2}\\
\lesssim r^{2}\sup_{B_{r}}|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\int_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|id+\nabla\phi|^{2}\\
+\sup_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}(|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\rho^{-2}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2})\int_{B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}}|(\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi,\sigma)|^{2}\\
+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-1}}|\nabla u^{\rm ext}|^{2}.

(4.24)

Next we derive bounds for the terms on the right hand side of (
4.24
).

Testing (
4.12
) with
u_{\rm hom}-u^{\rm ext}
we have

\|\nabla u_{\rm hom}\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\lesssim\|\nabla u^{\rm ext}\|_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\lesssim\|\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\nabla u\|_{B_{R}}.

(4.25)

The following estimates have been shown to hold for an
a_{\rm hom}
-harmonic function on
B_{R}
in
[
29
]
(here
R
is generic):

R^{2}\sup_{B_{R/2}}|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}+\sup_{B_{R/2}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{R}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2},

(4.26)

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\rho\int_{\partial B_{R^{\prime}}}|\nabla^{tan}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}.

(4.27)

Combining (
4.25
), (
4.26
) and recalling that
r\leq R^{\prime}/2
and
R^{\prime}\in(R/2,R-1)
we arrive at

r^{2}\sup_{B_{r}}|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{r}{R^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\fint_{B_{R^{\prime}}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2}\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

(4.28)

Applying (
4.26
) in the form

\rho^{2}|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla u_{\rm hom}(x)|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{\rho}(x)}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2},\quad x\in B_{R^{\prime}-\rho},

we have for all
x\in B_{R^{\prime}-\rho}

|\nabla^{2}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}(x)+\rho^{-2}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}(x)\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\fint_{B_{\rho}(x)}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\\
\leq\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)^{d}\fint_{B_{R^{\prime}}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(\frac{R}{\rho}\right)^{d}\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

(4.29)

Combining (
4.27
) with (
4.10
)

\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}-2\rho}}|\nabla u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\rho\int_{\partial B_{R^{\prime}}}|\nabla^{tan}u_{\rm hom}|^{2}\lesssim\frac{\rho}{R}\int_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

(4.30)

Now applying (
4.11
) and (
4.28
)-(
4.30
) to the right hand side of (
4.24
)
we arrive at

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|^{2}\lesssim\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}}\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|I+\nabla\phi|^{2}\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}\\
+\frac{R^{d}}{r^{d}}\left(\left(\frac{R}{\rho}\right)^{d+2}\frac{1}{R^{2}}\fint_{B_{R}}|(\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\phi,\sigma)|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{R}\right)\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

(4.31)

Applying Caccioppoli’s estimate (
4.7
) to the function
x_{i}+\phi_{i}-c_{i}
(observe that one can choose
c_{i}=\fint_{B_{2r}\cap\mathcal{M}}\phi_{i}
), we have

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{r^{2}}\fint_{B_{2r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|x_{i}+\phi_{i}-c_{i}|^{2}\lesssim 1+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\fint_{B_{2r}}|\phi_{i}^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{2r}}\phi_{i}^{\rm ext}|^{2}.

(4.32)

So we arrive at

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|^{2}\leq C\left((1+\delta)\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{R^{d}}{r^{d}}\left(\delta\left(\frac{R}{\rho}\right)^{d+2}+\frac{\rho}{R}\right)\right)\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

(4.33)

Let us prove (
4.4
). The upper bound is a consequence of (
4.32
). Thus, it only remains to show the lower bound. Let
\eta
be a cut-off function between
B_{r-1-\tau}
and
B_{r-1}
with
0<\frac{\tau}{r}
sufficiently small. Applying the Extension Theorem to
(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|_{\mathcal{M}}
(recall that our extension preserves affine functions) and Jensen’s inequality in the form
(\int\eta)^{-1}\int\eta f^{2}\geq((\int\eta)^{-1}\int\eta f)^{2}
we have

\int_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi}|^{2}\geq C\int_{B_{r-1}}\eta|\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}|^{2}\geq C\left(\int\eta\right)\left|\xi+\left(\int\eta\right)^{-1}\int_{B_{r-1}}\eta\nabla\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}\right|^{2}\\
=C\left(\int\eta\right)\left|\xi-\left(\int\eta\right)^{-1}\int_{B_{r-1}}\nabla\eta\,(\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{r-1}}\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext})\right|^{2}.

(4.34)

Further, one can easily check that

\int\eta\geq C(d)r^{d}(1-C(d)\frac{\tau}{r}).

(4.35)

Then we have

\left(\int\eta\right)^{-1}\left|\int_{B_{r-1}}\nabla\eta\left(\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{r-1}}\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}\right)\right|\leq C(d)\frac{1}{\tau r^{d}}\int_{B_{r-1}}\left|\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{r-1}}\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}\right|\\
\leq C(d)\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\fint_{B_{r-1}}\left|\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{r-1}}\phi_{\xi}^{\rm ext}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\leq C(d)\frac{r}{\tau}\delta^{1/2}|\xi|.

(4.36)

Choosing
\tau
such that
\frac{\tau}{r}=\delta^{1/4}
and combining (
4.34
)–(
4.36
) we arrive at (
4.4
).
∎

The next two statements — Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.3
— establish large-scale Schauder estimates for solutions of degenerate elliptic PDEs in divergence form, which will later be needed in the proof of Proposition
4.4
. Furthermore, Proposition
4.2
establishes growth properties (sublinearlty) of the corrector on scales bigger than the regularity radius
r_{*}
in the sense of (
4.39
).

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
there exists constants
C_{1}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)
,
C_{2}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)
with the following properties. Let
r_{*}
be defined as in (
2.15
) with
\mathcal{C}=C_{1}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)
. If

-\nabla\cdot a\nabla u=0\qquad\text{in}\ \widetilde{B}_{R}

(4.37)

for some
R\geq r_{*}
, where
\widetilde{B}_{R}
is defined as in Proposition
4.1
, then we have

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{r})\leq C_{2}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R})\qquad\forall r\in[r_{*},R]

(4.38)

and

\frac{1}{C}|\xi|^{2}\leq\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi}|^{2}\leq C_{2}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)|\xi|^{2}\qquad\forall r\geq r_{*},\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(4.39)

Furthermore, fixing
\alpha>0
(say,
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
), we have the mean value property

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq C(d,a_{\pm})\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}\qquad\forall r\in[r_{*},R].

(4.40)

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
\delta=\delta(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)\in(0,1)
be a sufficiently small parameter to be chosen later.

First note that for
\rho>1
one has

\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left|\phi-\fint_{B_{\rho}\cap\mathcal{M}}\phi\right|^{2}\leq C\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\phi^{\rm ext}-\fint_{B_{\rho}}\phi^{\rm ext}\right|^{2}\,.

(4.41)

Indeed, this bound is a consequence of the trivial estimate

\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left|\phi-c\right|^{2}\leq C\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\phi^{\rm ext}-c\right|^{2}

after one chooses

c=\fint_{B_{\rho}}\phi^{\rm ext}
in the right-hand side and optimises for
c
in the left-hand side. Choosing sufficiently large
\mathcal{C}
in (
2.15
) we guarantee that

\frac{1}{R^{\prime 2}}\fint_{B_{R}^{\prime}}\left|(\phi^{\rm ext},\sigma)-\fint_{B_{R}^{\prime}}(\phi^{\rm ext},\sigma)\right|^{2}\leq\delta\qquad\forall R^{\prime}\geq r_{*}.

(4.42)

Then by Proposition
4.1
one has

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi})|^{2}\\
\leq C\left((1+\delta)\frac{r^{2}}{R^{\prime 2}}+\frac{R^{\prime d}}{r^{d}}\left(\delta\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)^{d+2}+\frac{\rho}{R^{\prime}}\right)\right)\fint_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}

(4.43)

for all
r_{*}\leq r\leq R^{\prime}\leq R
, where the constant only depends on
d
and
a_{\pm}
.

Upon replacing
u\mapsto u-(\xi\cdot x+\phi_{\xi})
(which is also
a
-harmonic) and optimising w.r.t
\xi
one obtains from (
4.43
) that

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{r})\leq C\left((1+\delta)\frac{r^{2}}{R^{\prime 2}}+\frac{R^{\prime d}}{r^{d}}\left(\delta\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)^{d+2}+\frac{\rho}{R^{\prime}}\right)\right)\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})\,.

(4.44)

Let
\theta>0
so that
2C\theta^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3}\theta^{2\alpha}
.
Choose
\delta
so that
C\delta^{\frac{1}{d+3}}\theta^{-d}\leq\frac{1}{3}\theta^{2\alpha}
. Denote
R^{*}(\delta):=\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+3}}
, and assume that
R^{\prime}\geq R^{*}(\delta)
. Then set

\rho=\delta^{\frac{1}{d+3}}R^{\prime}\geq 1.

(4.45)

Notice that for such
\rho
one has

\delta\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)^{d+2}+\frac{\rho}{R^{\prime}}=2\delta^{\frac{1}{d+3}}.

(4.46)

Now choose
r
so that
\theta=\frac{r}{R^{\prime}}
. Under these assumptions (
4.44
) takes the form

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{\theta R^{\prime}})\leq\theta^{2\alpha}\,\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})\,\qquad\forall R^{\prime}\geq R^{*}(\delta),\ \theta R^{\prime}\geq r_{*}.

(4.47)

The estimate (
4.47
) can be iterated to obtain

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{\theta^{n}R^{\prime}})\leq\theta^{2n\alpha}\,\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})\,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\ \text{s.t.}\ \theta^{n}R^{\prime}\geq\max\{R^{*}(\delta),r_{*}\}.

(4.48)

For
\max\{R^{*}(\delta),r_{*}\}\leq r\leq R
, let
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
be such that
\theta^{n+1}<\frac{r}{R}\leq\theta^{n}
. Observe that this implies
\theta^{n}\frac{R}{r}<\frac{1}{\theta}
. By combining the latter inequality with the trivial estimate
\fint_{B_{r}}\lesssim\left(\frac{\theta^{n}R}{r}\right)^{d}\fint_{B_{\theta^{n}R}}
and using (
4.48
) with
R^{\prime}\mapsto R
, we arrive at

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{r})\leq\theta^{-d}\,\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{\theta^{n}R})\leq\theta^{-d}(\theta^{n})^{2\alpha}\,\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R})\\
\leq\theta^{-d-2\alpha}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2\alpha}\,\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R})\,,

(4.49)

that is, (
4.38
). Notice that for
R\leq R^{*}(\delta)
the bound (
4.38
) is trivial up to choosing sufficiently large constant
C(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)
.

The bound (
4.39
) follows from (
4.4
), upon choosing the constant
\mathcal{C}
in the definition of
r_{*}
sufficiently large.

We complete the proof with the argument for (
4.40
).
For
r_{*}\leq r\leq R
, let
\xi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the vector defined by the relation

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{r})=\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi_{r}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{r}})|^{2}

(4.50)

whose existence is guaranteed by the nondegeneracy condition (
4.39
). The first step towards proving (
4.40
) is to show that

|\xi_{\rho}-\xi_{R^{\prime}}|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})\qquad\text{for}\ \rho\leq R^{\prime}\leq 2\rho,\quad r_{*}\leq\rho\leq R^{\prime}\leq R.

(4.51)

By (
4.39
) we have

|\xi_{\rho}-\xi_{R^{\prime}}|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|(\xi_{\rho}-\xi_{R^{\prime}}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{\rho}-\xi_{R^{\prime}}})|^{2}\\
=\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|(\xi_{\rho}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{\rho}})-\nabla u+\nabla u-(\xi_{R^{\prime}}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{R^{\prime}}})|^{2}\\
\lesssim\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi_{\rho}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{\rho}})|^{2}+\fint_{B_{R^{\prime}}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u-(\xi_{R^{\prime}}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{R^{\prime}}})|^{2}\\
=\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{\rho})+\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})\,.

(4.52)

Note that in the second-to-last step we used the fact that
\rho\sim R^{\prime}
so that
\fint_{B_{\rho}}\lesssim\fint_{B_{R^{\prime}}}
. But (
4.52
) implies (
4.51
).

Next we use a dyadic argument. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{-(N+1)}R^{\prime}<r\leq 2^{-N}R^{\prime}
by (
4.51

|\xi_{r}-\xi_{2^{-N}R^{\prime}}|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{2^{-N}R^{\prime}}),\quad|\xi_{2^{-(n+1)}R^{\prime}}-\xi_{2^{-n}R^{\prime}}|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{2^{-n}R^{\prime}}),\,n=0,1,\dots,N-1.

Then from (
4.38
) it follows that

|\xi_{r}-\xi_{R^{\prime}}|^{2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sqrt{\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{2^{-n}R^{\prime}})}\right)^{2}\\
\lesssim\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}2^{-n\alpha}\sqrt{\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})}\right)^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u;B_{R^{\prime}})

(4.53)

Now the bound (
4.40
) follows by applying the triangle inequality and (
4.39
), (
4.38
) and (
4.53
):

\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u,B_{r})+\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\xi_{r}+\nabla\phi_{\xi_{r}}|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u,B_{r})+|\xi_{r}|^{2}\\
\leq\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u,B_{r})+|\xi_{r}-\xi_{R}|^{2}+|\xi_{R}|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u,B_{R})+|\xi_{R}|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,.

(4.54)

In the last step we used the chain of inequalities

|\xi_{R}|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla\phi_{\xi_{R}}+\xi_{R}|^{2}\leq\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u,B_{R})+\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{2}
and the definition of excess, which is defined as an infimum.
∎

Next, we prove an analogue of Proposition
4.2
for degenerate elliptic equations with nonzero source term.

Proposition 4.3
.

Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
r_{*}
and
\widetilde{B}_{R}
be as in Proposition
4.2
.
Suppose that
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in(L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{d}
,
h\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are related as

-\nabla\cdot a(\nabla u+g)=\chi_{\mathcal{M}}h\qquad\text{in}\ \widetilde{B}_{R}\,.

(4.55)

Then we have

\sup_{r\in[r_{*},R]}\frac{1}{r^{2\alpha}}\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u+g;B_{r})\lesssim\frac{1}{R^{2\alpha}}\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u+g;B_{R})\\
+\sup_{r\in[r_{*},R]}\frac{1}{r^{2\alpha}}\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\left|g-\fint_{B_{r}\cap\mathcal{M}}g\right|^{2}+r^{2}|h|^{2}\right)\,.

(4.56)

Furthermore, we have

\sup_{r\in[r_{*},R]}\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u+g|^{2}\lesssim\fint_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u+g|^{2}\\
+\sup_{r\in[r_{*},R]}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{2\alpha}\fint_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\left|g-\fint_{B_{r}\cap\mathcal{M}}g\right|^{2}+r^{2}|h|^{2}\right)\,.

(4.57)

Here
\lesssim
means
\leq C(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and put
\alpha^{\prime}:=\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\in(\alpha,1)
. Let
r_{*}
be the radius corresponding to the Hölder exponent
\alpha^{\prime}
.
Let
r_{*}\leq r\leq\rho\leq R
. We claim that

\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u+g;B_{r})\leq C_{1}(d,a_{\pm},\alpha)\left[\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{2\alpha^{\prime}}\mathrm{Exc}(\nabla u+g;B_{\rho})\right.\\
\left.+\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{d}\fint_{B_{\rho}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\left|g-\fint_{B_{\rho}\cap\mathcal{M}}g\right|^{2}+\rho^{2}|h|^{2}\right)\right].

(4.58)

In order to show this, put
\zeta:=\fint_{B_{\rho}\cap\mathcal{M}}g
and let
w
be the solution of

-\nabla\cdot a\nabla w=\nabla\cdot(a(g-\zeta))+\chi_{\mathcal{M}}h\quad\text{in}\ \widetilde{B}_{\rho-1},\quad w=0\quad\text{on}\ \partial\widetilde{B}_{\rho-1},

(4.59)

where
\widetilde{B}_{\rho-1}
is defined in the same way as
\widetilde{B}_{R}
from the statement of the proposition. It is not hard to see that (
4.59
) implies that the function
x\mapsto u+\zeta\cdot x-w
is
a
-harmonic in
\widetilde{B}_{\rho}
. Hence, by Proposition
4.2
we have

Appendix A

The purpose of this appendix is to provide a proof of a version of the classical hole filling lemma in the setting of perforated domains.

For this, we shall need the following simple lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

For

Let us begin by observing that the functional

to

0=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\eta^{2}\phi_{T}(\phi_{T}-c)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\nabla(\eta^{2}(\phi_{T}-c))\\
=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\eta^{2}\phi_{T}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta^{2}a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\cdot(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\\
-\frac{c}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\eta^{2}\phi_{T}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\eta(\phi_{T}-c)a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\cdot\nabla\eta-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta^{2}a(\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e})\cdot\mathbf{e}.

(A.12)

The latter implies, via Young’s inequality,

\int_{B_{2R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\eta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e}|^{2}\right)\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\int_{B_{2R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\eta^{2}|c|^{2}+R^{d}+\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_{R+1}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla\eta|^{2}|\phi_{T}-c|^{2}\\
\lesssim R^{d}\left(\frac{|c|^{2}}{T}+1\right)+\frac{1}{R^{2}}\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_{R+1}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}|\phi_{T}-c|^{2}\\
\lesssim R^{d}\left(\frac{|c|^{2}}{T}+1\right)+\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_{R+1}}|\nabla\phi_{T}^{\rm ext}|^{2}\,.

(A.13)

In the last step above we used Lemma
A.1
(adapted to the annulus) and Poincaré’s inequality.

By means of Jensen’s inequality and the properties of the extension, the estimate (
A.13
) yields

\int_{B_{R}}\chi_{M}\left(\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e}|^{2}+1\right)\\
\leq C_{0}\int_{B_{2R+1}\setminus B_{R}}\chi_{M}\left(\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e}|^{2}+1\right)\,.

(A.14)

Note that in the RHS of (
A.14
) we slightly enlarged the domain of integration: this accounts for the extension domains of the inclusions intersecting the boundary of
B_{2R}\setminus B_{R+1}
when estimating
\nabla\phi_{T}^{\rm ext}
by
\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\nabla\phi_{T}
.

Formula (
A.14
) in turn implies

\int_{B_{R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e}|^{2}+1\right)\\
\leq\theta\int_{B_{3R}}\chi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+|\nabla\phi_{T}+\mathbf{e}|^{2}+1\right)

(A.15)

with
\theta=\frac{C_{0}}{1+C_{0}}
.

By iterating (
A.15
) we arrive at (
A.10
).
∎
