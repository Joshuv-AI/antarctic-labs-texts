Two approaches to average stochastic perturbations of integrable systems.
Sergei Kuksin

Abstract
. We discuss two approaches to study the long-time behaviour and infinite-time behavious of solutions for integrable hamiltonian systems under small stochastic perturbations. Then we compare these results with those for deterministic perturbations of integrable systems.



1.
The setting

In symplectic space
(\mathbb{R}^{2n}_{x,y},dx\wedge dy)
we consider a Birkhoff–integrable Hamiltonian system – its
Hamiltonian
H
depends only on the actions
I_{j}=(x_{j}^{2}+y_{j}^{2})/2
. Introducing complex coordinates

v_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}
we write this system in the convenient complex form:

(1.1)

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{k}(t)=i\nabla_{k}H(I)v_{k},\;\;\;k=1,\dots,n,\quad I=I(v),\quad v(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Note that
I_{j}=\frac{1}{2}|v_{j}|^{2}
, and that
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
we use the real scalar
product

(v,w)\mapsto\Re\langle v,\bar{w}\rangle=\Re\sum v_{j}\bar{w}_{j}.

Study of small perturbations of this system for large values of time, either globally in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, or locally near an
equilibrium, is a classical problem of dynamical systems starting the end on XVIII century.
1
1

1

Another
classical question is to study solutions of perturbed systems near the tori
\{v:I_{k}(v)=I_{k}^{0}\neq 0,k=1,\dots,n\}
,
which are invariant for system (
1.1
). We do not touch it here.

There are two main settings:

A) the perturbation is a small smooth vector field,

B) the perturbation is a small Hamiltonian field.

We will consider a third case and then compare the obtained results with what is known for the cases A) and B).

Consider an
\varepsilon
-small stochastic perturbation of the integrable system (
1.1
). Firstly, only
for
0\leq t\leq T\varepsilon^{-1}
:

(1.2)

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{k}(t)=i{\nabla_{k}H}(I)v_{k}+\varepsilon P_{k}(v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}B_{kj}(v)\dot{\beta}_{j}^{c}(t),\;\;\;\qquad v(0)=v^{0},

where
k=1,\dots,n
,
and
\{\beta_{k}^{c}(t),1\leq k\leq n\}
are standard independent complex Wiener processes. That is,

\beta_{k}^{c}(t)=\beta_{k}^{+}(t)+i\beta_{k}^{-}(t)
, and
\beta_{k}^{\pm}(t)
are standard independent real
Wiener processes. It is convenient to
pass to the slow time
\tau=\varepsilon t
and re-write the
v
-equations above as

(1.3)

\dot{v}_{k}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla_{k}H(I)v_{k}+P_{k}(v)+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}B_{kj}(v)\dot{\beta}_{j}^{c}(\tau),\;\;\;k=1,\dots,n,

v(0)=v^{0}
. Here and below the upper dot stands for
d/d\tau
, and
\{\beta_{j}^{c}(\tau)\}
is another
collection of standard independent complex Wiener processes.
Or, in vector notation, as

(1.4)

\dot{v}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}\text{diag}\{{\nabla_{k}H(I)}\}v+P(v)+B(v)\dot{\beta}^{c}(\tau),\quad v(0)=v^{0},

where
v(\tau)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
0\leq\tau\leq T
. A solution of this equation will be denoted
v^{\varepsilon}(\tau)
.

We impose the following assumptions on equation (
1.4
):

(A)
1) (Anosov’s non-degeneracy):
For a.a.
I\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
components of the frequency vector
\nabla H(I)
are
independent over integers. That is,

(1.5)

if
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\nabla H(I)\cdot s=0
,   then
s=0
.

2) Vector-functions
\nabla H(I)
,
P(v)
and operator-function

B(v)
are locally Lipschitz, with at most polynomial growth at infinity.

3) The
v
-equation (
1.4
) is well posed for
0\leq\tau\leq T
and there the first few moments of
its solutions are bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
.

4) The noice in the equation
is non-degenerate: the rank of matrix
B(v)
equals
n
for all
v
(so
n_{1}\geq n
).

Remark 1.1
.

Assumption 1) (Anosov’s non-degeneracy) holds e.g. if
\nabla H
is a constant vector field with some
components
\lambda_{j}
, independent over integers. Then the unperturbed system (
1.1
)
is linear,

(1.6)

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{k}=i\lambda_{k}v_{k},\quad k=1,\dots,n.

The theorems below apply to this case,
but their stronger versions may be found in our previous work
[
4
]
. The results of
[
4
]

apply when the matrix
B(v)
in (
1.4
) is degenerate, and their suitable versions hold if the
frequencies
\{\lambda_{j}\}
are dependent over integers.
It is easy to see that 1) also holds if
H
is
C^{2}
-smooth and

\text{meas}\{I:\det d^{2}H(I)=0\}=0

(this is the Kolmogorov nondegeneracy condition).

Assumption 3) (the apriori estimates) is valid, for example,
if the matrix
B(v)
is uniformly bounded, and

\Re\langle P(v),\bar{v}\rangle\leq C|v|^{2}+C_{1}\qquad\forall v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

2.
First approach:
the fast-slow system.

Let us introduce in
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\{v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\}
the usual action-angle coordinates
(I,\varphi)
, where

I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n})\in\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{n}
:

I_{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}|v_{j}|^{2},\quad\varphi_{j}=\arg v_{j},\;\;\;j=1,\dots,n

(so
v_{j}=\sqrt{2I_{j}}\exp{i\varphi_{j}}
).
Then
v
-equation (
1.4
)
may be re-written in terms of these coordinates. The equations for the actions are:

(2.1)

\dot{I}^{\varepsilon}_{k}(\tau)=\Re(\bar{v}^{\varepsilon}_{k}\,P_{k}(v^{\varepsilon}))+\sum_{l}|B_{kl}(v^{\varepsilon})|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\Re\big(\bar{v}^{\varepsilon}_{k}B_{kj}(v^{\varepsilon})\big)\dot{\beta}_{j}(\tau),

where
k=1,\dots,n
and

\beta_{1}(\tau),\dots,\beta_{n_{1}}(\tau)
are independent standard real Wiener processes. (Recall that
in
\mathbb{C}
we use the real scalar product
(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\Re(z_{1},\bar{z}_{2})
).
Re-denoting the terms of these equations and using vector notation, we write system (
2.1
)
in the vector form:

(2.2)

\dot{I}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)=F(v^{\varepsilon})+G(v^{\varepsilon})\dot{\beta}(\tau),\qquad I^{\varepsilon}(0)=I^{0}:=I(v^{0}),

where
0\leq\tau\leq T
.
The
\varphi
- equations are:

\dot{\varphi}^{\varepsilon}_{k}(\tau)=\varepsilon^{-1}{\nabla_{k}H}(I^{\varepsilon})+\text{ term of order one with strong singularity at}\;\{I^{\varepsilon}_{k}=0\},

1\leq k\leq n
.
We have got a
slow-fast system
, where the actions

I^{\varepsilon}_{k}
’s are
slow variables
and the angles
\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{k}
’s are
fast variables.
As usual, we care the most
about the actions.
The logic of averaging suggests that their limiting behaviour as
\varepsilon\to 0

can be described by averaging in angles
\varphi
of the slow system (
2.2
).

Accordingly, let us average (
2.2
) in the fast variables
\varphi
,
using the rules of stochastic averaging:

(2.3)

\dot{I}(\tau)=\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle(I)+\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle(I)\dot{\beta}(\tau),\;\;\;I(0)=I^{0},\quad I(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.

Here
\beta(\tau)
is the standard Wiener process in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The vector field

\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle(I)
is the averaging in
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
of the field
F
, written as
F(I,\varphi)
. I.e.,

(2.4)

\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle(I)=\tfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F(I,\varphi)\,d\varphi.

Since

\ v_{j}=r_{j}e^{i\varphi_{j}}
,
where

r_{j}=\sqrt{2I_{j}}
,
and
F
is locally Lipschitz in
v
, then
\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle
is a locally Lipschitz vector-function of
r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
. So this is a
Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
function of
I
.
The real
n\times n
-matrix
\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle(I)
is an averaging of
G(I,\varphi)
, defined in a related, but
more complicated way, which still is explicit; see
[
3
]
and references there. A-priori it also is only Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
smooth. But

(2.5)

\begin{split}\text{if $B$ is a constant matrix, then $\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle=\,$diag$\,\big(b_{k}\sqrt{2I_{k}}\big)$, where all $b_{k}$ are positive numbers}.\end{split}

A partial description of the limiting as
\varepsilon\to 0

dynamics of actions in eq. (
1.4
) is
given by the following result (for a proof see
[
3
, Section 6]
, and see
[
8
]
for a similar assertion).

Theorem 2.1
.

The collection of curves of actions
I^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\ (0\leq\tau\leq T)

of solutions
v^{\varepsilon}(\tau)
for (
1.4
)
with
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
is precompact in the space of curves, in the
sense of distribution.

1) Every limiting (in the sense of distribution) random process

I^{0}(\tau)
is a weak solution of the averaged equation (
2.3
).

2) Eq. (
2.3
) always has at least one
solution
I^{0}(\tau)
. If such a solution is unique, then
I^{\varepsilon}(\tau)
converges
to
I^{0}(\tau)
in the sense of distribution as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We recall that in (
2.3
)
\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle
and
\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle
are Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
function of
I
,
so there is no reason for this equation
to have only one solution. Related to that we have the following facts (see
[
3
]
):

i) A result of H.Whitney
[
5
]
implies that
if the coefficients of the
v
-equation are
C^{2}
-smooth, then
\langle\langle F\rangle\rangle(I)
is
C^{1}
-smooth,
and

\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle(I)=\sqrt{K(I)}
, where matrix
K(I)
is
C^{1}
-smooth, selfadjoint non-negative,
and det
\,K(I)=0
if some
I_{j}
vanishes.

This is better than before, but still
\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle(I)
has a
\sqrt{I_{j}}
-singularity when
I_{j}=0
, and it is not
clear if solutions for equations like that are unique.

ii) Let in (
1.4
) the dispersion matrix
B
is constant. Then by (
2.5
)
the noise in (
2.3
)
is given by a (non-constant)
diagonal matrix which has square-root singularities when some
I_{j}
vanishes.
For this case the result of the paper
[
6
]

implies that eq. (
2.3
) has a unique solution. So item 2) of Theorem
2.1
applies, and (
2.3
) describes
the limiting behaviour of actions of solutions
v^{\varepsilon}(\tau)

for
0\leq\tau\leq T
.

But what happens if the matrix
B(v)
is not constant? Then
\langle\langle G\rangle\rangle(I)=\sqrt{K(I)}

has complicated Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
singularities when
I_{j}=0
for some
j
.
No uniqueness result is known for this case. On the contrary,

iii) In paper
[
7
]
the authors give an example of a system with Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
singularity at
I_{j}=0
where a solution
is not unique.

Conclusion.
Equation (
2.3
) describes the limiting dynamics of
actions of solutions
v^{\varepsilon}(\tau)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, only if in (
1.4
)

P(v)
is
C^{2}
-smooth and
B
is a constant matrix.

What can we do in the general case?

3.
Second approach: the effective equation.

Let us come back to the original
v
-equation (
1.4
).
I recall that the celebrated Krylov–Bogolyubov averaging for perturbations of linear conservative systems in

\mathbb{R}^{2n}
(see in
[
1
]
)
is performed in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
WITHOUT passing to the action-angles. It describes the limiting dynamics
of actions via
certain
effective equation
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, derived using the
interaction representation
.
2
2

2

Which is
some non-autonomous change of variables in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
; e.g.
see in
[
4
,
9
]
.

The latter does not exist for our
v
-equation (
1.4
), where the unperturbed equation (
1.1
) is
non linear.
Still, motivated by Krylov–Bogolyubov we GUESS a suitable effective equation.
It is obtained from the
v
-equation by:

– dropping its first term,

– suitably averaging the other two terms.

So the equation is

(3.1)

\dot{a}(\tau)=\langle\langle P\rangle\rangle(a)+\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle(a)\dot{\beta}^{c}(\tau),\qquad a(\tau)\lx@nobreakspace\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\quad a(0)\!=\!v^{0}.

Here the averaging of the vector field
P
is

\langle\langle P\rangle\rangle(a)=\tfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\Phi_{\omega}\circ P(\Phi_{-\omega}a)\,d\omega,

where
\Phi_{\omega}
is the rotation operator in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\Phi_{\omega}=\text{diag}\big(e^{i\omega_{1}},\dots,e^{i\omega_{n}}\big),\;\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{n}.

The averaging
\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle(a)
of the matrix-field
B(a)
is more complicated, but still is explicit.
This
\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle
is a non-degenerate complex
n\times n
-matrix.
Vector field
\langle\langle P\rangle\rangle(a)
and matrix field
\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle(a)
are as smooth as the components

P(v)
and
B(v)
of the
v
-equation. See in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the imposed assumptions A1)–A4) effective equation (
3.1
)
has a unique solution
a(\tau)
, which inherits the estimates in A3).
When
\varepsilon\to 0
, the actions
I^{\varepsilon}(\tau)=I(v^{\varepsilon}(\tau))
of solutions for
v
-equation (
1.4
)
with
0\leq\tau\leq T
converge in distribution to the actions of
a(\tau)
.

Now in addition to assumptions A1)–A4) let us assume that

A5)
v
-equation (
1.4
) is well posed for
\tau\geq 0
, and the first few moments
of its solutions are bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
and in
\tau

(this is an amplification of assumption A3) ).

Remark 3.2
.

Assumption A5) holds if
B(v)
is uniformly bounded, and
P(v)
is coercive:

(3.2)

\Re\langle P(v),\bar{v}\rangle\leq-\alpha_{1}|v|+\alpha_{2},\quad\alpha_{1}>0.

(This means that the vector field
P
“contains friction”.)

Theorem 3.3
.

If assumptions A1)-A5) are satisfied, then the solution
a(\tau)
of effective equation (
3.1
)
meets the estimates in A3) uniformly in
\tau\geq 0
,
and the convergence in Theorem
3.1
also holds uniformly in time
\tau
.

The proof of this result crucially uses the fact that since the first moments of solutions
a(\tau)
are
bounded uniformly in time and matrix
\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle(a)
is non-degenerate, then equation (
3.1
) is
mixing: in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
exists a measure such that for any
v^{0}
the distribution of solution
a(\tau)

converges to it, as
\tau\to\infty
. See
[
3
, Section 10]
and
[
4
, Section 7]
.

I recall that a vector field
\tilde{P}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is hamiltonian with a
C^{1}
-smooth
real
Hamiltonian
h
,
if

\tilde{P}_{k}(v)=2i\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\bar{v}_{k}}h(v),\qquad k=1,\dots,n.

Assume that in (
1.4
)

\;P(v)=P_{1}(v)+P_{2}(v),

where the field
P_{2}
is hamiltonian.

Theorem 3.4
.

If
P(v)
is as above, then the effective equation may be modified by removing
the averaging of the hamiltonian part
P_{2}
:

(3.3)

\dot{a}(\tau)=\langle\langle P_{1}\rangle\rangle(a)+\langle\langle B\rangle\rangle(a)\dot{\beta}^{c}(\tau),\qquad v^{\varepsilon}(0)\!=\!v^{0}.

That is, the assertions of Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
remain true if for
a(\tau)
one takes a solution of equation
(
3.3
).

In
[
9
]
this result is established for perturbations of linear systems (
1.6
). A proof for solutions of
(
1.4
) is similar and uses in addition the construction from
[
3
, Section 8]
.

4.
Local versions of the results.

Our starting assumption was that the unperturbed integrable system can be put to the global Birkhoff normal form
(
1.1
). This is restrictive. Now let us assume that the Birkhoff normal
form holds only locally, for
v\in Q
, where
Q
is a neighbourhood of the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the form

\{v:|v_{j}|\ \leq C_{j}>0,j=1,\dots,n\}
.
3
3

3

This may be achieved by using the Vey theorem, see
[
2
]
.

Consider the same perturbed
v
-equation as before:

\dot{v}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}\text{diag}\{{\nabla_{k}H(I)}\}v+P(v)+B(v)\dot{\beta}^{c}(\tau),\quad v(0)=v^{0}\in Q.

Then the assertions of Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
hold for
0\leq\tau\leq\theta
, where
\theta
is the random moment of time
when a solution of
(
1.4
) exits
Q
. This is a random variable of order one:

\text{probability that }\ \theta\leq\lambda\quad\text{is bounded by }\;C\sqrt{\lambda},\quad\forall\,\lambda>0.

See
[
3
, Section 9]
.

5.
Applications.

A more detailed discussion of applications of out results to the equations below see in
[
3
]
.

5.1.
Chains of oscillators

For the unperturbed integrable system (
1.1
) let us
take a chain of nonlinear oscillators:

(5.1)

\ddot{q}_{k}=-Q(q_{k}),\quad k=1,\dots,n,

where the potential
Q
is a smooth function, which is convex and odd. Assume that it is “nice” at infinity, while near zero

Q(q)=\alpha q+\beta q^{3}+o(|q|^{4}),\quad\alpha,\beta>0.

Then this system is globally integrable and may be written in the form (
1.1
).
Theorems
3.1
-
3.4
apply to study its stochastic perturbations. The results obtained in this way are
related to the non-equilibrium statistical physics.

5.2.
Damped/driven hamiltonian systems

Consider the following
v
-equation in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
:

(5.2)

\dot{v}_{k}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}{\nabla_{k}H}\big(I(v)\big)v_{k}+2i\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\bar{v}_{k}}h(v)-\nu_{k}v_{k}+b_{k}\dot{\beta}_{k}^{c}(\tau),\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

where
\nu_{k}>0
and
b_{k}\neq 0
for all
k
. Let
H,h\in C^{2}
, the frequency map
I\mapsto\nabla H(I)
is
Anosov-nondegenerate and A5) is fulfilled.
4
4

4

In particular, A5) holds if the hamiltonian field

i\nabla_{\bar{v}}h(v)
meets (
3.2
) with
\alpha_{1}=0
.

Then the assumptions A1)-A5) hold. The averaging does not change the vector field

\dot{v}=-
diag
\{\nu_{k}\}v,

so in view of
Theorem
3.3
, for a modified effective equation one may take the system

(5.3)

\dot{a}_{k}(\tau)=-\nu_{k}a_{k}+b_{k}\dot{\beta}_{k}^{c}(\tau),\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

For small
\varepsilon
the actions of solutions for eq. (
5.3
) approximate (in distribution)
the actions of solutions for eq. (
5.2
), uniformly in time. Note that eq. (
5.3
) may be integrated explicitly. (Its solution is a collection of
n

independent complex Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes.)

6.
Discussion

First we compare the results above with those, available for non-random perturbations of integrable
Hamiltonian systems.

6.1.
Hamiltonian perturbations of integrable systems

Consider the following perturbed integrable
system, written in the slow time
\tau
:

(6.1)

\dot{v}_{k}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}{\nabla_{k}H}\big(I(v)\big)v_{k}+2i\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\bar{v}_{k}}h(v),\quad 1\leq k\leq n,\qquad v(0)=v^{0}.

If
H
and
h
are analytic, then under the
steepness assumption on Hamiltonian
H
, Nekhoroshev’s theorem (see in
[
1
]
)
applies, provided that
v^{0}
lies
not too close to the locus

X=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:I_{k}(v)=0\;\;\text{for some}\;\;k\}.

It claims that for times
\tau\lesssim\!\exp{\varepsilon^{-a}}
,
a>0
, actions of a solution
v(\tau)

for (
6.1
) stay close to
the original action-vector
I(v^{0})
. Theorem
3.3
above deals with perturbed equations (
1.4
)
and allows to control actions of their solutions uniformly in time. This happens since the perturbation in
(
1.4
) contains noice and friction (see Remark
3.2
) which stabilise the dynamics.
We also note that in Theorem
3.3
we had to assume that the unperturbed system is
integrable in the whole space, while for the validity of Nekhoroshev’s theorem it suffices that the system is
integrable in some open domain which contains
v^{0}
(and is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{T}^{n}\times O
, where

O
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

Nekhoroshev’s theorem does not allow to take initial data
v^{0}
close to
X
since its proof uses the
action-angle coordinates, which are singular at
X
, while Theorem
3.3
does not need them. We note
that for some class of perturbed systems (
6.1
) Niederman in
[
10
]
established a version of
Nekhoroshev’s theorem in the vicinity of the origin, avoiding the action-angles. So his result applies to
solutions of (
6.1
) with small
v^{0}
. It seems that for the moment of writing there is no version of
Nekhoroshev’s theorem, valid for solutions with initial data in
X
, or close to it.

6.2.
Arbitrary perturbations of integrable systems

Now consider perturbed equations

(6.2)

\dot{v}_{k}(\tau)=i\varepsilon^{-1}{\nabla_{k}H}\big(I(v)\big)v_{k}+P_{k}(v),\quad 1\leq k\leq n,\qquad v(0)=v^{0},

where

and

-smooth. Let

[
1
]
) and

be a random vector,
v^{0}=v^{0}(\omega)
.
Then by the
Neishtadt–Bakhtin theorem for random parameters
\omega

outside some set of small measure
\ \lesssim\varepsilon^{b}
,
b>0
,
5
5

5

In particular, the initial data

v^{0}(\omega)
should avoid the vicinity of
X
.

for
0\leq\tau\leq T
actions of solutions for
eq. (
6.2
) stay close to solutions for the averaged equation for actions:

\dot{I}(\tau)=\langle\langle P\rangle\rangle(I),\qquad I(0)=I^{0},

where
\langle\langle P\rangle\rangle
is the averaging of

, defined as in
(
2.4
). This result is obviously related to our Theorem
3.1
, to its
local version, discussed in Section
4
, and to Theorem
3.3
.
The assertion of Theorem
3.3
is significantly stronger
than the result of Neishtadt–Bakhtin.
Again, this happens since the perturbed equation (

stabilised by noise and friction.
