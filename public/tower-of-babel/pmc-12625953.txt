Retention and effectiveness of group interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT-G) to treat depression at scale in Uganda and Zambia
Authors: Frey B Assefa, Leah Tanner, Hae-Young Kim, Ingrida Platais, Costella Mbabazi Tindyebwa, Roscoe Kasujja, Anna Bershteyn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepression is the most common mental disorder in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Group interpersonal therapy (IPT-G) is a recommended first-line treatment for depression, shown to be safe and effective in clinical trials. However, less is known about its real-world retention and effectiveness when delivered at scale in SSA. We describe retention patterns and associated factors in a large IPT-G programme in Uganda and Zambia.MethodsWe analysed routine data from adults (≥18 years) who initiated community-based IPT-G in Uganda and Zambia (2020–2021). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), and individuals with probable depression were offered IPT-G. We classified clients based on their attendance across eight IPT-G sessions using latent class analysis. Sociodemographic and programme-related factors associated with latent classes were examined using multinomial logistic regression. Changes in PHQ-9 scores from pretreatment to post-treatment were compared across latent classes. Statistical significance of differences was assessed using the Wald χ2 test.ResultsAmong 45 349 clients, overall average attendance was 82%. Four classes emerged from attendance patterns: high attendance (63%), moderate attendance (27%), early dropout (6%) and late dropout (4%). Relative to the high attendance class, the early drop-out class had higher odds of being age <25 (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.19, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.44), in teletherapy (aOR 3.46, 95% CI 2.89 to 4.13) and presenting with moderate than moderately severe to severe depression (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.40). The overall mean reduction in PHQ-9 scores was 13.0 (SD 4.2), but the early drop-out group showed smaller improvements (10.32, SD 8.78) compared with the high attendance group (13.3, SD 6.15, p=0.001).ConclusionRetention in a scaled IPT-G programme in Uganda and Zambia was high. Still, early dropout—linked to younger age, teletherapy and moderate depression—was associated with less reduction in depressive symptoms. These findings highlight areas for innovation in IPT-G implementation to improve engagement and outcomes.Keywords: Mental Health & Psychiatry, Health Services Accessibility, Cohort study, Global Health, Decision Making

Introduction

Depression is the most common mental disorder in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where estimated lifetime prevalence in the general population ranges from 3.3% to 9.8%.1 2 Depression, also known as major depressive disorder, is characterised by decreased mood, loss of interest in activities and functional impairment. Despite the immense need for mental health services in SSA, there is only one psychiatrist and nine mental health nurses per million residents in this region—far below the global average.3 Country-level data highlight this gap: Uganda has one psychiatrist per million residents, while Zambia has just 0.25 psychiatrists per million residents—only three in total for a population of 12 million.4 5 As a result, the mental health treatment gap in low- and middle-income countries is estimated to be as high as 85%, compared with 40% in high-income countries.6

Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) and its group adaptation, group interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT-G), is an evidence-based therapy modality that is time-limited and structured, focused on improving interpersonal relationships and social support. IPT-G is recommended by WHO as a first-line strategy for treating depression in SSA and other resource-limited settings.7 IPT-G can be delivered by lay health workers and has been found effective and durable at reducing depressive symptoms in multiple randomised, controlled trials in SSA.8,11 Additional benefits of IPT-G include improved function scores, household food security and children’s school attendance.12

Although IPT-G has been shown to have high efficacy in clinical trials, less is known about its effectiveness when delivered at scale in real-world programmes. A particular concern is premature discontinuation of therapy, which is a prevalent phenomenon in many forms of mental healthcare and is known to hinder otherwise effective psychological interventions.13 Retention in psychotherapy generally refers to a client’s continued engagement in therapy until their treatment goals are achieved or a course of treatment is completed.13 In resource-limited settings, structural and social barriers to retention in mental healthcare can be especially onerous.14 15 Structural barriers include travel, access to technology and competing responsibilities such as work and childcare.14 16 17 Social and attitudinal barriers include social stigma, personal stigma and lack of psychoeducation.18,21 Understanding the patterns and determinants of retention in depression therapy is important to curtail the negative consequences of premature treatment discontinuation.

We sought to understand patterns of retention in IPT-G and their association with client characteristics and therapy outcomes in real-world SSA settings. We analysed service delivery data from StrongMinds, a non-governmental organisation (NGO) that has provided free-of-cost community-based psychotherapy in low-income, rural regions of Uganda and Zambia since 2013. Our analysis sought to characterise (1) IPT-G retention patterns, (2) client and programme characteristic predictors of retention patterns and (3) the association between therapy retention patterns and reduction in depressive symptoms. These findings could help mental health programmes account for realistic IPT-G attendance when designing IPT-G delivery models, especially those delivered at scale in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Group interpersonal psychotherapy

StrongMinds provides IPT-G to individuals primarily in low-income and rural areas free of cost. At StrongMinds, IPT-G consists of a series of group counselling sessions led by a healthcare worker or trained peer or lay person. IPT-G sessions are held in person in healthcare or community settings, or, in an adaptation during the COVID-19 pandemic, via telephone conference call. IPT-G facilitators engage a group of clients with moderate to severe depressive symptoms in structured discussions and skill-building activities. Facilitators could be peers who underwent several months of training in IPT-G or staff with degrees in counselling or community psychology. The purpose of IPT-G sessions is to identify depression triggers and learn to leverage interpersonal skills and support systems to overcome mental health challenges over the treatment period as well as in the long term.7 A typical IPT-G schedule consists of 6 to 16 weekly sessions with the same facilitator and group of clients. IPT-G sessions are divided into three phases distinguished by different objectives. The initial phase focuses on building rapport and trust among group members. The middle phase or ‘working’ phase identifies depression triggers and undertakes collective, iterative problem-solving. Homework is assigned to clients in the working phase and administered on the phone if clients miss sessions. The final phase re-evaluates symptoms and develops individualised plans to manage triggers and sustain coping mechanisms.

Study design and population

We analysed deidentified individual-level programme data from StrongMinds, an international NGO that has delivered IPT-G at scale to over 600 000 clients in multiple SSA countries.22 The study population included 45 349 clients aged 18 years and older initiating an 8-session IPT-G programme in community-based settings in 42 Ugandan districts and 27 Zambian districts between 2020 and 2021. Recruitment into the StrongMinds programme was conducted using community outreach, with interested individuals referred to one-on-one depression screening with a facilitator. IPT-G was then offered to individuals with probable major depression at baseline, as indicated by a score of 10 or more points on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). The PHQ-9 was conducted in English or Luganda based on individuals’ preferences. Individuals requiring more intensive treatment, such as pharmacotherapy or inpatient care, were referred to local health systems. Individuals deemed eligible for IPT-G and providing informed consent were organised into therapy groups and assigned facilitators who were either professional facilitators or former clients (ie, peers) trained in facilitation and mentored by staff. Facilitators were also trained in data protection and privacy best practices to maintain the confidentiality of therapy sessions and data.

Measures

Demographic and programme characteristics

Participant characteristics included age (18–24 or >25), gender (woman or man) and country (Uganda or Zambia). Programme characteristics included facilitator type (peer or non-peer) and therapy delivery modality (in-person or by telephone). Peer facilitators are defined as trained non-specialist facilitators working in the community who previously completed IPT-G. Non-peer facilitators are StrongMinds or partner organisation staff with a degree or certification in psychology or community counselling.

IPT-G engagement

Engagement in IPT-G was defined as whether a participant attended or was absent from each session for the 8-session programme.

Depression

Depression was measured at baseline and at the end of therapy using the PHQ-9. The PHQ-9 has been previously validated among people living with HIV and individuals in the general population in Uganda23,25 and Zambia.25,27 It was also validated in other similar settings such as South Africa and Tanzania.28 29 PHQ-9 scores range from 0 to 27, with a result of 10 points or greater indicating probable clinical depression. The change in depression score from baseline to termination of therapy was calculated by subtracting the termination score from the baseline score. Baseline depression severity was defined as moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19) or severe (20–27). At the conclusion of treatment, a mean score improvement of >5 points is considered clinically significant, and clinically large if more than 10 points.30

Statistical analysis

To identify subgroups of IPT-G clients with similar underlying patterns of therapy retention, we used the three-step bias-adjusted latent class analysis method as theorised by Bolck et al.31 In the first classification step, the eight attendance variables of session 1 to session 8 were included as indicators for the latent classes. We started with a one-class model and added one additional class at a time. The final model was selected based on fit indices (Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion, entropy), the bootstrap likelihood ratio test, class prevalence estimates and class interpretability.32 The results from the bootstrap likelihood ratio test and the specific details of the Latent Class Analysis (LCA) model selection are outlined in the online supplemental materials. In the second step, we classified individuals into the latent classes based on their maximum posterior probability of class membership. In the third step, we used bivariate and adjusted multinomial logistic regression to predict class membership as a function of demographic characteristics and programme characteristics, including age, gender, baseline depression severity, facilitator type, IPT-G mode of delivery and country of IPT-G delivery. In the fourth and final step, we compared the change in PHQ-9 score from baseline to therapy termination across the latent classes. We also conducted a stratified analysis by gender to assess whether retention and its predictors differed for men and women. Analyses were performed using the SAS package PROC LCA.33,37

We performed multiple imputation for variables with <90% completeness that we determined to be missing at random. For these variables, we performed multiple imputation in SAS, using the factors associated with missingness as auxiliary variables (online supplemental table S1). Data were imputed 35 times, and analysis was repeated for each imputed dataset. Final results were then pooled across imputed datasets. Sensitivity analysis of the LCA was conducted on observed complete cases (online supplemental section 4).

Patient and public involvement

IPT-G clients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Of these, 18% had missing data on age, 11% had missing data on at least one session attendance variable and 9% had missing data on baseline depression severity. IPT-G facilitator type and therapy mode of delivery were significantly associated with missing age, attendance and baseline depression data, which were then used as auxiliary variables in multiple imputation.

Class membership

Four classes of IPT-G retention patterns emerged from latent class analysis, with model fit indices and average posterior probabilities provided in online supplemental section 3: high attendance (62.6%), moderate attendance (26.7%), early dropout (6.5%) and late drop-out (4.3%), each with distinct probabilities of attendance across the sessions (figure 2). The high attendance class consisted of individuals with a >90% probability of attending each IPT-G session. The moderate attendance class had a range of 70–90% probability of attending each IPT-G session. The early drop-out class began with a 72% probability of attending the first session, with the probability of attendance dropping to <10% by the fifth session. The late drop-out class was characterised by >70% attending the first four sessions and <60% attending subsequent sessions, with <10% attending the penultimate session. Stratified analysis by gender suggested similar latent class structures among men and women (online supplemental section 5).

Probability of attending each of eight IPT-G (group interpersonal therapy) sessions in each latent class of attendance patterns. Parenthetical values indicate the proportion of clients that fall within each class.

Predictors of latent class membership

Demographic and programme characteristics

Several factors were associated with membership in lower-attendance classes relative to the high attendance class (reference class) in the bivariate and adjusted results (table 2). Teletherapy and IPT-G in Zambia were significantly associated with membership in all three lower-attendance classes (early dropout, late dropout, moderate attendance) in both bivariate and adjusted models. Specifically, teletherapy was significantly associated with membership in the early drop-out class compared with high attendance when adjusting for covariates (adjusted OR (aOR) 3.46; 95% CI 2.89 to 4.13; p<0.001). Compared with Zambia, IPT-G clients in Uganda were significantly less likely to fall into the early drop-out class than the high attendance class (aOR 0.59; 95% CI 0.47 to 0.72; p<0.001). Younger age was significantly associated with membership in all lower-attendance classes, including early dropout (OR 1.38; 95% CI 1.26 to 1.51; p<0.001), in the bivariate results but this association was not significant in the adjusted models.

Compared with moderately severe to severe depression at baseline, clients with moderate depression were also significantly more likely to belong to all lower-attendance classes, including early dropout, in both bivariate (OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.19 to 1.40; p<0.001) and adjusted models (aOR 1.27; 95% CI 1.17 to 1.40; p<0.001). Non-peer-led IPT-G was significantly associated with membership in the late-dropout class in both bivariate and adjusted results, whereas it was associated with membership in the early-dropout class in the bivariate model only. Gender was not significantly associated with lower attendance classes in any of the adjusted models, but women were more likely to be in the early drop-out class in the bivariate model (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.12, p=0.02). In gender-stratified models (online supplemental section 5), younger age was associated with the higher attendance class among men but not among women. Teletherapy was linked to the dropout classes for both genders, although the association was weaker among men. Non-peer-led IPT-G was associated with dropout classes among women, whereas facilitator type was not significantly associated with retention among men.

Class membership and depression outcomes

Across all clients, the mean reduction in PHQ-9 score from baseline to end of treatment was 13.0 points with a SD of 4.2 points. All four latent classes had a mean score improvement that is considered clinically significant (>5 points) and large (>10 points), but mean score improvement varied across attendance classes (table 3). The largest score improvement was observed for the high attendance class (13.30, SD 6.15), while the smallest score improvement was observed in the early dropout class (10.32, SD 8.78), a difference that was statistically significant (p<0.001). The moderate attendance class also showed a smaller score improvement than the high attendance class (12.12, SD 35.40) with a large variance around the mean, a difference that was statistically significant (<0.001), although small in magnitude. Score improvement differences between the high attendance class and the late dropout class (12.76, SD 9.28) were both small in magnitude and not statistically significant.

Discussion

We present what is to our knowledge the first analysis of real-world attendance patterns in community-based IPT-G for depression treatment at scale in SSA. The majority of IPT-G clients were classified as having high attendance, with the second largest group of clients classified as having moderate attendance across eight sessions of therapy. This is a highly encouraging finding in the context of group therapy largely facilitated by lay health workers, which is often assumed to face major retention challenges in resource-limited settings. Importantly, all retention classes showed clinically significant improvement in depression symptoms, although the early drop-out class had the smallest improvement. This is consistent with previous findings on the negative consequences of premature termination from therapy on symptom reduction.13 38 Our findings and existing literature reinforce the importance of investing in strategies to promote early engagement and sustained participation in therapy.

Our findings align with a pilot study of IPT-G led by trained non-specialists in South Africa, in which a majority of participants completed therapy, although with a much smaller sample size.39 Our study was able to leverage a large sample size in a well-established, scaled-up depression treatment programme to classify retention patterns and identify factors associated with each retention pattern. These results are reassuring, suggesting that high retention can be maintained as IPT-G programmes grow to large scales. However, our results also underscore that therapy dropout remains a challenge: 1 in 10 clients was classified in either the early or late drop-out group, despite the programme being free of cost and largely community based.

Our study adds to evidence regarding predictors of retention in mental health services. These findings align with existing studies showing that older individuals and those experiencing more severe symptoms are more likely to engage in treatment.38 40 41 Surveys in multiple low- and middle-income countries covering multiple mental disorders suggest that premature therapy termination is more common among individuals with mild to moderate symptoms than those with more severe symptoms.42 Symptom severity and the desire for relief may be a motivator for therapy completion. We also found that high therapy attendance was more common in therapy groups facilitated by peer facilitators. Peer facilitators, many of whom are former IPT-G clients and often reside in or near the communities where therapy occurs, may serve as relatable role models who help reduce stigma and foster trust. This finding is thematically consistent with the WHO’s World Mental Health Survey, which found that community-based settings can support better treatment retention than medical settings.40 Our study supports the effectiveness of community-based mental health services and the training of local facilitators.

We found that the IPT-G modality had the strongest association with dropout, with clients in teletherapy significantly more likely to belong to two drop-out groups compared with those in in-person sessions. Several plausible explanations may explain this finding. Digital access and literacy remain substantial barriers in low-resource settings, with StrongMinds teletherapy clients often relying on shared mobile phones (eg, from partner or a neighbour), and were more likely to miss sessions when phones were unavailable or out of battery. Network disruptions and power outages can further compromise the quality and continuity of teletherapy sessions.43 44 Teletherapy in group settings may also reduce clients’ sense of accountability or engagement, as some individuals may remain silent or disengaged. In-person group therapy, on the other hand, may offer more structure, routine and social support. While teletherapy remains a valuable modality, especially when in-person access is limited, our findings suggest additional support (technology access, digital literacy training, etc) may be needed to improve engagement and retention. Individual preferences and comfort with technology may also play a role, particularly during the rapid shift to teletherapy during the pandemic.45 46 Further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms driving dropout from teletherapy in resource-limited settings.

In contrast to some prior studies, we found that gender was not significantly associated with drop-out class membership in our primary analysis. In gender-stratified analyses, retention classes were similar, with nearly 90% of clients of both men and women classified in the high and moderate attendance groups. Notably, younger men were significantly more likely to be in the high attendance class than younger women, contrasting a trend where men often drop out more frequently.47 The lack of a gender difference in our overall analysis may be related to context-specific gender norms, or to programme implementation factors. For example, the availability of both in-person and teletherapy options may have bolstered retention among younger men, as men are more likely to have more digital assets than women in low-income settings.48 Teletherapy may also reduce barriers men traditionally face, such as limited availability during working hours, stigma and reluctance to engage in community gatherings,49 by providing greater anonymity, autonomy and convenience. In our stratified results, teletherapy was a less significant predictor of drop-out among men compared with women. Nevertheless, further research is needed on scaling community-based depression therapy among men, given that the majority of StrongMinds clients are women.

The current study has limitations. First, the programme was implemented in 2020 and 2021 when the COVID-19 pandemic was ongoing. This crisis exacerbated depression triggers such as social isolation and economic hardship50 51 and also led to adaptations of IPT-G delivery, including a teletherapy option. Generalisation of findings, particularly teletherapy’s association with lower attendance, to other time periods should be approached with caution and revisited as new data become available. Additionally, due to limitations of the data available, we were not able to assess the impact of IPT-G on clients’ daily functioning, which is an important metric beyond depression symptom reduction. Future studies should investigate additional outcomes of IPT-G effectiveness beyond subjective symptoms of depression. We also acknowledge that the current study lacks a qualitative component, which would provide deeper insight into therapy retention barriers and help inform IPT-G scalability. We are currently conducting preliminary qualitative analysis to better understand factors influencing IPT-G retention. To assess the robustness of findings, we conducted a complete case analysis, which produced similar results in latent retention class structures and some reversals in the direction of associations, particularly for IPT-G modality and facilitator type (online supplemental table S5). These covariates were included in the imputation model as they were correlates of missingness in age, attendance and baseline depression severity (online supplemental table S1), so some difference in imputed and complete case results is expected. Additional details are provided in online supplemental section 1.

Finally, while a full comparison is beyond the scope of this study, it is important to contextualise IPT-G’s use alongside other evidence-based therapy modalities for depression, such as cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT). CBT has been found to be effective at treating depression globally, but it often requires trained clinicians and more structured training.52 53 In contrast, IPT-G has been adapted for use and scaled in low-resource settings as it is group-based and well-suited to be facilitated by trained lay providers who are often former clients. Similar to StrongMinds’ IPT-G model, the Friendship Bench programme in Zimbabwe delivers CBT through trained community health workers, although it is typically conducted individually and in clinic settings.54

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate retention in community-based group psychotherapy delivered at scale in low-income rural settings in SSA. We found that, despite barriers, most IPT-G clients exhibit high attendance across depression therapy sessions. However, a portion of clients, particularly those with younger age and with less severe baseline depression, is more likely to drop out early from IPT-G and have correspondingly smaller reductions in depression symptoms. Further investigation is needed to understand the determinants of IPT-G engagement and identify potential areas for innovation in IPT-G implementation.
